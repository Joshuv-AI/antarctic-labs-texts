Enhancing self-regulated learning: a comparison of medical students’ self-assessment and faculty evaluations on communication skills in simulated patient interviews
Authors: Hülya Akan, Hamza Yasin Delibaş, Serhat Koran, Pakize Yi̇ği̇t, Özlem Tanriöver
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelf-assessment enables medical students to gain an in-depth understanding of their own strengths and weaknesses, as part of their self-regulated learning which is an important factor for lifelong learning, and to set personal development. The aim of this study was to compare first year medical students’ self-evaluation of their performance in interviewing a simulated patient within the scope of communication skills training with the evaluation of trainers to give feedback as part of the training.MethodsThis study was a cross-sectional analytical study and included all first-year students of Istanbul Medipol University Faculty of Medicine. Two separate evaluation forms were prepared for educators and students by adapting already used ones. Students and educators completed the online version of the forms after the simulated patient interview. Descriptive statistics, Cohen’s Kappa coefficient to measure inter-rater reliability, correlation analysis and dependent sample t test were used to measure the agreement in Likert scale questions.ResultsAll first-year students in charge of the course, 193 students in total, participated in the study. Of the students participating in the study, 64.8% were female and 35.2% were male. In two-category questions, moderate agreement was observed in five questions and good agreement in two questions. No difference was observed between trainers and students in the overall global 10-point rating scale. In Likert-type questions, a moderate difference was observed between trainers and students in some questions. The self-assessment of male students was statistically higher than that of female students (p < 0.05).ConclusionsFirst-year medical students generally aligned with faculty evaluations in their overall communication skills, they tended to rate themselves more positively in certain sub-skills. Notably, male students demonstrated a more positive self-assessment compared to their female counterparts, whereas female students tended to assess themselves more realistically These results highlight the need for a more structured approach to self-evaluation in medical education, ensuring that all students develop accurate self-perception and receive targeted support to enhance their communication competencies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07794-4.

Background

Communication skills play a vital role in medical practice and constitute the cornerstone of an effective patient-physician relationship. In various studies, it has been reported that physicians with high communication skills are more successful in increasing patient satisfaction [1–3]ensuring adherence to treatment [4, 5] and improving patient outcomes [6] and are less exposed to burnout [7, 8] and violence [9]. Training of healthcare professionals in communication skills increases both performance and self-efficacy [9, 10]. In this context, communication skills training in under-graduate medical education is critical for professional development.

Feedback provided by trainers and other sources is critical in order to obtain a high level of efficiency from vertically structured and formation-oriented applied trainings such as communication skills. In particular, feedback from multiple sources has been shown to improve students’ clinical skills and communication competence [11, 12].

An important component of multiple sources is the individual themselves. Current literature shows that self-assessment of clinical skills in medical schools significantly increases students’ ability to assess their own competence. Self-assessment enables medical students to gain an in-depth understanding of their own strengths and weaknesses, which is an important factor for self regulated learning, lifelong learning, and to set personal development and realistic learning goals. In this way, they take responsibility for their own education and development and active learning is encouraged. Self-assessment improves critical thinking, self-regulation, reflective practice skills and motivation, as it increases awareness of learning processes and outcomes. As medical students engage in self-assessment, they become more adept at receiving feedback from their peers and trainers. Self-performance assessment techniques bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application by allowing students to critically evaluate their own interactions and clinical skills, especially during communication courses with simulated patients. The use of structured reflection forms and peer feedback mechanisms encourages students to identify their strengths and weaknesses, providing a comprehensive understanding of their performance. Moreover, integrating self-assessment into the curriculum fosters a culture of continuous improvement and accountability among students, ultimately preparing them for real-world medical settings [13, 14].

The existing literature on medical student self-assessment suggests mixed results. Compared to educators’ evaluations, some studies show that students overestimate their self-evaluation and others underestimate it. The dynamic interrelationships and underlying tensions between the components of self-assessment make its reliability open to debate [15]. On the other hand, self-assessment methods are valuable for formative rather than summative evaluation.

In our medical school, communication skills trainings are vertically structured in the preclinical period, conducted using many different learning methods, and simulated patient interviews constitute the majority of the training content. Students are evaluated with checklists and graded scales during both training and exams and feedback is given by trainers and simulated patients.

It is planned to include self-assessment methods among these evaluation approaches. In this context, the present study aimed to investigate the consistency between students’ self-evaluation and trainers’ evaluation of students’ performance in interviewing a simulated patient as part of the communication skills and history-taking training. Therefore; we hypothesized that: there would be no significant difference between students’ self-evaluation scores and trainers’ evaluation scores.

Method

This study was a cross-sectional analytical study and included all first-year students of Istanbul Medipol University Faculty of Medicine. To clarify the programme, cirruculum is described below.

Preclinical communication skills programme

Communication skills training is a component of the Basic Clinical Skills vertical corridor within the preclinical curriculum. In this course, all required skills defined by the National Core Competencies are delivered in a simulated environment using manikins and simulated patients. It is an obligatory course and should be completed before clinical years.

The Department of Family Medicine coordinates the three-year course; however, many trainers from both basic and clinical sciences also contribute to the instructional team. In addition, approximately twenty trained and experienced simulated patients are involved in the program. These simulated patients not only participate in the sessions but also occasionally provide feedback to students.

During training sessions, students sometimes conduct interviews in small groups—comprising two, three, or four students—simultaneously. However, most of the time, and especially during examinations, they conducted the interviews indivudially. Total course time is different for all terms. In the first year for communication, there are six hours theoretical, four hours drama work for each student, and four hours of simulated patient interviews for groups comprised of 20 students. The communication objectives of the course are structured as follows:

First Year: Introduction to basic communication principles, interpersonal interaction, and self-awareness. Students apply basic communication techniques and take a history of the chief complaint.Second Year: Students demonstrate the use of basic communication techniques and motivational interviewing. They take a detailed patient history and provide information to patients, including family members and pediatric patients.Third Year: Students deliver bad news, perform motivational interviews, take a complete patient history organized by organ systems, address sensitive issues, provide consultations, take culturally sensitive histories, and manage difficult patients.

As learning methods, the program employs interactive seminars, small group activities, drama-based exercises, video screenings, video demonstrations, trainer demonstrations, simulated patient interviews, and one-on-one feedback sessions. Scenarios and level of them are chosen according to the learning objectives of that term. As increasing years and increasing medical knowledge scenarios are getting more complexed. Also, scenarios are horizontally integrated with the core cirruculum which is organ system based and organised in comittes.

For assessment, midterm written examinations, direct observation, and primarily Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) are used. Self-assessment is in the pilot stage before implementation.

Students

Students received theoretical and practical trainings -scenario writing, role-playing, small and large group discussions- on patient-physician communication, taking the history of the main complaint within the scope of year 1 basic medical practices and communication vertical corridor. As the last application of the standard training program of the faculty, they are asked to do a patient-physician interview in a simulated environment.

Evaluation forms

The forms to be used in the study were developed by consensus of the researchers from forms already in use for training. The forms currently used by the faculty for patient-physician interview and communication assessment were revised to include only communication, and two separate assessment forms for trainers and students were prepared by consensus of the researchers. The forms consisted of identical questions with different verbs for trainers and students, including thirteen yes-no questions, ten five-point Likert questions, and one question asking for a general evaluation of communication on a 10-point scale.

Feedback forms to be filled in by students and trainers were uploaded to Google form and shared.

Study process

The study lasted for 4 weeks in total between 15.05.2024–15.06.2024, the students were organized in 16 groups, each group consisted of 12–13 students and the professional skills laboratory was organized so that 4 groups would come every week. Before the study, the students were given all the necessary information about the system and its functioning in the laboratory.

OSCE rooms were used for the study and 1 standard patient and 1 observer trainer were present in each room; total two consisted two trainers. Each trainer assessed about 20 students in a day. Simulated patients were given the same scenario in all areas. After the students entered the patient interview rooms one by one and made their interviews, they filled out the evaluation forms on Google forms on 4 tablets in a separate area of the laboratory. Two separate observers were assigned to monitor this order.

The trainers filled out the trainer forms on the tablet while the student was during the patient-physician interview or at the end of the interview.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics and Cohen’s Kappa coefficient were used for dichotomous questions, and correlation analysis and dependent sample t-test were used for multi-scored questions. p < 0.05 was considered significant in terms of difference. Ethical consent

The ethical approval of the study was approved by Istanbul Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Studies Ethics Committee with the number 491. We declare that this study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki - Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Participants.

Before the educational session, the study was explained to each group during the routine briefing and repeated for all groups. It was clearly stated that participation in the study was voluntary and not mandatory. Informed written consent was obtained prior to completing the self-evaluation form, from all participants.

Results

Total number of students in the first year was 210. A total of 193 first-year students participated in this study, 17 students did not attend to the class. Of the students participating in the study, 64.8% were female and 35.2% were male.

Kuder-Richardson reliability analysis for binary-valued questions was 0.343 for students and 0.323 for evaluators, which was considered as low reliability. The Cronbach’s Alpha value for Likert-type questions was 0.897 for students, which was considered high and very good reliability, and 0.783 for evaluators, which was considered acceptable and good reliability.

When the degree of agreement between students and assessors was examined in dichotomous questions (yes-no), moderate agreement (0.21–0.40) was observed in the answers given to “introducing oneself”, “starting with an open-ended question”, “using medical terms”, “dual question”, “making eye contact”. A better than average agreement (0.41–0.60) was observed for the questions “summarizing the interview” and “what the patient would like to add” (Table 2).

Discussion

This study examined the alignment between first-year medical students’ self-assessment and faculty evaluations of communication skills during simulated patient interviews. Our findings suggest that while students’ overall self-evaluations were generally in agreement with faculty assessments, they tended to rate themselves more positively in certain sub-skills. Interestingly, male students demonstrated higher self-evaluations compared to female students, indicating potential differences in self-perception and confidence levels. These results underscore the importance of incorporating structured self-assessment processes into communication skills training to enhance students’ ability to critically reflect on their strengths and areas for improvement. By refining self-evaluation practices, medical educators can foster more accurate self-awareness and support the development of essential communication competencies necessary for clinical practice.

In traditional assessment, the role of the assessor or supervisor is often seen as a power over the assessed and assessment is done against students rather than with them. In contrast, self-assessment changes this dynamic. It transforms assessment into a collaborative activity in which students are involved in establishing their own criteria and reflecting on their performance. This participatory approach can increase understanding, motivation and credibility in the assessment process because students are more likely to adhere to the standards they have helped to establish [16].

The literature states that for proper self-assessment, there should be transparency in the educational environment, an environment in which students can express their strengths and weaknesses without reservation, a trustworthy educational environment, supportive feedback, peer support, standardization of the educational environment and assessment criteria [16]. We believe that we have met these criteria to a large extent in this study.

In Blanch-Harrington’s article discussing the results of three meta-analyses of studies in which under graduation medical students self-evaluated their own performance, students evaluated themselves as lower or higher than they actually were according to selected criteria, and the results varied according to the objective criteria and analysis methods [17]. Students may not always be able to accurately evaluate their own performance according to selected criteria. Looking at the overall outcomes of our study, the students’ self-assessment of their performance in the overall communication assessment was in line with the trainers’ assessment [18, 19]. On the other hand, when individual questions are analyzed and analyzed by gender, there are discrepancies between instructors’ and students’ evaluations. Our students come to class prepared from the course booklet, which is provided to them at the beginning of the academic year and includes checklists and scales for the assessment and evaluation of the course. Before the simulated patient interview, they prepare for what points to pay attention to with scenario and drama activities. Self-assessment, like other skills, is a skill that needs to be learned and students need to be prepared before self-assessment [19, 20]. The results of meta-analyses also show that students tend to assess their performance more accurately when they have more information, especially when tasks and criteria are measured objectively [17, 20].

On the other hand, on a question-by-question basis, students evaluated themselves more positively than the trainers in the areas where there were differences in Likert-type questions. This may be due to various factors. It has also been shown in the literature that familiarity with the task affects self-assessment accuracy [18]. Although there was preparation, it was the students’ first simulated patient interview experience and may have affected their self-assessment. Students at the beginning of their training have been shown to perform less accurate self-assessments than students in later years, suggesting that self-assessment skills may improve with experience and practice [17]. On the other hand, there are studies that claim the opposite, i.e. no difference in accuracy despite regular self-assessment [20]. The main point here is the positive impact of self-assessment on the student’s professional development, not its incorporation into the student’s graded assessment [20]. As a result, students tended to overestimate their own skills. Since we were not able to make an evaluation on an individual student basis, it is not possible to make a comment on self-esteem perceptions in this study, but it is possible that personality traits also affect self-evaluation processes. Students may evaluate themselves differently and more positively than they would according to objective criteria because they do not understand what is expected of them, their general self-confidence is high because medical students are students with high past school achievement, ideal performance or effort is scored rather than actual, and defense mechanisms are activated against low performance [16]. Another point is the trainers’ own biases. Although standardized scales were used, trainers’ feedback to students may not be completely objective [19–21]. But, we believe that instructor bias may have had little effect on the results. In any case, this study is the first step in efforts to make self-assessment a part of learning in communication skills training. We consider these results to be instructive in terms of where trainers should give more feedback to students on an individual basis. This is because overestimating self-efficacy can be a barrier to development and vice versa. In this context, collaboration between the trainer and the assessment step can be more fruitful than the assessment of the trainer alone [20].

When we examined the relationships between the 2023–2024 OSCE score, students’ self-assessments, and educators’ assessments, the results revealed weak but statistically significant positive correlations between OSCE scores and both educators’ assessments (r = 0.271, p < 0.001) and students’ self-assessments (r = 0.237, p < 0.001), indicating that while alignment exists, self-perception may still differ from objective performance. These findings are in line with the observations of Knof et al. (2024), who reported a pronounced Dunning-Kruger effect among first-year medical students, where discrepancies between actual performance and self-assessment were common, particularly among lower-performing individuals who tended to overestimate their abilities [22].

Although some exceptions exist, the literature generally suggests that female students tend to underestimate their abilities, while male students are more likely to overestimate theirs. This gender-based pattern in self-assessment accuracy may be influenced by various factors, including social norms, cultural expectations, and individual traits [23]. Interestingly, our study revealed a slightly different pattern: female students showed a stronger correlation between their self-assessments and educators’ ratings (r = 0.278, p < 0.001), while male students’ correlations were weaker or non-significant. These findings, suggest that gender may continue to shape self-assessment accuracy in complex ways, potentially influenced by learning environments and feedback mechanisms [22]. Overall, the results emphasize the importance of supporting all students—particularly in the early stages of medical education—through structured feedback and reflective practices that enhance metacognitive awareness and mitigate the influence of gender-related biases.

The limitations of the study are that the students are in their first year of medical school and are being trained in a simulated patient environment for the first time. It only provides us with a cross-sectional picture and does not provide data on which factors influence the self-assessment processes. On the other hand, since it is planned to evaluate the students’ own performance and monitor their progress over the years, this limitation is also considered as an advantage for the researchers as a baseline study.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that while first-year medical students generally aligned with faculty evaluations in their overall communication skills, they tended to rate themselves more positively in certain sub-skills. Notably, male students demonstrated a more favorable self-assessment compared to their female counterparts. The importance of self-assessment in terms of lifelong learning, identifying educational and self-development needs and knowing one’s own limitations is clear; On the other hand, these results highlight the need for a more structured approach to self-evaluation, supported by clearly defined criteria, scenario-based reflection, and trainer-facilitated feedback sessions to enhance learner insight. This would help ensure that all students develop accurate self-perception and receive targeted support to improve their communication competencies. There is a need for further longitudinal studies to explore how self-assessment contributes to professional development, lifelong learning, self-awareness of limitations, and the ability to plan for personal growth.
