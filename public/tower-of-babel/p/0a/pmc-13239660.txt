Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease as a systemic risk factor for cancer: a nationwide cohort study in Korea
Authors: Joo-Hee Kim, Ho Suk Kang, Dae Myoung Yoo, Kyeong Min Han, Nan Young Kim, Hyo Geun Choi, Mi Jung Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is increasingly recognised as a systemic condition with extrapulmonary comorbidities including malignancy. While the association between COPD and lung cancer is well established, evidence for extrapulmonary cancers, particularly after accounting for lifestyle, demographic and socioeconomic factors, remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide population-based cohort study using the Korean National Health Insurance Service–Health Screening Cohort (2002–2019). After exclusions and propensity score overlap weighting, 532 174 patients with COPD and 1 891 601 matched controls were included. COPD and exacerbations were defined using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes and treatment records. Cox proportional hazards models and E-value analyses were used to estimate associations and assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding.ResultsDuring up to 16 years of follow-up, COPD was associated with a 52% higher overall cancer risk (HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.50 to 1.53), independent of demographic, lifestyle and clinical factors. Increased risks were observed for 11 of 14 site-specific cancers, with the strongest associations for lung (HR 3.68), bladder (HR 1.58), haematological (HR 1.45) and kidney cancers (HR 1.43). COPD exacerbations were also associated with increased overall cancer risk (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.46 to 1.50), with broadly similar patterns across site-specific cancers. Associations were consistent across sex, age and smoking strata and were unlikely to be explained by unmeasured confounding based on E-value analyses.ConclusionsCOPD and exacerbations were associated with increased risks of overall and multiple site-specific cancers beyond the lungs. These findings support the systemic nature of COPD.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory condition characterised by persistent airflow limitation and chronic inflammation, encompassing emphysema, central airway inflammation with mucociliary dysfunction, and structural changes in the small airways, including bronchiolitis.1 It poses a major global health burden, affecting over 170 million people and ranking as the third leading cause of death worldwide, according to the WHO.2 Between 1990 and 2015, COPD prevalence rose by 44.2%, reaching 174.5 million cases, and continues to increase, particularly in ageing populations and regions with high exposure to tobacco smoke and air pollution, including Korea.35 In the USA, prevalence has remained stable overall but increased among older adults, rural residents and ever-smokers, highlighting persistent disparities.6

Beyond its pulmonary manifestations, COPD is increasingly recognised as a systemic inflammatory condition with numerous extrapulmonary complications.7 Among these, malignancy—particularly lung cancer—is one of the most serious comorbidities.7 Meta-analyses indicate that COPD is associated with a two- to fourfold increased risk of lung cancer compared with the general population, even after adjustment for smoking status, reporting a prevalence of 2.06%–5.08% among patients with COPD versus less than 1% in non-COPD individuals and up to 9% in current smokers.8 While smoking remains a major contributor to this risk,9 recent findings indicate that COPD may increase the risk of malignancy even among never-smokers.10 For example, a Korean national cohort study found a 3.2-fold higher risk of lung cancer in never-smokers with COPD.10 Given that 39%–41.1% of patients with COPD are never-smokers, especially in East Asia,1012 the malignancy burden attributable to COPD may be underestimated.

The relationship between COPD and extrapulmonary cancers is less well defined and far less studied than the established association with lung cancer.1316 Emerging epidemiological evidence points to a modestly increased risk of non-pulmonary malignancies—including gastrointestinal (stomach, colorectal, pancreatic), genitourinary (bladder, kidney), prostate and haematologic cancers—likely reflecting shared risk factors such as smoking, though results remain inconsistent.131517 Importantly, such associations are underexplored in Asian populations, particularly with respect to appropriate adjustment for lifestyle, demographic and socioeconomic influences. Most prior studies have been conducted in Western settings or have focused narrowly on lung cancer in smokers, limiting their generalisability to more diverse populations.13 15 16 A recent meta-analysis further underscored regional heterogeneity, showing the highest prevalence of COPD-associated lung cancer in the Western Pacific region (7.78%; 95% CI 5.06% to 10.5%), compared with the Americas (3.25%; 95% CI 0.88% to 5.61%) and Europe (3.21%; 95% CI 2.36% to 4.06%).8 While such differences may be partially attributable to socioeconomic disparities,8 East Asian-specific patterns were not delineated, highlighting the need for region-focused studies that integrate socioeconomic context.

Another underexplored dimension is the potential role of COPD exacerbations—acute episodes of clinical deterioration—in modulating cancer risk.21 Exacerbations are known to accelerate disease progression and intensify systemic inflammation,7 22 possibly enhancing tumourigenic processes beyond the lungs.21 However, the impact of exacerbations on overall and site-specific cancer risk remains unclear, warranting further investigation.

Accordingly, this study sought to quantify the overall and site-specific cancer risks among patients with COPD and to examine how these risks vary by exacerbation status, smoking and age, while accounting for lifestyle, demographic and socioeconomic factors that may confound these associations. To this end, we leveraged a nationwide Korean health insurance database encompassing more than 2.4 million individuals and employed advanced statistical techniques—including propensity score weighting and E-value analysis—to minimise bias and improve the validity of the findings.

Methods

Study design and data source

This longitudinal cohort study used data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service–Health Screening Cohort (KNHIS-HSC), which comprises anonymised health screening records and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes for individuals aged 40–79 years.23 The cohort includes 514 866 participants who were randomly selected from individuals who underwent health screening in 2002–2003 and were followed up until 2019.23 The KNHIS covers more than 98% of the Korean population, ensuring high representativeness.24

The requirement for informed consent was waived owing to data anonymisation. Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research, as the analysis was based on anonymised secondary data from a national administrative database. The study adhered to national ethical guidelines for observational research and was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement.

Study population

Between 2002 and 2019, 663 982 participants with COPD and 2 525 984 control participants without COPD were identified from the KNHIS database. Exclusions included missing health screening data (n=4541 for COPD; n=31 042 for controls), missing sociodemographic information (n=14 659 for COPD; n=71 928 for controls), prior cancer diagnosis (n=37 404 for COPD; n=93 409 for controls), death before the index date (n=1 67 054 for COPD) or cancer diagnosis during the 3-year washout period (2002–2004) to avoid inclusion of pre-existing or subclinical malignancies (n=270 890 for COPD).

The index date for participants with COPD was defined as the date of the first COPD treatment claim accompanied by an ICD-10 diagnostic code in the health insurance claims database. For control participants, the index date was randomly assigned to match the age and sex distribution of the COPD group.

After applying the exclusion criteria, the final analytic cohort comprised 532 174 participants with COPD and 1 891 601 control participants without COPD. Propensity score overlap weighting was applied to balance baseline characteristics between the groups. Participants with COPD were further stratified according to exacerbation status (figure 1).

Definition of COPD and exacerbations

Patients with COPD were identified using diagnostic codes and medication records from the claims database.10 25 COPD was operationally defined as at least two claims with ICD-10 codes for unspecified chronic bronchitis (J42.x), emphysema (J43.x; excluding J43.0, unilateral pulmonary emphysema or Macleod’s syndrome), or other COPD (J44.x), together with at least two prescriptions for COPD-related medications. These medications included long-acting muscarinic antagonists, long-acting β2-agonists, inhaled corticosteroid/long-acting β2-agonist combinations, short-acting muscarinic antagonists, short-acting β2-agonists, short-acting muscarinic antagonist/short-acting β2-agonist combinations, phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors, systemic β-agonists and methylxanthines.

This claims-based definition has been widely used in Korean nationwide administrative database studies to improve diagnostic specificity by requiring both diagnostic coding and treatment records. In routine clinical practice in Korea, COPD diagnoses are generally established using spirometry in accordance with national and international guidelines, and the availability of pulmonary function test equipment has been reported to be 100% in tertiary hospitals, 98.27% in general hospitals, 87.46% in secondary hospitals and 62.26% in private clinics.25

Patients with COPD were further classified as having exacerbated COPD if they experienced hospitalisation or emergency department visits for COPD.21

Covariates and outcomes

Covariates included demographics (age, sex, income, residential area), lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol use, body mass index), clinical parameters (blood pressure, fasting glucose, cholesterol), and comorbidity burden assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), excluding respiratory and cancer diagnoses.26 27

The primary outcome was overall and site-specific cancer incidence, identified using ICD-10 codes together with special cancer claim codes (V193, V194), which are assigned only to pathologically confirmed cases. This dual-coding approach, widely used in Korean administrative databases, improves diagnostic specificity and reduces misclassification.

The secondary outcome examined the association between COPD exacerbation status (exacerbated vs non-exacerbated COPD) and the likelihood of developing overall or specific cancers. Fourteen cancer types were evaluated, including lung, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, haematologic and female-specific cancers.

Statistical analysis

Propensity score overlap weighting was applied based on age, sex, income, region, body mass index, blood pressure, glucose, cholesterol and CCI to balance baseline characteristics.28 29 Overlap weighting was used to create a pseudo-population in which the distribution of measured baseline covariates was balanced between COPD and control groups.

Covariate balance was assessed using standardised differences, with values ≤0.20 indicating acceptable balance.30 HRs and 95% CIs for cancer risk were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used for time-to-event analysis. Subgroup analyses were conducted by age, sex and smoking status. The proportional hazards assumption was tested and met.

Finally, E-values were calculated to assess the sensitivity of the observed associations to potential unmeasured confounding.31 The E-value represents the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome to fully explain away the observed association; larger E-values indicate greater stability of the observed associations.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute).

Association between COPD and cancer risks

During 16 years of follow-up, cancer incidence was higher among participants with COPD (20.57 per 1000 person-years) than among controls (12.98 per 1000 person-years), corresponding to an incidence rate difference of 7.59 per 1000 person-years (95% CI 7.44 to 7.73). Overall, COPD was associated with a 52% higher likelihood of cancer (HR 1.52; 95% CI 1.50 to 1.53; p<0.001), and this association was consistent across subgroups (table 2).

Subgroup analyses

In sex-stratified analyses, both males and females with COPD showed increased likelihood of lung cancer (HRs 3.68 and 2.24, respectively). Among males, notable increases were observed for bladder cancer (HR 1.76) and thyroid cancer (HR 1.41), whereas among females increased risks were observed for kidney cancer (HR 1.41) and haematologic malignancies (HR 1.38). No significant associations were identified for breast, cervical or uterine cancers (online supplemental table 2).

When stratified by age, overall cancer likelihood remained elevated in both groups (<65 years: HR 1.47; ≥65 years: HR 1.52). COPD was associated with increased likelihood of gastric, thyroid, lung, bladder, gallbladder and biliary duct, kidney, prostate and haematologic cancers in both age groups. Pancreatic cancer was associated with COPD only among participants aged <65 years (HR 1.10), whereas colorectal cancer and hepatic cancer were associated with COPD only among those aged ≥65 years (online supplemental table 3).

When stratified by smoking status, the association between COPD and cancer was strongest and most consistent for lung cancer, which remained significantly elevated across past smokers (HR 4.15), current smokers (HR 3.88) and never-smokers (HR 3.23). Elevated risks for several extrapulmonary cancers were also observed among former and current smokers, including bladder cancer (HR 1.62) and gastric cancer (HR 1.14). Among never-smokers, COPD remained most strongly associated with lung cancer, while associations with extrapulmonary cancers were generally weaker (online supplemental table 4).

Impact of COPD exacerbations on cancer risk

COPD exacerbations were associated with an increased overall cancer risk (HR 1.48; 95% CI 1.46 to 1.50; p<0.001). Among site-specific cancers, the strongest association was observed for lung cancer (HR 2.31), followed by haematologic malignancies (HR 1.36), bladder cancer (HR 1.19), colorectal cancer (HR 1.16), gastric cancer (HR 1.12), thyroid cancer (HR 1.12) and prostate cancer (HR 1.07) (all p values <0.01) (table 3).

Sex-stratified analyses showed increased risks in both males and females with exacerbated COPD (online supplemental table 5). Among males, the highest likelihood was observed for lung cancer (HR 2.32), followed by bladder cancer (HR 1.20) and colorectal cancer (HR 1.19). In females, lung cancer (HR 2.15) remained the most strongly associated cancer, followed by haematologic malignancies (HR 1.19) and thyroid cancer (HR 1.14).

Age-stratified analyses demonstrated that among participants aged <65 years, the highest likelihoods were observed for lung cancer (HR 2.97), haematologic malignancies (HR 1.37) and colorectal cancer (HR 1.31). Among those aged ≥65 years, lung cancer remained the most frequent cancer outcome (HR 2.15) (online supplemental table 6).

Among never-smokers with exacerbated COPD, lung cancer (HR 2.33) showed the strongest association, followed by haematologic malignancies, bladder cancer, colorectal cancer, thyroid cancer and gastric cancer (online supplemental table 7).

E-value sensitivity analysis

E-value analysis was performed to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding. COPD showed a moderate strength of association with overall cancer (E-value 2.40). Among site-specific cancers, lung cancer demonstrated the greatest resistance to unmeasured confounding (HR 3.68; E-value 6.83). Other cancers showed E-values ranging from approximately 1.29 to 2.53, indicating modest to moderate resistance to unmeasured confounding (online supplemental table 8). In contrast, breast cancer, uterine cervix cancer and uterine body cancer showed E-values close to 1.00, suggesting no meaningful association with COPD.

Similarly, COPD exacerbations were associated with an increased likelihood of overall cancer (HR 1.48), with an E-value of 2.31, indicating that a moderately strong unmeasured confounder would be required to fully explain this association (online supplemental table 9). Lung cancer showed the highest E-value (4.04), suggesting particularly strong resistance to unmeasured confounding, whereas other cancers showed more modest E-values ranging from approximately 1.34 to 2.06.

Discussion

In this nationwide Korean cohort including 532 174 patients with COPD and 1 891 661 control participants, COPD was associated with an increased risk of cancer during up to 16 years of follow-up. The incidence rate of cancer was 20.57 per 1000 person-years in the COPD group compared with 12.98 per 1000 person-years in controls, corresponding to an incidence rate difference of 7.59 per 1000 person-years (95% CI 7.44 to 7.73). Overall, COPD was associated with a 52% higher risk of cancer (HR 1.52; 95% CI 1.50 to 1.53). Increased risks were observed not only for lung cancer but also for several extrapulmonary malignancies. E-value analyses suggested that the observed associations—particularly for lung cancer—were robust to unmeasured confounding.

Among site-specific cancers, lung cancer demonstrated the strongest association with COPD (HR 3.68), followed by bladder cancer (HR 1.58), haematologic malignancies (HR 1.45), kidney cancer (HR 1.43), thyroid cancer (HR 1.32) and prostate cancer (HR 1.25). Additional modest but significant associations were observed for several gastrointestinal, pancreatic and hepatobiliary cancers. These findings are broadly consistent with previous epidemiological studies conducted in Europe and East Asia.121520 In a Danish nationwide hospital-based cohort including 236 494 patients with COPD, the standardised incidence ratio (SIR) for any cancer was 3.1 during the first year after COPD diagnosis and 1.4 thereafter, with the most pronounced increase observed for lung cancer (1-year SIR 8.5).15 Elevated risks were also reported for several tobacco-related malignancies, including cancers of the oral cavity, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, liver, pancreas, cervix uteri and urinary tract.15 Similarly, two Taiwanese nationwide studies reported increased overall cancer risks among patients with COPD (SIR 1.2 and adjusted HR 2.8, respectively), with higher incidences of lung cancer as well as extrapulmonary malignancies such as head and neck, oesophageal, liver, colorectal, breast, prostate, central nervous system and haematologic malignancies.6 10 European population-based studies from the UK and Norway (Bergen COPD Cohort Study) further corroborate a consistently elevated risk of lung cancer in COPD populations, with reported incidence rate ratios or ORs generally ranging from 2 to 5,13 32 whereas associations with non-smoking-related cancers have been less consistent.13 Taken together, these findings support a consistent pattern in which COPD is associated with increased risks of lung cancer and several extrapulmonary malignancies across diverse populations, consistent with the elevated risks for lung, bladder, kidney, haematologic and gastrointestinal cancers observed in our study.

In our study, both current and former smokers exhibited increased risks for lung, bladder, pancreatic, stomach and liver cancers, with lung cancer risk highest among former smokers (HR 4.15), followed by current smokers (HR 3.88). Although smoking is a major shared risk factor,33 34 a substantial proportion of patients with COPD—particularly in Asian populations—are never-smokers.10 11 Notably, lung cancer risk remained significantly elevated even among never-smokers with COPD (HR 3.23), suggesting that the association may persist beyond smoking exposure.10 32 However, because detailed smoking metrics such as pack-years and duration were unavailable, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded.

Subgroup analyses further demonstrated that several extrapulmonary cancers were also elevated across smoking categories. Among never-smokers with COPD, increased risks were observed for bladder cancer (HR 1.45), kidney cancer (HR 1.29), haematologic malignancies (HR 1.40) and gastric cancer (HR 1.12). Similar patterns were observed among past and current smokers, with particularly strong associations for bladder, kidney and haematologic cancers. These findings suggest that while smoking likely contributes to the observed cancer burden,12 13 15 20 COPD itself may be associated with increased susceptibility to several malignancies beyond smoking exposure.13 15 Nevertheless, smoking remains an important potential source of residual confounding.33 35 Although smoking status was included in the propensity score weighting and multivariable models, detailed smoking exposure information—including pack-years, smoking duration and passive smoke exposure—was not available in the database. Therefore, residual confounding related to smoking intensity cannot be fully excluded when interpreting these associations.

Although several extrapulmonary cancers showed statistically significant associations with COPD, the magnitude of these effects was generally modest. For example, colorectal cancer showed a small relative increase (HR 1.05), corresponding to incidence rates of 2.71 vs 2.62 per 1000 person-years. Similarly, pancreatic cancer (HR 1.06) showed nearly comparable incidence rates between groups (0.69 vs 0.66 per 1000 person-years). In contrast, cancers with larger relative risks also demonstrated more substantial absolute differences, particularly lung cancer (7.44 vs 1.54 per 1000 person-years). These findings underscore the importance of interpreting both relative and absolute risk measures in large population-based studies.36

The distribution of cancer types in our COPD cohort differed somewhat from national cancer statistics in the general Korean population.37 While thyroid, lung, colorectal, breast and stomach cancers are among the most common malignancies nationally,37 bladder cancer, kidney cancer, gallbladder and bile duct cancers and haematologic malignancies were relatively more prominent among individuals with COPD in our study. Notably, haematologic malignancies were the third most common cancer in our COPD cohort, consistent with Danish data in which they were reported as the second most common malignancy after lung cancer among patients with COPD.15 Previous studies have also reported regional variability in extrapulmonary cancers associated with COPD. For example, UK data reported a modest increase in gastric cancer risk,13 whereas a Canadian study based on Quebec administrative databases found no significant association.16 In Asia, liver, colorectal, breast, prostate, stomach, head and neck cancers and haematologic malignancies have been reported as prominent among COPD populations in Taiwan,12 14 while urinary tract and head and neck cancers have been more frequently observed in Japan.20 Taken together, these findings suggest that COPD may be associated with a broader pattern of systemic cancer susceptibility, with regional heterogeneity in the distribution of extrapulmonary malignancies.

Sex-stratified analyses showed that lung cancer remained the most strongly associated malignancy in both men (HR 4.05) and women (HR 2.24) with COPD, although the magnitude of association was greater in men.8 Interestingly, relatively higher excess risks of kidney cancer and haematologic malignancies were observed among women with COPD despite these cancers being generally more common in men in the general population.38 In contrast, female-specific cancers such as breast, cervical and uterine cancers were not significantly associated with COPD in our study, although some large-scale studies have reported increased risks of cancers such as breast and cervical cancer among patients with COPD.1215 In our study, these cancers showed E-values close to 1.00, suggesting minimal evidence for meaningful associations. This finding is consistent with UK population-based studies reporting little or no elevation in these cancers after adjustment for smoking.13

Cancer patterns also varied by age. Patients with COPD were predominantly older adults, reflecting the age distribution of the disease.4 Because both COPD and cancer incidence increase with age in elderly patients generally aged less than 70 years,15 this demographic pattern likely contributes to the higher occurrence of several malignancies among older patients with COPD.15 Accordingly, colorectal and hepatic cancers were more frequently observed among older individuals, whereas pancreatic cancer appeared relatively more common among younger patients with COPD in our study. However, the absolute differences in incidence rates were modest.36 One possible explanation for this pattern is survival bias, as pancreatic cancer is often diagnosed at an advanced stage and carries a poor prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate below 10%.39 Consequently, individuals who develop pancreatic cancer at older ages may die before being captured during long-term follow-up,40 which could lead to a relatively higher observed proportion of pancreatic cancer among younger patients with COPD.

Several biological mechanisms have been proposed that may potentially contribute to these associations. Chronic systemic inflammation in COPD may promote oxidative stress, DNA damage and impaired immune surveillance, while inflammatory mediators originating from the lungs may also affect extrapulmonary organs through systemic circulation.1 22 34 In addition, shared genetic susceptibility has been suggested, with genome-wide association studies identifying loci such as FAM13A that have been associated with both COPD and several cancers, including lung and renal cancers.18 41 Variants in DOCK1 have also been reported in relation to COPD and certain gastrointestinal cancers.17

A history of COPD exacerbations was associated with an increased overall cancer risk (HR 1.48). Among site-specific cancers, the strongest association was observed for lung cancer (HR 2.31), followed by haematologic malignancies (HR 1.36), bladder cancer (HR 1.19), colorectal cancer (HR 1.16), gastric cancer (HR 1.12), thyroid cancer (HR 1.12) and prostate cancer (HR 1.07), showing a broadly similar pattern to that observed in the overall COPD population. Exacerbation-stratified analyses also indicated elevated cancer risks across smoking categories, with somewhat stronger associations observed among current and former smokers. Exacerbation history may reflect more advanced disease severity.21 As exacerbations were defined using hospitalisation or emergency department visits, they may preferentially capture clinically significant events recorded through healthcare utilisation.25 The observed associations may therefore be influenced by differences in underlying risk profiles, competing risks and healthcare utilisation, and should be interpreted with caution.

This study has several strengths. It represents one of the largest population-based investigations examining the association between COPD and cancer risk. The cohort included more than 2.4 million individuals with up to 16 years of follow-up. In addition, the use of propensity score overlap weighting, extensive covariate adjustment and E-value sensitivity analyses strengthened the reliability of the findings.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because this was an observational study, causal relationships cannot be definitively established. Second, COPD was defined using administrative claims data, and spirometric measurements were not available in the database. Consequently, information on pulmonary function parameters such as the forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity ratio, GOLD stage, airflow limitation severity and COPD phenotypes could not be assessed, which may introduce some degree of diagnostic misclassification. In addition, because administrative claims databases contain only diagnostic codes recorded for reimbursement purposes, the authors were unable to directly verify spirometric findings or confirm diagnoses through chart review. Since COPD exacerbations were identified using hospitalisation or emergency department visits recorded in the claims database, the definition was based on healthcare utilisation and may not capture the full spectrum of exacerbation severity.

Another potential limitation is detection or surveillance bias. Participants with missing health screening or sociodemographic data were excluded, which may have introduced selection bias, as these variables included smoking status, alcohol consumption and body mass index. Excluded individuals may have differed systematically from those included, potentially in terms of overall health status, socioeconomic position or smoking behaviour. In addition, patients with COPD may have more frequent healthcare encounters, including outpatient visits, hospital admissions and diagnostic testing,25 which may increase opportunities for cancer detection compared with the general population. In Korea, healthcare utilisation among patients with COPD is generally high, with long-term outpatient follow-up rates exceeding 90%.25 Accordingly, increased medical contact may partly contribute to higher reported cancer incidence among patients with COPD. However, several cancers observed in this study—such as pancreatic, gallbladder and biliary tract and haematologic malignancies—typically require targeted diagnostic evaluation and are not commonly identified through routine population-based screening.42 Therefore, although surveillance bias cannot be excluded, it is unlikely to fully account for the observed associations.

In conclusion, this nationwide cohort study suggests that COPD and a history of COPD exacerbations are associated with increased risks of overall and several site-specific cancers beyond the lungs. Lung cancer showed the strongest association, although modest increases were also observed for several extrapulmonary malignancies. Because residual confounding, diagnostic misclassification and surveillance bias cannot be fully excluded, these findings should be interpreted as epidemiological associations rather than causal relationships.
