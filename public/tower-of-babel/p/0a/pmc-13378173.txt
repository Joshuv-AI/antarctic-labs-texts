Synthesis and characterization of ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 nanocomposite for thymol blue dye removal
Authors: Christen Tharwat, Omar A Fouad, E Girgis, Gehad G Mohamed, Mohamed M S Wahsh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHazardous organic pollutants, especially synthetic dyes, have been released into aquatic ecosystems more quickly as a result of rapid industrialization, posing serious dangers to the environment and public health. To cure harmful dyes, we created new nanocomposites alloys with different concentrations in this way using co-precipitate technique. ZnxNi1−xFe2O4 prepared using a modified template free technique showed an excellent dye removal efficiency of Thymol blue. Nanocomposites as ZnxNi1−xFe2O4, and TiO2; long used in dye removal purposes, have problems in their recyclability and production cost on large scale. However, we overcame these problems through the prepared nanocomposite, thus allowing low cost, controlled synthesis of these nanomaterials. In order to better understand the ability of ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 (x = 0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5) nanoparticles prepared by co-precipitation method to photodegrade Thymol Blue dye, a systematic analysis of their structural, micro-structural, surface area, hydrophilicity, and magnetic properties was conducted. The prepared NixZn1−xFe2O4 & NixZn1−xFe2O4/TiO2 were successfully examined using XRD, AFM, TEM, BET, hydrophilicity, VSM, and UV–Vis spectrometry. At optimum conditions, the fabricated ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 nanocomposite was efficient for degrading thymol blue dye in aqueous media; the percentage degradation was obtained up to 70% within 120 min. just for Zn0.8Ni0.2Fe2O4/TiO2. The results demonstrate a regulated, inexpensive synthesis pathway for high-performance nanomaterials with excellent recyclability, providing a scalable industrial wastewater treatment option.Keywords: Photodegradation, Nanocomposite, Thymol blue dye, Hydrophilicity, Co-precipitation method, Sol-gel

Introduction

Textile factories’ wastewater discharge and usage of chemically stable colors contribute to environmental degradation and water pollution. The textile sector uses around 12% of all dyes each year. When dye comes into touch with skin, it can cause itching, scaling, irritation, reddening, and blistering. It also causes irritation, redness, wetness, and itching in the eyes. Inhaling this substance destroys mucous membranes, causing respiratory discomfort in people [1].

Thymol Blue is a pH indicator and biological stain made of xanthene dye. Thymol Blue may irritate the gastrointestinal tract when taken in big doses. Skin-to-skin contact causes redness, swelling, irritation, and pain [2]. For the purpose of eliminating dye, a variety of oxides, noble metals, and oxide composites have been utilized [3–6]. Finding efficient materials with a high percentage of dye removal and activity is still difficult. Utilizing the materials in combination is therefore necessary to take use of their respective qualities. These days, nanocomposite structures are a lot of talk due to their intriguing applications in electronics, catalysis, and magnetism. Nanocomposite structures are made to incorporate several characteristics into a single system, in contrast to single-component catalysts. Moreover, magnetic fields make it easy to extricate composites with a magnetic material core [7].

Due to their exceptional qualities and plenty of uses in current technology, magnetic materials are gaining a lot of attention. This presents a new chance to create innovative, multipurpose electronic devices [8, 9]. Ferrites are among the magnetic materials that have drawn interest because of their strong magnetoelectric coupling behavior and potential uses in information storage, sensors, spintronics, and actuators. In addition to their ferromagnetic properties, they have significant electrical resistance [10]. As a result of their exceptional physical and chemical properties, nanostructured spinel ferrites with the structural formula AFe2O4 (where A stands for divalent cations like Ni, Co, Mn, etc.) have earned the focus of numerous researchers in recent decades [11].

Ferrites are potential candidates in the fields of magnetic sensors, color imaging, communications, high frequency circuits, biomedical sciences, high quality filters, audio and video equipment, etc. because low-cost materials with improved properties like high permeability, high electrical resistance, high saturation magnetization [8], low coercivity, and low eddy current losses can be designed to achieve these goals [9, 12]. The significance of these materials stems from their diverse variety of uses as well as the various compositional substitutions made into ferrites, which result in enhanced physical qualities [13].

As is often known, ferrite materials’ dopants and concentrations, as well as their microstructure, synthesis circumstances, impurity levels, and material composition, all have a significant impact on the attributes of ferrites [14]. There are multiple ways establishing these ferrites, such as, hydrothermal [15], co-precipitation [16], sol-gel [17], auto combustion process [18], mechano-chemical [19], etc.

Due to its high permeability, noticeable electrical resistance, and minimal electrical loss, nickel ferrite (NiFe2O4) has been thoroughly investigated and classified as a significant magnetic material. With the Fd-3 m space group, nickel ferrites were found in particular form in the cubic spinel structure [10]. With octahedral (B) sites occupied by divalent metal ions (Ni2+) and equal distribution of Fe3+ ions at tetrahedral (A) and octahedral (B) sites, it has an inverted spinel structure. At the nano regional level, Zn can even partially occupy B site when doped into the NiFe2O4 host matrix at the Ni site (A site). Consequently, the magnetic characteristics are significantly impacted by the distribution of these cations on sites A and B [9, 20]. As a consequence, the distribution of cations in ZnxNi1−xFe2O4 with a crystalline cubic structure that is doped with Zn2+ may be expressed as (Znx2+ Fe1−x 3+ )[Ni1−x 2+ Fe1+x 3+] O42− [10].

Additionally, zinc ions in the substrate lose their locations during the sintering or calcination process of ferrite materials, leading to the formation of cation vacancies or unsaturated oxygen ions, which may have an impact on the net magnetism of ferrites [21, 22]. Moreover, strong saturation magnetization, low coercivity, and high permeability are seen in Ni-Zn ferrites [9, 10, 23], which are significant for their viability in numerous possible industrial applications.

Furthermore, a well-known photocatalytic substance called titanium dioxide (TiO2) [24–28] is frequently utilized in the process of making self-cleaning glass [27]. Furthermore, studies on TiO2’s wettability are conducted, with a particular emphasis on its hydrophilic character [28–30]. Water spreads out fully across the surface due to the super hydrophilic feature of the surface, instead of staying in droplets [31]. Therefore, the process of synthesizing titania with a magnetic material core has offered a way to address issues related to the removal of titania photocatalyst from water that is treated with it [7, 32].

The ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 nanocomposite was previously synthesized using different techniques, but in our current work it was used for a specific dye removal which was not tried by other researchers before. So the current study aims to utilize nanocomposite to remove dye from water. The present investigation was aimed on the systematic analysis on the structural, micro-structural, surface area, hydrophilicity and magnetic properties of the NiFe2O4 and nanocomposite structure of ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 (x = 0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5) nanoparticles prepared by co-precipitation method, then study specific concentrations as a trial to examine their ability to photodegrade Thymol Blue dye.

The use of ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 nanocomposite may be a novelty in this aim of enhancing this process by using inorganic materials such as the as prepared nanocomposite. This is environmentally beneficial for removing dyes, purifying recycled water and makes it possible to reuse it from the other side. The findings highlight a controlled, low-cost synthesis route for high-performance nanomaterials with superior recyclability, offering a scalable solution for industrial wastewater treatment.

Experimental

Materials and reagents

All of the compounds utilized in this investigation were of the analytical variety. NiCl2 (M.W. 129.6 g/mol, 98%), ZnCl2 (M.W. 136.28 g/mol, >-98%), FeCl3 (M.W. 162.20 g/mol, 98%), and NaOH were obtained from Fisher, Titania isopropoxide was obtained from Sigma Aldrich (97%), and Thymol blue AR dye were obtained from Fine chemicals pH 1.2–2.8.

Synthesis of ZnxNi1-xFe2O4 nanoparticles

ZnxNi1−xFe2O4 nanoparticles have been synthesized using co-precipitation method. NiCl₂·6 H₂O solution (0.25 M), ZnCl₂·6 H₂O solution (0.25 M), and FeCl3 solution (0.25 M) were prepared by dissolving the metal chlorides in distilled water. The prepared solutions were mixed in molar ratio of Me2+/Fe3 + = 0.5 (Me2 + = Ni2 + and Zn2+) under constant stirring to get homogeneous solution with the heating rate of 5 °C/min up to 80 °C for 1 h. Ammonia was added to reach pH equal to 10. The precipitate was dried and fired at temperature of 800 °C for 1 h to form ZnxNi1−xFe2O4 nanoparticles (x = 0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5).

Synthesis of ZnxNi1-xFe2O4/TiO2 nanocomposite

Titania isopropoxide (Sigma Aldrich) was used as the starting chemical for synthesis of titania nanoparticles in the preparation of a ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 nanocomposite. Titania nanoparticles and ZnxNi1−xFe2O4 nanoparticles have a final weight ratio of 2:1. The hydrolysis of titanium isopropoxide began with 30 mL of distilled water, followed by 2–3 drops of nitric acid (HNO3) to complete the hydrolysis. The solution is then added to the mixed solution (100 mL) of water (80 mL) and ethanol (20 mL). The solutions were vigorously mixed and heated to 60–70◦C for 4 h. Magnetic nanoparticles (ZnxNi1−xFe2O4) were added to the mixed solution while it was still being stirred mechanically for 2 h. The gel preparation process began with the addition of the ammonia solution drop by drop and stirring until the gel formed. The formed gel was dried at 120◦C for 24 h. Finally, the powder was fired at 800 ◦C for 1 h.

The technique of light irradiation for photo-degradation

To find out how well the analyte thymol blue dye (0.02 gm dye / 500 ml distilled water) degrades in aqueous medium, dyes’ suspensions 25 ml containing 0.01 gm ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/ TiO2 were stirred for 30 min in the dark in order to reach adsorption equilibrium conditions where PH was adjusted at 8. For dye irradiation experiments, aqueous solutions were stirred in a 500 mL beaker.

5 mL samples of the dispersions were obtained at predetermined intervals (20 min), centrifuged, and then filtered. The UV-visible spectrophotometer was run where the thymol blue dye’s major characteristic peak absorption (λmax) at 430 nm during the irradiations [33].

Regarding the reuse and recycling, the nanomaterial granular elements are collected again for reuse several times, and finally they are collected in a designated place and disposed of by burying them in a sanitary burial place. Every experiment was carried out in triplicate (n = 3), and the mean value ± standard deviation is used to illustrate the results.

Results and discussion

XRD study

The powder XRD patterns of the synthesized samples of NiFe2O4 and ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/ TiO2 (x = 0.0, 0.1 and 0.4) nanocomposite is displayed in Fig. 1 (a). It has previously been proven that the synthesized zinc ferrite (ZnFe2O4) and nickel ferrite (NiFe2O4) nanocrystals have normal spinel structure and inverse spinel structure, respectively. These can be expressed using the formulas (Fe3+)tet[Ni2+, Fe3+]octO42−, and (Zn2+)tet[Fe23+]octO42−, where the tetrahedral and octahedral sites are represented by the “tet” and “oct” indices, respectively [34].

a XRD of NiFe2O4 (Ni), NiFe2O4/TiO2 (Ni/TiO2) and the different ratio of ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 (0.1 Zn/TiO2 and 0.4 Zn/TiO2) nanocomposite b Zoom in view of the most intense peak (311)

The powder XRD patterns of the samples revealed that the major peaks at 2θ were 30.19, 35.60, 37.25, 43.31, 53.78, 57.35 and 63.01º which ascribed to (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511) and (440) planes. This data was well agreed with the standard JCPDS No. 10–325 for Zn-Ni ferrite. XRD patterns exhibited cubic spinel structure with Fd-3 m space group [5]. Consequently, the XRD data validated that the synthesized Zn x Ni1−x Fe2O4 ferrites (x = 0.0, 0.1and 0.4) have a mixed spinel structure with (Zn2+, Fe3+)tet[Ni2+, Fe3+]octO4 2− formula.

Furthermore, from XRD pattern of samples, it can be revealed that the peaks position migrates towards lower angles by increasing Zn doping level as shown in an enlarged view of the most intense peak (311) for pristine and Zn-doped NiFe2O4 in the vicinity of 2θ∼ 35.6º (Fig. 1b). This behavior suggested that the lattice parameter rises as Zn doping increases in accordance with Vegard’s law; that is, the lattice parameter (a) progressively increases from 0.834 nm (x = 0.0) to 0.835 nm (x = 0.3) [9, 34, 35]. An explanation for the increase in lattice parameter (a) with x can be derived from the variations in the ionic radii of Zn2+ (0.82 Å) and Ni2+ (0.72 Å) [34, 36]. Therefore, when Zn2+ ions with a greater ionic radius replace Ni2+ ions with a smaller ionic radius, the unit cell dimension expanding which lead to rises in the lattice parameter [9, 36].

Additionally, the XRD pattern displayed the diffraction peaks of TiO2 (rutile) and (anatase) in the samples Ni/TiO2, 0.1 Zn/TiO2, and 0.4 Zn/TiO2 at 2θ = 25.38, 37.98, 48.15, and 55.18º as per card no. COD-9,009,086 with space group I 41/a m d (141) and at 2θ = 27.35, 38.92, and 56.63º for COD-9,015,662 with space group P 42/m n m (136), respectively [28, 37].

It was observed that when the quantity of Zn doping increased, the rutile phase diffraction peak gradually evolved. The rutile phase diffraction peaks are more intense than the anatase phase diffraction peaks when the Zn doping is 0.4, as indicated in Fig. 1a. Thus, increasing Zn doping into ZnxNi1−xFe2O4 caused the crystalline phase alteration of TiO2 from anatase to rutile, according to the XRD data [28].

So, according to these XRD results the preparation of the ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 was confirmed. Additionally, the diffraction peaks’ broadening supports nanocrystalline nature of the samples, which is supported by applying the Debye-Scherrer Eqs. [37, 38] which relieved the average crystallite size was 15.32, 18.71, 21.23 and 24.20 nm for the sample Ni, Ni/TiO2, 0.1 Zn/TiO2 and 0.4 Zn/TiO2. This average crystallite size results confirmed also that when the Zn doping increased, the lattice parameters also increased.

AFM analysis

The surface topography and roughness of NiFe2O4 (Ni), NiFe2O4/TiO2 (Ni/TiO2), and the varying ratio of ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 (0.1 Zn/TiO2 and 0.4 Zn/TiO2) nanocomposites were investigated by applying the atomic force microscope (AFM). The AFM analysis showed that the grain size varied between 43 and 75 nm, whereas the elevation diverse between 19.92 and 36.13 nm. Figures 2 D and 3D AFM images for samples Ni, Ni/TiO2, and 0.1 Zn/TiO2 nanocomposite reveal a spherical form, granular microstructure with a uniform surface. AFM analysis of the sample 0.4 Zn/TiO2 nanocomposite revealed virtually spherical shape grains on an irregular surface. Additionally, the roughness of the samples was assessed by analyzing the average roughness (Ra) and root mean square of the roughness (Rq) from AFM pictures (Table 1). The surface roughness result demonstrated that adding TiO2 to the NiFe2O4 nanoparticles to form the NiFe2O4/TiO2 nanocomposite increased both the surface roughness and the grain size. It also revealed that increasing the Zn ion doping, as in the samples with the 0.1 Zn/TiO2 and 0.4 Zn/TiO2 nanocomposite, caused the surface roughness and grain size to increase further until they caused an uneven surface, as in the sample 0.4 Zn/TiO2 nanocomposite. This is probable because the larger ionic radius Zn2+ ions replace the smaller Ni2+ ions, serving as a driving force for agglomeration and increases the crystallite size, as discussed in the XRD results [34].

The hydrophilicity

The measurement of contact angles has been used to evaluate the lipophilicity of the samples (Ni), (Ni/TiO2), and (0.1 Zn/TiO2 and 0.4 Zn/TiO2) nanocomposites. The average contact angle for the samples NiFe2O4 (sample Ni) and NiFe2O4/TiO2 (sample Ni/TiO2) has been well-measured to be 121.12° and 107.72°, respectively, which is significantly larger than 90° and indicated the hydrophobic nature of the these synthesized samples as shown below in Fig. 4; Table 3 [43, 44]. However, the samples (0.1 Zn/TiO2 and 0.4 Zn/TiO2) showed an average contact angle of 76.96° and 60.29°, respectively, relieving the hydrophilic nature of these samples [45]. This enhancement in the hydrophilicity character in the samples Ni/TiO2, 0.1 Zn/TiO2 and 0.4 Zn/TiO2 than the sample Ni because of the addition of the super hydrophobicity TiO2 on the ZnxNi1−xFe2O4 surface in form of shell which lead to an increase in the hydrophilicity nature and decrease the hydrophobicity nature of the samples. The reason for this is that two factors influence the action of water molecules near the TiO2 surface. First, the surface chemical reactions establish hydrogen bonds between the unsaturated oxygen atoms and the water molecules. The second factor is unsaturated surface Ti atoms offer sites for water adsorption or dissociation. The water dissociation caused an alteration on the surface chemistry of TiO2 resulting in the formation of new surface Ti-OH and O-H functional groups. These functional groups improved the hydrogen bond networking with the near-surface water molecules, resulting in excellent wettability [46, 47].

Photocatalytic mechanism

Mechanism for simultaneous de-colorization of Thymol Blue using ZnxNi1−xFe₂O₄/TiO₂ Nanocomposite, Thymol Blue molecules adsorb onto the catalyst surface via electrostatic interaction, π–π interactions with surface sites, and Hydrogen bonding with surface –OH groups. TiO₂ provides hydroxyl-rich surface and ZnxNi1−xFe₂O₄ enhances surface charge and active sites.

A synergistic adsorption–photocatalytic method is used to decolorize Thymol Blue over ZnxNi1−xFe₂O₄/TiO₂ nanocomposite. Initially, hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions cause dye molecules to be adsorbed onto the catalyst surface. Both the ferrite and TiO₂ phases produce electron-hole pairs when exposed to radiation. Effective charge separation is made possible by the creation of a heterojunction, in which holes build up in the ferrite phase and electrons go to the TiO₂ conduction band. Reactive oxygen species such •OH and O₂•⁻ are created when these charge carriers react with dissolved oxygen and water. These species then oxidize the dye molecules, resulting in the disintegration of chromophoric structures and total de-colorization. Although excessive loading may decrease activity due to increased recombination, Zn inclusion is essential for adjusting the band structure and improving photocatalytic efficiency.

One significant factor that may have an impact on the adsorption process is time. When the duration was increased from 20 to 120 min, the absorbance value dropped and subsequently efficiency increases. All things considered, we discovered that the produced Ni0.2Zn0.8Fe2O4/TiO2 & Ni0.4Zn0.6Fe2O4/TiO2 nanoparticles exhibited strong affinities for Thymol blue dye as an anionic dye, and the main adsorption process may have been the electrostatic attraction between the dye molecules and the charged nanoparticles surface [51].

From the literature we found that when the produced catalysts’ photocatalytic activity was assessed in light of previous research, it became clear that elemental doping is essential for getting over the drawbacks of pure ZnO. Even after 180 min of UV exposure, clean ZnO has a comparatively low breakdown efficiency of 20% for Methyl Orange (MO), as shown in Table 5. Dopants, on the other hand, enable a notable improvement in performance. In particular, Ni-doped ZnO showed a high efficiency of 84% in natural sunlight, but Cu-doped ZnO reached a peak degradation of 89.5% for Methylene Blue (MB). The narrowing of the bandgap and the suppression of electron-hole recombination are responsible for these improvements; these effects are most noticeable in the Co-doped and Al-doped devices, which attained 82% and 81% degradation, respectively, across different light sources. Additionally, the catalysts’ temporal efficiency was significantly increased. For example, Mn-doped ZnO achieved a 62% degradation of Malachite Green (MG) in just 60 min, which is a threefold decrease in irradiation time when compared to the pristine sample. All of these findings highlight how versatile doped ZnO frameworks are in breaking down a variety of organic contaminants in both artificial and solar light [52–54].

Conclusions

Nanocomposites were effectively prepared by in situ co-deposition then TiO2 shell. As anticipated, the results of the current investigation confirmed the concept we put up in order for harmful dyes (Thymol Blue) to be efficiently photocatalyzed. The prepared NixZn1−xFe2O4 & NixZn1−xFe2O4/TiO2 were successfully examined using XRD, AFM, TEM, BET, hydrophilicity, VSM, and UV–Vis spectrometry.

The contact angle measurements from hydrophilicity studies and the AFM study’s conclusions were in good agreement. Furthermore, the measurement of the contact angle revealed that the addition of TiO2 to the ZnxNi1−xFe2O4 surface increased its hydrophilic character. This suggests that TiO2 is deposited in the form of a shell to construct the ZnxNi1−xFe2O4 / TiO2 nanocomposite.

The synthesis of the nanocomposite structure is confirmed by the shift in crystal size and shape from cubic to spherical, and the size increase caused by an increase in Zn doping is consistent with the data from the XRD and AFM. Thus, the TEM result agrees well with the crystallite extent determined by AFM analysis and XRD data.

According to the findings and confirmed by Tamaki, T., et al., when TiO2 is coated on the surface of NiFe2O4 (sample Ni), the surface area of the NiFe2O4/TiO2 particles marginally decreases. Additionally, the ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 ratio showed a significant decrease in surface area as the Zn level increased. This results from the crystallite size expanding as additional large-sized Zn ions are doped, which reduces the surface area.

Using various exposure times, the dyes Thymol Blue was photocatalytically broken down. The dye’s maximum photo-degradation occurred when it was exposed to visible light for 120 min for Zn0.8Ni0.2Fe2O4/ TiO2, when the dye was suspended in 25 ml of distilled water with 0.01 gm ZnxNi1–xFe2O4/ TiO2, and when the dye was 0.02 gm in an aqueous media. By using an external magnetic field to retrieve the manufactured magnetic composite particles, the separation efficiency for water treatment and dye removal is improved. The adsorbent’s nanocomposite viability for scaled dye removal is highlighted by its remarkable reusability over five cycles, with around 85% removal in the fifth cycle.

We focused in this research on the synthesis technique beside a first trail for a photo-degradation of Thymol Blue dye, and then we will focus in the future on the effect of these different concentrations on the dyes degradations using UV to be compared with visible light. The use of ZnxNi1−xFe2O4/TiO2 nanocomposite may be a novelty in this aim of enhancing this process by using inorganic materials such as the as prepared nanocomposite. This is environmentally beneficial for removing dyes, purifying recycled water and makes it possible to reuse it from the other side. So we will focus in our future work to discuss this nanocomposite with other dyes and apply different conditions as PH, and dye concentration.
