Synergistic effect of carbon fabric metastructure and magnetic layers in a composite for electromagnetic radiation shielding
Authors: Yevhen Yashchyshyn, Alexander Zaichenko, Khrystyna Harhay, Nataliya Mitina, Konrad Godziszewski, Oleh Izhyk, Ivan Demydov, Roman Nebesnyi, Vyacheslav Khardikov, Vladimir Tuz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe widespread use of high-frequency electromagnetic radiation (EMR) in communication, defense, medical, and household technologies raises health and safety concerns, underscoring the demand for efficient shielding and absorption materials. This work presents flexible protective composites based on conductive carbon fabric coated with thin magnetic polymer films. Several multilayer configurations were prepared and characterized, consisting of carbon fabric coated with epoxy polymer layers containing Ni or FeO fillers. The shielding performance, primarily governed by the fabric’s regular metastructure, exhibits strong polarization dependence in the 90-330 GHz range. The combined interaction of conductive carbon fabric and magneto-responsive polymer layers enhances absorption, induces multiple resonances, and broadens the effective shielding bandwidth, resulting in significantly reduced reflection across a wide spectral range.Keywords: Carbon fabric, Shielding effectiveness, Electromagnetic shielding materials, Millimeter-wave absorbers, Sub-terahertz range, Magnetic filmsSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Extending the operating range of radars to higher frequencies necessitates the development of advanced composites and coatings capable of absorbing and shielding electromagnetic radiation (EMR) to protect both airborne and ground-based targets1. Protecting humans from EMR emitted by high-frequency radio engineering systems, mitigating radio-frequency pollution from electronic and telecommunication devices, countering electromagnetic weapons, ensuring airport security, and enabling medical imaging further emphasize the urgency of designing effective EMR-protective materials2,3. In recent years, intensive research has been devoted to developing composites for shielding electromagnetic radiation in the sub-terahertz frequency range for multifunctional applications4,5.

Systematic studies have shown that EMR absorbers typically consist of a matrix based on thermosetting polymers, thermoplastics, or their blends, and combined with fillers of defined size and composition6–12. Common fillers include conductive or semiconductive materials (e.g., carbon, soot, graphene, graphene oxide), materials with high permittivity (e.g., oxides of Fe, Ni, Zn, Sn), ferro- or ferrimagnetic particles with strong penetration capability to combine both magnetic and dielectric properties13,14. Traditional absorbers are typically fabricated using low-loss backings, such as silicone rubber, incorporating magnetic or dielectric foams and resins. The inclusion of magnetic materials has been shown to reduce coating thickness while broadening the absorption band.

In absorbers composed of a resistive layer on a metallic backing, the input impedance is determined by the surface resistance of the resistive layer, positioned at approximately one-quarter wavelength from the ground plane15. The resonant frequency of the coating configuration constrains the absorption band of such systems. Stepped absorber coatings, consisting of multiple resistive layers separated by quarter-wavelength distances, are particularly effective. Their gradual increase in resistance with distance from the ground plane allows electromagnetic waves to penetrate progressively deeper, minimizing reflection in the outer layers and enabling broadband absorption. However, while increasing the number of layers broadens the transmission band, it also reduces mechanical strength. Protective composite coatings containing dielectric, conductive, and magnetic particles such as graphite, iron, ferrites, silicon carbide, molybdenum disulfide, and spinels remain promising candidates for advanced absorbers16,17.

In recent years, extensive efforts have been devoted to developing lightweight and flexible electromagnetic shielding composites by incorporating conductive fillers (e.g., carbon nanotubes, graphene, MXenes) and magnetic components into polymer matrices, as well as by constructing multilayer or hierarchical architectures to enhance absorption performance18–24. These studies demonstrate the effectiveness of combining conductive and magnetic losses and optimizing structural design for EMI shielding, primarily in the MHz-GHz frequency range. However, experimental investigations targeting absorption-dominated shielding at millimeter-wave and sub-terahertz frequencies remain limited, and the role of intrinsic substructures in coupling with magnetic coatings to tailor high-frequency electromagnetic responses is still insufficiently explored.

Among emerging systems, thermoplastic matrices incorporating quasi-one-dimensional transition-metal trichalcogenides25, as well as porous composites based on carbon powder and tetraethoxysilane26, have drawn increasing interest. Carbon-based materials are especially attractive as EMR absorbers owing to their strong EMR-to-heat conversion, low cost, excellent chemical and thermal stability, high permittivity, and superior conductivity27. For instance, lightweight 3D aerogels (2.0-8.0 mg/cm) fabricated from graphene and alkaline lignin via sublimation have demonstrated shielding efficiency of 21.3-49.2 dB28. However, the high conductivity of carbon materials often degrades impedance matching, limiting microwave absorption29. To overcome this limitation, graphene-based absorbers are typically combined with low-dielectric () or magnetic components (e.g., polymers, SiO, NiO, α-FeO), which reduce the composite’s relative permittivity () and improve impedance matching30–32. Incorporating magnetic fillers (Fe, Co, Ni, ferrites) either within carbon particles or as surface coatings further enhances performance by reducing surface reflection and promoting magnetic loss mechanisms33.

Flexible sandwich-structured films based on polyester nonwoven fabric and thermoplastic polyurethane with ∼ 20 % graphene have demonstrated not only excellent flexibility and durability, but also shielding efficiency ( dB) up to 49.1 GHz across the 5.4-59.6 GHz frequency range, at a thickness of only m34. Woven and nonwoven reinforcements are also attractive for EMR-protective coatings because of their porous structure, controllable thickness, and low cost35,36. In these materials, pore size and internal structure strongly influence EMR loss via multiple resonances, enhancing shielding efficiency. Nonwoven-based composites, with their high porosity, large surface area, and strong interfacial interactions, are particularly effective for EMR absorption and scattering. For example, polysiloxane-fiber composites have achieved shielding efficiencies of 50-56 dB in the 8.2-26.5 GHz range37.

Advanced fabrication techniques, such as electrospinning, enable layer-by-layer construction of reinforced composites with in situ polymerization38. Conductive shielding composites can also be produced through surface activation followed by deposition of conductive polymer/inorganic layers39, by mixing with conductive fibers40, or by incorporating conductive and magnetic particles41. Materials with periodic arrangements of conductive or magnetic units (metamaterials) are another promising class42. Typically consisting of metallic patterns on dielectric substrates, they exhibit frequency-selective absorption and, in some cases, negative permittivity and/or permeability. Broadband absorption in monolayer metamaterial absorbers is pursued either by designing multi-frequency unit cells or by dynamically tuning electromagnetic parameters to shift resonant peaks. However, monolayer metamaterials rarely achieve true broadband absorption, motivating hybrid designs that combine conventional absorbers with metamaterials43. Such hybrid systems leverage multiple resonances in stratified coatings, significantly enhancing overall absorption.

Resonant phenomena play a central role in EMR shielding44. Resonances arise when structural, electrical, or magnetic features of a material align with the frequency of incoming radiation, leading to maximal energy transfer. Depending on design, resonance may enhance EMR absorption, scattering, or reflection, enabling applications ranging from radar stealth and signal blocking to medical shielding. Different types of resonances are exploited45:

Dielectric resonances, which occur when polar molecules or dipoles oscillate in phase with the incident field (e.g., BaTiO- or SiC-filled polymers). Attenuation arises from interfacial, dipolar, defect-induced polarization, and conductive loss.

Magnetic resonances, which occur when EMR frequencies match the precession of magnetic dipoles in fillers such as FeO, Co, Ni, or spinel ferrites. Energy is dissipated through spin-lattice and spin-spin relaxation. Spinel ferrites (MFeO, M = Fe, Co, Ni, Zn) are especially effective because they combine dielectric and magnetic losses, tunable via cation distribution.

By integrating materials with complementary electromagnetic properties (e.g., magnetic layers, conductive grids, and carbon nanotubes), it is possible to minimize reflection while maximizing absorption across multiple frequency bands. Such hybrid architectures enable precise tuning of resonant frequencies while optimizing shielding performance. Metamaterial-based absorbers, in particular, offer unique advantages over traditional absorbers, including miniaturization, tunability, and enhanced efficiency. Their performance depends on composition, microstructure, and periodicity, which together facilitate coupled dielectric, magnetic, and geometric interactions, paving the way for next-generation ultra-wideband EMR shielding materials. Building upon these insights, the present study introduces a flexible, multilayer carbon fabric-based composite that combines the geometric advantages of a woven metastructure with magnetic and conductive coatings for sub-terahertz EMR shielding.

In support of this methodology, this paper reports high microwave EMR absorption and shielding efficiency in a newly developed flexible carbon fabric-based composite. We attribute this performance to electromagnetic resonance effects arising from the coupled interaction between the anisotropic periodic metastructure of the woven carbon fabric and the magneto-dielectric response of the magnetic polymer/inorganic surface layers. The observed enhancement is interpreted as a combined effect of conductive, dielectric, magnetic, and geometric contributions that improve impedance matching, promote multiple resonances, and suppress reflection over a broad spectral range.

Materials and methods

Materials

The epoxy resin Epidian 57 (bisphenol-A-based; molecular weight 340 Da; epoxy group content 23.8 %) was supplied by HCIECH Sarzyna (Nowa Sarzyna, Poland). Polyethylene polyamine (PEPA), purchased from Silkor (Kyiv, Ukraine), was used as the curing agent for the epoxy resin. Dioctyl phthalate (DOP) was obtained from HLR (Kyiv, Ukraine) and used as the plasticizer. Graphite nanoplatelets (GNP6, RMC Remacon GmbH, Bad Säckingen, Germany) had a particle size of 3.5-6.0 μm and a carbon content of 99.5 %. Nickel carbonyl powder (Ukrstarline, Nikopol, Ukraine) exhibited a particle size >10 μm, Ni content of 99.9 %, and a face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal structure. Gamma iron oxide (γ-FeO) micropowder (Nanografi Advanced Materials, Jena, Germany) had a particle size of 1-3 μm, purity of 99.9 %, and an FCC crystal structure. Carbon fabric (12K, 480 tex; SkyCarbon, Kharkiv, Ukraine) has a thickness of 0.52 mm, an areal density of 400 g/m, tensile strength of 4480 MPa, and Young’s modulus of 220-245 GPa.

Samples preparation

The multilayer architecture and material compositions were selected based on a functionally graded electromagnetic design concept, in which each constituent layer performs a distinct and complementary role in the overall shielding response. The woven carbon fabric serves as a mechanically robust and electrically conductive substrate with an intrinsic anisotropic metastructure, enabling polarization-dependent geometric resonances. The polymer coating layers were then designed to progressively tailor impedance matching and loss mechanisms across the thickness of the composite. Carbon fabric was selected as the substrate for the composite EMR absorber owing to its inherent electrical conductivity and radar-absorbing properties. An epoxy resin was employed as the matrix, modified with dioctyl phthalate (plasticizer) and polyethylene polyamine (PEPA, curing agent) in a mass ratio of 10:1:1 (resin/plasticizer/hardener). The initial thickness of the carbon fabric substrate was mm.

Graphite was selected as a conductive filler in the outer polymer layers due to its moderate conductivity and broadband dielectric loss, which enhances ohmic dissipation while avoiding severe impedance mismatch associated with highly conductive fillers. Nickel and iron(III) oxide were chosen as representative magnetic fillers with distinct magnetic permeability dispersion, enabling the introduction of magnetic loss and resonance broadening without significantly increasing material density or rigidity. The selected filler concentrations were sufficient to activate dielectric and magnetic losses while preserving coating homogeneity, flexibility, and adhesion to the carbon fabric substrate.

Therefore, the absorber was fabricated as a multilayer composite using a sequential coating process. To evaluate the contribution of each structural component, three main samples were prepared: Sample #1 with a single coating layer, Sample #2 with two coating layers, and Sample #3 with three coating layers. Additionally, two auxiliary configurations (designated as Sample #2 and Sample #3) were fabricated. These auxiliary structures mimic the geometry and layer composition of their respective main counterparts (Samples #2 and #3) but completely omit Layer #1 to isolate and precisely evaluate its electromagnetic contribution.

The base polymer composite layer (Layer #1, mm) was prepared by dispersing graphite (6 wt %) into the resin mixture, homogenizing by manual stirring for 5 min, coating the carbon fabric uniformly, and curing at C for 15 hours. This configuration is designated as Sample #1.

Subsequently, a second coating (Layer #2, mm) was applied. For Sample #2, nickel powder (32 wt %) was incorporated into the resin, whereas for Sample #3, iron(III) oxide (23 wt %) was used as the filler. Each mixture was stirred for 5 minutes, applied to the cured base layer, and cured under identical conditions (C, 15 hours).

Finally, Sample #3 received a third layer (Layer #3, mm) containing graphite (6 wt %). This layer was prepared, applied, and cured following the same procedure as the previous layers. The total thickness of the sample is .

The carbon fabric used as the substrate has a twill (diagonal) weave. Unlike a plain rectangular weave, in which warp and weft threads alternate directly over and under each other, the twill structure introduces a lateral shift between successive rows. Each weft thread passes over multiple warp threads before going under the next ones, and the pattern is progressively offset in adjacent rows. This produces a diagonal orientation of the bundles and a repeating oblique stripe motif that strongly influences the fabric’s anisotropic electromagnetic response. Although SEM imaging primarily serves to verify the integrity and uniformity of the multilayer structure, the observed continuous, well-defined interfaces between the carbon fabric and successive polymer layers are also relevant to electromagnetic loss mechanisms. These interfaces act as sites for interfacial polarization and scattering, which may contribute to dielectric relaxation losses at millimeter-wave and sub-terahertz frequencies. Furthermore, the embedding of the woven fabric within the polymer matrix creates a tortuous internal geometry that facilitates multiple reflections and repeated interaction of the electromagnetic wave with lossy constituents.

Setup and measurement technique

The multilayer configuration was intentionally constructed as a non-optimized but physically guided system to illustrate the interplay between conductive, magnetic, and geometric loss mechanisms. Rather than maximizing shielding effectiveness through extensive compositional tuning, the present design emphasizes clarity of physical interpretation, allowing the individual and combined contributions of the carbon fabric metastructure, conductive graphite layers, and magnetic particle-loaded layers to be systematically evaluated.

The shielding efficiency of the composites was evaluated based on the scattering parameters obtained using a free-space quasi-optical measurement setup, as shown in Fig. 4. The setup consists of a vector network analyzer Agilent PNA-X N5245A (VNA) equipped with frequency extenders from Virginia Diodes Inc., two conical horn antennas, and a set of flat and off-axis parabolic mirrors. The quasi-optical geometry provides a collimated beam path and minimizes spurious reflections, enabling accurate characterization in the millimeter- and sub-terahertz frequency ranges.

(b) Corresponding schematic diagram of the optical path. The stage enables controlled rotation with respect to polarization angle φ and angle of incidence θ. Measurements were performed over the 90-330 GHz range using a VNA with frequency extenders. The beamwidth at the sample plane is approximately 15-20 mm, depending on frequency.

The parabolic mirrors focus the electromagnetic beam onto the sample under test. At the sample plane, the beamwidth measures approximately 15-20 mm, depending on the operating frequency. Each horn antenna is positioned so that its phase center coincides with the focal point of the corresponding parabolic mirror, ensuring optimal beam collimation. The focal points of the remaining mirrors converge at the sample holder, which is mounted on a two-axis motorized rotary stage.

The rotary stage provides precise control of the sample orientation. Rotation about the first axis (defined by the azimuth angle φ), which is perpendicular to the propagation direction of the incident wave, adjusts the alignment of the sample relative to the polarization of the incident field. Rotation about the second axis (defined by the elevation angle θ), changes the angle of incidence. In this study, only the first axis is utilized (, ), as small variations in incidence angle are found to have negligible influence on shielding efficiency.

Measurements are performed over the frequency range f ∈ [90, 330] GHz, which covers the WR-8, WR-5, and WR-3 waveguide bands. The frequency resolution is set to 10 MHz, and the VNA configuration provides a dynamic range of approximately 100 dB. This wide dynamic range ensures accurate measurements even for composites with very high shielding efficiency. For each sample under study, complex scattering parameters (, ) are recorded in angular increments of .

To improve measurement accuracy, time-domain gating is applied to suppress unwanted reflections from elements of the quasi-optical path. In this procedure, the measured frequency-domain scattering parameters are first transformed into the time domain using an inverse Fourier transform46. An appropriate time window is then applied to isolate the response of the sample and remove delayed reflections from the setup. Finally, the data are transformed back into the frequency domain, yielding reflection and transmission coefficients that more accurately represent the intrinsic response of the material.

This combination of free-space quasi-optical techniques, wideband frequency coverage, and time-domain post-processing ensures that the measured data reliably capture the shielding performance of the composite absorbers across the millimeter-wave and sub-terahertz ranges.

Finally, to compensate for transmission losses and determine the reflection and transmission coefficients, two reference measurements are performed: one without the sample and another with a metallic plate (mirror). Results and discussion

Experimental characterization

The principle of reciprocity in electromagnetic wave propagation states that, for a linear, time-invariant, and reciprocal medium, the transmission characteristics between two ports remain identical regardless of the direction of propagation49. Applied to multilayer structures, this implies that the transmission coefficient through the stack is the same whether the wave is incident from Side A or Side B.

When absorption is present in the layers, reciprocity still holds: the total transmitted power for a wave propagating forward is equal to that for a wave propagating in reverse. However, the reflection behavior may differ significantly depending on the direction of incidence, because the field distributions within the layers depend on the sequence of materials. As a result, while the transmission shielding efficiency is invariant under wave reversal, the reflection-related efficiencies (e.g., and ) can differ for opposite irradiation directions.

This distinction is particularly important for asymmetric multilayer stacks, where the order of lossy and reflective layers leads to different reflection responses, but reciprocity guarantees that the absorption and transmission balance is preserved between the two directions.

The use of a full two-port measurement setup makes it possible to evaluate the shielding performance of the samples from both directions of incidence simultaneously. This capability is particularly important because the prepared specimens are structurally asymmetric, and therefore efficiencies and can depend on whether the wave impinges from Side A or from Side B. In contrast, the transmission shielding efficiency, , is constrained by the reciprocity principle and thus remains identical for forward and reverse propagation. For this reason, the discussion that follows concentrates on the reflection and absorption contributions only.

The observed angular dependences of the shielding efficiency originate from the intrinsic anisotropy of the fabric metastructure (one vs two threads per period). The diagonal orientation of the bundles leads to two-fold rotational symmetry in the electromagnetic response, with polarization-dependent resonances aligned along and across the twill direction, such that the measured efficiency is invariant under a rotation of the polarization angle φ. Specifically, when the incident wave is linearly polarized along the x-axis (), the electric field is aligned with the direction containing a single thread per period. In contrast, at , the wave is polarized along the y-axis, where the fabric density is effectively doubled due to the presence of two threads per period. This difference in thread density along orthogonal directions gives rise to distinct electromagnetic responses for the two principal polarizations. For intermediate values of φ, the incident polarization is oriented diagonally with respect to the weave, thereby exciting both thread orientations simultaneously. As a result, the efficiency maps display a continuous angular modulation between these two limiting cases, reflecting the anisotropic effective permittivity and impedance of the fabric. Nevertheless, in the experimental data, this symmetry is somewhat violated due to the irregularity of the weaving and orientation of the sample in accordance with the incident wave polarization and the diagonal pattern of bundles.

For Sample #1 (single coating layer; Fig. 5), reflection is uniformly very low across most of the band (colorbar scale ∼ 0-5 dB), with slight angle- and frequency-dependent ridges, where small reflection peaks align with certain φ and f. Reflection differs slightly between sides A and B, consistent with the asymmetric stack. The absorption level is already high (tens of dB) across wide portions of the band, with pronounced resonance bands whose position shifts with φ, consistent with structural and dielectric resonances of the fabric plus coating.

For Sample #2 (two layers; Layer #2 containing Ni filler; Fig. 6), reflection remains low overall but shows more pronounced angle- and frequency-localized features, likely due to the increased conductivity and impedance mismatch introduced by Ni. The side-to-side differences are clearer, and the absorption bands broaden with higher overall efficiency, indicating magnetic losses from the Ni layer and improved impedance matching in the multilayer configuration.

For Sample #3 (three layers; Layer #2 with FeO and an additional outer graphite layer; Fig. 7), reflection is minimal across much of the upper-frequency range (>250 GHz), with only narrow reflection ridges at specific values of f and φ. The absorption maps reveal the strongest and broadest attenuation, with peaks reaching ∼ 75-80 dB and resonance bands merging into continuous regions of high absorption across wide φ intervals. Side A vs Side B differences remain, as expected for an asymmetric multilayer structure.

The progressive increase from single-layer to multilayer architectures has a direct impact on the dominant electromagnetic loss mechanisms. In the single-layer configuration (Sample #1), attenuation is governed primarily by dielectric relaxation and ohmic losses associated with the conductive carbon fabric and the graphite-filled polymer coating. The limited number of internal interfaces restricts interfacial polarization and internal reflections, resulting in discrete, polarization-dependent absorption bands.

In the two-layer structure (Sample #2), the introduction of a magnetic particle-loaded polymer layer adds a loss channel through magnetic permeability dispersion, while simultaneously increasing the number of internal interfaces. These interfaces enhance dielectric relaxation losses. Moreover, the impedance contrast between layers increases internal reflections, extending the effective propagation path of the electromagnetic wave and broadening the absorption bands.

The three-layer configuration (Sample #3) further amplifies these effects. The additional outer graphite-filled layer creates a more gradual impedance transition from free space to the conductive fabric substrate, improving impedance matching and reducing front-surface reflection. At the same time, the increased number of interfaces and layer thickness promotes multiple internal reflections and interference effects, effectively trapping electromagnetic waves within the structure and enhancing cumulative dielectric and magnetic losses. As a result, individual resonances merge into extended regions of high absorption across wide frequency and polarization ranges.

The observed evolution from discrete absorption bands in single-layer samples to broadband absorption in multilayer samples directly reflects the compositional complexity of the structure. As additional conductive and magnetic layers are introduced, overlapping dielectric and magnetic loss mechanisms lead to the merging of individual resonances into extended high-absorption regions. This behavior indicates that absorption enhancement arises not only from increased thickness, but from the spatial distribution and coupling of different loss mechanisms enabled by the multilayer composition (measured characteristics of the intermediate structures can be found in Supplementary Information, see Appendix A).

It should be noted that the increase in total shielding effectiveness from Sample #1 to Sample #3 cannot be attributed solely to increased thickness. Although the total thickness increases from 1.3 to 1.8 mm, the experimental results also reveal substantial changes in spectral shape, reflection suppression, and polarization-dependent resonance behavior, indicating that the redistribution of conductive and magnetic loss channels within the multilayer architecture plays a dominant role.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that all three composites are strongly absorption-dominant () across the entire 90-330 GHz band. Moreover, the systematic addition of magnetic and conductive filler layers (Ni, FeO, graphite) progressively enhances both the strength and bandwidth of absorption, while simultaneously suppressing reflection. The observed angular anisotropy, governed by the fabric weave, further illustrates how structural design can be harnessed to tailor the electromagnetic response. These findings confirm that multilayer carbon-based composites can achieve broadband, polarization-sensitive, and absorption-dominated shielding, making them promising candidates for advanced high-frequency electromagnetic protection applications.

It is important to distinguish the observed enhancement from a purely cumulative attenuation effect associated with increasing the number of lossy layers. If the performance improvement were solely additive, one would expect a relatively uniform increase in shielding effectiveness with thickness, without significant modification of the spectral or angular features. In contrast, the introduction of the magnetic layer leads to the emergence and broadening of absorption bands, a pronounced suppression of reflection, and a reshaping of polarization-dependent resonances governed by the fabric metastructure. These features indicate that magnetic losses are not acting independently, but are coupled to the geometric and conductive resonances of the carbon fabric, producing a non-additive response.

Effect of multilayer composition

The absorption performance of the composite is strongly influenced by the composition and sequence of the multilayer structure. In the present design, the graphite-filled polymer layers provide moderate electrical conductivity and broadband dielectric loss, which promote ohmic dissipation while maintaining impedance matching at the air-material interface. Excessively high conductivity would lead to dominant reflection; therefore, the selected graphite concentration balances dielectric loss and wave penetration.

The electronic transport and dielectric response of the intermediate layers are phenomenologically integrated into the macroscopic scattering behavior of the composite. The 6 wt.% graphite loading is chosen to target a moderate conductivity regime, enabling wave penetration and optimizing the balance between surface reflection and internal ohmic dissipation.

The incorporation of a magnetic particle-loaded polymer layer introduces an additional absorption mechanism through magnetic permeability dispersion and associated magnetic loss. Although the intrinsic magnetic resonances of Ni and FeO occur at lower frequencies, their frequency-dependent permeability contributes to loss broadening and impedance matching in the millimeter-wave and sub-terahertz ranges when combined with dielectric losses.

Importantly, the sequence of conductive and magnetic layers creates a graded electromagnetic response across the thickness of the composite. Incident waves experience a gradual transition from free space into regions of increasing dielectric and magnetic loss, reducing front-surface reflection and enabling deeper penetration into the structure. Once inside, electromagnetic waves undergo multiple internal reflections and repeated interactions with lossy constituents, leading to enhanced absorption. This compositionally graded loss distribution explains the superior absorption performance of the multilayer structure compared to single-composition or single-layer samples.

Retrieval of effective electromagnetic parameters

To further interpret the experimentally observed shielding behavior, the measured complex transmission () and reflection () coefficients were used to estimate the effective electromagnetic parameters of the multilayer composites. Because the investigated structures consist of multiple conductive and magnetic layers with finite thicknesses and internal interfaces, the retrieval was performed within an effective-medium approximation using a standard inversion procedure adapted for free-space measurements of stratified samples50. The resulting effective complex permittivity and permeability therefore represent equivalent constitutive parameters of the entire multilayer structure rather than intrinsic parameters of an individual homogeneous layer.

Conclusions

We have designed and characterized flexible multilayer composites for electromagnetic radiation (EMR) shielding in the 90-330 GHz band by combining conductive carbon fabric with magnetic polymer/inorganic coatings. The multilayer composition was selected using a functionally graded design strategy, illustrating how impedance matching and combined loss mechanisms may be achieved in thin, flexible composites without reliance on extensive empirical optimization. The carbon fabric substrate featured a regular twill (diagonal) metastructure, while sequential coatings of epoxy filled with graphite, Ni, or FeO introduced additional conductive and magnetic loss channels. This hybrid design yielded thin (∼ 1-2 mm), mechanically flexible composites with absorption-dominant shielding performance.

Measurements confirmed ultra-low reflection (<5 dB above 250 GHz) and exceptionally high absorption, with shielding efficiency reaching up to ∼ 80 dB (>99.99 % attenuation). The results demonstrate that (i) the carbon fabric metastructure governs the angular and polarization dependence of the response, (ii) magnetic layers broaden the absorption bandwidth through additional magnetic-loss-assisted dissipation and improved impedance matching, and (iii) the combined incorporation of conductive and magnetic coatings enhances absorption and suppresses reflection through coupled dielectric, magnetic, and geometric interactions within the multilayer architecture. The results further indicate that the absorption performance is governed by the compositional grading and sequence of conductive and magnetic layers, which jointly control impedance matching, internal reflections, and the coupling of dielectric and magnetic losses.

The combined interaction between the carbon fabric metastructure and magneto-responsive coatings provides a versatile strategy for tailoring impedance matching, resonance conditions, and broadband absorption. The developed composites exhibit promising potential for applications in radar stealth, 6G/terahertz communications, and medical shielding, where ultrahigh absorption and minimal backscattering are crucial.
