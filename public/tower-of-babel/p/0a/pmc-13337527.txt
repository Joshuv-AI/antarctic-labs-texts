Duocarmycin‐Bearing Antibody‐Mimetic Drug Conjugate Combined With an ATR Inhibitor Results in Complete Tumor Regression in a KPL‐4 Xenograft Model
Authors: Toshiya Tanaka, Akira Sugiyama, Juri Sakata, Toshifumi Tatsumi, Hiroto Katoh, Keisuke Maeda, Yuya Inagaki, Takefumi Yamashita, Takeshi Kawamura, Mayu Kuratani, Taiki Nakano, Masazumi Ishii, Mai Miura, Wakana Kamiya, Yuko Shiratori, Hisato Kitanoya, Michael Chansler, Naoki Harada, Masayuki Tsuchiya, Tsuyoshi Osawa, Shumpei Ishikawa, Tatsuhiko Kodama, Kenzo Yamatsugu, Hidetoshi Tokuyama, Motomu Kanai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) have demonstrated superior clinical outcomes in patients with HER2‐positive advanced breast cancer compared with previous treatment modalities. However, resistance and treatment‐limiting toxicities frequently emerge during repeated administration, underscoring an unmet need for strategies to overcome ADC failure. In this study, we investigated whether inhibition of ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3‐related protein (ATR) could enhance the antitumor efficacy of antibody‐mimetic drug conjugates (AMDCs), a targeted payload delivery platform. Combination treatment with a duocarmycin‐bearing HER2‐targeted AMDC (Duo‐HER2) and an ATR inhibitor markedly enhanced antitumor activity in a human HER2‐positive breast cancer xenograft model. In a limited cohort of treated mice, this combination induced sustained tumor suppression, with prolonged tumor‐free survival exceeding 100 days. Although transient body weight loss was observed during the early phase of treatment, no evidence of overt systemic toxicity was detected. Synergistic interactions with Duo‐HER2 were further confirmed in vitro using three clinically relevant ATR inhibitors—tuvusertib, ceralasertib, and berzosertib. Short‐term apoptosis analyses revealed only modest induction of cell death, whereas longer‐term viability assays demonstrated pronounced growth suppression. Cell cycle analyses showed S‐phase accumulation accompanied by a mild increase in the sub‐G1 population, suggesting that the combination initially exerts a cytostatic effect through replication stress–induced S‐phase arrest, followed by delayed cytotoxicity. Together, these findings indicate that Duo‐HER2 combined with ATR inhibitors synergistically suppresses tumor growth in HER2‐positive breast cancer models. Further studies employing optimized formulations, expanded in vivo cohorts, and additional tumor models will be required to fully define the therapeutic potential of this strategy and its relevance for overcoming ADC resistance.

Introduction

Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) represent a novel and promising class of targeted cancer therapies that combine the specificity of monoclonal antibodies with the potent cytotoxicity of small‐molecule payloads [1, 2, 3]. By selectively delivering highly toxic agents to tumor cells, ADCs aim to maximize antitumor efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity. Despite their clinical success, however, several challenges continue to limit the optimal development and long‐term efficacy of ADC‐based therapies [1]. One major limitation of current ADCs is the restricted repertoire of payload classes that can be safely and effectively utilized, which is largely confined to antimitotic agents, DNA‐alkylating drugs, and topoisomerase I inhibitors. Another challenge is achieving sufficient tumor specificity. Many tumor‐associated antigens, including HER2, are also expressed in normal tissues, which may cause off‐target toxicity. In addition, the prolonged circulation half‐life of conventional monoclonal antibodies, such as trastuzumab, can further increase systemic exposure and contribute to adverse effects. Consequently, despite the clinical success of approved HER2‐targeted ADCs such as trastuzumab emtansine (T‐DM1) and trastuzumab deruxtecan (T‐DXd), a substantial proportion of patients eventually develop resistance and/or experience severe toxicities, leaving significant unmet medical needs [4].

HER2‐positive breast cancer accounts for approximately 15%–20% of all breast cancers and is associated with an aggressive phenotype, high recurrence rates, and inferior survival outcomes if left untreated [5]. HER2‐targeted ADCs, particularly T‐DM1 and T‐DXd, have transformed the treatment landscape of HER2‐positive breast cancer and represent prototypical examples of successful ADC development [6]. At the same time, HER2‐positive breast cancer has become one of the most extensively studied clinical settings in which both primary and acquired resistance to ADCs have been documented. Reported mechanisms of resistance include downregulation of HER2 expression, impaired intracellular trafficking and lysosomal processing of ADCs, and induction of drug efflux transporters [7, 8]. Importantly, given that clinically approved HER2‐targeted ADCs such as T‐DM1 and T‐DXd deliver genotoxic payloads, accumulating evidence indicates that activation of the DNA damage response (DDR) and DNA repair pathways constitutes a key adaptive mechanism underlying acquired resistance [7]. Indeed, upregulation of ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3‐related protein (ATR)–CHK1 signaling and related DNA repair processes has been observed in ADC‐resistant HER2‐positive breast cancer models, highlighting the functional relevance of DDR activation in this disease context [7].

To address several intrinsic limitations of conventional ADCs, we have developed antibody‐mimetic drug conjugates (AMDCs) [9, 10, 11]. AMDCs are composed of a low‐immunogenic mutant streptavidin (Cupid) fused to antibody‐mimetic targeting moieties, such as affibodies or single‐chain variable fragments, together with a modified bis‐iminobiotin linker (Psyche) that binds Cupid with high affinity. Cupid forms a tetrameric structure in solution, enabling multivalent antigen binding and site‐specific noncovalent conjugation of two Psyche molecules. Psyche can be covalently linked to a wide variety of payloads, including radionuclides [10], photosensitizers [11], and cytotoxic agents, via appropriate linkers. Compared with conventional antibody‐based ADCs, AMDCs are smaller in size, exhibit faster blood clearance and shorter systemic half‐life, and demonstrate improved tumor penetration, thereby reducing systemic exposure and the risk of off‐target toxicity [9]. We previously demonstrated that HER2‐targeted AMDCs efficiently deliver the DNA‐alkylating agent duocarmycin into HER2‐positive cancer cells [12].

Duocarmycin, originally isolated from Streptomyces species, binds to the minor groove of DNA and irreversibly alkylates adenine residues, exerting extremely potent cytotoxicity at picomolar concentrations [13]. Although its clinical development as a free drug was halted due to a narrow therapeutic index and severe toxicity, duocarmycin has re‐emerged as a promising ADC payload, and multiple duocarmycin‐based ADCs are currently under clinical investigation. Importantly, recent studies have proposed that cancer cell–specific delivery of duocarmycin via ADCs may be particularly effective when combined with inhibitors of the DDR, especially ATR inhibitors [14]. Duocarmycin induces replication stress and stalled replication forks, and pharmacological studies suggest that ATR and CHK1 play central roles in mediating cellular tolerance to duocarmycin‐induced DNA lesions. Inhibition of ATR signaling therefore leads to pronounced synergistic cytotoxicity when combined with duocarmycin‐based ADCs [14].

Based on these mechanistic insights and the unique position of HER2‐positive breast cancer as the most clinically validated yet resistance‐prone indication for ADC therapy, the present study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of a targeted combination strategy using a duocarmycin‐bearing HER2‐targeted AMDC (Duo‐HER2) together with ATR inhibitors. To assess whether ATR pathway inhibition broadly enhances the activity of duocarmycin‐based ADCs, rather than reflecting a compound‐specific interaction, we employed three clinically relevant ATR inhibitors—berzosertib [15, 16], ceralasertib [17, 18], and tuvusertib [19, 20]. These agents represent different generations of ATR inhibitors with distinct chemical scaffolds, pharmacological profiles, and stages of clinical development, thereby enabling evaluation of ATR inhibition as a generalizable combinatorial strategy. We investigated the in vivo efficacy of this combination in a HER2‐positive human breast cancer xenograft model using ceralasertib and further characterized the cellular mechanisms underlying the observed synergy in vitro using all three representative ATR inhibitors.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Escherichia coli strain BL21(E3) (#314‐009073, Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan). pET‐45b (+) vector (#71327; Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). The 2× YT medium (#244020; BD Difco). L‐(+)‐Arginine Monohydrochloride (Nacalai, Kyoto, Japan). Duocarmycin‐Psyche was provided by Tokuyama et al. Ceralasertib (#S7693), tuvusertib (#E1411), and berzosertib (S7102) were purchased from Selleck Biotech (Kanagawa, Japan).

Duo‐HER2 Preparation

Preparing the Recombinant Anti‐HER2 VHH‐Cupid Fusion Protein Without His‐Tag

Preparation of the expression vector for the 6xHis‐free anti‐HER2 VHH‐Cupid (HER2‐Cupid) protein was performed by removal of His‐Tag via site directed mutagenesis from the His‐Tag expression vector previously reported by Sakata et al. [12]. The amino acid sequence of the expression protein is shown in Figure 1A, and the corresponding expression vector construct is shown in Figure 1B. BL21(DE3) competent cells were transformed with the HER2‐Cupid expression vector. The cells were grown at 37°C in 2× YT broth containing 100 μg/mL carbenicillin. Using 1 L of 2× YT media, the expression was induced with 1.0 mM IPTG at OD600 = 0.8, and the cells were cultured at 37°C. Cells were harvested 4 h later by centrifugation (7500× g, 10 min at 4°C) and stored at −80°C.

(B) Schematic representation of the anti‐HER2 VHH–Cupid expression vector constructed for expression in Escherichia coli . (C) Refolding procedure used to obtain active recombinant anti‐HER2 VHH–Cupid protein. Lane M indicates the molecular weight marker; lane #1, pooled sample before concentration; lane #2, concentrated sample.

Refolding and Purification of His‐Tag Free Anti‐HER2 VHH‐Cupid Protein

The method for purification of tetrameric HER2‐Cupid by refolding from the inclusion bodies (IBs) was following the previous report [12] (Figure 1C). In short, IBs were isolated from cryopreserved E. coli and lysed in 6 M guanidine‐containing buffer. The lysed sample was diluted and refolded in two steps with glutathione‐containing buffer, including a reducing agent; for tetramer formation, dialysis was performed in refolding buffer at pH 5.0. The purification of the tetramer was done using gel filtration chromatography. Purified fractions were pooled and concentrated using Vivaspin Turbo 15, 50,000 MWCO (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) and stored in a −80°C freezer until before use. The purity of the samples before and after concentration was checked by SDS‐PAGE and Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) staining (Figure 1D).

Duo‐HER2 Complex Formation

The Duocarmycin‐Psyche and HER2‐Cupid complexes (Duo‐HER2) were prepared using the method described in a previous paper [12] with some modifications. Duocarmycin‐Psyche and HER2‐Cupid were mixed to a molar ratio of 2:1 and incubated on ice for 10 min. To remove unbound Duocarmycin‐Psyche, the buffer was exchanged into PBS containing 200 mM arginine using PD‐10 or PD MidiTrap G‐25 columns (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Correct complex formation and tetrameric assembly were assessed by SDS–PAGE analysis, confirming the expected oligomeric migration pattern. For the in vivo efficacy studies, all animals were treated with Duo‐HER2 derived from a single preparation batch to eliminate batch‐to‐batch variability. Absence of nucleic acid contamination was confirmed by UV spectrophotometric analysis based on A260/A280 absorbance ratios.

In Vivo Efficacy Analysis Using Xenograft Mice

Animals

For the tumor xenograft experiments, 5‐week‐old female BALB/cSlc‐nu/nu nude mice (18–20 g) were purchased from Sankyo Labo Service Corporation Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). All the mice were housed in an animal facility under controlled temperature (24°C ± 2°C) with a 12 h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. Animals were acclimatized to the environment for at least 1 week before their use in the experiment. Sample size (n = 4–5) was determined based on the exploratory nature of the study and prior experience with xenograft models. Each in vivo experiment represents a single independent biological experiment using separate cohorts of animals. Formal power calculations were not performed, as the primary objective was to assess feasibility and detect a clear biological signal rather than to establish definitive efficacy estimates. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: RAC210005) and was conducted according to the University of Tokyo Animal Experimentation Regulations.

Cell Culture

The human breast cancer cell line KPL‐4 [21] was kindly provided by Dr. Junichi Kurebayashi (Kawasaki Medical Hospital, Kurashiki, Okayama, Japan). KPL‐4 cells were cultured and maintained in low‐glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (#041‐29775; FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals Corporation, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (#15140122; ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 atmosphere. KPL‐4 was tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

In Vivo Efficacy Analysis of Targeted Combination Therapy

KPL‐4 cells (7.5 × 106) were inoculated subcutaneously into the right flank of the mouse. Once tumors reached the predefined size threshold (> 200 mm3), mice were assigned to treatment groups and treatment was initiated in a paired manner, such that Duo‐HER2 and Duo‐HER2 plus ceralasertib groups were started concurrently. For ATR inhibitor treatment, ceralasertib was administered orally (PO) once daily for 14 days. Ceralasertib was first dissolved in DMSO at 50 mg/mL (solubility in DMSO ≈ 82 mg/mL) and then diluted in 3% Arabic gum to a final concentration of 5 mg/mL. Mice were dosed at 10 mL/kg to achieve a target dose of 50 mg/kg. This preparation ensured complete solubilization and safe oral administration. Tumor volumes were measured using a vernier caliper twice a week and calculated (0.5 × length × width2). In addition, body weight was assessed twice a week. Blinding was not applied during treatment administration or outcome assessment in this exploratory study. However, objective endpoints, including caliper‐based tumor volume measurements and longitudinal body weight monitoring, were used to minimize potential observer bias. After 55 days, mice in the Duo‐HER2 alone group were sacrificed because 3 of 5 mice had reached predetermined tumor size, whereas mice in the Duo‐HER2 and ceralasertib combination group were sacrificed on over 100 days according to the animal experimental guidelines. Tumor volumes were compared between treatment groups at each time point using an unpaired two‐tailed t‐test. For each comparison, p‐values and 95% confidence intervals for the mean differences were calculated. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Pathological Analysis

Subcutaneous xenograft tumor regions and other organs (liver, kidney, heart, lung, upper and lower gastrointestinal tracts, pancreas, brain parenchyma, gynecological organs, spleen, and bone marrow) were resected from treated mice. The tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde phosphate buffer solution (#163‐20145; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) for 48 h at 4°C, then embedded in paraffin following standard histopathological procedures. Histological specimens were deparaffinized and rehydrated by immersing them in xylene (#241‐00091; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by successive immersions in 100%, 90%, 80%, and 70% solutions of ethanol (#057‐00451; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) for 3 min each at room temperature. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; #6187‐4P and #8660; Sakura Finetek Japan, Tokyo, Japan) solutions were used for H&E staining according to the manufacturer's protocols. Finally, the stained slides were dehydrated via immersion in ethanol and xylene. The slides were covered with glass coverslips using Marinol (#4197193; Muto Pure Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan) and histopathologically examined using the OLYMPUS cellSens Standard system (OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan).

In Vitro Analysis of Targeted Combination Therapy

Viability Assay of Duo‐HER2 Combination Therapy With 3 ATR Inhibitors

The cytotoxicity assay using the KPL‐4 cell line was performed as described previously. Briefly, 3 × 103 cells/well with 50 μL of medium were seeded in 96‐well plates and cultured overnight. The cells were treated with different concentrations of Duo‐HER2 alone, ATR inhibitors (ceralasertib, tuvusertib, and berzosertib) alone, and their combination for 6 days. After drug treatment, 10 μL of CCK‐8 reagent (#CK04, Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was added per well, the plates were incubated at 37°C for 1.5 h, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (iMark, Bio‐Rad). The analysis also included measurements for blank wells (culture medium and CCK‐8 reagent) and control wells (untreated cells, culture medium, and CCK‐8 reagent). All viability assays were performed in at least three independent biological experiments, each conducted using independently cultured cells. The synergy score was calculated on the SynergyFinder web application (version 3.0) (https://synergyfinder.fimm.fi) [22] with “Matrix” format and inhibition‐Phenotypic Response, using the ZIP method [23] after 6‐ day drug treatment.

Apoptosis Assay of Duo‐HER2 Combination Therapy Using Tuvusertib

KPL‐4 cells were plated 1.5 × 105 cells/well in 6‐well plates and cultured overnight. The cells were treated with 20 ng/mL of Duo‐HER2 and 30 nM of tuvusertib. For the apoptosis assay, KPL‐4 cells were harvested 72, 96, and 120 h after drug treatment. In brief, cells were washed in PBS and resuspended with 100 μL of Annexin V binding buffer (#422201, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Cells were stained with 5 μL Annexin V‐APC (#640919, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and 5 μL 2.5 mg/mL propidium iodide (PI) (#165‐26283 FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals Corporation, Osaka, Japan) and incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. After adding 400 μL Annexin V binding buffer, analyzed for apoptosis with BD FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Cellular debris and aggregates were excluded based on forward scatter area (FSC‐A) and side scatter area (SSC‐A) characteristics. A consistent gating template was applied to all samples to ensure uniformity. For each treatment, 10,000 events were acquired. Cell populations were classified as follows: live cells (Annexin V‐APC negative/PI negative), early apoptotic cells (Annexin V‐APC positive/PI negative), late apoptotic/PI‐positive cell populations (Annexin V‐APC positive/PI positive), and naked nuclei (Annexin V‐APC negative/PI positive) (Figure S1A). Data represent results from a minimum of three independent biological experiments, each performed on separate occasions using independently prepared cell cultures.

Cell Cycle Assay of Duo‐HER2 Combination Therapy Using Tuvusertib

KPL‐4 cells were plated 1.5 × 105 cells/well in 6‐well plates and cultured overnight. The cells were treated with 20 ng/mL of Duo‐HER2 and 30 nM of tuvusertib. Cells were harvested at 72, 96, and 120 h after drug treatment, and fixed with 70% ethanol. Cell pellets were washed once with PBS, and stained with 50 μg/mL propidium iodide (PI) containing 100 μg/mL RNase (#313‐01461, NIPPON GENE, Toyama, Japan) for 30 min at 37°C. Cell cycle analysis was performed by flow cytometry using a BD FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). The target subset for analysis was selected based on SSC‐A/FSC‐A scatter properties. Single cells were discriminated from aggregates and non‐specific signals by gating on a PI‐width (PI‐W) versus PI‐area (PI‐A) dot plot. DNA content distribution was then visualized on a PI‐A linear histogram to quantify cell cycle phases. A consistent gating template was applied to all samples to ensure uniformity (Figure S1B). For each treatment, 10,000 events were acquired. Data represent results from a minimum of three independent biological experiments, each performed on separate occasions using independently prepared cell cultures.

Statistical Analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test was carried out using BellCurve for Excel (Social Survey Research Information Co. Ltd. Tokyo, Japan), and a p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Duo‐HER2 and Ceralasertib Combination Effectively Suppresses Tumor Growth in a Xenograft Model

To evaluate the in vivo synergistic effect of combined Duo‐HER2 and ceralasertib treatment, we used a nude mouse model xenografted with KPL‐4 cells. Based on our previous reports [12], the Duo‐HER2 group received twice the number of intravenous injections of Duo‐HER2 at 200 μg/body on days 0 and 7. The combination therapy group of Duo‐HER2 plus ceralasertib received 50 mg/kg of ceralasertib orally for 14 days in addition to treatment with Duo‐HER2 twice on days 0 and 7 (Figure 2A). Duo‐HER2 monotherapy led to transient tumor reduction until day 21 when the tumor re‐progressed. The Duo‐HER2 monotherapy experiment was terminated on day 55 because the tumor volumes for 3 of 5 mice met the criteria for termination. The combination of Duo‐HER2 and ceralasertib showed stronger antitumor effects than monotherapy. Tumor volumes differed significantly between groups from day 25 onward (unpaired two‐tailed t‐test, p < 0.05; Table S2). All animals treated with Duo‐HER2 and ceralasertib (4 of 4) exhibited tumor‐free survival until the end of the observation period (over 15 weeks) (Figure 2B). Transient body weight loss was observed following initial Duo‐HER2 administration, while no additional progressive weight loss was observed upon combination with ceralasertib during the observation period. The recovery of weight of mice treated with combination therapy was observed after 14 days of administration (Figure 2C).

(B) Combination treatment with ceralasertib and Duo‐HER2 suppressed tumor growth more effectively than Duo‐HER2 monotherapy. Spider plots show tumor size changes in individual mice. In the Duo‐HER2 monotherapy group, body weight loss correlated with tumor recurrence. As shown in Figure 2A–C, following combination therapy with Duo‐HER2 and the ATR inhibitor, no macroscopic recurrence of the subcutaneous tumors was observed for over 100 days. To verify the absence of tumor recurrences more precisely, histopathological analyses of the areas surrounding macroscopically disappeared subcutaneous tumors were conducted. The results indicated no recurrence of tumors in the subcutaneous areas of any mice (Figure 2D). Histologically, the areas where tumors were originally present exhibited fibrosis, deposition of cholesterol crystals, microcalcifications, infiltration of foamy macrophages, and hemosiderin‐laden macrophages (Figure 2D), with no detectable viable tumor cells, findings that are consistent with tissue remodeling following complete tumor regression by combination therapy with Duo‐HER2 and the ATR inhibitor. Immunohistochemical analyses of proliferation or apoptosis markers were not performed in this study, and the assessment of tumor eradication was therefore based on morphological criteria. No histological tumor metastasis was observed in any other organs in any mice (liver, kidney, heart, lung, upper and lower gastrointestinal tracts, pancreas, brain parenchyma, gynecological organs, spleen, and bone marrow) (Figure S2). All organs histologically exhibited no significant pathological changes, suggesting the absence of overt histopathological abnormalities associated with the combination therapy under the conditions tested (Figure S2). The numerical tumor volume data corresponding to Figure 2 are provided as mean ± SEM in Table S1.

Synergistic Cytotoxicity of Duo‐HER2 and ATR Inhibitor Combination in KPL‐4 Cells

To investigate the synergistic cytotoxic activity of the Duo‐HER2 and ATR inhibitor combination in the HER2‐expressing human breast cancer cell line, KPL‐4 cells were exposed to the drugs for 6 days and a CCK‐8 assay was performed. Treatment of less than 10 ng/mL of Duo‐HER2 had a minimal effect on cell viability, and 20 and 30 ng/mL Duo‐HER2 resulted in only modest cytotoxicity, with a reduction in cell viability ranging from approximately 4% to 23%. KPL‐4 cells treated with tuvusertib (Figure 3A), ceralasertib (Figure 3B), and berzosertib (Figure 3C) for 6 days exhibited a dose‐dependent decrease in cell viability. The IC50 values were 133.9 nM, 2.1 μM, and 949.3 nM, respectively. Afterward, we performed an analysis using a zero‐interaction‐potency (ZIP) synergy score [22, 23] map to visualize the synergistic effects between Duo‐HER2 and the ATR inhibitors. In the presence of 10 ng/mL Duo‐HER2, which does not exhibit cytotoxic activity, the IC50 values of tuvusertib, ceralasertib, and berzosertib were reduced to 13.8, 339.8, and 38.1 nM, respectively, indicating a strong synergistic effect. The ZIP synergy scores for the Duo‐HER2 and ATR inhibitor combinations were all higher (> 10) than the average synergy score. These results suggest that the Duo‐HER2 and ATR inhibitors provide stronger efficacy than the individual compounds while enabling strong antitumor efficacy at concentrations that showed limited cytotoxicity as single agents. The corresponding numerical cell viability data for all tested conditions, including Duo‐HER2 treatment alone at each fixed concentration, are provided in Table S3.

Duo‐HER2 and Tuvusertib Combination Increases Apoptosis‐Associated Cell Populations in KPL‐4 Cells

To investigate the synergistic cytotoxic activity of the Duo‐HER2 and ATR inhibitor on the human breast cancer cells, we treated cells with 20 ng/mL Duo‐HER2 and 30 nM tuvusertib and evaluated changes in cell death‐associated populations through Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) co‐staining. Cells stained with Annexin V and PI were analyzed through flow cytometry and classified into four categories based on their proportions [24, 25]. The Annexin V+/PI‐ cell population was identified as early apoptotic cells, while Annexin V+/PI+ cells were operationally defined as late apoptotic cell populations. Annexin V‐/PI‐ cells, along with Annexin V‐/PI+ cells, were identified as live cells and naked nuclei cells, respectively (Figure 4A). In cells treated with 20 nM tuvusertib, no significant effects on the distribution across the four categories were observed from 72 to 120 h compared to the control group (Figure 4B). However, in cells treated with 20 ng/mL Duo‐HER2, a slight but significant increase in the proportion of Annexin V+/PI+ cells was observed at 72 and 120 h (4.05% ± 0.07% vs. 5.59% ± 0.12%, p < 0.05, 9.59% ± 0.16% vs. 12.31% ± 0.15%, p < 0.05, respectively). In the group treated with both Duo‐HER2 and tuvusertib, a significant increase in the counts of Annexin V−/PI+ cells and Annexin V+/PI+ cells was observed after 72 h compared to vehicle‐treated control cells (4.05% ± 0.07% vs. 10.11% ± 0.09%, p < 0.01, 5.16% ± 0.09% vs. 19.66% ± 0.25%, p < 0.01, 9.59% ± 0.16% vs. 25.39% ± 0.04%, p < 0.01, respectively). These findings indicate that the combination treatment induces late‐stage cell death–associated phenotypes under the conditions tested.

The Combination of Duo‐HER2 and Tuvusertib Arrests the S‐Phase and Induces Apoptosis in KPL‐4 Cells

To investigate whether the synergistic cytotoxicity of the Duo‐HER2 and tuvusertib combination was due to impaired progression of the cell cycle, we tried to analyze the cell cycle by measuring DNA content using flow cytometric analysis (Figure 5). The Duo‐HER2 and tuvusertib combination treatment resulted in a significant increase in the percentages of cells in the S‐phase after 72, 96, and 120 h treatment compared to vehicle‐treated control cells (13.54% ± 1.76% vs. 26.88% ± 1.24%, p < 0.01, 12.24% ± 0.68% vs. 28.86% ± 0.49%, p < 0.01, and 10.07% ± 0.64% vs. 23.18% ± 0.33%, p < 0.01, respectively) (Figure 5A,B). A significant increase in the number of cells in the sub‐G1‐phase population was also observed in the Duo‐HER2 and tuvusertib combination treated KPL‐4 cells compared to control cells (0.26% ± 0.07% vs. 0.75% ± 0.05%, p < 0.01, 0.64% ± 0.08% vs. 1.69% ± 0.13%, p < 0.01, and 2.09% ± 0.25% vs. 3.82% ± 0.33%, p < 0.01, respectively). However, Duo‐HER2 and tuvusertib mono‐treatment did not cause S‐phase arrest or an increase in the sub‐G1 phase population (Figure 5B). These results indicate that the Duo‐HER2 and tuvusertib combination induced apoptosis and caused an arrested cell cycle in the S‐phase in KPL‐4 cells.

Taken together, these results indicate that the combination of Duo‐HER2 and ATR inhibition initially suppresses tumor cell proliferation through S‐phase arrest, followed by delayed increases in cell death–associated phenotypes, ultimately resulting in durable tumor suppression in vivo. These effects are consistent with a synthetic lethal–like interaction between duocarmycin‐induced replication stress and ATR pathway inhibition, achieved at tolerable doses while minimizing untoward effects associated with the individual agents.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate the preclinical potential of a targeted combination therapy through intravenous administration of a Duo‐HER2 (a duocarmycin‐bearing anti‐HER2 AMDC) together with oral ATR inhibitor treatment in vivo, and further demonstrate synergistic antitumor activity in vitro using the HER2‐positive human breast cancer cell line KPL‐4.

Previous studies have shown that ATR inhibitors enhance the anticancer activity of various DNA‐damaging or DNA synthesis‐targeting agents, including topotecan, cisplatin, gemcitabine, and poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors [26]. In addition, ATR inhibitors have been reported to potentiate the antitumor effects of duocarmycin‐bearing ADCs [14]. The results presented here are consistent with these previous observations. Notably, Rieker et al. reported tumor recurrence in a xenograft model of HER2‐expressing human gastric cancer (NCI‐N87) approximately 50 days after treatment with ceralasertib combination therapy and approximately 90 days after treatment with ATRi‐1 combination therapy [14].

As an initial step to evaluate whether combination therapy using duocarmycin‐bearing AMDC and an ATR inhibitor provides a synergistic therapeutic benefit, we conducted in vivo efficacy studies. In our xenograft model, macroscopic tumor recurrence was not observed for more than 100 days following treatment with the Duo‐HER2 and ceralasertib combination (Figure 2). Histopathological analyses of the subcutaneous regions corresponding to the original tumor sites revealed no evidence of residual or recurrent tumor cells, and no overt histopathological abnormalities were detected within the scope of the analyses performed. These findings indicate that the combination of AMDC and ATR inhibition can exert a durable antitumor effect in a preclinical mouse model.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although no severe toxicities were detected within the scope of the analyses performed, the safety evaluation was necessarily limited. In particular, a transient reduction in body weight was observed during the early phase of Duo‐HER2 administration (Figure 2). While this effect was reversible and did not compromise antitumor efficacy, it precludes a definitive conclusion regarding the absence of treatment‐related adverse effects. Notably, subsequent formulation refinement eliminated this early weight loss without reducing antitumor activity, suggesting that formulation‐related impurities, rather than intrinsic drug toxicity, contributed to this observation. These findings highlight the need to optimize formulation quality, dosing, and administration schedules.

Second, the in vivo efficacy of the combination therapy was evaluated in a single HER2‐positive xenograft model with limited sample sizes. Although the observed antitumor effects were robust and durable within this experimental framework, further validation in larger cohorts and in additional in vivo tumor models—including those with varying levels of HER2 expression and distinct molecular backgrounds—will be required to establish the generality and translational relevance of these findings. Moreover, systematic evaluation of this combination therapy in HER2‐negative cell lines and in panels of tumor cells expressing different levels of HER2 will be necessary to rigorously assess target specificity and to define the therapeutic window of optimized Duo‐HER2 formulations.

Third, comprehensive pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) analyses were not performed in this study. Given that AMDCs are designed to exhibit faster blood clearance and shorter systemic exposure than conventional antibody‐based ADCs, detailed characterization of systemic exposure, tumor accumulation, payload release, and ATR pathway inhibition in vivo will be essential to optimize dosing schedules and to better define the therapeutic window of this combination strategy.

Given that ATR inhibitors are known to potentiate replication stress induced by DNA‐damaging agents, the timing and sequence of administration may significantly influence treatment outcomes [27]. For example, sequential administration strategies—such as delivering the AMDC payload first to induce replication stress, followed by ATR inhibition—may enhance therapeutic selectivity while reducing systemic toxicity. Alternatively, intermittent dosing schedules of ATR inhibitors may help balance efficacy and tolerability, as continuous ATR inhibition could exacerbate toxicity in normal proliferating tissues [16, 17]. In addition, although no overt systemic toxicity was observed in the present preclinical model, the potential for treatment‐related adverse effects in humans requires careful consideration. Both duocarmycin‐based payloads and ATR inhibitors have been associated with hematological and gastrointestinal toxicities in clinical settings [16, 17, 28]. Therefore, evaluation of dose‐limiting toxicities, tolerability, and safety margins in clinically relevant models will be essential for future development. Furthermore, further mechanistic characterization of this combination strategy will be important to refine patient selection and optimize therapeutic benefit. In particular, identification of predictive biomarkers—such as markers of replication stress, DDR activation status, or genomic instability—may enable stratification of patients who are most likely to benefit from this approach [29].

Although immunogenicity was not directly assessed, it represents an important translational consideration for AMDC‐based therapeutics. His‐tags have been reported to contribute to immunogenic adverse effects and were therefore removed from the Duo‐HER2 construct used in this study [30, 31]. Moreover, previous work has demonstrated that the immunogenicity of streptavidin can be substantially reduced through structure‐guided mutagenesis while preserving functional integrity [32]. Nevertheless, comprehensive immunogenicity assessments in appropriate preclinical models, as well as evaluation of manufacturing feasibility and quality control parameters—including residual DNA content, endotoxin levels, HER2‐binding affinity, and drug‐to‐protein ratio (DAR)—will be required prior to clinical translation. In this context, comprehensive optimization of the Duo‐HER2 preparation and quality control workflow is currently ongoing and will be critical for future translational development.

To elucidate the mechanism underlying the synergistic effect of a duocarmycin‐bearing AMDC in combination with ATR inhibition, we performed a series of in vitro studies using three clinically relevant ATR inhibitors. Previous reports have shown that the IC50 values for ATR inhibition by tuvusertib, ceralasertib, and berzosertib are approximately 5, 1, and 0.2 nM, respectively [33]. In our cell‐based assays, none of these ATR inhibitors exhibited marked cytotoxicity as single agents at the tested concentrations (Figure 3). In contrast, when combined with AMDC‐mediated duocarmycin delivery, all three ATR inhibitors produced pronounced synergistic antitumor effects, characterized by S‐phase accumulation and increased cell death–associated phenotypes (Figures 4 and 5). Among the ATR inhibitors tested, tuvusertib showed the strongest synergistic effect with Duo‐HER2.

An important conceptual insight provided by the present study is that the antitumor efficacy of the Duo‐HER2 and ATR inhibitor combination is best explained by a time‐dependent transition from an initial cytostatic effect to delayed cytotoxicity. While the combination treatment induced only modest levels of apoptosis within 72–120 h, as assessed by Annexin V/PI staining, a pronounced reduction in cell viability was observed in the CCK‐8 assay over a 6‐day period. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by the distinct biological processes captured by each assay. The CCK‐8 assay reflects cumulative cell number and metabolic activity, whereas Annexin V/PI analysis detects apoptotic events at defined time points. Future studies should evaluate long‐term proliferative capacity using direct assays, such as EdU incorporation or colony formation assays. Such studies will also help clarify the temporal sequence of cytostatic and cytotoxic effects and support rational optimization of dosing strategies for clinical translation.

Our data suggest that the combination treatment initially exerts a cytostatic effect. This is characterized by S‐phase arrest induced by replication stress and ATR checkpoint inhibition. At early time points, cell death is initiated but does not yet represent the dominant outcome. Sustained replication stress in the absence of ATR signaling is expected to result in replication catastrophe and delayed cell death. This model is supported by the progressive increase in the sub‐G1 population observed over time in vitro and, more strikingly, by the durable tumor‐free survival observed in vivo. Thus, the therapeutic efficacy of the Duo‐HER2 and ATR inhibitor combination appears to arise from an initial blockade of tumor cell proliferation, followed by irreversible loss of proliferative capacity and durable tumor suppression. However, while the present study demonstrates S‐phase accumulation followed by apoptosis upon combination treatment, direct molecular evidence of replication stress–associated DNA damage, such as γH2AX induction or modulation of ATR/CHK1 signaling, was not directly assessed and therefore remains to be confirmed in future studies.

Mechanistically, this cytostatic‐to‐cytotoxic transition can be explained by the interplay between duocarmycin‐induced replication stress and ATR checkpoint inhibition. Duocarmycin forms covalent DNA adenine adducts that impede replication fork progression [34], leading to accumulation of cells in early S‐phase [35]. Replication stress is a major source of genomic instability, and the ATR signaling pathway plays a central role in coordinating cellular responses to this stress [26, 36]. ATR regulates origin firing, stabilizes stalled replication forks, and activates cell cycle checkpoints to allow time for DNA repair [35]. Inhibition of ATR in the context of persistent replication stress therefore abrogates these protective mechanisms, exacerbates DNA damage, and promotes replication catastrophe and cell death [37, 38], particularly in cancer cells already burdened with genomic instability (Figure 6). These findings support the concept of a synthetic lethal–like interaction achieved through targeted combination therapy using duocarmycin‐bearing AMDC and ATR inhibition.

Proposed model of replication stress–mediated cytotoxicity induced by combination therapy. Combined treatment with a duocarmycin‐bearing antibody–drug conjugate (AMDC), which induces replication stress, and an ATR inhibitor, which impairs replication stress responses, is proposed to result in replication catastrophe and delayed cell death–associated outcomes in cancer cells.

In conclusion, combining Duo‐HER2 with ATR inhibitors more effectively suppresses tumor growth than either agent alone in a breast cancer preclinical model. This combination therapy is characterized by an initial cytostatic S‐phase arrest followed by delayed induction of cell death, ultimately resulting in durable tumor suppression in vivo, consistent with a synthetic lethal–like interaction between duocarmycin‐induced replication stress and ATR pathway inhibition, achieved at tolerable doses while minimizing adverse effects associated with individual agents.
