Genome‐wide association studies of TDP‐43 proteinopathy and hippocampal sclerosis reveal shared genetic associations with APOE and TMEM106B

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONTransactive response DNA binding protein 43 kDa (TDP‐43) proteinopathy has been linked to cognitive decline and often co‐occurs with hippocampal sclerosis (HS). To identify genetic markers of TDP‐43 proteinopathy and HS, we performed genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) of HS and TDP‐43 inclusions.METHODSGenetic data were obtained through the Alzheimer Disease Genetics Consortium and collaborating sites (HS: N = 9509; TDP‐43: N = 4669). Statistical analyses included association analysis with HS and TDP‐43 inclusions and meta‐analysis, a mediation analysis, and fine mapping of the transmembrane protein 106B ( TMEM106B) region.RESULTSTwo regions achieved genome‐wide significance with TDP‐43: apolipoprotein E (APOE) and TMEM106B. Three loci reached genome‐wide significance with HS: APOE, TMEM106B, and granulin precursor (GRN). Fine mapping of TMEM106B identified 93 variants in the credible set. Mediation analyses showed that genetic effects on HS were partially mediated through TDP‐43 proteinopathy.DISCUSSIONWe identified associations with TDP‐43 inclusion and HS, showing shared genetic risk across frontotemporal lobar degeneration, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer's disease, and limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy.Highlights HS and TDP‐43 contribute to dementia and often co‐occur.The etiology and relationship of HS and TDP‐43 remains unclear.HS and TDP‐43 share genetic risk factors in the genes APOE and TMEM106B.Genes have direct effects on HS, with additional effects mediated through TDP‐43.

BACKGROUND

Transactive response DNA binding protein 43 kDa (TDP‐43) proteinopathy describes the accumulation of misfolded TDP‐43 proteins in the cytosol of neurons and has been linked to cognitive decline resembling Alzheimer's disease (AD) dementia. 1 TDP‐43 inclusions have been observed in a spectrum of neurodegenerative diseases, including frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) 2 and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Subsequently, another pattern of TDP‐43 proteinopathy was observed in older individuals without FTLD or ALS: limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy (LATE). LATE describes a predominantly amnestic dementia with characteristic neuropathologic changes (LATE‐NC) in the amygdala, hippocampus, and isocortical regions. 1

LATE‐NC is commonly comorbid with other age‐related neurodegenerative diseases, especially hippocampal sclerosis (HS), though either can occur in isolation. 3 HS is characterized by pyramidal neuronal loss and gliosis in the hippocampus 3 and has been observed in a variety of contexts, including FTLD, ALS, AD, or in isolation in older individuals. 1 , 4 , 5 The existing literature shows that TDP‐43 proteinopathy may be a precursor of HS, 6 , 7 with as much as 57% to 72% of brain autopsies exhibiting LATE‐NC, HS of aging, and AD neuropathology concurrently. 8 , 9 Recent findings from the 90+ Study showed that 100% of participants with LATE‐NC plus HS also demonstrated moderate to severe AD neuropathology. 10 Another study showed that LATE‐NC made up 17.3% of the attributable risk for dementia in older individuals (behind AD and vascular injury 39.4% and 24.8%, respectively). 1 The relationships between the pathologic changes underlying these age‐related neurodegenerative diseases are complex, and large gaps remain in our understanding of how these diseases operate in isolation and together.

These neuropathological phenotypes may also be related at the genetic level, though our understanding remains incomplete. The primary genetic contributors to FTLD include mutations in microtubule associate protein tau (MAPT), 11 progranulin (GRN), 12 , 13 and repeat expansions in the chromosome 9 open reading frame 72 (C9Orf72), 14 , 15 with mutations in GRN and C9Orf72 primarily linked to FTLD‐TDP and mutations in MAPT linked to FTLD‐tau. 16 , 17 , 18 One FTLD‐TDP genome‐wide association study (GWAS; N = 515) identified transmembrane protein 106B (TMEM106B) (rs1990622) as a genetic risk factor. 19 , 20 This same variant was associated with TDP‐43 in the Religious Order Study/Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP). 21 Variants in TMEM106B have also been associated with clinical diagnosis of ALS, 22 Parkinson's disease, 23 AD, 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 and aging. 28 A more recent GWAS meta‐analysis of FTLD‐TDP (N = 1150) did not replicate the TMEM106B association (possibly due to differences in study design). 29 The largest contributor to genetic risk for AD dementia is the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele 30 , 31 and has also been reported to confer risk for LATE‐NC but not for HS. 6 , 7 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: The authors reviewed the scientific literature on TDP‐43 proteinopathy and HS using traditional sources (e.g., PubMed), focusing particularly on genetic and genomic studies. Despite the growing scientific interest in TDP‐43 proteinopathy, most studies had small sample sizes and focused on specific disease contexts for TDP‐43 (e.g., ALS, FTLD). Studies examining TDP‐43 proteinopathy together with HS in a genomics context were limited. Relevant studies are cited in the manuscript. Interpretation: Our findings identify a shared genetic control of HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy, particularly through the APOE and TMEM106B loci. Mediation analyses show a complex relationship between genetic factors, HS, and TDP‐43, where the genetic factors impact HS and TDP‐43 directly but also impact HS through a mediation effect of TDP‐43. Future directions: Our study shows a link between genetic factors, TDP‐43, and HS. However, the exact mechanism of these links remains unclear. Future studies of TDP‐43 proteinopathy and HS should explore the functional genomic etiologies of the phenotypes. Large‐scale genomic studies of TDP‐43 and HS should expand the sample size to be better powered to identify novel genetic variants.

GWASs of HS have typically suffered from small sample size, lack of replicability, and lack of TDP‐43 proteinopathy phenotyping. One early GWAS by Beecham et al. in 2014 investigated HS of aging (310 cases; 2576 controls) and identified nominally significant association with a variant in the KCNMB2 locus. 36 Another by Nelson et al. in 2014 (373 cases; 2303 controls) identified significant associations with variants on ABCC9 and CTAGE5/FBXO33 but not TMEM106B and GRN. 37 Finally, a sex‐specific GWAS of AD and related dementia (ADRD) neuropathology identified a male‐specific significant association of a variant (rs34331204) proximal to BZW2 with larger hippocampal volume. 38 However, these studies were limited in sample size and did not include TDP‐43 proteinopathy. Candidate gene studies found significant associations with HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy in individuals with European ancestry 39 and with HS (ABCC9) in individuals with African ancestry. 40 Nevertheless, these studies were still relatively small and relied on previously identified gene variants and clinical diagnosis as the endpoints. 39 , 40 Replication in a pathologic context is required to confirm these previous associations and possibly uncover new ones. To address these gaps, we performed the largest GWAS to date on the HS of aging (N = 9509) and TDP‐43 proteinopathy (N = 4669) in a multisite research cohort with brain autopsy, regardless of clinical diagnosis.

METHODS

Sample acquisition and selection

Participants were enrolled and assessed clinically through the Alzheimer's Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC) and collaborating sites. These include participants enrolled through the National Institute on Aging (NIA)‐funded Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs), the Adult Changes in Thought study (ACT; Eric B. Larson, PI), ROSMAP (David Bennett, PI), and the University of Pittsburgh ADRC(UPitt; Ilyas Kamboh, PI). Additional samples come from the Neurological Tissue Bank at the Biobank‐Hospital Clinic, Instituto de Investigaciones Biomédicas August Pi i Sunyer, Barcelona (IDIBAPS; Laura Molina‐Porcel, PI). All participants (or representatives) provided written informed consent; all protocols and assessments were performed with approval by the appropriate internal Institutional Review Boards (Table S1). Data related to the NIA‐ADRC samples were obtained through the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC; Walter Kukull, PI). 41 Samples were excluded from analysis if the age at death was less than 50 years.

Neuropathologic evaluation

HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy were assessed using consensus criteria, 42 with harmonized variable coding through the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project Phenotype Harmonization Consortium (ADSP‐PHC; Hohman, Cuccaro MPIs; Beecham, Montine Domain‐PIs). HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy were both coded as binary measures (absence/presence); TDP‐43 proteinopathy was also measured ordinally in a subset of sites; these data were used in some secondary analyses (Table S2).

Genotyping and quality control

Genotyping of ADRC and ADGC collaborating centers was performed using high‐density genotyping arrays (Table S3). 43 , 44 IDIBAPS participant samples underwent genotyping with the Illumina global screening array (GSA) plus the NeuroBooster array at the University of Miami, Center for Genomic Technology. Samples from the ADRCs were genotyped in batches and include all ADRC releases with neuropathology samples (ADRC batches 1 to 15). These batches were then grouped by genotyping array type, resulting in six different ADRC batches.

The standardized ADGC quality control pipeline was performed at the sample and variant levels. 43 , 44 Briefly, samples or variants with low call rates (sample missingness > 2%; variant missingness > 5%), sex discordance, or deviations from Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (among controls) were dropped. Relatedness checks were performed with the KING algorithm from the SNPRelate package in R. 45 , 46 The analysis revealed identical pairs (kinship ≥ 0.480) that were subsequently dropped. For pairs with lower kinship (0.177 ≤ kinship < 0.480), only one individual from each related pair was kept, whichever had the most non‐missing neuropathology phenotype information. The samples were imputed with the Trans‐Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) imputation server, to the TOPMed reference panel (r 2). 47 Genetic variants with minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.01 and imputation quality score, r 2 ≥ 0.40, were used for analysis. From the genotype posterior probabilities, we calculated the estimated dosage of the alternate allele for each variant (i.e., 0×p(RR)+1×p(AR)+2×p(AA), where RR is referent homozygote, AR is the heterozygote, and AA is the alternate homozygote).

Population substructure was assessed at multiple levels. Prior to genotype imputation, principal components (PCs) were generated by the ADGC using EIGENSTRAT and plotted together with 1000 Genomes Phase 3 data to confirm similarity to continental populations. Northern Europeans from Utah (CEU) and Tuscans from Italy (TSI) were used to represent non‐Hispanic Whites. Population outliers (> 5 standard deviations based on top 10 PCs) were removed.

As the overall ADGC sample set was much larger than the neuropathological subset, we extended principal component analysis (PCA), restricting the analysis to the neuropathology samples. Specifically, after imputation, PCA was conducted using PC‐AiR to account for population substructure. 48 Outliers for genetic ancestry (>6 standard deviations from mean within any of the first 10 PCs) were dropped. The top three PCs were used as covariates in the association analyses, based on visual inspection of PC plots and skree plots of eigenvalues.

APOE genotype was derived using the two single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs; rs7412 and rs429358) that together define the APOE ε2, ε3, and ε4 alleles. We derived the dosage of the TMEM106B rs1990622 A allele (allele frequency = 0.571), a risk allele for TDP‐43 proteinopathy. 19

Statistical analyses

GWAS analyses

We applied a logistic regression model using estimated dosages adjusting for age at death, sex, and the first three PCs using RVtests (version 20171009) within datasets (12 sets for HS and 11 sets for TDP‐43 proteinopathy). 49 Variants with effect estimates (beta coefficients) with absolute values > 5 were dropped before the meta‐analysis. Dataset‐specific results were then combined in a fixed‐effect meta‐analysis with an inverse‐variance‐weighted approach, as implemented in METAL, excluding genetic variants appearing in less than 30% of datasets. 50 Q–Q plots were generated along with genomic inflation factors. As a sensitivity analysis, we conducted a GWAS that also included APOE ε4 count, in addition to age at death, sex, and PCs 1 to 3.

Candidate variants were identified from a recent clinical AD GWAS meta‐analysis (excluding APOE; taken from Table S32 in Bellenguez et al.). 27 Of the 83 variants, 78 passed our quality control (QC) process. The five that failed were dropped due to either low MAF (MAF < 0.01) or did not impute well (r2 < 0.40).

Mediation analysis

To better understand the impact of genetic factors (APOE, TMEM106B, and GRN) on HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy, we conducted a mediation analysis. In our hypothesized path model, we posit that APOE, TMEM106B, and GRN may have direct effects on the risk for both TDP‐43 proteinopathy and HS. Additionally, we explore the possibility of an indirect effect, where TDP‐43 proteinopathy serves as a mediator, potentially explaining how the genetic factors contribute to the risk of developing HS. This approach allows us to investigate the intricate pathways through which genetic factors may affect these pathologies. To run the mediation analysis, we used the lavaan package in R. 51 Sex and age at death were included as covariates in the mediation model. Structural equation modeling was performed using a maximum likelihood estimator with bootstrapping. As a sensitivity analysis, we also used a Monte Carlo quasi‐Bayesian approach as implemented by the mediation package in R. 52

Post‐GWAS analyses

In silico analyses

Primary genome‐wide association meta‐analyses were supplemented with additional analyses using Functional Mapping and Annotation (FUMA version 1.8.0). 53 Assessments include gene and pathway/set based analyses using MAGMA; gene sets include Gene Ontology (GO) terms, the curated sets from MsigDB, and GTEx version 8 tissue sets.

Additionally, variants in linkage disequillibrium (LD) with index SNPs were identified through FUMA. We, independently of FUMA, annotated these using combined annotation‐dependent depletion (CADD) 54 1.7 and RegulomeDB version 2.2. 55

Fine mapping of TMEM106B locus

To pinpoint additional potential causal variants within the TMEM106B region, we used whole‐genome sequencing data that were available for select participants to fine map the variants in the locus (chr7:121,500,000‐123,000,000). Data were available through the AD Sequencing Project (ADSP) Release 3 (https://dss.niagads.org/), available for select NACC samples with autopsy. After filtering and relatedness checks, 1604 participants were included. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and short indel variants with an allele frequency greater than or equal to 0.01 were included. Multi‐allelic variants were excluded. Structural variants (SVs) in ADSP Release 3 were called using Biograph (version 6.0.4) against the GRCh38 reference genome and merged using truvari (version 3.0.0) with default parameters. 56 , 57 SVs less than 50 bp in length and with allele frequency less than 0.01 were excluded.

Analysis of each variant (SNVs, indels, and SVs) was performed using logistic regression, with HS and TDP‐43 (presence/absence) as dependent outcomes and sex and age at death as covariates in the model, with alternate allele count as an additive effect (i.e., 0, 1, 2). The 95% credible set was defined using the susieR package (0.12.35) with effect sizes, standard errors, and variant correlations estimated from the sequence data. 58 , 59 Variants in the credible set were annotated with Variant Effect Predictor's online tool. 60 Finally, a conditional analysis to identify multiple signals within the region and to assess which variants provide the best model fit was performed and described in detail in the supplemental material (Supplemental Text 2).

RESULTS

Cohort characteristics

A total of 9509 autopsied participants of European ancestry and age at death > 50 years were included in the analysis for HS and 4669 in the analysis for TDP‐43 proteinopathy (Table 1). Of the participants with both traits assessed (N = 4618), most had neither HS nor TDP‐43 proteinopathy (57%), 27% had TDP‐43 proteinopathy alone, 5% had HS alone, and 11% had both HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy. The correlation between the two pathologies was moderate (Spearman ρ = 0.28; Figure S1), consistent with reports that the two traits co‐occur more often than predicted by chance alone. 3 , 61 , 62 Both HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy were mildly positively correlated with AD/ADRD pathologies (Spearman ρ between 0.07 and 0.15; Figure S1). Neuritic plaques were present most frequently in the HS‐positive groups: 85% among those with HS alone, and 82.4% among those with HS with TDP‐43 (relative to 73.1% among those with neither; Table S4).

Genome‐wide association analyses

GWAS of HS

We performed a GWAS on HS with a total of 9509 individuals of European ancestry (1203 with HS and 8306 without HS at autopsy across 12 subsets). Results were not inflated for false positives (λ = 1.01; Figure S2). Three loci achieved genome‐wide significance with HS: APOE (index SNP: rs429358; p = 4.1×10−9), TMEM106B (rs4721058; p = 1.9×10−15) and GRN (rs5848; minimum p = 1.3×10−10) (Table 2; Figure S3). Regional association plots show many variants in high LD at the TMEM106B (Figure 1), APOE (Figure S4), and GRN (Figure S5) loci. These loci were tested in multiple datasets, with the TMEM106B locus showing only nominal heterogeneity (heterogeneity p = 0.047) (Figures S6–S8). Two additional loci showed suggestive significance (p < 5.0×10−7; Table S5): a variant on chromosome 1 (LINC01031; rs114769806, p = 1.5×10−7) and a variant on the Protein Phosphatase 2 Regulatory Subunit B gamma isoform (PPP2R2C; rs28464919, p = 4.0 ×10−7).

GWAS on TDP‐43 proteinopathy

We also performed a GWAS meta‐analysis on TDP‐43 proteinopathy in 4669 samples of European ancestry (1808 with TDP‐43 proteinopathy and 2861 without TDP‐43 proteinopathy at autopsy across 11 subsets). Results were not inflated for false positives (λ = 1.015; Figure S9). Variants in both APOE and TMEM106B were associated with TDP‐43 proteinopathy at a genome‐wide level (APOE: rs429358; p = 2.0×10−20; Table 2; TMEM106B: rs1468804, p = 7.4×10−11; Table 2). Regional association plots showed support for both TMEM106B (Figure 1) and APOE (Figure S4). These loci were tested in multiple datasets, and forest plots showed no evidence of heterogeneity in effect size (Figures S10 and S11). Two additional variants reached suggestive significance (p < 5.0×10−7) with the TDP‐43 proteinopathy GWAS (Table S5): one variant on the Corticotropin Releasing Hormone Receptor 1 gene (CRHR1; rs4458044, p = 4.3×10−7) and another on the One Cut Homeobox 2 gene (ONECUT2; rs56094304, p = 2.9×10−7). The GRN locus was nominally associated with TDP‐43 but was just outside suggestive significance criteria (rs5848, p = 8.3×10−7).

Analysis of candidate variants

TDP‐43 proteinopathy and HS variants

We additionally assessed a TMEM106B variant previously identified in a FTLD‐TDP GWAS (rs1990622). 19 , 21 This variant was associated with both HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy (pHS = 1.2×10−14 and pTDP‐43 = 1.7×10−10). However, the variant is in strong linkage disequilibrium with the two TMEM106B index SNPs we identified in our GWAS (R 2 rs4721058 = 0.954; R 2 rs1468804 = 0.962), suggesting that all three represent the same underlying signal.

Previously implicated associations of HS with variants on KCNMB2 (rs9637454) 36 and ABCC9 (rs7966849) 37 only showed nominal association in our GWAS of HS (prs9637454 = 0.01; prs7966849 = 0.001), but not TDP‐43 proteinopathy (prs9637454 = 0.54; prs7966849 = 0.38) (Table S6). Candidate variants on genes associated with FTLD and ALS (DPP6, UNC13A, HLA‐DQA2, and a proxy SNP for C9Orf72) were not associated with either pathologic phenotype (Table S6).

AD candidate variants

We assessed the association between HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy and 78 previously identified AD loci. 27 In total, 15 of the 78 variants were nominally associated (p < 0.05) with either HS or TDP‐43 proteinopathy. Specifically, the HS analyses showed nominal association with nine loci, as did the TDP‐43 proteinopathy analyses (nine loci at p < 0.05) (Table 3). This enrichment of associated AD loci is statistically significant (p = 0.016) based on a binomial distribution (nine out of 78, p = 0.05). All but three of the 15 variants showed odds ratios in the same direction of effect, as previously reported (Table 3). 27

Post‐GWAS analyses

In silico analyses

To better understand potential mechanistic consequences of the GWAS outcomes, we conducted follow‐up analyses in FUMA. 53 Gene‐based tests showed only genes around genome‐wide significant variants (i.e., APOE, TMEM106B, and nearby genes such as APOC1, TOMM40, FAM171A2) met criteria for gene‐based association (p < 2.6 × 10−6). A gene‐set‐based analysis (based on the top 10 genes from the HS gene‐based analyses) identified an enrichment in the GO set “Negative Regulation of Lipid Bioythetic Process” that survive a multiple‐testing correction (p = 0.00752). No other pathways met study‐wide criteria for significance for either HS or TDP‐43, though it is worth noting that lipid processing, clearance, and metabolic processes tended to dominate the lists (Tables S7 and S8).

Functional annotation identified three non‐synonymous variants in LD with index SNPs in the HS sample set: rs3173615 (r 2 = 0.98 with the TMEM106B index variant), rs5911 in the ITGA2B gene (r 2 = 0.66 with the GRN index variant), and rs429358 (the index variant for APOE). Four variants showed potential functionally disruptive activity (CADD score > 10 and RegulomeDB score < 5, as per Kunkle et al.). 44 These include two of the exonic variants noted earlier (rs429358 for APOE; rs5911 in ITGA2B), as well as rs17648926 (CADD1.7 = 11.64, regulomeDB = 1f) and rs6460898 (CADD1.7 = 11.52, regulomeDB = 1f).

Fine mapping of TMEM106B locus

Given that multiple variants in the TMEM106B region have been reported, we performed statistical fine mapping in the TMEM106B region. We used whole‐genome sequence (WGS) data from the ADSP to fine map the TMEM106B region in a subset of individuals (N HS = 1,472 and N TDP‐43 = 420). These individuals largely overlap with our GWAS samples (and are thus not independent), but the WGS represents a denser variant map and includes structural variation called through the ADSP Structural Variation working group. 63 In the WGS data there were 456 variants nominally associated (p < 0.05) with HS and 224 with TDP‐43 proteinopathy in the primary model adjusting for sex, age at death, and PCs 1 to 3. There were 93 variants contributing to the 95% Bayesian credible set within the HS analysis, only one of which was exonic (chr7:12,229,791:C:G, rs3173615). This is a missense variant resulting in a threonine‐to‐serine change at amino acid 185 and was previously reported in the FTLD literature. 19 Common annotation prediction methods, such as SIFT and PolyPhen, predict the variant to be benign/tolerated. A recently reported Alu insertion (at chr7:12,242,077) was also in the credible set. 64 The Alu was associated with HS in the ADSP data (p = 3.4×10−7; TDP‐43 p = 0.0034) but was also in high LD with the index SNP (Spearman ρ = 0.95) (Table S9). While there were other structural variants in the region, none were in the credible set. The conditional association analyses (Supplemental Text 2) were not able to resolve the causal variant in the region. The LD in the region is sufficiently tight that any variants in the credible set can nearly completely explain the signal. As such, additional approaches will be needed to identify the causal variants.

TDP‐43 proteinopathy partially mediates the effects of APOE ε4, TMEM106B, and GRN on hippocampal sclerosis

We used mediation analyses to assess the relationships between APOE ε4, TMEM106B, GRN, TDP‐43 proteinopathy, and HS. Mediation analysis involves studying how the presence or absence of a mediating variable impacts the magnitude of an observed effect in multiple regression models. If the effect stays the same, there is a direct effect, and no mediation is present. If the effect changes with the mediating variable, it indicates an indirect effect, and the variable is considered a mediator. For this study, mediation analyses assess whether the genetic factors are associated with HS directly or are mediated by through TDP‐43 proteinopathy. Three models were tested: one with APOE ε4, TDP‐43 proteinopathy, and HS; a second with TMEM106B genotype, TDP‐43 proteinopathy, and HS; and a third with GRN genotype, TDP‐43 proteinopathy, and HS (Figure 2; Table S10). Results reported here use the previously reported rs1990622A genotype as the TMEM106B variant; 19 however, sensitivity analyses using our GWAS‐defined index SNPs (rs4721058, rs1468804) showed similar results (Tables S11 and S12).

Summary of results from mediation analyses. The analysis included 4618 individuals who had data available on transactive response DNA binding protein 43 kDa (TDP‐43) proteinopathy and hippocampal sclerosis. APOE ε4 (top), TMEM106B rs1990622A (middle), and GRN (bottom) may either have direct effects (blue lines) on hippocampal sclerosis but may also operate through TDP‐43 proteinopathy (yellow lines). All direct (blue) and indirect effects (yellow) were significant at p < 0.05. Full summary statistics from the mediation analysis are found in Supplementary Table 10.

For all mediation models, partial mediation was observed, with TDP‐43 proteinopathy acting as an intermediary (Figure 2). We observed direct effects of TMEM106B on TDP‐43 proteinopathy and HS, as well as an indirect effect on HS (i.e., TDP‐43 mediation). This suggests that a portion of TMEM106B association with HS is through the impact of TMEM106B on TDP‐43 proteinopathy. The same was true with APOE ε4 and GRN. Specifically, TDP‐43 proteinopathy partially mediated 27.6% of the statistical effect of TMEM106B on HS and 25% of the statistical effect of APOE ε4 on HS (Figure 2). We also observed that the magnitude of TMEM106B’s risk effect for TDP‐43 proteinopathy was stronger than for HS (direct effect on TDP‐43 = 0.064, direct effect on HS = 0.033; Supplementary Table 10). The same was true for APOE (direct effect on TDP‐43 = 0.084, direct effect on HS = 0.051) and GRN (direct effect on TDP‐43 = 0.044, direct effect on HS = 0.038; Table S10).

DISCUSSION

We present here a GWAS on HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy. We observed association signals for both HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy with several variants in and around the TMEM106B locus. TMEM106B encodes transmembrane protein 106B, which localizes to the endo‐lysosomal membrane and regulates the size, stability, motility, trafficking, and acidification of lysosomes. 65 A loss of function of TMEM106B has been shown to increase risk for GRN‐associated FTLD‐TDP via reduction of protein turnover in the lysosomal system. 66 , 67 Our analyses confirm the association of TMEM106B with HS and show that the locus is broadly associated with TDP‐43 proteinopathy.

When fine mapping the TMEM106B locus, there were many variants significantly associated with HS that contributed to the credible set, including a previously reported SNV and a structural variant associated with FTLD. 19 , 64 Both variants were associated with HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy in our data and were in high LD with the index variants from our HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy GWAS, as well as with each other, making identification of the causal variant difficult using statistical methods alone.

In addition to the TMEM106B locus, we showed association with APOE (for HS and TDP‐43) and GRN (for HS) at a genome‐wide level. We also observed suggestive associations with both TDP‐43 proteinopathy (variants on CRHR1 and ONECUT2) and HS (variants on LINC01031, and PPP2R2C), which will require further replication. CRHR1 in particular has been linked to neurodegenerative disease, such as Parkinson's disease, 68 and several psychiatric disorders. The gene is on chromosome 17 and is both physically close to and in linkage disequilibrium with MAPT, which has also been linked to many neurodegenerative disorders.

HS is generally seen as a downstream consequence of TDP‐43 proteinopathy. 1 There are also indications that TDP‐43 proteinopathy without co‐occurring HS may be an early or transitional phase in this proposed progression. There are some conflicting reports on the factors affecting this pathway, like APOE and TMEM106B. One study showed that APOE did not directly impact the likelihood of HS but rather indirectly via TDP‐43. 7 In another study, APOE and TMEM106B still have a significant effect on HS risk when adjusting for the presence of TDP‐43 proteinopathy; however, that effect is attenuated. 6 In our mediation analyses, the effect of TMEM106B on HS was partially mediated by TDP‐43 proteinopathy but also showed a statistically significant direct effect on HS that was independent of TDP‐43 proteinopathy (Figure 2). As for APOE, one study showed that it did not directly impact the risk of HS when accounting for TDP‐43 proteinopathy, but our study disagreed. 7 In this work, APOE operated through multiple paths in the mediation model (significant direct and indirect effects). The exact mechanisms for these effects are unclear. One clue may be multiple connections across distinct pathologies (e.g., FTLD, AD). The variants here (TMEM106B, GRN, APOE) have all been linked to both clearance‐ and inflammation‐related pathways. It is possible that dysfunctional clearance pathways could lead to initial proteinopathy, while dysfunctional inflammatory response could increase the severity of the proteinopathy and exacerbate neuronal loss. A model with multiple mechanisms like this could explain both the incomplete mediation we see here and some of the genetic overlap across neuropathologies. Clearly, though, some of this is speculative, and future studies should build upon these mediation and pathway models to capture a deeper understanding of the contributors to neurodegeneration. Additionally, these results highlight the need for future studies assessing multiple pathologies concurrently (e.g., presence of HS and TDP‐43 jointly).

This study has a few limitations. For one, the autopsied cohorts used for this study had genetic ancestry most similar to European referent populations, so findings may not generalize to other ancestral groups. Future efforts should seek to enroll more heterogeneous populations to enable analyses across a broader spectrum of ancestry and to generalize HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy research to more people. Additionally, heterogeneous populations often have different linkage disequilibrium patterns, which may help better narrow the region of interest for TMEM106B. Second, though we accounted for batch effects by conducting a meta‐analysis, patient ascertainment across batches differs (e.g., community‐based like ACT and ROSMAP vs. clinic‐based like NACC) and may include differences in education, social determinants of health, age at death, and sex. These ascertainment differences, and the presence of other pathologies (especially AD pathologies), may introduce unforeseen biases. Given the robustness of our results across the meta‐analyzed datasets, we do not feel this is a major issue, but it should not be completely discounted. Finally, because of sample size, we were only powered to detect associations with common variants, and additional genomic studies are important to better capture the missing heritability of ADRD. Even though this was the largest autopsy‐derived TDP‐43 proteinopathy and HS dataset in an aging context, it still does not compare to the size of recent GWASs on AD dementia. 26 As such, we recommend a continued emphasis on promoting the expansion of autopsy programs and on the inclusion of HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy in neuropathology assessment protocols.

In conclusion, we identified novel genome‐wide genetic associations relevant to the pathogenesis of TDP‐43 proteinopathy and HS that were previously only seen in GWASs on FTLD‐TDP and ALS. Given the complexities of clinically defined AD and ADRDs, our study shows that pathologically defined HS and TDP‐43 proteinopathy share genetic risk across FTLD, ALS, AD, and LATE. We also demonstrated the utility of detailed neuropathologic scoring and reporting from autopsy programs, such as those available through NACC and the ADSP‐PHC. We especially show the utility of assessing TDP‐43 proteinopathy and HS in uncovering one piece of the complex genetic landscape of AD and ADRD.
