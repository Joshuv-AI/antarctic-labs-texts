Acalabrutinib in patients with mantle cell lymphoma subjected to allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation. a phase 2 study by the Polish Lymphoma Research Group (PLRG12 trial)
Authors: Sebastian Giebel, Anna Czyż, Iwona Hus, Lidia Gil, Krzysztof Giannopoulos, Joanna Romejko-Jarosińska, Maciej Majcherek, Agnieszka Szeremet, Anna Wache, Paulina Kwaśnik, Ryszard Swoboda, Wojciech Jurczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis phase 2 trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of acalabrutinib used to reduce tumor burden before allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT) and as a maintenance therapy after transplantation in patients with relapsed/refractory mantle cell lymphoma (R/R MCL). Patients were treated with acalabrutinib 100 mg BID for 3–6 months. Those achieving complete or partial response (CR, PR) were referred for allo-HCT. Acalabrutinib was restarted 30–90 days after allo-HCT and administered for the subsequent 9 months. The remaining patients continued acalabrutinib until progression or unacceptable toxicity. Out of the 28 included patients, 16 subjects (57%) responded, with CR in 14 cases (50%) and PR in 2 cases (7%). Fifteen patients (54%) underwent allo-HCT. Twenty-three patients (79%) had grade 3–4 adverse events, most commonly neutropenia (34%) and infections (21%). Five patients died of treatment-related toxicity, including infections in 4 cases. he probabilities of overall and progression-free survival (PFS) at 2 years were 60% (95%CI, 40–79) and 41.5% (23–60), respectively. PFS rate for patients treated with allo-HCT was 67% (44–92) at 18 months. Acalabrutinib generates high response rates in patients with R/R MCL and may serve as a “bridge” to allo-HCT and maintenance after transplantation. Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a relatively rare subtype of B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL) with an incidence of 0.1–1.27 cases per 100,000 person-year [1]. First-line treatment comprises immunochemotherapy, which in fit individuals may be consolidated with high-dose therapy supported by autologous hematopoietic cell transplantation (auto-HCT) and followed by rituximab maintenance [2]. Recently, it has been demonstrated that the addition of Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitor, ibrutinib, increases efficacy of standard immunochemotherapy [3]. However, despite treatment advances resulting in improved response and survival rates, the vast majority of patients ultimately relapse [4, 5]. BTK inhibitors used in relapsed/refractory (R/R) setting offer high chance of disease control, however, few patients maintain response in long-term perspective [6, 7]. Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cells are effective in case of failure to BTK inhibitors with over 90% response rate, however, durability of these responses remains uncertain.8.

To date, allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT) is the only treatment option with proven curative potential in R/R MCL. Long-term progression-free survival (PFS) rates are in a range of 30–50%.9–12 This is mainly thanks to a well-demonstrated graft-versus-lymphoma reaction justifying administration of reduced-intensity or non-myeloablative conditioning regimens [9, 10]. Disease status at transplantation is one of the most important prognostic factors affecting the risk or progression and a chance of PFS after allo-HCT. Better outcomes are achieved in case of complete or partial response (CR, PR) compared to refractory disease [10–12]. Hence, effective therapeutic intervention prior to allo-HCT appears to be an important component of the therapeutic strategy aiming to achieve cure. Furthermore, post-transplant maintenance may reduce the risk of relapse and contribute to further improvement of the outcome.

Acalabrutinib, a second-generation BTK inhibitor was shown to produce 81% overall response rate (ORR) and 40% CR rate in patients with R/R MCL [7]. Matching-adjusted indirect comparison demonstrated efficacy of acalabrutinib similar to ibrutinib with improved toxicity profile reported for acalabrutinib [13]. The aim of this clinical trial was to evaluate safety and efficacy of acalabrutinib used to reduce tumor burden prior to allo-HCT and as a maintenance after transplantation in order to prevent relapse.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a multicenter, open-label phase 2 study conducted in 6 centers assigned to the Polish Lymphoma Research Group (PLRG) (clinicaltrials.gov: NCT04716075). It was originally designed to include patients with either R/R MCL or R/R high-risk chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL). However, in the initial study period standards of CLL treatment changed restricting substantially the role of allo-HCT. As a consequence, only one patient with CLL was enrolled in this study. The steering committee decided to exclude this patient from the efficacy analysis while including them in the safety analysis. Study design is depicted in supplementary file (Figure S1). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization of Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the review board of the PLRG and independent ethics committee. All patients provided written informed consent.

Key inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age ≥ 18 years, (2) R/R MCL with no history of BTK inhibitors and up to 4 lines of preceding therapy, (3) Patients referred for allo-HCT, having HLA-matched identical sibling donor (MSD) or with initiated search for an unrelated donor (URD), (4) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 or 1. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in supplementary Appendix 1.

Forty patients were planned to be enrolled and approximately 25 patients were expected to respond to initial treatment and proceed to allo-HCT. However, the recruitment was slower than expected due to SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. Therefore sponsor decided to prematurely terminate the recruitment after enrolling 29 patients.

Treatment regimen

Patients intended for allo-HCT were treated with acalabrutinib 100 mg BID for 3–6 months. Those who achieved CR or PR and had appropriate donor were referred for allo-HCT. Acalabrutinib was restarted 30–90 days after allo-HCT in case of hematopoietic recovery (neutrophil count ≥ 1 × 109/L and platelet count ≥ 50 × 109/L) and administered for subsequent 9 months. Patients not achieving CR or PR after initial treatment continued acalabrutinib until progression or unacceptable toxicity.

Allo-HCT could be performed using either MSD, URD or haploidentical donor. Peripheral blood (PB) was recommended source of stem cells in case of MSD-HCT and URD-HCT while for haploidentical HCT both PB and bone marrow were acceptable. Conditioning regimen for MSD-HCT or URD-HCT was either BFR (bendamustine 130 mg/m2 i.v. daily on days − 5 to – 3, fludarabine 30 mg/m2 i.v. daily on days − 5 to -3, rituximab 375 mg/m2 i.v. on day − 6) or total body irradiation (TBI)/F/R (TBI 4 Gy on day − 1 or 0, fludarabine 30 mg/m2 i.v. daily on days − 5 to -3, rituximab 375 mg/m2 i.v. on day − 6). In case of haploidentical HCT conditioning regimen consisted of cyclophosphamide 14.5 mg/kg i.v. daily on days − 6 and − 5, fludarabine 30 mg/m2 i.v. daily on days − 6, -5, -4, -3 and − 2 and TBI 2 Gy on day − 1 (TBI/Cy/F/R). Immunosuppressive regimen in case of MSD-HCT and matched URD-HCT was based on tacrolimus or cyclosporine with short-course methotrexate and rituximab (on days + 1 and + 8). In case of matched URD-HCT patients received additionally anti-thymocyte globulin. For mismatched URD and haploidentical HCT, immunosuppression was based on post-transplant cyclophosphamide administered on days + 3 and + 4 (50 mg/kg i.v. daily) followed by tacrolimus and mycophenolate mofetil. Patients received supportive care with antibiotics, antifungals, antivirals and immunizations after allo-HCT according to institutional guidelines.

End points, assessments and statistical analysis

The primary objectives were to evaluate the safety profile and response to the treatment. Safety parameters included frequency of adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs (SAEs). AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities while the severity of AEs was graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Primary efficacy end-point was ORR. Secondary end-points included CR rate, PR rate and the rate of CR with negative status of measurable residual disease (MRD) as well as relapse incidence (RI), non-relapse mortality (NRM), PFS and overall survival (OS). CR and PR were evaluated using Lugano criteria after 3 and 6 months from start of the treatment. Among patients referred for transplantation the disease status was assessed subsequently 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15 and 18 months after allo-HCT procedure [14]. In the rest of the patients, response rates were evaluated any time when investigator suspected progression. MRD was assessed using flow cytometry in peripheral blood in a central laboratory. Exploratory end-points referred solely to the patients actually treated with allo-HCT and included the incidence of acute and chronic graft-versus-host-disease (GVHD) as well as GVHD and relapse-free survival (GRFS).15.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline demographic and disease characteristics, response to treatment, and safety outcomes. Probabilities of OS and PFS were calculated using Kaplan-Meier estimates. Cumulative incidence curves were used to estimate the probabilities of RI and NRM in a competing-risk setting [16].

Results

Patients

Twenty-eight patients with MCL and one patient with CLL were enrolled between 19th August 2019 and 25th May 2023. The median age of MCL patients was 61 years (range, 43–68 years). Thirteen (46%) patients were classified as high risk according to mantle cell lymphoma international prognostic index. The median number of previous treatment lines was 1 (range, 1–5). Eleven patients (39%) experienced progression within 6 months after first-line therapy. Twelve (46%) out of 26 subjects with available data had confirmed resistance to the last line of systemic therapy. At study entry, 8 (29%) patients had bulky disease and 17 (61%) patients had extranodal lesions. Detailed patients’ characteristics are listed in Table 1.

Efficacy and study conduct

Out of 28 patients with MCL, 16 subjects responded (ORR, 57%), with CR in 14 cases (50%) and PR in 2 cases (7%). CR with MRD negative status was documented in 6 (21%) cases. The median duration of acalabrutinib induction treatment was 177 days (range, 169–339 days). Fifteen patients (54%) underwent allo-HCT from either URD (n = 10), MSD (n = 3) or haploidentical donor (n = 2). The conditioning regimens were: BFR in 8 cases, FluCyTBI(2 Gy) – in 4 cases, FluTBI(4 Gy) – 1 subject, FluTBI (8 Gy) – 1 subject, RFluTBI (4 Gy) – 1 subject. One responding patient was not administered allo-HCT due to AE. Among allo-HCT recipients 13 patients were initiated acalabrutinib maintenance with median post-transplant treatment duration of 230 days (range, 0-321 days).

With the median follow-up of 24 months, the probability of OS and PFS at two years was 60% (95% confidence interval [CI], 40–79%) and 41.5% (95% CI, 23–60%), respectively (Figs. 1 and 2). The cumulative incidence of progression was 36% (95% CI, 18–55%) while NRM was 22% (6–38%) (Fig. 3).

PFS rate for 15 patients treated with allo-HCT was 67% (95% CI, 44–92%) at 18 months post-transplant (Fig. 4). The probability of OS at 18 months was 73% (95% CI, 51–96%) while the incidence of NRM and progression was 11% (95% CI, 0–23%) and 33% (95% CI, 15–51%), respectively. No events were reported beyond 12 months after allo-HCT. Acute GVHD was reported in 5 (33%) patients, including grade 1 in one case and grade 2 in 4 cases. Chronic GVHD was reported in 5 (33%) individuals, including moderate stage in 4 patients and severe stage in one patient. The probability of GRFS at 18 months was 40% (95% CI, 15–65%).

Safety

Adverse events of any grade were reported in 27 out of 29 (93%) enrolled subjects (Table 2). The grade ≥ 3 AEs were observed in 23 (79%) subjects, most frequently neutropenia (n = 10, 34%), lymphocytopenia (n = 5, 17%), and infections (n = 8, 28%). Of note, prior to allo-HCT, grade ≥ 3 AEs occurred in 45% of patients during acalabrutinib treatment while during post-transplant maintenance the incidence of ≥ 3 AEs was 80% (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Ten patients (34%) experienced SARS-CoV-2 infection, which was grade ≥ 3 in 6 (21%) cases. There were 29 SAEs reported in 15 (52%) individuals. Five patients died due to SAE, which were predominantly infections (n = 4), including COVID-19 in 2 cases. One patient died of hemorrhage (hematoma). Two fatal events occurred during initial treatment with acalabrutinib, while three deaths occurred during post-transplant maintenance (Supplementary Table S1 and S2). In 3 subjects AE led to study treatment discontinuation.

Discussion

Allo-HCT is the only treatment option with proven curative potential in R/R MCL. In this prospective, multicenter trial, for the first time BTK inhibitor - acalabrutinib, was used to augment the efficacy of allo-HCT. Its administration as induction let majority of patients proceed to transplantation, while its use post-transplant was feasible and could contribute to maintaining response.

According to recommendations by the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy published in 2020, allo-HCT should be considered a standard of care for patients with R/R MCL, including those relapsing after auto-HCT [17]. This statement was supported by results of a large retrospective analysis by the European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EBMT) including 324 allo-HCT recipients out of whom 60% received 3 or more lines of preceding therapy and 27% were refractory to last line of treatment [10]. The cumulative incidence of relapse was 40% at 5 years, PFS was 31% at 4 years, and OS was 40% at 5 years. Patients who developed a chronic GVHD and/or patients who did not receive an in vivo T-cell depletion with CAMPATH had a significantly lower relapse rate, suggesting the existence of a graft versus MCL effect. The risk of progression and overall mortality was more than two times lower for patients with chemo-sensitive disease compared to those with disease refractoriness [10].

In view of the EBMT data, attempts to reduce tumor burden before allo-HCT are fully justified. Historically, salvage regimens were based on chemotherapy with or without rituximab. High rates of response could be achieved only with intensive chemotherapy regimens at the cost of severe myelotoxicity and high risk of life-threatening complications [18–20]. In contrast, conventional-dose salvage regimens were associated with only modest response rates and short remissions [21, 22]. Acalabrutinib was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration agency based on results of phase 2 single arm study including 124 patients with R/R MCL. Acalabrutinib monotherapy was effective and associated with favorable toxicity profile [7]. An updated analysis showed 37% probability of PFS at 3 years, however, progressions were reported beyond 4 years with little chance for long-term plateau [23]. In our study the ORR was lower compared to the registration trial (57% vs. 81%), while the CR rate was slightly higher (50% vs. 40%). The toxicity profile was similar. While majority of patients experienced grade 3–4 AE, they were almost exclusively restricted to cytopenias and infections. It must be stressed that the study was conducted during SARS-Cov-2 pandemic, which could increase the risk of fatal events. Indeed, two patients died of COVID-19.

In our study 15 patients proceeded to allo-HCT. Their outcomes (67% PFS rate at 18 months) compare favorably with those previously reported by the EBMT for patients allografted in first chemo-sensitive response (PFS 47% at 18 months) [10]. Importantly, no progressions in our study were reported beyond 12 months post-transplant. This could result from more profound remission status before allo-HCT (13/15 patients transplanted in CR), but also from acalabrutinib maintenance. To our best knowledge no prospective studies nor retrospective analyses have been published to date on the use of BTK inhibitors after allo-HCT to prevent progression. Small study population does not allow to draw definitive conclusions, however, it seems that administration of acalabrutinib does not increase the risk of GVHD. In fact, none of the patients developed severe acute GVHD while the incidence of chronic GVHD (33%) was within expected range. According to the EBMT guidelines, another BTK inhibitor, ibrutinib is recommended as treatment option for patients with steroid-refractory chronic GVHD [24]. In a retrospective study including 22 MCL patients treated with ibrutinib as a “bridge” to allo-HCT, the probabilities of NRM, progression, PFS and OS at 12 months were 5%, 19%, 76%, and 86%, respectively [25].

In recent years CAR T-cells have been approved for the treatment of R/R non-Hodgkin’s lymphomas, including brexucabtagene autoleucel in MCL. The registration trial included 68 patients who failed 1–5 lines of therapy, including BTK inhibitors [8, 26]. ORR was 91% and CR rate was 68%. The probability of PFS at 2 years was 53% but several progressions were reported beyond 3 years. Hence, despite very high early efficacy, the curative potential of this therapy remains uncertain. At the time of our study initiation brexucabtagene autoleucel was not yet approved for the treatment of MCL. According to current EBMT recommendations, allo-HCT should mainly be offered to patients progressing after brexucabtagene autoleucel [27]. Results of our study suggest alternative approach i.e. CAR T-cells for patients not responding to BTK inhibitors while allo-HCT for those with R/R MCL who achieve at least PR on BTK inhibitors. It must be stressed, however, that only minority of patients with MCL are suitable for allo-HCT due to advanced age and/or co-morbidities.

Our study has some major limitations. In particular, its population was relatively small and follow-up was short. Nevertheless, we conclude that acalabrutinib generates high response rates in patients with R/R MCL and may serve as a “bridge” to allo-HCT as well as maintenance after transplantation. Infections are most frequent life-threatening complications. Further prospective studies are needed to define optimal treatment algorithm for patients with R/R MCL.
