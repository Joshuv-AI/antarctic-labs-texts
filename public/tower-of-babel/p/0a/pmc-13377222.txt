Serum tuberculosis biomarkers and their association with clinical characteristics among hospitalized adults living with HIV in Uganda
Authors: Lydia Nakiyingi, Bernard Kikaire, Olive Mbabazi, Rose Nabatanzi, Louis Kamulegeya, Elizabeth Nakabugo, Bernard Bagaya, Damalie Nakanjako, Willy Ssengooba, Harriet Mayanja-Kizza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) among people living with HIV (PLHIV) remains challenging, particularly in high TB/HIV burden settings. We evaluated serum biomarkers associated with active PTB and their relationship with TB-related clinical characteristics among hospitalized PLHIV in Uganda.MethodsWe analysed archived serum samples from adult PLHIV (n = 60) categorized into three groups (20 each): (i) sputum culture and/or GeneXpert-confirmed PTB (active PTB), (ii) presumptive TB with negative TB culture/GeneXpert (symptomatic PTB-negative), and (iii) asymptomatic PLHIV attending routine HIV care (asymptomatic PLHIV controls). Inflammatory biomarkers including C-reactive protein (CRP) and leptin as well as immune activation markers (interferon gamma–induced protein 10 -IP-10 and β2-microglobulin) were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and Bio-Plex Pro assays. Biomarker levels were compared between active PTB and TB-negative groups. Logistic regression identified biomarkers independent associations with active PTB and Spearman rank correlation assessed biomarkers correlations with TB-related clinical characteristics among active PTB participants.ResultsMedian serum concentrations of IP-10 (19,566 pg/mL; p = 0.001), β2-microglobulin (33.9 ng/mL; p = 0.023), and CRP (1.77 mg/dL; p = 0.05) were significantly higher among active PTB participants compared to symptomatic PTB-negatives and asymptomatic PLHIV controls. Conversely, leptin levels were significantly lower in the active PTB (35.0 pg/mL) than in asymptomatic PLHIV controls (102.4 pg /mL; p = 0.001). IP-10 (adjusted odds ratio-aOR 2.74; 95% CI: 1.54–4.88; p < 0.001) and β2-microglobulin (aOR 2.09; 95% CI: 1.13–3.86; p = 0.019) were the strongest independent predictors of active PTB. Biomarker concentrations did not correlate significantly with TB-related clinical characteristics.ConclusionIP-10, β2-microglobulin, and CRP were elevated in PLHIV with active PTB, whereas leptin levels were reduced. These biomarkers did not correlate with clinical characteristics. Our findings show that serum biomarkers may aid in identifying PTB among hospitalized PLHIV but with limited value in assessing clinical presentation. Further research is warranted to rigorously evaluate their diagnostic performance in both simple and combined biomarkers models and to evaluate their point-of-care utility for TB screening and timely treatment initiation in hospitalized PLHIV.

Indroduction

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), remains a major global infectious disease. HIV co-infection substantially complicates TB diagnosis and clinical management, accelerating TB disease progression, morbidity, and mortality. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Report 2025 [1], an estimated 150,000 HIV/TB co-infected people died in 2024. Uganda remains among the WHO's 30 high TB and TB/HIV burden countries, with an estimated 96,000 incident TB cases annually. Approximately 40% of the new TB cases occur among people living with HIV (PLHIV) and an estimated 30,000–34,000 TB-related deaths are reported each year [1].

TB is often the first clinical manifestation of HIV-infection and remains the leading cause of death among PLHIV [1]. However, recognition of TB in this population is challenging as many patients present with atypical clinical features and are susceptible to multiple opportunistic pulmonary infections that mimic TB. Furthermore, standard diagnostic tools including Xpert MTB/RIF and sputum smear microscopy have limited sensitivity in PLHIV [2], [3]. As a result, TB often remains undiagnosed or is diagnosed late. Delayed or missed TB diagnosis remains a critical barrier to effective TB control and improved patient outcomes. Diagnostic difficulties are further compounded by limited sputum production in severely ill patients, reduced relevance of sputum-based tests in extra-pulmonary disease, and biosafety concerns related to specimen collection.

TB diagnosis challenges among PLHIV remarkably contribute to the ongoing community transmission and underscore the need for improved case-finding strategies. There is an increasing interest in the development and application of non-sputum-based biomarkers to improve TB diagnosis, guide TB preventive therapy decisions and in monitoring TB treatment response. Biomarkers, defined as objectively measurable indicators of biological processes, disease states or therapeutic responses, offer several potential advantages including convenience, sensitivity, specificity, non-invasiveness, reproducibility and cost-effectiveness [4]. Several candidate biomarkers have been identified for TB diagnosis [4], [5], [6], however evidence supporting their utility among PLHIV remains limited. Serum biomarkers such as interferon gamma -induced protein-10 (IP-10), leptin, β2 microglobulin, and C-reactive protein (CRP) have shown potential roles in predicting active TB, supporting disease surveillance, assessing treatment efficacy, and informing prognostic evaluation [4]. However, their clinical relevance and utility in hospitalized PLHIV, particularly their relationship with TB-related clinical characteristics is not well established [4], [6].

This study evaluated serum biomarkers associated with PTB, comparing serum levels of leptin, CRP, β2 microglobulin, and IP-10 among hospitalized PLHIV with and without active PTB and examined correlation of the serum biomarkers with TB-related clinical characteristics in Uganda. This work contributes contextualisation data for the selected biomarkers in a high TB/HIV burden sub-Saharan African setting where hospitalized, severely immunosuppressed PLHIV represent a population in whom diagnostic delays are common and carry the greatest clinical consequences including substantial morbidity and mortality. The aim is to generate evidence to support the development of non-sputum-based diagnostic approaches in this population.

Methodology

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study in which we analysed clinical data and archived serum samples obtained from adult PLHIV previously enrolled for a TB diagnostics research consortium evaluating accuracy of novel TB diagnostics including a lateral flow urine TB-LAM test for TB among PLHIV [7]. The parent study which run for a period of five years from January 2011 to January 2016, recruited participants primarily from the inpatient wards of Mulago National Referral Hospital. Asymptomatic HIV-positive controls were from the outpatient adult Infectious Diseases Institute (IDI) Clinic, Kampala, Uganda.

Study population

The parent study enrolled HIV-infected adults (≥18 years) clinically suspected of having active TB. A patient was suspected to have active TB if he/she reported having at least one of cough, fever, night sweats or weight loss. Individuals were excluded if they had taken anti-TB medication for more than two days within 60 days prior to enrolment. Both male and female participants were enrolled. After informed consent, sociodemographic and clinical data were collected, including TB signs and symptoms, CD4 cell counts, age and body weight. Diagnostic evaluations including sputum smear microscopy, sputum Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tube (MGIT) culture, sputum GeneXpert, and TB blood culture tests were performed to confirm MTB. Blood was collected in serum separation tubes (SST) from which serum was processed, frozen and stored at −80 °c at the Immunology Laboratory of Makerere University, Kampala. Interferon-Gamma Release Assay or tuberculin skin test for latent TB assessment were not performed. Out of 477 participants from the parent study; 101 were positive for PTB by sputum TB culture and/or GeneXpert; 287 were negative on all the TB tests (sputum smear, TB culture and GeneXpert); 79 patients had both PTB and mycobacteremia; while 10 patients had mycobacteremia only.

For this sub-study, samples for each study group (20 each) were selected using simple random sampling. The three study groups included: i) sputum TB culture and/or GeneXpert confirmed PTB (active PTB), ii) symptomatic presumptive TB individuals with any of the TB symptoms (cough, fever, night sweats, or weight loss) and negative TB tests (sputum smear microscopy, MGIT culture, and GeneXpert) (symptomatic TB-negative), and iii) asymptomatic PLHIV attending routine HIV outpatient care clinic at IDI (asymptomatic PLHIV controls). The TB-negative participants were not further categorized by their specific alternative diagnoses in the parent study. While the asymptomatic HIV control group were from the outpatient HIV clinic, the two symptomatic groups (active PTB and TB-negatives) were hospitalized patients. Serum samples from the study groups were retrieved. The sample of 20 per group was determined by specimen availability and the primary aim of generating preliminary data on biomarker differences across well-characterized groups rather than a formal hypothesis-testing study.

Specimen handling and laboratory procedures

Archived serum samples stored at −80 °c were retrieved for analysis of TB biomarkers that was performed at the Makerere University Immunology Laboratory. CRP, Leptin and β2- microglobulin concentrations were measured by capture ELISA method, while IP-10 concentrations were determined using the Luminex bio-plex proᵀᴹ assay (Bio Rad laboratories, Inc., USA), all performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Specifically, leptin and β2- microglobulin were analysed using Abcam®, USA, while CRP was analysed using Abnova, USA. For the ELISAs, serum samples were diluted 1: 100 and run in duplicate. Mean optical density values for each set of duplicates were used to construct standard curves, and concentrations were derived using GraphPad software. For IP-10 concentration determination using the Luminex platform, serum samples were diluted 1:4. Mean fluorescence intensity values were converted to concentrations using the manufacturer's standards. Median concentrations and optical density values for the biomarkers under study were recorded for analysis. Assay performance met manufacturer specified quality control criteria per manufacturer documentation, with intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CV) within acceptable ranges. Samples with inter-duplicate CVs >15% were repeated. Archived specimens had undergone no more than two freeze-thaw cycles prior to analysis.

Statistical analysis

The primary study outcome was the level of the serum biomarkers including CRP, β2-microglobulin, leptin and IP-10 across the three study groups; active PTB, symptomatic (presumptive TB) TB-negative, and asymptomatic PLHIV controls. For descriptive analysis, continuous variables were summarized using medians and inter-quartile ranges (IQR) while categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Mann Whitney U statistical test was used to compare the differences in serum biomarkers levels between participants with active PTB and without PTB. Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between the serum biomarker levels and active PTB, with active PTB status as the dependent variable, while CRP, IP-10, leptin, and β2-microglobulin levels as independent variables. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated from the logistic regression analysis. Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used to assess correlations between the levels of serum biomarkers and TB-related clinical characteristics. All statistical tests were two-sided, and the level of significance was set at p < 0.05. Potential sources of bias were minimised through simple random sampling of samples from the parent study. Measurement bias was reduced by blinding laboratory staff to group allocation during biomarker quantification. There were no missing biomarker values in the laboratory dataset. Participants with missing clinical variables were excluded from those specific correlation analyses only. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA® version 18.0 (StataCorp, 4905 Lakeway Drive College Station, Texas USA) and the GraphPad Prism program version 10 (GraphPad software, USA).

Ethics statement

The study received approval from the institutional review board (IRB) of the Joint Clinical Research Centre, Mulago Hospital research and Ethics committee (MREC 133, April 2016), and the Uganda National Council for science and technology (UNCST HS 2045, May 2016). Participants enrolled in the parent study consented to participation, sample storage, and future use of their samples for research. A waiver of consent was obtained from the IRB to perform tests on the archived samples.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated serum biomarkers including CRP, leptin, β2-microglobulin, and IP-10 for PTB and examined their relationship with TB-related clinical characteristics among hospitalized adult PLHIV in Uganda. We found that CRP, IP-10, and β2-microglobulin were significantly elevated, while leptin levels were reduced in PLHIV with active PTB compared to TB-negative individuals. Among the serum biomarkers evaluated, serum IP-10 and β2-microglobulin levels were the strongest independent predictors of active PTB. Although biomarker levels differed significantly between the active PTB and TB-negative PLHIV groups, no significant correlations were found between biomarkers and TB-related clinical characteristics, including cough, fever, oxygen saturation, CD4 cell count, or body weight. The findings could imply that, although CRP, β2-microglobulin, and IP-10 could have a role in the identification of HIV-associated PTB, their role in predicting TB disease presentation maybe limited. However, these are exploratory findings from a small cohort and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating data.

Our study findings align with earlier reports on TB biomarkers which have identified IP-10, CRP, and β2-microglobulin as promising biomarkers for active TB [4], [8], [9]. The findings support the concept that inflammatory and immune activation markers, as indicators of heightened immune activation and systemic inflammation, remain elevated in HIV/TB coinfection despite the immunosuppression associated with HIV-infection [10], [11].

Our study found IP-10, an interferon-γ–inducible chemokine, was not only significantly elevated but was the strongest independent predictor of active PTB among hospitalized PLHIV. Earlier biomarker evaluation studies have similarly shown significant IP-10 elevation in active TB [4], [8], [9] and having a much better performance than other conventional inflammatory markers among PLHIV [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]. A metanalysis by Qiu X et al. (2019) [15] identified IP-10 as a promising marker for differentiating PTB from non-TB, providing evidence for the clinical utility of IP-10 for PTB. An earlier study conducted in Italy by Vanini V et al. (2012) [17] has also described IP-10 as an additional marker for TB detection in PLHIV. However, unlike in our study, both of these earlier reports have limited focus on hospitalized PLHIV, and moreover Vanini's study was conducted in a low-TB endemic setting. Our study extends these findings to the specific context of hospitalized, severely immunosuppressed PLHIV in a high TB/HIV burden sub-Saharan setting. In our study, IP-10 levels were also moderately elevated in symptomatic TB-negative PLHIV, although to a lesser extent than in the active PTB group, reflecting its known non-specificity. Elevated IP-10 may indicate the presence of other inflammatory disease conditions which occur commonly in PLHIV [15], [17], which would limit its standalone specificity. However, the consistently elevated IP-10 values across active PTB cases highlight its promise as a host biomarker for TB screening. Several earlier studies have demonstrated IP-10 utility in both adult and paediatric TB diagnosis, and its ability to detect early or paucibacillary TB disease, making it particularly appealing in immunocompromised [15], [17], [18], [19].

Regarding CRP, multiple studies from high-burden TB countries have consistently reported high CRP levels in patients with TB and HIV co-infection, which has led to its wide use as a triage test in TB screening [20], [21], [22], [23]. Consistent with these earlier observations, our study found elevated CRP levels in active PTB compared to the asymptomatic PLHIV controls. However, our study also found elevated CRP levels among the symptomatic TB-negative patients, with a non-significant difference between the TB-negative group and the active PTB, underscoring CRP's known non-specificity as a biomarker. This observation limits the use of CRP as a specific biomarker for active TB as it is elevated across diverse infectious and inflammatory opportunistic conditions common in PLHIV [24], [25].

Our study found higher β2-microglobulin levels in the active PTB compared to TB-negative PLHIV individuals, and β2-microglobulin was a strong independent predictor of active PTB among hospitalized PLHIV. The findings are consistent with prior studies identifying β2-microglobulin as a marker of systemic immune activation [26], [27], associated with active TB [27], [28], [29]. Similar findings have been reported among PLHIV. For instance, Wanchu et al. 2001 [30] demonstrated higher concentrations of β2-Microglobulin among PLHIV with concomitant active TB than those without any opportunistic infection and healthy controls. However, it is noted that β2-Microglobulin is inherently elevated in HIV-infection per se, which may limit its TB-specific utility in this population.

Consistent with previous reports [31], [32], [33], we observed reduced leptin levels among participants with active PTB. TB disease has been shown to reduce leptin levels due to disease associated nutritional depletion, systemic inflammation, poor nutrition, wasting, and metabolic dysregulation normally seen in chronic disease [33], [34]. Also similar to prior studies [31], [32], we observed lack of correlation between leptin concentration and body weight in both active TB and TB-negative groups, which suggests additional mechanisms such as cytokine-mediated suppression of leptin production, which may contribute to associated leptin dysregulation. Given the weak and non significant associations observed, and the inability to control for nutritional status and BMI in this study, our findings suggest that leptin may only have limited or no standalone utility as a biomarker for TB in PLHIV.

We further demonstrate the absence of significant correlation between serum biomarker concentrations and TB-related clinical features among hospitalized active PTB PLHIV. Neither IP-10, CRP, β2-microglobulin, nor leptin correlated significantly with fever, cough, oxygen saturation, CD4 count, or weight loss. The only moderate relationship observed was a negative correlation between IP-10 and fever which was counterintuitive and may reflect heterogeneity in host response rather than a true biological association. Earlier studies have described moderate associations between CRP with fever and weight loss [35], [36] and between IP-10 and TB disease presentation [37], [38]. The absence of such correlations in our cohort may reflect the profound immune dysregulation in HIV-infection, which blunts typical inflammatory responses and alters the relationship between biomarkers and clinical symptoms [39]. These findings support the understanding that clinical manifestations of TB in advanced HIV disease are highly variable and often atypical. Advanced immunosuppression due to HIV may dampen classic TB symptoms and modify inflammatory pathways, leading to a disconnect between the clinical presentation and systemic biomarker levels. In addition, the observed absence of correlation between the biomarkers and symptoms in our study unlike earlier reports could be explained by differences in study populations; our cohort consisted primarily of hospitalized PLHIV with potentially advanced disease, in whom clinical presentation and host inflammatory response are often atypical. The weak or lack of correlations observed in our study highlights the limitations of relying on serum cytokine biomarkers to predict TB-related clinical features. The lack of correlation between the studied biomarkers and CD4 count also suggests that, although elevated in active PTB, these markers do not reliably reflect the degree of immunosuppression.

This study has notable strengths. First, we evaluated multiple serum biomarkers spanning inflammatory and immune activation pathways in three well-characterized patient groups representing a range of TB and HIV disease. Secondly, inclusion of both symptomatic TB-negative and asymptomatic HIV-infected controls strengthens the comparison and enhances interpretability of biomarker differences. Lastly, the unique focus on hospitalized PLHIV in a high TB/HIV burden African setting extends the biomarkers research findings to a real-world clinical context.

On the other hand, we acknowledge the important limitations. The relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect modest correlations and subgroup effects, and therefore our results should be considered exploratory. Use of archived specimens, although stored at favourable conditions at —80C, could have affected levels of the studied biomarkers to a limited extent. Additionally, clinical data, particularly radiological findings which may have provided additional insights into disease severity, were not incorporated into the correlation analysis. Viral load data, latent TB status, and ART duration were not available, representing important unmeasured confounders. Unmeasured co-infections common in advanced HIV, such as bacterial, cryptococcal and other fungal infections, may have influenced biomarker concentrations, particularly among symptomatic TB-negative participants thereby introducing potential residual confounding. Finally, the absence of formal diagnostic performance analysis with ROC/AUC in this study means that clinical conclusions about optimal cutoffs, sensitivity and specificity must await future, adequately powered evaluations.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that IP-10, β2-microglobulin, and CRP are significantly elevated in PLHIV with active PTB, while leptin levels are reduced. Although, these serum biomarkers distinguished active PTB from TB-negative presumptive TB and asymptomatic PLHIV controls, they did not show significant correlation with TB-related clinical features. These exploratory findings suggest that biomarkers particularly IP-10 and β2-microglobulin, are promising candidates for PTB in hospitalized PLHIV, warranting rigorous evaluation in future research. Further research should formally evaluate the diagnostic performance of serum biomarkers in larger hospitalized HIV-positive cohorts, explore combined biomarker models, and assess their utility in point-of-care diagnostic platforms to support TB screening and timely treatment initiation in this vulnerable population.
