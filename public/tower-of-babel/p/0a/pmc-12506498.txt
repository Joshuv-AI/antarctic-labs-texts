Retrospective study: analysis of risk factors and construction of prediction model for pancreatic steatosis
Authors: Wanli Zan, Jingxin Yang, Yi Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic steatosis, closely related to various metabolic diseases has no unified standard for the diagnosis. The purpose of this study was to construct and validate a risk prediction model of pancreatic steatosis.Methods854 cases admitted to Xi’an Mei Zhao physical examination center were divided into modeling set (n = 598) and validation set (n = 256) according to the ratio of 7:3, and the risk prediction model was constructed and validated based on the risk factors.ResultsOf 598 cases in modeling set, 232 were in the pancreatic steatosis group and 366 were in the non pancreatic steatosis group. There were significantly statistical differences in Age, Gender, Body Mass Index (BMI), Glucose (GLU), Total Cholesterol (TC), Triglycerides (TG), High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL), Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL), Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST), Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT), Ratio of Aspartate Aminotransferase to Alanine Aminotransferase (AST/ALT), Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD), Pancreas computed tomography (CT) value, Spleen CT value, Liver CT value as well as Pancreas/Spleen CT value (P < 0.05) between pancreatic steatosis cases and non pancreatic steatosis cases. Cox regression showed that Age (97.5% CI 0.981–1.030), BMI (97.5% CI 1.198–1.436), GLU (97.5% CI 0.921–1.521), TC (97.5% CI 0.800–2.130), HDL (97.5% CI 0.074–0.649), LDL (97.5% CI 0.603–1.877), AST/ALT (97.5% CI 0.241–0.917), Pancreas CT value (97.5% CI 0.788–0.904), Liver CT value (97.5% CI 0.962–1.015), and Pancreas/Spleen CT value (97.5% CI 0.001–0.649) were independent risk factors for pancreatic steatosis (P < 0.05). Based on above variables, a pancreatic steatosis model was successfully constructed, the validation results showed the model had good credibility and predictive value.ConclusionAge, BMI, GLU, TC, HDL, LDL, AST/ALT, Pancreas CT value, Liver CT value, Pancreas/Spleen CT value are risk factors for pancreatic steatosis, and this model can identify the patients at high risk of pancreatic steatosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04306-y.Keywords: Pancreatic steatosis, Prediction model, LDL, NAFLD, Computed tomography (CT)

Introduction

At present, there is no unified disease name for pancreatic steatosis, which is often described as “fatty infiltration of the pancreas”, “non-alcoholic fatty pancreas”, “pancreatic lipomatosis”, etc [1–3]. The incidence of pancreatic steatosis in the general population is estimated to be between 16% and 35% [4, 5]. The occurrence of pancreatic steatosis is related to various factors, mainly including: obesity, metabolic syndrome, alcohol consumption, non alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) as well as other factors such as sex hormone levels, low birth weight, changes in intestinal microbiota and high-fat diet [6–8]. Moreover, the development of pancreatic steatosis can affect the function of pancreatic beta cells which can lead to abnormal insulin secretion [9], or trigger the onset of acute pancreatitis which can cause necrosis of pancreatic acinar cells through a series of mechanisms, and finally lead to diabetes, pancreatitis and even pancreatic cancer [10, 11].

The diagnosis of pancreatic steatosis has its current existing problems, which there is no clear consensus or guideline [12]. Unlike hepatic steatosis, the diagnosis of pancreatic steatosis does not have a unified standard and relies on the experience of imaging doctors for judgment through imaging examinations such as ultrasound scanning, multi-echo Dixon MRI and CT [11, 13, 14]. For example, pancreatic steatosis is related to higher visceral fat tissue area (VFA), a cut-off value of VFA ≥ 107.2 cm2 may predict pancreatic steatosis [15]. Numerous previous studies have confirmed that CT values of the pancreas and spleen can serve as surrogate markers for pancreatic fat deposition [16, 17]. However, to date, no studies have reported the pancreas/spleen CT ratio as a marker for pancreatic fat deposition.

In addition to imaging examinations, laboratory tests, including the detection of related indicators such as glucose (GLU), triglycerides (TG), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), can also help to evaluate the functional status of the pancreas [18]. The accumulation of fat outside subcutaneous adipose tissue often has adverse effects on systemic metabolism. Preliminary studies have shown that pancreatic fat is associated with metabolic syndrome, impaired glucose metabolism, and reduced insulin secretion. Therefore, when referring to diseases related to fat and pancreas, NAFLD often accompanies them because they have similar pathological basis and pathogenesis [14]. On the one hand, elevated serum triglyceride levels lead to their ectopic deposition in organs such as the liver or pancreas, causing related diseases [19]. On the other hand, inflammation and lipid disorders are key factors leading to the release of free fatty acids, ectopic fat deposition, and pancreatic steatosis [20]. Meanwhile, inflammation mediated by adipocyte cytokines can cause oxidative stress in the pancreas, leading to dysfunction and apoptosis of beta cells and acinar cells, ultimately resulting in endocrine and exocrine dysfunction of the pancreas [21]. However, to date, it remains unclear whether these biological indicators are involved in the early warning of pancreatic steatosis.

At present, there is a lack of relevant research on pancreatic steatosis in the world. In order to prevent the occurrence of pancreatic steatosis, current study aims to analyze independent risk factors, builds a prediction model of pancreatic steatosis, guides early diagnosis, improves diagnosis and treatment programs, and ultimately reduces the incidence of pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer [22, 23].

Materials and methods

Study population

871 cases were collected from Xi’an Mei Zhao physical examination center, with the screening criteria including (1) 18–60 years old, (2) complete clinical data. The screening process for current study was showed in Fig. 1. After excluded the cases with incomplete information or not meeting inclusion, 854 cases divided into modeling set (n = 598) and validation set (n = 256) according to the ratio of 7:3. Among modeling set, 318 cases were patients with pancreatic steatosis, while 536 of them were patients without pancreatic steatosis. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shaanxi Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine (No. AF/SQ-02/01.2).

Definition of pancreatic steatosis

The diagnosis of pancreatic steatosis was as followed: the density of pancreatic tissue parenchyma was slightly higher, while the density of the spleen at the same level is smooth at all edges, or slightly higher than the density level of the kidney at that level. After the pancreas was infiltrated by fat, the tissue shrinks, the shape becomes listless, and partial patchy or low-density shadows could be seen inside, accompanied by changes in tissue edge morphology and decreased solid density. Due to the increase of adipose tissue in pancreatic glandular cells, the density of pancreatic parenchyma decreased, which was lower than that of the spleen at the same level and comparable to that of the kidneys. In the current study, CT attenuation values were measured in different pancreatic regions (head/body/tail), compared them with the mean splenic attenuation, and used a P/S ratio < 0.7 as the diagnostic criterion for pancreatic steatosis.

Definition of NAFLD

The diagnosis and classification of NAFLD were as followed: anyone who meets any of the following criteria ①-⑤, or meets the criteria either ⑥ or ⑦, could be diagnosed with NAFLD: ① Risk factors: obesity, type 2 diabetes, hyperlipidemia, etc.; ② No history of alcohol consumption or alcohol consumption equivalent to less than 140 g per week for males and 70 g per week for females; ③ excluding specific diseases that can cause fatty liver, such as viral hepatitis, drug-induced liver disease, total parenteral nutrition, Wilson’s disease, and autoimmune liver disease; ④ in addition to the clinical manifestations of the primary disease, there may be symptoms and signs such as fatigue, dull pain in the liver area, and enlarged liver and spleen; ⑤ increased levels of serum transaminase or γ-glutamyl transpeptidase, transferrin; ⑥ Meet the imaging diagnostic criteria for fatty liver disease; ⑦The histological changes observed in the liver biopsy are consistent with the pathological diagnostic criteria for fatty liver disease.

Data collection

① General data: Age and Gender; ② Laboratory indicators including BMI, GLU, TC, TG, HDL, LDL, AST, ALT and AST/ALT were collected from a biochemical instrument (BECKMAN COULTER AU680); ③Imaging data: Pancreas CT value, Spleen CT value and Liver CT value were collected using Brilliance CT (PHILIPS).

Statistical analysis

The IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 software was used to complete data analysis. Measurement data were analyzed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data that conforms to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), and independent sample T-test was used to analyze the differences between the two groups; Data that do not conform to a normal distribution were represented by the median (interquartile range) [M (Q1, Q3)], and non parametric Mann Whitney U was used for inter group comparison. The count data of two groups of patients were expressed as the number of cases (percentage) [n (%)], and the Chi Squared Test (χ2) test is used to analyze the differences between the two groups. Risk factors were identified via Lasso regression, and a prediction model was constructed based on Cox regression. The predictive performance of the model was compared using the C-index. In the Cox model, hazard ratios (HRs) were computed via backward stepwise regression; these served as weights to derive the risk score for prognostic prediction. The importance of each predictor in the RSF model was evaluated using the variable importance (VIMP) method. The C-index and calibration curve were employed to assess the model’s discriminative ability and consistency, respectively. The R version 4.2 software was used for the draw of nomogram, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and calibration curve. P < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference.

Study population

As showed in Tables 1 and 598 cases were included in modeling set: 232 were cases with pancreatic steatosis and 366 were case without. Cases without pancreatic steatosis were younger (P < 0.001), more often female (P < 0.001), had a lower BMI (P < 0.001), had lower levels in GLU (P < 0.001), TC (P < 0.001), TG (P < 0.001), HDL (P < 0.001), LDL (P < 0.001), AST (P < 0.001) and ALT (P < 0.001), had a higher AST/ALT value (P < 0.001), had a higher Pancreas CT value (P < 0.001), had a lower Spleen CT value (P < 0.001), had a higher Pancreas/Spleen CT value (P < 0.001) compared to the cases with pancreatic steatosis. Moreover, cases with pancreatic steatosis have a higher proportion accompanied with NAFLD (P < 0.001).

Prediction model built based on lassoCox regression

The training set was utilized to develop the model, and the validation set was employed to test this developed model. Lasso regression was adopted to filter parameters, and the variation patterns of these variables’ coefficients are illustrated in Fig. 2A. The variable assignment were showed in Table 2. A 10-fold cross-validation approach was utilized for iterative analyses, yielding an optimal-performing model with the fewest variables when λ reached 0.009 (Log λ=−4.724) (Fig. 2B). The selected variables comprised Age, BMI, GLU, TC, HDL, LDL, AST/ALT, Pancreas CT value, Liver CT value, Pancreas/Spleen CT value. Based on these Lasso-screened parameters, a Cox regression model was further constructed (Table 3).

Nomogram as a tool for visualization

Based on the multivariate Lasso regression analysis, to enhance clinical utility, the model was visualized by drawing the nomogram using the R language software, as showed in Fig. 3. The application process involves summing the scores of all variables included in the model. Then, draw a vertical line at the total score to intersect with the lines representing predicted pancreatic steatosis. The values corresponding to these intersection points indicate the individual’s predicted pancreatic steatosis rate. For instance, consider a 37-year-old male with NAFLD, with a BMI of 35.3, a GLU level of 5.48 mmol/L, a TC level of 4.17, an HDL level of 0.92 mmol/L, an LDL level of 2.644 mmol/L, an AST/ALT ratio of 0.44, a pancreatic CT value of 30.9, a hepatic CT value of 44.8, and a pancreas/spleen CT value of 0.690. His total score is 87.1151, with a risk of 0.99. This underscores that the nomogram is more clinically user-friendly compared to cumbersome mathematical formulas.

Validation of the predictive efficiency of prediction model

As shown in Fig. 4A, The ROC curve results of the modeling set show that the area under curve (AUC) is 0.892 (95%CI 0.866 ~ 0.918), indicating a good discriminative ability with current model. The calibration curve results show that the mean absolute error (MAE) is 0.034, and the calibration curve is close to the ideal curve, indicating that current model had good calibration performance and was relatively reliable and stable Fig. 4B. As shown in Fig. 4C, the AUC of the validation set is 0.888 (95%CI 0.847 ~ 0.929).

Discussion

Pancreatic steatosis is a common disease of the pancreas. Surprisingly, its prevalence is higher than the sum of type 2 diabetes (T2DM, the most common pancreatic endocrine disease) and acute pancreatitis (the most common pancreatic exocrine disease) [7]. Its pathogenesis may be related to multiple factors such as obesity, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, leptin resistance, and genetics [24]. The occurrence of pancreatic steatosis is closely related to factors such as age, metabolic syndrome, and fatty liver [12]. However, it has not been given sufficient attention in clinical practice due to the lack of criteria about its diagnosis.

Current LD, pancreatic CT value, and pancreatic/spleen CT value were the main factors causing pancreatic steatosis in cases. Here, current study manifested that cases with pancreatic steatosis showed higher age and BMI, higher serum LDL level, lower pancreatic CT value as well as pancreatic/spleen CT value, and were more common in male populations and NAFLD patients. Therefore, early diagnosis of pancreatic steatosis is particularly important as it may alter the clinical outcome of patients, but extensive prospective studies are still needed to observe the long-term effects and prognosis of pancreatic steatosis.

As early as 1978, it was reported that pancreatic fat content increased with age in both males and females, and the scoring system for increased pancreatic fat was first proposed [1]. In a study targeting males aged 20–70, it was found that the content of fatty in pancreas began to increase between the ages of 50–70, with a fat content approximately twice as high as that of males aged 20–50 [25]. Similarly, the pancreatic fat content of postmenopausal women begins to increase and is significantly higher than that of premenopausal women of the same age [26]. In addition to epidemiological data support, age can also be explained as a risk factor for pancreatic steatosis from a physiological mechanism perspective. Naturally aging cells are associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid accumulation, which promote inflammation, tissue decline, lipid metabolism disorders, cancer, etc [27]. Insulin resistance also occurs at this time, manifested as reduced fat production, increased insulin resistance, elevated inflammatory levels, and other metabolic disorders [28]. However, this explanation is also a consistent reason for the occurrence of age-related metabolic diseases [29]. It is worth noting that with the prevalence of obesity, the incidence rate of pancreatic steatosis in children has increased, and the prevalence of pancreatic steatosis in children deserves attention because of the lack of data [30]. In terms of gender, there are different opinions on whether gender is an independent risk factor in domestic and foreign research [31, 32], which may be due to differences in regional dietary habits and lifestyles. At the same time, differences in hormone levels should also be considered, as male and female hormones affect tissue immunity, fat regulation, etc [33, 34]. However, it was certain that decreased testosterone concentration in men and endogenous hormone in women also related to insulin resistance [35], which may ultimately leading to further deposition of fat in organs such as the pancreas. In the case of this study, perhaps due to the small sample size and single source, this result is inconsistent with other domestic studies [36].

In addition, among patients with BMI overweight, the probability of developing pancreatic steatosis is 71.9%, which is an important independent risk factor for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [17]. Therefore, for people who already have NAFLD or BMI overweight, controlling weight and delaying the progression of NAFLD are important methods to prevent the occurrence of pancreatic steatosis, indicating that the results of current prediction model was consistent with previous study [14].

Establishing a risk prediction model through blood and imaging indicators is reliable. The information from two different sources more comprehensively reflects the patient’s condition and prognostic risks, providing strong support for clinical decision-making [37]. Previous studies on predicting pancreatic steatosis have been based on parameters such as CT values [15, 38]. In contrast, incorporating both CT and biological parameters makes the model more accurate. Therefore, in addition to general information, current study also collected blood and imaging indicators. Serum LDL can enter pancreatic cells, causing intracellular lipid accumulation that not only affects the exocrine function of the pancreas (such as the secretion of digestive enzymes), but also affect the endocrine function of the pancreas (such as insulin secretion) [39]. Meanwhile, LDL can also indirectly participate in the occurrence of pancreatic steatosis. For example, elevated LDL is often associated with metabolic diseases such as obesity and hyperlipidemia [40], which themselves increase the risk of pancreatic steatosis. CT scan diagnosis of pancreatic steatosis is achieved by comparing pancreatic tissue with the spleen, which shows extensive or focal low-density signals. The changes in liver/spleen CT value are commonly used for the diagnosis of NAFLD [41]. Based on this, current study proposed for the first time the pancreas/spleen CT value for the determination of pancreatic steatosis. The results showed that the pancreas/spleen CT value is one of important independent risk factors that can be used for predicting pancreatic steatosis. At the same time, current study synthesized multiple influencing factors and constructed a nomogram prediction model to present the risk of patients developing pancreatic steatosis in the form of a column chart, resulting in more intuitive results and higher predictive value. Moreover, we have further demonstrated the high predictive value of our model by drawing calibration curves, decision curves, and validation sets, providing a reference for predicting pancreatic steatosis.

It is worth noting that this study did not adopt a multicenter design, which may be a Limitation. Variables such as country, geographical region, dietary habits, genetic factors, and ethnic differences could potentially influence the results. For example, in studies conducted on patients from other countries with a higher average age, the rate of pancreatic steatosis is up to 70% [42]. Multicenter studies typically enhance the external validity of findings by including diverse populations and settings, and the absence of such a design here may restrict the generalizability of the conclusions. These limitations underscore the need for future studies with age-diverse samples, multicenter designs, and broader ethnic/racial representation to further validate and extend our results.

Conclusion

Age, BMI, GLU, TC, HDL, LDL, AST/ALT, pancreatic CT value, hepatic CT value, and pancreatic/spleen CT value are identified as risk factors for pancreatic steatosis, and the established model is capable of identifying patients at high risk of this condition, providing a theoretical and practical basis for early screening, targeted prevention, and personalized intervention of pancreatic steatosis.
