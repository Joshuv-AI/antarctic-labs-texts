Hidradenitis suppurativa is associated with inflammatory arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and other autoimmune diseases: A retrospective population-level study
Authors: Alexa Kassels, Sarah Park, Jennifer L. Hsiao, Christina N. Kraus
Published: 2024

To the Editor: Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, inflammatory, recurrent skin disease that is characterized by painful nodules, sinus tracts, and scarring typically in intertriginous regions.1 HS has been associated with Crohn’s disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), ankylosing spondylitis (AS), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), and other autoimmune conditions.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Herein, we performed a population-level retrospective study using large-scale data from health care organizations throughout the United States to further evaluate the association between HS and autoimmune conditions including inflammatory arthritides and connective tissue diseases.

We retrospectively generated a patient cohort from the US Collaborative Network on TriNetX, a global health research network with over 113 million patients from 64 health care organizations. Our study included data between January 2004 and 2024. International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10) code L73.2 was used to identify HS patients and L70.0 to identify acne vulgaris patients as our control cohort. Patients with HS were excluded from the control cohort. General Equivalence Mappings were used to map diagnoses prior to ICD-10 codes. Cohorts were matched by age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking status/history, and body mass index, resulting in a 1:1 propensity score. We utilized ICD-10 codes to identify the diagnosis of other disease conditions following a diagnosis of HS and generated odds ratios with 95% CIs.

A total of 184,722 HS patients were matched with the control cohort (Table I). Patients with HS had an increased odds of CD, UC, enteropathic arthropathies, juvenile arthritis, PsA, AS, RA, inflammatory polyarthropathy, type 1 diabetes mellitus, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and psoriasis. While prior studies have linked HS to inflammatory arthritis,3 our study adds to the literature by supporting the association of HS and PsA, AS, and RA and also highlighting an association with juvenile arthritis that has previously been underreported. Our study found an association with SLE, consistent with a previous study using a claims database1; however, interestingly, our study additionally found decreased odds for both subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus and discoid lupus erythematosus. Our findings also align with prior studies associating HS with CD and UC.2

It is important for dermatologists to be aware of the association between HS and inflammatory arthritis and involve rheumatology early if patients have inflammatory joint symptoms, including in pediatric patients. Dermatologists should also consider screening HS patients for psoriasis with the increased association of HS and psoriasis, especially given systemic therapies utilized for both conditions may have overlapping therapeutic effects. Finally, clinicians should also have a low threshold to screen HS patients for SLE, even if there are no cutaneous signs. Limitations of this study include a US-only cohort, retrospective design, possible misclassification due to use of ICD-10 diagnoses, potential residual confounding bias, and inability to stratify HS by disease severity.

Overall, HS patients had an increased odds of developing several autoimmune conditions. Future studies are needed to better understand the pathogenesis behind these associations.
