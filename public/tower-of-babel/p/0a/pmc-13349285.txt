Idiopathic Limb Myokymia in Children: First Reported Cases Involving the Extensor Digitorum Brevis and Flexor Hallucis Brevis Muscles
Authors: Sedat Işikay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Idiopathic limb myokymia is an uncommon presentation of peripheral nerve hyperexcitability, characterized by spontaneous, continuous, grouped motor unit discharges in the absence of an identifiable structural, metabolic, or autoimmune cause. Although peripheral nerve hyperexcitability is frequently associated with voltage-gated potassium channel complex antibodies, antibody-negative cases are well recognized and are thought to reflect functional membrane instability or ion channel dysfunction at the level of the distal motor axon. Pediatric presentations are rare, particularly when limited to intrinsic foot muscles. We report two cases of pediatric patients with isolated distal lower limb myokymia. A 13-year-old girl presented with persistent involuntary toe movements. Needle electromyography (EMG) demonstrated typical myokymic discharges, consisting of rhythmic or semi-rhythmic, grouped motor unit potentials firing at 2–60 Hz in the extensor digitorum brevis muscle. A 15-year-old boy exhibited continuous contractions of the right foot, localized to the flexor hallucis brevis muscle, with similar myokymic discharges confirmed on EMG. Comprehensive evaluation, including laboratory and neuroimaging studies, excluded secondary etiologies in both cases, supporting a diagnosis of exclusion. Both patients were treated with carbamazepine, a sodium channel blocker aimed at reducing axonal hyperexcitability, resulting in complete clinical resolution within 1 month, with no recurrence during follow-up. These cases emphasize that focal intrinsic foot muscle myokymia can occur in children without detectable autoimmune markers and likely reflects localized motor axon hyperexcitability. Recognition of characteristic EMG features is critical to differentiate myokymia from fasciculations, neuromyotonia, or other movement disorders and to guide appropriate therapy.

Introduction

Myokymia is a rare peripheral nerve hyperexcitability disorder characterized by continuous, involuntary, and wave-like muscle contractions. These contractions typically manifest as mild muscle rippling or tremors and generally do not lead to significant muscle weakness or atrophy. The pathophysiology of myokymia is associated with spontaneous discharges of peripheral motor nerve fibers and may be linked to autoimmune, demyelinating, paraneoplastic, infectious, or toxic conditions.[12] When no underlying cause is identified, the condition is classified as idiopathic myokymia. Idiopathic limb myokymia is particularly rare and is usually localized to the distal extremity muscles, especially those of the hands or feet. Clinically, patients present with intermittent or continuous involuntary movements, which may sometimes be accompanied by discomfort, cramps, or mild pain. Electromyography (EMG) is the gold standard for diagnosis, typically revealing fasciculations and myokymic discharges, while nerve conduction studies and imaging are usually normal.[23]

Idiopathic limb myokymia in pediatric patients is extremely rare.[34] Involvement of distal lower extremity muscles, particularly small muscles such as the extensor digitorum brevis (EDB) or flexor hallucis brevis (FHB) muscles, has not been reported in the literature. This article presents two pediatric cases of idiopathic limb myokymia with myokymic activity in the EDB and FHB muscles. The clinical characteristics, electrophysiological findings, and response to carbamazepine treatment in these cases contribute to the literature and expand the spectrum of pediatric idiopathic limb myokymia.

Case report 1

A 13-year-old female patient presented with complaints of continuous involuntary movements in the toes of both feet. These movements were intermittent throughout the day, decreasing and ceasing during sleep at night. The patient reported pain and discomfort in her feet due to these movements. The complaints had been present for 1 year, with a progressive increase in severity over the past 2–3 months. Physical examination was normal. Intermittent involuntary movements were observed in the toes of both feet [Video 1, Video 2, Section 1]. Nerve conduction studies were normal. Needle EMG of the EDB muscle revealed myokymic discharges [Video 2]. Routine blood tests (hemogram, liver, renal, glycemic, lipid, and thyroid profiles), infectious workup (human immunodeficiency virus [HIV] 1, 2, hepatitis B and C), and immune workup (erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, antinuclear antibody, and profile) were normal. Antibodies for N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor, anti-contactin-associated protein-like 2 (CASPR2) antibody, anti-α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid 1 (AMPA1), AMPA2, voltage-gated potassium channels (VGKC), anti-leucine-rich glioma inactivated-1 (LGI1), anti-γ-aminobutyric acid-B receptor, and anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase were negative. Brain and spinal magnetic resonance imaging were normal. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with idiopathic limb myokymia. Carbamazepine treatment (10 mg/kg/day) was initiated. The patient’s clinical symptoms subsided within 1 month. No recurrence or complications were observed during 1 year of follow-up.

Case report 2

A 15-year-old male patient presented with complaints of involuntary movements in the sole of his right foot. The patient had experienced these symptoms for approximately 6 months. Initially, the contractions occurred 1 to 2 days a week for 1–2 h, but over the past month, they had become daily and persisted for hours. Physical examination revealed myokymic contractions localized to the FHB muscle in the sole of the right foot [Video 3]. EMG showed normal nerve conduction studies, but needle EMG of the affected muscle revealed myokymic discharges. The same investigations performed for Case 1 were conducted, and no etiological cause was identified. The patient was diagnosed with idiopathic limb myokymia. Carbamazepine treatment was initiated at a dose of 10 mg/kg/day. The patient’s symptoms resolved within 1 month. No recurrence was observed during follow-up.

Dıscussıon

Myokymia is a motor hyperexcitability disorder resulting from spontaneous discharges of peripheral nerves. It is most commonly observed following post-infectious, demyelinating, or autoimmune processes; however, in some cases, no underlying cause can be identified, leading to a diagnosis of idiopathic myokymia. Idiopathic myokymia typically presents with localized, wave-like, small muscle contractions, particularly in the distal extremities, especially in the lower limb muscles.[1234] In both cases presented, the presence of localized, continuous involuntary movements, their reduction during sleep, and the detection of myokymic discharges on EMG supported the diagnosis.

Normal nerve conduction studies, a negative antibody panel, and the absence of systemic findings ruled out secondary causes. Idiopathic limb myokymia remains a diagnosis of exclusion, and careful evaluation is required to rule out metabolic, toxic, structural, and immune-mediated causes, such as Guillain–Barré syndrome, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, or paraneoplastic syndromes.[56] Neuroimaging and laboratory investigations in our patients did not demonstrate evidence of central or peripheral lesions, metabolic disturbances, or autoimmune disease, supporting an idiopathic etiology.

In the literature, idiopathic myokymia is generally described as a benign condition with a stable course over time and a good response to treatment. Idiopathic limb myokymia is a rare condition.[23] Most reported cases involve facial muscles or proximal limb muscles, such as the gastrocnemius, soleus, and tibialis anterior, whereas intrinsic foot muscle involvement is extremely uncommon. Our cases involving the EDB and FHB muscles are the first documented pediatric presentations of this type.[78]

Pathophysiologically, myokymia results from peripheral motor axon hyperexcitability due to voltage-gated ion channel dysfunction, particularly potassium channels. While some cases are associated with VGKC complex antibodies, including CASPR2 and LGI1, many idiopathic cases are antibody-negative, reflecting localized axonal membrane instability or subclinical channel dysfunction.[5910] Segmental demyelination and ephaptic transmission may further facilitate spontaneous repetitive discharges.

Electrophysiologically, myokymic discharges on needle EMG are characterized by rhythmic or semi-rhythmic, grouped motor unit potentials firing at 2–60 Hz, often described as “marching soldiers.” Differentiation from fasciculations (isolated, irregular discharges), neuromyotonia (high-frequency, 150–300 Hz discharges), and cramp potentials is critical for accurate diagnosis.[139] The consistent presence of grouped repetitive discharges in both our cases confirmed the diagnosis.

Myokymia typically does not require treatment; however, treatment is indicated for prolonged symptoms, significant discomfort, or functional impairment. Membrane-stabilizing drugs, such as carbamazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin, and lamotrigine, as well as occasionally benzodiazepines, can reduce repetitive firing by modulating axonal sodium and potassium channels.[1234111213] In our cases, carbamazepine was initiated due to prolonged symptoms and patient distress, resulting in complete clinical improvement. Although there is no standardized treatment duration, gradual tapering after sustained remission with close follow-up is recommended, given the benign natural history of idiopathic limb myokymia.[1112]

Comparison with previously reported focal or limb myokymia series highlights that our patients share a benign course, normal nerve conduction studies, and a favorable response to membrane-stabilizing therapy. However, they differ with respect to age, distal muscle involvement, and the absence of detectable autoantibodies, expanding the clinical spectrum of pediatric idiopathic limb myokymia.[78]

Conclusıons

This case report highlights that idiopathic limb myokymia should be recognized as a rare but benign peripheral nerve hyperexcitability syndrome. A negative antibody profile, normal nerve conduction studies on EMG, and characteristic needle EMG findings supported the diagnosis. Appropriate pharmacological treatment led to significant symptom improvement. Recognition of intrinsic foot muscle involvement in children is important to avoid unnecessary investigations and to guide targeted therapy.
