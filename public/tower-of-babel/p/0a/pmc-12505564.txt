Dens invaginatus as a diagnostic challenge: evaluating large language models against expert endodontic reasoning
Authors: Damla Erkal, Turgut Felek, Oana-Paula Butean, Kürşat Er
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study hypothesized that large language models (LLMs) would underperform compared to expert clinicians in diagnosing and managing complex endodontic anomalies, such as dens invaginatus, when provided with periapical radiographs. Although LLMs have shown promise in dental education and basic diagnostics, their effectiveness in nuanced clinical reasoning has remained unclear.MethodsNineteen anonymized periapical radiographs depicting challenging endodontic conditions were paired with clinical vignettes. Six advanced LLMs and one expert endodontist independently answered six structured clinical questions per case. Each response was scored against a reference key. Accuracy rates were compared using Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests. Chi-square tests were used to evaluate model performance across question types.ResultsThe expert achieved 100% accuracy, while all LLMs performed significantly lower (P < 0.05). Copilot demonstrated the lowest scores across all questions. The most substantial performance drop was observed in anomaly classification tasks, particularly in identifying and categorizing dens invaginatus. No significant performance differences were found among the top-performing LLMs.ConclusionsWhile LLMs showed competence in basic diagnostic tasks, they failed to replicate expert-level decision-making in complex endodontic scenarios. Their current capabilities remain insufficient for unsupervised clinical use. This study is among the first to assess LLMs using real radiographic data in endodontics and highlights the need for further multimodal model development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06987-z.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) systems hold great promise for transforming medical and dental practices by offering innovative approaches to complex clinical challenges and streamlining the decision-making processes faced by healthcare professionals [1]. One of the primary advantages of AI is its capacity to rapidly process and interpret extensive datasets. This feature is especially advantageous in dental radiographic analysis, where AI-based models can detect subtle patterns or abnormalities that may elude human observers [2]. The emergence of advanced large language models (LLMs), including ChatGPT 3.5 and 4 developed by OpenAI, has marked a significant milestone in the evolution and practical deployment of AI Technologies [3].

Dental radiographs frequently reveal complex endodontic conditions such as dens invaginatus (DI), internal resorption, canal obliteration, or broken instruments, each of which poses significant challenges for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning [4]. The accurate identification and management of such anomalies often rely on both advanced imaging interpretation skills and sound clinical reasoning.

Despite the expanding capabilities of LLMs in medical and dental applications, their potential to support or replicate expert-level performance in endodontic diagnosis and treatment planning has not been adequately explored. Current literature focuses predominantly on caries detection and basic radiographic assessments, leaving a significant gap in understanding how LLMs might perform in more intricate clinical situations [5]. This gap is particularly evident in cases involving developmental anomalies such as DI, a condition characterized by enamel and dentin invagination into the pulp space, which often leads to complex morphological presentations and diagnostic challenges on radiographs [6]. Accurate identification and classification of DI typically require advanced imaging interpretation skills and substantial clinical experience, making it an ideal test case for evaluating the reasoning capabilities of LLMs.

Misdiagnosis of DI may lead to delayed or inappropriate treatment, persistent infection, perforation risk during instrumentation, and compromised long-term prognosis. These consequences underscore the importance of accurate anomaly classification beyond detecting periapical pathology [4].

Therefore, this study aims to assess the diagnostic, therapeutic, and prognostic decision-making performance of advanced LLMs when presented with standardized clinical scenarios based on periapical radiographic images. By comparing their accuracy to that of an experienced endodontist, the study seeks to evaluate whether LLMs can serve as reliable tools in supporting expert-level decision-making in complex endodontic cases.

Materials and methods

Study design

This observational and comparative in vitro study was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic, treatment planning, and prognostic capabilities of LLMs in interpreting dental radiographic images. The periapical radiographs used in this study were obtained from DI cases referred to the Department of Endodontics, Akdeniz University, between 2015 and 2025.

Assessment form and question structure

A total of 19 dental periapical radiographs from past clinical cases were used in this study. The images were selected to represent clear endodontic pathologies, including internal resorption, DI, periapical radiolucency, canal obliteration, periapical cysts, and separated files. Each radiograph was accompanied by a case scenario structured to enhance clinical context, including patient age, gender, symptoms, and anamnesis.

A custom-designed questionnaire consisting of case-based diagnostic scenarios was developed to compare the clinical reasoning of LLMs with that of expert endodontists. The questionnaire was tailored to focus on dens invaginatus, a diagnostic challenge in endodontics, and has not been previously published. The English version of the questionnaire is available as Supplementary File 1. Each case was evaluated based on a structured questionnaire consisting of 6 clinical decision-making questions. The questions addressed: (1) presence of pathology (2), anomaly diagnosis (e.g., type of dens invaginatus) (3), need for treatment (4), proposed treatment type (5), treatment difficulty, and (6) prognostic evaluation (Fig. 1). Each question had a single correct answer and was scored in a binary fashion (1 = correct, 0 = incorrect).

Participant groups and model characteristics

Seven evaluators assessed the cases, including one expert endodontist and 6 LLMs. We evaluated the following models:

GPT-4o (OpenAI; released May 13, 2024), gpt-4o-mini (OpenAI; July 18, 2024), o1 (OpenAI; announced Sept 12, 2024), and o1-pro (OpenAI; introduced Dec 5, 2024). We also included Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic; June 20, 2024) and Microsoft Copilot (Microsoft; model routing service). For consistency, we refer to ‘gpt-4o-mini’ instead of ‘GPT-o4-mini-high’ and ‘o1-pro’ instead of ‘GPT-o1 Pro’ throughout the manuscript.

Two additional LLM systems, Gemini (Google) and DeepSeek (DeepSeek AI), were initially considered for inclusion. However, Gemini LLM was excluded as it did not support visual data input, and DeepSeek LLM was unable to process image uploads, rendering both models inapplicable for this visual diagnostic task.

The expert evaluator was an endodontist with at least 5 years of clinical experience. All evaluators were presented with randomized and anonymized cases in a standardized order. Interactions with the AI models were conducted through text prompts, and their responses were manually transcribed into the evaluation forms by the researchers.

Scoring of responses

Each response provided by the models or the expert was scored based on the reference answer provided by the expert evaluator. Answers were classified as either correct or incorrect, resulting in a total of 114 responses per group (19 cases × 6 questions). Both total scores and question-level data were included in the analysis.

Before any model interaction, a reference answer key was developed a priori by the expert based on established diagnostic criteria, including Hülsmann’s dens invaginatus classification, and evidence-based treatment principles outlined in Cohen’s Pathways of the Pulp [12th edition]. The key defined a single correct answer for each of the six questions per case. Once finalized, the key was locked and not modified after data collection.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of total scores for each evaluator was assessed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. As the data were not normally distributed, non-parametric statistical methods were employed.

Differences in total correct responses among the 7 groups were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. When a significant difference was found, pairwise group comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test as a post-hoc analysis. These comparisons included both ‘expert vs LLM’ and ‘LLM vs LLM’ evaluations. For 21 pairwise comparisons, Bonferroni-adjusted significance was α = 0.05/21 = 0.00238.

Additionally, Chi-square tests were used to compare the distribution of response accuracy among different AI models and the expert clinician for each question. A significance level of P < 0.05 was adopted for all statistical analyses.

To evaluate whether there were statistically significant differences in total correct response scores between the expert clinician and the various AI models, a Kruskal-Wallis H test was performed to compare the diagnostic performance among the groups. The results indicated a statistically significant difference between them (P < 0.05). To identify the specific group differences responsible for this significance, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were carried out using the Mann-Whitney U test, as shown in Table 1. These analyses revealed that the expert clinician’s performance differed significantly from that of all AI models. (P < 0.05). The expert clinician exhibited statistically significantly higher accuracy scores than all AI models (P < 0.05). Furthermore, Copilot performed significantly worse than Claude, GPT-4o, GPT-o1, GPT-o1 Pro, and GPT-4 Mini (P < 0.05), indicating that Copilot was the least accurate among all models. No statistically significant differences were observed among the other AI models (e.g., Claude vs. GPT-o1, GPT-o1 vs. GPT-4 Mini, etc.), suggesting broadly comparable performance across higher-performing AI systems.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate and compare the diagnostic, treatment planning, and prognostic decision-making accuracy of 6 advanced LLMs (GPT-4o, gpt-4o-mini, o1, o1-pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Microsoft Copilot). in interpreting endodontic conditions based on periapical radiographs, using a structured clinical question format. The results revealed that although all LLMs demonstrated a certain degree of agreement with the expert evaluator in some domains, statistically significant differences existed between the expert and each AI model in terms of overall accuracy. These findings suggest that current LLMs, despite their increasing sophistication, have not yet reached expert-level proficiency in managing complex endodontic scenarios.

The study employed a structured and clinically relevant protocol by using actual anonymized periapical radiographs accompanied by case scenarios. This approach allowed the LLMs to be tested in a context that mimics real-world diagnostic challenges. In particular, the study included different types of DI cases that required detailed interpretation of radiographic and clinical information. The questionnaire was structured to evaluate clinical reasoning by including questions on the type of malformation, the incidence of malformation, the classification type of malformation, other names of this malformation, as well as associated anomalies and treatment approaches. This format enabled a multidimensional evaluation of the diagnostic and decision-making performance of the LLMs based on radiographic case scenarios.

In contrast to previous studies [7, 8] that primarily relied on simulated cases or purely text-based prompts, the present study incorporated radiographic data, thereby offering one of the earliest assessments of LLM performance in diagnostic tasks grounded in visual clinical information. Recent advancements in multimodal AI models, such as GPT-4 V, have demonstrated potential in medical image analysis [9], yet applications in dentistry remain limited. Accordingly, the present study contributes to the early exploration of LLM capabilities in image-based diagnostic reasoning within endodontics.

Statistical analysis using the Kruskal-Wallis test revealed significant differences between the expert and all LLMs in terms of total correct responses (P < 0.05). Mann-Whitney U post-hoc tests further confirmed that all LLMs performed significantly below expert-level accuracy. This aligns with previous findings in the literature, where LLMs have struggled with complex, context-driven decisions despite excelling at factual or guideline-based queries [10]. Interestingly, there were no statistically significant differences among the LLMs themselves. This suggests a shared ceiling of performance under the current design and perhaps limitations tied to the underlying GPT architecture. These models, though based on different versions of ChatGPT, may have similar language understanding capabilities when deprived of true visual integration [11, 12]. Chi square test results showed that the most significant discordance occurred in Question 3, which asked about the classification of the malformation. This finding suggests that LLMs may struggle to distinguish between nuanced diagnostic categories in developmental anomalies such as DI. Such classification requires not only recognizing the malformation but also understanding its subtype based on radiographic presentation, a task which remains challenging for current LLMs [11, 13].

The findings highlight that while LLMs have the potential to support clinical workflows, they are not yet suitable as autonomous diagnostic tools in endodontics. Their high performance in basic tasks (e.g., identifying pathology presence or estimating prognosis) shows promise, particularly for educational purposes or clinical decision support systems. However, their underperformance in scenarios involving anomaly classification (e.g., DI subtyping) limits their applicability in frontline care.

The findings of this study align with a growing body of literature demonstrating that, although LLMs may perform adequately on standard or factual tasks, they often fall short in complex clinical cases when compared with experienced clinicians [14]. The failure of LLMs to consistently recognize or appropriately manage such conditions underscores the importance of expert oversight. This reflects a broader trend observed in medical AI, where model hallucinations or shallow reasoning can lead to potentially harmful recommendations [15, 16].

These findings are consistent with prior research in dental AI applications that compared the performance of LLMs to human clinicians. For example, in a systematic review by Schwendicke et al. [5], it was reported that while AI systems, including deep learning models, performed well in caries and periapical lesion detection, expert clinicians consistently outperformed AI in complex diagnostic scenarios. Similarly, Kınıkoğlu et al. [17] compared ChatGPT and Gemini in the context of the Turkish Dental Specialty Exam (DUS) and found that while both models achieved high accuracy in knowledge-based questions, their performance notably declined in image-based tasks, and neither matched expert-level interpretation. Importantly, both studies, in line with our findings, noted that although different LLMs were tested, their diagnostic performance was statistically similar-highlighting a shared limitation in their interpretive reasoning capabilities, particularly when visual inputs are involved.

Although Claude and Gemini have performed well in other domains [18], Gemini was excluded here due to lack of image input support at the time. Future multimodal LLMs with direct image processing capabilities may close the gap observed in this study. Research into GPT-4 V and Gemini 1.5 Pro models has already demonstrated promising results in medical imaging interpretation [17].

A key limitation of this study is that LLMs relied solely on text prompts derived from radiographs rather than directly analyzing the images. This may have constrained their diagnostic depth. Future studies should evaluate multimodal LLMs capable of processing radiographic data directly. Another limitation of our study is that a single experienced endodontist served as the reference standard. Although appropriate for an initial benchmarking study, using one rater prevents estimation of inter-rater variability. Future work should involve multiple experts and report inter-rater agreement (e.g., ICC or κ) to quantify potential variability in the reference key. The small case set (n = 19) limits the generalizability of the findings, and heterogeneity in periapical radiograph quality (clarity, resolution, exposure) may have introduced input noise and biased decisions; future studies should standardize image acquisition or rate image quality, and include larger and more diverse case sets.

Furthermore, expanding the range of pathologies and including pediatric or traumatic cases may yield a more generalizable assessment of LLM competence. Evaluating native multimodal models and exploring fine-tuning or instruction-tuning on dental datasets are logical next steps.

Conclusion

These findings provide a realistic baseline for diagnostic support systems in endodontics, highlighting clear targets—robust visual integration and domain-specific training—for future development. While LLMs show potential in dental diagnostics, their current capabilities remain below expert-level accuracy in complex endodontic decision-making, suggesting they may be best suited as adjunctive tools, particularly in educational contexts or preliminary screening. However, continued advancement, especially in multimodal integration and domain-specific fine-tuning, will be critical before they can be reliably implemented in routine dental practice.
