Prevalence of microalbuminuria in type 2 diabetes mellitus patients and its association to overweight and obesity in Aljouf, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Ahmad Alqadheb, Daifallah Alruwaily, Abdulrahman Shalwala, Sumayyah Albedaiwi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:The worldwide prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is progressively increasing. Its subsequent complications, such as microalbuminuria, an early stage of diabetic nephropathy, are rapidly rising as one of the major T2DM complications.Aims:To determine the prevalence of microalbuminuria in T2DM patients in Aljouf, Saudi Arabia, and its association with overweight and obesity.Settings and Design:A retrospective cross-sectional observational study conducted at the primary healthcare services of Al-Jouf Comprehensive Specialized Clinics for the Security Forces.Methods and Material:A total of 128 T2DM patients aged 35 years and older were enrolled. Data on demographics, medical history, anthropometric measurements, and microalbuminuria status (assessed by urine dipstick) were extracted from electronic medical records.Statistical Analysis Used:Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).Results:Microalbuminuria was present in 35.9% of patients, with the majority having an albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) of 30 mg/g. Most participants were older (>51 years: 83%) with multiple comorbidities (88.3%). The most prevalent comorbidities were dyslipidemia (67.2%) and hypertension (61.7%). HbA1c was significantly higher in patients with microalbuminuria ≥80 mg/g (P = 0.046). No significant association was found between microalbuminuria and BMI.Conclusions:A high prevalence of microalbuminuria was observed among T2DM patients in Aljouf. Regular screening and early intervention are imperative to slow the progression of diabetic nephropathy.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is emerging as a significant global health problem, with an alarming rise in prevalence across the world, which is called “Diabetes Pandemic” by the World Health Organization.[1] The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) estimates that approximately 537 million people aged over 20 years were living with diabetes mellitus in 2021, a figure projected to reach 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045.[23] This remarkable increase places a huge burden on healthcare systems and economies worldwide. T2DM is a complex metabolic disorder characterized by chronic hyperglycemia due to true or relative insulin deficiency, ultimately leading to long-term injury of the eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart, and blood vessels.[4]

Diabetic nephropathy (DN), a severe microvascular complication of diabetes, is a leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) globally.[5] It is characterized by a progressive decline in kidney function, often asymptomatic in its early stages, making sensitive markers for early detection critical.[67] Microalbuminuria, defined as urinary albumin excretion of 30–300 mg/day, is recognized as an early indicator of DN and reflects increased glomerular permeability. It is also associated with higher cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in individuals with T2DM. Studies have reported a prevalence of microalbuminuria ranging from 20% to 40% among individuals with T2DM.[89]

Overweight and obesity are well-established risk factors for T2DM and its complications.[10] Excess adiposity contributes to insulin resistance, inflammation, and oxidative stress, all of which can accelerate the progression of DN. Several studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between body mass index (BMI) and the risk of microalbuminuria in T2DM patients.[1011]

In Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of T2DM and its associated complications is a growing concern. A study at a primary care center for security forces in 2019 reported a microalbuminuria prevalence of 9.8% among 283 diabetic patients.[12] Another large-scale study in Riyadh found that 41.3% of 76,184 screened patients had microalbuminuria.[13] Despite these data, there remains a paucity of region-specific evidence from Aljouf. This study was therefore conducted to address this gap by determining the prevalence of microalbuminuria among T2DM patients attending primary healthcare services in Aljouf, Saudi Arabia, and to assess its association with overweight and obesity.

Aims and Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of microalbuminuria in T2DM patients attending primary healthcare clinics in Aljouf, Saudi Arabia. The secondary objective was to examine the relationship between microalbuminuria and BMI categories (overweight and obesity) and to identify clinical factors—such as glycemic control (HbA1c), fasting blood sugar, and comorbidities—associated with elevated microalbuminuria levels.

Relevance for practitioners and patients

For clinical practitioners, the findings of this study offer several practical benefits. By establishing region-specific prevalence data for microalbuminuria in Aljouf, practitioners can better calibrate screening thresholds and prioritize high-risk patients for early nephropathy surveillance. Understanding the relationship between BMI categories and microalbuminuria equips primary care physicians with evidence-based justification to incorporate routine weight management counselling into diabetes care protocols. The study further highlights which patient subgroups—particularly older individuals with multiple comorbidities such as hypertension and dyslipidemia—warrant more aggressive and frequent monitoring, enabling targeted allocation of healthcare resources.

For patients, early identification of microalbuminuria provides an opportunity to intervene before irreversible kidney damage occurs, potentially delaying or preventing progression to ESRD—a condition associated with significant morbidity, reduced quality of life, and substantial financial burden. Recognizing the role of overweight and obesity as modifiable risk factors empowers patients to take proactive steps through lifestyle modifications, including dietary changes, physical activity, and weight reduction, which can meaningfully slow the progression of diabetic nephropathy. Ultimately, this study contributes to building a locally relevant, evidence-based framework that bridges epidemiological data and individualized patient care, supporting improved long-term outcomes for T2DM patients in the region.

Subjects and Methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at Al-Jouf Comprehensive Specialized Clinics for the Security Forces, Saudi Arabia. Data were extracted from electronic medical records of T2DM patients who attended primary healthcare services at the institution. The study aimed to determine the prevalence of microalbuminuria among patients with T2DM and assess its association with overweight and obesity.

The study included T2DM patients aged 35 years and above who had been diagnosed with T2DM for at least 1 year. Patients with a known history of renal disease or urinary tract infections were excluded to avoid confounding effects on urinary albumin excretion. Patient data included demographic information (age, sex), medical history, and anthropometric measurements. BMI was calculated from recorded weight (kg) and height (m). Microalbuminuria was defined based on the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), measured using a low-cost urine dipstick screening tool. BMI classification followed the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines: overweight (BMI: 25.0–29.9 kg/m²), obesity class I (BMI: 30.0–34.9 kg/m²), obesity class II (BMI: 35.0–39.9 kg/m²), and obesity class III (BMI: ≥40 kg/m²).

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations; categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The prevalence of microalbuminuria was calculated as the proportion of T2DM patients with a positive ACR. An independent samples t-test was used to compare continuous variables between patients with and without microalbuminuria above 80 mg/g. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Committee of Al-Jouf Comprehensive Specialized Clinics for the Security Forces. Patient confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing all data, and no identifiable personal information was included in the dataset. Patient consent was waived by the ethics committee given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of deidentified records.

Age and comorbidity profile

The majority of participants were older adults: 39.1% were aged 51–60 years and 43.8% were over 60 years, with only 7.8% under the age of 40 [Figure 1]. This pattern reinforces the well-established association between T2DM and advancing age.

Age distribution of T2DM patients attending primary healthcare in Aljouf, Saudi Arabia (n=128)

Comorbidities were highly prevalent; only 11.7% of participants had no additional health conditions. The most prevalent comorbidities were dyslipidemia (67.2%) and hypertension (61.7%), followed by hypothyroidism (21.1%) and asthma (7.8%). Other conditions including osteoarthritis, hyperuricemia, epilepsy, and Behçet’s disease were each present in ≤2.3% of the sample.

Microalbuminuria prevalence

Microalbuminuria was detected in 35.9% of participants. Among those positive, the majority had an ACR of 30 mg/g (26.6%), while 6.3% had an ACR of 80 mg/g and 3.1% had an ACR of 150 mg/g. The remaining 64.1% had negative microalbuminuria results [Figure 2].

BMI distribution

The mean BMI was 32.1 kg/m², indicating that the average patient fell within the obese category. The largest BMI group was obesity class I (30.0–34.9 kg/m²), representing 30.6% of participants, followed by the preobesity group (28.2%) [Figure 3]. Microalbuminuria was present across all BMI categories with no statistically significant trend [P = 0.885; Figure 4].

Discussion

Prevalence of microalbuminuria

This study found a microalbuminuria prevalence of 35.9% among T2DM patients in Aljouf, Saudi Arabia. This rate is consistent with the range of 20%–40% reported in the global literature.[89] Within Saudi Arabia, comparable rates have been reported in different regions. Aleidan et al.[12] found a prevalence of 9.8% at a primary care center for security forces in Riyadh, while Alzaid et al.[13] reported 41.3% in a large tertiary clinic in the same city. The variation likely reflects differences in sample characteristics, diagnostic thresholds, and clinical settings. Compared to these studies, our prevalence falls within the midrange, suggesting that Aljouf has a significant but not exceptional burden of early diabetic kidney disease.

Internationally, a large multicenter European study reported microalbuminuria in approximately 28.8% of T2DM patients,[8] while a Chinese cross-sectional study demonstrated a prevalence of 30.1%.[9] Our finding of 35.9% is higher than many of these figures, which may reflect the older age profile and high comorbidity burden of the Aljouf cohort. Studies in Middle Eastern populations have generally reported higher prevalence rates than those in East Asian cohorts, potentially due to differences in dietary habits, physical activity levels, and genetic susceptibility.

Age and comorbidity associations

Consistent with previous research, older individuals were disproportionately represented, with 83% of participants aged over 51 years.[14] Ageing is associated with declining beta-cell function, increased insulin resistance, and reduced glomerular filtration reserve, all of which predispose to both T2DM and DN. Hypertension (61.7%) and dyslipidemia (67.2%) were the most prevalent comorbidities in our study, similar to other Saudi Arabian T2DM cohorts.[12] The coexistence of these conditions significantly amplifies the risk of progressive nephropathy and cardiovascular disease, underscoring the need for integrated multifactorial management.

Microalbuminuria and Glycemic Control

A key finding of this study was the significant association between HbA1c and microalbuminuria ≥80 mg/g (P = 0.046). Patients with higher microalbuminuria levels had a mean HbA1c of 8.53% compared to 7.52% in those without. This aligns with evidence from the UKPDS and other large trials demonstrating that poor long-term glycemic control drives glomerular damage and albumin leakage.[6] Similarly, Ou et al.[15] found that HbA1c was an independent predictor of albuminuria in a cohort of 1,872 T2DM patients. Fasting blood sugar was numerically higher in the microalbuminuric group but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.287), likely owing to the modest sample size.

Microalbuminuria and BMI

Unlike several prior studies, the present study found no significant association between BMI and microalbuminuria (P = 0.885). This is consistent with findings by Mykkänen et al.,[10] who demonstrated that microalbuminuria was associated with insulin resistance but not with central obesity in nondiabetic subjects. Savage et al.[15] similarly found that BMI was not independently associated with urinary albumin excretion in T2DM. In contrast, Chen et al.[16] reported a graded increase in microalbuminuria prevalence from 14.6% in normal-weight individuals to 31.8% in obese patients, and Kim et al.[17] found that both BMI and waist circumference were independently predictive of microalbuminuria. A more recent study by Ou et al.[18] in 2021 found that multiple obesity-related indices—including BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, and waist-to-height ratio—were significantly associated with albuminuria in multivariable analyses.

The discrepancy between our results and these studies may be attributed to several factors. First, our sample size was relatively small (n = 128), limiting statistical power to detect moderate-sized associations. Second, our study population had a uniformly high average BMI (mean, 32.1 kg/m²), reducing variability and the capacity to differentiate between BMI groups. Third, our study used a urine dipstick rather than laboratory ACR measurement, which may introduce classification imprecision. Fourth, the absence of waist circumference data precluded assessment of central adiposity, which may be a more relevant measure than BMI in the context of DN risk.

How this study differs from previous research

This study is one of the few to report region-specific microalbuminuria data from Aljouf, a northern region of Saudi Arabia that has been largely underrepresented in the diabetes nephropathy literature. While prior Saudi studies were based in major urban centers (Riyadh, Jeddah), our data reflect the burden in a smaller, predominantly older, security-force-affiliated population with a high prevalence of concurrent metabolic comorbidities. Additionally, this study examined the specific relationship between BMI categories and ACR levels using a graded classification aligned with WHO guidelines, providing a nuanced analysis not present in older Saudi Arabian studies.

Conclusions

This study investigated the prevalence of microalbuminuria among T2DM patients in Aljouf, Saudi Arabia, and its association with overweight and obesity. A microalbuminuria prevalence of 35.9% was observed, consistent with previously reported Saudi and international figures. Older patients and those with comorbidities—particularly hypertension and dyslipidemia—were predominant in the study population. While no significant association was found between microalbuminuria and BMI, poor glycemic control (elevated HbA1c) was significantly associated with higher microalbuminuria levels, underscoring the primacy of glucose management in preventing diabetic nephropathy.[19]

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the study was conducted at a single specialized primary care center, which may limit generalizability to the broader Aljouf or national population. Third, microalbuminuria was assessed using a urine dipstick rather than a laboratory-based spot urine ACR or timed 24-hour urine collection, which may introduce measurement variability. Fourth, the relatively small sample size (n = 128) may have reduced statistical power, particularly for detecting associations between BMI categories and microalbuminuria. Fifth, the absence of data on waist circumference, diabetes duration, and medication use (e.g., renin-angiotensin system blockers) limited the scope of multivariable analyses.

Future directions

Future research should include larger, multicenter, prospective cohort studies across diverse regions of Saudi Arabia to establish nationally representative prevalence estimates and identify temporal trends. Studies should incorporate laboratory-confirmed ACR measurements, detailed anthropometric profiling (including waist circumference), and information on pharmacological treatments. Longitudinal designs will be essential to establish causal relationships and evaluate the effectiveness of early screening and intervention programs in reducing the progression from microalbuminuria to overt nephropathy and ESRD.[20]
