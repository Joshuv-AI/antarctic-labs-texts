Evolution of malignant tumors as neoorgan and predictions of future scenarios resulting from inappropriate treatment
Authors: Qianqian Chen, Shuai Tian, Enqiang Linghu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignant tumors have long been viewed as uncontrolled cell proliferation driven by somatic mutations. However, emerging multi-omics and microenvironment evidence challenges this paradigm. Advanced tumors transcend cellular abnormality to form a “neoorgan” – a complex tissue ecosystem with multicellular coordination, functional autonomy, metabolic symbiosis, neural integration, and evolutionary potential. Genomic instability generates lineage-specific karyotypes; tumors reactivate ancient gene modules from primitive multicellular organisms; they actively remodel immune function via mitochondrial transfer; and they establish functional connections with the nervous system. Based on this evolutionary trajectory, we propose testable predictions: tumors may progress from a parasitic neoorgan toward an independent species, as exemplified by naturally occurring transmissible tumors and the “tumor-derived animal” hypothesis. Recognizing cancer as an evolutionary entity striving for independence – rather than a mere genetic malfunction – demands a fundamental shift in therapeutic strategy. Instead of solely cytotoxic “killing”, rational approaches may aim to reintegrate the tumor into host regulatory networks or guide its evolution toward self-destruction. This framework expands tumor biology and challenges conventional boundaries between disease, life, and species.

Is cancer a disease, or an alternative experiment in evolution life?

For a century, malignant tumors viewed as uncontrolled proliferation driven by somatic mutations. However, integrative evidence from multi-omics and microenvironment studies overturnsthis view: advanced tumors form a “neoorgan” characterized by high tissue complexity, functional autonomy, and evolutionary potential. They are no longer passive parasites but evolutionary entities striving for independence [1].

Beyond parasitism: from cellular defection to organ formation

Advanced tumors are complex ecosystems of heterogeneous cancer cells, fibroblasts, immune cells, endothelium cells, and nerve endings [2]. This complexity embodies the hallmarks of an organ: multicellular coordination, functional division, self-maintenance, and interaction with the host.

Yet tumors differ from normal organs. Their genomes display “stability within instability” [3]. Their metabolic networks reactivate ancient gene modules from primitive multicellular organisms [4]. Their immune interactions are not passive evasion, but active remodeling: tumors transfer mutated mitochondria to lymphocytes [5].

Cancer is not a malfunction but a neoorgan “born” within the host, striving to transcend a parasitic existence.

Limitations of the neoorgan analogy

However, the analogy of tumors as a “neoorgan” has its limitations. Unlike normal organs, tumors do not arise from a dedicated lineage of organ-specific stem cells; their cellular constituents are largely recruited or co-opted from fully differentiated cells [6], 7]. The tumor “organ” lacks defined boundaries and exhibits extreme genetic heterogeneity. Moreover, tumors do not serve any homeostatic function beneficial to the host. Thus, the “neoorgan” analogy is a heuristic tool, not a claim of full organ identity.

Future forms: from neoorgan to independent species

As a speculative scenario based on the current evolutionary trajectory, morphological structure trends toward functional minimalism. Highly evolved tumors may retain only core survival modules: a stem-like center, a mesenchymal periphery, and a perivascular zone [14]. The neuro-tumor interface may develop specialized docking structures. Internally, metabolic symbiosis links oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)-dependent and glycolysis-dependent cells [15].

In this hypothetical progression, immune evasion could escalate to active suppression via mitochondrial transfer, exosomes, and Treg expansion [5], 12]. Tumors might also manipulate the nervous system [13].

The possibility of host departure. Although the transition to a fully independent species faces formidable hurdles, existing transmissible tumors demonstrate that path is theoretically open. Canine transmissible venereal tumor has persisted as aclonal lineage in dogs for over 8, 500 years [16]. The “tumor-derived animal” hypothesis suggests myxosporeans may have evolved from transmissible tumors in cnidarians [17].

Barriers to tumor “independence”

The transition from neoorgan to independent species faces formidable barriers: (i) host-to-host transmissibility (rare outside transmissible cancers) [13]; (ii) cross-species immune evasion; and (iii) re-evolution of multicellular complexity. These barriers are not insurmountable on evolutionary timescales – as exemplified by canine transmissible venereal tumor (CTVT) [16] – but they represent stringent filters.

The speciated by cancer development animals (SCANDAL) hypothesis [17] posits that some transmissible tumors may have evolved into independent multicellular lineages. While provocative, significant barriers warrant consideration. First, transmissible tumors in mammals typically elicit immune rejection [8], 13]. Second, re-acquiring complex multicellular functions is of uncertain genetic feasibility. Third, myxosporeans may have alternative evolutionary explanations. Thus, we present the SCANDAL as an intriguing but unproven conjecture, not established fact.

Theoretical implications and testable hypotheses

Redefining cancer as a “neoorgan beyond parasitism” yields testable hypotheses.

Speciation marker hypothesis. Single-cell lineages in advanced tumors may exhibit genetic isolation between distinct metastatic deposits [3], 18].

Neural dependence hypothesis. Selective nerve ablation should collapse tumor “organ function” [13], 19].

Transmissible potential hypothesis. Circulating tumor cells from long-term survivors may carry immune privilege genes [5], 16].

Validation of these hypotheses would fundamentally impact therapeutic strategy [20]. Current paradigms focus on “killing” tumors. A more rational approach may be to reintegrate the tumor into host regulatory networks or guide its evolution toward self-destruction [21], 22].

Therapeutic implications: concrete examples

This conceptual shift carries concrete implications.

First, neural modulation. Sympathetic denervation (e.g., 6-Hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA)) suppresses tumor progression in preclinical models [23], 24]; β-blockers merit clinical investigation.

Second, metabolic intervention. Dual targeting of OXPHOS (metformin) and glycolysis (2-DG) disrupts tumor metabolic symbiosis.

Third, guided self-destruction. Ergodynamic anticancer therapy (EDAT) selectively enhances chromosomal instability beyond a tolerable threshold to drive mitotic catastrophe [25], 26].

Conclusions

The evolution of tumors is a “second attempt at multicellularity” within the host, replaying ancient genetic programs across evolutionary deep time. This challenges our concepts of disease, life, and species boundaries. Facing an entity that seeks to become a new species, we need not only better drugs but a new way of thinking.
