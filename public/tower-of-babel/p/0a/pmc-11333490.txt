Breast milk dominant phyla and probiotic bacteria in the obese lactating women compared with normal weights
Authors: Shahla Karami, Seyedeh Neda Mousavi, Reza Shapouri, Hasti Naderloo, Siamak Heidarzadeh, Davoud Afshar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe main purpose was to determine the abundance of dominant phyla, Bifidobacterium spp., and Lactobacillus in breast milk of obese mothers versus normal-weights in fourth month of lactation in Iranian population. Sixty health women at the fourth month of breastfeeding, aged 18–40 years, were included and categorized based on body mass index (BMI) to the obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) and normal-weights (18.5 ≤ BMI ≤ 24.9). Bacterial DNA was extracted and qPCR of the 16S region was performed after human milk donation in a sterile condition. A multiple linear mixed model was used to determine the effective factors on the phyla population. Bifidobacterium spp. was significantly higher in milk of normal-weight group than the obese. The current weight showed a significant effect on the Actinobacteria abundance in milk. The Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes were significantly lower in mother’s milk with cesarean section (p = 0.04). Pre-pregnancy obesity decreased the Firmicutes and Lactobacillus abundance in maternal milk (p = 0.04 and p = 0.01). The Actinobacteria and Bifidobacterium spp. showed a significant effect on infant’s height (p = 0.008 and p = 0.04). The maternal current and pre-pregnancy weight showed an important effect on abundance of Actinobacteria and Bifidobacterium spp., as the good phyla and genus in milk which are associated with the infant’s height.Keywords: Milk, Bacterial phyla, Probiotic bacteria, ObeseSubject terms: Microbiology, Gastroenterology, Health care

Introduction

Human breast milk is considered the best feeding for newborns up to the first six months due to its ability to provide complete needs of infant to growth1. Importance of maternal diet in the first 1000-days of life and its impact on child development has been proved2. Despite the initial notions of being sterile, breast milk is now recognized as a bio-fluid, which can be a good source of beneficial bacteria3. The Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria are introduced as the main milk bacterial phyla4. Some species belong to these phyla are pathogenic while others such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium spp. have the probiotic effects which exert health-promoting impacts on child5. Maternal diet during pregnancy and lactation has permanent effects on child by impacting on microbiom and epigenome6,7. The composition of breast milk phyla is affected by maternal, dietary and environmental factors which transfer during breastfeeding from mother to infant with long-lasting health effects in adulthood6. Despite the bacterial variation during the lactation period, there are few cross-sectional studies, which have collected milk samples at the different times after delivery, in this field7,8. The effect of maternal mode of delivery and obesity on the bacterial population of milk has been studied in some countries with conflicting results9,10. In total, gestational age, infant gender, route of delivery, mode of feeding, lactation stage, geographical location, maternal diet, social network density and maternal health status are important determinants of the bacterial composition of human milk11. Considering the multiplicity of effective factors, there is a need to conduct a study on the composition of human milk phyla in different countries that have various dietary habits and special features in terms of geographical area for residence which effect on the abundance of bacteria. Based on the literature review, no study was found on the composition of human milk phyla in obese Iranian population, up to now. For the first time, maternal milk phyla were compared in obese breastfeeding women versus normal-weights in an Iranian population and the most effective factors have been determined on this abundance.

Materials and methods

Study participants and sample collection

The present study has been ethically approved by the ethics committee of Zanjan Azad University under the code of IR.IAU.Z.REC.1401.068. All methods were performed in accordance with the Helsinki guidelines and regulations. In the present case–control study, sixty mother-infant pairs were enrolled. Mothers aged 18–40 years, exclusively breastfeeding, who had single birth and were attending to the health centers for the growth assessment living in Zanjan city with Iranian ethnicity were participated. Participants, who were not smoker, addict, or alcohol/drugs consumers were included. Participants were grouped based on the current body mass index (BMI) status to the normal-weight (18.5 ≤ BMI ≤ 24.9 kg/m2) or obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) by dividing weight (kg) to height square (m)2 ratio. Sample representativeness was performed by the cluster sampling method which collected samples from all area of the city to assimilate economic, social and cultural properties in the selected participants. Participants were included in the study after obtaining oral, written informed consent and based on the inclusion criteria. Participants who consumed probiotic, prebiotic, symbiotic or antibiotics at least during the past two months were excluded. Participants with any type of chronic diseases including type 1 or 2 diabetes, renal, kidney, heart, immune, psychiatrics and thyroid disorders not included in the study. Participants with any type of gastro-intestinal disorder including nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, celiac or mal-absorptive and inflammatory situation were excluded. Women who had preterm labor did not include. Lactating women with a history of diabetes or hypertension during gestation were not included. Mothers with small and large newborns for gestational age were excluded. The maternal milk samples were collected in the morning at the health center after washing and sterilizing the hands, cleaning the nipples and the surrounding areas with chlorhexidine 2% solution prior to collecting 3–5 mL of milk in sterile falcons. All samples were collected from both breasts, manually. Before the DNA extraction, all samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 20 min for fat separation and were stored at − 75 °C for three months to final analysis. Demographic, dietary and anthropometric assessment

The demographic and anthropometric data including maternal age, education, mode of delivery, birth anthropometric indices including the weight, height and head circumference, and gender were recorded. Weight and height of mothers were measured using a calibrated Seca scale which is done by a trained nutritionist using a standardized 1 kg weight, and 0.5 cm height to determine the scale accuracy measurement. Calibrating was performed before each measurement. Weight and height of participants were measured with minimal clothing and without shoes and an inflexible meter, without shoes in a standing state, look forward, respectively. A food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was completed for all participants, validated by a three day food record (two regular days and one holiday). To measure weight and length of infants without clothes and nappy, a calibrated Seca baby scale (Harlow Healthcare, London, UK) and a standard Harpenden infantometer (Harlow Health Care, London, UK) were used to the last 0.1 kg and 0.5 cm, respectively12. Head circumference was measured by a flexible metal tape measure from the most prominent part of the forehead (1–2 fingers above the eyebrow) around to the widest part of the back of the head13.

DNA extraction and polymerase chain reaction

200 µl milk sample, 200 µl binding buffer and 40 µl proteinase K were added to a 1.5 ml micro centrifuge tube, mixed immediately and incubated at 70 °C for 10 min. Then, 100 µl isopropanol was added and mixed well. Samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 1 min, after purification through filter inserted to collection tubes. Filter tubes were combined in a new collection tube, and 500 µl inhibitor removal buffers was added to the upper reservoir and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 1 min. The last step was repeated two times by adding 500 µl washing buffer. After discarding the flow through, the entire high pure assembly was centrifuged for 10 s at full speed. To exclude the DNA, filter tube was inserted to a sterile 1.5 ml micro centrifuge tube, 200 µl pre-warmed elution buffer was added and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 1 min. The DNA quality was examined by running a small amount on the agarose gel and concentration of the extracted DNA was determined by a Nano drop spectrophotometer. The extracted DNA was kept in − 20 °C refrigerator to final analysis. DNA Amplification of 16S rRNA gene was done by qPCR method using universal bacterial primers (5′ to 3′) as follows: the Firmicutes phylum; F: GGAGYATGTGGTTTAATTCGAAGCA, R: AGCTGACGACAACCATGCAC. The Bacterioidetes phylum; F: GGARCATGTGGTTTAATTCGATGAT, R: AGCTGACGACAACCATGCAG. The Actinobacteria phylum; F: TACGGCCGCAAGGCTA, R: TARTCCCCACCTTCCTCCG. Proteobacteria; F: CATGACGTTACCCGCAGAAGAA, R: CTCTACGAGACTCAAGCTTGC. Bifidobacterium spp..; F: TCGCGTCYGGTGTGAAAG, R: CCACATCCAGCRTCCAC. Lactobacillus; F: AGCAGTAGGGAATCTTCCA, R: CACCGCTACACATGGAG.

These primers were verified in the primer-BLAST database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). ABI Step-One sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems, California, USA) was used for the real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR).

20 µL micro tubes were used, with 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix (Amplicon, Denmark), 4 µL of DNA template, and 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer. 4.5 µL purified water free of DNA and RNA was added. 16SrRNA was used as the positive control in each run and a blank (purified water free of DNA and RNA) was added for each bacterial phylum.

The cycle threshold (CT) values were normalized against 16SrRNA as a negative control. The initial denaturation included one cycle: 95 °C for 10 min. The amplification profile included 40 three-step cycles, including denaturation, annealing, and extension steps: 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 30 s. Sample size and statistical analysis

Considering the power of 80% and type 1 error, based on the previous study14 on bacterial profile of human milk, forty participants were considered in each group. Participants were matched by age. Fifty percent of participants had cesarean section and half of them vaginally delivered. Dietary information was inserted into the N4 software (Nutritionist 4, USA). All dietary intakes converted to grams per day and data transferred to SPSS software, version 18. The Kolmogorov Smirnoff test was used to assess the distribution form of data. In the normal distributed data, an independent sample t-test was used to determine differences between the two groups. However, non-parametric tests were used for the non-normal distributed data. Qualitative variables were compared by the chi2 square test. A linear mixed model was used to determine the effect of each assessed parameter on the bacterial population.

The birth weight and height of infants had no statistically significant difference between the two groups. To determine the effect of baseline assessed parameters and dietary components on milk phyla, a linear mixed model was used. Cesarean section showed a significant decreasing effect on the Bacteroidetes (odds ratio (OR) − 0.35, 95%CI − 3.42, − 0.01; p = 0.04), and the Firmicutes (OR − 0.29, 95 = − 6.21, − 0.43; p = 0.04) population in maternal milk. Maternal pre-pregnancy weight showed a significant negative effect on the Firmicutes population in milk (OR − 0.62, 95% CI − 0.52, − 0.01; p = 0.04) (Tables 4, 5). Milk Actinobacteria population was 39% higher in the normal-weight mothers than the obese (95%CI 0.06, 9.6; p = 0.03). In addition, the milk Actinobacteria population was significantly higher in mothers with a girl-baby than the boys (95%CI 0.35, 6.3; p = 0.03). The milk Actinobacteria population showed a significant positive effect on the infant’s height (p = 0.008). Maternal dietary intake of SFAs showed a decreasing effect on the Actinobacteria population (p = 0.04) (Table 6). The milk Proteobacteria showed a significant negative effect on the infant’s birth height (OR − 0.4, 95% CI − 1.36, − 0.03; p = 0.03) (Table 7). Mothers with more history of labor had lower Bifidobacterium spp. population in milk by 50% (95%CI − 8.2, − 0.32; p = 0.03). The Bifidobacterium spp. population was significantly lower in milk of mothers with a boy-baby than the girls (OR − 0.52, 95%CI − 10.3, − 2.002; p = 0.005). The Bifidobacterium spp. showed a positive effect on the Infant’s height (p = 0.04). Maternal pre-pregnancy weight showed a significant effect on the Lactobacillus population in milk (p = 0.01).

Discussion

Despite the Bifidobacterium spp., other breast milk phyla and Lactobacillus genus showed no significant difference between the obese and normal-weight mothers. Based on the mixed model, the maternal current and pre-pregnancy weight showed a significant decreasing effect on the Actinobacteria and Bifidobacterium spp. abundance in milk of mothers at the fourth months of lactation. The Actinobacteria and Bifidobacterium spp. showed a significant positive effect on the infant’s height. The maternal pre-pregnancy weight showed a decreasing effect on the Lactobacillus abundance in milk. Milk of mothers with a girl-baby had higher Actinobacteria and Bifidobacterium spp. than the boys. Higher dietary intake of SFAs showed a decreasing effect on milk Actinobacteria abundance. The early-life guts bacterial colonization which is under the influence of maternal milk composition, impacts on health and predicts the risk of chronic diseases in adulthood. Exclusive breast-feeding is encouraged due to the bioactive, hormonal and nutritional components in human milk, which contribute to infant’s health15,16. The development of gut microbiota begins at birth and continues to be shaped until 2–3 years old. After this period, reaches a relatively stable level that makes up the adult taxonomic microbiome17. There are very limited studies assessing maternal milk phyla. Previous studies reported that milk is dominated by staphylococci and streptococci, followed by corynebacteria, lactic acid bacteria, bifidobacteria, and propionibacteria which the main sources of these microorganisms are the infant’s and mother’s mouth and skin, breast tissue, and predominantly the maternal gut composition. But the problem is that these studies measured different baseline variables and even the milk sampling was performed in the different times of lactation period, which can affect the results because bacterial strains vary greatly between women and change over time18,19. In a recent cross-sectional study on healthy women, milk samples were collected at 2, 6, and 12 weeks postpartum and the phyla population was assessed. Results showed that maternal postpartum distress is an effective factor on Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria population in milk20. In the present study no difference was found in terms of Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria abundance between the two groups. One reason for this inconsistency may be related to maternal mood status that is not assessed in our study. In another cross-sectional study which was performed in Mexican women breastfeeding, the adiposity of the woman before pregnancy and during lactation have been introduced as the important effective factors on the abundance of Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria in human milk21. In our study, same results were observed about the maternal pre-pregnancy and current weight in time of lactation on abundance of Actinobacteria and Bifidobacterium spp. that belong to this phylum. But Bacteroidetes population showed no difference in our study. This may be related to differences in women ethnicity, diet, and time of milk sampling and geographical influences. In a recent pilot small cohort study which was performed on Australian lactating women, the maternal current BMI, mode of delivery, use of breast pump and infant pacifier were identified as the effective factors on the bacterial composition of human milk especially abundance of the Streptococcus and Staphylococcus22. These two species belong to the Firmicutes phylum are mostly related to the skin of breast and the baby's mouth, which can be transferred to the breast milk23. Sampling method (manually or using a milk pump), and breast skin cleaning during sample collection are the most effective factors on the abundance of Firmicutes24. Moreover, we didn’t assess use of infant pacifier in the present study that may effect on the results. In another case–control study, milk samples were collected from American lactating women, nine times up to six months postpartum. Results showed a higher abundance of Granulicatella, as a nutritional variant of Streptococcus, in milk of overweight and obese mothers compared with the healthy-weight women. Moreover, the SFAs and MUFAs were inversely associated with the abundance of Corynebacterium belong to the Actinobacteria. Similarly, our findings showed that the SFAs have a decreasing effect on the Actinobacteria abundance in maternal milk. Total carbohydrates and lactose showed a negative effect on the Firmicutes; however protein consumption showed positive effect on the Gemella, belong to the Firmicutes phyla in the mentioned study25. Our results showed no effect of dietary carbohydrate and protein consumption on milk bacterial abundance. This inconsistency may be related to the various dietary patterns in different countries. Three different hypotheses have been proposed to explain the mechanism of bacterial presence in milk including entero-mammary, the retrograde inoculation pathway and resident mammary microbiota26. The entero-mammary pathway points the translocation of maternal gut bacteria to the mammary glands through openings in tight junctions of epithelial cells created by the dendritic cells and possibly macrophages. Hormonal changes during the last month of pregnancy provide this condition that help travelling of dendritic cells through the lymphatic and blood circulation to the mammary ducts where they release the bacteria in milk26. It is noted that presence of anaerobes such as the Bifidobacterium spp. and Faecalibacterium in breast milk cannot fully explained by the retrograde inoculation pathway. This means that both pathways may be potential sources of the breast milk microbiota27. Differences in the lactation period effect on milk microbiota, because bacterial population differ during the various stages24. Maternal diet can influence on population of milk phyla by metabolite production. Short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are the most important metabolites which are produced from undigested carbohydrates, oligosaccharides and some proteins that can induce changes in the host through multiple ways directly or indirectly, at the local or systemic levels. Locally, these metabolites can influence immune cell populations or functions and administrate downstream metabolic pathways. In addition, some of microorganisms can produce metabolites that influence other members of the phylum or pathogens, altering the global composition or function/behavior of some of its members28. One study concluded that the composition of phylum in maternal milk and infant feces are related to each other however vary geographically29. Therefore, the differences in dietary pattern and type of foods consumed among various countries justify the result’s inconsistencies. In summary, the present study showed the main effect of maternal pre-pregnancy and current weight on the Actinobacteria and Bifidobacterium spp., as beneficial probiotic bacteria, which were lower in milk of obese mothers. This bacterium showed a significant effect on the infant’s height. The infant’s height is a main determinant of health and diseases in the future.

This was the first analytical observational study on the composition of human milk phyla in lactating women living in Iran with a special diet, geographical location and ethnicity. There are some limitations like all studies. The case–control design makes us unable to determine a clear causal-relationship. Half of participants in the present study vaginally labored and others had a cesarean section. Route of delivery is an important factor on milk bacterial composition. Low sample size in each route of delivery (15 participants in each group), may be one of the effective factors on the results. Future studies with large sample size considering one of delivery method should be designed to reach clear results. The sex of infants is another effective factor on the maternal milk phyla. Number of each sex is not enough to determine variations in the present study and further studies are encouraged. Moreover, the infant’s fecal microbiota must be evaluated to determine the association of infant gut microbiota with maternal milk. Intervention studies with special diets are encouraged to manipulate maternal milk bacterial composition and abundance to make this bio-fluid healthier for infants. Moreover, studies on the association between the breast-milk Bifidobacterium spp. with neurodevelopmental complications are encouraged.

Conclusions

In summary, the dominant phylum, Bifidobacterium spp., and Lactobacillus genera were assessed in milk of the obese versus normal-weight lactating mothers. Our results showed that the maternal current and pre-pregnancy weights have an important effect on the abundance of Actinobacteria and Bifidobacterium spp., as the good phyla and genus, which positively effect on the infant’s height. Further dipper investigations are needed to understand the main pathways and effective factors on the milk phylum considering dietary habits and environmental exposures.
