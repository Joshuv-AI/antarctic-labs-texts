Insulin resistance assessed by estimated glucose disposal rate and risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases incidence: the multi-ethnic study of atherosclerosis
Authors: Jiayi Yi, Chao Qu, Xiang Li, Hai Gao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the relationship between estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), a surrogate indicator of insulin resistance, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (ASCVD) incidence risk.MethodsThis prospective cohort study utilized data from the 6026 participants from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis. The eGDR (mg/kg/min) was computed as 21.158 − (0.09 × waist circumference [cm]) − (3.407 × hypertension [yes/no]) − (0.551 × HbA1c [%]). The population was categorized into four subgroups according to the quartiles (Q) of eGDR. Cox proportional hazard models were applied to assess the associations between eGDR and ASCVD incidence, and restricted cubic spine (RCS) was employed to examine the dose–response relationship.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 63.6 ± 10.1 years, comprising 3163 (52.5%) women. Over a median follow-up duration of 14.1 years, 565 (9.4%) developed ASCVD, including 256 (4.2%) myocardial infarctions, 234 (3.9%) strokes, and 358 (5.9%) fatal coronary heart disease. Compared to the lowest quartile, the adjusted hazard ratios (95% confidence intervals) for incident ASCVD for Q2–Q4 were 0.87 (0.68−1.10), 0.63 (0.47−0.84), and 0.43 (0.30−0.64), respectively. Per 1 standard deviation increase in eGDR was associated with a 30% (HR: 0.70, 95% CI 0.60−0.80) risk reduction of ASCVD, with the subgroup analyses indicating that age and hypertension modified the association (P for interaction < 0.05). RCS analysis indicated a significant and linear relationship between eGDR and ASCVD incidence risk.ConclusioneGDR level was negatively associated with incident ASCVD risk in a linear fashion among the general population. Our findings may contribute to preventive measures by improving ASCVD risk assessment.

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), which mainly includes ischemic heart disease and stroke, continues to be a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality [1–3]. According to estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, ischemic heart disease and stroke result in approximately 8.8 and 7.1 million premature deaths and 188.3 and 160.4 million disability-adjusted life-years, respectively [4, 5]. This imposes a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems and presents a significant public health challenge. ASCVD is largely influenced by modifiable factors [6]. Despite major efforts to control conventional risk factors, significant residual risk persists [7]. Identifying non-traditional determinants of cardiometabolic health could be essential for further reducing the ASCVD burden.

Insulin resistance (IR), characterized by decreased responsiveness of target tissues to insulin, impairs blood glucose utilization and is a major contributor to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [8]. Although the exact mechanisms explaining the association of IR with ASCVD are not fully understood, factors such as inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, metabolic disturbance, and oxidative stress have been proposed [9]. Several methods have been developed to evaluate IR, with the hyper-insulinemic-euglycemic (HIEG) clamp considered the gold standard. However, due to its complexity and cost, the clinical utility of HEIG in assessing IR in large-scale populations is limited [10]. Recently, the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) has emerged as a simpler surrogate marker for IR. Previous studies have found that eGDR was inversely related to risks of stroke, coronary artery disease, and all-cause mortality, particularly in diabetic individuals [11–13]. Yet the link between eGDR and incident ASCVD in the broader population remains largely underexplored.

To address the knowledge gaps mentioned above, in this prospective cohort study, we aim to investigate the association between eGDR and the incidence of ASCVD in the general population in the US. This investigation will enhance our understanding of the role of IR in ASCVD development and potentially inform targeted prevention strategies for non-conventional risk factors of ASCVD.

Methods

Study design and population

This study used data from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), a multiethnic and community‐based prospective cohort study aimed at understanding the progression of subclinical cardiovascular disease. MESA enrolled 6814 men and women aged 45–84 years from six U.S. communities, all free of cardiovascular disease at baseline. All participants provided informed consent, and institutional review board approval was obtained at each study center. The current study was conducted following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [14].

MESA is a longitudinal study that includes multiple exams (Exam 1–5). Since hemoglobin A1c was firstly measured at Exam 2, this study used Exam 2 as the baseline visit, and all characteristics of participants were obtained from Exam 2. Of the 6814 participants from Exam 2, 734 participants with missing data on variables needed to calculate eGDR were excluded from the study. We further excluded 5 individuals who lost follow-up and 49 individuals with ASCVD incidents within Exam 1 and Exam 2. Data collection and definition

Demographic information including age, sex, race (categorized as Caucasian, Chinese, African American, and Hispanic), education attainment (categorized as below high school, high school, and college or above), smoking status (categorized as never, former, and current smoker), medical history (hypertension and diabetes), and medication information were collected through standardized questionnaires at baseline visits. Physical examinations recorded standing height, weight, waist circumference (WC), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by the square of height (m2). Blood samples measured low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride, glycosylated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and fasting glucose were also included in this study. Hypertension was identified using the latest hypertension guidelines, as a self-reported history of hypertension, use of antihypertensive medication, and physical examination measured SBP ≥ 140 mm Hg, or DBP ≥ 90 mm Hg [15]. Blood glucose status were categorized as diabetes mellitus, impaired fasting glucose, and normal. Diabetes mellitus was defined by self-reported diabetes history, use of diabetes medication or insulin, or with fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dl, and/or having HbA1c level ≥ 6.5% at baseline [16]. Impaired fasting glucose was defined as non-diabetes with fasting glucose within 100–125 mg/dL [16]. Medication (including aspirin, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors [ACEI] or angiotensin II receptor antagonist [ARB], β-blockers, statins, insulin, and oral hypoglycemic medication) use was assessed by self-report questionnaires. Metabolic syndrome was determined according to the International Diabetes Federation definition [17]. Estimated 10-year CVD risk was calculated by the Framingham Equation [18].

Ascertainment of exposure and outcomes

The exposure of this study was eGDR (mg/kg/min) at baseline. The main outcome of interest was incident ASCVD, a composite event of nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), fatal or nonfatal stroke, and fatal coronary heart disease (CHD) during follow-up [20, 21]. The definitions, criteria, and adjudication procedures for these outcomes were established and executed by an expert panel, with detailed descriptions available in prior publications [20]. Briefly, participants were contacted regularly to inquire about hospitalizations, diagnoses, and deaths. Medical records were obtained to verify self-reported events, with records available for 98% of reported hospitalized cardiovascular events and 95% of reported outpatient encounters. Trained personnel reviewed the records, and two physicians independently classified and dated the events. MI was identified based on combinations of symptoms, electrocardiogram, and cardiac biomarkers. Stroke was classified by neurologists based on focal neurologic deficit and relevant brain imaging findings. Fatal CHD required documented MI, chest pain, or history of CHD, with no known non-atherosclerotic or non-cardiac cause of death.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) or median [interquartile range] as appropriate. Comparisons of baseline data for normally and skewed distributed variables were performed using analysis of variance and the Kruskal–Wallis H test, respectively. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, with differences determined through chi-square tests.

Kaplan–Meier curves were applied to illustrate the cumulative incidence of ASCVD, with differences compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazard models were used to determine hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations of continuous eGDR and eGDR quartile groups with ASCVD incidence. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and no obvious violations were observed. Three Cox models were applied. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, race, education, BMI, and blood glucose status (normal, impaired fasting glucose, and diabetes). Model 3 further adjusted for LDL-C, HDL-C, triglycerides, and medication usage. Trend tests were conducted using the median value of each quartile of the eGDR. To investigate the dose–response relationship between eGDR and ASCVD incidence, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) based on the Model 3 Cox models was employed, with the eGDR value at HR = 1 treated as the reference.

The C-statistic was used to assess the predictive value of eGDR on the incidence of ASCVD. To further estimate the additional predictive power of eGDR and metabolic syndrome category beyond the Framingham CVD risk, the net reclassification improvement (NRI) was also computed.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the effects of eGDR (continuous) on the incidence of ASCVD in several subgroups, including age (< / ≥ 65 years), sex (female/male), race (Caucasian/non-Caucasian) diabetes (yes/no), and hypertension (yes/no). The P values for the production terms between eGDR and the stratified factors were used to estimate the significance of interactions. To evaluate the robustness of the main findings, several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, the analysis was repeated among participants with normal fasting glucose levels (< 126 ml/dL). Second, eGDR was redefined based on a stricter hypertension definition (130/80 mm Hg). Finally, participants who developed ASCVD within 2 years of follow-up were excluded to reduce the potential for reverse causation bias.

Statistical tests were 2-sided, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.2.0 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Associations of baseline eGDR with incident ASCVD

During a median follow-up period of 14.1 years, there were 565 (9.4%) cases of incident ASCVD, including 256 (4.2%) cases of MI, 234 (3.9%) cases of stroke, and 358 (5.9%) fatal CHD were recorded. The incidences of ASCVD among participants with Q1−Q4 were 11.45, 9.16, 5.84, and 3.22 per 1000 person-years, respectively.

As shown in Fig. 2, Kaplan–Meier survival curves indicated that individuals within higher eGDR quartile groups had a lower cumulative incidence of ASCVD, MI, stroke, and fatal CHD (all log-rank P < 0.001). Discussion

This study adds to the growing body of evidence demonstrating the significant role of IR, as measured by the eGDR, in predicting ASCVD risks. Using data from MESA, results from this study demonstrate that eGDR was inversely associated with long-term ASCVD incidence risk among middle-aged community residents in the US. Dose–response analysis indicated linear relationships between eGDR and the incidence of ASCVD, MI, stroke, and fatal CHD. Subgroup analysis indicated that the inverse association between eGDR and ASCVD is more pronounced in younger individuals and those without hypertension. Similarly, the negative correlation between eGDR and stroke is stronger among younger individuals, while the inverse relationship between eGDR and fatal CHD is stronger in individuals without hypertension. Results from the study underscore the need to focus on non-conventional risk factors like IR, which may contribute significantly to the residual risk of ASCVD even in the presence of traditional risk factor control. Incorporating eGDR or similar surrogate markers of IR into routine clinical assessments could enhance the ability to identify high-risk individuals and implement targeted preventive strategies.

Previous studies have indicated that IR is a strong predictor of ASCVD [8, 22]. The Insulin Resistance Atherosclerosis Study was the pioneering epidemiologic research to establish a link between IR and CVD in a large-scale multiethnic cohort. This relationship persisted even after adjusting for confounding factors such as glucose tolerance, fasting insulin, LDL-C and HDL-C levels, smoking, hypertension, and BMI [23]. Likewise, Botnia et al. identified IR as an independent predictor of increased CVD risk among nondiabetic individuals over a 6.9-year follow-up period [24]. Consistent findings have also been reported in the Verona Diabetes Study [25], the Bruneck study [26], and the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study [27].

Mechanisms that account for the correlation between IR and ASCVD have been described. In states of IR, β-cells detect the severity of IR and increase insulin secretion to compensate for defects in insulin action [28]. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that high concentrations of insulin can accelerate the atherosclerotic process through various mechanisms. These include (i) stimulation of de novo lipogenesis, which leads to increased synthesis and secretion of very-low-density lipoprotein due to the activation of sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1C and inhibition of acetyl-coenzyme A carboxylase [29, 30], (ii) growth and proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells [31], (iii) activation of genes involved in inflammation [32], (iv) increased collagen synthesis [33], and (v) enhanced transport of LDL-C into arterial smooth muscle cells [34]. The accumulation of fat in the arterial wall induces inflammation, which promotes atherogenesis and causes endothelial dysfunction, subsequently accelerating atherosclerosis and insulin resistance [35].

The association of several surrogate indicators of IR, including the homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, and its related indices, with ASCVD incidence risk among the general population has been widely examined in previous studies [25, 36, 37]. The results consistently indicated elevated TyG index or HOMA-IR contributed to increased risk of ASCVD or severity of coronary heart disease [38, 39]. However, the calculation of HOMA-IR relies on fasting blood glucose and insulin levels, which limits its clinical utility since fasting insulin is not routinely measured in the general population. Additionally, factors such as the use of insulin, insulin sensitizers, and insulin secretagogues can interfere with HOMA-IR measurements, leading to potential misclassification [40]. Similarly, the TyG index has shown variable sensitivity and specificity under certain scenarios [41, 42].

eGDR, as a novel indicator of IR, has demonstrated similar accuracy to the HIEG in assessing IR and has shown good predictive ability for CVD incidence [43, 44], and the relative risk of CVD may be partly explained by each component in the eGDR formula [45]. Zhang et al. revealed that eGDR had a better predictive performance for CVD incidence compared to the TyG index in the elder population [46]. It has been postulated that the enhanced performance of eGDR could be attributed to the integration of clinical and laboratory data in eGDR calculations, providing a more comprehensive assessment of IR [46]. Given the invasiveness and cost of traditional methods for assessing IR, eGDR is more suitable for large-scale screening in real-world scenarios.

Our findings suggest that the lower glucose leakage rate observed in the elderly and individuals with metabolic syndrome components may indicate distinct metabolic processes or genetic influences in diverse populations. This aligns with research from other regions, such as Asia [45, 46], where higher eGDR is related to cardiovascular risk factors. These consistent trends underscore the importance of considering population-specific factors when developing prevention and treatment strategies for ASCVD. In addition, we also found that lower eGDR was more prevalent in lower education attainment and former smokers, which indicates that socioeconomic and lifestyle factors might also contribute to the IR progression. Further investigation into genetic, lifestyle, and environmental contributors will be essential to further understand the mechanism of modification effect of age and metabolic disorders with glucose leakage across different populations.

Previous studies have indicated that increased eGDR levels were independently associated with decreased risks of adverse cardiovascular events. Zabala et al. reported that a lower eGDR significantly elevated the risk of stroke and mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes [43]. In align with our results, a study involving 5512 elder individuals found that per SD increase in eGDR was related to a 17% lower risk (HR: 0.83; 95% CI 0.78–0.89) of CVD incidence during 6.7 year follow-up period [46]. The China National Stroke Registry III study also demonstrated that eGDR is a reliable predictor of functional outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke [42]. However, many of these studies focused on specific populations, particularly those with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, which are known risk factors for ASCVD. This focus could potentially exaggerate the detrimental effects of low eGDR on cardiovascular outcomes. A recent study highlighted that eGDR is associated with an increased risk of CVD in the general population, regardless of diabetic status, and that eGDR may be more sensitive in predicting CVD among non-diabetic individuals [45]. This observation is consistent with our sensitivity analysis, which excluded prediabetic individuals and involved participants with normal glucose status.

Previous research suggests that diabetic individuals might exhibit reduced sensitivity to predictive indicators due to additional risk factors, known as the ceiling effect [47]. Identifying risk factors in general populations could lead to earlier intervention efforts, significantly impacting disease burden reduction. Given these considerations, we investigated the association of eGDR with incident ASCVD among community-dwelling populations without present ASCVD using a multiethnic cohort. Our robust findings extend the understanding of the relationship between IR and ASCVD.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several noteworthy strengths. First, it is the first attempt to comprehensively evaluate the associations between eGDR and ASCVD incidents among the general population. Our results highlight the promising application of eGDR in the primary prevention of ASCVD. In addition, the prospective, population-based study design provides robust evidence of the link between the eGDR and ASCVD.

However, some limitations need to be considered. Firstly, observational studies, by their nature, do not establish causality and may even include reverse causation. Nevertheless, the impact of reverse causation appears minimal, as results remain consistent after excluding individuals who developed ASCVD within the first two years of follow-up. Secondly, despite adjusting for potential covariates, residual confounding cannot be eliminated, which is a common issue in observational research. Additionally, while HbA1c determination generally uses standard methods, certain conditions, such as iron deficiency anemia, erythropoietin administration, and splenectomy, can affect the measurement accuracy [48]. Thirdly, the parameters for eGDR were derived from the type 1 diabetes population. The parameter estimates may differ when applied to healthy individuals. In addition, the formula does not account for sex differences in waist circumference, which is a critical factor reflected in metabolic syndrome thresholds. This might reduce the generalizability of our findings and limit the clinical implications of this study. Finally, the study only included Western individuals aged 45 and older, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Further research is needed to confirm these results across broader and more diverse populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study revealed the significant inverse relationship between eGDR and the incidence of ASCVD, highlighting the importance of managing IR to mitigate the risk of ASCVD and improve cardiovascular outcomes in diverse populations. These findings suggest that eGDR could serve as a valuable marker for cardiovascular risk assessment and guide targeted interventions aimed at reducing the burden of cardiovascular disease. Future research should explore the mechanisms underlying this association and evaluate the potential benefits of interventions designed to improve eGDR in various populations.
