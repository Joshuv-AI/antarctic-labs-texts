Effects of standardized patient simulation combined with online case-based precision learning on early on-call anxiety among junior pediatric surgeons: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Yang Li, Pengxiang Wang, Junfei Chen, Shaobin Jin, Shengling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJunior pediatric surgeons often experience marked anxiety when undertaking independent on-call duties for the first time, largely due to limited clinical experience, high decision-making pressure, and heightened sensitivity to patient safety. Traditional lecture-based teaching models may be insufficient in preparing trainees for complex, dynamic, and high-risk on-call situations. This study aimed to investigate whether a precision teaching model combining emergency-oriented standardized patient simulation (SPS) with online case-based learning (CBL) could alleviate on-call–related anxiety and enhance self-perceived clinical competence among junior pediatric surgeons.MethodsThis retrospective controlled study included junior pediatric surgeons enrolled in standardized residency training at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University who had not yet commenced independent on-call duties. Participants were assigned to an intervention or control group according to departmental teaching arrangements. The intervention group received SPS of emergency scenarios combined with structured online CBL, whereas the control group received traditional teaching primarily based on classroom lectures and routine clinical supervision. Primary outcomes included anxiety levels measured using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7). Secondary outcomes were assessed using the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES). Between-group comparisons were performed 1, 3, and 6 months after participants began independent on-call duties.ResultsA total of 25 participants were included (intervention group, n = 14; control group, n = 11). No significant differences were observed between groups in baseline demographic characteristics or anxiety scores. After the intervention, the intervention group demonstrated lower SAS and GAD-7 scores than the control group at early follow-up time points, particularly at 1 month after on-call initiation, indicating reduced anxiety levels. In addition, self-efficacy scores were significantly higher in the intervention group during the early post–on-call period. These findings suggest that the blended educational intervention had positive effects on reducing on-call–related anxiety and enhancing self-perceived clinical competence.ConclusionsSPS combined with online CBL may partially alleviate early on-call anxiety among junior pediatric surgeons and promote improvements in self-perceived clinical competence. This blended teaching model demonstrates good feasibility and potential for wider implementation as an educational strategy to improve on-call preparedness and psychological well-being in pediatric surgical training.

Background

Pediatric surgery is a highly specialized discipline characterized by a broad disease spectrum, rapid changes in patient conditions, and stringent requirements for timely and accurate clinical decision-making [1]. Junior pediatric surgeons, particularly during the early phase of independent on-call duty, often experience significant anxiety due to limited emergency management experience, diagnostic uncertainty, and concerns about adverse clinical outcomes. On-call–related anxiety not only affects physicians’ mental health but may also adversely influence clinical performance and decision quality [2].

Traditional surgical education has long relied on lecture-based learning (LBL) and opportunistic clinical exposure [3]. However, such approaches are often poorly standardized and unable to realistically reproduce the complexity, uncertainty, and pressure inherent in on-call environments. As a result, they provide limited opportunities for repeated, systematic deliberate practice in high-risk situations and may be insufficient for cultivating critical analysis and evidence-based decision-making skills [4]. With the increasing adoption of competency-based medical education, growing attention has been directed toward innovative teaching strategies that enhance clinical preparedness [5].

Standardized patient simulation (SPS) enables learners to engage in interactive, scenario-based clinical decision-making within a risk-free environment [6]. Online CBL, through structured case analysis and discussion, promotes active learning and the development of clinical reasoning skills [7]. Although both approaches have demonstrated effectiveness in improving knowledge acquisition and clinical thinking, their impact on psychological outcomes—such as on-call–related anxiety—among junior surgeons has not been well studied. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the effects of SPS combined with online CBL on on-call anxiety and self-perceived clinical competence among junior pediatric surgeons.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective observational study included junior pediatric surgeons who underwent standardized residency training in the Department of Pediatric Surgery at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University between July 2021 and July 2025 and had not yet begun independent on-call duties at baseline.According to the training schedule, the 11 junior physicians trained between July 2021 and June 2023 were all assigned to the control group, whereas after the implementation of SPS and online CBL in July 2023, the 14 junior physicians enrolled in the autumn training program were assigned to the intervention group (Fig. 1).

All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. Participants were allocated to either the intervention or control group according to departmental teaching arrangements.

Educational intervention

The intervention group received a four-week blended educational program consisting of the following components:

Standardized patient simulation of emergency scenarios

Participants completed multiple interactive simulation cases covering common and high-risk pediatric surgical on-call scenarios, including but not limited to acute abdomen, severe infection, pediatric trauma, polytrauma, and postoperative complication management. Each simulation required diagnostic reasoning and treatment decision-making and provided immediate automated feedback and structured reflection prompts.

Online case-based learning (CBL)

Daily online case discussions were organized, with case content aligned with the simulation themes. Discussions focused on clinical reasoning, decision-making under uncertainty, and management strategies specific to nighttime on-call situations.

The control group received traditional teaching primarily based on classroom lectures and routine clinical supervision, covering the same clinical content as the intervention group but without SPS or structured case-based discussions.

According to the training schedule, the 11 junior physicians trained between July 2021 and June 2023 were all assigned to the control group, whereas after the implementation of SPS and online CBL in July 2023, the 14 junior physicians enrolled in the autumn training program were assigned to the intervention group.

Outcome measures

Assessments were conducted at 1, 3, and 6 months after participants began independent on-call duties. Anxiety levels were evaluated using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7). Self-perceived clinical competence was assessed using the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 19.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent-sample t-tests. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Between July 2021 and July 2025, 25 junior pediatric surgeons undergoing standardized residency training at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University were included. All participants had obtained physician licensure for the first time and were eligible to undertake independent on-call duties under institutional regulations. The intervention group included 14 participants, and the control group included 11 participants. No significant differences were observed between groups in age or training duration.

Changes in anxiety levels (SAS and GAD-7)

At baseline (T0), no significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups in SAS scores (24.57 ± 1.87 vs. 24.18 ± 1.47, P = 0.577) or GAD-7 scores (0.64 ± 0.74 vs. 0.81 ± 0.87, P = 0.590) ( Tables 1 and 2).

At 1 month (T1), anxiety levels increased in both groups. The intervention group demonstrated significantly lower SAS scores than the control group (58.00 ± 6.80 vs. 64.10 ± 5.56, P = 0.025). GAD-7 scores were also lower in the intervention group (12.93 ± 2.13 vs. 14.91 ± 1.81) (P = 0.020).

At 3 months (T3), anxiety levels in both groups declined compared with T1. SAS scores were 43.29 ± 5.22 in the intervention group and 47.10 ± 7.15 in the control group (P = 0.137). By 6 months (T6), SAS and GAD-7 scores in both groups had further decreased and approached baseline levels, with no significant between-group differences.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that emergency SPS combined with online precision CBL can significantly alleviate on-call anxiety among junior pediatric surgeons.This blended educational model was conceptualized as a form of precision teaching, as the training content was tailored to high-risk on-call scenarios and individual learners’ early-career needs. This finding suggests that digital teaching interventions designed around on-call scenarios not only enhance trainees’ knowledge acquisition and technical skills but also exert a positive impact on psychological well-being, helping to strengthen mental preparedness and confidence when facing real clinical situations. For junior pediatric surgeons in the early stage of undertaking independent on-call duties, such psychological improvements carry important educational and clinical implications, facilitating a smoother transition into the role of “primary responsible physician” and adaptation to independent on-call clinical practice.

Traditional pre–on-call training has primarily relied on clinical lectures and passive case demonstrations. Such approaches often fail to replicate the ambiguity and pressure inherent in real clinical settings and provide limited opportunities for active learner engagement. Consequently, they are less effective in systematically cultivating key competencies such as critical analysis and evidence-based decision-making, and are insufficient to meet the demands of the ongoing transformation in modern medical education [4]. On-call anxiety and stress are distinct from generalized anxiety in that they are specifically centered on the evaluative and high-stakes clinical environment [8]. During their first experience of independent on-call duty, junior physicians often undergo an abrupt transition from being “supervised trainees” to becoming the “primary responsible decision-makers.” This rapid role transition frequently outpaces internalization of clinical competence and represents a major source of anxiety. The absence of immediately available senior supervision means that all clinical decisions and their potential consequences must be borne independently, with legal and ethical responsibilities becoming explicitly salient. Even after completing formal assessments and training, junior physicians commonly experience persistent doubts, such as “Can I perform this procedure correctly?” “Am I capable of managing this independently?” and “What if complications occur?”

The reduction of on-call anxiety is largely attributable to repeated exposure to high-risk and high-uncertainty on-call scenarios within a safe and controllable educational environment. SPS, through highly contextualized case design, enables learners to practice diagnostic reasoning and therapeutic decision-making without risk to real patients, thereby gradually familiarizing them with on-call workflows and enhancing their perceived control over complex clinical situations [9]. Through repeated practice and immediate feedback, learners can engage in structured reflection on their decision-making processes—a mechanism widely regarded as critical for strengthening self-efficacy and alleviating anxiety [10].

Online CBL played an important complementary role in this blended teaching model. Through guided online discussions centered on real or simulated cases, learners were able to analyze clinical decisions from multiple perspectives with peer interaction and expert facilitation [7]. This collaborative learning approach not only helps consolidate clinical reasoning and integrate knowledge, but also alleviates the psychological stress experienced by junior physicians when “facing complex problems alone” during on-call duties.

Previous educational studies have largely focused on the effects of simulation-based training on knowledge, skills, and clinical reasoning, often overlooking the psychological challenges associated with role transition during early independent practice [11]. Moreover, the present study further extends its application value in the psychological dimension, particularly with regard to alleviating on-call–related anxiety. This finding underscores that, in medical education, the objectives of instructional interventions should not be confined to improving technical competence alone, but should also address learners’ psychological well-being and professional adaptability. In summary, SPS combined with online CBL offers a more comprehensive on-call preparedness strategy for junior pediatric surgeons by simultaneously targeting cognitive, behavioral, and psychological domains. This scenario-oriented educational model, grounded in real clinical contexts, may hold long-term value in reducing on-call anxiety, enhancing clinical confidence, and promoting sustainable professional development.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The small sample size and single-center design limit the generalizability of the findings. GAD-7 and SAS are not specifically designed to assess on-call-related anxiety.In addition, the time-based, non-randomized group allocation may have introduced potential confounding factors. Anxiety outcomes were assessed using self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to reporting bias. Long-term follow-up and objective clinical performance outcomes were not evaluated.

Implications and future directions

Incorporating SPS combined with online CBL into pediatric surgical training may simultaneously enhance clinical preparedness and psychological well-being among junior surgeons in the short term. Future studies employing multi-center designs, larger sample sizes, randomized methodologies, and longer follow-up periods are warranted to confirm the durability of these effects and to explore their impact on real-world clinical performance.

Conclusions

SPS combined with online CBL may be an effective approach for reducing early on-call anxiety and enhancing self-perceived clinical competence among junior pediatric surgeons. This blended digital teaching model represents a promising and scalable strategy for contemporary pediatric surgical education.
