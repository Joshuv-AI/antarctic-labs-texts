Performance of propensity score methods in observational studies: a systematic review
Authors: Mahin Tatari, Stefano Rosato, Paola D’Errigo, Giovanna Jona Lasinio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveObservational studies are increasingly used in health research. Although several studies have examined the performance of propensity score (PS) methods, a critical need remains for a comprehensive synthesis of these approaches. This review aimed to summarize existing evidence and offer practical guidance for researchers to estimate treatment effects in observational studies.Methods and materialsThis narrative synthesis systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and included peer-reviewed studies published between 2000 and 2025. Eligible studies reported methodological performance indicators such as treatment effect estimation, bias, mean squared error (MSE), and confidence interval (CI) coverage. All the search results were managed via Zotero to remove duplicates. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts for relevance. Studies were categorized by design, estimand, and the PS method used for comparison.ResultsPropensity score matching (PSM) and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) generally improved covariate balance and acceptable CI coverage across multiple evaluation metrics. Caliper-based optimal and full matching reduce bias and improve covariate balance for odds ratios (ORs) and marginal hazard ratios (MHR). IPTW, particularly when associated with doubly robust (DR) methods or stabilized weights, offers low MSE and reliable inference. PS adjustment is also effective in reducing bias and estimating relative risk (RR). In contrast, PS stratification often underperforms due to its sensitivity to effect size, treatment prevalence, and PS distribution, leading to greater bias and less precise estimates.ConclusionsPSM, DR-IPTW, and full matching are among the most effective methods for reducing bias, achieving covariate balance, minimizing MSE, and enhancing precision in observational studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03092-2.

Introduction

Observational studies are common in medical research [1, 2]. When treatment assignment is ignorable, propensity score (PS) methods can provide unbiased treatment effect estimates [1–6]. However, despite their robust theoretical basis and increasing utilization in medical research, the actual performance of PS methods remains uncertain. Simulation and empirical studies have increasingly been used to assess their effectiveness under various conditions, including binary and time-to-event outcomes, under different types of treatment–outcome relationships, model specifications, and censoring mechanisms. Several foundational studies have compared the performance of various PS methods for various types of treatment effects. In this field, Peter C. Austin’s work was highly influential and provided substantial contributions. Austin (2007) demonstrated covariate adjustment of the PS estimates of RR with the lowest MSE, whereas PSM yielded the least biased estimates of marginal ORs [7, 8]. In a subsequent study, Austin (2010) reported that IPTW was the preferred method for estimating absolute risk reduction (the difference between the proportion of untreated subjects experiencing the outcome and the proportion of treated subjects experiencing the outcome in the matched sample), especially the absolute treatment effect in a larger sample of patients [9]. Further simulation studies have provided additional empirical evidence supporting this hypothesis. Pirracchio et al. (2012) reported that PSM was more effective in small samples, whereas IPTW resulted in greater precision in large samples [10]. Concurrently, Ellis et al. (2013) and Hade and Lu (2013) emphasized the impact of covariate overlap and model misuse on treatment effect estimates [11, 12]. Recent studies have focused on survival analyses. A simulation study described by Austin (2013) in the context of time-to-event data revealed that matching reduced bias, whereas IPTW improved estimate precision [13]. Austin and Schuster (2016) reported that IPTW yielded more accurate estimates of the average treatment effect (ATE; estimating the effect of treatment in the overall population) than stratification did, which rather introduced bias [14]. Similarly, Franklin et al. (2017) noted that matching weights and spline-based regression yielded reliable results with rare outcomes and complex data, whereas IPTW and stratification were unstable at the extremes of the PS distribution [15].

The complexity of real-world data has also drawn attention to issues of model misspecification and heterogeneity in treatment effects. Kurth et al. (2006) reported that nonuniform treatment effects, especially among patients with low PS, contributed to variability across adjustment methods [16]. Abdia et al. (2017) and Austin and Stuart (2017) reported that IPTW and full matching remained reliable under conditions of weak to moderate confounding, even when the PS model was misspecified [17, 18]. Although matching designs are robust to misspecification, inaccurate estimation of the PS compromises its function as a balancing score and violates the ignorability assumption [19]. Elze et al. (2017) reported that 1:1 nearest neighbor PSM was slightly less precise due to patient exclusion [20]. Burden et al. (2017) emphasized that although PSM and IPTW achieved better covariate balance, exact matching resulted in greater patient exclusion and residual imbalance (standardized differences within ± 10%) [21].

Biomedical researchers often work with survival data that measure the time until an event occurs in a study with a predefined and exogenous duration [8, 14]. The distinguishing feature of survival data is that the event may not have occurred and that participants may still be alive at the end of the study period; additionally, they may have been lost to follow-up during the study or may have experienced a competing event [22]. These incomplete observations in the survival context are often referred to as censored observations. The most common censoring mechanism in medical research is right censoring, which is observed when the event of interest has not occurred by the end of the study duration [23]. In observational research, the Cox proportional hazard model is the standard method for modeling censored survival data, considering the impact of confounding factors. PS methods were designed to emulate the conditions of randomized experiments by balancing the covariates between the treatment groups. However, their performance in the context of censored data, both non-informative and informative censoring, remains a significant concern. Penning De Vries and Groenwold (2018) used real-world examples and simulations to highlight that PSM may not adequately address bias resulting from censoring. In such cases, treatment effect estimates could be significantly biased, particularly when they are correlated with both treatment and prognostic factors [24]. Similarly, Li et al. (2017) reported that methods such as PS adjustment and IPTW produced biased or unstable estimates in censored data or when PS models were misspecified [25]. Recent research has shown that even noninformative censoring can introduce bias in estimates obtained via PS methods. In fact, simulations by Hinman (2021) revealed that covariate balance decayed over time as censoring increased. PSM increased the imbalances in unobserved covariates, and IPTW became unstable with high censoring rates [26]. Barros and Häggström (2024) reported that increasing censoring led to systematic bias in both IPTW and PSM approaches, and PS adjustment alone ensured unbiased estimates in the presence of censorship [27].

Despite the growing body of literature, a notable gap remains in evaluating PS techniques for the analysis of time-to-event data. Many simulation studies have assumed that all individuals experience an event of interest, thereby eliminating censoring from their analyses. This assumption is often unrealistic in medical research, where loss to follow-up and censoring are prevalent. Consequently, the findings of these studies may not fully reflect the complexity of real-world data, limiting their practical applicability. The most recent review paper we found, by Vošner et al. (2025), focuses specifically on the use of PS matching in nursing studies [28]. This narrower scope further underscores the need for an updated and comprehensive systematic review addressing a broader range of applications and methodological considerations. The primary objective of this contribution was to systematically evaluate the performance of various PS methods in estimating treatment effects in observational studies across different types of outcomes and study settings. A secondary objective is to highlight methodological gaps, particularly in studies that neglect censoring in time-to-event analyses, and to provide practical recommendations for researchers selecting PS methods in applied contexts when analyzing survival data in the presence of censoring.

Methods and materials

This systematic review was conducted to evaluate the performance of different PS methods, such as PSM, stratification, IPTW, and PS adjustment, across different types of observational studies and outcome measures, including both binary and time-to-event data under different types of censoring. A comprehensive literature search was performed using the most common scientific databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar) covering articles published in English from January 2000 to March 2025. This period was selected to capture recent advancements in the use of PS-based methods for observational data. The search strategy combined medical subject heading (MeSH) terms and keywords via Boolean operators, including AND and OR, considering “performance of the propensity score”, (“performance of the propensity score,” AND “observational data,”), (“performance of the propensity score,” AND “time-to-event,”), (“performance of the propensity score” AND “treatment effect”), (“performance of the propensity score” AND “survival analysis”), (“propensity score”[Title/Abstract] AND (“survival analysis” OR “time-to-event”) AND (“treatment effect”). The inclusion criteria encompassed peer-reviewed studies that evaluated PS-based methods in observational data, either simulated or real-world, and reported methodological performance indicators such as treatment effect estimation, bias, SE, MSE, covariate balance, and CI coverage. The main exclusion criteria were randomized controlled trials; studies without methodological comparisons of PS approaches; and non-peer-reviewed materials such as conference abstracts, commentaries, and editorials. We also excluded grey literature from this systematic review due to strong concerns over its quality, given mainly by the lack of a rigorous peer-review process. While it can provide valuable new information not currently found in peer-reviewed published literature, its inclusion is time-consuming, and it may, potentially, add unrecognized bias. Too often, grey literature lacks detailed methodological descriptions, making it difficult to assess the quality of the research. The review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [29] 2020 guidelines as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1. A total of 1054 records were identified through database searches. After removing seven duplicate records, 1047 records were screened based on their titles and abstracts. During the title and abstract screening phase, inter-rater agreement between the two independent reviewers was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic [30]. Both reviewers included 78 studies and excluded 973 studies, with disagreements on three records. Disagreements at the screening and extraction stages were resolved through consensus discussions, and when consensus could not be reached, a third senior reviewer was consulted for adjudication. The observed agreement was 99.7%, and the kappa coefficient was 0.98. After a full-text review, 25 studies were included in the systematic review. All the search results were managed with Zotero to remove duplicates. Two independent reviewers trained in systematic review methodology and the use of bias appraisal tools, such as the risk of bias in nonrandomized studies of interventions (ROBINS-I) for observational studies and the ADEMP [31] (Aims, Data-generating mechanism, Estimand and other targets, Methods, Performance measures) framework for simulation studies [32]. We used ROBINS-I to assess study quality. This tool requires an assessment of whether a study is at critical, serious, moderate, or low risk of bias (or no information) in seven domains: confounding, selection bias, bias in measurement classification of interventions, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing data, bias in measurement of outcomes, and bias in selection of the reported result. A standardized data extraction form, created in Microsoft Word, was used to collect study characteristics, including author(s), publication year, PS methods assessed, type and mechanism of censoring, simulation design or data source, measure or outcome type, estimand (ATE, ATT (average treatment effect on the treated)), performance metrics (e.g., bias, MSE, covariate balance, CI coverage and SE), key findings, and limitations (Supplementary Table S1). The studies were grouped by design (simulation vs. empirical) and are listed in Supplementary Tables S2–S3, and a comparison of the PS methods is listed in Supplementary Table S4. The synthesis focused on identifying patterns in bias, efficiency, and the conditions under which specific PS methods performed best or failed. While every effort was made to ensure comprehensive coverage, limitations due to the English language may have influenced manuscript inclusion. The heterogeneity of the methods and outcome measures restricted the possibility of conducting meta-analyses or direct quantitative comparisons, which were not performed.

Results

A comprehensive synthesis of empirical and simulation studies comparing different PS methods revealed notable differences in their performances. Among the 25 included studies (Supplementary Table S1), 11 were simulation-based, six used only empirical data, and eight combined simulations with real-world data. The most frequently evaluated methods were PSM, IPTW, and PS adjustment. The studies covered a range of outcome types, including binary and time-to-event outcomes. Most studies estimated the ATE or ATT. The common performance metrics assessed included bias, MSE, covariate balance, and CI coverage. Several studies have also examined the effects of PS model misspecification and sample size on estimator performance. Results are presented separately for each PS method, followed by a comparative analysis of their performance across multiple evaluation metrics, as listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Propensity score matching

PSM consistently reduced bias relative to unadjusted treatment effect estimates by attenuating upward bias toward the null, thereby addressing confounding factors [16, 21, 33]. It was particularly effective in estimating RRs and ORs [7, 8, 13]. However, across a range of settings, PSM generally results in greater variance and MSE than other PS methods do, particularly for small samples or when the PS model is misspecified [9, 10, 13, 34]. The choice of matching strategy had a substantial effect on the covariate balance and estimator performance. PSM maintains a more consistent covariate balance, even in the presence of substantial initial imbalance [35]. Hade and Lu (2013) and Franklin et al. (2017) emphasized the importance of covariate overlap, with optimal or matching weights, reducing bias in cases of limited overlap or rare outcomes [12, 15]. By improving covariate balance and reducing, full matching (constructing strata consisting of either one treated subject and at least one control subject or one control subject and at least one treated subject) with caliper restrictions outperformed 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching, especially under strong confounding [18]. Network-optimized full matching (a subclassification assembles treated and control units that are comparable, ideally units with small distances. The best subclassification is a full matching [36]) which further enhances performance, demonstrating lower bias and MSE than traditional matching approaches [34]. Performance also improved when PSM was combined with DR estimators or integrated into full matching approaches, leading to reduced bias and more accurate SE estimates, even under PS model misspecification [34]. These methodological advantages make full matching a preferred approach for estimating both the ATE and ATT in complex, high-dimensional, or strongly confounded datasets [14]. In empirical evaluations, both PSM and IPTW outperform exact matching in balancing covariates [21]. However, when treatment effects were large or samples were small, PSM failed to detect treatment effects identified by IPTW, underscoring its lower efficiency in high-confounding or high-variance scenarios [10, 11]. PSM also yields more stable variance estimates than does IPTW in scenarios with low treatment prevalence or well-balanced covariates [9, 13, 18]. In terms of estimating SE, PSM generally produces the largest SEs among PS methods, particularly when the true treatment effect is null [8, 9].

Inverse probability of treatment weighting

IPTW consistently demonstrated low bias across various treatment effect estimates, including risk differences, ORs, and HRs, particularly under strong confounding and correctly specified models [9, 13, 37]. Compared with PSM and PS stratification, IPTW yielded more accurate effect estimates and superior performance in estimating the marginal effects. In survival analysis contexts, IPTW using the Cole and Hernán approach provided the most accurate and least biased estimates for both ATE and ATT [14]. Full matching or sample restrictions improved CI coverage, while trimming extreme weights enhanced agreement with PSM and reduced bias due to instability from large weights [11, 18]. Doubly robust (DR) estimation combines outcome regression (regression model) with weighting by PS model such that it is robust to misspecification of one (but not both) of these models. IPTW estimators further improve performance by maintaining low bias and MSE even under model misspecification [25, 34]. These advantages were particularly evident in administrative data and subgroup analyses, where IPTW retained robustness despite heterogeneous data structures [38, 39]. However, its performance declines under poor covariate overlap, high levels of non-informative censoring, or violations of positivity assumptions, especially in time-to-event outcomes [27]. While IPTW improved covariate balance overall, it occasionally failed to eliminate residual imbalance in subgroups unless supported by stabilized or truncated weights [34, 40]. In terms of SEs, the performance of IPTW varies depending on the model specification and outcome type. The DR–IPTW estimator produced the smallest SEs and the most accurate variance estimates, with the ratio of MSE to empirical standard deviation near 1, indicating strong precision in estimating uncertainty [9]. In contrast, standard IPTW approaches often overestimate SEs by 17–27%, particularly in marginal Cox models or when extreme weights are present [9, 14]. Izem et al. (2020) reported that IPTW using a fully specified model, including covariates, subgroup indicators, and their interactions, achieved the lowest variance in small subgroups [38]. However, in clustered or multilevel designs, IPTW produces imprecise SE estimates, regardless of the number of clusters [41]. Variance inflation was also observed when IPTW was applied to datasets with rare events or sparse data structures [10]. Finally, censoring was found to influence the estimator performance for time-to-event data. Ciminata et al. (2022) reported consistent results across PS methods in a comparison of two anticoagulants, although inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) showed slightly greater effects due to censoring sensitivity [40]. Barros et al. (2024) highlighted that IPTW and PSM resulted in substantial bias when there was moderate to high censoring, underscoring the need for appropriate handling of censoring in survival analysis [27]. Abdia et al. (2017) reported that IPTW and PS stratification were more accurate than regression or PSM for ATT [17].

PS stratification

PS stratification has demonstrated moderate effectiveness in reducing confounding bias; however, it frequently results in residual covariate imbalance between treatment groups, particularly in the extreme quintiles of the PS distribution [33, 37]. These residual imbalances often bias treatment effect estimates toward the null, limiting the method’s accuracy, especially under conditions of poor covariate overlap or in the presence of rare exposures [42]. The performance of stratification bias is notably inferior to that of IPTW and DR estimators, particularly in studies with strong confounding or model misspecification [17]. In terms of MSE, stratification typically underperformed relative to IPTW and full matching, with higher MSE values observed across a range of treatment effect scenarios [9]. The method also showed limited ability to adjust for confounding in cluster-randomized designs or datasets with sparse event counts, where it produced highly variable and less reliable estimates [41]. Although stratification achieved nominal 95% CI coverage for RRs, its coverage tended to be conservative, and performance deteriorated when larger treatment effects were estimated or when heterogeneity in baseline risk was present [8]. With respect to the SEs, stratification exhibited moderate precision. Its SEs were generally comparable to those of PS covariate adjustment and slightly more precise than those obtained through PSM under null effects [8, 9]. However, SE estimates are often overestimated in practice, particularly in cluster-based designs or when the treatment prevalence is low [41]. Additionally, stratification was more efficient than other methods for estimating log-RRs under the null hypothesis, making it potentially useful in situations with weak or no treatment effects [8]. Despite this, it was consistently outperformed by caliper-based matching and full-matching approaches in terms of both bias and SE estimation [18].

Covariate adjustment using PS

PS adjustment consistently demonstrated strong performance in reducing bias and minimizing MSE, particularly under scenarios involving null treatment effects or when the number of covariates was large [8, 16, 33]. This method is especially effective in high-dimensional settings and small-cluster designs, where it outperforms both stratification and traditional multivariable regression [40, 41]. When integrated with DR estimators or machine learning–based PS models (e.g., Super Learner), PS adjustment maintains a low bias even under PS model misspecification, demonstrating increased flexibility in complex real-world applications [25, 34]. In terms of SEs, PS adjustment produced stable and accurate estimates across a wide range of settings. Austin et al. (2007) reported that PS adjustment yielded slightly lower SEs than PSM did, particularly when the true treatment effect was null. The SE and MSE were minimized when the adjustment model included only true or potential confounders, excluding irrelevant or weakly associated variables [8]. Compared with stratification and IPTW, PS adjustment often produces more reliable SE estimates, particularly in settings with modest treatment effect sizes or limited outcome variability [8, 9]. Leyrat et al. (2014) further showed that PS adjustment maintained accurate variance estimation in small-cluster and clustered trial designs, whereas other PS methods tended to overstimate or underestimate uncertainty [41]. CI coverage was generally close to nominal levels under PS adjustment; however, it was outperformed by IPTW and stratification in large-sample or time-to-event analyses [41]. As with other PS-based methods, the inclusion of outcome-only predictors or irrelevant covariates could improve precision in some cases but also introduce instability, particularly when multicollinearity is present [10].

Discussion

In this review of extensive simulation studies and real-world analyses, we highlight how different PS approaches, such as PSM, IPTW, stratification, and PS adjustment, affect key outcomes such as treatment effect estimation, bias reduction, balance, model misspecification, precision, and CI coverage.

Bias reduction and treatment effect estimation

The unadjusted estimates were typically biased upward. Compared with PS-based methods, logistic regression tends to overestimate treatment effects, especially when strong prognostic factors are present, whereas PS-based methods—particularly IPTW and IPTW-DR estimators—offer superior bias reduction and greater reliability across diverse settings and large samples; however, their performance is influenced by covariate overlap, model specification, and censoring. PSM achieves accurate estimates under ideal conditions but may result in greater bias in small samples or without additional covariate adjustments. Advanced approaches such as full matching and entropy balancing (a preprocessing procedure that allows researchers to create balanced samples for the subsequent estimation of treatment effects [39]) increase robustness, especially for censored and time-to-event data. Nonetheless, under high non-informative censoring and when the marginal HR deviates from unity, even IPTW and PSM can produce biased estimates [2, 7, 8, 10, 11, 13–18, 21, 22, 24, 26, 27, 34, 37, 38, 40–43].

Covariate selection and model specification

Covariate selection is critical for PS model validity, influencing balance, bias, and overall estimator performance. Empirical evidence consistently supports the inclusion of variables associated with both treatment and outcome, while omitting confounders increases bias and type I error rates. Simple, well-specified models often outperform overly complex alternatives by improving precision, particularly in smaller datasets. Including outcome predictors enhances IPTW performance, although overly complex models may reduce match quality in PSM [11, 15–17, 24, 34, 37]. Achieving covariate balance is a central aim of PS methods, and PSM and IPTW outperform PS stratification and regression adjustment in most scenarios. Full matching with caliper restrictions excels even in the presence of strong confounding or model errors. Although high model discrimination does not guarantee balance, entropy balancing is highly effective, particularly in large datasets. The persistent imbalance in some key covariates (e.g., sociodemographics) emphasizes the need for postadjustment diagnostics [16, 18, 21, 35, 37, 40, 42].

Precision and MSE

The SE estimates also differ across methods. PSM typically produces larger SEs because of greater variability, especially when irrelevant covariates are included. Doubly robust IPTW estimators offer the most accurate and smallest SEs under appropriate confounder adjustment. Traditional IPTW methods often overestimate SEs by 17–27%, thereby reducing their efficiency. Full matching with calipers improves SE performance, especially in the presence of confounding factors or model misspecification [8, 9, 13, 14, 18, 24, 26, 34]. In terms of MSE, the DR estimators and IPTW typically yield the lowest values, particularly under strong confounding and correct model assumptions. PS adjustment performs reliably when the true treatment effect is null, whereas 1:1 PSM often incurs a higher MSE without additional refinements. PS stratification shows more variable performance—effective when treatment effects are small but less so as effect sizes increase. Parsimonious PS models that include only true confounders reduce MSE, whereas the inclusion of irrelevant variables inflates MSE. Full matching with calipers and DR estimators consistently delivers favorable MSEs, even under model misspecification [8, 9, 13, 16, 18, 37, 38].

Confidence interval coverage

With respect to CI coverage, the PSM and PS adjustments generally maintain nominal 95% coverage under null effects, although coverage deteriorates with stronger effects. Stratification tends to yield overly conservative and erratic coverage. The DR-IPTW methods provide the most reliable coverage for risk differences and HRs but may falter under extreme weights or low treatment prevalence—conditions that full matching can mitigate. In survival and subgroup analyses, IPTW offers accurate CI coverage when models are well specified and samples are large but struggles under misfit or limited data [8, 9, 13, 18, 26, 34].

Methodological strengths of PS methods

PS methods have become essential for addressing confounding factors in observational research, with various implementations offering distinct strengths tailored to the study design, data structure, and causal estimand. PSM, including full matching, generally provides the highest precision by creating well-balanced treatment and control groups, which reduces the variability in effect estimates. However, precision can decrease if the PSM process leads to sample loss or if overly complex models are used. Full matching with caliper restrictions further enhances the precision and robustness, even in the presence of model misspecification. IPTW retains the entire sample and typically yields a precise estimate of the treatment effect, particularly in large datasets. DR-IPTW estimators increase precision by protecting against model misspecification. Nonetheless, IPTW precision can be compromised by extreme weights and small sample sizes, which may require weight trimming or diagnostics to maintain stability. PS stratification offers moderate precision, generally better than traditional regression but lower than that of the PSM or IPTW methods. Its precision improves when combined with a regression adjustment within the strata. However, the precision of stratification may decline with greater treatment effects or stronger confounding factors, limiting its effectiveness in certain settings. PS adjustment preserves the sample size and achieves precision comparable to that of DR-IPTW when the PS model is correctly specified. This method is particularly useful for small samples or censored data but is more vulnerable to precision loss under model misspecification, especially with no collapsible effect measures such as ORs and HRs. Robust diagnostics are essential for all PS methods to ensure valid causal inferences. Combining standardized difference metrics with clinical expertise guides the optimal matching criteria and model selection. Advances in machine learning, such as gradient boosting and super learner algorithms, have improved PS estimation by reducing bias from model misspecification. Moreover, advanced balancing approaches, such as entropy balancing, extend beyond mean covariate adjustment to address higher-order moments, enhancing balance and estimator reliability. Simulation studies under realistic conditions, including limited covariate overlap and treatment effect heterogeneity, emphasize that no single PS method is universally superior; the choice of method should align with study goals, estimands, and data features. Recent methodological developments for handling censoring in time-to-event analyses, including weight modification on the basis of event probabilities, have strengthened the robustness of causal effect estimation. Complementing HR estimates with conditional HRs or survival curves further improves their interpretability and bias mitigation. Applications to real-world data demonstrate the practical utility of PS methods in comparative effectiveness research when randomized trials are not possible. Addressing challenges such as extreme weights, poor overlap, and misspecification through rigorous diagnostics and trimming safeguards the validity of the inference. Overall, PS methods provide versatile and powerful tools for confounding adjustment, with their performance contingent upon careful implementation, diagnostics, and often the combination of multiple approaches to strengthen causal conclusions.

Methodological limitations of PS methods

Despite their advantages, PS methods have significant limitations that can impact causal inference. These can be grouped into the following key thematic challenges:

Unmeasured confounding and model misspecification

PS methods only adjust for the observed covariates. Consequently, they cannot account for unmeasured confounding, which remains a persistent threat to causal inference. Most PS approaches rely on logistic regression for PS estimation, which may fail to capture complex or nonlinear relationships, particularly in high-dimensional settings. Despite the availability of more flexible machine learning techniques, these remain underutilized. Future studies should explore robust estimation approaches that are less sensitive to model misspecification and more capable of adjusting for hidden biases.

Matching-related issues

PSM has several important limitations that should be considered. First, it can reduce the sample size by excluding individuals who cannot be matched, which may limit how well the results apply to the broader population. Common methods such as 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching with calipers may not work well in different study settings. PSM usually estimates the ATT, making it difficult to compare with methods that estimate the ATE. In addition, SEs are more complex with matching, and the results can underestimate uncertainty. Balance checks in PSM often focus only on average values and overlook deeper differences in the distribution of variables. If there is a lack of overlap between groups or if treatment effects vary across individuals, the results may be less valid. Using more advanced matching strategies, such as full matching or machine learning–based approaches, along with better diagnostic tools, could help solve some of these problems.

Weighting instability and overlap concerns

IPTW can be unstable when there is poor overlap in covariates between treatment groups. This can lead to very large weights, which increase variability and make the results less reliable. IPTW also depends heavily on how well the PS model is specified; misspecification can introduce substantial bias. A common issue is the underestimation of variance. Compared to PSM, IPTW also lacks robust diagnostic tools to assess PS model quality and weight distribution. While truncation or stabilization of weights can reduce instability. These challenges are amplified in small sample settings. Future research should focus on developing improved diagnostics for weight quality and refining variance estimation techniques that properly account for uncertainty in PS estimation.

PS stratification and PS adjustment limitations

PS stratification and PS adjustment methods face several limitations that can compromise the accuracy of treatment effect estimates. PS stratification may yield imprecise estimates, especially when strata are small or sparse, and often overlooks intra-strata correlation, leading to underestimated variance. The assumption of comparability within strata may not hold in complex or high-dimensional datasets, further limiting its validity. Similarly, PS adjustment does not directly enforce covariate balance, making it difficult to assess the adequacy of confounding control. Both approaches commonly lack intuitive diagnostic tools, reducing transparency and hindering the evaluation of bias reduction effectiveness.

Handling censoring in survival data

A major gap in the literature concerns the handling of censoring in time-to-event analyses. Standard PS methods often assume non-informative censoring and may overlook biases introduced when this assumption is violated. Informative censoring, where the probability of being censored is related to treatment assignment or prognostic factors, can lead to substantial bias in treatment effect estimates. Despite its importance, many studies fail to adjust for censoring or to explicitly distinguish between censoring mechanisms in their design, limiting the validity of their conclusions. Limitation of this review

This review has some limitations that should be acknowledged. The review protocol was not pre-registered, which may affect the transparency and reproducibility of our methods. Future reviews should consider protocol registration and broader inclusion criteria to enhance methodological rigor. Our synthesis was also constrained by heterogeneity in study designs, outcomes, and performance metrics, preventing formal meta-analysis. Future research should aim to standardize reporting and directly compare methods across common benchmarks. Additionally, further exploration of PS method performance in complex settings, such as high-dimensional data or non-proportional hazards, remains necessary.

Conclusion

No single PS method is universally optimal, but PSM and IPTW consistently demonstrate strong performance across key metrics. Optimal and full matching with caliper restrictions effectively reduce bias, achieve excellent covariate balance, yield smaller SEs, and provide accurate estimates. IPTW—particularly when combined with DR estimators or stabilized weights—excels in estimating ATEs with low MSE and reliable inference under proper model specification and weight management. Overall, PSM, DR-IPTW, and full matching are the most robust methods, ensuring covariate balancing, bias reduction, precision, and interpretability in complex observational studies. Despite methodological advances, key challenges remain—particularly regarding performance under high censoring, model misspecification, and heterogeneous treatment effects. Researchers should choose PS methods based on study goals, data structure, estimand of interest, and sensitivity to PS model quality.
