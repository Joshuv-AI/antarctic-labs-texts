Physical inactivity in preclinical Alzheimer's disease: Risk factor, early symptom, or both?
Authors: Jiawei Zhao, Xin Li, Mengxuan Wu, Zhanjun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical inactivity is a key modifiable risk factor for Alzheimer's disease (AD). However, emerging evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship where inactivity also serves as an early manifestation of AD pathology. This perspective synthesizes recent findings from long‐term epidemiological cohorts, brainstem neuropathology, and digital phenotyping to reconceptualize the role of inactivity in preclinical AD. Early tau accumulation in catecholaminergic nuclei (locus coeruleus and ventral tegmental area) disrupts circuits essential for reward valuation and effort calculation. We hypothesize that this neurobiological failure may manifest as prodromal apathy and declining physical activity years before cognitive impairment. Standard metrics like step counts fail to capture these nuances. We propose a multidimensional digital phenotype – incorporating temporal complexity, circadian rhythms, and life‐space mobility – to distinguish “behavioral” from “biologically constrained” inactivity. This framework has critical implications for trial enrichment and the design of personalized lifestyle interventions.

FROM UNIDIRECTIONAL RISK FACTOR TO BIDIRECTIONAL RELATIONSHIP

The dementia field has shifted from viewing Alzheimer's disease (AD) as largely unavoidable to recognizing a substantial potential for risk reduction. Synthesizing global evidence, the 2020 and 2024 Lancet Commissions estimate that up to 40% to 45% of dementia cases could be prevented or delayed by addressing 12 to 14 modifiable risk factors across the life course. These factors encompass a broad spectrum of health domains, ranging from metabolic and cardiovascular profiles (including hypertension, obesity, diabetes, and high low‐density lipoprotein [LDL] cholesterol) and sensory health (hearing and vision loss) to psychosocial and behavioral engagement (physical inactivity, depression, and social isolation). 1 , 2 This evidence has underpinned a strong public health narrative: Inactivity increases risk; activity protects.

Parallel cohort studies generally support this view. In a large United Kingdom (UK) Biobank analysis, Del Pozo Cruz et al. reported a dose–response association between daily steps and incident dementia, with risk reduction evident at modest step counts and a plateau around 9800 steps/day. 3 Nguyen et al. found in very old women that higher accelerometer‐measured steps and moderate to vigorous activity were associated with lower incidence of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or probable dementia over nearly 5 years. 4 Earlier work using actigraphy and self‐report, including the Rush Memory and Aging Project and other cohorts, also linked greater late‐life physical activity to reduced risk of AD and slower cognitive decline. 5 , 6

However, these associations have come under scrutiny as follow‐up periods have lengthened and more sophisticated analytic approaches have emerged. In the Whitehall II study, which tracked self‐reported physical activity over 27 years, midlife activity was not clearly protective against dementia once long follow‐up and competing risks were considered. 7 Instead, those who ultimately developed dementia showed a distinctive decline in physical activity that began ∼9 years before diagnosis. 7 An individual‐participant meta‐analysis of nearly 400,000 adults from 19 cohorts similarly found that physical inactivity was strongly associated with cardiometabolic diseases, but its apparent association with dementia was largely attenuated once reverse causation was addressed. 8

Building on this critique, recent studies advocate for a bidirectional framework, viewing physical activity and brain health as a reciprocal loop rather than a linear causal chain. 9 , 10 , 11 Utilizing cross‐national data from the UK, United States, and China, Zhang et al. identified moderate‐intensity activity as the optimal “dose” for cognitive resilience, demonstrating its stable, mutually reinforcing link with cognitive ability across diverse cultures. 9 Conversely, Wang et al. highlighted the “reverse effect,” where cognitive decline and depressive symptoms act as primary barriers to activity maintenance, potentially fueling a vicious cycle of physical and mental deterioration. 10 As shown in Figure 1 (left panel), these findings challenge the traditional “modifiable risk factor” model by addressing reverse causality and prodromal apathy.

Layer 4: Wearable and mobile sensors detect these deficits not merely as low step counts but as specific digital phenotypes – reduced fractal motor activity regulation, circadian fragmentation, and constricted life‐space – which could serve as candidate digital biomarkers for early circuit failure in preclinical AD and for enrichment and stratification in prevention trials.

Reflecting on such evidence, the 2020 and 2024 Lancet Commissions explicitly concluded that the association between physical activity and dementia was likely bidirectional – late‐life declines in activity can be early manifestations of disease, which, if unaccounted for, can inflate observational estimates of inactivity as a causal risk factor. 1 , 2 For preclinical AD, where pathology is detectable years or decades before cognitive symptoms, this issue becomes particularly acute. In this perspective, we first review the evidence linking objectively measured physical activity to Alzheimer's pathology (Section 2). We then propose a neurobiological framework wherein early tau accumulation in monoaminergic nuclei disrupts motivational circuits, manifesting as prodromal apathy (Sections 3 and 4). To capture this circuit‐level failure, we advocate for transitioning from simple step counts to multidimensional digital phenotypes (Section 5). Finally, we discuss the implications of this framework for precision medicine, including trial enrichment and personalized lifestyle interventions, while acknowledging current translational challenges (Sections 6 and 7).

OBJECTIVELY MEASURED ACTIVITY, PATHOLOGY, AND COGNITION

Recent work using actigraphy in neuropathologically confirmed cohorts has begun to clarify how physical activity shapes preclinical AD trajectories. In the Rush Memory and Aging Project, Buchman and colleagues showed that higher total daily actigraphy‐measured activity predicted lower risk of AD and slower rates of global cognitive decline in older adults. 5 Importantly, Memel et al. later combined longitudinal actigraphy with post mortem pathology in this cohort and found that while physical activity did not significantly reduce AD pathology burden, it substantially attenuated the impact of multiple pathologies on cognitive decline, supporting a resilience rather than resistance model. 6

In the Harvard Aging Brain Study, Rabin et al. showed that in clinically normal older adults with baseline amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), higher pedometer‐measured step counts – particularly among amyloid‐positive individuals – were associated with slower cognitive decline and less structural brain change, independent of vascular risk. 12 Extending this work, Yau et al. combined up to 14 years of cognitive follow‐up with longitudinal amyloid and tau PET to test whether these associations were mediated by Alzheimer's pathology; they found that baseline physical activity was not related to baseline or longitudinal amyloid burden but that higher step counts were associated with slower inferior temporal tau accumulation in amyloid‐positive participants and that this attenuation of tau largely mediated the relationship between higher activity and slower cognitive and functional decline. 13 Dose–response analyses further indicated that the marginal benefits of additional activity were greatest at low step levels and that associations with tau and cognition plateaued at approximately 5000 to 7500 steps per day – well below the conventional 10,000‐step target 13 – a pattern consistent with meta‐analytic evidence on steps and mortality showing curvilinear benefits that level off around 6000 to 8000 steps per day in older adults. 14

Taken together, emerging evidence from these observational cohorts suggests a model in which physical activity does not meaningfully alter amyloid accumulation but may dampen tau propagation and neurodegeneration, enhancing resilience to existing pathology. 6 , 12 , 13 , 15 However, it is crucial to note that this hypothesis is currently supported by a limited number of studies in highly selected, relatively healthy older adults. Furthermore, some randomized controlled trials have yielded null findings regarding the effect of exercise on AD biomarkers, underscoring the complexity of these relationships and the need for larger, diverse longitudinal studies. Yet, they do not fully resolve directionality: Activity was measured only once; the history and trajectory of inactivity were not captured; and participants were highly selected, cognitively unimpaired, and relatively healthy at baseline. 12 , 13 Thus, to understand when inactivity is cause, consequence, or both, we must look to pathology and behavior at earlier levels of brain organization.

EARLY MONOAMINERGIC PATHOLOGY AND THE “WILL TO MOVE”

Neuropathological staging long emphasized the transentorhinal cortex and medial temporal lobe as the earliest cortical sites of AD pathology. Work by Braak and Del Tredici extended this framework to the brainstem, showing that hyperphosphorylated tau often appeared first in the noradrenergic LC and related monoaminergic nuclei, sometimes in young adults decades before clinical symptoms. 16 , 17 In a series covering ages 1 to 100 years, LC tau pathology was almost ubiquitous by late midlife, suggesting that noradrenergic dysfunction may be a very early feature of AD. 17

The LC provides diffuse noradrenergic input to cortex and subcortex and is essential for arousal, attention, and mobilization of energy for action. 18 The neighboring VTA, which accumulates tau somewhat later, supplies dopaminergic input to mesocortical and mesolimbic circuits that compute reward prediction and effort valuation. 18 , 19 Together, these nuclei form a core substrate for the “will to move”: deciding that an action is worth its energetic and opportunity costs (layer 1 in Figure 1).

Pharmacologic and lesion studies across species indicate that reducing catecholaminergic tone biases behavior toward low‐effort, low‐reward options, even when motor capacity is intact. 19 Humans with disorders affecting dopaminergic or noradrenergic systems – Parkinson's disease, major depression, traumatic brain injury – often present with profound reductions in spontaneous activity and goal‐directed behavior (apathy), despite preserved muscle strength. 19 , 20 While direct prospective human evidence linking brainstem tau to objectively measured activity declines within the same individuals remains limited, we hypothesize that early tau‐mediated dysfunction in LC and VTA in preclinical AD may similarly blunt internal drive, raising the neural “action threshold” required to initiate movement (layer 2 in Figure 1).

APATHY AS A PRODROME OF DEMENTIA

Clinically, this circuit‐level failure may manifest as apathy: A quantitative reduction in self‐initiated, goal‐directed behavior that is not fully explained by motor disability, sedation, or primary sadness. In the context of preclinical and prodromal AD, this aligns closely with the established construct of mild behavioral impairment (MBI), specifically its domain of decreased motivation. 21 Rather than claiming novelty in identifying apathy as a prodrome, our framework builds upon the MBI literature by proposing that this broad reduction in goal‐directed behavior – which also encompasses social withdrawal and reduced cognitive engagement – may be partly captured through continuous digital physical activity phenotypes. Levy and Dubois proposed a three‐part model of apathy – emotional‐affective, cognitive, and auto‐activation – mapping each subtype onto partially distinct prefrontal–basal ganglia–thalamic loops. 20 In the schematic (Figure 1, layer 3), we highlight two illustrative presentations – initiation and executive apathy – that broadly align with the auto‐activation and cognitive dimensions of this framework. 19 , 20 Husain and Roiser further framed apathy and anhedonia as transdiagnostic manifestations of disrupted value‐based decision‐making and effort allocation. 19

Epidemiological studies support apathy as an early harbinger of dementia. In the community‐based Health ABC cohort, Bock et al. found that severe apathy in older adults nearly doubled the risk of probable dementia over 9 years, even after adjusting for depressive symptoms and vascular risk. 22 Among individuals with MCI, multiple studies and meta‐analyses have shown that apathy predicts faster progression to dementia. 23 Imaging and biomarker work links apathy in non‐demented elders to higher cortical amyloid burden and regional tau pathology, particularly in prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortices. 23 , 24

From a mechanistic viewpoint, apathy represents a failure within the same frontostriatal and limbic circuits that translate internal goals into sustained motor output. When these circuits are compromised by early amyloid and tau pathology, individuals may retain adequate musculoskeletal capacity but experience a striking reduction in the internal drive to move. Temporally, this implies that a subclinical motivational deficit – a blunting of reward anticipation and effort valuation – takes root before overt changes in daily step counts become apparent. As the threshold for action initiation rises, individuals begin to opt for lower‐effort sedentary behaviors. In a preclinical AD context, such apathy may thus precede, and help explain, the observed decline in physical activity years before dementia diagnosis. 7 , 8 , 22 , 23 , 24

FROM STEP COUNTS TO DIGITAL SIGNATURES OF CIRCUIT FAILURE

Most epidemiological and interventional studies summarize physical activity with metrics like step counts or minutes of moderate to vigorous activity per day. While intuitive and easy to communicate, these measures collapse complex temporal, spatial, and contextual patterns into a single scalar. Yet, if inactivity in preclinical AD is partly driven by circuit dysfunction, we should expect changes not only in how much people move, but in how and when they move.

Fractal motor activity and circadian rhythms

Human motor activity exhibits fractal‐like fluctuations – similar statistical properties across multiple timescales – reflecting the flexible integration of cortical, subcortical, and autonomic networks. Gao et al. quantified “fractal motor activity regulation” from actigraphy in cognitively normal older adults and found that degraded fractal patterns were associated with worse cognition and higher levels of amyloid and tau biomarkers, with notable sex differences. 25 This suggests that subtle changes in the temporal complexity of movement may index early network disruption.

Circadian organization is another promising window. Using wrist actigraphy in clinically normal older adults, Musiek et al. showed that fragmentation and reduced amplitude of rest–activity rhythms were associated with amyloid positivity and predicted cognitive decline, independent of traditional risk factors. 26 Importantly, the LC and related brainstem structures play central roles in circadian integration and arousal regulation. 18 , 26 Thus, early LC tau pathology could plausibly manifest as increased nighttime activity, irregular sleep–wake cycles, and daytime inactivity – features detectable long before self‐reported sleep problems.

Life‐space mobility and spatial exploration

Beyond temporal structure, where individuals move carries additional information. “Life‐space” describes the spatial extent of a person's movements, from bedroom to community and beyond. In two well‐characterized aging cohorts, James et al. found that constricted life‐space was associated with higher risk of incident AD, MCI, and accelerated cognitive decline. 27 A subsequent systematic review by De Silva et al. confirmed moderate associations between reduced life‐space mobility and poorer cognitive performance, particularly in executive and processing speed domains. 28

Historically, life‐space has been assessed via questionnaires, but GPS‐enabled smartphones and wearables now allow continuous, objective measurement of spatial trajectories. Sturge et al. used the concept of “activity space” to examine social health in older adults with memory problems living at home, showing that changes in the diversity and range of daily destinations were linked to social engagement and functional status. 29

In preclinical AD, progressive constriction of life‐space and increased stereotypy of daily routes could reflect multiple converging influences: apathy (reduced exploratory drive), subtle executive dysfunction (difficulty planning multi‐step trips), fear of getting lost, or sensorimotor and vascular limitations. As with fractal motor and circadian measures, these patterns may emerge while standard cognitive tests remain in the normal range.

Toward a composite digital phenotype

Taken together, these observations support a richer, theory‐driven conceptualization of late‐life “physical inactivity,” as illustrated in layer 4 of Figure 1, in which inactivity is described by a multidimensional composite digital phenotype rather than a single scalar index. Key dimensions include (1) movement quantity, indexed by total daily steps and aggregate activity counts, which are robustly associated with cognitive and clinical outcomes in large cohorts 3 , 4 , 12 , 13 ; (2) temporal complexity of activity, including fractal scaling, autocorrelation structure, and burstiness over minutes to days, reflecting the flexibility and integrity of distributed neural and autonomic networks 25 , 26 ; (3) circadian organization, captured by the amplitude, stability, and fragmentation of rest–activity rhythms, which is closely linked to brainstem and hypothalamic function and to preclinical AD pathology 26 ; and (4) spatial exploration, quantified by life‐space radius, diversity of destinations, and route stereotypy, which indexes the everyday behavioral expression of motivational drive and executive control. 27 , 28 , 29

Different pathologic profiles – amyloid‐dominant versus mixed vascular–degenerative disease, LC‐predominant versus hippocampal‐predominant tau – may yield distinct digital signatures, especially when combined with neuropsychiatric scales (apathy, depression, anxiety) and cognitive testing. Integrating such measures with blood and imaging biomarkers could enable more biologically informative stratification of “sedentary” older adults.

IMPLICATIONS FOR TRIALS AND CLINICAL INTERVENTIONS

Recognizing that inactivity in preclinical AD can be both risk factor and symptom has several concrete implications for translational research and clinical practice.

Screening and enrichment for preclinical trials

Preclinical AD trials increasingly rely on amyloid and tau PET or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers, which are costly and invasive. In the future, pending robust prospective validation, multidimensional digital inactivity phenotypes – captured passively via wearables – could serve as a pre‐screening layer to enrich trials for individuals at high likelihood of early monoaminergic and tau pathology. However, we must acknowledge that long‐term (e.g., 1 to 2 years) digital phenotyping carries its own burdens, including device adherence, data privacy concerns, and analytical complexity, meaning it may not strictly be “low cost” at present. Furthermore, because apathy and physical inactivity are not specific to AD and can be driven by other prevalent age‐related conditions, such as cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) or major depression, digital metrics alone are insufficient for differential diagnosis. To realize their potential in precision medicine, these digital phenotypes must be integrated with scalable AD‐specific fluid biomarkers (e.g., plasma phosphorylated tau at threonine 217). 30 Establishing robust clinical cut points or utilizing intra‐individual longitudinal trajectories will be essential before these digital tools can be routinely deployed for trial enrichment.

For instance, older adults exhibiting (1) declining step counts, (2) worsening fractal motor regulation, (3) increased circadian fragmentation, and (4) rapid life‐space constriction over a 1‐ to 2‐year window could be prioritized for confirmatory biomarker testing. 13 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 This approach would complement risk stratification based on age, apolipoprotein E genotype, vascular risk, and family history. 1 , 2 , 31 , 32

Interpreting mixed results from lifestyle and exercise trials

Multidomain lifestyle interventions such as the FINGER trial have shown that combining diet, exercise, cognitive training, and vascular risk management can slow cognitive decline in at‐risk older adults. 33 However, single‐modality exercise trials in preclinical or prodromal AD have often yielded modest or null effects on biomarkers or cognition. For example, a 52‐week aerobic exercise intervention in amyloid‐positive, cognitively normal elders improved cardiorespiratory fitness but did not significantly change amyloid burden or cognition. 15

Incorporating digital activity phenotyping into such trials could help explain this heterogeneity. Participants whose baseline inactivity reflects modifiable environmental barriers (e.g., limited walkable space) may respond well to behavioral coaching. In contrast, those whose inactivity reflects LC/VTA and frontostriatal dysfunction may be “biologically constrained”: Even with coaching, they may struggle to substantially increase activity without additional pharmacologic or neuromodulatory support.

Mechanism‐based subtypes of late‐life inactivity

From a precision‐medicine perspective, it is inappropriate to group all participants with low step counts under a single label of “sedentary,” as this masks underlying etiological diversity in the determinants of inactivity. Digital phenotypes offer a finer, hypothesis‐driven stratification by identifying distinct subgroups based on their baseline digital and clinical profiles: (i) Behaviorally/environmentally constrained inactivity: Defined as low total movement volume (e.g., low step counts) with preserved temporal complexity, intact circadian rhythms, and retained life‐space radius/diversity, suggesting that neurobiological capacity for complex movement is largely intact but activity is limited by external barriers or modifiable habits.(ii) Biologically constrained inactivity: Defined as low total movement volume coupled with degraded digital signatures – loss of fractal motor complexity, highly fragmented circadian rhythms, and progressively constricted life‐space – particularly when accompanied by clinical apathy, which may signal underlying neurobiological constraints (e.g., monoaminergic/frontostriatal dysfunction and/or vascular network disruption) that limit the drive or capacity to move.(iii) Physically/systemically constrained inactivity: Defined as low movement primarily attributable to limited physical reserve (e.g., multimorbidity, sarcopenia, osteoarthritis‐related pain/limitations, advanced cardiopulmonary disease, or severe vascular disease).

Operationalizing these subtypes requires explicit, reproducible measurement rather than qualitative labels. Table 1 summarizes candidate metrics for each digital domain, the data source from which they are derived, and the direction that denotes abnormality. Concretely, “low movement volume” is operationalized as mean daily step count and total accelerometer activity counts (optionally moderate to vigorous activity minutes); “temporal complexity” as the detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA) scaling exponent of fractal motor activity regulation, with degradation reflected by the large‑timescale exponent drifting from ∼1.0 toward 0.5 (loss of long‑range correlation) 25 ; “circadian fragmentation” as elevated intradaily variability (IV) together with reduced interdaily stability (IS) and relative amplitude (RA), derived from non‑parametric rest–activity analysis 26 ; and “life‑space constriction” as reduced Life‑Space Assessment score and/or GPS‑derived radius of gyration, fewer unique destinations, lower location entropy, and increased time at home. 27 , 28 , 29 A central measurement question is whether abnormality should be defined against normative values or sample‑specific distributions. Because harmonized population norms currently exist only for coarse volume metrics, whereas universally accepted cut points for fractal, circadian, and life‑space indices do not, we propose a two‑tier, provisional strategy. First, where validated norms are available (e.g., the ∼5000 to 7500 steps/day plateau associated with tau and cognition 13 ), age‑ and sex‑referenced cut points can be applied. Second, for metrics lacking consensus norms, values should be standardized within‑cohort (z‑scores or percentiles) and, more importantly, interpreted as intra‑individual longitudinal change (e.g., a decline of ≥1 SD relative to a person's own baseline over 12 months), which is likely more robust than any single absolute threshold given the large inter‑individual variability in baseline mobility. Finally, subtype assignment is best treated as a multivariate classification problem – combining these digital features with brief clinical assessment through data‑driven methods such as latent‑profile analysis or clustering – rather than a rigid sequence of univariate cut‑offs. We emphasize that these definitions are offered as a hypothesis‑generating research agenda: Prospective validation, cross‑device harmonization, and consensus on computation and interpretation remain essential before the phenotypes can be reliably distinguished in research or clinical settings. 34

Achieving reliable phenotype definitions will ultimately require formal, community‐wide consensus rather than isolated single‐cohort efforts. We therefore advocate for a structured Delphi process – iteratively convening experts in digital biomarkers, movement science, sleep and circadian biology, geriatric neurology, and biostatistics – to converge on (1) which candidate metrics best index each domain, (2) standardized computation pipelines and minimum data requirements (e.g., minimum wear‐time, sampling frequency, and epoch length), and (3) provisional abnormality thresholds together with transparent reporting standards. Such consensus exercises have already proven feasible for related digital mobility outcomes and would provide the shared vocabulary needed for cross‐study comparison and pooled validation. 34 , 35 Nevertheless, several challenges should be anticipated. First, device and algorithm heterogeneity – differing sensors, proprietary preprocessing, and firmware updates – complicates harmonization and reproducibility across cohorts. Second, normative reference data stratified by age, sex, culture, and comorbidity are largely absent for fractal, circadian, and life‐space indices, constraining any absolute cut point. Third, the limited disease specificity of these signals means that consensus must explicitly address how to disentangle AD‐related change from that driven by depression, CSVD, or normal aging. Fourth, sustained passive monitoring raises non‐trivial concerns regarding participant burden, data completeness, privacy, and equitable access to wearable technology. Confronting these hurdles explicitly – rather than presuming rapid clinical translation – is essential to keep this framework a rigorous, incremental research agenda.

Differentiating these subgroups could refine inclusion criteria, inform adaptive trial designs, and prevent premature dismissal of exercise or lifestyle interventions as ineffective, thereby enabling more precise, personalized approaches to prevention in older adults at risk for cognitive decline. Operationally, classification should combine digital features with brief clinical assessment (e.g., apathy and depression scales), physical function measures (e.g., gait speed/frailty indices), and comorbidity profiles, rather than relying on step counts alone.

Integrating vascular and molecular mechanisms

The pathways linking physical activity, tau, and cognition are likely multifactorial. Objectively measured physical activity is associated with better cerebral blood flow, particularly in frontal and medial temporal regions. 36 , 37 Cardiorespiratory fitness modifies the relationship between AD polygenic risk and CSF amyloid/tau ratios, suggesting that fitter individuals tolerate higher genetic risk without equivalent biomarker escalation. 31 Exercise upregulates hippocampal brain‐derived neurotrophic factor via peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor‐gamma coactivator (PGC)‑1α/FNDC5 and increases circulating irisin, which in animal models and humans regulates synaptic plasticity and cognition. 38 , 39 Crucially, this framework must also account for CSVD. CSVD is a ubiquitous co‐pathology in late‐onset AD and a primary driver of both vascular cognitive impairment and late‐life apathy. White matter hyperintensities and microinfarcts frequently disrupt the same frontostriatal and thalamocortical networks targeted by early tau, leading to profound apathy, gait disturbances, and subsequent physical inactivity. 40 Thus, the “biologically constrained” digital phenotype we propose likely reflects the converging, additive burden of both monoaminergic tau accumulation and CSVD‐related network disconnection.

These mechanisms may help explain why long‐term inactivity can increase risk and why midlife and early late‐life exercise may be neuroprotective. But they also underscore a crucial point: The very circuits required to engage in sustained physical activity – LC, VTA, frontostriatal networks – are themselves modulated by activity. In individuals where these circuits are already compromised by tau and vascular pathology, the “dose” of exercise needed to produce benefit may exceed what can be achieved without parallel efforts to restore motivational capacity.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS AND CONCLUSION

To advance from association to mechanism and from generic recommendations to targeted interventions, several lines of research are particularly critical. First, life‐course, multimodal cohorts that repeatedly assess device‐based activity and digital mobility metrics, apathy and other neuropsychiatric symptoms, and serial amyloid/tau biomarkers across midlife and late life are needed to disentangle long‐term protective effects of activity from early disease‐related decline and to identify sensitive windows for intervention. 7 , 8 , 12 , 13 , 25 , 26 Second, rigorous causal‐inference approaches – such as target‐trial emulation, Mendelian randomization, and longitudinal mediation models – should be applied to clarify directional pathways linking physical activity, pathology, and cognition, building on prior work that has questioned simple unidirectional interpretations of observational associations. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 41 Third, mechanistic intervention trials are warranted in which small, deeply phenotyped samples receive combined aerobic or resistance training plus pharmacologic agents targeting noradrenergic or dopaminergic systems, specifically in individuals with evidence of LC/VTA involvement, to test whether augmenting motivational circuitry can enhance both behavioral engagement and biomarker trajectories. 15 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 31 , 36 , 38 , 39 Parallel efforts are needed to standardize digital biomarkers. A major translational hurdle is defining what constitutes an “abnormal” digital phenotype. Given the vast inter‐individual baseline differences in mobility, establishing universal absolute cut points (e.g., a specific fractal scaling exponent or life‐space radius) may be less effective than tracking intra‐individual longitudinal changes (e.g., a rapid constriction of life‐space relative to a person's own baseline over 12 months). Furthermore, integrating these metrics into clinical practice faces substantial barriers, including the cost of providing medical‐grade wearables, patient adherence over long periods, and the need for automated, US Food and Drug Administration‐cleared data processing pipelines that can translate complex time‐series data into interpretable clinical dashboards. 34 Therefore, reaching consensus on the computation and interpretation of fractal motor indices, circadian metrics, and life‐space measures across devices and populations, and on their specificity for AD versus other neurologic and psychiatric conditions, is essential to ensure that digital phenotyping approaches are equitable and feasible for widespread clinical use. 1 , 2 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29

In conclusion, physical inactivity in preclinical AD is neither a simple lifestyle choice nor a purely unidirectional risk factor (right panel in Figure 1). Rather, it operates on two intertwined axes: as a behavioral exposure that may exacerbate tau propagation, vascular dysregulation, and neurodegeneration 6 , 12 , 13 , 15 , 31 , 32 , 36 , 38 , 39 and as an early symptom revealing disease‐related disruption within monoaminergic and frontostriatal circuits that support effortful, goal‐directed movement. Recognizing this bidirectional nature has important implications for translational research and clinical care. The central challenge is to design interventions and trials that respect both facets: On the one hand, continuing to promote physical activity as a key component of dementia prevention; on the other, developing methods – grounded in biomarkers, digital phenotypes, and clinical assessment – to identify when a person's inability to “get moving” signals that AD has already begun to attenuate the neural will to move and, thus, requires therapeutic strategies that go beyond conventional exercise prescriptions.
