Randomized Trial of Dietary Nitrate Supplementation in CKD Pregnancy (ORCHARD-BEET)
Authors: Priscilla Smith, Danielle Ashworth, Rachel Hung, Yanzhong Wang, Andrew J Webb, Kathryn Dalrymple, Katherine Clark, Lucy C Chappell, Kate Wiles, Kate Bramham, ORCHARD Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNearly half of pregnant women with moderate-to-severe chronic kidney disease (CKD) will require dialysis or lose ≥ 25% kidney function by 12 months after delivery with high rates of neonatal complications. No targeted approaches have been developed to improve maternal outcomes.MethodsPregnant women with stage G2 to G5 CKD (< 25 weeks’ gestation) at 8 centers in the UK were randomized to standard care or daily beetroot juice (dietary nitrate 400 mg) within an observational cohort. Exclusions were established dialysis, age < 18 years, major congenital fetal abnormality, multifetal pregnancy, and beetroot allergy. The primary outcome was recruitment rate/site/mo. Secondary outcomes included feasibility measures as well as maternal and fetal or neonatal clinical and safety events.Results108 participants were randomized (54/arm); overall mean recruitment rate was 1.01/site/mo (SD: 0.90). Women receiving dietary nitrate with prepregnancy estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 45 ml/min per 1.73 m2 tended to have lower median creatinine (Cr) concentrations postpartum than those on standard care (6 weeks: Cr, 176 [interquartile range, IQR: 165–194] μmol/l vs. 226 [IQR: 169–296] μmol/l, P = 0.23; 6 months: Cr, 173 [IQR: 152–175] μmol/l vs. 216 [IQR: 149–295] μmol/l, P = 0.18). Admission to a neonatal unit or neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) tended to be lower with dietary nitrate than with standard care (7/30 [23.3%] vs. 21/53 [39.6%], P = 0.13). Seven women receiving dietary nitrate (23.3%) experienced 11 serious adverse events (AEs, SAEs) compared with 27 women receiving standard care (50.9%) who had 53 SAEs (P = 0.02). In post hoc analysis, fewer participants in the dietary nitrate group took antihypertensive medications during pregnancy (11/30 [36.7%] vs. 35/53 [66.0%], P = 0.01).ConclusionWe successfully recruited to a multicenter interventional study of pregnant people with CKD with suggestion of maternal kidney and neonatal benefit. Further evidence of efficacy is needed from a powered randomized controlled trial.Keywords: CKD, dietary nitrate, feasibility study, kidney disease, pragmatic trials, pregnancy

CKD is common with its global prevalence in 2026 increasing to 850 million (14.6%) and highest numbers observed in low resource settings and marginalized groups.1,2 Pregnancy with CKD remains rare with estimated prevalences reported at 0.2% to 6%3 but is increasing because of risk factors such as diabetes,4,5 obesity,6, 7, 8 hypertension9 and women delaying first pregnancy.10 Pregnancies complicated by CKD are at increased risk of maternal and fetal adverse outcomes, including preeclampsia, preterm birth, fetal growth restriction and pregnancy-associated progression of CKD.11 Advances in fetal and neonatal care have improved outcomes and most pregnant people with CKD are able to have successful pregnancies. However, previous reports describe women with stage G3 to G5 CKD having a 46% chance of losing > 25% of prepregnancy eGFR or requiring kidney replacement therapy during pregnancy or within 12 months postdelivery.12

There are no targeted interventions available to treat or prevent this progression and there has been no improvement in kidney outcomes the last 20 years.11,13 Furthermore, optimal kidney protective treatments such as renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors are not advised throughout pregnancy and breastfeeding because of the risk of fetal harm (especially after first trimester) and insufficient breastfeeding data.14 Dialysis in resource-limited environments may not be available in this vulnerable group,15 and is challenging for all new parents, particularly those with preterm infants; thus, cost-effective accessible interventions to prevent pregnancy-associated progression of kidney disease are urgently needed.

Rationale for study

Nitric oxide (NO) mediates hemodynamic adaptation, including augmented kidney blood flow in pregnancy. NO deficiency is associated with progression of CKD outside of pregnancy.16 Reduced NO availability in CKD is secondary to impaired synthesis17 and high concentrations of NO synthase inhibitors (e.g., asymmetric dimethylarginine)18,19; supplementation approaches are therefore needed that augment NO bioavailability independently of these pathways. Augmentation of NO with dietary nitrate (via the nitrate-nitrite-NO pathway) has been associated with multiple cardiac and kidney benefits in CKD and non-CKD populations, including reduced progression of CKD, reduction in acute kidney injury, reduced blood pressure (BP), reduced atherosclerosis (carotid and coronary),20,21 and decreased major adverse cardiac events.21, 22, 23, 24 Beetroot juice is naturally high in dietary nitrate and has been used in a concentrated formulation previously in pregnancy without CKD with high tolerability and no reported safety concerns.22 Based on the accumulated knowledge summarized briefly above, we hypothesized that supplementation of dietary nitrate with beetroot juice may be a safe and acceptable approach to restore hemodynamic adaptation in pregnant women living with CKD and improve maternal and fetal or neonatal outcomes.

This feasibility study was designed to test the hypothesis that pregnant women with CKD are willing to be recruited to a multicenter interventional study and to assess recruitment rates to a dietary nitrate supplementation trial to inform planning for a fully powered efficacy study. Data on secondary clinical outcomes were collected to ascertain whether any signal of benefit or evidence of harm exists.

Methods

Trial Design

The ORCHARD-BEET randomized controlled feasibility trial was conducted in 8 UK-based tertiary hospital sites from February 2020 to April 2024. It was designed pragmatically as a trial within a cohort (modified Zelen design). The methodological approach and rationale for using this to reduce confounding has been published.25 In brief, participants were recruited from routine antenatal visits to the ORCHARD observational cohort and if consented to be contacted for additional research were then randomized to either dietary nitrate or standard care in a 1:1 allocation ratio. Only those randomized to dietary nitrate were then approached for further consent to participate in the randomized controlled trial with standard care participants automatically enrolled. Participants randomized to the dietary nitrate arm who did not consent were not included in the randomized controlled trial analysis but remained in the observational cohort (Figure 1).

Trial within a cohort design.

The trial was prospectively registered online (ISRCTN 91211980), and ethical approval was granted by West of Scotland REC3 (19/WS/0197) and London–City REC (20/LO/0009) before commencement of any trial activities. The funder was not involved in protocol development or trial conduct. Patient voice was represented in pretrial planning through Patient and Public Involvement focus group input and throughout the trial with a patient representative member on the Trial Steering Committee.

Trial Participants

Participants with CKD Stage G2 to G5 as defined by Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes26 and singleton pregnancy were identified from routine antenatal appointments and screened for eligibility. Adults with capacity were eligible if prepregnancy eGFR was < 90 ml/min per 1.73 m2 or pregnancy Cr > 70 μmol/l with no evidence to suggest concurrent acute kidney injury. Exclusions were multifetal pregnancy, established dialysis at recruitment, known major congenital abnormality, aged < 18 years and beetroot allergy or intolerance. Recruitment to the observational cohort was any time before 25 weeks’ gestation but dietary nitrate was only started after 12 weeks if randomized to the intervention.

Trial Procedures

Study participants had ≤5 visits depending on gestation at recruitment; at baseline (0–24+6 weeks), second trimester (20–24+6 weeks), third trimester (32–35+6 weeks), 6 weeks and 6 months postpartum. All visits occurred during usual planned clinical care appointments either in antenatal or kidney clinic and second trimester visit was omitted if baseline visit occurred after 20 weeks’ gestation. BP, concomitant medications, blood and urine samples, and AEs were assessed at all visits.

Participants randomized and consenting to dietary nitrate took 70 ml “Beet-It-Sport shot” daily provided by the study from consent or 12 weeks (whichever was later) until delivery. Adherence to intervention was assessed at 2nd trimester, 3rd trimester, and 6-week postpartum visits by the number of reported missed doses. Acceptability was assessed at 2 to 4 weeks after recruitment, 32+0-35+6 weeks’ gestation, and 6 to 12 weeks after delivery using the following 3 statements: (i) I enjoyed taking my beetroot juice, (ii) I would take beetroot juice again in another pregnancy, and (iii) I would recommend beetroot juice to other pregnant women with kidney disease) with responses on a 5-level Likert scale.

Participants in both the intervention and standard care arms received routine care, including aspirin from 12 weeks unless contraindicated, first trimester trisomy screening, 20-week anatomy scans, and medication as per national guidelines in tertiary centers with local adaptation as needed. Growth scans, additional fetal monitoring along with all other antenatal care was provided according to guidelines at treating clinician’s discretion. Decisions regarding mode and timing of delivery were made by the treating maternity team.

Outcome Measures and AEs

The primary outcome for the trial was recruitment rate of pregnant participants with CKD per site/mo and is reported as mean (SD). Secondary outcomes were maternal kidney outcomes (change in kidney function from prepregnancy to 6 months postpartum, proteinuria, BP, and requirement for kidney replacement therapy), maternal obstetric outcomes (fetal loss or stillbirth, gestational hypertension or preeclampsia/hemolysis elevated liver enzymes low platelets, indications for and mode of delivery, blood transfusion units, high dependency or intensive care admission), neonatal outcomes (gestational age, birthweight, neonatal unit or NICU admission), acceptability and tolerability of the intervention as measured by adherence and score on a 5-point acceptability scale, and AEs occurring in mother or offspring. Feasibility outcomes included the number of eligible women with CKD, reasons for declining to participate, number and total proportion of beetroot juice intervention taken, reasons for nonadherence, acceptability of taking the intervention during pregnancy, mean and SD for change in eGFR before and after pregnancy to inform sample size of a definitive trial (progression of CKD following pregnancy).

Serum Cr (μmol/l) was measured to assess changes in kidney function during and after pregnancy. CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration 200927 was used to calculate eGFR for samples taken outside of pregnancy only. Proteinuria was measured as per local practice with random albumin-to-Cr ratio (mg/mmol), protein-to-Cr ratio (mg/mmol) or 24-hour protein excretion. Proteinuria quantification was converted to an estimated albumin-to-Cr ratio using a validated model to enable comparison.28 Visit BP was measured and recorded at each study visit and maximum BP was captured at the 6-week postpartum visit based on readings recorded any time during pregnancy and ≤6 weeks postpartum either during a study visit or captured in the medical record. Results are presented as mean systolic BP (SBP) mm Hg (SD) and mean diastolic BP (DBP) mm Hg (SD).

AEs were defined as any untoward event occurring during the trial to pregnant participant or baby irrespective of causality as per international guidelines.29 SAEs were defined as those leading to admission, prolongation of admission, life-threatening, leading to disability or death or congenital abnormality. All AEs were recorded; however, because of the high-risk nature of pregnancy in women with CKD, some expected SAEs did not require reporting within 24 hours but were routinely captured (including peripartum admission, preeclampsia, admission for monitoring baby or mother, preterm labor, low birthweight, neonatal intensive care, or special care admission). Starting hemodialysis as an outpatient did not meet criteria for SAE but was considered an important medical event.

AEs of special interest due to possible mechanisms of action of dietary nitrate were hyperkalemia (K > 6.0 mmol/l) and symptomatic hypotension, which were reported as SAEs.

Data were recorded in a MACRO; Elsevier database hosted on a server at King’s College London and managed by King’s Clinical Trials Unit.

Randomization and Allocation

Once consent was provided and baseline assessments were completed within the observational cohort, the participants were randomized (1:1 ratio) using an online system designed and maintained by the King’s Clinical Trials Unit. Minimization variables were the following: study center, prepregnancy eGFR (< 45 or ≥ 45 ml/min per 1.73 m2) or serum Cr at recruitment to ORCHARD-Obs (< 110 or ≥ 110 μmol/l) and gestational age at recruitment (< 12 weeks, 12–15+6 weeks, 16–19+6 weeks, ≥ 20 weeks). Confirmation emails were automatically generated and sent to relevant individuals detailing the selection for dietary nitrate intervention or standard care by King’s Clinical Trials Unit, and allocated an additional ORCHARD BEET study ID.

Blinding or masking of treatment allocation was not feasible given the lack of a placebo control because of funding limitations. An independent data monitoring committee reviewed safety data during the study. There were no interim analyses of nonsafety data because of feasibility nature of the trial.

Statistical Analysis

The results were stratified by treatment arm following a modified intention-to-treat principle.30 All participants consenting to dietary nitrate were included in the analysis. Participants randomized to dietary nitrate who declined to consent were not included in the analysis as per protocol. Binary and categorical variables were presented using counts and percentages. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using coefficients of skewness and summarized accordingly. eGFR was calculated at 6-weeks and 6-months postpartum and compared with prepregnancy eGFR. All analyses were completed in Stata 17.0 (StataCorp. 2021. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Sample Size

We aimed to recruit ≥114 participants to allow for 8% drop out rate and a final sample size of ≥105 to allow ≥25 to 50 participants taking the intervention as recommended for feasibility testing.28, 29, 30

Results

One hundred nineteen participants who met the eligibility criteria were identified from those recruited to the ORCHARD observational cohort, and 108 were randomized. All participants identified as women. Of the 11 women not randomized; 6 had declined to participate to future intervention studies, 2 were enrolled in other studies, 2 were > 25 weeks’ gestation before randomization so no longer eligible, and 1 was unable to be randomized because of temporary lack of online randomization system availability (Figure 2).

There were fewer participants of Black ethnicities in dietary nitrate than in standard care arms; however, there were no differences in other variables, including prepregnancy eGFR. Primary Outcome

Recruitment occurred over 31 months and was paused for 6 months because of the Covid-19 pandemic. Thirty of 54 (55.5%) and 53 of 54 (98.1%) agreed to participate with recruitment rates of 0.94 and 1.7 participants/mo in dietary nitrate and standard care, respectively; and an overall mean recruitment rate of 1.01 participants/site/mo (SD: 0.90).

Secondary Outcomes

Clinical outcomes are presented in Table 2.31 No overall differences were observed in kidney function compared with 6-weeks or 6-months postpartum but participants with prepregnancy eGFR < 45 ml/min per 1.73 m2 tended to have lower Cr concentrations after delivery if they had received dietary nitrate compared with standard care (6 weeks postpartum: median serum Cr 176 [IQR: 165–194] μmol/l vs. 226 [IQR: 169–296] μmol/l, P = 0.23; and 6 months postpartum: median serum Cr 173 [IQR: 152–175] μmol/l vs. 216 [IQR: 149–295] μmol/l, P = 0.18; Figure 3a, b, and c). Highest serum Cr any time from baseline until 6 weeks postpartum was likewise lower in the dietary nitrate arm than in the standard care arm (114 [IQR: 93–165] μmol/l vs. 127 [IQR: 93–177] μmol/l, P = 0.40). One standard care and no dietary nitrate participants started hemodialysis before delivery.

No differences were observed in urinary albumin-to-Cr ratio between dietary nitrate or standard care arms measured at routine study visits or highest recorded levels from baseline to 6-week postpartum (50.5 [2.8–122.9] mg/mmol vs. 31.1 [6.7–149.1] mg/mmol, P = 0.39). Similar numbers of participants in each arm had ever taken angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker prepregnancy (12/30 [40%] vs. 28/53 [52.8%]) with no difference in outcomes reported compared with never users.

Planned delivery for maternal hypertension or preeclampsia occurred in 5 of 30 (16.7%) of the dietary nitrate arm and 15 of 53 (28.3%) of the standard care arm (P = 0.24). Mean gestational age and live birth rates were high with no differences between arms. Participants receiving dietary nitrate tended to have infants with higher median birthweight (3060 g, IQR: 2310–3200 vs. 2750 g, IQR: 2190–3200; P = 0.68) and lower neonatal unit or NICU admission rates (7/30 [23.3%] vs. 21/53 [39.6%], P = 0.13) compared with standard care; however, this did not reach significance, and there were no differences in birthweight centiles.

No SAEs were intervention related, and no major congenital abnormalities were reported (Table 3). The proportion of participants with SAEs during the trial was lower in those receiving dietary nitrate (7/30 [23.3%]) versus standard care (27/53 [50.9%]). Participants in the intervention arm were less likely to experience an SAE than in the control arm (odds ratio: 0.29, 95% confidence interval: 0.11–0.80, P = 0.017) (Table 3). There were no cases of symptomatic hypotension and 1 case of hyperkalemia > 6 mmol/l in each arm.

Adherence data were available for 24 of 30 participants in the dietary nitrate arm and was recorded as the number of missed beetroot juice shots from consent to delivery collected at second trimester, third trimester, and 6-week postpartum visits. There was no missed shots reported in 7 of 24 (29.2%), 1 to 20 missed shots in 10 of 24 (41.7%), > 20 missed shots in 5 of 24 (20.8%), and full discontinuation before delivery in 2 of 24 (8.3%). Post hoc analysis demonstrated that fewer participants taking dietary nitrate took any antihypertensive medications during pregnancy (11/30 [36.7%] versus 35/53 [66.0%] P = 0.0097). Discussion

Major Findings

This feasibility randomized controlled trial has confirmed that recruitment of pregnant people with CKD to an interventional trial is achievable. The observed recruitment rate of 1.01 participant/site/mo provides data to inform planning for future trials in this population. No signals of harm associated with the dietary nitrate intervention were reported, including congenital abnormalities, hyperkalemia, or symptomatic hypotension. All AEs, including SAEs, nonserious events, and important medical events occurred less frequently in participants taking dietary nitrate than in those with standard care, proportion of women requiring antihypertensives was lower in those taking dietary nitrate, and there was a trend toward lower rates of highest and overall SBP and DBP and NICU admissions compared with standard care. There was a suggestion that Cr concentrations during pregnancy and up to 6-months postpartum was lower in those receiving dietary nitrate than in standard care, particularly in those with more advanced CKD prepregnancy (eGFR < 45 ml/min per 1.73 m2).

Strengths and Weaknesses Compared With Other Studies

To our knowledge, this is the only targeted interventional trial in pregnant women with CKD for 30 years32 and so comparison with other contemporary trials is limited. The high rates of pregnancy and maternal kidney outcomes occurring in our study are consistent with published observational cohorts and participant demographics are reflective of the pregnant CKD population in the UK.12,33 Three-quarters (76.8%) of eligible randomized participants completed the study, which is higher than observed in other recent high-risk pregnancy studies,22 with high rates of follow-up, likely reflecting an engaged patient cohort and high level care provided in expert centers used as sites.

Our study showed a 17% lower NICU admission rate in the dietary nitrate arm compared with the standard care arm but was not powered to show a statistical significance. Nevertheless, the magnitude of this reduction is comparable with that reported in a recently updated Cochrane review of antenatal corticosteroid in those at risk of preterm birth (risk ratio: 0.8, 95% confidence interval: 0.65–0.99).34

No signals of harm related to beetroot juice were identified for mother or baby, which is reassuring. Although further research in pregnancy is needed, studies in nonpregnant African populations with hypertension have shown benefits from dietary nitrate, with similar or greater magnitude of effect on BP lowering and no additional harm.35,36 Further, demonstrating potential utility beyond a high income setting and exploration of this widely available, acceptable, and low cost intervention, compared with pharmacological interventions and dialysis, is needed in high burden settings.

Magnitude of mean differences in highest BP between dietary nitrate and standard care arms were 6 mm Hg SBP and 4 mm Hg highest DBP, and overall 3 mm Hg SBP and 2 mm Hg DBP at study visits, which are comparable with those achieved in targeted hypertension trials in pregnancy, including the recent CHAP trial,37 which reported associated reduction in adverse maternal obstetric outcomes, including preeclampsia and severe hypertension without signs of neonatal harm but included only small numbers of women with CKD.38

Ormesher et al.22 investigated beetroot juice in pregnancy complicated by chronic hypertension but without CKD. Their study demonstrated that plasma NO levels can be augmented via the nitrate-nitrite-NO pathway with beetroot juice as a source of dietary nitrate in pregnant women and comparable overall changes in mean BP to those in our study were reported. Our findings are similar to BP differences in a recent systematic review of dietary nitrate trials, including 325 hypertensive, nonpregnant patients without CKD.39 However, a study of patients with CKD stage G2 to G4 (mean eGFR: 41.6 ml/min per 1.73 m2) reported larger BP responses to dietary nitrate than those in participants without CKD with statistically significant reductions in SBP, DBP, and mean arterial pressure > 8 mm Hg. This is consistent with the increased benefits of dietary nitrate seen in participants with prepregnancy GFR < 45 ml/min per 1.73 m2 who are likely to have lower available NO, thus had greater benefit from the intervention than those with adequate NO bioavailability. This effect may be exaggerated in pregnancy when NO concentrations are augmented as a healthy physiological adaptation.40

Limitations

Despite being disproportionately affected by CKD, women from lower socioeconomic groups and ethnic minorities were underrepresented in the intervention arm and further efforts should have been made to address this; however substantial delays in site setup and recruitment were encountered because of the COVID-19 pandemic and sites caring for higher proportions of these women had more restrictions with recruitment. Furthermore, assessment of these characteristics was not prespecified for data monitoring committee activities, and will need to be included in future trials for the findings to be relevant.

Previous studies of dietary nitrate supplementation have highlighted the impact of the oral microbiome on NO production and need for more study of this in pregnancy.22,41 We had planned to include saliva samples and tongue scrapings in our trial participants to gain more mechanistic insight by measuring salivary nitrate and nitrite levels along with assessment of impact of oral microbiome; however, the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in significant limitations on additional research procedures, which could increase infection risk and so this was abandoned. Women randomized to the dietary nitrate intervention were advised to avoid mouthwash, which can alter the microbiome and nitrate reducing capacity. Further investigation in future studies would be warranted to better understand some of the variability in BP response related to oral microbiome to the intervention in women both in and out of pregnancy.

Clinical outcome data, though encouraging, must be interpreted with extreme caution because of the small size of the intervention group. There were differences in the proportion of women with preexisting chronic hypertension and Black ethnicities, recognized risk factors for CKD progression, which may have contributed to differences in outcomes between arms but was not possible to explore in this feasibility study.

Analyzing the study according to a modified intention-to-treat method risked not fully maintaining randomization because of loss of participants who were randomized to the intervention but subsequently declined to participate. The classic modified zelen trial analysis would include all participants randomized according to their allocated group irrespective of whether they consented or took any of the intervention. This maintains full randomization and represents a pragmatic approach, which more closely resembles clinical practice. Because of the ethical approvals received, we were unable to include outcomes from the women who declined participation and this would need to be addressed in the design of a powered future study. The allocation ratio of 1:1 resulted in a lower number of participants in the dietary nitrate arm because of the consent processes in this pragmatic trial design. If pursuing this design in a future study, consideration of an intervention-weighted allocation ratio may provide more data on the impact of the intervention.

Comparison with placebo (beetroot juice with nitrate extracted) would have further enhanced robustness of findings but was not possible with the resources available for this feasibility study. Recruitment rates in this study are likely to underestimate potential rates in lower resource, high CKD burden settings; however, they provide an estimate for future international multicenter trial design.

The inclusion of large numbers of women with milder CKD may have reduced our ability to detect a significant benefit from the dietary nitrate intervention. However, this approach was justified at the planning stage given the risk of CKD progression and adverse outcomes reported at all levels of CKD in pregnancy, and was further supported by patient focus groups who emphasized the importance of these risks. Kidney benefit from dietary nitrate may be limited in participants with prepregnancy eGFR > 45 ml/min per 1.73 m2 and future studies should aim to include those with more advanced CKD12 and most likely to benefit from additional intervention. Moreover, those with higher GFR experienced a significantly lower AE rate in the dietary nitrate arm than in standard care, suggesting a possible benefit even in less advanced CKD.

Interpretation

We successfully recruited high-risk pregnant women to a targeted interventional study of pregnancy with CKD, which has not been attempted since 1995.32 Mean gestational age and live birth rates were high with no difference between intervention and standard care arms and no signals of harm. Women receiving dietary nitrate had significantly lower rates of SAEs and use of antihypertensives and trends for higher mean birthweight, lower neonatal intensive care admission, lower BP, and reduced Cr concentrations after delivery (in those with lower eGFR) than with standard care. These novel findings suggest potential benefit both for mother and baby from dietary nitrate, particularly in those with more severe kidney disease; however, these findings require assessment in a fully powered trial. Impact may be even higher in populations where access to medical care may be limited than the setting of our trial, and so future studies should aim to include a broader representation of medical systems and ethnic diversity.
