Process optimization and transcriptomic profiling reveal an inverse relationship between gene expression and succinic acid production from sugarcane leaves
Authors: Napapat Sitthikitpanya, Nantharat Wongfaed, Sureewan Sittijunda, Sompong O-Thong, Prawit Kongjan, Rattana Jariyaboon, Pensri Plangklang, Alissara Reungsang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuccinic acid is a valuable platform chemical widely used in the food, pharmaceutical, and bioplastics industries. This study combines process optimization with transcriptomic analysis to investigate succinic acid production from sugarcane leaf hydrolysate (SLH) using Actinobacillus succinogenes TISTR 1994 and to gain insight into the molecular mechanism underlying succinate biosynthesis from SLH-derived sugars. Systematic optimization identified MgCO3 concentration, sugar concentration, and initial pH as key factors influencing succinic acid production. Under optimal conditions (52.84 g/L MgCO3, 49.68 g/L sugar, pH 7.94), succinic acid production reached 18.87 ± 0.42 g/L with a yield of 0.59 g/g, productivity of 0.39 g/L/h, and 95.3% sugar utilization efficiency, compared to 76.0% under non-optimized conditions. Genome-wide transcriptomic analysis revealed 1075 differentially expressed genes during mid-exponential phase. Notably, non-optimized conditions exhibited higher expression of key reductive tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle genes, including malate dehydrogenase (4.84-fold), fumarate reductase (2.00-fold), and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (1.55-fold), yet resulted in lower succinate titers. This inverse correlation between transcript levels and metabolic output suggests that environmental factors may influence fermentation performance potentially through mechanisms beyond transcription alone. Instead, optimized environmental conditions—particularly MgCO3 concentration and pH—appeared to support succinate biosynthesis by providing favorable biochemical conditions, including pH stability for enzyme function and sufficient CO2/HCO3⁻ availability for carboxylation reactions. These findings suggest that process optimization influences succinic acid production from lignocellulosic biomass and that transcript levels alone may be insufficient predictors of fermentation performance, pointing to a potential contribution of post-transcriptional regulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13068-026-02751-9.Keywords: Succinic acid, Agricultural waste valorization, Transcriptomics, Gene expression–productivity relationship, Bioprocess optimization

Background

Succinic acid is a four-carbon dicarboxylic acid that serves as a key intermediate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, playing a crucial role in microbial metabolism. Due to its diverse chemical properties, succinic acid is widely utilized as a platform chemical in various industrial sectors, including food and beverages, pharmaceuticals, polymers, cosmetics, inks, and paints [1]. Additionally, it acts as a precursor in the synthesis of several high-value chemicals, such as 1,4-butanediol, tetrahydrofuran, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone, 2-pyrrolidone, and γ-butyrolactone [2, 3]. Recognizing its commercial importance, the United States Department of Energy has classified succinic acid as one of the top bio-based platform chemicals, which has contributed to the increasing global demand for succinic acid. In 2022, the market for succinic acid was valued at approximately USD 158 million and is expected to reach USD 205.6 million by 2028, growing at a compound annual growth rate of 9.1%, driven by rising industrial demand [4].

Traditionally, succinic acid has been synthesized via the catalytic hydrogenation of maleic anhydride derived from petroleum-based feedstocks, which requires costly catalysts [2, 5, 6]. However, increasing concerns over environmental sustainability, fossil resource depletion, and petroleum market volatility have driven a transition toward renewable-based production [7, 8]. One promising approach is lignocellulosic biomass fermentation, which utilizes renewable resources to produce succinic acid. This sustainable approach addresses waste management challenges while creating economic value through biochemical production within agricultural systems. Lignocellulosic waste, a widely available renewable resource, is derived from various sources such as agricultural waste, forestry residues, and energy crops [4]. Among these, sugarcane leaves—often discarded as waste in sugarcane-producing regions—represent a promising feedstock for succinic acid production. In Thailand's sugarcane industry, annual cultivation generates over 18 million tons of sugarcane leaves as a by-product, which are typically not utilized and are often disposed of by burning, causing significant environmental issues [9, 10]. Sugarcane leaves contain high amounts of cellulose (35–45%) and hemicellulose (17–28%), which serve as important fermentable sugar sources [9, 11, 12]. Therefore, the use of sugarcane leaves for succinic acid production offers a sustainable, cost-effective alternative that utilizes agricultural waste, reducing environmental impact, and promoting a circular economy. However, the structure of sugarcane leaves also contains lignin, approximately 10–20%, which can hinder the breakdown of cellulose [9, 12]. Therefore, pretreatment processes are necessary to enhance the accessibility of these sugar sources for microorganisms during fermentation.

Pretreatment can be accomplished using various methods, including physical, chemical, and biological approaches [13]. In this study, a synergistic combination of three techniques—physical size reduction, alkaline pretreatment, and enzymatic hydrolysis—was employed. These processes effectively convert raw sugarcane leaves into sugarcane leaf hydrolysate (SLH), making the sugars more accessible to microorganisms [14]. This transformation facilitates the release of fermentable sugars, thereby increasing substrate availability for succinic acid production. Beyond substrate preparation, fermentation conditions play an important role in microbial activity and production performance. Recent studies have demonstrated that substrate concentration, nutrient composition, and environmental parameters significantly influence succinic acid biosynthesis. Zhong et al. [4] investigated succinic acid production from lignocellulosic hydrolysates using engineered Yarrowia lipolytica and found that substrate concentration significantly influenced production efficiency. High concentrations of undetoxified hydrolysates, particularly those containing toxic compounds such as furfural, inhibited cell growth and succinic acid synthesis. Similarly, Nagime et al. [15] studied succinic acid production from palm oil mill effluent using Enterococcus gallinarum and reported that twofold diluted palm oil mill effluent yielded the highest succinic acid concentration. Using Plackett–Burman Design (PBD), MgCO3 and peptone were identified as the most influential nutrients for efficient production [15]. Additionally, Moshokoa et al. [16] explored succinic acid production by E. faecium and demonstrated that temperature, pH, and ZnCl2⋅4H2O concentration significantly affected production. The highest succinic acid concentration (1.9 g/L) was obtained at 42 °C and 0.5% ZnCl2⋅4H2O under uncontrolled pH conditions, while no production was observed at pH 5.0 (37 °C) or pH 5.6 (42 °C). These findings underscore the importance of balancing substrate and nutrient concentrations, as well as maintaining favorable environmental conditions, to increase succinic acid production and reduce inhibitory effects.

While numerous studies have focused on optimizing production parameters, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying enhanced fermentation performance remains limited. In this context, transcriptomic analysis offers valuable tools for exploring gene expression patterns, revealing key metabolic pathways and regulatory mechanisms that drive fermentation efficiency [17, 18]. For instance, Zeng et al. [19] conducted a transcriptomic analysis of Kluyveromyces marxianus under succinic acid stress and observed substantial shifts in metabolic pathways, including repression of the TCA cycle and activation of glycolysis. Pateraki et al. [20] applied transcriptomic analysis to Actinobacillus succinogenes during succinic acid production from spent sulphite liquor and reported the upregulation of genes involved in the TCA cycle, acetyl-CoA pathways, and oxidative phosphorylation, providing a deeper understanding of metabolic responses to lignocellulosic inhibitors. Similarly, Xie et al. [21] investigated the transcriptional response of A. succinogenes under varying fermentation conditions and identified the arginine biosynthesis pathway as a key factor for growth and succinic acid production under low pH conditions. These findings demonstrate the power of transcriptomics in guiding strain improvement and fermentation optimization for enhanced bioproduction.

Among succinic acid-producing microorganisms, A. succinogenes has emerged as one of the most promising native producers due to its natural capacity for high-yield succinate biosynthesis via the reductive TCA cycle. However, transcriptomic studies on this organism remain limited to specific stress conditions. Despite these advances, no study has systematically compared transcriptomic profiles between optimized and non-optimized fermentation conditions to understand how process optimization influences succinic acid production. This knowledge gap limits our ability to rationally design improved bioprocesses.

However, previous studies have primarily focused on either process optimization or transcriptomic analysis independently, without integrating these approaches to understand the mechanistic basis of improved performance. Therefore, this study aims to investigate succinic acid production from SLH using A. succinogenes TISTR 1994 through an integrated strategy that combines systematic process optimization with comprehensive transcriptomic profiling. This comprehensive approach improves bioconversion efficiency and identifies an inverse relationship between transcript levels and metabolic output, suggesting that factors beyond transcription may influence fermentation performance, providing new insights for sustainable, biomass-based production systems.

Materials and methods

Microorganism and inoculum preparation

The bacterial strain A. succinogenes TISTR 1994 was used to produce succinic acid. This strain was obtained from the Thailand Institute of Scientific and Technological Research (TISTR), Thailand. The culture medium was prepared in 120-mL serum bottles containing 50 mL of medium with the following composition (g/L): 0.07 MnCl2, 1.5 CaCl2·2H2O, 3.3 NaH2PO4, 4.4 Na2HPO4, 2 MgCl2·6H2O, 2 urea, 30 yeast extract, and 30 MgCO3 [22]. The pH was adjusted to 7.0 ± 0.2 using 5 M NaOH or 5 M HCl. To eliminate oxygen from the headspace, all bottles were sealed with rubber stoppers and flushed with CO2 for 5 min. The culture medium and individual sugars (glucose, xylose, and arabinose) were sterilized separately at 121 °C for 15 min to prevent caramelization. After cooling to room temperature, the sugars were added to the culture medium to achieve final concentrations (g/L) of 30 glucose, 10 xylose, and 1 arabinose, corresponding to the sugar composition of SLH. The medium was inoculated with 10% (v/v) culture and incubated at 37 °C and 150 rpm for 12 h. Subsequently, a subculture was prepared by transferring 10% (v/v) of the actively growing culture into fresh medium and incubating under the same conditions for an additional 12 h. This procedure ensured that the bacterial cells were in the active exponential (mid-log) phase and metabolically active prior to use as the inoculum for succinic acid production.

Preparation of sugarcane leaf hydrolysate

Sugarcane leaves, a by-product from post-harvest sugarcane processing, were collected from fields in Khon Kaen, Thailand. The leaves were sun-dried until the moisture content was below 10% (w/w) [23], then ground into powder and sieved through a 0.5-mm mesh. The chemical composition of the sugarcane leaves consisted of 10.35% lignin, 17.49% hemicellulose, 36.69% cellulose, and 12.62% ash (all on a dry weight basis). The sugarcane leaf powder was subjected to alkaline pretreatment using 2% (w/v) NaOH at a solid loading of 10% (w/v). This was followed by enzymatic hydrolysis with cellulase (Cellic® CTec2, Novozymes, Denmark) at an enzyme loading of 15 filter paper unit per gram of sugarcane leaves and a solid loading of 5% (w/v), according to the method described by Sitthikitpanya et al. [24]. After that, the obtained SLH was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min and filtered through Whatman GF/C glass microfiber filter paper to remove suspended solids. The supernatant was then collected and used as a substrate for succinic acid production. Compositional analysis of the hydrolysate revealed a total sugar concentration of 49.73 ± 0.11 g/L, consisting of 25.90 ± 0.67 g/L glucose, 9.67 ± 0.13 g/L xylose, and 1.20 ± 0.11 g/L arabinose.

Plackett–Burman design for screening of medium components

The PBD was employed using Design-Expert software (version 13.0; Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) to evaluate the effects of individual components of the fermentation medium on succinic acid production. The screening process involved nine actual variables and two dummy variables, as the PBD requires a minimum of 11 variables. The 11 variables consisted of MgCl2·6H2O (X1), CaCl2·2H2O (X2), Na2HPO4 (X3), NaH2PO4 (X4), MnCl2 (X5), MgCO3 (X6), yeast extract (X7), urea (X8), NaCl (X9), and two dummy variables. Each variable was evaluated at two coded levels: low (− 1) and high (+ 1), as shown in Table 1.

Box–Behnken design for optimization of succinic acid production

Factors that significantly influenced succinic acid production, as identified by the PBD, were further investigated to determine their optimal concentrations using RSM based on the BBD. In addition, to account for other variables affecting succinic acid production, two process parameters—the sugar concentration of SLH and the initial pH—were also included in the optimization study. The experimental design and statistical analysis were conducted using Design-Expert software. Three independent variables were evaluated at three levels: MgCO3 concentration (X1: 15.0, 37.5, and 60.0 g/L), sugar concentration (X2: 20.0, 34.85, and 49.70 g/L), and initial pH (X3: 5.0, 7.0, and 9.0), coded as − 1, 0, and + 1, respectively (Additional file 1: Table S1). Batch fermentation for succinic acid production from sugarcane leaf hydrolysate

Batch fermentation experiments based on the PBD were initially conducted in 120-mL serum bottles containing 50 mL of SLH supplemented with varying concentrations of nutrients according to the experimental design. The initial pH was adjusted to 7.0 ± 0.2 using 5 M NaOH or 5 M HCl. The bottles were sealed with rubber stoppers and aluminum caps, purged with CO2 gas for 5 min, and sterilized at 121 °C for 15 min in an autoclave. After sterilization, the medium was inoculated with 10% (v/v) of A. succinogenes TISTR 1994 and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 48 h. Fermentation broth samples were collected before and after fermentation to determine concentrations of sugars (glucose, xylose, and arabinose), organic acids (succinic, lactic, formic, and acetic acids), and pH.

Subsequently, BBD experiments were performed under similar conditions. Each serum bottle contained SLH and MgCO3 at concentrations specified by the experimental design, with additional nutrients supplemented according to Li et al. [22]. The initial pH was adjusted to the levels defined by the BBD. Batch fermentation was conducted following the same protocol as the PBD experiment. Fermentation broths were collected before and after fermentation for analysis of pH, sugar concentrations, and organic acid profiles.

Finally, a confirmation experiment was performed under the optimal conditions predicted by the BBD. The results were compared with those obtained under non-optimized control condition (MgCO3 30.0 g/L and initial pH 7.0), as reported by Li et al. [22]. This fermentation followed the same procedure as described above. Samples were collected every 3 h to monitor concentrations of sugars, organic acids, and cell biomass, while pH values were determined before and after fermentation.

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Succinic acid yield was calculated by dividing the final succinic acid concentration by the initial total sugar concentration, which was defined as the sum of glucose, xylose, and arabinose concentrations measured at the beginning of fermentation. Succinic acid productivity was calculated by dividing the final succinic acid concentration by the fermentation time.

Analytical methods

Total solids, volatile solids, ash content, and moisture content were determined according to standard methods established by the American Public Health Association (APHA). Sample pH was measured using a calibrated pH meter (pH-500, Queen, USA). Reducing sugar and total sugar concentrations were quantified using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) and phenol–sulfuric acid methods, respectively, with glucose used as the standard. The composition and concentrations of sugars and organic acids were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Shimadzu LC-20AD system (Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an Aminex HPX-87H column (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and a refractive index detector, following the method described by Sitthikitpanya et al. [25]. Cell density was determined by measuring the optical density at 660 nm (OD660) using a UVmini-1240 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). Prior to measurement, insoluble MgCO3 particles were removed by acidifying the samples with 0.5 M HCl. Dry cell weight (DCW) was determined by centrifuging the culture broth at 5,000 rpm for 5 min, repeated three times. The resulting cell pellet was washed twice with distilled water and dried at 80 °C for 24 h until a constant weight was achieved.

RNA extraction, sequencing, and transcriptomic data analysis

A. succinogenes TISTR 1994 was cultivated under optimized (Test1: MgCO3 52.84 g/L, pH 7.94) and non-optimized (Test2: MgCO3 30.00 g/L, pH 7.00) conditions. Samples were collected at four time points: 0 h (initial), 6 h (early exponential phase), 9 h (mid-exponential phase), and 15 h (late exponential phase). These sampling points were selected based on fermentation kinetics to capture key metabolic transitions during succinic acid production. Due to resource constraints, RNA sequencing was performed on single biological samples per condition at each time point (n = 1 per condition per timepoint). However, all fermentation experiments for phenotypic characterization were performed in triplicate, validating the metabolic differences between conditions. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). RNA-Seq libraries were prepared using the NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit (New England Biolabs, USA) and the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Gold Kit (Illumina, USA). Library quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), and library concentrations were quantified by quantitative PCR (qPCR). Paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform at Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea). Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality filtering using Trimmomatic v0.38 [26]. Clean reads were aligned to the A. succinogenes 130Z reference genome (NCBI assembly name: ASM1724v1) using Bowtie (version 1.1.2). The resulting alignment files were processed using SAMtools [27], and read counts were generated using HTSeq (version 0.10.0) [28]. After filtering, a total of 2,183 genes were retained for downstream analysis. Gene expression data were normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method [29]. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using edgeR [30]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis [31] was conducted to identify pathways associated with succinic acid biosynthesis. Data visualization was carried out using Microsoft® Excel.

Results and discussion

Screening of significant medium components

The concentration of succinic acid produced ranged from 7.55 ± 0.32 to 16.19 ± 0.33 g/L, depending on the fermentation medium composition. In run 11, acetic, formic, and lactic acids were detected at concentrations of 9.62 ± 0.15, 6.29 ± 0.20, and 0.45 ± 0.04 g/L, respectively. These by-products collectively accounted for 50.3% of the total organic acid production.

The results are consistent with the research of Nagime et al. [15], which reported that MgCO3 significantly positively impacts succinic acid production from palm oil mill effluent. They found that optimizing the concentration of MgCO3 could increase succinic acid production to a maximum of 23.7 g/L. Similarly, Wu et al. [33] reported that variations in MgCO3 concentration substantially influence the fermentation process, particularly with respect to pH regulation, sugar utilization, and succinic acid biosynthesis [33]. Carbonate (CO32⁻) and bicarbonate (HCO3⁻) salts, such as MgCO3, are utilized as indirect sources of CO2 donors to increase its dissolved concentration in the fermentation broth, as gaseous CO2 has a limited solubility at 1 atm. MgCO3 helps maintain optimal pH levels during fermentation by acting as an alkaline neutralizer, preventing the inhibition of microbial activity due to excessive acidity [34]. It supplies magnesium ions (Mg2+), an essential cofactor for enzymes involved in metabolic pathways, promoting efficient microbial growth and succinic acid synthesis [15]. Additionally, the excess undissolved MgCO3 prevents cell flocculation, thus ensuring the cells are uniformly dispersed in the medium [35]. The addition of MgCO3, combined with gaseous CO2, enhances the dissolved concentrations of HCO3⁻, CO32⁻, and CO2, achieving equilibrium in the fermentation broth [34]. HCO3⁻ and CO2 can serve as co-substrates for phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) carboxylase [34], enhancing succinic acid production. Specifically, CO2 affects carbon metabolic flux and PEP carboxykinase activity, with its source and dosage influencing the efficiency and controlling the carbon flow toward succinic acid production in the TCA cycle [35, 36].

Optimization of succinic acid production

The influence of MgCO3 concentration (X1), sugar concentration (X2), initial pH (X3), and their interactions on succinic acid production were investigated using RSM with BBD. Table 4 presents the 15 experimental runs of the BBD design along with the response results, including the experimental data and the model-predicted values. The highest succinic acid concentration (17.95 ± 0.74 g/L) was obtained in Run 8 under the following conditions: 60 g/L MgCO3, 49.70 g/L sugar, and an initial pH of 7.0. Under these conditions, the model predicted a succinic acid concentration of 16.61 g/L.

A three-dimensional (3D) response surface (Fig. 1), derived from Eq. 2, was constructed to evaluate the interaction effects of the three factors on succinic acid production, with one variable fixed at its optimal level while the remaining two variables were varied within the experimental range. Figure 1a shows the effects of sugar and MgCO3 concentrations on succinic acid production, demonstrating that increasing the concentrations of sugar and MgCO3 led to higher succinic acid concentrations. The results suggest that utilizing SLH at its highest sugar concentration (49.70 g/L) did not induce substrate inhibition. This finding aligns with the study by Pakchamni et al. [1], which reported that a sugar concentration of 50 g/L was optimal for succinic acid production by A. succinogenes TISTR 1994. They observed that further increasing sugar concentration led to substrate inhibition, prolonging the lag phase as the microorganism adapted to the new environment and the high-sugar medium before entering exponential growth [1, 37].

Three-dimensional response surface plots showing interactions between key parameters affecting succinic acid production from sugarcane leaf hydrolysate: a MgCO3 and sugar concentrations, b MgCO3 concentration and initial pH, and c sugar concentration and initial pH

An excessive increase in MgCO3 concentration resulted in a decrease in succinic acid concentration (Fig. 1b). The results align with Bumyut et al. [38], who studied the impact of MgCO3 on succinic acid production by A. succinogenes. They found that 10 g/L of MgCO3 led to the highest succinic acid concentration, whereas 20 g/L reduced production. This decrease was due to increased pH from MgCO3 dissociation, which decreased CO2 solubility and converted carbon into HCO3⁻ and CO32⁻ forms, which bacteria use less efficiently [38]. Therefore, optimizing MgCO3 concentration is crucial for balancing pH and CO2 availability to maximize succinic acid production. The effect of pH on succinic acid production, as shown in Fig. 1b and c, clearly demonstrates that the highest yield was achieved within a near-neutral to slightly alkaline pH range (7.5–8.5). Excessively high and low pH levels resulted in a significant reduction in production, emphasizing the importance of carefully controlling pH to optimize succinic acid yield. The studies by Stylianou et al. [39] and Moshokoa et al. [16] highlight the impact of pH on succinic acid production by different microorganisms. Stylianou et al. [39] demonstrated that a gradual reduction of pH from 6.0 to 5.5 during fermentation enhanced succinic acid yield and concentration in Y. lipolytica, while Moshokoa et al. [16] identified pH 4.6 as the optimal condition for E. faecium. These differences underscore the significance of selecting the optimal pH according to the specific microbial strain and other fermentation conditions, such as temperature and nutrients, for maximizing succinic acid yield.

According to the model predictions, the optimal conditions for maximizing succinic acid production from SLH were achieved with MgCO3 concentration, sugar concentration, and an initial pH of 52.84 g/L, 49.68 g/L, and 7.94, respectively. Under these conditions, the predicted succinic acid concentration was 18.66 g/L. To validate the model, a confirmation experiment was performed under optimal conditions. The results indicated that succinic acid production reached 18.87 ± 0.42 g/L (Table 5), with only a 1.12% deviation from the predicted value. These findings confirm the reliability of the quadratic model in optimizing conditions for succinic acid production.

Validation of optimal conditions using sugarcane leaf hydrolysate

The metabolic profile during succinic acid fermentation from SLH using A. succinogenes TISTR 1994 is depicted in Fig. 2. The fermentation was carried out under two conditions: an optimized condition (MgCO3 52.84 g/L, initial pH 7.94) and a non-optimized condition based on Li et al. [22] (MgCO3 30.00 g/L, initial pH 7.00), to compare the effects of these parameters on the fermentation process. The primary monosaccharides—glucose, xylose, and arabinose—present in SLH were monitored throughout the fermentation process. In the optimized condition, the concentrations of glucose, xylose, and arabinose rapidly decreased after 6 h of fermentation. Xylose was completely consumed within 9 h, while glucose and arabinose were depleted by 12 h (Fig. 2a). Overall, approximately 95.3% of the initial total sugars (31.90 ± 0.94 g/L) were consumed under this optimized condition (Table 5). Notably, this value represents total sugar consumption, including sugar utilized for succinic acid production, biomass formation, and other minor by-products. This sugar consumption correlated with microbial growth and succinic acid production. Cell biomass concentration increased rapidly to 7.09 ± 0.43 g-DCW/L at 12 h and remained stable thereafter. Meanwhile, succinic acid production increased quickly during 12 h (18.52 ± 0.43 g/L) and slightly rose to a maximum of 18.87 ± 0.42 g/L at 48 h, achieving a yield of 0.59 ± 0.00 g/g and productivity of 0.39 ± 0.01 g/L/h (Table 5). These results indicate that this condition supported microbial growth and more complete substrate consumption, which was associated with succinic acid production.

Metabolic profiles of succinic acid fermentation from sugarcane leaf hydrolysate using Actinobacillus succinogenes TISTR 1994 under two conditions: a optimized (MgCO3 52.84 g/L, initial pH 7.94) and b non-optimized (MgCO3 30.00 g/L, initial pH 7.00). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from three biological replicates (n = 3)

In contrast, the non-optimized condition exhibited low microbial growth and incomplete sugar utilization (Fig. 2b). The cell biomass concentration increased to 4.61 ± 0.76 g-DCW/L at 6 h and remained stable throughout the fermentation. Arabinose was gradually consumed until 15 h, while glucose and xylose were rapidly utilized in the first 6 h and then consumed slowly. However, glucose and xylose were not completely consumed, with 4.98 ± 0.99 and 2.15 ± 0.25 g/L remaining at 48 h, respectively. Overall, only about 76.0% of the initial total sugars (32.03 ± 1.04 g/L) were consumed in this non-optimized condition (Table 5), indicating less efficient sugar utilization compared to the optimized condition. Simultaneously, succinic acid production increased rapidly until 12 h (14.26 ± 1.75 g/L) and then gradually reached a maximum of 16.81 ± 1.56 g/L at 48 h, yielding 0.54 ± 0.07 g/g and a productivity of 0.35 ± 0.03 g/L/h (Table 5). The lower succinic acid yield may be attributed to insufficient MgCO3 concentration during fermentation. MgCO3 serves as an alkaline neutralizer, maintaining optimal pH and preventing excessive acidity that inhibits microbial activity [34]. An inadequate concentration (30 g/L) failed to stabilize pH, causing it to drop from 7.00 ± 0.02 to 5.52 ± 0.26 (Table 5), which was unfavorable for succinic acid production. Moreover, since MgCO3 supplies essential Mg2⁺ ions for microbial metabolism and enhances CO2 dissolution, its insufficient concentration limits these functions, thereby reducing microbial growth and succinic acid production [15, 34].

Other fermentation by-products, including acetic acid and formic acid, were detected during succinic acid production. Under optimized conditions, high concentrations of formic acid (11.96 ± 0.23 g/L) and acetic acid (13.94 ± 0.16 g/L) were observed (Table 5), accounting for 57.86% of the total acids produced. The accumulation of these by-products suggests a diversion of carbon flux away from succinic acid biosynthesis, resulting in a reduced yield. Furthermore, the decrease in pH caused by the accumulation of these acids inhibited microbial growth and metabolic activity [40]. Lin et al. [40] reported that formate significantly inhibited succinic acid fermentation, with complete growth inhibition observed at 16 g/L. The inhibitory mechanism is attributed to chemical interference with phosphate transport, which disrupts adenosine triphosphate generation and increases the energy demand for microbial metabolism [40]. These findings highlight the impact of by-product accumulation on succinic acid fermentation and indicate a key challenge for improving process performance.

Table 6 summarizes succinic acid production from SLH in comparison with other feedstocks reported in the literature. The succinic acid concentration achieved in this study (18.87 ± 0.42 g/L) was lower than those reported in some studies, such as 44.86 g/L from sweet potato [41] and 54.20 g/L from corn straw [33]. However, the yield (0.62 ± 0.01 g/g) was within a comparable range and even higher than several studies, including 0.42 g/g from tobacco stems [42] and 0.54 g/g from sweet potato [41]. These findings suggest that SLH is a promising substrate for succinic acid production by A. succinogenes TISTR 1994. The moderate concentrations may be attributed to the limited sugar concentration of SLH and the accumulation of inhibitory by-products, particularly acetic acid (13.94 ± 0.16 g/L) and formic acid (11.96 ± 0.23 g/L), which are known to hinder succinic acid biosynthesis [40] (Additional file 1: Table S3). These limitations highlight the need for further process refinement to reduce by-product formation and improve carbon efficiency. Nevertheless, SLH, as an abundant agricultural residue, represents a cost-effective and sustainable feedstock for succinic acid production.

Global transcriptomic response to process optimization

Transcriptomic analysis of 2,183 genes revealed extensive transcriptional reprogramming under optimized versus non-optimized fermentation conditions, with the most pronounced differences occurring at mid-exponential phase (1,075 differentially expressed genes, 49.2% of analyzed genes). Notably, elevated gene expression in non-optimized conditions was associated with lower succinate production, suggesting that increased transcript abundance may reflect compensatory stress responses rather than enhanced metabolic capacity. This observation aligns with established findings that transcript levels do not necessarily predict metabolic output [48]. RNA sequencing yielded high-quality data with sequencing depths ranging from 30.0 to 49.7 million reads per sample and excellent quality metrics (Additional file 1: Table S4). After quality filtering, reads were mapped to the A. succinogenes 130Z reference genome, achieving an average mapping rate of 94.6%. From 2,218 total genes, 2,183 (98.4%) passed quality thresholds and were retained for analysis.

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between optimized (Test1: MgCO3 52.84 g/L, pH 7.94) and non-optimized (Test2: MgCO3 30.00 g/L, pH 7.00) conditions across all fermentation time points (Fig. 3a). The first two components explained 72.5% of total variation. Visual inspection of the PCA plot suggested that samples clustered primarily by fermentation condition rather than time point, indicating that MgCO3 concentration and initial pH substantially influenced the global transcriptional profile.

Differential expression analysis identified varying numbers of differentially expressed genes at each time point (Fig. 3b). Minimal differences were observed at 0 h (24 genes), increasing to 280 genes at early exponential phase (6 h). The most pronounced differences occurred at mid-exponential phase (9 h) with 1075 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), corresponding to the active growth and production phase. By late exponential phase (15 h), differentially expressed genes decreased to 902, possibly reflecting cellular adaptation to accumulated end-products and decreasing substrate availability [20]. Hierarchical clustering analysis (Fig. 3c) confirmed that samples segregated primarily by fermentation condition rather than time point.

The volcano plot visualizations (Fig. 4) provide statistical insight into gene expression changes during fermentation, showing the distribution of significantly upregulated and downregulated genes across different fermentation phases. Notable is the transition from a relatively balanced differential expression pattern in early timepoints to a highly skewed distribution at the 9 h mid-exponential phase (Fig. 4a–d).

Conversely, the general transcriptional upregulation under non-optimized conditions was observed to be uncorrelated with productivity. In the optimized state, though there was less general transcription for the important pathway genes, higher succinate concentrations (18.87 g/L vs. 16.81 g/L) and succinate productivity (0.59 g/g vs. 0.54 g/g) were achieved. From this inverse correlation between transcript levels and productivity, one may hypothesize that parameters other than gene expression might play important roles for fermentative efficiencies. However, one should take notice that this study was unable to examine protein levels and activities and/or metabolic fluxes within the cells conclusively. Below are further discussions on the responses of general pathway genes toward optimization and possible hypotheses on the observed relation between transcript expression and fermentative efficiencies.

Transcriptional regulation of succinic acid biosynthetic pathways

Reductive TCA cycle (C4 pathway) gene expression

Analysis of genes encoding the core succinate biosynthetic machinery revealed an inverse relationship between transcript levels and succinate productivity: non-optimized conditions exhibited higher expression of key pathway genes yet produced less succinate (Fig. 5, Additional file 1: Table S5). This pattern suggests a complex relationship between gene expression and metabolic output that warrants further investigation.

Integrated transcriptional landscape of central carbon metabolism in Actinobacillus succinogenes TISTR 1994. Metabolic pathway diagram with superimposed temporal gene expression profiles (0, 6, 9, 15 h) for key genes. Each gene symbol displays four data points representing log2 fold-change (Test2/Test1) at successive time points; positive values (blue-gradient) indicate higher expression in non-optimized conditions, negative values (red gradient) indicate higher expression in optimized conditions. Green-highlighted genes: C4 pathway (succinate biosynthesis); red-highlighted genes: C3 pathways (mixed-acid fermentation)

Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (pckA) catalyzes the carboxylation of phosphoenolpyruvate to oxaloacetate. Its expression was altered over time, depending on the conditions: 1.27-fold at 0 h (p = 0.48), 1.28-fold at 6 h (p = 0.15), a 1.55-fold upregulation at 9 h (p < 0.01), then a shift to −1.36-fold at 15 h (p = 0.04) when comparing non-optimized to optimized setups. The mid-exponential increase in non-optimized conditions might indicate some cellular response to cope with the reduced MgCO3 concentration (30.00 g/L vs. 52.84 g/L), thereby lowering CO2/HCO3− levels. However, this increase in succinate production remained lower even though pckA transcript levels were higher, suggesting that the simple elevation of pckA transcription was not sufficient to remove the substrate limitation imposed by the reduced CO2 availability.

Malate dehydrogenase (mdh) exhibited the highest differential expression among the metabolic genes analyzed in this work and its progressive increase under non-optimized conditions reached 1.37-fold at 0 h (p = 0.29), 1.29-fold at 6 h (p = 0.14), a steep 4.84-fold at 9 h (p < 0.01), and 2.22-fold at 15 h (p < 0.01). This constitutes the greatest fold-change observed for core metabolic genes in this work at the 9 h mark. Notably, mdh transcript abundance moved inversely with succinate productivity. The pronounced upregulation of mdh (4.84-fold) under non-optimized conditions, which experienced a greater pH decline (final pH 5.52 vs. 6.87), is consistent with acid stress responses reported in A. succinogenes. Omwene et al. [49] demonstrated that cell growth significantly decreases below pH 6.0, with bacterial activity ceasing below pH 5.3, indicating the organism's sensitivity to acidic conditions. More recently, Xie et al. [21] conducted transcriptomic analysis and reported that A. succinogenes exhibits significant transcriptional reprogramming under low pH conditions, with the arginine biosynthesis pathway playing a key role in acid tolerance. The coordinated upregulation of reductive TCA cycle genes (mdh, frdA, pckA) under non-optimized conditions may represent a compensatory response to maintain redox balance and sustain ATP production under acid stress, rather than indicating enhanced productive metabolism.

Fumarate reductase (frdA), which catalyzes the final reduction of fumarate to succinate, was upregulated 2.00-fold in non-optimized conditions at 9 h (p < 0.01). This coordinated increase extended to other frd subunits (frdA: 2.00-fold, frdC: 2.66-fold, frdD: 2.31-fold), consistent with the operon structure of the frdABCD gene cluster, which has been characterized in related bacteria [50, 51].

Regarding this gene, as is the case for other C4 pathway genes, higher frdA expression under non-optimized conditions did not translate to more succinate production. This suggests transcript levels were not the bottleneck in these conditions for succinate formation, though confirming this would require direct enzyme activity measurements.

Competing fermentation pathways (C3 branch) and carbon flux redistribution

Pyruvate kinase (pyk), which catalyzes the conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate, was significantly downregulated (−2.36-fold) in non-optimized conditions at 9 h (p < 0.01). This downregulation may reflect attempted redirection of carbon flux from the C3 pyruvate pathway toward the C4 succinate pathway under stress conditions.

The aldehyde–alcohol dehydrogenase (adhE) gene, encoding a bifunctional acetaldehyde-CoA/alcohol dehydrogenase, exhibited 7.18-fold higher expression in non-optimized conditions at 9 h (p < 0.01). This transcriptional upregulation corresponded with higher ethanol accumulation: 0.10 ± 0.04 g/L under non-optimized conditions compared to 0.05 ± 0.01 g/L under optimized conditions (Additional file 1: Table S3).

Acetate pathway enzymes including phosphate acetyltransferase (pta, −2.83-fold) and acetate kinase (ackA, −2.97-fold) were significantly downregulated in non-optimized conditions at 9 h (p < 0.01). This coordinated downregulation suggests transcriptional repression attempting to minimize carbon loss to acetate. However, substantial acetate accumulation occurs in both conditions (13.94 g/L vs. 6.08 g/L), indicating that post-transcriptional mechanisms may override transcriptional control in determining actual pathway activity.

D-Lactate dehydrogenase (dld) exhibited contrasting temporal regulation between conditions: 3.74-fold upregulation in non-optimized conditions at 9 h (p < 0.01), but −9.86-fold at 15 h (p < 0.01). This shift may reflect changing metabolic states during fermentation, though the specific regulatory mechanisms remain to be determined. Notably, lactate was not detected in either condition (Table 5), suggesting that dld transcript levels did not translate to lactate accumulation, possibly due to post-transcriptional regulation or substrate availability.

Systems-level integration: transcriptional patterns and metabolic reality

The transcript analysis showed that there was a consistent trend wherein non-optimized conditions triggered higher expression for both C4 (succinate-producing) and several C3 (by-product-producing) gene pathway expressions while obtaining lower succinate amounts and yields. This observation suggests that transcript abundance alone may not reliably predict metabolic output, consistent with previous findings that mRNA levels often correlate poorly with protein abundance and metabolic flux [52].

There may be several reasons for this trend. Firstly, environmental conditions, specifically pH and CO2 availability, can influence enzyme function irrespective of gene expression levels. The reduced final pH under non-optimized conditions (5.52 compared with 6.87) may impair the activity of pH-sensitive enzymes, as previous studies have shown that A. succinogenes cell growth significantly decreases below pH 6.0 and bacterial activity ceases below pH 5.3 [49, 53].

Secondly, the availability of substrate can impede pathway activity irrespective of the amount of enzymes; the reduced MgCO3 content implies lower availability of CO2/HCO3− for carboxylation reactions that form the C4 cycle. Thirdly, higher gene expression for non-optimized conditions may be a response to a stress stimulus that uses more cellular resources without any significant enhancement of metabolism.

This analysis is limited to transcriptomics data alone. Furthermore, the transcriptomic analysis should be interpreted with caution due to the lack of biological replicates for RNA-seq. While phenotypic differences between conditions were validated with triplicate fermentation experiments, the differential expression patterns require independent validation, ideally through RT-qPCR of key genes or additional RNA-seq replicates. Verification of these hypotheses would also necessitate experiments involving protein levels, enzymatic activities, intracellular metabolite pools, and flux measurements employing isotope labeling methods.

The estimated C4/C3 carbon ratio was found to be lower under optimized conditions (0.88) than non-optimized conditions (1.70). This apparently counterintuitive result—lower C4/C3 ratio despite higher succinate production—can be explained by distinguishing between relative carbon distribution and absolute product formation. Under optimized conditions, substantially higher substrate consumption (95.3% vs. 76.0%) provided more total carbon for metabolism, resulting in higher absolute amounts of both succinate (18.87 g/L vs. 16.81 g/L) and by-products (formate: 11.96 g/L vs. 6.08 g/L; acetate: 13.94 g/L vs. 6.08 g/L). Thus, while the proportion of carbon directed to C4 relative to C3 pathways was lower, the absolute flux through the C4 pathway was higher under optimized conditions.

This may be attributed to several factors: [1] higher substrate utilization under optimized conditions provided additional carbon for all metabolic processes; [2] higher MgCO3 levels might sustain metabolism through both C4 and C3 pathways; and [3] ATP generation through by-product formation (e.g., via acetate kinase) might support cellular metabolism and indirectly enhance succinate production.

However, the impact of carbon distribution on succinate production is complex and such observations would be more reliable if validated with direct flux analysis. Also consistent with higher overall metabolic activity is the increased biomass concentration supported under optimized conditions (7.09 g-DCW/L vs. 4.61 g-DCW/L). However, direct analysis for intracellular ATP would be necessary for validating impact on energy metabolism.

Conclusions

This study suggests that environmental optimization influences succinic acid production from sugarcane leaf hydrolysate through mechanisms that may extend beyond transcriptional regulation. Under optimized conditions (52.84 g/L MgCO3, 49.68 g/L sugar, pH 7.94), succinic acid production reached 18.87 g/L with a yield of 0.59 g/g and productivity of 0.39 g/L/h. Transcriptomic analysis revealed an inverse relationship between gene expression and succinate production: non-optimized conditions exhibited higher expression of key reductive TCA cycle genes (mdh, frdA, pckA) yet achieved lower succinate titers. This observation suggests that elevated transcript abundance does not necessarily correspond to higher metabolic output, and that optimized environmental parameters—stable pH and adequate CO2/HCO3⁻ availability—may support succinate biosynthesis independent of transcriptional induction. Furthermore, optimized conditions achieved higher absolute succinate production despite a lower estimated C4/C3 carbon ratio (0.88 vs. 1.70), demonstrating that enhanced substrate utilization (95.3% vs. 76.0%) can be more determinative of final product titers than relative pathway flux distribution. This finding suggests that bioprocess optimization strategies should prioritize overall metabolic efficiency over selective pathway enhancement. Future work should address by-product accumulation (acetate: 13.94 g/L; formate: 11.96 g/L) to improve carbon efficiency, and employ proteomics and 13C-metabolic flux analysis to validate the post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms suggested by this study.
