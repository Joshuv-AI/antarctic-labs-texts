Diabetes Mellitus in Post-renal Transplant and Nephrotic Syndrome Children on Tacrolimus: (Case Report)
Authors: Mastaneh Moghtaderi, Soodeh S. Moghadam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pathogenesis of post-transplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM) in renal transplantation is not well established, but both IR and insulin deficiency are necessary for the appearance of PTDM. β-cell dysfunction is not the only player in the pathogenesis of diabetes; insulin resistance (IR) is also a risk factor for the development of T2DM in the general population. Generally, IR is highly prevalent in renal transplant recipients. Calcineurin inhibitors are still the mainstay of immunosuppression and can decrease insulin release from pancreatic β-cells. Changing tacrolimus to Cyclosporine to improve glucose metabolism in stable renal transplant recipients has been suggested in much of the previous literature. In studies comparing the efficacy of tacrolimus and Ciclosporin in preventing acute rejection and graft loss, tacrolimus has always been superior. However, the majority of studies comparing the two drugs reported higher incidents of PTDM with tacrolimus than Ciclosporin.

Introduction

Post-transplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM) is a frequent and serious complication after renal transplantation. It does not occur immediately after transplant but may appear at different times. PTDM implies the exclusion of diabetes mellitus (DM) before transplantation and appearance of diabetes occurs only after transplantation.[1234] It is a complex and multifactorial disease that resembles T2DM in several aspects. PTDM does not occur in all renal transplant recipients but is a significant complication and can affect graft survival and increase the risk of sepsis and cardiovascular events.[5] The prevalence of PTDM varies from 20% to 30% at different stages after transplantation.[1] There are some studies indicating PTDM occurs most frequently during the first year after transplantation.[56] The diagnostic criteria of PTDM are the same as diabetes in otherwise healthy individuals including for diabetes are (a) fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL (7 mmol/L) in more than one instance, (b) random glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) (c) 2-hour plasma glucose of ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) during an oral glucose-tolerance test, and (d) hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c) ≥6.5%.[7] Its pathogenesis involves genetic, environmental, and physiological factors. Some conditions increase the risk of post-transplant diabetes including high BMI, pre-diabetic state and calcineurin inhibitors use. Risk factors for PTDM have been classified as modifiable and non-modifiable. Pre-transplant non-modifiable risk factors are obesity (BMI >30), pre-diabetes state, and genetics. Post-transplant modifiable risk factors are steroids and immunosuppressive drugs. Other modifiable risk factors include CMV, Hepatitis C virus (HCV), and Hypomagnesemia. Non-modifiable risk factors include a family history of diabetes mellitus, polycystic renal disease (PCKD), and ethnicity. Modifiable risk factors have a significant role in the development of PTDM.[68] Immunosuppressives remain the major modifiable risk factor for the development of PTDM.[1] The diabetogenic role of calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) is well-described and more important for tacrolimus than cyclosporine. In contrast to calcineurin inhibitors and mTOR inhibitors, no link has been established between the use of antiproliferative agents such as mycophenolate mofetil or azathioprine and the risk of PTDM.[9] Calcineurin (CNI) is a protein expressed in β-cells of the pancreas. The effect of CNIs on insulin secretion seems to be dose and time-dependent. This effect seems to disappear with chronic exposure and lower TAC concentrations.[8] Moreover, the diabetogenic effect of TAC also depends on the presence of pre-existence of associated risk factors such as obesity and metabolic syndrome. Management of PTDM is a multidisciplinary approach that includes the change in lifestyle, proper selection of immunosuppressive regimens, early insulin initiation, or other oral anti-diabetic drugs.[7]

Case Report 1

A 14-year-old girl with renal failure due to neurogenic bladder received a deceaseddonor renal transplant. The patient received induction therapy with ATG and Methylprednisolone and then Maintenance therapy with Tacrolimus, Cellcept, and Prednisolone. Two weeks after starting the treatment, the patient developed hyperglycemia, and FBS in two measuring times was ≥126 mg/dL. Her BMI at this time was 30 kg/m2. Metformin was started for the patient and tacrolimus was changed to cyclosporine. During the follow-up, the patient’s blood sugar returned to normal and metformin was gradually tapered and stopped.

Case Report 2

A 14-year-old girl underwent a renal transplant due to ARPKD and underwent induction treatment with ATG and Methylprednisolone and maintenance therapy with Prednisolone, Cellcept, and Tacrolimus. One week after starting the treatment, the patient had tachypnea and polyuria, in lab tests performed, hyperglycemia with a blood sugar of 700, metabolic acidosis (PH = 7.27/PCO2 = 31/HCO3 = 14.6), glucosuria (+3), and ketonuria (+3) were detected. The patient was diagnosed with diabetic ketoacidosis and started on a fixed rate of intravenous insulin infusion, as well as IV fluids according to the hospital’s DKA guidelines. After getting out of the acute phase of the disease and resolvent acidosis, she was started on a basal-bolus insulin regimen, with Lantus and Novorapid. Also, tacrolimus was changed to Cyclosporine. After three months of treatment, the patient’s blood sugar normalized and the patient’s insulin was gradually tapered and finally stopped at all.

Case Report 3

A 13-year-old girl with steroid-depenent nephrotic syndrome received prednisolone and tacrolimus for two years. Renal pathology reported diffuse mesangial proliferation and her urea and creatinine were in normal range. Her BMI was 28. Recently she had recurrency, so we decided to increase the tacrolimus dosage while serum tacrolimus level was controlled. She was admitted due to nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and polyuria. Lab tests revealed a blood sugar of 530, metabolic acidosis (PH = 7.23/PCO2 = 29.7/HCO3 = 12), prerenal azotemia (BUN = 43, Cr = 1.5), and also glucosuria and ketonuria. The patient had been diagnosed with diabetic ketoacidosis and was managed with insulin drip and fluid therapy according to therapeutic guidelines. Tacrolimus was switched to Cyclosporine. After the stabilization of vital signs and when she had oral tolerance, she received subcutaneous insulin injections. During the follow-up, blood sugar was checked closely. Four months after starting insulin, her insulin was gradually tapered and then stopped.

Discussion

PTDM is a known side effect in transplant recipients on immunosuppressant drugs, such as CNIs which are the mainstay of immunosuppression and decrease insulin release from pancreatic beta cells. In this category of immunosuppressive drugs, tacrolimus is more diabetogenic than cyclosporine.[5] Similar patient and graft survival rates were reported by these two drugs despite higher PTDM incidence when compared to cyclosporine.[89] As in our experiences, IDDM might occur in every patient who received calcineurin inhibitors and there should be awarenes of it in every case specially during the first year of transplantation. All of our cases had reversible diabetes mellitus after receiving tacrolimus. Two of them were obese and one of them had steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome and received increased doses of tacrolimus due to recurrence. Repeated evaluation of FBS and HbA1c and awareness and close monitoring of signs and symptoms of diabetes are very crucial in patients who receive immunosuppressive drugs specifically calcineurin inhibitors. In the management of PTDM, lifestyle modification including dietary advice, an increase in physical activity, and weight loss in allograft renal recipients was reported to facilitate the reversal of impaired glucose tolerance to normal glucose tolerance within 6-months in a nonrandomized study.[5] Our patients were followed up for 6-18 months when no one had signs and symptoms of diabetes so far.
