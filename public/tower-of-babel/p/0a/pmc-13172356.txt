Autism-associated learning patterns show reduced credit assignment to outcome-irrelevant features
Authors: Ido Ben-Artzi, Liron Rozenkrantz, Nitzan Shahar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHumans often make decisions based on information that is irrelevant to actual outcomes, a tendency widely documented in behavioral economics and cognitive psychology as a hallmark of biased or suboptimal learning. While prior research has shown that individuals with autism may be less susceptible to such biases in structured, static decision-making contexts, it remains unclear whether this resistance generalizes to more complex, dynamic learning environments. In this pre-registered adversarial collaboration, we used a reinforcement learning task known to reflect outcome-irrelevant learning, where irrelevant stimulus features (e.g., spatial location) are systematically assigned with value, resulting in suboptimal decisions. This design allowed us to test two competing hypotheses. The first posited that autistic individuals may engage more in value updating of irrelevant features, due to increased susceptibility to process irrelevant information. The second, based on an enhanced rationality framework, predicted that autistic individuals would show greater resistance to outcome-irrelevant learning and demonstrate more optimal learning compared to non-autistic individuals. Computational modeling and model-agnostic analyses revealed that participants reporting a clinical autism diagnosis, compared to non-autistic participants, exhibited substantially reduced outcome-irrelevant learning. Importantly, reduced outcome-irrelevant learning was also significantly correlated with higher autistic traits across the full population sample, suggesting that it reflects a broader, dimensional cognitive profile rather than only a categorical group difference. These findings demonstrate, for the first time, enhanced resistance to outcome-irrelevant learning biases in autism, revealing a domain in which autistic cognition may confer advantages for rational decision-making in noisy environments. Our results add to growing evidence for domain-specific cognitive advantages in autism and extend to suggest that this cognitive style, observed across the entire cohort, may offer a more adaptive approach to managing biases in decision-making.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Autism spectrum disorders

Introduction

Accurately assigning credit to relevant features of an action, based on known causal associations, is essential for effective learning and decision-making. In many real-world scenarios, however, individuals might struggle to ignore salient but irrelevant features that can bias this process. For example, after enjoying the taste of ice cream, one might mistakenly associate the positive experience not only with the flavor or texture, but also with the color of the spoon or the specific table at the ice cream shop in which it was consumed. The integration of outcome-irrelevant information into decision-making has been extensively documented in behavioral economics and cognitive psychology, where classic decision-making biases demonstrate how irrelevant cues can distort value-based behavior [1–3]. These biases, which hamper our ability to maximize rewards by prioritizing the wrong cues, pose a significant challenge in dynamic learning environments. More broadly, the tendency to integrate outcome-irrelevant information into decisions is one key hallmark of what is often termed “irrationality”, a deviation from optimal, bias-resistant reasoning. From this perspective, outcome-irrelevant learning offers a mechanistic window into one specific component of human irrationality.

Evidence from the autism literature suggests that individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) may be less susceptible to several cognitive biases, including those driven by outcome-irrelevant information. Across multiple studies, individuals with autism have shown reduced susceptibility to framing effects, sunk cost fallacies, and multiple other well-documented biases arising from salient but irrelevant features [4–13]. These findings have contributed to the view that autistic cognition may be characterized by “enhanced rationality”, namely, enhanced selectivity for task-relevant information and diminished susceptibility to misleading contextual cues. However, this literature has primarily focused on static decision-making tasks, such as choosing between fixed options or evaluating predefined scenarios. What remains underexplored is how individuals with autism engage with biases in more dynamic, real-time learning environments, where outcome-irrelevant information may continuously influence the process of learning and deciding based on previous experience.

Several reviews examining reinforcement learning (RL) in autistic individuals have found overall performance to be similar between autistic and non-autistic individuals [14–17]. This is further supported by meta-analyses showing no differences between autistic and non-autistic individuals in implicit learning tasks (e.g., the serial reaction time and contextual cueing tasks), indicating that autistic individuals are able to learn latent environmental regularities through experience [18, 19]. However, these studies did not assess the ability to ignore outcome-irrelevant features that may bias learning and decision-making. In real-world environments, successful learning often requires not only detecting regularities but also filtering out information that is not causally related to outcomes. Failure to do so can lead to suboptimal credit assignment, where irrelevant features are mistakenly associated with rewards. Thus, while prior work demonstrates intact learning in autism, it remains an open question whether autistic individuals differ from non-autistic individuals in their susceptibility to such distraction by outcome-irrelevant action features.

Here, we address this gap by directly testing outcome-irrelevant learning in individuals who provided an online self-disclosure of a clinical ASD diagnosis using a reinforcement learning task specifically designed to dissociate learning from relevant versus irrelevant features. Our group has previously demonstrated a robust and replicable tendency for outcome-irrelevant learning in the general population [20–24]. Notably, this tendency persists even when participants are explicitly instructed about which features are relevant and after prolonged practice with the task [25]. These findings suggest that outcome-irrelevant associations are not easily suppressed and may reflect a default learning bias in typical cognition. Such associations introduce noise into decision-making and ultimately impair reward maximization [26].

In the current study, we report a pre-registered adversarial collaboration, addressing the question of how individuals who provided an online self-disclosure of a clinical ASD diagnosis engage with biases in dynamic, real-time learning environments. We employed a task designed to assess whether outcome-irrelevant information influences learning and decision-making, and examined performance in individuals with and without autism to evaluate two competing hypotheses.

The first is the cognitive-control hypothesis, which predicts increased outcome-irrelevant learning in autistic individuals compared to non-autistic individuals. The cognitive control hypothesis builds on prior findings showing reduced cognitive control abilities and executive functions deficits in autism [27–33]. Executive functions refer to a set of cognitive control processes that enable individuals to maintain task goals actively in mind, resist interference from distracting or irrelevant information, and flexibly adapt behavior in response to changing contexts [34–37]. Several studies suggest that individuals with autism exhibit reduced cognitive control, as evidenced by increased processing of irrelevant stimuli, such as flankers surrounding a target (Flanker task [38]). Differences have also been observed in working memory abilities among individuals with ASD, with meta-analytic evidence indicating reduced accuracy during tasks requiring the updating and manipulation of information [39]. Working memory plays a critical role in the regulation of attention and the suppression of task-irrelevant information, as it actively maintains task representations in the face of distraction [40, 41]. Thus, reduced working memory abilities in ASD may exacerbate difficulties in shielding goal-relevant information from competing, irrelevant stimuli [34]. Building on prior findings that outcome-irrelevant learning is elevated in individuals with lower working memory capacity [20], the present evidence supports the hypothesis that individuals with ASD may exhibit heightened outcome-irrelevant learning tendencies.

The second is the enhanced rationality hypothesis, which predicts reduced outcome-irrelevant learning in autistic individuals compared to non-autistic individuals. This hypothesis builds on evidence suggesting that individuals with ASD are less prone to certain cognitive biases and are more likely to rely on rule-based, analytical processing. Theoretical support comes from the Dual Process Theory, which proposes that human decision-making is shaped by a tension between intuitive, heuristic-based responses (System 1) and slower, more deliberate reasoning (System 2) [42, 43]. According to this view, enhanced rationality in autism may stem from a diminished influence of intuitive processes and a greater reliance on deliberative, task-relevant information processing [13]. Empirical findings support this view, individuals with autism display reduced susceptibility to cognitive biases such as those introduced by irrelevant framing, sunk cost, and decoy options [5, 6, 9]. Moreover, individuals with higher autistic traits have been found to rely more on logical reasoning and less on intuitive or affectively driven judgments [4, 44, 45], but also see [46]. These findings suggest that ASD may be associated with a cognitive style characterized by enhanced selectivity for task-relevant input and resistance to misleading contextual cues [44]. In the context of our outcome-irrelevant learning measure, this hypothesis predicts that individuals with autism will be more likely to ignore or suppress learning from salient but outcome-irrelevant features, such as stimulus location, and assign credit more accurately to the actual reward-predictive information. Importantly, we do not equate reduced outcome-irrelevant learning with rationality in a broad sense. Rather, we view it as one potential mechanism that may support more bias-resistant learning under specific conditions. This cognitive framework, therefore, underpins our second hypothesis, which predicts that autistic individuals will show reduced outcome-irrelevant learning tendencies compared to non-autistic individuals.

The prediction of reduced outcome-irrelevant learning is further supported by theoretical work examining how prior information shapes learning in autism [47–49]. Within the predictive-coding framework, perception and learning arise from the integration of prior expectations with incoming evidence, weighted by their relative precision [50, 51]. Empirical work suggests that autism may be characterized by attenuated reliance on priors, and this perspective has been used to account for a broad range of autistic characteristics, including sensory sensitivities, cognitive rigidity, and differences in social communication. Extending this perspective, we previously proposed that predictive mechanisms also shape higher-order cognition, influencing decision-making and reasoning more generally [52]. In reinforcement learning tasks, participants are often given explicit instructions designed to induce an accurate representation of the current environment (e.g., “ignore the locations of the cards when deciding”). Often, such instructions are meant to override prior tendencies that may have been formed due to broad, real-world regularities, suggesting the opposite (i.e., locations often tend to be of high relevance). Accordingly, previous work on outcome-irrelevant learning has demonstrated that even when participants are clearly instructed that card locations are entirely random, they nonetheless assign value to these locations, resulting in reduced gains [25]. One likely explanation is that the privileged status of spatial location as a highly salient feature in natural environments makes it difficult to suppress even when explicitly instructed as irrelevant [22, 53]. Consistent with this view, studies have shown that people struggle to inhibit the representation of spatial information even when it has no relevance for the task at hand [54, 55]. From this perspective, prior experience where locations are typically crucial for decisions, can not be easily overridden even by explicit instructions and evidence supporting their irrelevance - leading individuals to assign them credit for no adequate reason. Given the tendency of individuals with autism to rely less on prior information, this leads to the prediction that they would exhibit reduced outcome-irrelevant learning.

To test these two opposing hypotheses, which offer distinct accounts of learning in autism, one informed by limitations of executive control and the other by strengths in information-processing selectivity, we recruited 154 participants (75 individuals who self-reported having a clinical ASD diagnosis, hereafter referred to as ASDSR, and 79 individuals who self-reported not having an ASD diagnosis, labeled henceforward non-ASDSR). Participants completed a multi-armed bandit decision-making task specifically designed to dissociate outcome-relevant from outcome-irrelevant learning. By applying a trial-by-trial reinforcement learning computational model to a task previously shown to elicit robust outcome-irrelevant learning, we aimed to test which of these cognitive frameworks better accounts for autistic behavior. Our results provide compelling evidence that autism is associated with reduced outcome-irrelevant learning: while non-autistic individuals consistently misattributed reward to irrelevant action features, ASDSR individuals effectively inhibited such learning. This was further supported by a negative correlation between self-reported autistic traits, measured via the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ [56]), and outcome-irrelevant learning tendencies, extending the effect to subclinical variation in autistic traits. Using a computational reinforcement learning model, we describe a suboptimal credit assignment process prevalent in the general population but notably reduced in individuals who reported having an ASD diagnosis.

Results

We investigated whether individuals who provided an online self-disclosure of a clinical ASD diagnosis exhibit heightened or diminished tendencies for outcome-irrelevant learning compared to non-autistic adults. Participants were recruited online via Prolific and screened based on self-reported diagnostic status. We then followed a preregistered online verification procedure to validate a clinical ASD diagnosis, building upon protocols used in prior large-scale online studies (see Methods and Appendix A for details) [46, 57–62]. Because participants reported a prior clinical diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder but were not directly assessed within the study, this group is referred to as self-reported ASD (ASDSR). To assess outcome-irrelevant learning, participants completed a modified version of a multi-armed bandit task. In each trial, participants were offered two out of four arms (indicated by abstract shapes). After selecting one of the two offered shapes, participants observed a monetary outcome (reward in the form of a golden coin, + 1 or no reward, + 0; see Fig. 1A). These play coins were later converted into a monetary bonus. Each arm had a reward probability that slowly drifted over time according to a random walk and was unknown to participants (see Fig. 1B). Participants were asked to follow outcome observations in order to make choices that would lead them to obtain rewards more frequently. Critically, the computer randomized the positions of the shapes on the screen (left or right) on every trial, making location irrelevant for predicting outcomes. Importantly, participants were explicitly instructed to base their choices solely on outcome-relevant features (i.e., the shape) and to disregard outcome-irrelevant features (e.g., the location of the shapes on the screen). To ensure comprehension, all participants completed a quiz verifying their understanding of the task instructions before proceeding. Consequently, we labeled credit assigned to the shapes as “outcome-relevant learning” and credit assigned to the locations as “outcome-irrelevant learning”, since by design, assigning value to the spatial-motor components in this task could only reduce performance.

Task description.

A Participants completed a modified multi-armed bandit task in which two out of four arms (indicated by abstract shapes) were randomly presented on each trial. Importantly, the locations of the shapes (i.e., left vs. right) were randomized in each trial, a detail clearly conveyed to participants through detailed instructions and a designated quiz. Thus, any credit assigned to the location, rather than the shape itself, was classified as outcome-irrelevant learning. B Selecting a shape resulted in a binary outcome (reward: +1, or no reward: 0), determined by reward probabilities that drifted slowly and randomly across the 50 trials in each block. The random walk required participants to continually update their learning throughout the block. Each block featured four new shapes with unique reward probabilities, counterbalanced across participants to control for order effects.

Computational modeling

We tested separately for each group (ASDSR vs Non-ASDSR) two nested models, with and without outcome-irrelevant learning components, to identify the minimal set of parameters that best predict choice behavior. In a full model, which included both outcome-relevant and irrelevant learning, we specified a latent value for each shape and location. We followed well-established feature-based learning algorithms in which, on each trial, the value of each feature is updated independently using a feature-specific prediction error rule [63–65]. That is, the value of each selected shape and location was updated separately based on the difference between the observed outcome and the feature-specific expected value. Importantly, the model included a λ free parameter that controlled the influence of outcome-irrelevant location values on choice behavior. Specifically, to estimate the value of the two offers, which included shape-location combinations, the model integrated the values across the two features. This was done by a weighted sum of both features, controlled by the λ parameter that ranged from 0 to 1. Here, a λ value of 1 indicated that the integrated value relied entirely on the value of the offered shape, a λ value of 0.5 reflected equal weighting of shape and location, and a λ value of 0 meant choices were based solely on location values. Since λ was allowed to freely change between 0 and 1, λ captured how much participants relied on outcome-relevant (shapes) versus outcome-irrelevant (location) values when making value-based choices. We further designed a reduced model that was identical to the full model, with the only difference that only outcome-relevant learning was accounted for, and the free parameter λ was fixed to 1.

Model validation

Both the full and reduced models were designed using a hierarchical structure accounting for both population and individual-level parameters, and estimated using Bayesian posterior MCMC estimation in Stan [66]. To meet required benchmarks for computational modeling, we performed three simulation tests adhering to established best-practice guidelines before continuing to examine the empirical results [67]. First, we completed a model recovery analysis that suggested excellent recovery properties for the full vs. reduced model (see Methods). Second, we estimated the parameter recovery of each parameter in the full model. We found excellent recovery on both population and individual level for all parameters (correlations between true and recovered parameters were all 0.7 or above; see Methods and Fig. 6). Third, we demonstrated that the λ parameter is tightly associated with a model-agnostic regression analysis that can be performed on a subset of trials without having to make mechanistic assumptions. Here, we take only pairs of trials where two different shapes were offered on each trial (e.g., A and B offered at trial n, C and D offered on trial n + 1; see Fig. 1). We estimate the influence of the previous outcome (unrewarded vs. rewarded) on the probability of staying with a previously chosen location. Since the previously selected shape is not reoffered in trial n + 1, any influence of previous reward on location stay probability is due to outcome-irrelevant learning. We found that λ was tightly associated with this effect, such that lower λ values (more outcome-irrelevant learning) led in model simulations to stronger previous-outcome effects in the regression analysis. Therefore, all benchmark tests were successful, showing excellent model recovery, parameter recovery, and an identified model-agnostic signature for outcome-irrelevant learning.

Group differences in outcome-irrelevant learning

We next estimated the goodness-of-fit for the full model (including outcome-irrelevant learning) and the reduced model (without outcome-irrelevant learning) on empirical data by calculating the leave-one-out predictive choice accuracy for each group and model. In the non-ASDSR group, we found a > 99.99% chance that the λ-model will outperform the reduced model when predicting new data (Non-ASDSR elpd difference = 66.3, SE = 12.4), and a 98.5% chance in favor of the full model in the ASDSR group (ASDSR elpd difference = 15.9, SE = 7.3 [68, 69]). We note that despite being close to 100%, the result indicates that the chance that the full model would outperform the reduced model in predicting new data was in fact ~300,000 times bigger in the non-ASDSR compared to the ASDSR group (1 to 22,337,484 chance in the non-ASDSR group compared with 1 to 68 chance in the ASDSR group that the full model will outperform the reduced model). This means that the full model provides better choice predictions in both groups, yet the added predictive value of the outcome-irrelevant learning component is much more pronounced for the non-ASDSR group. We next examined the posterior distrbution for the λ parameter for each group in the hierarchical model which also suggested smaller involvment of outcome-irrelevant learning in the non-ASDSR compared to the ASDSR group demonstrated in higher λ values for the ASDSR group parameter (λ posterior median = 0.96, HDI95% [0.90, 0.99]) compared to that of the non-ASDSR group (λ posterior median = 0.89, HDI95% [0.82, 0.96]; λ group difference posterior median = 0.06, HDI95% [−0.02, 0.15], pd = 93%; see Fig. 2A).

To further demonstrate the greater influence of outcome-irrelevant learning on non-ASDSR participants’ choices, we fitted a Bayesian hierarchical logistic regression model predicting choices based on the posterior estimated location Q-values. Specifically, for each trial, we predict whether participants will choose the shape located on the left or the shape located on the right, based on the estimated Q-value difference between the right and left locations. We found the influence of the difference in location Q-values on choice was greater in non-ASDSR individuals compared to individuals with autism (interaction posterior median = −0.18, HDI95% [−0.41, −0.01], pd = 98%; see Fig. 2C-D).

Group differences in outcome-irrelevant learning were found to be robust when controlling for individual differences in working memory ability and an intelligence test score. Specifically, we fitted a linear regression predicting each participant’s posterior mean λ based on group (ASDSR vs. Non-ASDSR), IQ, and working memory capacity. We found that the group effect persisted above and beyond these covariates (posterior median = 0.07, HDI95% [0.02, 0.12], pd = ~100%). This suggests that the increased outcome-irrelevant learning in the ASDSR group cannot be fully explained by differences in IQ or working memory capacity.

Overall, computational modeling results indicate a smaller influence of outcome-irrelevant location values on the choice of ASDSR group participants compared to non-ASDSR individuals.

Model-agnostic regression signature

To further demonstrate this result, we evaluated the outcome-irrelevant learning model-agnostic regression signature on empirical data. Specifically, we analyzed trials where the two shapes offered in the previous trial (trial n-1) were not reoffered in the current trial (trial n). Increased tendency to repeat the selected location following a rewarded compared to an unrewarded outcome in the previous trial can be considered a behavioral signature for outcome-irrelevant learning (see Fig. 1A; [20, 26]). We performed a hierarchical Bayesian logistic regression, where previous-outcome, group, and their interaction predicted the probability of repeating location selection from trial n to n + 1. In alignment with our computational findings, non-ASDSR participants demonstrated sensitivity to the previous outcome when making their location choice, showing a higher probability of repeating the same location choice following rewarded (49%) compared to unrewarded (45%) trials even though two different shapes than before were offered (posterior median difference = 0.19, HDI95% = [0.02, 0.36], probability of direction = 99%; see Fig. 3B-C). Conversely, in the ASDSR group, participants’ probability of repeating the same location choice was nearly identical following rewarded (46%) and unrewarded trials (46%; posterior median difference = −0.03, HDI95% = [−0.20, 0.15], pd = 63%; see Fig. 3B,C). Note that the lower than 50% location repetition probability is explained by a reward-independent tendency to change locations as evidenced by the negative ρlocation parameter estimates in our model (see Model description and Table S1). Examining the interaction effect of Group x Previous outcome suggested a notably smaller effect in the ASDSR group compared to non-ASDSR individuals (posterior median difference = −0.19, HDI95% = [−0.42, 0.02]; see Fig. 3B,C), with a 96% probability that this difference is negative at the population level (probability of direction = 96%). To validate the association between λ parameter estimates for each individual in each group, and the model-agnostic regression analysis for outcome-irrelevant learning, we examined the moderation effect of individual λ values on the effect of the previous outcome in the model-agnostic regression model predicting the probability of staying with a previous location choice (see Fig. 3A). We found that lower estimated λ values correspond to a greater outcome-irrelevant learning behavioral signature (interaction posterior median = −1.6, HDI95% [−1.96, −1.23], pd = ~100%). Overall, our results indicate a robust outcome-irrelevant learning effect in the non-autistic group, which is reduced in individuals with autism.

Autistic traits are associated with outcome-irrelevant learning

We next wanted to examine whether autistic traits, as measured by the Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ [56]), predict outcome-irrelevant learning across groups. While no prior work that we are aware of has examined this relationship, previous reports did suggest that higher self-reported obsessive-compulsive symptoms (as measured by the Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory Revised [70]) were found to predict stronger outcome-irrelevant [24]. Since autistic traits are known to be positively correlated with obsessive-compulsive tendencies (in our dataset, the association between AQ and OCI-R was r = 0.36, HDI95% [0.21, 0.51]), we aimed to estimate the association of autistic traits with outcome-irrelevant learning, while controlling for the association with obsessive-compulsive tendencies. We therefore fitted a linear regression model where the level of autistic traits (AQ total score) and obsessive-compulsive tendencies (OCI-R total score) predicted λ estimates (λ posterior mean for each individual) across groups. We replicated previous findings by Shahar et al. [24], showing that higher OCI-R scores are associated with increased outcome-irrelevant learning, with a 10-point increase in OCI-R scores predicting a 0.017 decrease in λ (HDI95% [−0.036, 0.0003], pd = 96%; see Fig. 4B). In contrast, we found that higher AQ scores were associated with reduced outcome-irrelevant learning such that a 10-point increase in AQ predicted a 0.037 increase in λ (HDI95% [0.012, 0.062], pd = ~100%; see Fig. 4A). Together, these findings reveal a compelling dissociation: while higher autistic traits are associated with reduced outcome-irrelevant learning, higher compulsivity is linked to increased value-based learning for such irrelevant features.

To further decipher the positive association between AQ and λ, we investigated, on an exploratory basis, which of the five subscales of the AQ questionnaire are the ones driving this association. For this purpose, we fitted a multiple regression predicting λ by each AQ subscale score and the OCI-R total score. We found that the only AQ subscale showing a clear positive association with λ was the “Communication” subscale, such that every reported autistic communication characteristic (out of ten possible in total) was associated with a 0.025 increase in λ (posterior median = 0.025, HDI95% [0.001, 0.03], pd = 98.2%). Other AQ subscales showed no clear association with λ (Imagination - HDI95% [−0.015, 0.01], pd = 65%; Attention to detail - HDI95% [−0.007, 0.015, pd = 73%; Attention switching - HDI95% [−0.014, 0.014], pd = 53%; Social skill - HDI95% [−0.015, 0.014], pd = 60%).

Discussion

In this study, we conducted an adversarial collaboration to examine whether individuals who provided an online self-disclosure of a clinical ASD diagnosis (ASDSR) differ from non-autistic individuals (non-ASDSR) in their tendency to exhibit outcome-irrelevant learning. Participants were recruited online via Prolific and underwent a preregistered diagnosis verification process. We used a multi-armed bandit task, allowing us to test whether participants assign credit to outcome-irrelevant spatial locations, despite clear instructions that shape locations are randomly allocated and thus are non-predictive of reward. Our results provide strong evidence that individuals with ASDSR exhibit reduced outcome-irrelevant learning compared to non-ASDSR individuals. This finding was supported by both computational model estimates and a model-agnostic regression signature. Importantly, higher self-reported autistic traits were associated with lower levels of outcome-irrelevant learning across the sample. In particular, we found that the AQ subscale most strongly associated with lower outcome-irrelevant learning was the “Communication” subscale. That is, individuals reporting greater autistic communication characteristics exhibited reduced outcome-irrelevant learning.

These results align with and extend a growing body of research highlighting cognitive strengths often observed in autism [13, 71–75], and more specifically, the superior information processing capacities of individuals with autism [5, 6, 8, 9, 76, 77]. Specifically, individuals with autism seem to exhibit more input-driven information processing, relying less on top-down shortcuts (or heuristics) compared to non-autistic individuals. Consequently, they often exhibit decision-making that is less biased and more rational, leading to more optimal outcomes. The term “enhanced rationality” was previously claimed to characterize this phenomenon, emphasizing the reduced susceptibility of individuals with autism to cognitive biases typically arising from heuristic reliance [13]. Our findings substantiate this notion, demonstrating that individuals in the ASDSR group show a pronounced tendency to selectively focus on outcome-relevant cues during decision-making, resulting in more accurate credit assignment compared to non-autistic individuals.

One intriguing question that arises is whether this pattern is specific to individuals with ASDSR or rather associated with autistic traits regardless of diagnosis. Our results suggest that this cognitive pattern is not exclusive to the ASDSR group but is also observed in non-autistic individuals who report higher levels of autistic traits. This supports the idea that these cognitive differences may exist along a continuum, suggesting that autistic traits can influence decision-making in broader populations [56, 71, 73].

Despite substantial behavioral evidence for enhanced rationality in ASD across various decision-making tasks, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. One key limitation is that traditional decision-making tasks primarily assess final choices rather than the dynamic process through which information is evaluated and integrated, limiting our ability to thoroughly understand the cognitive processes underlying enhanced rationality. More recent task developments have addressed this gap by incorporating paradigms that track information evaluation and processing during decision-making. Comparisons between ASD and non-autistic individuals using these paradigms have highlighted a tendency in autism to integrate information more equitably, contrasting with the positively biased processing often observed in non-autistic individuals [8]. While representing a significant advancement, these paradigms often fail to capture the trial-by-trial dynamics of learning and decision-making, making it difficult to disentangle whether reduced bias in ASD stems from differences in how information is initially processed, how new evidence is prioritized, or how decision strategies evolve over time. Computational modeling, and reinforcement learning in particular, offer a promising approach to addressing these gaps by providing a framework to quantify how individuals assign value to incoming information and adjust their decisions accordingly.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to specifically investigate enhanced rationality using computational reinforcement learning modeling. Applying a dynamic computational cognitive model, which examines learning on a trial-by-trial basis, allowed us to elucidate at least one mechanism contributing to enhanced rationality in ASD. We found that individuals in the ASDSR group assigned less weight to outcome-irrelevant cues, such as spatial location versus visual shape. This suggests that information processing that is less biased by irrelevant yet potentially salient cues, and the ability to consider relevant information only, may lead to “enhanced rationality”. Our findings, therefore, may indicate that autistic individuals optimize their decision-making by focusing on relevant information while effectively filtering out irrelevant information.

One possible reason why non-autistic participants do not fully disregard location information, despite explicit instructions, is that spatial location is a highly salient feature in most ecological environments, making it more difficult to ignore even when deemed irrelevant [22, 78, 79]. This may lead to a strong prior on the relevance of spatial-motor features to reward prediction. From that perspective, the ability of individuals with ASD to disregard locations despite their saliency aligns well with emerging Bayesian and predictive coding frameworks applied to autism, which provide overarching theories to explain the diverse characteristics of ASD. These accounts emphasize reduced reliance on prior expectations and greater weighting of sensory input during perception [47–49]. Expanding this framework beyond sensory processing, we previously posited that predictive mechanisms also shape higher-order cognitive processes, affecting decision-making and reasoning [52]. The results of the current study support this extension, demonstrating that individuals with ASDSR prioritize outcome-relevant information over heuristics that might introduce bias.

The altered-priors framework accounts for a range of autistic characteristics, from sensory sensitivities and cognitive rigidity to challenges in social communication [47–49]. Interestingly, we find that lower outcome-irrelevant learning was most strongly associated with communication-related autistic traits. Challenges in communication are a core feature of autism spectrum disorder and often have the greatest impact on everyday social functioning [80]. Autistic communication is frequently characterized by a more literal style of interpretation. As a result, individuals with higher communication-related autistic traits may be less inclined to infer latent or unintended meanings beyond what is explicitly stated. We speculate that this tendency may have facilitated more faithful adherence to the task instructions regarding outcome-relevance, or a more accurate representation of the randomness of locations with respect to rewards. Taking, for example, an item such as “I find it easy to 'read between the lines' when someone is talking to me,” lower endorsement of this statement, which points towards ASD traits, may reflect a reduced tendency to search for hidden meanings. In the context of the task, such a literal interpretive style may have helped participants rely more directly on explicitly relevant information, thereby reducing learning from outcome-irrelevant features. More generally, the altered-priors perspective may provide an overarching mechanism that links differences in credit assignment with a reduced ability to navigate highly dynamic spaces, such as social communication, a central aspect of the autistic phenotype.

What are the potential downsides of enhanced rationality in ASD? One possibility is that a stronger reliance on relevant input, coupled with a tendency to disregard irrelevant information, may impair flexibility in dynamic or changing environments [81]. Research in autism has typically shown reduced cognitive flexibility in ASD individuals when doing the Wisconsin Card Switching Task [82, 83]. Accordingly, it was found that autistic traits in the general population were associated with worse performance in volatile environments where reward probabilities shift often [84]. Future studies could empirically test whether reduced outcome-irrelevant learning in ASD leads to slower adaptation when environmental contingencies shift, for example, when previously irrelevant features (e.g., locations) become predictive of reward, and formerly informative cues (e.g., visual shape) lose their relevance [26]. This distinction between static and dynamic environments is particularly intriguing given that one of the most unpredictable domains is the social world, where autistic traits are most prominently expressed [85]. Completely disregarding any information about outcome-irrelevant action features may lead to superior performance as long as the environment is stable, yet prove maladaptive if meaningful signals emerge from previously irrelevant dimensions [86]. It therefore remains an open question whether this reduced susceptibility to bias would be beneficial or detrimental in more dynamic contexts, where previously irrelevant features become predictive of reward. In such volatile settings, individuals with ASD may continue to filter out information that has become predictive of reward, or, paradoxically, be more prone to misattributing outcomes to non-causal cues that were previously causally associated with the outcome.

From a broader perspective, enhanced rationality is particularly significant as it challenges traditional views of human information processing. Conventional economic models of human reasoning and decision making, such as those proposed by Kahneman and Tversky [3, 87], posit that biases in cognitive processes stem from the brain’s limited capacity for information processing, compelling individuals to prioritize efficiency over accuracy [88]. These limitations have informed dominant models of bounded rationality, suggesting that biases are inherent to typical neural functioning [89]. In contrast, the enhanced rationality observed in individuals with ASD challenges these long-held assumptions and could be seen as defying the inherent limitations of human brain function. This paper takes a first step at elucidating the mechanisms contributing to this phenomenon, which may therefore be relevant to information processing more broadly.

Our findings did not support the alternative, cognitive control hypothesis, which predicted increased outcome-irrelevant learning in autism due to attentional or working memory limitations. This absence of evidence should be interpreted in light of our sample and design. The study included individuals who demonstrated intact performance on standardized cognitive measures, including intelligence and working memory abilities (see Table 1). This likely reflects the nature of the study, which involved a relatively complex task requiring sustained attention and comprehension of abstract rules. As in many studies on autism [90–92], including those of enhanced rational decision making [13], a key limitation here, yet one that is tightly linked to task demands, is that our findings may not generalize to the full autistic population, but rather to those with relatively intact cognitive abilities. Importantly, this subgroup of autistic individuals is far from rare: recent epidemiological data estimate that approximately 60% of children diagnosed with ASD do not meet criteria for intellectual disability [93, 94]. Moreover, intellectual ability represents only one dimension of functioning in autism. Many individuals with average or above-average IQ still experience substantial challenges in adaptive behavior and daily life skills. It should be noted that a regression analysis including working memory and intelligence scores as covariates revealed that group differences in outcome-irrelevant learning remained substantial, suggesting that differences in cognitive abilities alone do not explain the observed effects in our cohort. Nevertheless, it is possible that executive control-related task difficulties may be more prominent among individuals with more pronounced cognitive challenges.

It is important to clarify that our conclusion pertains to a specific mechanistic component of rationality, namely, the ability to suppress learning from outcome-irrelevant information during reward-based decision-making. We do not suggest that reduced outcome-irrelevant learning in ASDSR reflects a general enhancement in rational thinking across all domains. Rather, our task isolates one bias-resistant learning mechanism that supports more accurate credit assignment, and enhanced rationality, in this context, refers to a reduced susceptibility to assign undue credit to irrelevant yet salient information. As mentioned in our review paper, this tendency underlies common cognitive biases, including the decoy/attraction effects, sunk cost bias, and more [13]. Yet, other well-documented forms of cognitive bias, such as loss aversion, susceptibility to framing effects, or intuitive versus analytical reasoning, likely involve different processes that may not show the same pattern in autism. This study, therefore, represents a first step in what we hope will be a broader effort to uncover the specific cognitive pathways that contribute to bias-resistant reasoning.

While our findings provide novel insights into the computational mechanisms underlying enhanced rationality in ASDSR, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our study focused on a specific reinforcement learning paradigm, emphasizing the utilization of relevant over irrelevant cues when reaching decisions. Future work should examine different mechanisms that could underlie enhanced rationality in other contexts. Second, participant allocation to the self-reported ASD diagnosis group was based on a series of open-ended questions in which individuals described the clinical process they went through to receive a clinical ASD diagnosis by an expert. In recent years, there has been an increase in the use of online samples of people self-reporting having an ASD diagnosis, as it enables more rapid and scalable investigation of cognition in ASD [46, 58–62, 95, 96]. However, a major concern regards the validity of such reports, as the clinical process of receiving an ASD diagnosis is not regulated by design. This raises several concerns, of which the major ones are impersonation by humans or AI bots. In the present study, we extend previous methods by incorporating a self-report questionnaire to validate a clinical ASD diagnosis, in which participants were asked to describe their diagnostic process. Participants provided detailed responses regarding what led them to seek a diagnosis, as well as descriptions of the diagnostic procedure itself. We believe that this method has the potential to increase the validity of online research in ASD while mitigating the risk of receiving AI-generated responses. We have fully shared participants’ responses and the review process conducted by our team, to increase other teams’ ability to adopt our method (see Appendix A). However, we acknowledge that even when using a self-report questionnaire, the online approach is not without limitations. Some participants who self-reported having ASD may not fully meet clinical criteria if brought into the lab to partake in a clinical diagnosis. Moreover, when diagnoses are verified via online self-reports, the possibility that some individuals inaccurately reported their condition cannot be fully excluded. We therefore decided throughout the paper to refer to this group as “self-reported ASD” or ASDSR [62], to highlight the potential downfalls of this online method.

To conclude, in this pre-registered, hypothesis-driven study, we provide a computational cognitive mechanism underlying the enhanced rationality observed in individuals with ASDSR. Our findings suggest that individuals with autism possess a unique capacity to prioritize relevant information, which is evident in a trial-by-trial learning process, and correlates with autistic traits. This emphasizes a strength-based approach to understanding information processing capacities in ASD, which may entail social trade-offs, a link that should be further explored in future studies. As enhanced rationality in ASD offers unique insights into human information processing capabilities, examining the cognitive dynamics giving rise to these abilities not only enriches our understanding of autism but also offers valuable insights to the broader field of human cognition.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited online through the Prolific platform and categorized into ASD and non-ASD groups based on self-reported clinical diagnosis, a recruitment strategy consistent with established protocols in recent large-scale studies [46, 57–62]. We analyzed data of 154 adults aged 18 to 61 who were recruited via Prolific, including 75 individuals who self-reported holding an ASD diagnosis (labeled henceforward ASDSR; Mean age = 30.9, SD age = 9.3; 46 female, 25 male, 4 other) and 79 who self-reported not having an ASD diagnosis and not having a first degree relative with ASD (labeled henceforward non-ASDSR; Mean age = 31.3, SD age = 10.3; 47 female, 32 male). The study was approved by Tel Aviv University’s Research Ethics Council, and all participants gave informed consent. We pre-registered our hypotheses and provided sample size justification using a Bayesian precision analysis (10.17605/OSF.IO/KWJG3).

Sample recruitment, diagnosis validation, and drop-out

Because data collection was conducted online, group assignment relied exclusively on participant reports without the acquisition of clinical documentation. To ensure data integrity and mitigate the risk of fraudulent responses, such as those from automated bots or individuals misrepresenting their status, we implemented a multi-stage validation process (see also Appendix A). Specifically, our design included group categorization for “self-reported ASD” and “self-reported non-ASD” participants based on preregistered criteria (see 10.17605/OSF.IO/KWJG3). Participants were recruited via the Prolific platform using two separate invitations, one targeting individuals who reported a prior clinical diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (N = 287) and one targeting individuals who reported no such diagnosis (N = 319). Group assignment was then verified using a two-stage procedure (All questions and data for this process are detailed in Appendix A).

In Part A, participants in the ASDSR group were required to reconfirm that they held a prior clinical ASD diagnosis. Participants in the non-ASDSR group were required to confirm the absence of an ASD diagnosis for themselves and for any first-degree relatives. Only participants who met these criteria were eligible to proceed to further testing (ASDSR: N = 198; Non-ASDSR: N = 240).

In Part B, participants completed a questionnaire assessing details of their diagnostic history. The ASDSR group addressed 11 open-ended questions regarding the clinical context and professional setting of their diagnosis. Conversely, non-ASDSR participants responded to four open-ended questions concerning any past suspicions of undiagnosed ASDSR or prior intentions to seek professional clinical consultation. The open-text responses from Part B were independently reviewed by two graduate students who rated them for authenticity and coherence on a scale of 1 to 4, following preregistered criteria. Authenticity was defined as the extent to which a response reflected a genuine, human-authored account of personal experience. Low authenticity ratings were assigned to responses that were insufficiently detailed or suspected of being AI-generated. Coherence ratings assessed whether the answers logically aligned across the entire questionnaire. For example, if the participant described communication difficulties as the cause for his parents to seek a diagnosis, but then later contradicted this claim. Disagreement between the two reviewers was defined as cases in which either score was equal to 3, and was resolved by a principal investigator (L.R.).

In the ASDSR and non-ASDSR groups, 151 out of 198 and 150 out of 240 participants, respectively, have passed the validation process. Specifically, 15/3 participants, respectively, were excluded for receiving low ratings in both authenticity and coherence. An additional 12/4 participants were excluded due to low coherence ratings only, and 10/0 participants were excluded due to low authenticity ratings only. Nine/four participants were further excluded due to disagreement between reviewer judgments that was resolved by a principal investigator’s decision. One additional participant in the ASDSR group was excluded for failing to provide responses to two or more questionnaire items, for example, by not specifying the age at diagnosis or the type of professional who issued the diagnosis. Finally, in the non-ASDSR group, 79 individuals were excluded after reporting at least some concern that they might have ASD. Following these exclusions, the sample consisted of 151 participants in the ASDSR group and 150 in the non-ASDSR group.

Thus, overall, 301 individuals were invited to continue the study on the next day. Of these, 240 participants (123 ASDSR, 117 Non-ASDSR) returned the following day and were able to complete the multi-armed bandit task. On the third day, 199 participants (101 ASDSR, 98 Non-ASDSR) completed a working memory assessment. Finally, 188 participants (92 ASDSR, 96 Non-ASDSR) completed a series of self-report questionnaires assessing autism-related traits and general mental health, as well as a short-form intelligence test.

Autistic traits across groups

We further estimated autistic traits across both groups using the Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ [56]; see Table 1). We found the groups to differ substantially on the AQ score, with the ASDSR group showing much higher scores (M = 33.8, SD = 8.1) compared to the non-ASDSR group (M = 19.9, SD = 7.2), by a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.82, HDI95% [1.8, 1.85]; see Table 1). The ASDSR group mean AQ score fell within the range reported in a large meta-analysis of clinically diagnosed autistic individuals (M = 35.19, range = 27.6–41.1; [97]), and aligned with distributions reported in similar samples recruited through Prolific [46, 61]. Accordingly, the vast majority of autistic participants (81%) scored at least 26, indicative of clinically relevant levels of autistic traits, while 71% scored 32 or above on the AQ (i.e., exceeding the commonly used clinical cutoff [56]. In contrast, nearly all non-ASDSR participants scored below these thresholds, with 98% scoring below 32, and 71% scoring under 26. These proportions are consistent with the AQ’s known sensitivity and specificity for autism diagnoses [98]. Since autistic traits are also associated with various psychopathologies, we further estimated group differences on a range of clinical self-report measures to verify that the major differences between the groups are autistic traits. While the ASDSR group showed higher scores than the control group across all psychiatric questionnaires, we found that autistic traits were by far the largest group difference, as the group difference effect size of the AQ was between 3.1 and 6.3 times larger compared to estimates of obsessive-compulsive tendencies, depression, anxiety, IQ, and working memory capacity (see Table 1).

Measures

Multi-armed bandit task

Participants completed a modified version of the reinforcement learning bandit task. In each trial, participants were offered two out of four arms (indicated by abstract shapes; randomly allocated by the computer). Participants then chose a shape freely using a right/left corresponding response-key press (“s” or “k” keys in a QWERTY keyboard; until-response with a 6 s deadline). After making a choice, the unchosen shape disappeared, and the chosen shape remained on the screen to allow choice feedback (500 ms). Shapes led probabilistically to reward ($0 or $1; outcome presented for 1000 ms) according to a true expected value, slowly drifting according to a random walk. The task included 4 blocks with 50 trials in each block. Each block included four new shapes and a different random walk reward function (counterbalanced between blocks). Participants were asked to do their best to make choices that would maximize monetary return. To further ensure participants’ motivation, we rewarded them with a monetary payment bonus at the end of the study according to their gains in the task. Participants were explicitly made aware of the fact that only the shape itself influences reward probability. Thus, they knew with high certainty that the shape’s location (i.e., whether it currently appears on the left or right of the screen) is random and thus should not be learned. This fact is important since it renders any credit assignment to the location/response key as outcome-irrelevant learning. Before starting the task, participants were asked to complete a short verbal quiz to ensure they read and understood the instructions, including a specific question regarding which task features predict a monetary outcome. Participants had to show 100% accuracy in the quiz, and if they scored lower, they were prompted to the beginning of the instructions phase and asked to retake the quiz (see SI for instructions and quiz).

Working memory assessment

To measure their visual working memory capacity, participants were asked to maintain and retrieve visual information in a change detection task under varying load conditions [99, 100]. On each trial, participants were shown a visual array of colored squares (set-size of 4 or 8 squares; see SI). Squares in each array had distinct colors and were evenly spread across the screen. Each trial started with the presentation of the squares array (i.e., encoding phase, 200 ms), followed by a fixation cross (i.e., retention phase, 900 ms) and then a target (i.e., retrieval phase, until-response with a 6-second deadline). The target screen included one colored square randomly selected by the computer in a color that was either the “same” or “different” color as the square that appeared in the same location in the memorized array. Participants were asked to respond “same” or “different” by pressing “s” or “k” response-keys (keyboard mapping was counterbalanced between participants). Following the target screen, participants saw feedback indicating whether their response was correct or incorrect, followed by a fixation screen (inter-trial interval, 500 ms fixation). Each participant completed 120 trials aimed at identifying individual differences in working memory capacity. In each condition (set size 8/4), capacity was calculated according to K = set_size · (2 · accuracy-1), and the average between the two conditions was used as a measure of overall capacity [99, 101].

Self-report psychopathology questionnaires

Participants completed a series of validated self-report questionnaires to assess individual differences in autism-related traits and general mental health. These included the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ [56]), the Obsessive Compulsive Inventory-Revised (OCI-R [70]), the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI [102]), and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI [103]).

General intelligence test

Participants completed the I-CAR test, a public-domain measure of general intelligence designed for online administration. Its reliability and validity have been supported by prior research, showing strong correlations with established measures such as the Shipley-2 (0.81) and the full Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (0.85 [104]).

Procedure

Participants were recruited via Prolific and completed the study over four consecutive days. Sessions 1 and 2 (day 1) involved diagnostic screening using Qualtrics questionnaires tailored to each group. Eligible participants were then invited to complete sessions 3–5 on days 2–4: session 3 included the multi-armed bandit task, session 4 a working memory task, and session 5 an intelligence test and self-report psychopathology measures. These sessions were implemented using the jsPsych package [105], and data were stored on Pavlovia (www.pavlovia.org). Demographic data were obtained from Prolific. Participants were paid after completing all sessions; ineligible participants were compensated for their time.

Data treatment

Multi-armed bandit task

Based on pre-registered exclusion criteria, we excluded trials and participants showing strong indications of inattentive responding. Specifically, trials were omitted if no response was made, if reaction times (RTs) were implausibly fast (<300 ms) or exceptionally slow (>4000 ms), resulting in the exclusion of 5.4% of trials from the non-ASDSR group and 6.8% from the ASDSR group. Participants were excluded if more than 20% of their trials met these criteria (21 participants in total: 7 non-ASDSR [6%], 14 ASDSR [11%]), or if they repeated the same key response on more than 70% of trials (1 participant, ASDSR group). Additionally, we tracked cursor activity to ensure participants remained on the task screen. Participants who left the task window and repeated this behavior after a warning were excluded (17 participants: 10 Non-ASDSR [9%], 7 ASDSR [6%]). In total, 39 participants were excluded from the initial sample of 240 (15% of the non-ASDSR group and 18% of the ASDSR group).

Working memory task

We applied pre-registered criteria to exclude participants showing chance-level behavior on the working memory task, based on the subset of individuals who had passed data quality checks for the multi-armed bandit task (186 total; 93 in each group). We excluded 5 participants who had higher accuracy in set size four than in set size eight (2.2% of the non-ASDSR group, 3.2% of the ASDSR group), 2 participants who had less than 50% accuracy in set size four (1.1% of the non-ASDSR group, 1.1% of the ASDSR group), and 5 participants (5.4% of the ASDSR group) who switched away from the task window more than once (after receiving a warning the first time). In total, out of 186 participants, 12 participants’ data were discarded from the working memory task (3.3% of the non-ASDSR group and 9.7% of the ASDSR group).

Self-report session

Finally, we excluded participants based on attention-check failures in the self-report session, among those who had passed data quality checks for both the decision-making and working memory tasks (169 participants, 81 ASDSR, 88 non-ASDSR). Two participants were excluded for selecting an incorrect response on the AQ questionnaire’s attention-check question (1.1% of the non-ASDSR group, 1.2% of the ASDSR group), and 13 participants were excluded for switching to other windows more than once after an initial warning (8% of the non-ASDSR group, 6.2% of the ASDSR group). Thus, 15 participants were excluded based on self-report data (9.1% of the non-ASDSR group and 7.4% of the ASDSR group), leaving a final dataset of 154 participants (79 non-ASDSR, 75 ASDSR) with complete data across all sessions (see Fig. 5).

Computational modeling

To model the trial-by-trial value updating process, we use a feature-based reinforcement learning model [64, 106, 107]. Here, action feature values were updated using a temporal difference learning algorithm (i.e., there are separate Q-values for the relevant visual feature and irrelevant spatial-motor feature). On each trial, the reward was used to calculate prediction errors for each action feature (Eq. 1; denoted δf). These prediction errors were then used to update the values of the chosen action features (i.e., visual and spatial), where α is a learning rate (free parameter; Eq. 2).

To integrate the feature values, the model first included a vector denoting the relevance of the features according to the task instructions, representing the externally transmitted knowledge. Here, since the visual domain was always outcomerelevant, and the spatial-motor was not, this vector was defined as 1 for the relevant visual feature and 0 for the irrelevant spatial-motor feature (Eq. 3).

The model then included an integration of the features’ values based on the instructions’ relevance weights. In addition to reward-based learning, the model also included reward-independent choice tendencies to repeat or switch action features. For each chosen feature (i.e., shape or location), E-values were updated on each trial based on a feature-specific free parameter (⍴f; see Eq. 5). Positive ⍴ values reflect a tendency to perseverate on previously chosen features, while negative values capture a tendency to explore less-sampled alternatives. Before each choice, E-values decay at a feature-specific rate (ηf), giving greater weight to more recent choices (Eq. 6).

We included E-values separately for the visual and spatial-motor domains, allowing us to capture perseveration (or switching) tendencies for each modality separately.

The decision policy of the model included both a reward-dependent (Q-values) and a reward-independent (E-values). Q-values were integrated according to feature-specific decision weights (wf) determined by λ (Eq. 4). To account for random exploration tendencies, Q-values were multiplied by an inverse-noise parameter β (free parameter; Eq. 7).

Finally, the policy was determined by a softmax function transforming the combination of all three values of a given option (i.e., Qnet, Evisual, and Espatial) into action propensities (see Eq. 8).

Parameter recovery

To assess the validity of our model, we conducted a parameter recovery analysis to determine whether we could accurately recover known generative parameters from simulated data [67]. Specifically, we simulated the behavior of 200 agents, each assigned a diverse set of parameters sampled from a broad distribution [α ∼ N(0,1.5); β ∼ N(4,1.5); λ ∼ N(0,1.5); ⍴shapes ∼ N(0,1.5); ⍴locations ∼ N(0,1.5); ηshapes ~ N(0,1.5); ηlocations ~ N(0,1.5); α,λ, ηvisual and ηspatial were logistically transformed to constrain their values between 0 and 1]. Simulation data had 200 trials in 4 blocks for each agent and was based on the empirical random-walk set. Our results demonstrated robust recovery of all parameters at both the population and individual levels. For each parameter, we observed strong correlations between the true (simulated) and estimated values across individuals (see Fig. 6). These findings support the reliability of our model and justify its application to empirical data.

Model recovery

Before applying model comparison to empirical data, we conducted a model recovery analysis to validate our modeling approach [67]. For this analysis, we generated data from 200 agents, where the generative model was either the reduced model or the full model, which also included the λ parameter estimating outcome-irrelevant learning. Using the loo package, we estimated out-of-sample predictive accuracy through a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV) procedure. Specifically, we calculated the expected log-predictive density (elpd) for each trial using Pareto-smoothed importance sampling. We followed a standard guideline indicating that an elpd difference greater than twice its standard error (SE) suggests a substantial difference between models [68, 69, 108]. The results demonstrated successful model recovery, with the full model showing significantly better goodness-of-fit when it generated the data (elpd difference = 1553, SE = 52). When using data generated from the reduced model, the full model appropriately inferred that λ was unnecessary, with the posterior median of λ approximating 1 (indicating convergence with the reduced model). Thus, consistent with expectation [69], both models demonstrated identical predictive accuracy on held-out data (elpd difference = 0.5, SE = 0.8).

Association between λ and the model-agnostic regression signature in simulation

We used a simulated dataset to show that λ is positively associated with the model-agnostic regression signature. Specifically, we fitted a logistic regression model in the simulated data, where the previous outcome interacted with λ to predict the tendency to stay with the same location choice when two different cards than before are offered (see Fig. 3A). We found a clear negative association such that lower λ was associated with a greater model-agnostic outcome-irrelevant learning effect (interaction posterior median = −1.23; HDI95% [−1.63, −0.83], pd = ~100%). Thus, we can confidently claim that model-derived λ is tightly associated with our model-agnostic regression signature.

Bayesian parameter estimation

Bayesian logistic regression and reinforcement learning computational modeling analyses were performed using “brms” and “cmdstan” packages in R [66, 109]. Models included population-level (fixed effects) and participant-level (random effects) parameters and were sampled with weakly informative priors. All chains were visually examined using trace plots, pairs plots, and R-hat estimates and were found to show good convergence. Instead of relying on a binary criterion, such as checking whether zero is excluded from the highest density interval, we quantified the strength of each dynamical effect using the probability of direction (pd), which represents the proportion of the posterior distribution that shares the same sign as the median value of the estimate. We report the median, HDI95%, and probability of direction for parameters’ posterior distributions [110–112] (for logistic regression, estimates are on the log-odds scale). For computational models, parameter estimation was conducted separately for each group using the cmdstan package in R [66]. Fitting results had 2000 samples for each of the 4 chains, allowing us to estimate uncertainty in population-level and individual-level parameter estimates.
