Continuous adductor canal block versus continuous femoral nerve block for analgesia after open reduction and internal fixation of patellar fractures: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Man Li, Yin Zhou, Fu Yao, Zhijun Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective To investigate the analgesic efficacy, impact on quadriceps muscle strength, and safety of continuous adductor canal block (CACB) versus continuous femoral nerve block (CFNB) in patients undergoing open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of patellar fractures, and to provide evidence for selecting the optimal perioperative analgesic regimen in clinical practice.MethodsA total of 80 patients scheduled for primary unilateral ORIF of patellar fractures at our hospital from August 2018 to May 2019 were enrolled in this randomized controlled trial. Patients were randomly allocated to the CACB group (experimental group) or CFNB group (control group) at a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated random number table, with 40 patients in each group. Ultrasound-guided nerve block catheterization was performed before anesthesia induction in both groups, with an initial bolus of 20 mL 0.2% ropivacaine. Postoperative patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) with 0.175% ropivacaine was administered in both groups. The primary outcome was quadriceps muscle strength at 2 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included intraoperative sufentanil consumption, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain scores at rest and during 45° active knee flexion at multiple postoperative time points, rate of rescue analgesia, incidence of adverse events, and patient satisfaction with analgesia.ResultsThere were no significant differences in baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between the two groups (all P > 0.05). No significant differences were found in intraoperative sufentanil consumption, VAS scores at rest and during movement at all postoperative time points, or rescue analgesia rate between the two groups (all P > 0.05). Quadriceps muscle strength at 2 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h postoperatively was significantly higher in the CACB group than in the CFNB group (all P < 0.001). The total incidence of adverse events was significantly lower in the CACB group (17.5%) than in the CFNB group (32.5%, P = 0.045), and patient satisfaction with analgesia was significantly higher in the CACB group (P = 0.048).ConclusionUltrasound-guided CACB yields comparable analgesic efficacy compared with CFNB in patients undergoing ORIF of patellar fractures; CACB resulted in significantly less impairment of quadriceps muscle strength than CFNB, with fewer adverse events and higher patient satisfaction. CACB creates favorable conditions for early postoperative functional rehabilitation, aligns with ERAS principles, and is a safe and effective regional analgesic technique worthy of further clinical validation and consideration in appropriate patient populations undergoing patellar fracture surgery.Trial registrationThis trial was registered with ChiCTR (ChiCTR1800017828) on August 16, 2018.

Introduction

As the largest sesamoid bone in the human body, the patella plays an irreplaceable role in the biomechanical stability and flexion-extension function of the knee joint. Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) with Kirschner wires and tension band fixation is the gold-standard surgical treatment for displaced patellar fractures, with well-established technical reliability and satisfactory fixation outcomes [1, 2]. Standardized early postoperative functional exercise is the core determinant of knee function recovery, and is critical to reducing long-term complications including joint adhesion, quadriceps atrophy, and post-traumatic osteoarthritis.

Moderate to severe acute pain is extremely common after patellar fracture surgery. The knee joint capsule has an abundant neurovascular supply, which amplifies nociceptive stimulation. Uncontrolled postoperative pain not only causes severe physical and psychological distress, but also directly restricts early functional exercise, prolongs hospital length of stay, and increases the risk of life-threatening complications such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). Therefore, a safe and effective perioperative analgesic strategy is essential to facilitate early rehabilitation in patients undergoing patellar fracture ORIF.

Current mainstream postoperative analgesic modalities have notable limitations in this patient population. Intravenous opioid-based analgesia is associated with high interindividual variability in efficacy and a high incidence of opioid-related adverse events including nausea, vomiting, respiratory depression, and ileus. Neuraxial analgesia carries a risk of hemodynamic instability, urinary retention, and puncture-related complications such as epidural hematoma. Continuous femoral nerve block (CFNB) is a well-established regional analgesic technique for knee surgery, with reliable analgesic efficacy and opioid-sparing effects. However, the femoral nerve is a mixed sensorimotor nerve, and CFNB inevitably blocks the motor branches innervating the quadriceps femoris, resulting in significant muscle weakness, impaired ambulation, and an increased risk of postoperative falls [3]. These drawbacks directly contradict the core principles of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS), which prioritizes early mobilization and functional recovery.

In recent years, ultrasound-guided continuous adductor canal block (CACB) has garnered increasing attention for its unique property of preferential sensory blockade with relative preservation of motor function [4]. The saphenous nerve, the target of CACB, is the largest pure sensory cutaneous branch of the femoral nerve, with no motor branches to the quadriceps muscle throughout its course. Theoretically, CACB can provide targeted analgesia for the anterior and medial knee, the primary region of nociception in patellar fracture surgery, while preserving quadriceps strength and motor function [5]. However, existing research on CACB in patellar fracture ORIF is limited, and there is no consensus on whether CACB can provide equivalent analgesia compared with the traditional CFNB in this specific surgical population.

Accordingly, we conducted this prospective, randomized, double-blind controlled trial to compare the analgesic efficacy, impact on quadriceps muscle strength, and safety of CACB versus CFNB in patients undergoing patellar fracture ORIF. We hypothesized that CACB would achieve equivalent analgesia compared with CFNB, with significantly less impairment of quadriceps muscle strength and fewer adverse events.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This single-center, prospective, randomized, double-blind, parallel-group controlled trial was conducted at Sichuan Orthopedic Hospital from August 2018 to May 2019. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Sichuan Orthopedic Hospital (2018-7-31-1), and was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry(ChiCTR1800017828) on August 16, 2018. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the CONSORT 2010 guidelines (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) for randomized controlled trials.The CONSORT participant flow diagram and detailed random allocation concealment flowchart are attached as supplementary materials; the full statistical analysis plan is elaborated in the Statistical analysis subsection as required by CONSORT checklist. This manuscript strictly adheres to the CONSORT guidelines for the reporting of randomized controlled trials, and all relevant trial details are reported in full compliance with the checklist requirements. All enrolled patients provided written informed consent before any study procedures were performed.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible patients were randomly allocated to the CACB group (experimental group) or CFNB group (control group) at a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated random number table. Randomization sequence generation and allocation concealment were performed by an independent statistician who was not involved in patient care, anesthesia administration, surgical procedures, or outcome assessment. The randomization allocation was sealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, tamper-proof envelopes. A dedicated research nurse who was not involved in patient care opened the envelope immediately before nerve block procedure to ensure allocation concealment.

Double-blinding was implemented for patients, operating surgeons, outcome assessors, and data analysts. The anesthesiologist performing the nerve block was unblinded to group allocation, but had no involvement in postoperative patient care or outcome assessment. All study drugs were prepared in identical syringes by the unblinded anesthesiologist, and all outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation throughout the study period.

To avoid outcome assessors identifying grouping via different catheter puncture locations, all catheter puncture sites of two groups were fully covered and fixed with identical sterile occlusive dressings with unified wrapping range, completely shielding the anatomical difference of puncture sites; outcome assessors only finished index evaluation and were forbidden to participate in catheter dressing change and postoperative catheter management.

Anesthesia and analgesia protocols

All patients fasted for 8 h and abstained from clear fluids for 4 h preoperatively, with no premedication administered. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was established, including 3-lead electrocardiogram (ECG), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), heart rate (HR), and pulse oximetry (SpO₂).

Nerve block procedures were performed by the same senior attending anesthesiologist with > 5 years of experience in ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia, before induction of general anesthesia in both groups.

CACB group

The patient was placed in the supine position with the affected limb slightly abducted and externally rotated. The saphenous nerve was identified in the adductor canal at the junction of the proximal and middle third of the thigh, adjacent to the superficial femoral artery deep to the sartorius muscle, using a high-frequency linear array ultrasound transducer. An in-plane puncture technique was used, and after negative aspiration for blood, an initial bolus of 20 mL of 0.2% ropivacaine was injected. A continuous block catheter was then advanced 3–5 cm beyond the needle tip, secured to the skin, and connected to an electronic patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump postoperatively.

CFNB group

The patient was placed in the supine position. The femoral nerve was identified in the short axis view at the level of the inguinal crease, lateral to the femoral artery and deep to the fascia iliaca, using ultrasound guidance. An in-plane puncture technique was used, and after negative aspiration, an initial bolus of 20 mL of 0.2% ropivacaine was injected. A continuous block catheter was placed, secured, and connected to an identical PCA pump postoperatively.

The PCA pump regimen was identical for both groups: 0.175% ropivacaine, with a background infusion rate of 5 mL/h, a patient-controlled bolus of 2 mL, and a lockout interval of 30 min.

Twenty minutes after completion of the nerve block, general anesthesia was induced in both groups with intravenous sufentanil 0.3 µg/kg, rocuronium bromide 0.6 mg/kg, and propofol 2 mg/kg. An appropriately sized laryngeal mask airway (LMA) was inserted, and mechanical ventilation was initiated to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO₂) between 35 and 45 mmHg. Anesthesia was maintained with 1–3% sevoflurane in oxygen combined with a continuous intravenous infusion of propofol 4–12 mg/kg/h, with the bispectral index (BIS) maintained between 40 and 60. Intraoperative hemodynamic parameters were maintained within 20% of baseline values. Additional boluses of sufentanil were administered as needed for hemodynamic response to surgical stimulation, and the total intraoperative sufentanil consumption was recorded. All surgeries were performed by the same team of orthopedic surgeons using a standardized ORIF technique with Kirschner wires and tension band fixation. At the end of surgery, patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) and extubated once they met standard emergence criteria.

After catheter fixation on skin, specialized ward nurses checked catheter fixation status daily after surgery to observe catheter shift, kinking or dislodgement; no catheter slippage or obvious migration was observed among all enrolled subjects during follow-up.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

Quadriceps muscle strength of the affected limb at 2 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h postoperatively, assessed using the Medical Research Council (MRC) Manual Muscle Testing scale for quadriceps femoris: 0 = no contraction; 1 = flicker of contraction; 2 = active movement with gravity eliminated; 3 = active movement against gravity; 4 = active movement against partial resistance; 5 = full strength against maximum resistance. All assessments were performed with the patient in supine position, knee fully extended, and resistance applied at the distal tibia.

All MMT assessments were completed by a single fixed professionally trained attending physician who remained blinded to group assignment; unified supine position and standardized evaluation criteria were applied for all participants to eliminate inter-examiner bias.

Secondary outcomes

Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain scores (0 = no pain, 10 = worst imaginable pain) at rest and during active 45° knee flexion, assessed preoperatively, 30 min, 2 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h postoperatively;

Total intraoperative consumption of sufentanil (µg);

Rate of postoperative rescue analgesia, defined as the proportion of patients requiring additional analgesic medication for VAS score ≥ 4. Rescue analgesia was standardized as intravenous tramadol 100 mg, administered by ward nurses blinded to group allocation. The total dose of rescue analgesics was recorded.

Incidence of postoperative adverse events within 72 h, including nausea and vomiting, dizziness, pruritus, puncture-related complications (hematoma, nerve injury), and falls;

Patient satisfaction with analgesia, assessed at 72 h postoperatively using a dichotomous scale (satisfied / dissatisfied).

Sample size calculation

Sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome of quadriceps muscle strength at 12 h postoperatively. Based on our pre-experimental data and previous published studies, we assumed a 1-point difference in MMT score between the CACB and CFNB groups, with a standard deviation of 1.5 points. A 1-point difference in MRC grade is considered clinically significant as it represents the ability to transition from non-weight-bearing to partial weight-bearing ambulation, which is a key milestone in early postoperative rehabilitation. Using a two-sided t-test with a type I error (α) of 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 90%, we calculated a required sample size of 36 patients per group. Accounting for a 10% dropout rate, we enrolled a total of 80 patients, with 40 patients in each group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 6.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Before formal comparison, Shapiro-Wilk test was adopted to verify normality of continuous data. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s), and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Ordinal categorical data (MMT grades) were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze the effects of group, time, and group×time interaction on VAS pain scores and MMT grades. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Categorical data were presented as number (percentage, %), and compared using the chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test when theoretical frequency < 5. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All 80 patients completed the study, with no withdrawals, dropouts, or protocol violations (Fig. 1).

Intraoperative sufentanil consumption and postoperative pain scores

There was no significant difference in total intraoperative sufentanil consumption between the CACB group and the CFNB group (20.8 ± 3.2 µg vs. 21.2 ± 2.9 µg, P = 0.559).

VAS pain scores at rest and during active 45° knee flexion at all postoperative time points were comparable between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences (all P > 0.05) (Table 2). Repeated measures ANOVA showed no significant group effect (F = 0.32, P = 0.573) or group×time interaction (F = 0.87, P = 0.512) for VAS scores at rest or during movement. The rate of postoperative rescue analgesia was 10.0% (4/40) in the CACB group and 7.5% (3/40) in the CFNB group, with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.692).

Adverse events

The total incidence of postoperative adverse events within 72 h was significantly lower in the CACB group (17.5%, 7/40) than in the CFNB group (32.5%, 13/40; χ²=4.021, P = 0.045). The incidence of dizziness was higher in the CFNB group (10.0% vs. 2.5%), while there were no significant differences in the incidence of nausea and vomiting, pruritus, puncture-related complications, or falls between the two groups (all P > 0.05, Fisher’s exact test for events with n < 5) (Table 4). All adverse events were mild and resolved with conservative management, with no severe adverse events reported in either group.

Patient satisfaction with analgesia

The proportion of patients reporting satisfaction with analgesia at 72 h postoperatively was 95.0% (38/40) in the CACB group, which was significantly higher than 82.5% (33/40) in the CFNB group (χ²=3.914, P = 0.048).

Discussion

This prospective, randomized, double-blind controlled trial compared the efficacy and safety of CACB versus CFNB in patients undergoing ORIF of patellar fractures. The key findings of our study are that CACB provides equivalent analgesic efficacy compared with CFNB, with significantly less impairment of quadriceps muscle strength, a lower overall incidence of adverse events, and higher patient satisfaction. These results support the use of CACB as a preferred regional analgesic technique for patellar fracture surgery, particularly in the context of ERAS pathways.

CFNB has long been considered the gold-standard regional analgesic technique for major knee surgery, with well-documented opioid-sparing effects and reliable analgesia [3]. However, the femoral nerve contains motor fibers that innervate the quadriceps, iliacus, and pectineus muscles, and CFNB inevitably causes dose-dependent motor blockade. This results in significant quadriceps weakness, which is the primary barrier to early ambulation and functional exercise, and increases the risk of postoperative falls [3]. Our study confirmed this limitation: patients in the CFNB group had significantly lower quadriceps muscle strength at all postoperative time points compared with the CACB group, which is consistent with previous studies in total knee arthroplasty populations [4, 6].

In contrast, the saphenous nerve is a pure sensory branch of the femoral nerve, with no motor fibers to the quadriceps muscle. CACB targets the saphenous nerve within the adductor canal, providing targeted sensory blockade of the anterior and medial knee, which is the primary region of nociception in patellar fracture surgery [7]. Ultrasound guidance allows for precise needle placement and local anesthetic delivery, ensuring reliable sensory blockade while avoiding spread to the femoral nerve motor branches [8]. Our study demonstrated that VAS pain scores at rest and during movement, intraoperative sufentanil consumption, and rescue analgesia rates were comparable between the CACB and CFNB groups, confirming that CACB provides equivalent analgesic efficacy for patellar fracture ORIF. These findings align with previous studies in knee arthroscopy and total knee arthroplasty [4, 9].

The preservation of quadriceps muscle strength is the most significant advantage of CACB over CFNB. In our study, patients in the CACB group had significantly higher quadriceps strength at all postoperative time points, which theoretically creates the physiological prerequisite for early active knee flexion and ambulation, as preserved quadriceps strength is essential for initiating weight-bearing and functional exercise. Early functional exercise is critical to preventing joint adhesion, muscle atrophy, and post-traumatic osteoarthritis after patellar fracture surgery, and is the core goal of ERAS pathways. The preservation of motor function also reduces the risk of falls, which is a major safety concern in orthopedic postoperative patients, particularly in older adults.

In terms of safety, the total incidence of adverse events was significantly lower in the CACB group compared with the CFNB group. The higher incidence of dizziness in the CFNB group is likely related to prolonged immobilization due to quadriceps weakness, as well as potential systemic absorption of local anesthetic. All adverse events in both groups were mild and transient, with no severe complications such as local anesthetic systemic toxicity, nerve injury, or major bleeding reported.

Preoperative nerve block, also known as preemptive analgesia, has been shown to reduce central sensitization and postoperative hyperalgesia, improving overall analgesic outcomes [10]. In our study, both groups received nerve block before surgical incision, which may have contributed to the overall excellent analgesic outcomes in both groups, and is consistent with current best practices for perioperative pain management.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study include its prospective, randomized, double-blind design, which minimizes selection and measurement bias. We focused on a specific, understudied surgical population (patellar fracture ORIF), filling an important gap in the existing literature. Our study also assessed multiple clinically relevant outcomes, including analgesic efficacy, motor function, safety, and patient satisfaction, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the two analgesic techniques.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, we only assessed short-term outcomes up to 72 h postoperatively, and did not evaluate long-term functional outcomes such as knee range of motion, time to full weight-bearing, or incidence of post-traumatic osteoarthritis. Third, we used a fixed concentration of ropivacaine for both groups, and did not perform a dose-finding analysis to identify the optimal local anesthetic concentration for CACB in this patient population. Fourth, we implemented daily bedside inspection of catheter fixation but did not perform postoperative ultrasonic re-examination to quantify precise catheter position; subtle catheter migration cannot be fully excluded according to previous research. Relevant literature confirmed mild catheter displacement commonly occurs in continuous peripheral nerve block, which may alter local anesthetic diffusion and individual analgesic effect; standardized daily nursing inspection could effectively reduce catheter dislocation risk [11, 12]. Future multicenter, large-scale randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusion

Ultrasound-guided continuous adductor canal block yields equivalent analgesic efficacy compared with continuous femoral nerve block in patients undergoing open reduction and internal fixation of patellar fractures; CACB resulted in significantly less impairment of quadriceps muscle strength than CFNB, with a lower incidence of adverse events and higher patient satisfaction. CACB creates favorable preconditions for early postoperative functional rehabilitation, aligns with ERAS principles, and is a safe and effective regional analgesic technique worthy of further clinical validation and consideration in appropriate patient populations undergoing patellar fracture surgery.
