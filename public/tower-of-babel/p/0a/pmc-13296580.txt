Quantitative Neuromuscular MRI in CIDP Patients: Interest of a Multiparametric Approach
Authors: A Guérémy, E Fortanier, C P Michel, G Corazza, A Ogier, A Trabelsi, A Grapperon, A Verschueren, E Salort‐Campana, M Guye, E Delmont, D Bendahan, S Attarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveChronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is characterized by heterogeneous clinical presentations and the absence of validated imaging biomarkers. Although several quantitative MRI (qMRI) parameters have been investigated individually, their respective diagnostic and clinical relevance remains unclear.MethodsTwenty‐eight CIDP patients (21 typical, 7 multifocal) were prospectively enrolled and compared with 13 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls and 17 patients with symptomatic hereditary transthyretin amyloid neuropathy (vATTR). All participants underwent 1.5 T qMRI of the sciatic and tibial nerves and of the thigh and leg muscles. Nerve and muscle volume, magnetization transfer ratio (MTR), fat fraction (FF), and T2 relaxation time were extracted. Clinical assessment included ONLS, RODS, and MRC sum score, while electrophysiological evaluation included fibular compound motor action potentials and tibialis anterior MUNIX. Imaging parameters were compared between groups and correlated with clinical and neurophysiological measures.ResultsCIDP patients showed significant differences compared with controls in both nerve and muscle metrics. Nerve volume was increased and MTR decreased at both sciatic and tibial levels (p < 0.005). At the muscle level, leg muscles showed increased fat fraction and decreased MTR, while T2 values and muscle volume did not differ significantly. MTR showed the strongest correlations with disability scores and MUNIX. Compared with vATTR patients, CIDP patients had lower sciatic nerve MTR, whereas vATTR patients exhibited larger nerve volumes and more pronounced muscle abnormalities.ConclusionsNerve volume appears to be a sensitive diagnostic marker, while MTR may reflect disease burden. A multiparametric qMRI approach may improve diagnosis and monitoring in CIDP.Keywords: biomarkers, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy, CIDP, fat fraction, magnetization transfer ratio, quantitative MRI

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is an immune‐mediated neuropathy characterized by a motor or sensory disability, with a disease course that may be progressive or relapsing [1]. The diagnosis and management of CIDP is complicated by the diversity of clinical presentations and the variable disease trajectories. It is further challenged by the absence of reliable and objective biomarkers which could be used for the diagnosis and the tracking of disease progression [2]. The most recent EFNS/PNS revision of diagnostic criteria [3] for CIDP delineates six clinical forms including typical CIDP and five CIDP variants, that is, distal, multifocal, focal, motor, and sensory CIDP. While electrophysiological studies remain the cornerstone for establishing a diagnosis and defining CIDP subtypes, they are technically variable, time‐consuming, and provide limited information for longitudinal follow‐up, which often relies on clinical scales with modest reproducibility [4]. Over the past years, neuromuscular imaging has gained increasing attention as a valuable complement to conventional diagnostic approaches. Imaging techniques such as ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have been used to visualize nerve morphology. MRI is offering the added advantage of assessing deep or proximal nerve segments, such as the lumbosacral plexus or the sciatic nerve [5]. However, conventional neuromuscular MRI assessments are largely qualitative and do not provide metrics that could be used for longitudinal follow‐up. In contrast, quantitative MRI (qMRI) can deliver objective and reproducible metrics, enhancing its potential clinical utility [6, 7]. Several studies have investigated the sensitivity and usefulness of various nerve and muscle MRI biomarkers for the diagnosis and monitoring of CIDP often with some discrepancies. While nerve volume—mainly assessed through cross‐sectional area (CSA)—appears to hold some diagnostic value [8], most studies have been conducted in a limited number of subjects. In addition, the diagnostic utility of other MRI parameters, such as T2 relaxation times, fat fraction (FF), or magnetization transfer ratio (MTR) is still a matter of debate. Moreover, there is limited consensus regarding the potential correlations between these imaging biomarkers and the main clinical electrophysiological or scores [9, 10]. In addition to nerve evaluation, qMRI of lower limb muscles might be of high interest if one intends to assess secondary changes related to denervation, atrophy, and fatty infiltration. Few studies [11, 12] have investigated the diagnostic and prognostic value of nerve‐ and muscle‐based qMRI metrics in CIDP. To fill this gap, we systematically compared the most conventional qMRI biomarkers in a well‐characterized cohort of CIDP patients to understand how this tool could be integrated into future diagnostic guidelines. Our objective was first to identify the most sensitive muscle and nerve qMRI markers that distinguish CIDP patients from healthy controls. We further sought to determine correlations with clinical and electrophysiological measures of disease severity, and to clarify the respective diagnostic and monitoring value of nerve‐ and muscle‐based qMRI metrics. Finally, to compare CIDP with a group of axonal neuropathies that can cause diagnostic problems, we selected a group of symptomatic amyloid neuropathies that underwent the same imaging protocol to analyze the most robust qMRI metrics.

Methods

Patients

Twenty‐one typical and seven multifocal CIDP patients were prospectively included in the Referral center for neuromuscular diseases and ALS in La Timone Hospital University in Marseille from December 2016 to November 2021. CIDP patients included in the study were those fulfilling the EAN/PNS electrophysiological criteria [2] (typical or variant forms) and who underwent a lower limb qMRI during their clinical follow‐up. Patients were compared with an age‐matched cohort of 13 healthy controls and 17 symptomatic genetically confirmed hereditary transthyretin amyloid neuropathy patients followed in our center who had been scanned using an identical qMRI protocol. Only patients presenting with a motor or sensory deficit, or an electrophysiological abnormality in the imaged lower limb, were retained for analysis. All CIDP patients but three were treated with intravenous immunoglobulins (IVIG). Patients were excluded from the study if they had another neuromuscular disorder. For patients with CIDP, demographic data and results of functional scores were recorded including Overall Neuropathy Limitation Scale (ONLS) [13] and Rasch‐built Overall Disability Scale (RODS) [14]. The MRC sum score was calculated by summing the MRC scores of four upper limb muscles (deltoids, wrist extensors, abductor pollicis brevis, first dorsal interossei) and four lower limb muscles (psoas, quadriceps, tibialis anterior (TA), and gastrocnemius‐soleus complex). Age of symptoms onset, disease duration and age at evaluation were also recorded. For patients with amyloid neuropathy, only demographic data (age, sex, height, and weight) were collected. This prospective and observational study was approved by the French Ethic Committee (RCB No. 2016‐A00852‐49) and has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Nerve Conduction Study

Nerve conduction study (NCS) was performed using a Medtronic Keypoint G4 device. Compound muscle action potentials (CMAP) and F wave latency were registered on the imaged side by trained neurologists (E.D., E.F., S.A., A.G.). CMAP was recorded from the extensor digitorum brevis, and the flexor digitorum brevis for common fibular and tibial nerves, respectively. Motor conduction velocity was not included as a study parameter because it could only be calculated when a stepped motor conduction study was performed, which was not systematically available, particularly in patients with low CMAP amplitudes where measurements are often unreliable. In total, motor EDX parameters included tibial and fibular nerves distal CMAP and latency and F wave latency. Sensory nerve conduction recordings were performed antidromically by positioning surface electrodes on the dorsum of the foot, between the first and second metatarsal bones and posterior to the lateral malleolus for the superficial fibular and sural nerves, respectively. The MUNIX [15] (Motor Unit Number Index) and MUSIX (Motor Unit Size Index) scores were calculated for all patients based on recordings from the tibialis anterior muscle of the limb assessed by MRI as described in a previous study [16]. For all CIDP patients, the interval between nerve conduction study clinical assessment and MRI did not exceed 2 weeks.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging

MRI examinations were performed on a 1.5 T scanner (MAGNETOM Avanto; Siemens AG, Healthcare Sector, Erlangen, Germany) with patients in the supine position. Leg and thigh of the clinically and electrophysiologically affected limb were imaged using a combination of multi‐channel flexible coils (Siemens) on the top and the spine coils integrated in the scanner bed on the bottom. Following the initial localization process conducted in the three orthogonal planes, axial images were acquired using the following sequences as previously described [17]:

a 3D T1‐weighted turbo‐spin echo (TSE) sequence for anatomical reference,

a 3D T1‐weighted turbo‐spin echo (TSE) sequence for anatomical reference,

a 3D gradient‐recalled‐echo sequence with selective water excitation (GRE‐WE),

a 3D gradient‐recalled‐echo sequence with selective water excitation (GRE‐WE),

a 3D multiecho gradient‐recalled‐echo sequence (DIXON),

a 3D multiecho gradient‐recalled‐echo sequence (DIXON),

a 2D multi‐echo TSE sequence.

a 2D multi‐echo TSE sequence.

a 3D gradient‐recalled‐echo sequence acquired both with and without a magnetization transfer saturation pulse.

a 3D gradient‐recalled‐echo sequence acquired both with and without a magnetization transfer saturation pulse.

All MRI sequence parameters are reported in the Table S1. Images were acquired from 20 cm length regions from both thigh and leg. As part of a normalization procedure, each region was identified from specific proximal landmarks, that is, the appearance of the short head of the biceps femoris muscle for the thigh and the disappearance of the popliteus muscle for the leg. For volume quantification, the same number of slices was analyzed in all subjects within a given anatomical region. To achieve this, the minimum number of common slices available across all participants was applied to every segmentation of the corresponding region. Accordingly, 12 slices were selected for the thigh and 11 slices for the leg in all participants.

Each acquisition was used to generate a voxel‐wise quantitative map. As previously described, the 3D multi‐echo gradient echo (GRE) acquisition was processed to generate separate water and fat images and to compute the proton density fat fraction (PDFF) [18]. T2 values of water and fat were computed using the AMARES [19] algorithm with a priori information related to the T2 fat component derived from the PDFF map (2T2W). Magnetization transfer ratio (MTR) maps were computed from the relative difference between MT‐saturated (I 1) and non‐saturated (I 0) images, using the formula: MTR = 100 × (I 0 – I1)/I 0. This value was corrected for B1 inhomogeneities using a dedicated B1 map as previously described [20]. In addition to total muscle volume, the contractile muscle volume was computed as the fat‐free volume. MTR values of tibial and leg muscles could not be computed for one patient due to technical issues.

Manual segmentation of the sciatic and tibial nerves was performed using FSLeyes (FMRIB Software Library, University of Oxford [21]) on the T1‐weighted images, by a trained neurologist (AG), blinded to the clinical diagnosis. Nerve volume was measured (in cm3), and values were normalized to body surface area (BSA). Muscle segmentation of 16 individual muscles was performed automatically using a nnU‐Net trained neural network as previously described [22, 23] (Figure 1). AI‐based segmentations were quality‐checked (QC) by the same blinded operator. Segmented masks were then resampled and registered to the quantitative maps (MTR, T2, water T2, fat fraction) and the mean values within each region of interest (ROI) were computed.

Statistical Analysis

Clinical and electrophysiological data are presented as median values (IQR). Data distributions were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Given the non‐normal distribution of most variables and the predefined comparisons of interest, group analyses were performed using non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U tests (Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests) for pairwise comparisons. Specifically, comparisons were conducted between CIDP patients and healthy controls, and between CIDP patients and vATTR patients. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. ROC curves were constructed for qMRI parameters showing significant group differences in order to assess their diagnostic performance, using AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and optimal threshold values.

For correlation analyses, multivariate linear regression models were performed using a stepwise selection approach, starting from a set of candidate MRI variables including nerve and muscle MTR, volume and fat fraction, in order to identify the predictors most strongly associated with clinical or electrophysiological scores. Variables were retained based on their explanatory contribution according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. No additional adjustment for covariates was performed.

Missing data were handled as follows: one patient had missing muscle MTR values due to technical acquisition failure and was excluded only from analyses involving this parameter. All other analyses included the full cohort (n = 28). All statistical analyses were performed blinded using R (version 4.2) and RStudio.

qMRI Comparison Between CIDP and Controls

Nerve Metrics Analysis (Figure 2)

Relevant nerve and muscle MRI metrics between CIDP patients and controls. Significance levels: **p < 0.01 (Mann–Whitney test).

Nerve Volume

Sciatic nerve and tibial nerve volume were significantly higher in CIDP patients compared with healthy controls (5.49 vs. 3.69 cm3, p = 0.001 and 0.66 vs. 0.55 cm3, p < 0.001, respectively).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed good discrimination of CIDP patients from healthy controls for both sciatic and tibial nerve volumes, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.82 (95% CI 0.69–0.95) for the sciatic nerve and 0.84 (95% CI 0.71–0.96) for the tibial nerve. Optimal thresholds were 4.12 cm3 (sensitivity 78.6%, specificity 84.6%) and 0.61 cm3 (sensitivity 75%, specificity 77%), respectively.

MTR

Sciatic and tibial nerve MTR values were significantly lower in CIDP patients compared with controls (25.23% vs. 29.04%, p = 0.026 and 38.95% vs. 43.19%, p = 0.023, respectively).

ROC analysis showed moderate discrimination of CIDP versus controls for sciatic (AUC = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.56–0.90) and tibial nerve MTR (AUC = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.56–0.88), with optimal thresholds of 30.6% for the sciatic nerve (sensitivity 75%, specificity 84.6%) and 42.3% for the tibial nerve (sensitivity 77.8%, specificity 69%), respectively.

T2

No significant difference in T2 values was observed between groups for either the sciatic or tibial nerves.

Muscle Metrics Analysis (Figure 2)

Volume

Total thigh and leg muscle volume did not differ significantly between CIDP and controls.

Fat Fraction (FF)

FF values were significantly higher in the leg muscles of patients with CIDP compared to healthy controls (median 6.97% vs. 5.87%, p = 0.002). No differences were observed in the thigh muscles.

ROC analysis showed good discrimination for leg muscle fat fraction (AUC = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.66–0.94) and moderate discrimination for thigh fat fraction (AUC = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.50–0.85), with optimal thresholds of 6.33% (sensitivity 71%, specificity 69%) and 5.77% (sensitivity 82%, specificity 54%), respectively.

MTR

Median leg muscle MTR was lower in patients with CIDP compared with healthy controls (47.81 vs. 49.93%, p < 0.001). No differences were observed in the thigh muscles.

ROC analysis showed good discrimination for leg muscle MTR in identifying CIDP versus controls (AUC = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.72–0.96), while discrimination was moderate at the thigh level (AUC = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.49–0.83), with optimal thresholds of 48.6% (sensitivity 82%, specificity 69%) and 50.4% (sensitivity 71%, specificity 69%), respectively.

T2

No significant difference in T2 values was observed between groups for either the thigh or leg muscles.

Discussion

To our knowledge, only limited studies [24] have combined multiparametric quantitative MRI of both peripheral nerves and muscles with detailed clinical and electrophysiological assessments in CIDP and none have systematically compared these findings with the axonal non‐inflammatory neuropathy group such as hereditary transthyretin amyloid neuropathy to assess the discriminative value of these imaging biomarkers.

In the present study, sciatic and tibial nerve volumes were significantly increased in CIDP patients as compared to controls. Interestingly, patients with symptomatic amyloid neuropathy exhibited even larger nerve volumes, with significant differences between vATTR and CIDP for both sciatic and tibial nerves. This result is consistent with previous reports showing nerve enlargement—from cross‐sectional area (CSA) measurements of the roots [9], sciatic, and tibial [10] nerves. Although nerve volume has to be computed from multiple slices, it might be more clinically meaningful than CSA which is often measured from a single slice. It might be advocated that 3D nerve volume better reflects the multifocal and heterogeneous nature of nerve involvement in CIDP, whereas CSA is limited by its inability to capture segmental variability and its susceptibility to partial volume effects, which may reduce measurement accuracy and reproducibility. This supports the notion that imaging biomarkers could capture the spatial heterogeneity of nerve injury in multifocal CIDP, and potentially aid in phenotypic differentiation when used alongside clinical and electrophysiological data. In CIDP, nerve enlargement is likely driven by inflammatory demyelination, remyelination, and endoneurial oedema, together with Schwann cell proliferation. In contrast, in amyloid neuropathy, the marked increase in nerve volume probably reflects progressive and homogenous extracellular amyloid deposition within endoneurial and perineurial compartments, leading to a more pronounced infiltrative enlargement.

In agreement with multiple previous studies [16, 25], no correlation was found between the volumes of the sciatic and tibial nerves and clinical or electrophysiological scores. On the contrary to earlier studies [26], we did not observe a correlation between nerve volume and disease duration. This may reflect the complex, non‐linear course of CIDP, where inflammatory and structural changes fluctuate over time and are influenced by treatment. Recurrent cycles of demyelination and remyelination [27], along with phases of clinical stability, may disrupt any straightforward relationship with disease duration. Moreover, chronic inflammatory processes—such as macrophage infiltration and endoneurial fibrosis—may lead to secondary axonal degeneration over time, which could reduce nerve volume and potentially mask the hypertrophy typically associated with inflammatory activity, particularly in more advanced stages. These opposing mechanisms may contribute to the lack of correlation observed in our cohort.

Magnetization transfer ratio (MTR) is an interesting quantitative MRI parameter reflecting tissue microstructure through interactions between free and bound protons. In a previous study [28] involving a small CIDP cohort, reduced MTR values were reported in leg muscles, with strong correlations to distal muscle strength but not to disease duration. Our results further support and extend these findings. MTR reductions were observed not only in muscles but also in peripheral nerves, particularly in the tibial and sciatic segments. Of interest, a similarly nerve MTR decrease has been reported in other neuropathies such as CMT [29], diabetic polyneuropathy [30], and in hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis [31]. In our cohort, MTR reduction was more pronounced at the sciatic nerve level in CIDP compared with vATTR, which is consistent with the predominantly proximal involvement observed in inflammatory demyelinating neuropathies. In contrast, amyloid neuropathy typically follows a length‐dependent pattern with preferential distal axonal degeneration, which may explain the less marked proximal MTR alteration in this group.

On the contrary, in our cohort, muscle MTR values were lower in the leg than in the thigh, consistent with a distal‐to‐proximal gradient. This distribution may reflect the typical pattern of denervation in CIDP and could be similar to the previously reported fat fraction gradient in CMT patients [10]. One explanation is the technical correlation between MTR and FF, as an increase in intramuscular FF can lead to a decrease in MTR. It therefore seems interesting to assess the MTR proximally for the nerve and distally for the muscle.

Importantly, muscle and nerve MTR in the lower limbs were the qMRI metrics most strongly correlated with clinical and electrophysiological markers, including muscle strength (MRC of TA) and motor amplitudes (common fibular nerve). These findings support the use of MTR as a sensitive, non‐invasive marker of disease burden and progression in peripheral neuropathies.

Water T2 relaxation times of nerves were similar in patients with CIDP, vATTR and healthy controls. Nerve T2 values did not significantly correlate with clinical or electrophysiological parameters, suggesting limited utility as a marker of disease progression. This is consistent with the heterogeneous and often conflicting findings reported in the literature. Some plexus‐level studies have shown marked T2 increase which has been assigned to an acute endoneurial oedema and demyelination [32]. In other studies, conducted in chronically treated CIDP patients, T2 values from the sciatic nerve were normal, likely reflecting oedema resolution. In our cohort, muscle T2 values did not differ significantly between CIDP patients and healthy controls, which is concordant with the literature. In contrast, vATTR patients showed significantly higher muscle T2 values compared with CIDP patients in both the thigh and the leg. Several studies in axonal neuropathies, including CMT [33] and diabetic polyneuropathy [34], have reported increased muscle T2 values, interpreted as reflecting chronic denervation, extracellular expansion, and structural remodeling. The more pronounced muscle T2 alterations observed in our amyloidosis cohort are therefore physiopathologically coherent, supporting the notion that muscle T2 may be more sensitive to advanced axonal degeneration than to predominantly demyelinating inflammatory processes such as CIDP.

Previous studies have shown that muscle FF, as assessed by quantitative MRI, can reliably distinguish patients with chronic neuropathies from healthy controls [10]. Moreover, a distal‐to‐proximal gradient has been reported through the assessment of FF measured indirectly via the assessment of both total and contractile muscle volumes [25], reflecting the length‐dependent pattern of axonal degeneration. Our findings are consistent with this pattern. Muscle fat fraction showed a distal‐to‐proximal gradient in both CIDP and vATTR patients, consistent with length‐dependent denervation. While FF was moderately increased in CIDP, values were generally higher in vATTR patients at the thigh level, while differences at the leg level did not reach statistical significance. In CIDP, where axonal damage is secondary and potentially mitigated by treatment, fatty replacement may remain limited. In contrast, the progressive axonal loss characteristic of amyloid neuropathy results in more pronounced and widespread fat infiltration. Together, these findings suggest that FF captures the chronic burden of axonal degeneration, whereas MTR may be more sensitive to microstructural alterations and ongoing tissue remodeling. Of note, our FF values were slightly low (≤ 7% in the leg, ≤ 5% in the thigh) and definitely lower than the 15%–25% values typically reported in long‐standing hereditary neuropathies [35]. This likely reflects the earlier disease stage in our cohort and the potentially slowing denervation and limiting fat replacement occurring as a result of the treatment, which could limit its use as a longitudinal biomarker in this acquired neuropathy.

In total, besides pronounced differences in most muscle and nerve qMRI metrics as compared to healthy controls, we also identified a distinctive qMRI pattern allowing discrimination between CIDP and vATTR neuropathy. vATTR patients exhibited homogenous larger sciatic and tibial nerve volumes, together with lower muscle MTR and higher fat fraction at the thigh level, and increased muscle T2 values at both thigh and leg levels. Interestingly, sciatic nerve MTR was significantly decreased in CIDP patients compared to vATTR patients.

This study has some limitations. Its cross‐sectional and monocentric design may restrict the generalizability of the findings, and the absence of histopathological correlation limits the ability to directly validate qMRI‐derived biomarkers. In addition, not all clinical variants of CIDP were represented, which may limit the applicability of these results to the broader spectrum of CIDP phenotypes. Another limitation is that inter‐rater and test–retest reliability of MRI measurements were not formally assessed in this study. Although nerve and muscle segmentations were performed or quality‐checked by a trained evaluator blinded to clinical data, no intraclass correlation coefficients were calculated to quantify reproducibility. The uniform treatment regimen, with most patients receiving intravenous immunoglobulin, may also have introduced a treatment‐related bias potentially affecting qMRI measurements. Future studies should include dedicated reliability analyses to confirm the technical robustness of these quantitative MRI measures.

Overall, our multiparametric qMRI approach revealed complementary spatial features of CIDP, with diffuse nerve hypertrophy and distal muscle involvement. The observed correlations suggest an association between nerve and muscle changes, without implying a direct pathological link. Within the current diagnostic framework, qMRI should not be viewed as a replacement for established tools such as nerve ultrasound, which remains more accessible and already integrated into clinical practice. Rather, our results suggest that qMRI may provide complementary information, particularly by offering a comprehensive and quantitative assessment of both proximal nerve segments and downstream muscle alterations in a single examination. In this context, nerve volume appears diagnostically valuable, while MTR and FF seem to reflect disease severity and chronic denervation. This combined strategy warrants further validation across neuropathy subtypes and in longitudinal studies before any consideration of integration into routine diagnostic algorithms.

Conclusion

Quantitative MRI of nerves and muscles in CIDP provides complementary information on structural nerve and muscle alterations. While nerve volume demonstrated discriminatory capacity in our cohort, its diagnostic performance remains moderate and should not be interpreted as a stand‐alone diagnostic tool. Muscle MTR and fat fraction values appear to reflect chronic structural changes and may represent promising biomarkers of disease severity and progression. Overall, a multiparametric qMRI approach may serve as a complementary assessment tool within the current diagnostic framework, particularly in complex or atypical clinical presentations, but requires further validation in prospective and diagnostically challenging populations.
