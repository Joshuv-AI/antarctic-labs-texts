Childhood Adiposity and Risk of Major Clinical Heart Diseases in Adulthood: A Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Yan Xiong, Yijia Tang, Jie Zhou, Yang Tian, Fuli Chen, Gang Li, Hui Huang, Hao Huang, Long Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe causal relationship between childhood adiposity and adult risk of heart diseases has not been clearly demonstrated. This study aims to ascertain whether genetically predicted childhood body mass index (BMI) and childhood obesity are causally associated with adult coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and pulmonary heart disease.Methods and ResultsTo investigate the causative relationships and underlying mechanisms between childhood adiposity and adult heart diseases, 3 main methods of Mendelian randomization were used: 2‐sample Mendelian randomization, multivariable Mendelian randomization with controlling for several cardiometabolic risk variables, and mediation analysis. Every 1‐SD rise in genetically predicted childhood body mass index was associated with 24% (odds ratio [OR], 1.24 [95% CI, 1.12–1.37]), 28% (OR, 1.28 [95% CI, 1.14–1.42]), 28% (OR, 1.28 [95% CI, 1.14–1.42]), and 27% (OR, 1.27 [95% CI, 1.04–1.49]) higher risk of coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation, respectively. Every 1‐unit increase in log‐odds in childhood obesity was associated with 11% (OR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.06–1.16]), 14% (OR, 1.14 [95% CI, 1.04–1.23]), 10% (OR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.03–1.18]), and 20% (OR, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.08–1.32]) higher risk of coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation, respectively. The link between childhood adiposity and adult heart diseases was found to be mediated by high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglyceride, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes.ConclusionsOur findings support the causal relationships between childhood adiposity and risk of adult coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and atrial fibrillation. Blood lipids, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes are factors that mediate the aforementioned associations.

Childhood obesity has been referred to as a global pandemic. In both developed and developing countries, the prevalence of childhood obesity has significantly increased over the past 40 years. 1 According to estimates, the prevalence of US children aged 6 to 11 years who are obese went from 11.3% in 1988 to 1994 to 19.6% in 2007 to 2008, and then remained unchanged in 2013 to 2014. 2 Over the past 40 years, there has been a comparable rise in the prevalence of obesity among Chinese children. A report from the Chinese National Survey on Students' Constitution and Health states that the prevalence of obesity in children and adolescents aged 7 to 18 years has increased from 0.1% in 1985 to 7.3% in 2014. 3

Several observational studies have linked childhood obesity to an increased adult risk of cardiovascular diseases, but the findings are still debatable. The relationship between body mass index (BMI) in childhood (ages 7–13 years) and coronary heart disease (CHD) in adulthood was examined in a prospective cohort study conducted in Copenhagen. According to the study, the risk of a CHD incident increased considerably with every unit increase in BMI z score for boys and girls between the ages of 7 and 13. 4 A population‐based cohort study from Denmark found that above‐average BMI in childhood (ages 7–13 years) was associated with an increased risk of atrial fibrillation (AF) in adulthood. 5 However, a study from the Scottish Mental Survey concluded that childhood BMI was essentially unrelated to the risks of total cardiovascular disease, CHD, myocardial infarction (MI), and heart failure (HF) after a maximum of 67 years of follow‐up. 6

Observational studies may yield contradictory results because of variations in the study population, confounding factor selection, follow‐up duration, and other factors. In situations where conducting a randomized controlled trial would be impractical or impossible, Mendelian randomization (MR), a technique for evaluating potential causal relationships between modifiable risk factors and diseases using genetic variants associated with exposures of interest, has been widely regarded as analogous to an randomized controlled trial. 7 , 8 The potential causal relationship between childhood BMI and adult CHD risk has been investigated in 2 prior MR analyses. 9 , 10 Two other MR studies investigated the associations of genetically predicted childhood obesity with adult risks of CHD 11 and AF. 12 Most of the aforementioned research employed a 2‐sample MR study design and did not take into account the links between childhood obesity and cardiometabolic risk factors. Heart disease is the leading cause of death worldwide and encompasses a variety of disorders related to the circulatory system. 13

The present study aims to use 2‐sample MR, multivariable MR with adjustment for multiple cardiometabolic risk factors, and mediation analysis to determine whether genetically predicted childhood BMI and childhood obesity are causally associated with several major clinical heart diseases, such as CHD, MI, HF, AF, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and pulmonary heart disease.

Methods

All data and materials have been made publicly available at the University of Bristol's Medical Research Council Integrative Epidemiology Unit OpenGWAS Project Database and can be accessed at https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/.

Study Design

Three steps comprised the study design for the current investigation. Using a 2‐sample MR design, we first explored the potential causal effects of childhood BMI and childhood obesity on the risk of 6 main clinical heart disease traits (ie, CHD, MI, HF, AF, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and pulmonary heart disease). To further evaluate the influence of other cardiometabolic risk variables on causal estimations, we used the multivariable MR approach if 2‐sample MR analysis showed a causal association between childhood obesity and heart disease. Cardiometabolic risk factors that attenuated the association between childhood obesity and heart disease to no longer significant were further studied via mediation analysis to assess the mediation effects on the causal pathway from childhood obesity to adult heart disease. Data Sources

Summary‐level genetic data for childhood BMI were extracted from a meta‐analysis consisting of 26 studies with a total sample size of 39 620 children of European ancestry by Vogelezang et al. 14 As another exposure, genetic data for childhood obesity were extracted from the Early Growth Genetics Consortium, which included 5530 cases (≥95th percentile of BMI) and 8318 controls (<50th percentile of BMI) with European ancestry. 15 For clinical heart disease outcomes, CHD and MI were obtained from the Coronary Artery Disease Genome‐Wide Replication and Meta Analysis Plus the Coronary Artery Disease Genetics consortium. 16 Summary statistics for HF, AF, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and pulmonary heart disease were extracted from the FinnGen (Finnish Genomics) study, which is a research project combining imputed genotype data generated from newly collected and legacy samples of Finnish biobanks. 17 Summary‐level data for confounders and mediators were obtained from the UK Biobank for adulthood BMI, 18 low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), 19 high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), 19 triglycerides, 19 and hypertension 20 ; and from a European‐specific genome‐wide association study for type 2 diabetes. 21 A description of the data sources included in the present study is shown in Table 1. Studies included in this analysis received ethical approval from their respective institutional review boards. Informed consent was obtained from all participants of the original studies.

Single‐Nucleotide Polymorphism Selection

We identified single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) linked to childhood BMI or childhood obesity at a genome‐wide significance level (P<5×10−8). Lack of linkage disequilibrium among the selected SNPs is a prerequisite for MR analysis, which helps avoid potential confounding by SNPs that have significant associations with other risk factor loci. 22 To ensure that SNPs are independent, we pruned the variants by linkage disequilibrium using clump_data() function (clumping window of 10 000 kb, r2 threshold of 0.001). Additionally, the selected SNPs must not be directly related to the outcome of the MR analysis. SNPs that had robust (P<5×10−8) associations with corresponding clinical heart disease outcomes were thus excluded. Furthermore, every SNP must affect the risk of the outcome exclusively through exposure in MR analysis because including SNPs that contribute through pleiotropic pathways could skew estimates. We employed the MR Egger intercept test to assess whether genetic variants have pleiotropic effects on the outcome that differ on average from 0 (directional pleiotropy). An estimate of the average pleiotropic effect across all genetic variations can be determined by using the estimated value of the intercept in Egger regression. 23 We used the F statistic to determine risk of weak instrument bias. When the F statistic is >10, it can be considered that the selected genetic instruments are unlikely to be affected by weak instrument bias. More details on SNP selection principles have been explained elsewhere. 24 , 25

Statistical Analysis

The summary data for childhood adiposity exposures and heart disease outcomes were harmonized so that the effect estimates of SNPs on exposure and outcome were shown for the same alleles. Since the inverse variance‐weighted (IVW) method is thought to be the most reliable MR method when pleiotropy is absent, it was chosen as the main strategy for 2‐sample MR analysis. To strengthen the credibility of the results, we also used 5 complementary 2‐sample MR approaches (fixed‐effect model, random‐effect model, maximum likelihood method, weighted median method, and MR Egger regression method) as supplemental methods. Sensitivity analyses using the leave‐1‐out approach were performed to verify that the addition of SNPs did not introduce random error into our results. Additionally, a multivariable MR approach was used to intervene in the influence of potential cardiometabolic risk factors on causal associations between childhood adiposity and clinical heart disease outcomes in adulthood with controlling for adulthood BMI, LDL‐C, HDL‐C, triglycerides, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes, respectively. The Benjamini and Hochberg method with a false discovery rate of 0.05 was used to correct P values in multivariable MR analysis. To examine the mediation effects on the causal pathway from childhood adiposity to adult heart diseases, cardiometabolic risk factors that significantly attenuated the link between childhood adiposity and heart disease outcome were also examined using mediation analysis. The effect estimates are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs of heart disease outcomes per 1‐SD increment in childhood BMI or ORs with 95% CIs of childhood obesity per 1‐unit‐higher log‐odds. All analyses were performed using R version 3.6.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

The characteristics of selected SNPs associated with childhood BMI or childhood obesity are presented in Tables S1 and S2. Six SNPs were found to be significantly associated with childhood obesity, and 17 SNPs were found to be significantly associated with childhood BMI at a genome‐wide significance level. The strength of the genetic instruments denoted by the F statistic was >10 for all the childhood BMI and childhood obesity variants and ranged between 30 and 109. The total variance explained by SNPs in childhood BMI and childhood obesity–related instrumental variables was 2.25% and 1.84%, respectively. None of the SNPs were in linkage disequilibrium with one another at r2>0.001. A single SNP (rs571312) was removed from the CHD outcome due to its significant (P<5×10−8) association with CHD. Two palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies from childhood obesity– and childhood BMI–associated SNPs (rs1040070 and rs2076308) were also removed. Thus, in the CHD‐related analyses, 4 SNPs related to childhood obesity and 15 related to childhood BMI were retained. The MR analyses for other outcomes found 5 SNPs linked to childhood obesity and 16 SNPs associated with childhood BMI. A flowchart that depicts SNP screening is shown in Figure S1.

The results of the two‐sample MR analysis showed that, although there was no significant correlation with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or pulmonary heart disease, both genetically predicted childhood BMI and childhood obesity were positively associated with the risk of CHD, MI, HF, and AF (Figure 2). In our analysis, estimates from the MR Egger regression were inconsistent with those from other approaches. However, IVW estimates were prioritized for interpreting results when the P value of pleiotropy obtained from the Egger intercept test was >0.05. The results from the IVW method indicated that a 1‐SD increase in childhood BMI are causally associated with 24% to 28% increases in the risk of CHD (OR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.12–1.37]; P=4.54×10−4), MI (OR, 1.28 [95% CI, 1.14–1.42]; P=4.09×10−4), HF (OR, 1.28 [95% CI, 1.14–1.42]; P=7.59×10−4), and AF (OR, 1.27 [95% CI, 1.04–1.49]; P=4.19×10−2). We also found that 1‐unit‐higher log‐odds in childhood obesity are causally associated with 10% to 20% increases in the risk of CHD (OR, 1.11 [95% CI, 1.06–1.16]; P=7.20×10−5), MI (OR, 1.14 [95% CI, 1.04–1.23]; P=8.80×10−3), HF (OR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.03–1.18]; P=1.10×10−2), and AF (OR, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.08–1.32]; P=3.91×10−3). MR leave‐1‐out sensitivity analysis showed a robust relationship between childhood adiposity and adult heart diseases (see Figures S2 and S3).

The results of multivariable MR associations of childhood BMI and childhood obesity with CHD, MI, HF, and AF risk after respectively adjusting for several cardiometabolic risk factors are shown in Figure 3. There were substantial associations of childhood BMI and childhood obesity with the 4 outcomes for heart disease listed above, even after accounting for genetically predicted adulthood BMI. However, the relationship between childhood BMI and CHD became less significant when adjusting for LDL‐C, HDL‐C, triglycerides, or type 2 diabetes. Likewise, the association between childhood BMI and MI also lost significance when adjusting for LDL‐C, HDL‐C, triglyceride, or type 2 diabetes, and the relationship between childhood BMI and AF was no longer significant after adjusting for LDL or triglyceride. Additionally, the association between childhood BMI and HF was unaffected by accounting for cardiometabolic risk variables. Although there are some minor differences in specifics, as illustrated in Figure 3, the multivariable MR analysis results of childhood obesity and 4 outcomes for heart disease are generally comparable to childhood BMI. To verify whether cardiometabolic risk factors that significantly decreased the causal relationship as shown in multivariable MR analyses were mediating the effects of childhood BMI and childhood obesity on heart disease outcomes, we further conducted mediation analyses (Tables 2 and 3). We found that the effects of childhood BMI on the risk of CHD and MI were attenuated by LDL‐C, with a mediation effect of −0.0202 (95% CI, −0.0324 to −0.0081) for CHD and − 0.0189 (95% CI, −0.0305 to −0.0074) for MI. We also found that HDL‐C mediated the effect of childhood BMI on CHD and MI with a mediated proportion of 11.6% (95% CI, 6.9%–16.3%) and 9.5% (95% CI, 5.4%–13.6%), respectively. Similarly, the effects of 18.0% (95% CI, 5.8%–30.1%) on MI, 16.1% (95% CI, 5.0%–27.2%) on HF, 20.4% (95% CI, 6.7%–34.1%) on CHD, and 35.2% (95% CI, 12.1%–58.3%) on AF were all mediated by hypertension. Furthermore, we found that type 2 diabetes mediated the effects of 13.0% (95% CI, 1.5%–24.6%) on HF and 17.9% (95% CI, 6.1%–29.7%) on MI. Discussion

In this study, we applied a variety of approaches, including 2‐sample MR, multivariable MR, and mediation effect analysis, to determine that genetically predicted childhood BMI and childhood obesity were causally associated with the risk of CHD, MI, HF, and AF in adulthood, and that these associations were mediated by cardiometabolic risk factors. We additionally found that there were no associations of genetically predicted childhood BMI or childhood obesity with the risk of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and pulmonary heart disease in adulthood.

Our findings are in line with several previous cohort studies. A cohort of Danish schoolchildren found that higher BMI during childhood (ages 7–13 years) is associated with an increased risk of CHD in adulthood. 4 A meta‐analysis of 4 cohorts demonstrated that there was a 14% (95% CI, 8%–21%) higher risk of adult CHD for every 1‐SD increase in BMI in children aged 7 to 11 years. 26 The BEST (BMI Epidemiology Study), a population‐based cohort study conducted in Sweden, reported that childhood BMI at age 8 years demonstrated a significant linear association with the risk of HF (hazard ratio, 1.13 per SD [95% CI, 1.03–1.26]) without adjusting for BMI change during puberty. 27 A cohort study of 314 140 schoolchildren demonstrated that BMI at all ages from 7 to 13 years was positively associated with an AF diagnosis in adulthood. 5

Other than cohort studies, some MR analyses demonstrated a causal link between childhood BMI or obesity and certain adult heart diseases individually. Two previous MR analyses explored the causal association between genetically predicted childhood BMI and adult CHD. According to Geng et al, there was a 28% increase in the risk of CHD in adulthood (OR, 1.28 [95% CI, 1.17–1.39]; P=2.13×10−8) for each 1‐SD increase in childhood BMI. 9 According to Dong et al, there was a 9% increase in the risk of developing CHD in adulthood (OR, 1.09 [95% CI, 1.06–1.11]; P=4.28×10−11) for every 1‐SD increase in childhood BMI. 10 One MR analysis previously examined the causal relationship between genetically predicted childhood obesity and adult CHD. The 2‐sample MR analysis suggested that each 1‐unit increase in the log‐odds of having childhood obesity was causally associated with an increased risk of adult CHD (OR, 1.07 [95% CI,1.02–1.12]; P=0.004) on the basis of the IVW method. 11 To date, only 1 MR study has evaluated the causal relationship between childhood BMI and childhood obesity with AF. Chen et al used a 2‐sample MR design and found that both a genetically predicted 1‐SD increase in childhood BMI (OR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.11–1.34]; P<0.001) and higher log‐odds of childhood obesity (OR, 1.09 [95% CI, 1.04–1.14]; P<0.001) were significantly associated with an increased risk of AF. 12 Unfortunately, even though the results of these investigations generally support our findings, they did not provide insight into the possible mechanism by which factors altered or mediated the effect of childhood obesity on adult heart disease risks.

We performed multivariable MR analyses with adjustment for various cardiometabolic risk factors, such as LDL‐C, HDL‐C, triglycerides, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes. These cardiometabolic risk indicators were chosen because prior research indicated a strong and positive relationship between childhood obesity and adult triglycerides and blood pressure, as well as a significant and negative relationship between childhood obesity and adult HDL‐C. 28 Previous MR analyses have also demonstrated that childhood BMI and childhood obesity were positively associated with adult type 2 diabetes. 9 , 11 Multivariate MR analysis results indicate that when certain cardiometabolic risk factors are considered, the association between childhood adiposity and adult heart diseases weakens. This suggests that certain cardiometabolic risk factors may mediate the role of childhood adiposity in the development of adult heart diseases. We then employed mediation analysis, an approach frequently used in clinical research to investigate the course of a disease's development, 29 , 30 to verify this hypothesis and ascertain whether these cardiometabolic risk variables mediate the effects of childhood adiposity on adult heart diseases. We found that HDL‐C, triglycerides, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes mediated the association between childhood adiposity and adult CHD, MI, HF, and AF. We also found that LDL‐C attenuated the association between childhood adiposity and adult CHD as well as MI because path A (childhood adiposity on LDL‐C) and path B (LDL‐C on CHD or MI) are of opposite signs but both paths are significant. 31 LDL‐C is a well‐established risk factor for MI and CHD. Our study, along with other previous studies, has demonstrated a positive causal relationship between childhood adiposity and CHD, while we also found a negative correlation between childhood adiposity and LDL‐C. 9 , 11 For the reasons listed above, LDL‐C may counteract the negative impact of childhood obesity on the risk of MI and CHD. More studies are desperately needed to completely understand the inverse association between childhood adiposity and LDL‐C.

The biological processes underlying childhood adiposity‐related SNPs and heart disease are not well understood. FTO, the first identified obesity susceptibility gene, was predicted to be a 2‐oxyglutarate Fe(II)‐dependent demethylase with a potential function in nucleic acid repair or modification. 32 In our analysis, childhood BMI‐related rs17817449 and childhood obesity–related rs9941349 were both FTO SNPs. FTO regulates growth and translation by linking amino acid availability to mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 signaling. 33 FTO SNPs have been linked to an increased risk of type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, HF, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome, as well as elevated insulin levels, liver enzymes, and inflammatory markers. 34 ADCY3, another obesity susceptibility gene, encodes the membrane‐associated enzyme adenyl cyclase 3, which catalyzes the synthesis of cAMP from ATP. cAMP is a second messenger that facilitates intracellular signal transduction. This messenger is involved with the action of kinases in a variety of biological processes, including glucose and lipid metabolism. 35 Childhood BMI–related rs61765651 and rs11676272 in our analysis were ADCY3 SNPs, and ADCY3 was previously identified as a key gene in carotid unstable plaques. 36 The possible mechanisms mentioned above are only speculations based on existing literature, and further research is needed in the future to determine the exact mechanisms underlying the link between childhood adiposity–related SNPs and heart disease.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study is the first to use the MR approach to thoroughly and methodically investigate the causative association of childhood obesity and BMI with 6 main clinical heart diseases, along with mediation analysis on possible mechanism evaluation. Before this, a study examined the causal relationship between body size (thinner/about average/plumper) at age 10 years and several heart diseases in adulthood. However, the participants' recollections and self‐reports provided information on body size, which could have led to differential social desirability bias. 37 There are still issues regarding our study's limitations. First, the summary genome‐wide association study data we used in our analysis came from the European population, which may restrict how we interpret our results and make it difficult to generalize them to populations with different genetic origins. Second, we are unable to determine how population stratification affects our findings because our research relies on genome‐wide association study summary data. Third, it may be challenging to determine which SNPs are particularly linked to childhood BMI since there may be some overlap between the SNPs associated with childhood and adult BMI. Multivariable MR analysis, however, revealed that the causative association between childhood BMI and adult heart diseases remains unchanged when adult BMI is taken into account. Fourth, several outcomes were identified from the FinnGen database, and the control of FinnGen varies for different disease states, which may have an impact on the results. Therefore, it is important to pay attention to this when interpreting the results. Fifth, the results obtained by using MR Egger regression in 2‐sample MR analysis are not consistent with other methods. Although it is theoretically believed that when the P value of pleiotropy is >0.05, MR Egger regression should be considered as a type of sensitivity analysis rather than as an alternative to the more common approaches such as IVW and weighted median methods, 38 the starkly different results provided by the MR Egger suggest that potential pleiotropy may cause biases that undermine main results. Finally, we are unable to provide specific definitions of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and pulmonary heart disease because they were identified via International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes as recorded in the hospital and death registration systems. In the FinnGen study, codes I42.1 and I42.2 were used to identify hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, while codes I26 and I27 were used to identify pulmonary heart disease.

In conclusion, our results provide evidence for the causative associations of childhood obesity and BMI with a higher risk of adult CHD, MI, HF, and AF. The aforementioned associations are mediated in part by cardiometabolic risk factors, including blood lipids, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes. Our findings add credence to the concept that childhood obesity prevention programs in public health could lower the adult incidence of major clinical heart diseases.
