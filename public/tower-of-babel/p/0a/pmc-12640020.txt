Clinical Efficacy of a Home-based Peritoneal Dialysis Management Program
Authors: Do Hyoung Kim, Hayne Cho Park, AJin Cho, Jeonghwan Lee, Ji Hyeon Park, Gang-Jee Ko, Ho Sik Shin, Won Min Hwang, Jinseog Kim, Young-Ki Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPatients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD) require strong self-management support to reduce the risk of PD-related complications; however, the best methods are unclear. The Korean Ministry of Health and Welfare has initiated a chronic disease management pilot program. This study aimed to determine the clinical outcomes and potential effects of a home-based PD management program.MethodsWe analyzed National Health Insurance Service claims data from December 2019 to October 2021. A total of 6086 patients on PD were divided into 2 groups based on whether they were enrolled in a home-based management program. Outcomes included hospitalization rates, emergency room visits, mortality, and technical survival. Cox proportional hazard models were used to analyze the risk of death and technical failure in these groups.ResultsThe proportion of patients enrolled in home-based management and those not enrolled were 51.4% and 48.6%, respectively. Patients in the intervention group had fewer hospitalizations and fewer hospital days per hospitalization than those in the control group. They had significantly fewer emergency room visits than those in the control group. The intervention group had a lower mortality and a reduced risk of technical failure than the control group. This finding was similar among prevalent and incident patients.ConclusionThe home-based management program for PD was clinically effective in improving hospitalization, technical survival, and mortality. This program may provide an efficient treatment option for patients on PD, and positive outcomes could lead to expanded programs that include more patients.

The prevalence of chronic kidney disease and the number of patients requiring kidney replacement therapy is increasing worldwide. Compared with hemodialysis, which is primarily a facility-based treatment, PD is a home-based kidney replacement therapy. PD has several advantages, including fewer hospital visits, fewer dietary restrictions, less stress on the heart and blood vessels, and preservation of residual kidney function.1,2 PD improves overall quality of life and allows patients to maintain their education and socioeconomic activities.3 However, they are at risk of infectious and noninfectious PD-related complications, including peritonitis, exit-site or tunnel infections, abdominal pain, sleep disturbances, and automated PD machine errors.4

Whereas in-center patients on hemodialysis experience frequent monitoring during dialysis, patients on PD are at risk of inappropriate treatment without such monitoring.5 Approximately 28% of patients are noncompliant with prescribed exchanges, and low adherence to treatment prescriptions may be associated with poor outcomes.6 If the home treatment of patients on PD is monitored regularly, problems can be detected early and inadequate dialysis delivery can be corrected.5 Increased public access to and use of information technology and device platforms have enabled remote patient monitoring without the need for face-to-face encounters, helping clinicians provide optimal patient care.7 Communication tools such as mobile phones, e-mail, and software platforms embedded in automated PD cycles can play an important role in delivering the most effective care.8 A cloud-based platform (Sharesource) with a 2-way communication system is routinely used to monitor automated PD treatments.

Patients on PD require strong self-management support to mitigate the risk of various PD-related complications.4 However, the best methods recommended for effective PD education remain unclear.9,10 Ensuring adequate self-management support and training programs is necessary because of the importance of patient education in reducing PD technical failure.4,11 In particular, there is evidence that educating patients on the correct PD technique can reduce the incidence of peritonitis.12,13 It is important to implement refresher programs because the incidence of peritonitis tends to increase over time.14

Considering the importance of continuous self-management for patients on PD, the South Korean Ministry of Health and Welfare launched a home-based management pilot project for health care providers and patients in December 2019. The program provides continuous educational consultations using a multidisciplinary approach involving experts in each field, including doctors and nurses. It has enabled health care providers to provide face-to-face care without the need for hospital visits through regular remote patient monitoring for clinical concerns.

This study compared clinical outcomes and mortality between patients enrolled in a home-based management program and a control group, and evaluated the effectiveness and clinical significance of a home-based management program for patients on PD.

Methods

Data Source and Study Population

This cohort study included the entire adult patients on PD in Korea between December 2019 and October 2021. Data were analyzed using the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service claims data, and patients on PD were extracted using disease codes and PD activity codes. Patients aged ≥ 18 years on PD for ≥ 1 month were included in the study. And patients were categorized as incident PD (those who initiated PD after the start of the home-based management program in December 2019) or prevalent PD (those who had already been receiving PD at program initiation). The intervention group included patients who were enrolled in the home-based management program and had ≥ 1 prescription for either the education consultation fee or the patient monitoring fee. Patients who were not enrolled in the home-based management program comprised the control group. All patients in the intervention group provided written informed consent to participate in the home-based management program. Data were analyzed to determine differences in clinical outcomes (emergency room visits, hospitalizations, and technical survival) and patient deaths between the intervention and control groups.

Demographic and clinical data were obtained from the Health Insurance Review and Assessment database. The demographic factors collected included age, sex, and health insurance status. Sex was defined as biological sex (male or female) as recorded in the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service claims database, and information on gender identity was not available. Medical comorbidities of the participants were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes from the health insurance claims database from January to December, 2019. The comorbidities included diabetes mellitus (E10–14), hypertension (I10–13 and I15), ischemic heart disease (I20–25), cerebrovascular disease (I60–64 and I69), and atrial fibrillation (I48). Pertinent patients were defined as those who had already undergone PD at the start of the study (December 2019). Patients with incidental PD were defined as those who first underwent PD after the study began.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcomes were hospitalization rates, emergency department visit frequency, and all-cause mortality. Hospitalization rate was defined as the number of admissions per patient-year, and emergency visit rate as the number of emergency department visits per patient-year. All-cause mortality was determined based on the date of insurance loss.15 The secondary outcome was technique failure, defined as a transfer from PD to hemodialysis for > 30 days. Technique failure because of death were not considered as technique failure.16,17

Standard Care in the Control Group

Patients in the control group received standard care routinely provided to patients on PD in Korea. At PD initiation, all patients undergo 1 structured education session by both a physician and a PD-trained nurse. Thereafter, additional education is generally provided only if complications occur—such as peritonitis or drainage problems—rather than on a regular basis. Although the general approach to PD care was similar across institutions, there was some variability in the depth and frequency of follow-up depending on staffing and institutional protocols.

Home-Based Management Program for Patients on PD

The home-based management program was designed to provide patients on PD with professional education and regular monitoring of their health status and was launched nationwide by the Ministry of Health and Welfare of Korea in December 2019. All hospitals providing PD care in Korea were informed about the program, and hospitals that agreed to participate were enrolled. Among patients on PD treated at these hospitals, those who consented to participate were assigned to the intervention group. Currently, 54 hospitals nationwide participate in the program, and the program team at each facility consists of ≥ 2 professionals: a doctor (a physician) and a nurse (with ≥ 3 years of experience). To ensure program fidelity, quarterly online meetings were held among participating physicians and PD nurses to review progress, share best-practice cases, and discuss implementation strategies. The program consisted of 3 main components—Education I, Education II, and Monitoring—with standardized educational content as follows: (i) face-to-face educational consultation when patients visit the hospital (Education I and II), and (ii) regular patient monitoring via 2-way phone calls or telehealth interactive messages (Monitoring).18 In contrast to the control group who visited the hospital as usual, the intervention group received Education I, a 1-on-1 educational consultation with a physician or nurse for ≥ 15 minutes; and Education II, a detailed information about PD principles and methods, dialysis fluids, catheter care, weight management, complication prevention and detection, activities of daily living (exercise, bathing, etc.), diet or nutrition, and medication counseling. For Monitoring, medical staff typically checked in with patients twice a month (or once a month if they used Sharesource to monitor patient data) via phone, text, or other 2-way communication methods to determine their condition and clinical indicators, including environmental care, catheter care, dialysis fluid infusion and drainage, dialysis fluid status, and worsening symptoms (shortness of breath, abdominal pain, constipation, edema, decreased urine output, itching, sleep disturbance, nausea, and vomiting). Further details on program structure, service components, and reimbursement criteria are provided in Supplementary Table S1.18

Ethics Statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hallym University Kangnam Sacred Heart Hospital (No. HKS 2021-07-014). The Institutional Review Board waived the need for written informed consent from the patients because the study participants were not identified.

Statistical Analyses

The participants were divided into 2 groups based on their participation in the home-based management program. Baseline characteristics were compared using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the independent t test for continuous variables. Continuous variables are expressed as means and SDs, and categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Kaplan–Meier analysis and log-rank tests were used to compare the risk of death between the groups. Multivariate-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models were used to determine whether the home-based management program was an independent predictor of mortality or PD technique failure. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 was adjusted for Medical Aid status and the factors included in model 1. Model 3 was adjusted for medical comorbidities (diabetes, ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular accident, and atrial fibrillation) and the factors included in model 2. In addition to analyses in the total cohort, all primary and secondary outcomes were evaluated separately for incident and prevalent patients. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Proportion of Program Components

In the home-based PD management Program, Education I and Education II sessions were delivered face-to-face in the hospital, whereas the monitoring component was performed remotely at the patient’s home. During the study period, a total of 52,182 program sessions were delivered, comprising 4019 sessions of Education I (7.7%), 11,564 sessions of Education II (22.2%), and 36,599 monitoring sessions (70.1%). Thus, more than two-thirds of all program activities were performed at patients’ homes. Detailed distributions are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Mortality

Overall, 430 deaths (7.0%) occurred during the 16.0 ± 6.5 months. The crude mortality rate was 52.7 per 1000 person-years (Table 2). The crude mortality rate was lower in the intervention group than in the control group (33.7 vs. 69.5 per 1000 person-yrs, P < 0.001). Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed that the intervention group had a lower risk of patient death than the control group (P < 0.001; Figure 3a). Cox proportional hazard models were used to identify risk factors associated with patient mortality (Table 3). In univariate analysis, the control group had a higher mortality rate than the intervention group (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.95; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.58–2.41; P < 0.001). After adjusting for age and sex (model 1), the control group was an independent predictor of mortality compared with the intervention group (HR: 1.85; 95% CI: 1.49–2.28; P < 0.001). After adjusting for health insurance in addition to the factors included in model 1 (model 2), it remained an independent risk factor for patient mortality in the control group compared with the intervention group (HR: 1.82; 95% CI: 1.48–2.25; P < 0.001). Among the patients in the intervention group, 166 (5.3%) experienced technique failure compared with 301 (10.1%) in the control group (P < 0.001). Kaplan–Meier analysis of enrollment in the home-based management program showed that the intervention group had a higher rate of technique failure-free survival than the control group (P < 0.001; Figure 4a). Clinical Outcomes in Incident and Prevalent PD Patients

In prevalent patients, the intervention group had significantly fewer hospitalizations than the control group (2.10 vs. 2.91 hospitalizations/person/yr; P < 0.001), and shorter average length of stay per hospitalization (7.07 vs. 8.11 days, P < 0.001; Figure 1b). Emergency room visits were lower in the intervention group (0.86 vs. 1.37 visits per person-yr, P < 0.001; Figure 2b). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated lower mortality in the intervention group (P < 0.001; Figure 3b), and multivariable analysis confirmed the control group as an independent risk factor for death (HR: 1.65; 95% CI: 1.32–2.07; P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S5). Technique failure rates were lower in the intervention group (Figure 4b).

In incident patients, the intervention group likewise had fewer hospitalizations (2.10 vs. 5.65 per person-yr; P < 0.001) and shorter stays (5.60 vs. 10.58 days; P < 0.001; Figure 1c). Emergency room visits were reduced (0.93 vs. 1.92 per person-yr; P < 0.001; Figure 2c), and survival was better in the intervention group according to Kaplan–Meier curves (P < 0.001; Figure 3c). After adjustment, the control group remained independently associated with higher mortality risk (HR: 2.37; 95% CI: 1.26–4.48; P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S6). Technique failure incidence was lower in the intervention group (Figure 4c).

Discussion

The importance of self-management and educational assistance has been emphasized for patients on PD compared with those with other chronic diseases.4 However, little attention has been paid to specific interventions to optimize self-management in patients on PD. We demonstrated that establishing a system to provide multidisciplinary telehealth services by assembling a team of clinical professionals can improve patient outcomes. The home-based management program reduced the number of hospitalizations, hospital days, and emergency room visits; it reduced the risk of death and technique failure in patients on PD. Clinical improvements in the home-based management program were similar in patients in both the prevalent and incidental groups.

Home-based management refers to a broad range of services that people can receive at home instead of visiting a hospital for illness or injury. This can include home visits, telephone or contactless methods, and training by health care organizations to help patients manage themselves. Health care providers, delivery methods, and service types vary widely across countries. In recent years, home-based management has become increasingly important because of the growth of the elderly population, the rise of single-person households, the prevalence of chronic diseases, advances in technology, and patients' increased need for health care.

PD training is crucial for ensuring patient independence and preventing PD-related complications.4 Proper education of patients undergoing PD is essential to reduce the incidence of peritonitis; however, little attention has been paid to the role of PD education in reducing this complication.4,19 The International Society of Peritoneal Dialysis recommends that each PD unit implement a PD education program to improve self-management.9,11 However, the optimal method, location, time, and instructors for effective PD training remain uncertain.4 In the PD Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study, PD training was most commonly provided in the facility (81%) and in a 1:1 ratio (87%) by a facility-based nurse (79%).20 Studies have shown that a 1:1 nurse-to-patient ratio is associated with better self-care ability.21,22 Moreover, retraining programs appear to play an important role in reducing the risk of peritonitis and improving self-management abilities.23 Several studies have shown that retraining is associated with a reduction in peritonitis, hospitalizations, and emergency room visits.4,24 In this study, patients had a 1:1 educational consultation with a doctor or nurse for ≥ 15 minutes when they visited the hospital. The patients received an average of 7.3 educational consultations/yr, approximately once every 2 months.

Although PD offers safe home-based dialysis treatment, one of the disadvantages of home treatment is the risk of patients receiving inappropriate treatment without frequent monitoring.5 Ideally, daily monitoring for patients on PD would allow early detection of problems and correction of inappropriate dialysis delivery.5 PD offers safe home-based dialysis treatment; however, one of the disadvantages of home treatment is the risk of patients receiving inappropriate treatment without frequent monitoring. Ideally, home treatment for patients would be monitored daily so that problems can be detected early and inappropriate dialysis delivery corrected.5 The home-based management program allowed patients on PD to discuss with their health care team the medical issues they experienced in their daily lives that were not adequately addressed during regular office hours. From the health care provider's perspective, they could respond to patient inquiries in real time and monitor the patient's actual lifestyle to provide individualized support for behavior modification.25

It is necessary to develop educational programs and standardized materials to achieve optimal patient outcomes. The Korean Society of Nephrology has provided educational materials for counseling on the Korean Society of Nephrology website and made them available to each institution (https://ksn.or.kr/bbs/?page=1&code=peritoneal1). In addition, patient management checklists (e.g., edema, environmental management, dialysate monitoring, exit management, and daily routine) that should be identified in patient monitoring have been offered by each institution.

The COVID-19 pandemic has led to the reassessment of traditional health care delivery models and support for home dialysis, which can help reduce the risk associated with COVID-19.26,27 Owing to the lower risk of COVID-19 exposure in home dialysis compared with in-center dialysis, supporting patients to receive dialysis at home will help protect individuals from COVID-19–associated morbidity and mortality, and may help limit the transmission of infection to the community.26 The Canadian Society of Nephrology has suggested that home dialysis be offered preferentially to reduce the risk of COVID-19 transmission by reducing contact with clinics and hospitals compared with in-center dialysis.27,28 The NICE guidelines in the UK suggest considering the provision of home dialysis for new incident patients in the COVID-19 era and using remote monitoring technology, if possible.27 Home-based management programs are a useful method for non–face-to-face education in disaster situations such as COVID-19. In Korea, a pilot project for home-based management for patients on PD was launched in December 2019, followed by pilot projects for type 1 diabetes, home ventilators, and pregnant women in vulnerable areas of labor. In particular, the home-based management program for patients on PD requires active support from health authorities, as it can reduce medical costs.

This study had some limitations. First, the results can only be applied to the Korean medical environment. In Korea, PD is primarily practiced in secondary and tertiary care hospitals with sufficient medical staff and nurses performing most patient-related tasks. Patient monitoring may require additional tasks such as phone calls, 2-way messages, and counseling, which require sufficient staffing in advance. Therefore, it is difficult to apply the results of this study to other countries with different health care environments. Second, this was a nonrandomized, nationwide study, including the entire PD patient population in Korea during the study period, and participation in the home-based management program was voluntary. Consequently, patients in the intervention and control groups may have differed in unmeasured characteristics, such as health literacy, self-management motivation, or socioeconomic status. Although we adjusted for multiple baseline variables using multivariable Cox models, residual confounding cannot be excluded. In addition, we did not perform propensity score matching or inverse probability of treatment weighting, which could have further minimized selection bias. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as associations rather than definitive causal effects. Third, the nature of the claims data limited our ability to evaluate the relationship between the home-based management program and specific complications such as infections or cardiovascular disease, because the cause of patient death or technique failure could not be determined. Technique failure was defined as the time point when a PD patient transitioned to hemodialysis for > 30 days, but we could not differentiate whether the cause was infectious (e.g., peritonitis) or noninfectious (e.g., catheter malfunction and inadequate dialysis). Fourth, although outcomes were analyzed separately for incident and prevalent PD patients, we did not further stratify by PD vintage beyond this classification. Potential differences in program efficacy according to the duration of PD treatment could not be assessed. Finally, the study period was relatively short (22 months); longer follow-up is required to confirm the long-term effects of the program. Further research over a longer period is required to determine the long-term effects of the program.

In conclusion, home-based management is an important option for patient management as technological innovations are applied to real-world PD. This study demonstrated that a home-based management pilot program significantly improved clinical outcomes for patients on PD. Further studies are required to investigate the long-term effects and feasibility of this program.
