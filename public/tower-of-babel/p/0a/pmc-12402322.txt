Beta blockers linked to reduced 1-Year mortality in critically ill congestive heart failure patients with differential benefits across subgroups
Authors: Kai Wang, Haitao Zhang, Yanming Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The efficacy of beta (β) blockers in critically ill patients with congestive heart failure (CHF) remains uncertain. This study investigated the association of β blockers use with 1-year all-cause mortality in these patients. Utilizing a retrospective cohort, the Medical Information Mart in Intensive Care-Ⅳ database, we identified critically ill CHF patients between 2008 and 2022. Stratified by β blockers use, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox models examined the association. Subgroup analyses investigated its effect across various patient characteristics. It comprised 13,908 eligible patients, of whom 74.3% received β blockers. β blockers use was associated with a reduction in mortality (HR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.49–0.54, p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses revealed more benefits in non-elderly, male, hypertensive, diabetic or sepsis patients, as well as those undergoing mechanical ventilation or continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Patients with elevated baseline heart rates or reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) experienced greater benefits (p for interaction < 0.001). Additionally, selective β1 blockers offered superior survival benefits than non-selective β blockers in patients with LVEF of 50% or higher, and these benefits were pronounced in those with elevated baseline white blood cell counts (p for interaction = 0.022). The use of β blockers was linked to a reduction of all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with CHF, particularly in subgroups characterized by younger age, male gender, hypertension, diabetes, sepsis, undergoing CRRT or mechanical ventilation, and those exhibiting elevated baseline heart rates or reduced LVEF. These findings are potentially influenced by unmeasured confounders and need to be confirmed by prospective randomized controlled trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17483-3.

Introduction

Congestive heart failure (CHF) remains an epidemic with persistently high morbidity and mortality1. From 1990 to 2019, the worldwide prevalence of CHF surged by 106.3%, culminating 56.2 million cases in 20192. Patients with CHF are at risk of acute decompensation, which could lead to multi-organ dysfunction and critical illness and often necessitate admission to the intensive care unit (ICU)3. These critically ill patients typically exhibit elevated inflammation and neuroendocrine dysregulation, complicating their management4.

Beta (β) blockers, which counteract sympathetic overactivation and may downregulate inflammation5are fundamental in management of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF)6 and with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF)7,8. Researches have demonstrated that the withdrawal of guideline-directed therapy in patients with acute exacerbations of CHF, particularly HFrEF, elevates the risk of mortality and readmission post-discharge9,10. However, the impact of beta-blocker therapy in critically ill patients with CHF remains unclear across all ejection fraction categories.

Notably, this uncertainty is particularly pronounced for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Unlike the established benefits in HFrEF and HFmrEF, evidence for β blockers use in HFpEF comes primarily from observational studies and a few trials involving gerontic patients with inconsistent results. Data derived from the TOPCAT study and the PINNACLE registry indicated a potential association between β blockers use and an elevated risk of heart failure hospitalization in HFpEF patients11,12and the PRESERVE-HR trial suggested that β blockers may enhance exercise tolerance in individuals with HFpEF13. In contrast, the DELIVER trial, which included over 80% of participants on β blockers, demonstrated the reduced heart failure events and cardiovascular mortality14. A recent systematic review reported decreased all-cause mortality with β blockers therapy in HFpEF, though readmission rates remained unchanged15. A pooled analysis of four trials revealed no indication that β blockers use was linked to the adverse outcomes, even after comprehensive adjustment for other prognostic factors16. In addition, several randomized controlled trials have indicated the potential benefits of reduced sympathetic activity. However, the SENIORS study specifically investigated the efficacy of nebivolol in elderly patients with heart failure17while the SHIFT study assessed the impact of ivabradine in patients with HFrEF in sinus rhythm18.

Given these knowledge gaps and conflicting findings, the relationship between β blockers use and prognosis in critically ill patients with CHF requires clarification, particularly those with HFpEF. Therefore, we undertook a comprehensive analysis utilizing the Medical Information Mart in Intensive Care-Ⅳ (MIMIC-Ⅳ) database, incorporating a substantial cohort of patients. Moreover, subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential responders to β blockers therapy.

Methods

Database source

This study was taken from the MIMIC-Ⅳ 3.1 database, a single-center retrospective cohort, which included data from ICU patients spanning the years 2008 to 2022. Massachusetts State Registry of Vital Records and Statistics compiled data on out-of-hospital mortality, by matching records using patients’ names, social security numbers, and dates of birth. The database has been authorized for Kai Wang as one of the authors (certification number: 64734176).

At Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, the Human Institutional Review Board approved the human studies. It involved a secondary analysis of anonymized data and these data cannot be used to directly or indirectly identify individuals. Therefore, the requirement for obtaining informed consent from patients and additional ethical review was waived.

Participant selection

The diagnosis of CHF was established by the attending physician in accordance with the most recent clinical guidelines and expert consensus available at that time. These diagnoses were recorded using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions. We extracted all CHF patients admitted to various types of ICUs using specific codes (Supplementary Table 1). Inclusion criteria: all patients with CHF with ICU admission for the first time. Exclusion criteria: (1) under 18 years old, (2) ICU stay of less than 24 h (Fig. 1).

Data extraction and outcomes

Within 24 h of ICU admission, clinical data were extracted from the first measurement. The variables with missing values exceeding 25% were discarded. The data included age, gender, heart rate, blood pressure, saturation of peripheral oxygen (SpO2), respiratory rate, comorbidities (acute kidney injury, stage of acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, hypertension, hypoglycemia, acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and sepsis), laboratory data (white blood cell (WBC), platelets, red cell distribution width (RDW), hemoglobin, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin (TBil), albumin, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose, partial pressure of oxygen and carbon dioxide, potential of hydrogen, lactate, anion gap), disease severity scores (APS III and SOFA19, inhibitors of renin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS inhibitors, covering angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors), antibiotics, β blockers, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and mechanical ventilation. Sepsis was defined based on the Sepsis 3.0 diagnostic criteria20. Additionally, data of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was recorded due to its special role. In particular, the extraction of drug information was obtained through the retrieval of the names of the drugs, which covered the administration mode and the start and end times of the drugs.

The exposure factor was defined as the administration of oral β blockers at any time, dosage and frequency during the ICU stay. To provide a more comprehensive analysis of exposure factors, we systematically extracted data on β-blocker usage throughout the ICU stay, within the first 7 days post-admission, and within the initial 48 h of admission. The β blockers were categorized into selective β1 blockers (atenolol/tenormin, metoprolol/toprol-xl, esmolol, bisoprolol and betaxolol) and non-selective β blockers (nadolol, sotalol, propranolol, nebivolol, pindolol, carvedilol and labetalol). For reasons of sample size, both alpha (α)-β blockers and β blockers were classified as non-selective β blockers.

The study endpoint was defined as 1-year mortality from all causes.

Statistical analysis

To address the issue of missing data, multiple imputation techniques were employed for variables with less than 25% missing values. Specifically, multiple imputation utilizing random forests was conducted using the mice package, under the assumption that the data were missing at random. Once the complete dataset was generated, the imputation results for the missing values were visualized to illustrate the distribution of both imputed and observed values. Furthermore, to evaluate the robustness of the missing value treatment, multiple imputation was performed using a method based on classification and regression trees. This resulting dataset was subsequently utilized for sensitivity analysis. Due to the fact that LVEF data was missing for approximately 40% of cases and was not suitable for imputation, only patients with available LVEF data were included in the LVEF-related data analysis.

The mean (standard deviation (SD)) or median (first and third quartiles) were used to express continuous variables, whereas number and percentage were used to express categorical variables. The analyses were run in R Studio (version 4.4.1) with significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Firstly, Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by β blockers use during ICU stay and Cox models evaluated mortality risk. These models were adjusted for a range of confounders to minimize confounding bias and obtain hazard ratio (HR) for all-cause mortality. Covariates were selected based on previous research and knowledge of clinical practice, but not data-driven strategies21including demographics (gender, age), comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, CKD, AMI and sepsis), markers of anemia and inflammation (hemoglobin, WBC and RDW), liver and kidney function (ALT, AST, TBil, creatinine and BUN), therapy-related factors (CRRT and ventilation), and disease severity (SOFA and APS III). Multicollinearity was identified by calculating variance inflation factors, with values exceeding 5 indicating potential multicollinearity among variables. The E-value was used to estimate the impact of unknown or unmeasured confounding22. To enhance the robustness of the results, Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by β blockers use within the first 48 h and the first 7 days in ICU were utilized, alongside multivariate Cox models.

Then, subgroup analysis and interaction testing were conducted by fully adjusted Cox models to evaluate the HR across various patients. The selection of subgroups was primarily based on the reference to available reports and the consideration of potential interactions between heart disease and chronic, pulmonary, or renal diseases. Additionally, considering the effect of β blockers on the heart rate and its potentially different effects in patients with different LVEF categories, we evaluated the continuous interaction between heart rate and β blockers use in all patients, and between LVEF and β blockers use in patients with available LVEF data. This analysis was executed using the interactionRCS package.

Subsequently, the benefit from different types of β blockers on patients at varying LVEF levels was examined in detail among patients with LVEF data available. β blockers were categorized into non-selective β blockers and selective β1 blockers. In addition, considering the important effects of inflammation on CHF and the modulating effect of β blockers on inflammation5,23,24 we investigated the benefit of non-selective β blockers and selective β1 blockers in patients with different levels of baseline WBC counts.

Finally, to further improve the robustness of the study results, all baseline information was controlled using the overlap weighting (OW) and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). OW- or IPTW-adjusted Kaplan-Meier survival curves and Pepe-Flemming test were used to compare the effect of β blockers use during ICU on risk of all-cause mortality25. These analyses were conducted using the WeightIt package. The relevant R code was stored in the supplemental file (Supplementary Table 2). Moreover, these two weighted analyses were additionally conducted using the complete dataset obtained through the multiple imputation method based on classification and regression trees.

Results

Description of population

Based on established criteria, we included a total of 13,908 eligible patients. The density map of complete dataset following multiple imputations was plotted (Figure S1). Baseline characteristics, stratified by β blockers use, were presented in Table 1. Among them, 60.1%, 56.5%, and 10.4% of patients presented with comorbidities associated with AKI stages 2 or 3, sepsis, and AMI, respectively. Additionally, 86.4% of the patients required mechanical ventilation. The mean scores for the SOFA and APS III were 5.38 and 48.91. The overall incidence of endpoint events was 39.5%.

Baseline information according to the use of β blockers during intensive care unit stay.

Compared with those not admitting β blockers, we found that patients receiving the medication exhibited declined levels of platelets, RDW, renal impairment, anion gap, SOFA and APS III, as well as lower proportions of diabetes, hypertension, COPD, CKD or CRRT, but higher levels of heart rate and WBC, as well as elevated proportions of sepsis or ventilation.

We presented the baseline characteristics for populations with and without LVEF data (Supplementary Table 3). The groups differed significantly in the prevalence of hypertension, sepsis, and hypoglycemia; use of β blockers, RAAS inhibitors, CRRT, and mechanical ventilation; and laboratory values including WBC, hemoglobin, aminotransferases, BUN, and anion gap. However, disease severity scores (SOFA and APS III) were similar between groups.

Benefits from β blockers

We found that 74.3% (10338/13908) of the eligible patients administered β blockers during their ICU stay, 74.0% (10289/13908) did during the first 7 days and 40.5% (5626/13908) did during the first 48 h. Our Kaplan-Meier survival curves indicated a reduction in mortality among patients receiving β blockers, regardless of whether the analysis was conducted over the entire ICU duration with 1-year mortality of 56.1% (2003/3570) and 33.8% (3493/10338), within the first 7 days with 1-year mortality of 55.2% (1996/3619) and 34.0% (3500/10289), or within the first 48 h with 1-year mortality of 43.5% (3600/8282) and 33.7% (1896/5626) (log-rank test, p < 0.001 for all, Fig. 2A and B).

For the Cox proportional hazards regression models, our analysis confirmed that the exposure factors met the Proportional Hazard hypothesis (p for Schoenfeld individual test = 0.117), and the existence of multicollinearity was not considered (Figure S2). We used the Cox models to reveal an adjusted HR of 0.51 (95% CI: 0.49 ~ 0.54) compared to patients not receiving β blockers, with an E-value of 2.56 (Table 2). Consistent findings were observed across model 0, 1, and 2. Furthermore, our analysis of β blockers use within the first 7 days and within the first 48 h corroborated these above results, with 46% (HR: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.51 ~ 0.57) and 16% (HR: 0.84, 95% CI: 0.79 ~ 0.89) reduction in the 1-year mortality risk.

In addition, we analyzed the hypoglycemic event, considering that non-selective β blockers may be associated with a higher risk of this comorbidity than selective β1 blockers. The results showed that 4.3% (43/995) of patients on non-selective β blockers experienced comorbid hypoglycemic events, in contrast to 2.5% (232/9310) among those using selective β1 blockers. Hypoglycemic events were balanced in the subsequent sensitivity analyses as potential confounders.

Benefits across subgroups

Referring to existing reports and considering possible interactions of heart disease with chronic, pulmonary, and renal diseases, we conducted the subgroup analyses across diverse gender, age, hypertension, diabetes, COPD, sepsis, CKD, CRRT and mechanical ventilation (Fig. 3). The results suggested that the benefits from β blockers therapy were pronounced in non-elderly, male, hypertensive, diabetic or sepsis patients, as well as those receiving CRRT and mechanical ventilation (p for interaction < 0.05 for all).

Sensitivity analysis

In contrast to the partial-corrected regression model, we employed the OW and IPTW to achieve balance across all baseline variables presented in Table 1. Subsequently, our Kaplan-Meier curves adjusted using OW or IPTW demonstrated results consistent with the previous section, indicating that patients receiving β blockers exhibited a reduced risk of mortality in comparison to those not receiving β blockers, with HR of 0.68 (95% CI, 0.64 ~ 0.72) and HR of 0.70 (95% CI, 0.65 ~ 0.74), respectively (Figure S3). Moreover, we conducted the two weighted analyses on the other dataset, which utilized multiple imputation based on classification and regression trees, revealed the comparable results (Figure S4).

Discussion

In this extensive cohort, the use of β blockers was associated with reduced 1-year all-cause mortality in critically ill patients with CHF. This benefit was particularly pronounced in subgroups characterized by younger age, male gender, hypertension, diabetes, sepsis, and those undergoing CRRT or mechanical ventilation, as well as in patients exhibiting elevated baseline heart rates or reduced LVEF. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for all baseline variables further supported these findings. Notably, selective β1 blockers were associated with greater benefits compared to non-selective β blockers in critically ill patients with LVEF of 50% or greater, and advantages of selective β1 blockers may be more pronounced in those with elevated baseline WBC counts.

β blockers have been consolidated as a cornerstone in the treatment of HFrEF and HFmrEF6. However, the application of β blockers in critically ill patients with CHF was rarely reported. In critically ill patients, the autonomic nervous system often becomes dysregulated, leading to sympathetic overstimulation26. The use of β blockers in such scenarios may help mitigate these effects by reducing heart rate and myocardial oxygen demand, as well as potentially modulating the immune response and reducing inflammatory cytokine production26. However, clinical evidence supporting the use of β blockers in critically ill patients with CHF remains limited. This underutilization of β blockers may stem from concerns regarding their potential to cause hypotension and bradycardia, particularly in those with already compromised hemodynamics27. Our study offered evidence addressing this question. After adjustment for confounding factors, our findings indicated that β blockers use decreased the risk of all-cause mortality within 1 year among critically ill patients with CHF. This aligned with findings from a recent study28. It found that β blockers therapy demonstrated benefits in reducing in-hospital mortality and hospitalization duration in ICU patients with CHF28. Nevertheless, our research distinguished it by concerning the long-term clinical outcomes but not in-hospital mortality, and utilizing an updated MIMIC database, a larger sample size, and more sophisticated statistical methodologies particularly including OW and IPTW. In this analysis, patients receiving β blockers exhibited a lower severity of illness, likely attributable to contraindications present in patients not receiving β blockers. However, we have accounted for confounding variables by adjusting for all baseline characteristics, covering impaired liver and kidney function, as well as SOFA and APS III. The final analysis continued to indicate a benefit from β blockers independent of the severity of the patient’s disease. The relatively large E-values (2.56, greater than 2.0) indicated that these findings were robust. Therefore, given the independent association between the use of β-blockers and a reduced risk of mortality, we may recommend the administration of β-blockers in this population, excluding contraindications.

Our subgroup analysis revealed that the therapeutic effects of β blockers were pronounced in non-elderly, male, sepsis, hypertensive, diabetic patients, as well as those undergoing CRRT and mechanical ventilation. This observation aligned with findings from previous studies. The use of β blockers in elderly patients with HFrEF and renal impairment was associated with a better prognosis29. β blockers use in critically ill patients, particularly those with sepsis or requiring CRRT or ventilation, has been associated with improved outcomes, suggesting that the pharmacological benefits may extend to those with acute conditions, where β blockers could help stabilize hemodynamics and reduce stress responses. Moreover, the distinctive interactive RCS analysis indicated that patients exhibiting higher baseline heart rates and reduced LVEF derive greater benefit from β blockers therapy. β blockers was particularly effective in HFrEF patients, where managing heart rate was crucial for enhancing clinical outcomes30. In HFrEF patients, reductions in heart rate following β blockers were associated with a lower incidence of adverse events31. Changes in heart rate held significant prognostic value in patients with atrial fibrillation, particularly those presenting with higher baseline heart rates31. Furthermore, patients with reduced LVEF levels often demonstrated a more pronounced response to β blockers therapy11,32. Meanwhile, from control subjects to individuals with heart failure, characterized by preserved, mildly reduced and reduced ejection fractions, muscle sympathetic nerve activity, heart rate and plasma norepinephrine increased progressively and significantly33. These evidence presented may substantiate and interpret our findings. In sum, understanding the nuanced effects of β blockers in these specific patient populations is vital for optimizing therapeutic strategies, including the relatively young, male, sepsis, hypertensive, diabetic, or severe heart failure patients receiving CRRT or mechanical ventilation, especially in those with high baseline heart rates or low levels of LVEF. Consequently, our findings may practically advocate for the use of β blockers in this specific subset of patients.

Our study revealed that selective β1 blockers conferred greater benefits than non-selective β blockers in these populations with LVEF ≥ 50%. Recent research indicated that selective β1 blockers, such as bisoprolol, may lead to improved outcomes in patients with COPD following myocardial infarction, compared to non-selective β blocker34. This disparity in therapeutic effects may be attributed to the distinct mechanisms of action, as selective β1 blockers predominantly target cardiac β adrenergic receptors. The evidence suggested that non-selective β blockers may be linked to adverse effects, in contrast, selective β1 blockers seem to alleviate these risks35,36. This distinction was important given the heterogeneous nature of HFpEF, where patient responses to treatment could vary considerably37. Moreover, the PARAGON-HF study, a randomized controlled trial, aimed to assess the therapeutic benefits of sacubitril/valsartan in patients with heart failure with LVEF of 45% or greater38. The predetermined subgroup analysis indicated that sacubitril/valsartan was associated with a reduced risk of heart failure readmission in patients with LVEF of 45–57% (relative risk: 0.78) and in female patients (relative risk: 0.73). Consequently, sacubitril/valsartan may confer clinical benefits to HFpEF patients who had LVEF of ≤ 57%38. In comparison to HFpEF patients with LVEF ranging from 50 to 59%, those with LVEF exceeding 60% (accounting for approximately 43–46%) generally demonstrate reduced left ventricular volume and increased coronary microvascular dysfunction. Mavacamten, a myosin inhibitor currently, may enhance diastolic function and myocardial energy metabolism in HFpEF patients39. The EMBARK-HFpEF trial involving HFpEF patients with LVEF of 60% or higher indicated that mavacamten use was correlated with enhancements in ventricular wall pressure and reductions in biomarkers indicative of myocardial injury40. Building on these, we further stratified the HFpEF population into two groups: those with 50% ≤ LVEF < 60% and those with LVEF ≥ 60%, for detailed analysis. Eventually, we found that selective β1 blockers may be preferred in HFpEF, even non-selective β blockers may not be advantageous for critically ill patients with LVEF of 60% or higher. In summary, this insight potentially offers a basis for explaining the underlying causes of variable outcomes observed with β blockers therapy in HFpEF, even could be instrumental in informing the design of future randomized controlled trials investigating the use of β1 blockers in HFpEF patients.

In CHF, sympathetic activity is augmented, characterized by cardiac remodeling, norepinephrine spillover, and altered receptor levels. Norepinephrine interacts with the heart’s β1 receptors, enhancing electrical activity and myocardial contraction, while also aberrantly activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, ultimately resulting in cardiac fibrosis and compromised cardiac function41. Notably, sympathetic neurotransmitters can also engage the heart’s β2 receptors, which may downregulate inflammation within the cardiac tissue and throughout the body42. Inflammation plays a significant role in the pathogenesis and progression of CHF across the subtypes of HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF43. However, there appears to be a stronger correlation between inflammatory markers and HFpEF, while HFrEF is more closely associated with biomarkers indicative of cardiac stretch23,44. This difference may be partially attributed to the higher prevalence of comorbid conditions in HFpEF patients45. The precise mechanisms by which SGLT2 inhibitors reduce hospitalization and mortality in patients with HFpEF remain incompletely elucidated; however, these inhibitors also appear to mitigate inflammation. Meta-analyses of rodent studies have demonstrated that SGLT2 inhibitors were associated with reductions in inflammatory markers46. Recent randomized controlled clinical trials, namely STEP-HFpEF47 STEP-HFpEF DM48 and SUMMIT49 have demonstrated that treatment with semaglutide or tirzepatide decreased the risk of adverse events in patients with HFpEF and markedly reduced systemic inflammation. Consequently, we hypothesize that both selective and non-selective β blockers could have a beneficial impact on heart failure with HFrEF, where cardiac stretch and myocardial interstitial fibrosis are the predominant pathological changes. In contrast, within the context of HFpEF, where inflammation constitutes a significant aspect of pathogenesis, the use of selective β1 adrenergic blockers could circumvent the inhibition of cardiac β2 receptors. Similarly, our findings suggested that the benefits of selective β1 blockers were indeed stronger in patients with high levels of systemic inflammation. Conversely, the protective effect of non-selective β blockers in patients was not related to baseline WBC count levels. Such a mechanism may elucidate some of the observations reported in this study. In conclusion, considering the critical role of selective β1 blockers in CHF management, it is essential to generate robust clinical evidence regarding the application of β1 blockers in these patients, particularly those with HFpEF. Specifically, critically ill patients with HFpEF may be included and randomized into selective β1 blockers and placebo groups on the basis of established treatment guidelines, with consideration of the predefined subgroups with LVEF ≥ 60%. This approach aims to provide higher-quality evidence regarding the benefits of selective β1 blockers in critically ill patients with HFpEF, particularly concerning the simultaneous inclusion of those with LVEF ≥ 60%.

However, this study has several limitations. First, this research was conducted at a single center, and the applicability of these findings to external populations warrants further investigation. Second, the study cohort was drawn from various types of ICUs, not exclusively cardiac care units, which may have contributed to participant heterogeneity. Nonetheless, this approach reflects real-world conditions. Third, in MIMIC, it could be challenging to determine whether CHF is the primary cause of non-ICU admission, which makes the application and interpretation of the results require rigorous evaluation. However, ICU admission is often caused by critical conditions of multiple organs, including CHF, and CHF often results in multi-organ failure. Consequently, distinguishing between primary causes may not be necessary in such contexts. Fourth, it should be noted that the study could not ascertain whether β blocker exposure was continuous or initiated anew, as the database did not capture medication use prior to admission. This issue necessitates investigation through prospective studies that include pre-defined subgroup analyses based on patients’ β blockers use status prior to admission. Fifth, despite adjustments for significant risk factors and the analysis of E-values, the presence of unmeasured confounders remains a possibility. Specifically, factors such as the frailty index, BNP/NT-proBNP levels, troponin levels, inotrope/vasopressor requirements, detailed hemodynamic parameters, trajectory of LVEF, and the absence of randomization, confounding by indication, misclassification of drug exposure, and lack of information on β blocker dosage, titration, or discontinuation all could contribute to the risk. Sixth, LVEF data were missing for 40% of patients, which may introduce population selection bias in subgroup analyses based on varying LVEF levels. Consequently, this aspect warrants cautious interpretation.

Conclusions

β blockers use was associated with a reduction in all-cause mortality among critically ill patients with CHF, particularly those characterized by younger age, male gender, hypertension, diabetes, sepsis or those undergoing CRRT or mechanical ventilation, as well as in patients exhibiting elevated baseline heart rates or reduced LVEF. Selective β1 blockers may be associated with greater benefits in critically ill patients with HFpEF compared with non-selective β blockers, and these benefits may also be more pronounced in patients with elevated baseline WBC counts. However, these were explorations from retrospective cohort study, and further prospective trials are required to furnish high level of evidence.
