Phase behavior and reservoir fluid classification of the deep Permian Jiamuhe formation gas condensate in Zhongjia area, Junggar Basin
Authors: Fan Yang, Feng Wang, Hechong Liu, Wenqi Yan, Wei Niu, Zuqiang Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSignificant breakthroughs have recently been achieved in deep Permian Jiamuhe Formation petroleum exploration within the Zhongguai Uplift on the northwestern margin of the Junggar Basin, exemplified by the high commercial flow obtained from well ZJ14. However, the complex and poorly understood hydrocarbon phase behavior in this area constrains exploration and development planning. This study systematically investigates the phase characteristics and evolution of formation fluids from the Jiamuhe Formation in well ZJ14, integrating high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) fluid property experiments, phase behavior modeling, and multiple empirical identification methods. Experimental results reveal that: (1) The formation fluid exhibits a producing gas‑oil ratio of 7621 m3/m3, a dew‑point pressure of 44.29 MPa, nd a substantial pressure difference between initial reservoir pressure and dew-point pressure of 7.71 MPa; a pronounced retrograde condensation occurs when the pressure falls below the dew point, posing a potential impact on gas‑well productivity. (2) The wellstream composition is characterized by “enrichment in light hydrocarbons and scarcity of heavy hydrocarbons”, with a C1 content of 90.50% and a C5+ content of 3.50%, indicating a high‑maturity, near‑critical rich‑gas‑condensate system that provides the material basis for oil‑rim formation. (3) Integrated identification using multiple methods consistently confirms that the reservoir is a gas‑condensate system with an oil rim; discrepancies in oil‑rim scale estimation from different methods reflect the heterogeneity in oil‑rim distribution, and the fluid composition lies near the phase boundary between gas‑condensate and volatile‑oil systems, suggesting a high risk of retrograde condensation during production. (4) Temperature significantly influences phase behavior, with lower temperatures leading to increased dew‑point pressure and aggravated retrograde condensation; therefore, attention should be paid to near‑wellbore cooling effects during development, and measures such as gas injection for pressure maintenance are recommended to mitigate retrograde‑condensation damage and enhance overall reservoir recovery. The findings provide important insights for phase‑behavior identification and development‑strategy formulation in this area and analogous deep gas‑condensate reservoirs.Keywords: Junggar basin, ZJ14 well, Jiamuhe formation, Hydrocarbon reservoir type, Condensate gas reservoirSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

In recent years, the global oil and gas industry has increasingly shifted towards deep, unconventional, and complex fluid reservoirs. This transition has introduced a series of challenges—including high temperature and pressure, multiple hydrocarbon charging events, strong reservoir heterogeneity, and complex fluid compositions—that collectively complicate hydrocarbon phase behavior1,2. Consequently, accurately identifying the in-situ phase state and distribution patterns of hydrocarbons is of critical importance for the exploration and development of deep to ultra-deep reservoirs. This is particularly vital during the early stages for determining reservoir type, evaluating reserves, and selecting optimal development strategies3,4. Significant differences exist in the seepage characteristics, occurrence states, and physicochemical properties of reservoirs with different hydrocarbon phases. These variations directly influence the driving forces required for hydrocarbon migration and accumulation, the requisite physical properties of the reservoir, and the evolutionary pathways of the accumulation process. Ultimately, these distinctions profoundly shape our understanding of hydrocarbon accumulation mechanisms1,2.

The formation and evolution of hydrocarbon phase behavior are governed by a complex interplay of multiple factors, which can be categorized into four primary groups: fluid composition, temperature–pressure conditions, geological evolution, and reservoir characteristics5. Fluid composition serves as the fundamental determinant of phase state. Temperature and pressure directly control the critical conditions for phase transitions. Geological evolution dictates the primary generation phase of hydrocarbons, following a maturation sequence from immature oil → liquid petroleum → condensate → wet gas → dry gas. Reservoir properties, including nano-scale confinement effects, wettability, and burial depth, indirectly influence phase behavior by altering fluid-rock interactions and pore-scale physics. Beyond these four primary factors, basin-specific thermal regimes play a crucial role. In basins with low geothermal gradients, organic matter experiences slower thermal maturation at equivalent depths, resulting in a broader and deeper oil window. This enables the preservation of complex hydrocarbon phases, such as oil pools and condensate gas reservoirs, even under the high-temperature conditions of deep to ultra-deep settings6. Furthermore, secondary processes significantly contribute to the formation of light oil and condensate accumulations at great depths. These include the thermal cracking of crude oil during high-maturity stages of source rock evolution and the compositional mixing effects arising from multiple episodes of hydrocarbon charging7. Condensate gas reservoirs represent a unique fluid system intermediate between oil and dry gas, exhibiting dual characteristics of both. Their formation results from the combined effects of various geological factors throughout the processes of hydrocarbon generation, migration, and preservation. The complex phase behavior of condensate fluids persists throughout the entire field development lifecycle, posing significant challenges for efficient recovery. This complexity is particularly acute during later development stages, as continuous reservoir pressure depletion exacerbates formation damage caused by retrograde condensation. Consequently, a fundamental and thorough understanding of condensate gas phase behavior remains the cornerstone of all related research and effective development planning.

The Junggar Basin, located in western China, is a large and prolific hydrocarbon-bearing basin. In recent years, significant exploration breakthroughs have been achieved in the deep to ultra-deep strata of the Permian Jiamuhe Formation within the Zhongguai Uplift on the basin’s northwestern margin. The Zhongjia Nose structural belt of this uplift has now emerged as a promising area with the potential for billion-ton-scale, high-efficiency reserve growth. Particularly in the ZJ14 block of this belt, all four drilled wells have successfully tested high commercial flows exceeding a hundred cubic meters per day, underscoring its substantial exploration potential8. Well ZJ14, a key exploratory well in this block, encountered a hydrocarbon accumulation in the Permian Jiamuhe Formation. The well yields condensate gas and condensate oil from the upper section of the formation, while producing condensate gas alongside black oil from the lower section, indicating a complex vertical distribution of fluid phases. Exploration results confirm the coexistence of multiple hydrocarbon phases—including black oil, condensate oil, condensate gas, and dry gas—within the ZJ14 block, presenting a highly complex fluid system. Although preliminary studies on the deep hydrocarbon phase behavior in and around the ZJ14 block have been conducted, a clear understanding of the specific phase types and their spatial distribution patterns remains elusive. This knowledge gap directly constrains accurate resource assessment and hampers the formulation of optimal exploration and development strategies, thereby impeding further breakthroughs in deep hydrocarbon exploration across the northwestern margin.To address this, the present study conducts a comprehensive phase behavior analysis of fluids from Well ZJ14. This involves high-pressure fluid property experiments and quantitative compositional analysis. By integrating Pressure–Temperature(P–T) phase diagrams, ternary composition diagrams, and empirical identification methods, this work aims to accurately characterize the fluid phase state. Based on these findings, the reservoir type and hydrocarbon distribution model will be further discussed, providing a critical scientific basis for subsequent exploration and development activities in the region.

Geological setting

The northwestern margin of the Junggar Basin is characterized by a large-scale thrust structural system. This system has created major tectonic-depositional units, such as the Karamay-Baikouquan fault zones and Hongche fault zones, which collectively form one of the basin’s most prolific hydrocarbon accumulation belts and host several billion-ton-scale oil and gas fields9–11. Located at a critical structural transition zone along this northwestern margin, the Zhongguai Uplift acts as a structural pivot connecting the Karamay-Baikouquan and Hongche fault zones12,13. It is bounded to the east by the Dabasong Uplift. To the northeast, it transitions via a gentle slope into the Mahu Sag, while to the south, it plunges as a monoclinal structure towards the Shawan and Pen-1 Well West Sags (Fig. 1a). From a regional hydrocarbon migration and accumulation perspective, the Zhongguai Uplift is uniquely positioned. It is surrounded by three prolific hydrocarbon-generating sags—the Shawan, Pen-1 West, and Mahu Sags—placing it in a long-term, favorable convergence area for hydrocarbon migration. Its structural geometry is defined by an inherited nose-shaped uplift, providing excellent geological conditions for hydrocarbon accumulation and enrichment14,15.

Well ZJ14 is a key exploratory well located at the structural high of the Zhongguai Uplift. Its primary objectives were to extend the exploration success from adjacent areas and to evaluate hydrocarbon potential near the stratigraphic pinch-out line, making it significant for exploring tight sandstone gas in the Permian Jiamuhe Formation16. The well was completed in the Lower Permian Jiamuhe Formation (P1j) at a total depth of 4550 m, with subsequent testing yielding a high commercial flow exceeding 100 cubic meters per day, confirming the hydrocarbon potential of the target interval. The well encountered a complete stratigraphic sequence, which, from bottom to top, includes the Permian Jiamuhe Formation (P1j) and Upper Urho Formation (P3w); the Triassic Baikouquan Formation (T1b), Karamay Formation (T2k), and Baijiantan Formation (T3b); the Jurassic Badaowan Formation (J1b), Sangonghe Formation (J1s), Xishanyao Formation (J2x), and Toutunhe Formation (J2t); and the overlying Cretaceous (K), Paleogene (E), and Neogene (N) systems, reflecting the comprehensive sedimentary fill of the Zhongguai Uplift17. The study area experienced multiple compressive events from the Late Hercynian to Yanshanian periods, resulting in three regional unconformities—Cretaceous-Jurassic, Jurassic-Triassic, and Triassic-Permian—which played a critical role in controlling hydrocarbon migration and accumulation12,18. The Permian Jiamuhe Formation is the primary target and is subdivided into three members. The third member (P1j3) has been completely eroded on the southern slope due to tectonic uplift, forming an angular unconformity with the overlying Upper Urho Formation17. The lower part of the first member (P1j1) consists predominantly of dark gray tuffaceous breccia and porphyritic trachyandesite, while the upper part comprises interbedded andesite, basalt, silty mudstone, and black mudstone. This unit exhibits moderate organic matter richness, predominantly Type III kerogen, and has reached high to post-maturity, making it a favorable source rock interval. However, its great depth has prevented any well from fully penetrating it19. The second member (P1j2) is divided into two sand groups (P1j21and P1j22). Both are primarily composed of grayish-brown tuffaceous glutenite containing laumontite. Dissolution of laumontite has created “sweet spot” reservoirs, making this member the main gas-bearing interval. The reservoirs are characterized by low porosity and low permeability, with pore types dominated by secondary dissolution pores and microfractures17. Well ZJ14 specifically targeted the P1j21 sand group, achieving a test production of 21.23 m3/day of oil and 12 × 104 m3/day of gas through a 4.0 mm choke, indicating considerable exploration potential (Fig. 1b).

Crude oil property analysis

Well ZJ14 was tested at the top of the first sand group within the second member of the Permian Jiamuhe Formation (P1j21), yielding a high commercial flow rate exceeding 100 cubic meters per day. Measured surface oil density was 0.8035 g/cm3, with a producing gas-oil ratio (GOR) as high as 7621 m3/m3. According to established classification criteria for typical hydrocarbon reservoirs in the Junggar Basin20,21, a GOR exceeding 2000 m3/m3 generally indicates the presence of a gas reservoir. Concurrently, a surface oil density within the 0.80–0.82 g/cm3 range is typically associated with condensate gas reservoirs possessing an oil rim (Table 1). Analysis shows that the measured oil density from Well ZJ14 falls within this specific range, and its GOR significantly surpasses the critical threshold. Based on this, the well is preliminarily interpreted to have encountered a condensate gas reservoir, potentially underlain by an oil rim. However, the definitive determination of reservoir type and the accurate assessment of its scale are influenced by multiple operational and geological factors. These include the specifics of the well test program, perforation intervals, and well placement design22. Consequently, relying solely on single-well fluid property parameters is insufficient for drawing a robust conclusion. To achieve reliable characterization, a subsequent phase of work must integrate multiple datasets, including mud logging, wireline logs, formation test data, and 3D seismic surveys. Systematic reservoir delineation and fluid phase behavior modeling based on this integrated approach are essential to unambiguously define the reservoir type, delineate its spatial distribution, and evaluate the resource potential of the area.

Phase behavior analysis of formation fluids

Accurately characterizing the phase behavior of formation fluids is a fundamental prerequisite for the efficient exploration and scientific development of condensate gas reservoirs. The core of this endeavor lies in obtaining physical property parameters that reliably reflect fluid behavior under true reservoir conditions22. High-pressure, high-temperature (HPHT) fluid property (PVT) analysis serves as the most direct and reliable method for elucidating fluid composition, phase transitions, and flow characteristics23. In this study, a comprehensive experimental program was conducted on recombined reservoir fluid samples from Well ZJ14. The recombination was based on the well’s actual producing gas-oil ratio (7621 m3/m3). The present study was conducted using a Fluid Eval 500 PVT phase analyzer (with measurement accuracies of ± 0.03 MPa for pressure, ± 0.08‰ for composition analysis, and ± 0.3% for volume) under simulated reservoir temperature (103.0°C) and pressure (52.00 MPa) conditions. The test series systematically included constant-mass depletion (CMD), constant-composition expansion (CCE), and constant-volume depletion (CVD) experiments. These experiments precisely determined key phase behavior parameters, including wellstream composition, dew-point pressure, and maximum retrograde liquid volume. The results systematically reveal the retrograde condensation behavior and phase evolution patterns of the formation fluid. This work provides a robust experimental foundation for establishing a reliable fluid phase behavior model and for optimizing the development strategy of condensate gas reservoirs in this region.

Constant-mass depletion (CMD) test

A constant-mass depletion (CMD) test was conducted on the fluid sample from Well ZJ14 at the reservoir temperature of 103.0 ℃. The test determined a producing gas-oil ratio (GOR) of 7621 m3/m3, a dew-point pressure of 44.29 MPa, and a gas deviation factor (Z-factor) of 1.1729 at the dew point. The results indicate a significant initial pressure difference of 7.71 MPa between the original reservoir pressure (52.00 MPa) and the dew-point pressure. This substantial pressure margin signifies considerable inherent elastic energy within the reservoir, providing a critical basis for leveraging this natural energy advantage during early development. It informs the strategic choice between natural depletion and pressure-maintenance recovery methods23. However, as reservoir pressure declines below the dew point during production, retrograde condensation will occur, causing liquid hydrocarbon (condensate) to drop out within the formation. This condensate tends to accumulate and become trapped in the near-wellbore region and along the flow path, leading to pore-throat blockage and a marked reduction in gas relative permeability—a phenomenon commonly termed “condensate banking” or “retrograde condensate damage.” This damage severely impairs well productivity and ultimate recovery24. Consequently, development planning must proactively assess the risk of such condensate-induced formation damage. Mitigation strategies, such as gas injection for pressure maintenance, should be integrated into the design to counteract this detrimental effect.

Wellstream composition

The composition of the wellstream forms the fundamental material basis governing hydrocarbon phase behavior and reservoir type. Accurate determination of this composition is therefore a primary prerequisite for conducting detailed phase behavior studies2. In this work, gas chromatography was employed to analyze the formation fluid sample from the Jiamuhe Formation in Well ZJ14, yielding detailed compositional data (Table 2). The results indicate a methane (C1) mole fraction of 90.50%, with nitrogen (N2) at 1.80% and a low carbon dioxide (CO2) content of 0.20%. The mole fractions of ethane and propane (C2-C3) are 2.80% and 1.00%, respectively, while the total mole fraction of butane and heavier components (C4+) is 4.30%. Overall, light components (C1 + N2) dominate the composition at 92.30%. The combined content of intermediate components (C2-C6 + CO2) is 5.20%, and heavy components (C7+) constitute only 1.90%. Compared to typical lean condensate gases, this fluid exhibits a significantly increased content of heavier components, displaying characteristics transitional towards a rich gas-condensate system25,26.

Constant-composition expansion (CCE) test

The constant-composition expansion (CCE) test is a critical method for characterizing the phase behavior evolution of reservoir fluids. By establishing the pressure–volume (P–V) relationship, it enables precise determination of key phase parameters, including dew-point pressure, relative volume, and liquid content at saturation pressure30. A systematic CCE test was conducted on the recombined fluid sample from Well ZJ14. The experimental results show that at the formation temperature (103.0 °C), as pressure decreases, an inflection point (dew‑point pressure) appears on the curve near 44.29 MPa (Fig. 3). However, due to the very low rate of liquid dropout of the ZJ14 fluid around the dew‑point pressure (the maximum liquid volume fraction throughout the test is only about 2%), the transition on the relative‑volume versus‑pressure curve manifests as a gradual change in slope rather than an abrupt kink. As pressure continues to decline, the relative volume of the fluid (defined as the volume at a given pressure divided by the volume at the dew‑point pressure) increases monotonically from 1.0 to 9.081 (Fig. 3). Quantitative analysis of the liquid dropout volume fraction reveals a distinct non-linear progression characterized by “slow-fast-slow” dynamics (Fig. 4). Immediately below the dew point, liquid volume increases gradually. Upon entering the main retrograde condensation region, the growth rate accelerates markedly until reaching the maximum liquid dropout. Subsequently, in the lower pressure region, the rate of liquid accumulation decreases again due to enhanced vaporization effects. The influence of temperature on phase behavior is also pronounced. Comparing test results at 88.0°C, 103.0°C, and 118.0°C reveals that the dew-point pressure increases as temperature decreases, measuring 45.74 MPa, 44.29 MPa, and 42.58 MPa, respectively. Furthermore, the maximum liquid volume fraction at 88.0°C is 8.42% higher than that at the reservoir temperature of 103.0 °C. This confirms that lower temperatures promote more severe condensate dropout31.

In summary, the phase behavior of the condensate gas reservoir fluid in Well ZJ14 exhibits high sensitivity to pressure changes, with temperature being a secondary but significant factor. This understanding provides clear guidance for development strategy formulation. During production, it is crucial to actively manage both the rate and magnitude of reservoir pressure decline. Simultaneously, attention must be paid to localized cooling in the near-wellbore region, which can result from pressure depletion or the injection of cooler fluids. Both pressure drop and temperature reduction can induce severe condensate banking, significantly impairing reservoir flow capacity32. To mitigate this retrograde condensate damage, pressure maintenance strategies such as dry gas (or CO2) injection are recommended. These methods work by sustaining reservoir pressure above the dew-point pressure and/or by using the injected gas to strip (extract) the condensed liquid hydrocarbons from the pore space. Implementing such approaches is essential for achieving efficient recovery from this gas condensate reservoir33.

Constant-volume depletion (CVD) test

The constant-volume depletion (CVD) experiment is a key laboratory method for simulating the depletion-driven development process of condensate gas reservoirs and revealing the dynamic evolution of formation fluid composition and phase behavior34. Test results on the fluid sample from Well ZJ14 indicate that during depletion, the mole fractions of light hydrocarbon components from C1-C6 generally exhibit an increasing trend as pressure declines (Fig. 5). Specifically, the methane (C1) content increases from 97.014 to 97.665%, while the content of heavy components (C7+) decreases significantly from 0.919 to 0.299%. This compositional shift demonstrates that the formation fluid becomes progressively lighter during production, driving the overall system phase behavior towards a drier gas state.

Composition of produced fluids from the constant‑volume depletion (CVD) experiment on Well ZJ14.

The development of condensate gas reservoirs differs fundamentally from that of other hydrocarbon reservoir types, as it requires strategies to maximize gas recovery while also maintaining condensate oil recovery. The compositional changes induced by pressure depletion in the ZJ14 well fluid significantly impact its phase behavior. Taking the increase in methane (C1) content as a key example, condensate dropout occurs once pressure falls below the dew point. The rising C1 content subsequently lowers the system’s critical temperature and critical pressure, promoting a shift towards a gaseous phase. This shift helps to suppress the retention of liquid condensate within the formation35. Furthermore, as pressure decreases, a slight increase in wet gas components (C2-C4) coupled with a continuous decline in heavy liquid hydrocarbons (C7+) collectively reduces the system’s dew-point pressure. This reduction delays the onset of retrograde condensation and enhances the potential recoverable fraction of the condensate36. Figure 6 illustrates the variation in retrograde liquid volume fraction with pressure during the constant-volume depletion (CVD) experiment at reservoir temperature. Once the producing pressure drops below the dew-point pressure (44.29 MPa), liquid dropout initiates and increases rapidly with further pressure decline, reaching a peak of 1.96% at approximately 18.00 MPa. Beyond this point, increased vaporization effects at lower pressures cause the liquid volume to decrease, falling to 1.62% at the assumed abandonment pressure.

Based on the aforementioned insights, optimizing the development strategy for Well ZJ14 must address the dual objectives of maximizing both gas and condensate recovery. The produced associated gas, which becomes compositionally lighter during depletion, can be reinjected as an effective means of pressure maintenance and phase behavior control. Gas reinjection not only replenishes reservoir energy but, due to its high methane (C1) content, further lowers the system’s dew-point pressure. This action suppresses retrograde condensate formation and mitigates formation damage, thereby enabling a synergistic enhancement of both gas and condensate recovery factors37. Consequently, it is recommended to adopt a development scheme centered on associated gas reinjection for this well. This approach is proposed to maximize the overall economic and recovery efficiency of the condensate gas reservoir.

Based on preliminary research, the development plan recommends adopting a “one-injection-two-production” well pattern. When the well spacing is too small, gas channeling and well shutdown are likely to occur; when it is too large, displacement efficiency decreases. Therefore, a well spacing of 300 m is identified as the optimal injection-production distance, and a horizontal section length of 1200 m is determined as optimal. Under these conditions, the recovery rate of condensate oil is maximized (33.4%). The injection-production ratio should be controlled between 0.9 and 1.0 to suppress gas channeling, and gas injection timing should be prioritized as early as possible to achieve higher pressure maintenance and recovery rates. The final recommendation is to implement hydrocarbon gas injection development with a 300 m well spacing, using horizontal wells for both injection and production, and deploying a three-dimensional W-shaped horizontal well network to optimize development outcomes (Fig. 7).

Hydrocarbon reservoir fluid type identification

Accurate identification of hydrocarbon reservoir fluid type exerts a significant influence on understanding accumulation mechanisms, estimating resource volumes, and designing development strategies38. The most widely used methods for fluid type identification include the Pressure–Volume-Temperature (PVT) Phase Diagram method and various Empirical Parameter methods.

Methods and basis for phase-state identification of hydrocarbon reservoirs

To enhance the reliability of fluid-phase-state identification in the Jiamuhe Formation reservoirs, this section systematically elaborates the empirical discrimination methods applied, including the basis of their threshold values, associated uncertainties, and regional calibration procedures. The original discrimination boundaries of the φ1 method39, Z1/Z2 composite parameter method39, C5+ vs. C1/C5+ ratio method39, and boxplot method40 were derived from large-sample statistics of typical hydrocarbon reservoirs worldwide. However, the unique tectonic–thermal evolution history of the Permian Jiamuhe Formation in the study area may influence natural gas composition, and direct application of universal thresholds could introduce bias. Therefore, compositional data from an adjacent well, ZJ9 – confirmed through PVT analysis and production testing as a condensate gas reservoir with an oil rim – were selected as calibration “anchor points.” Data points from this well consistently fall within the classical “condensate gas reservoir with oil rim” domain across all discrimination plots, indicating that the selected empirical thresholds remain effective and indicative in this region. This study does not rely on a single method; instead, it adopts a comprehensive discrimination strategy based on cross-validation of multiple methods to strengthen the scientific validity and reliability of the conclusions.

PVT phase diagram method

The Pressure–Volume-Temperature (PVT) phase diagram method represents the most accurate approach for identifying the phase state of subsurface hydrocarbon fluids. For a given fluid composition, the shape of its phase diagram and the position of the critical point on the phase envelope are uniquely determined41. Consequently, the fluid phase can be identified by comparing the actual reservoir temperature and pressure conditions relative to this critical point. The interrelationships between the critical temperature (Tc), cricondentherm (maximum temperature on the phase envelope, Tm), and cricondentherm pressure (Pm) exhibit distinct patterns for different reservoir types. In general, for a gas reservoir, the pressure at the cricondentherm (Pm) corresponds to a temperature that is higher than the critical temperature (Tc) but lower than the cricondentherm itself (Tm). Conversely, for an oil reservoir, the critical temperature (Tc) is higher than the temperature at the cricondentherm pressure, yet still lower than the cricondentherm (Tm). Condensate gas and volatile oil reservoirs display characteristics intermediate between these two end-member patterns42.

Based on high-temperature, high-pressure physical property test data of formation fluid from the Jiamuhe Formation in well ZJ14, the phase diagram was generated by fitting with PVTsim software(Fig. 8). The simulation results show that the critical temperature of the fluid sample is 185.26 K, the critical condensate temperature is 514.23 K, the critical pressure is 45.01 MPa, and the critical condensate pressure is 27.12 MPa. The simulation agrees well with constant-volume depletion experimental data. Formation temperature and pressure are two key external factors controlling the phase behavior of hydrocarbon reservoir fluids43. The formation temperature of well ZJ14 is 374.45 K, which lies between the critical temperature and the critical condensate temperature, while the formation pressure is 55.16 MPa. The dew-point pressure at formation temperature is 41.87 MPa. Since the formation pressure exceeds the dew-point pressure, with a positive pressure difference of 13.29 MPa, the fluid exists in the condensate gas phase under reservoir conditions (Fig. 8).

Pressure–temperature phase diagram of well ZJ14 fluids.

The phase behavior of underground hydrocarbons differs from that of fluids produced at the surface. This discrepancy arises from the progressive changes in temperature and pressure along the wellbore during production, which induce phase separation of the hydrocarbon components43. Assuming that temperature and pressure gradually decrease to surface conditions (15 °C, 0.1 MPa) during production, the phase evolution follows the dashed-line path shown in Fig. 8. During this process, when the pressure declines to the dew‑point pressure, the system enters the retrograde condensation region. In this region, gaseous hydrocarbons separate from the liquid phase, resulting in the coexistence of oil and gas phases.

For well ZJ14, the molar fraction of C5+ is 3.50% and the C1/C5+ ratio is 25.86, which satisfies both conditions, thereby confirming it as a condensate gas reservoir with an oil rim (Fig. 10).

NMR logging identification

While well-log data play a crucial role in fluid-phase identification and dynamic formation evaluation44–46, it is regrettable that we were unable to obtain the original well-log data for this study. As a result, our analysis has been confined to the available nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) logging data. Nuclear magnetic resonance logging enables effective identification of reservoir fluid properties based on differences in hydrogen nucleus relaxation characteristics among different fluids47. By applying a T2 cutoff value (1.5 ms) to distinguish movable fluid from bound water, and conducting a detailed analysis of the T1-T2 spectrum for Well ZJ14, vertical differentiation in fluid phase behavior was clearly observed. As shown in Fig. 12, the upper reservoir interval (Fig. 12a) exhibits a prominent movable gas signal in the T1-T2 spectrum, indicating the presence of a free gas cap. In contrast, the lower reservoir interval (Fig. 12b) shows a spectral response dominated by a strong movable oil signal, confirming the development of a substantial oil rim at the base. These findings are consistent with the phase-state discrimination results derived from PVT analysis and light hydrocarbon compositions, providing mutual verification and jointly supporting the conclusion that the study area is a “condensate gas reservoir with an oil rim”.

Phase type determination results and uncertainty analysis

To ensure the reliability of fluid phase-state identification in the Jiamuhe Formation reservoirs, it is essential to systematically evaluate the limitations of empirical methods and the sensitivity of the underlying data. The methods applied in this study all rely on the accurate determination of C1-C5+ components, with the C5+ content being a key source of uncertainty. Loss of light hydrocarbons during sampling or analysis could significantly alter compositional ratios and lead to misinterpretation. Therefore, all samples were collected using high-pressure physical property sampling and analyzed via chromatography in accordance with national standards to ensure data quality.

At the methodological level: the φ1 method is sensitive to biodegradation, yet no obvious alteration signs were observed in the samples from the study area, supporting its applicability here; the statistical coefficients of the Z1/Z2 method were validated as effective using the local “anchor” well (Well ZJ9); and the empirical rules of the boxplot method were statistically supported by a 90% correct-classification rate for the “anchor” samples. Through rigorous quality control, geological constraints, and cross-validation of multiple methods, this study effectively reduced uncertainties and enhanced the reliability of the conclusions.

Based on formation fluid composition and high-pressure/high-temperature physical property experimental data, the fluid phase-state characteristics of the Permian Jiamuhe Formation reservoirs in Well ZJ14 were systematically identified by integrating PVT phase diagram analysis, multiple empirical discrimination formulas, and NMR logging results. The findings (Table 4) indicate that the formation fluid is a condensate gas reservoir with an oil rim. This phase-state understanding provides an important basis for optimizing development plans and analyzing production performance. It should be noted, however, that the current phase behavior understanding, derived from conventional PVT experiments, does not account for the potential capillary pressure effects in nanoscale reservoir pores48. Investigating such effects represents an important direction for future research aimed at deepening the understanding of this block and similar reservoirs.

Conclusion

PVT experiments on formation fluids from the Permian Jiamuhe Formation in Well ZJ14 indicate typical condensate gas phase behavior. The producing gas‑oil ratio is as high as 7621 m3/m3, and the formation‑dew‑point pressure difference is 7.71 MPa, confirming that the fluid exists as a single‑phase gas under reservoir conditions. Once the formation pressure falls below the dew‑point pressure (44.29 MPa), retrograde condensation occurs in the system, with a maximum retrograde liquid dropout of 1.96%, which may potentially affect gas well productivity.

Compositional analysis of the wellstream reveals a typical high-maturity near-critical fluid system, exhibiting characteristic features of a gas-condensate reservoir: light components (C1–C4) dominate, reflecting the absolute predominance of the gas phase under reservoir conditions and indicating a high gas–oil ratio system. The content of heavy components is low, resulting in an overall compositional structure characterized by “enrichment in light hydrocarbons and scarcity of heavy hydrocarbons.” Notably, the C5+ content is 3.50%, classifying the fluid as a rich gas condensate and potentially providing the material basis for the formation of an oil rim. Therefore, when deploying wells in structurally low positions, emphasis should be placed on the identification and co‑development of the oil rim. It is recommended to integrate seismic attribute analysis with detailed structural interpretation to further assess the distribution and development potential of the oil rim.

However, the different methods yield slightly varying assessments of the oil-rim scale, which reflects the heterogeneity and uncertainty inherent in the distribution of oil rims in such reservoirs. This study demonstrates the significant influence of temperature on phase behavior: under lower‑temperature conditions, the dew‑point pressure increases and retrograde condensation liquid volume rises. During field development, close attention should be paid to localized temperature decreases near the wellbore caused by pressure depletion or the injection of cold fluids. It is recommended to adopt development strategies such as gas injection for pressure maintenance to suppress retrograde condensation damage, thereby enhancing the overall development efficiency of the gas reservoir.
