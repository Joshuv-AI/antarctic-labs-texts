Effects of progressive aerobic training on neural synchrony and functional connectivity in internet gaming disorder: a randomized controlled fMRI study
Authors: Qinxuan Li, Xin Luo, Meiting Wei, Shaoyu Cui, Shenji Yang, Yandan Li, Shunchao He, Guang-Heng Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInternet gaming disorder (IGD) has gained increasing attention due to its rising global prevalence, with significant negative impacts on mental health, academic performance, and social functioning. Aerobic exercise, due to its low cost and high adherence, has emerged as a potential intervention. This study aimed to examine the behavioral and neural effects of a moderate-intensity, progressive aerobic exercise program in individuals with IGD.MethodsEighty college students with IGD were randomly assigned to a progressive aerobic training group (PAT) or a free training group (FT), with 64 completing the study. The PAT group engaged in 20 supervised treadmill sessions over six weeks, with intensity progressively increasing from 46 to 55% VO₂max. The FT group exercised freely without restrictions. Behavioral assessments (IAT, DSM-5 IGD scale, QGU) and fMRI scans were conducted before and after the intervention. Neural synchrony was assessed using intersubject correlation (ISC) analysis, and seed-based connectivity (SBC) analysis was performed to examine changes in functional connectivity of addiction-related brain regions.ResultsThe PAT group showed significant reductions in IAT and DSM-5 scores, with a notable group × time interaction for IAT. fMRI results revealed increased ISC in the left postcentral gyrus and decreased ISC in the left caudate and right precentral gyrus. Caudate ISC changes correlated with IAT improvements. SBC analysis showed reduced connectivity between the left caudate and right prefrontal cortex in the PAT group.ConclusionsProgressive moderate aerobic exercise reduced IGD symptoms and craving, alongside altered neural synchrony and connectivity in reward and control networks. Caudate-related markers may serve as potential targets for intervention. Future studies should adopt larger, longitudinal designs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07419-6.Keywords: Internet gaming disorder, Aerobic exercise, Craving reduction, FMRI, Reward system, Executive control network

Introduction

Internet gaming disorder (IGD) is characterized by excessive and uncontrollable gaming behavior, accompanied by symptoms such as tolerance, withdrawal, and impairments in cognitive and behavioral functions [1]. Since 2013, IGD has been included in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) as a condition warranting further study. Epidemiological studies estimate the global prevalence of IGD to range from 0.3% to 5.9% [61], with rates reaching as high as 8.8% among adolescents [20]. IGD not only impairs emotional regulation [39] and academic performance [52], but is also closely associated with various psychological problems such as anxiety [63] and ADHD [25]. Thus, developing low-cost, easy-to-implement, and widely applicable intervention strategies with high adherence has become an urgent practical need.

Previous studies have identified the core characteristics of IGD from both behavioral and neural perspectives. Behaviorally, individuals with IGD often show heightened sensitivity to gaming cues [10, 13, 77], impaired craving regulation [67], deficits in inhibitory control [12, 62, 82], and abnormal decision-making [35, 70, 71]. Neuroimaging studies have further revealed abnormal activation in the prefrontal cortex and related functional areas in IGD, particularly in domains such as executive control [11, 75, 81], craving regulation [75], and reward processing [12]. Gaming cues, as salient reward signals, often lead to hyperactivation of the reward system in individuals with IGD [13, 64, 79].

Several interventions for IGD have demonstrated efficacy, including cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) [23, 76–78], mindfulness training [51], and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) [31, 66]. Among these, aerobic exercise is gaining prominence as a promising non-pharmacological intervention due to its low cost, simplicity, and lack of side effects. Research shows that aerobic exercise can alleviate psychological problems such as depression [37, 60], anxiety [3], and ADHD [4, 7]. It has also shown positive effects on behavioral addictions such as internet addiction [65, 80] and smartphone addiction [41]. In substance addiction, aerobic exercise has been found to reduce cravings [6] and support addiction recovery across various stages—initiation, maintenance, withdrawal, and relapse [43]. Its potential mechanisms may involve enhancing executive function [19, 26] and reducing the reactivity of the reward-processing network to addiction-related cues [27].

In the IGD field, emerging evidence suggests the potential of exercise-based interventions. For example, Maden [44] found that both virtual reality exercise and aerobic exercise significantly reduced gaming time and IGD severity. Hong [26] further demonstrated that combining CBT with moderate-intensity aerobic exercise was more effective than CBT alone in alleviating IGD symptoms and enhancing left prefrontal alpha asymmetry. However, research on exercise interventions for IGD remains limited, and there is a lack of systematic evidence regarding the neural mechanisms and effectiveness of structured aerobic exercise as a standalone intervention. Standardization of intervention protocols and exploration of underlying mechanisms are still insufficient.

To address this research gap, the present study adopts moderate-intensity aerobic exercise as the core intervention, a method that has been widely applied in addiction treatment due to its high safety and effectiveness [6, 28, 40, 44, 65]. A progressive training program is integrated to enhance motivation and adherence [57]. This study aims to systematically investigate the effects of aerobic exercise on IGD symptoms, gaming cravings, and related neural mechanisms.

In exploring the neural mechanisms of IGD, intersubject correlation (ISC) analysis has recently gained increasing attention as a data-driven fMRI approach based on naturalistic tasks. ISC is used to assess the synchronization of brain activity across individuals exposed to the same naturalistic stimuli (e.g., videos)[17, 24]. Unlike traditional model-based approaches (e.g., GLM with HRF convolution), ISC does not rely on strict experimental control or predefined response models. Instead, it captures shared neural response patterns in ecologically valid contexts by computing the temporal correlation of fMRI signals across subjects [50]. Studies have shown that ISC and GLM yield highly consistent activation regions [54], further supporting ISC’s effectiveness in identifying stimulus-related brain areas.

Specifically, we focus on two core research questions: (1) Can aerobic exercise effectively alleviate IGD symptoms and reduce craving for gaming? (2) Does the intervention induce specific changes in intersubject neural synchronization (ISC) and functional connectivity (FC) in brain regions? We hypothesize that aerobic exercise will not only improve behavioral symptoms and craving in individuals with IGD but also modulate ISC and FC patterns within key brain networks related to reward processing and executive control. This study will help elucidate the neural mechanisms underlying aerobic exercise interventions for IGD and provide theoretical and practical insights for developing scalable and effective addiction intervention strategies.

Methods

Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Yunnan Normal University (Approval No. yunnuethic2024-044). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation, and appropriate remuneration was provided after completion of the experiment. This randomized controlled trial was Designed and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement, with reference to the updated 2025 checklist. The trial was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2400090834, https://www.chictr.org.cn) on 2024–10-14.

Participants

The online questionnaire was distributed to the university student population through the internet. Initially, 998 subjects were enrolled, and 918 subjects were excluded after strictly screening (750 with DSM-5 score less than 5; 105 in sports-related programs; 36 with BMI less than 15 or more than 28; and 27 unwilling to participate in further experiments). Finally, 80 subjects were included and were randomly assigned into the intervention group or the control group.

Criteria for inclusion in the experiment: (1) Internet Addiction Test (IAT; Cronbach’s α = 0.902–0.920, demonstrating good reliability and construct validity in previous studies: [36, 46, 69]) total scores ≥ 50 and DSM-5 scores ≥ 5; (2) online gaming history > 1 year or more; (3) right-handed; and (4) In order to ensure that participants are effective in short-term aerobic interventions, a range was set 15 ≤ BMI ≤ 28.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Exercise-related specialties; (2) history of psychiatric illness, medical treatment for internet gaming disorder, tobacco dependence addiction, and alcohol dependence addiction in the past year; and (3) participants in the category of psychotic disorders eligible for the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI).

Group assignment and intervention procedures

Eighty participants were randomly assigned, using a computer-generated randomization sequence, to either the progressive aerobic training (PAT) group (N = 40) or the free training (FT) group (N = 40). All participants completed baseline questionnaires prior to training, including the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for IGD [1], the Internet Addiction Test (IAT; [74]), and the Questionnaire on Gaming Urge (QGU), which was adapted from the Brief Questionnaire of Smoking Urges (QSU-Brief; [9]) and has been previously applied in IGD research [15, 32, 51].

The aerobic training protocol was Developed in accordance with the guidelines of the American College of Sports Medicine, involving a progressive intensity Design: 46% of estimated maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂ max) in the first 10 sessions and 55% in the subsequent 10 sessions. Each session lasted 40 min, and a total of 20 sessions were conducted over approximately 6 weeks [38, 53, 55]. The PAT group exercised exclusively on treadmills, with individualized speeds Determined by the results of the McArdle 3-min step test [45]. In contrast, the FT group was not subject to any prescribed exercise intensity, modality, or duration. Participants were free to decide their activity type (e.g., running, slow walking, jumping rope, stationary cycling, or resistance training) and could terminate the session at any point within the 40-min period, thereby reflecting their natural exercise habits.

All participants trained in the same facility but remained unaware of the presence or grouping of others. Only the recruitment staff were aware of the group labels (i.e., which arm corresponded to PAT or FT) in order to notify and manage participants. The actual implementation of the training was carried out solely by trained intervention staff, who were not informed of the study hypotheses and were unaware of which group constituted the “intervention” arm. Both training programs were presented under neutral labels (“Program A”/“Program B”), without indicating which corresponded to the intervention or control condition.

To ensure safety and adherence, participants were continuously monitored during all exercise sessions for potential adverse events (e.g., musculoskeletal discomfort, dizziness, fatigue). No adverse events were observed or reported throughout the intervention. Detailed intervention protocols for both groups are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

No interim analyses or stopping guidelines were prespecified.

Parameters of fMRI scans

All MRI data were collected at the Neuroimaging Center of Yunnan Normal University (YNNU) using a 3.0 Tesla Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel head/neck coil.

At the beginning of each scanning session, a short localizer scan was performed for slice positioning. Subsequently, participants underwent a high-resolution structural scan. T1-weighted structural images were acquired using a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2200 ms, echo time (TE) = 3.42 ms, inversion time (TI) = 1030 ms, flip angle = 9°, acquisition matrix = 224 × 224, field of view (FOV) = 224 × 224 mm2, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, no gap, parallel imaging acceleration factor (GRAPPA) = 2, yielding an isotropic voxel size of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3. The total scan duration was approximately 4.5 min.

After a short rest period (~ 2 min), participants watched game-related video clips while undergoing functional imaging. T2*-weighted functional images were acquired using a gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with simultaneous multi-slice (SMS, multiband) acceleration. Parameters were as follows: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, multiband factor = 3, 54 axial slices, slice thickness = 3.0 mm, no gap, acquisition matrix = 92 × 92, FOV = 220 × 220 mm2, resulting in a voxel size of 2.4 × 2.4 × 3.0 mm3. The total acquisition time was approximately 5.2 min, yielding 155 volumes per run.

Behavioral data analysis

To evaluate the effects of different intervention strategies on IGD severity and craving levels, repeated-measures ANOVAs with a 2 (Group: PAT vs. FT) × 2 (Time: Pre vs. Post) design were conducted separately for the IAT, DSM-5, and QGU scale scores. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics).

fMRI data preprocessing

Functional MRI data preprocessing was performed using the DPABI toolbox [68] implemented in MATLAB 2020 (based on SPM12). The pipeline included the following steps: (1) discarding the first 10 volumes to allow signal stabilization; (2) slice-timing correction and realignment for head motion; (3) skull stripping and tissue segmentation of T1-weighted anatomical images, followed by spatial normalization to the MNI152 standard space using the New Segment + DARTEL approach; functional images were co-registered to individual T1 images, normalized, and resampled to an isotropic voxel size of 2 × 2 × 2 mm3; (4) spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel (FWHM = 4 mm).

To reduce confounding effects, nuisance regression was conducted including the Friston 24-parameter model for head motion, as well as white matter, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and global signal regressors. The residual time series were band-pass filtered (0.01–0.10 Hz) to retain low-frequency neural fluctuations.

Head motion was strictly monitored. All participants exhibited maximum translational displacement < 1.5 mm and maximum rotational displacement < 1.5°. No participants were excluded for excessive motion, and no scrubbing procedures were applied, as intersubject correlation (ISC) and functional connectivity (FC) analyses require temporal continuity of the data. Group-level head motion, quantified by mean Framewise Displacement (FD; Jenkinson method), was as follows: Pre-test PAT = 0.060 ± 0.022 mm; Pre-test FT = 0.050 ± 0.017 mm; Post-test PAT = 0.050 ± 0.017 mm; Post-test FT = 0.060 ± 0.024 mm. No significant between-group differences were observed.

All preprocessing steps followed standardized and widely adopted pipelines, ensuring rigorous control of motion artifacts and data quality for subsequent group-level analyses.

Intersubject correlation (ISC) analysis

First-level analysis

First-level analysis

ISC computations were performed using the BrainIAK toolbox in a Python 3.9 environment (https://brainiak.org/). The leave-one-out approach was adopted: for each participant, voxel-wise time series were extracted and correlated (using Pearson’s r) with the mean time series of the remaining participants at the corresponding voxel. This procedure generated an individual ISC map, in which each voxel’s value represents the degree of synchrony between the participant’s brain activity and the group-averaged response.

(2)Second-level analysis

Second-level analysis

To examine the effects of the intervention on neural synchrony, a 2 (Group: PAT vs. FT) × 2 (Time: Pre vs. Post) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted at the group level using DPABI. The key focus was on the interaction effect: (PATpost − PATpre)—(FTpost − FTpre). Non-parametric permutation testing (5,000 iterations) was applied to enhance statistical robustness. Voxel-level significance was assessed using the Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) algorithm. Results were corrected for multiple comparisons using Family-Wise Error (FWE) correction, with a two-tailed threshold of p < 0.05.

Definition and selection of regions of interest (ROIs)

From the group-level ISC analysis, we identified brain regions showing significant group × time interaction effects, indicating specific modulation of neural synchrony due to the intervention. For each significant cluster, a spherical ROI with an 8 mm radius was created centered on the peak activation coordinate.

Mean ISC values within each ROI were extracted for all participants across the four experimental conditions (PATpre, PATpost, FTpre, FTpost). To identify ROIs with intervention-specific effects, simple effect analyses were conducted within each group separately, comparing pre- and post-intervention ISC values. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. Only ROIs showing significant ISC changes in the PAT group (but not in the FT group) were retained as target regions for subsequent functional connectivity (FC) and brain-behavior correlation analyses.

Correlation between ROI-based ISC changes and behavioral measures

To investigate whether changes in neural synchrony were associated with improvements in IGD symptoms and craving levels, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted within the PAT group. Specifically, ISC change scores (Post—Pre) for each target ROI were correlated with changes in the IAT, DSM-5, and QGU scores. This analysis aimed to determine whether intervention-induced ISC changes could predict behavioral improvements, thereby providing insights into underlying neural mechanisms.

Seed-based functional connectivity (SBC) analysis

To further explore whether progressive aerobic training modulates functional connectivity patterns of addiction-related brain regions, seed-based connectivity analyses were conducted using the CONN toolbox (https://web.conn-toolbox.org/). The analysis proceeded in two stages:

Individual-Level Analysis: Each ROI was defined as a seed, and whole-brain voxel-wise functional connectivity maps were computed for each participant. The strength of connectivity was estimated using a weighted general linear model (GLM) and transformed into Fisher’s Z-scores, reflecting the temporal correlation between the seed and each voxel across the brain.

Individual-Level Analysis: Each ROI was defined as a seed, and whole-brain voxel-wise functional connectivity maps were computed for each participant. The strength of connectivity was estimated using a weighted general linear model (GLM) and transformed into Fisher’s Z-scores, reflecting the temporal correlation between the seed and each voxel across the brain.

Group-Level Analysis: A 2 (Group: PAT vs. FT) × 2 (Time: Pre vs. Post) repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to identify interaction effects in FC patterns. Voxel-level results were initially thresholded at p < 0.001 (uncorrected), with cluster-level correction using false discovery rate (FDR) at p < 0.05.

Group-Level Analysis: A 2 (Group: PAT vs. FT) × 2 (Time: Pre vs. Post) repeated-measures ANOVA was performed to identify interaction effects in FC patterns. Voxel-level results were initially thresholded at p < 0.001 (uncorrected), with cluster-level correction using false discovery rate (FDR) at p < 0.05.

For clusters showing significant group × time interaction effects, average connectivity strength was extracted, and simple effect analyses were conducted in SPSS to examine within-group changes (Pre vs. Post) for the PAT and FT groups separately. This analysis aimed to determine whether the observed FC changes were primarily driven by the PAT intervention, thereby strengthening the causal interpretation of the intervention’s neural effects.

Results

Participant flow and recruitment

A total of 80 eligible participants were randomized into the progressive aerobic training (PAT; N = 40) and free training (FT; N = 40) groups. Sixteen participants discontinued the intervention (PAT: 7; FT: 9), mainly due to lack of time or unwillingness to continue exercise training. Consequently, 64 participants completed the full intervention program (PAT: N = 33; FT: N = 31) and were included in the primary outcome analyses. Demographic and baseline characteristics of completers are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Recruitment and intervention were conducted from November 30, 2024 to February 28, 2025. Participant adherence was high, intervention staff strictly followed the prescribed protocols, and no concomitant care or additional treatments were provided to participants in either group during the trial.

Simple effect analysis indicated that the PAT group showed a significant decrease in IAT scores following the intervention (F (1, 61) = 29.260, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.321), whereas no significant change was observed in the FT group (p = 0.147). Neural mechanism results induced by the intervention

Brain–behavior correlations following intervention

Within the PAT group, ISC change scores (Post—Pre) from the three target ROIs were extracted and correlated with changes in behavioral scores on the IAT, DSM-5, and QGU using Pearson correlation analysis. The results revealed a significant positive correlation between changes in IAT scores and ISC changes in the left caudate nucleus (r = 0.354, p = 0.043), suggesting that improvements in neural synchrony within the caudate were associated with reductions in IGD symptom severity. This relationship is illustrated in Fig. 3C, where increased ISC reduction in the caudate is associated with greater symptom improvement.

Functional connectivity changes induced by aerobic training

Based on the above-defined ROIs (left caudate, left postcentral gyrus, and right precentral gyrus), seed-based connectivity (SBC) analysis was conducted. A significant group × time interaction effect [(PATpost − PATpre) − (FTpost − FTpre)] was found in functional connectivity between the left caudate and the right prefrontal cortex (MNI: x = + 28, y = + 60, z = −12; p-FDR = 0.009; Fig. 4A).

Further extraction of connection strength across conditions revealed that, in the PAT group, caudate–prefrontal connectivity significantly decreased following the intervention (F (1,62) = 13.877, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.183), whereas no significant change was observed in the FT group (p = 0.387; Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that progressive aerobic training effectively modulated the functional connectivity between the caudate and the prefrontal cortex, indicating its specific impact on addiction-related neural circuits.

Discussion

This study compared a structured, intensity-progressive, and exercise-type–controlled aerobic training program (PAT) with an unrestricted free training (FT) condition. For ecological validity, PAT was designed as a standardized and scalable intervention model, whereas FT more closely reflected individualized exercise habits and lifestyle patterns—participants were free to choose both exercise type and intensity. This design allowed us to capture the natural variability of exercise behaviors in individuals with IGD, while specifically testing the therapeutic efficacy of PAT. By integrating behavioral assessments with naturalistic fMRI analysis, we found that PAT significantly improved IGD symptoms, altered neural synchrony, and modulated functional connectivity.

PAT alleviates IGD symptoms

At the behavioral level, significant improvements were observed in the progressive aerobic training group, as reflected by reductions in IAT and DSM-5 scores. A significant group-by-time interaction was found for the IAT, and a marginally significant interaction for DSM-5, indicating that structured aerobic intervention was more effective than free training in alleviating IGD symptoms. Although the group-by-time interaction for QGU was not significant, within-group analysis showed a significant reduction in craving in the aerobic group but not in the free training group. These findings align with previous studies suggesting the beneficial effects of exercise on addiction-related symptoms [26, 44], reinforcing the potential of aerobic exercise as a viable non-pharmacological intervention.

PAT modulates IGD neural synchrony

At the neural level, ISC analysis showed that the intervention modulated synchrony in the caudate nucleus, precentral gyrus, and postcentral gyrus. Specifically, the left caudate and right precentral gyrus exhibited decreased ISC, while the left postcentral gyrus showed increased ISC.

PAT reduced caudate synchrony

The caudate nucleus is a key node of the reward system [21, 49]. Prior studies have demonstrated that individuals with IGD exhibit hyperactivation in the reward system in response to gaming cues, reflecting enhanced automatic reward responses [5, 10, 13, 34, 64]. The decreased ISC in the caudate observed here may indicate that the intervention disrupted a uniform reward response pattern, reflecting reduced automatic reward processing. This is consistent with prior research showing that aerobic exercise reduces reward system hyperactivation [27, 28]. Furthermore, ISC reduction in the caudate was significantly correlated with IAT score improvement, suggesting that modulation of this region may play a key role in symptom alleviation. These results reaffirm the caudate's central role in IGD [2] and support its use as a neural marker of intervention efficacy.

PAT decreased precentral synchrony

The precentral gyrus, part of the primary motor cortex, is associated not only with movement execution but also with motor intention [18, 22]. Prior studies have shown that activation in the right precentral gyrus predicts IGD severity [72], and increased activation during No-Go trials reflects impulsive behavior tendencies [11]. The observed decrease in ISC may reflect reduced motor readiness in response to gaming stimuli, implying alleviated impulsive tendencies and enhanced behavioral self-regulation.

PAT increased postcentral synchrony

The postcentral gyrus (BA43, parietal operculum), a secondary somatosensory cortex, is involved in somatosensory integration [47, 58], attention modulation [47, 73], and external stimulus processing [56]. Increased ISC in this region suggests enhanced intersubject consistency in somatosensory processing after intervention. This aligns with findings that aerobic exercise improves attentional function [16]. The observed increase in postcentral ISC may reflect improved somatosensory awareness and attentional regulation, potentially weakening the immersive experience of gaming. According to immersion theory [29, 30], gaming often leads to the suppression of real-world sensory input. Our findings suggest that exercise may reduce this immersion by improving real-world perception, thereby supporting behavior regulation.

PAT modulates prefrontal-striatal connectivity

Functional connectivity analysis revealed a significant decrease in connectivity between the left caudate and the right prefrontal cortex post-intervention, driven primarily by the progressive aerobic training group. The prefrontal-striatal circuit is known to play a key role in inhibitory control in addiction [14, 48]. IGD individuals often show hyperconnectivity in this circuit in response to gaming cues [33, 42], reflecting impaired inhibitory control. The observed FC reduction may indicate a decreased demand on prefrontal regulation due to attenuated caudate reactivity, consistent with the ISC results and suggesting improved cognitive control efficiency. This aligns with findings that aerobic exercise enhances executive function [8, 59].

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the short intervention period prevents firm conclusions about long-term effects. Third, although ISC analyses provided ecologically valid insights, the interpretations remain indirect and speculative, as they relied mainly on prior literature and correlational associations rather than direct behavioral or task-based validation. Future studies should combine naturalistic paradigms with task-based fMRI and behavioral assessments to provide stronger mechanistic evidence. Finally, this study did not establish independent a priori ROIs or an external validation dataset. This limitation reflects the practical challenges of IGD intervention research, where recruiting clinical participants and implementing parallel intervention cohorts are resource-intensive and often infeasible. Nonetheless, our findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and serve as a foundation for future replication. Multi-site collaborations with larger and more diverse samples will be critical to confirm the robustness and external validity of the present results.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that moderate-intensity progressive aerobic training effectively improves IGD symptoms and craving levels. It also reshapes addiction-related neural mechanisms by modulating ISC in the caudate, precentral, and postcentral gyri, and reducing functional connectivity between the caudate and prefrontal cortex. These findings expand our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying exercise-based addiction interventions and provide empirical support for developing low-cost, scalable, and adherent therapeutic strategies.
