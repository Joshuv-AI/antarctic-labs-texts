Responsive Mealtime Matters: Feeding Autonomy and Varied Settings Were Associated With Problem‐Solving Skills of Ethiopian Children Aged 6–23 Months
Authors: Hailu Hailemariam, Barbara J Stoecker, Zelalem Tafese Wondimagegne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough Ethiopia has established an early childhood development (ECD) policy framework and implemented various nutrition and health interventions, substantial risks for poor ECD outcomes persist. This study aimed to determine the association between responsive feeding (RF) practices and problem‐solving skills of children aged 6–23 months. A community‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted from March to June 2024 among 401 randomly selected mother‐child dyads in Hawassa Zuria woreda/district and Hawassa City Administration, Sidama Region. Data were collected using pre‐tested questionnaires with Kobo Toolbox. RF practices were assessed using the locally translated and adapted 15‐item RF Practices Assessment Tool (RFPAT), while child problem‐solving skills were evaluated using the Ages & Stages Questionnaires, Third Edition (ASQ‐3). Data analysis was performed in SPSS version 20, employing bivariate analysis and logistic regression, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. The median (IQR) problem‐solving skill development score was 30 (IQR: 20–60). Approximately two out of five children were at risk for problem‐solving development skills. The problem‐solving skills domain demonstrated a significant positive correlation with both language (r = 0.69, p < 0.001) and composite motor skills (r = 0.81, p < 0.001). Children living in a household with a larger family had 1.2 times higher odds of being on track in problem‐solving skills compared to those who were part of smaller families (AOR = 1.2, p < 0.05). Mothers or caregivers who rarely fed their child in a place designated only for feeding had 2.6 times higher odds of the child being on track in problem‐solving skill development compared to those who always fed their child in a designated feeding place (AOR = 2.6, p < 0.05). Mothers who rarely allowed their child to practice self‐feeding or did not encourage feeding autonomy had 70% lower odds (AOR = 0.3, p < 0.05) of their child being on track in problem‐solving skills compared to those who strongly encouraged feeding autonomy. Promoting feeding autonomy, household interaction and feeding a child in varied and stimulating environments may offer a strategic opportunity to promote problem‐solving skills of young children in Ethiopia. Longitudinal intervention studies are required to clarify cause and effect relationship between RF practices and ECD domains.Keywords: early childhood development (ECD), Ethiopia, feeding autonomy, problem‐solving skills, responsive feeding (RF)

Introduction

Early childhood, from pregnancy to 8 years of age, is a critical period characterized by rapid problem‐solving, motor, language, socio‐emotional, and physical growth. The most intense brain development occurs within the first 1000 days starting from conception to 2 years of age (American Academy of Pediatrics 2024; Black et al. 2017; Georgieff et al. 2018; UNICEF 2024; Ursache and Noble 2017; WHO 2018).

Problem‐solving skills of young children are a signature attribute of adult humans (Keen 2011) and like other early childhood development (ECD) domains, problem‐solving skills build on adequate nutrition, responsive caregiving, stimulating environment, good health, early learning, and other social interactions (Britto et al. 2017; UNICEF 2022; WHO 2018; World Health Organization 2017).

A disadvantaged start in life can hinder ECD, leading to delays in problem‐solving, motor skills, and language acquisition. These developmental setbacks may later contribute to reduced adult earnings and increased social tensions (Black et al. 2017; Lu et al. 2016). In spite of the recognized benefits of ECD, about 250 million, a number approaching half of the children under five in low and middle‐income countries (LMICs), are at risk of not reaching their full potential due to poverty and stunting (Black et al. 2017; Lu et al. 2016).

Despite improvements in children's nutritional status and health outcomes in Ethiopia over the past two decades (EPHI and ICF 2021), the risk of poor ECD outcomes remains high (Ministry of Health 2020), placing the country among the LMICs with many children not reaching their full developmental potential (Lu et al. 2016; USAID 2021; World Bank 2023). Studies in Ethiopia using the Ages and Stages Questionnaire‐3 (ASQ‐3) have identified ECD delays, particularly in fine motor skills, communication, and problem‐solving, with malnutrition being a significant contributing factor (Delbiso et al. 2024; Workie et al. 2020).

Ethiopia's poor ECD outcomes are also evident in global indices such as the human capital index (HCI), where the country scores 0.38, below the sub‐Saharan African average, indicating that a child born in Ethiopia today may only reach 38% of their potential, due to various factors, including responsive caregiving (World Bank 2021).

Growth and development of young children are influenced not only by the quality and quantity of the food they consume, but also by the way they are fed, including feeding practices, caregiver‐child interactions during meals, and the overall feeding environment (Black et al. 2022). Early childhood feeding environments, including responsive feeding (RF) practices, not only affect children's nutritional status but also provide children with a variety of external stimulations to affect their brain development, especially in the first 2 years of life (Liu and Chang 2023; WHO 2018). Early experiences in an environment with nurturing care (WHO 2018) directly or indirectly affect ECD with a lasting impact on well‐being and academic performance, as well as social and emotional outcomes throughout the lifespan (Britto et al. 2017; Georgieff et al. 2018).

The WHO nurturing care framework highlights responsive caregiving as a key domain, focusing on the dynamic interplay between mothers and young children. Responsive caregiving involves attentiveness to the child's emotional and physical needs, interactive communication through speech and play, and supportive nonverbal signals like touch and eye contact (WHO 2018). Within this broader domain, RF represents a specific and critical application of responsive caregiving, particularly during feeding interactions, where caregivers recognize and respond appropriately to children's hunger and satiety cues (Foster et al. 2025). Understanding RF as an integral component of responsive caregiving helps situate its role in promoting not only adequate nutrition but also early learning and developmental outcomes.

RF is essential for promoting self‐regulation and enhancing problem‐solving and socio‐emotional development (Pérez‐Escamilla et al. 2017; WHO 2018). RF is an interactive feeding strategy that prioritizes nurturing, reciprocal exchanges, promoting independence, healthy food preferences, and age‐appropriate recognition of hunger and fullness cues (Bentley et al. 2011; Black and Aboud 2011; Grantham‐McGregor et al. 2007; Pérez‐Escamilla et al. 2017, 2019, 2021; Pérez‐Escamilla and Segura‐Pérez 2020; Sall et al. 2020; Walker et al. 2007).

Considering the high risk of poor ECD outcomes and their long‐lasting consequences, the Ethiopian government has shown its commitment to ECD via implementation of various nutrition and health interventions and by developing policy recommendations aligned with the WHO nurturing framework (WHO 2018; World Bank 2023). The ECD policy aims to create a structured and coordinated approach to support the holistic development of young children in the country (Ministry of Health 2020).

Most nutritional recommendations in developing countries focus on food alone, without attention to the feeding context. Parents may adopt controlling behaviors without considering children's cues which may contribute to ECD delays (Black and Aboud 2011). Although RF is a global priority (Pérez‐Escamilla et al. 2017), many LMICs, including Ethiopia emphasize more about what to feed rather than how to feed infants and young children.

Ethiopia's infant and young children feeding (IYCF) strategy promotes RF practices such as recognizing hunger cues, avoiding force, minimizing distractions, and fostering bonding during meals (Ojewunmi and Federal Ministry of Health 2020). However, to our knowledge, despite the high number of children in the country failing to reach their full developmental potential, there is limited data on the development of problem‐solving skills or on RF practices in resource‐limited settings of Ethiopia. Therefore, this study assessed associations between problem‐solving skills development and the RF practices of mothers among children aged 6–23 months in the study area.

Methods and Materials

Study Design and Setting

A community‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted from March to June 2024 among children aged 6–23 months residing in selected woredas or districts and the city administration of the Sidama region. The region, one of the twelve regions in Ethiopia, consists of four Zones and one city administration. In this study, Hawassa city administration was selected as one of the study areas in addition to Hawassa Zuria woreda located within the central Sidama region. The region is situated approximately 283 km south of the national capital, Addis Ababa (Figure 1), and has a population of approximately four million, with 95% living in rural areas (Population Census Commission 2015).

Map showing location of the study area (Yaekob Chiriko 2021).

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Procedure

The sample size was calculated using Open Epi (Sullivan 2021) considering the following assumptions: 19% expected frequency of ECD delay (Workie et al. 2020), a confidence limit of 5%, 95% confidence interval, design effect of 1.5% and 15% for non‐response rate. Thus, the required sample size was calculated to be 407. The study population comprised randomly selected children aged 6–23 months and their respective mothers residing in the study area.

A multi‐stage sampling approach was employed involving: (1) random selection of one woreda and one city administration, (2) lottery‐based selection of five kebeles, smallest community of sampling (Gallo Ejersa, Jara Gallalcha, Jara Hinnessa, Sama Ejersa and Gebeya dar), (3) proportionate allocation of study subjects based on the total number of children aged 6–23 months residing in the selected kebeles and (4) random sampling of households using health extension registries (lists of children aged 6–23 months maintained by the local health extension workers), while excluding twins and children with medically confirmed developmental delays.

Data Collection Procedure

Face‐to‐face interviews were conducted with mothers in their compounds with minimal distraction. Data were collected on tablets equipped with Kobo Toolbox, an open‐source, online‐offline platform for collecting and managing survey data (Kobotoolbox 2024) by four experienced and well trained nutritionists paired with local Health Extension Workers (HEWs) from the kebeles. The data collectors gathered all objective data including socio‐economic characteristics, dietary diversity, RF practices, and ASQ‐3 questions. All questionnaires were initially prepared and adapted in English by the principal investigator (PI) and translated into the local language (Sidaamu Afoo).

Data Collection Tools and Measurements

Socio‐Demographic Questionnaires

Semi‐structured questionnaires were prepared based on reviewing the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EPHI and ICF 2021) and multiple indicator cluster surveys (MICS) (National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) and United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) 2022). Age of the child was determined after reviewing a birth certificate or immunization card or by recall of different socio‐political events in the past 2 years accompanied by probing of mothers to remember the age of the child.

Responsive Feeding (RF) Practices

The RF Practices Assessment Tool (RFPAT) was adapted from the version used by Pallewaththa and colleagues (Pallewaththa et al. 2021) after translating and contextualizing it into local language. The RFPAT consists of 15 RF items that integrate nutrition and responsive caregiving components within the nurturing care framework. These items assess key feeding practices related to feeding environment, feeding time, food preparation, and maternal feeding behaviors. The locally translated and adapted 15‐item RFPAT is provided as a supplemental table in this manuscript.

During the adaptation process, the RFPAT response options were reduced from five to three to make them clearer and easier for understanding by the study participants. Mothers were asked how often they practiced each specific RF practice, with response options of ‘rarely', ‘sometimes', or ‘always’. Finally, the results of the RF practices were summarized by analyzing the frequency of each response (Pallewaththa et al. 2021).

In addition to the 15 RF practice questions, we included two questions addressing paternal factors that may influence problem‐solving skills. These questions assessed whether fathers take time to feed the child or are involved in the purchase of food items needed for the child. The inclusion of these items was informed by our contextual observations, that paternal involvement in child feeding and stimulation is variable.

Dietary Diversity

Dietary data of the children were collected for the prior 24‐h. Mothers were asked to recall and freely list all foods and beverages consumed by their child in the previous 24 h before the survey, and data collectors subsequently categorized the items into eight standard food groups. These food groups were: breast milk, grains, legumes, dairy, flesh foods, eggs, vitamin‐A‐rich fruits and vegetables and other fruits and vegetables. Dietary diversity score was then calculated by summing the number of food groups consumed by each child based on the WHO and UNICEF guidelines for assessing IYCF practices (WHO and UNICEF 2021).

Ages & Stages Questionnaires, Third Edition (ASQ‐3)

The Ages and Stages Questionnaire‐3 (ASQ‐3) (Squires and Bricker 2009) was used to assess children's problem‐solving skills. The ASQ‐3 has demonstrated high sensitivity (0.82) and specificity (0.92) and has been validated and adapted for use in multiple countries, including Kenya, Zambia, Turkey, and Colombia (Lu et al. 2016; Rubio‐Codina et al. 2016).

The ASQ‐3 measures developmental skills (problem‐solving/cognition, language/communication, motor skills and personal social/social emotional skills) of children aged 1 month to five and a half years. For each age category, the ASQ‐3 contains six parent‐centered questions. The ASQ‐3 is scored as: “Yes” (10 points) if the child demonstrated the ability to do the specific activity described by the item, “Sometimes” (5) if the skill was emerging, or “Not yet” (0) if the child had not yet shown evidence of the ability to do that specific activity.

The problem‐solving domain score (0–60, age‐adjusted) reflects the child's ability, with higher scores indicating children on a developmental track. The ASQ‐3 cutoff scores for the child's specific age group were dichotomized and used to classify the problem‐solving developmental status. Children scoring above the cutoff were considered on track, while those scoring below the cutoff were identified as at risk of not achieving their developmental potential. These children below the cutoff are considered to need learning activities or monitoring and further assessment by professionals (Squires and Bricker 2009).

Quality Assurance

Prior to the actual study, the data collection tools were pretested with 40 mother‐child pairs (not part of the final sample) and refined based on the pretest feedback. A supervisor and the PI closely monitored the entire data collection process, with ongoing data checks conducted to maintain the accuracy and reliability of the results. Following daily data submission by the field team, the PI reviewed the data for quality and completeness.

Data Management and Analysis

SPSS version 20 was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, standard deviations, and prevalence were used to report the socioeconomic and RF characteristics and developmental status of the children. Bivariate analysis was performed and variables with p ≤ 0.15 were included in the logistic regression model. Normality was checked for continuous variables using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test. Model fitness for logistic regression was checked using the Hosmer and Lemeshow test. Multicollinearity between different explanatory variables was checked using the variable inflation factor (VIF) (Vatcheva and McCormick 2016). Finally, the association between the predictors and the outcome variable was determined using logistic regression, and p < 0.05 was used to define statistical significance.

Ethics Statement

The study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the College of Medicine and Health Sciences at Hawassa University (IRB/021/21). Support letters were obtained from Hawassa University, Sidama Regional State Health Bureau and local authorities. The study followed all essential requirements for working with human subjects. Informed written consent and assent was obtained from the mothers or caregivers. The research team protected confidentiality and anonymity of study participants' data throughout the entire study. All data were stored on a password‐protected computer.

Results

This section presents the main findings of the study beginning with a description of participant characteristics, followed by an overview of children's problem‐solving skills and RF practices. Associations between RF practices and the development of problem‐solving skills are examined. Data collected from 401 (98% response rate) mother–child dyads provided several insights about the surveyed population.

Children, Mothers and Household Characteristics

Three‐quarters of the study participants resided in rural areas. Slightly more than half (53.9%) of the study children were females. Education levels of the mothers varied, with a small proportion (7.5%) attaining college or higher education and 4.7% having no formal education.

Almost all mothers attended at least one antenatal care during their last pregnancy, and 80.5% received at least one prescription for iron/folic acid tablets. Child health interventions, such as Vitamin A supplementation in the past 6 months, reached 89% of children. However, gaps existed in child development monitoring: 95% of children had no assessments of their problem‐solving, motor or communication skills, and 56.6% lacked weight or growth measurements within the past 3 months.

Access to technology and information was mixed. While 77.1% of mothers owned a functional mobile phone, only 10.7% had ever used the internet. Dissemination of child development information was lacking, with 83.3% of mothers being unaware of developmental milestones. Problem‐Solving Skill Development

The median (IQR) problem‐solving skill score was 30 (IQR: 20–60), with 40.4% of the children identified as at risk of delayed problem‐solving development. Notably, the problem‐solving skills score demonstrated a significant correlation with both language skills (r = 0.69, p < 0.01) and composite motor skills development (r = 0.81, p < 0.01). Additionally, the composite motor skill score showed a significant correlation with language skills development (r = 0.78, p < 0.01).

Responsive Feeding Practices

Overall, most mothers did not consistently practice several RF behaviors. More than half (57.9%) did not always feed their child in a place designated only for feeding, and only one third always based the feeding times for their child on their experience of hunger cues. Likewise, only about 30% of the respondents did always offered foods that are appropriate for their child's age. More than half of the mothers did not report always engaging their child in conversation and making eye contact during feeding. In addition, only about one‐third of the respondents always allowed their child to practice self‐feeding or promoted feeding autonomy.

In addition to maternal responsive feeding practices, paternal involvement in provision of food items and child feeding was queried. Only a few fathers, 10.8% were consistently involved in feeding their child. Paternal participation was somewhat higher in food provision with nearly one‐third (31.2%) of fathers involved in purchasing food items for their child. Association Between Problem‐Solving Skills Development and Responsive Feeding

Bivariate analysis was conducted before the multivariate logistic regression between the outcome and explanatory variables. In addition to the 15 item RF practices, the bivariate analysis included the following explanatory variables; maternal age category, marital status, maternal occupation, maternal education, total family size, wealth index, child diet diversity, ownership of functional mobile phone, ever used internet, received IYCF education in 3 months before the survey, ANC attendance, child birth place, paternal involvement in provision of food items and in feeding the child.

Following the bivariate analysis, eligible explanatory variables were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. The model was finally adjusted and fitted with the 14 RF practices except item number nine from Table 2 and the following variables: maternal age, total family size, ownership of a functional mobile phone, ever used internet and IYCF education in past 3 months and paternal involvement in the provision of food items for their child.

Subsequently, the logistic regression analysis revealed that larger family size, feeding the child in a varied and stimulating environment, and promoting feeding autonomy were significantly associated with children's problem‐solving skills. Larger family size was significantly associated with higher odds of children being on track in problem‐solving skills (AOR = 1.2, p < 0.05).

Children of mothers who rarely fed their child in a particularly designated place (i.e. carried their child around while feeding) had 2.6 times higher odds of their child being on track in problem‐solving skill development compared to those who always feed their child in a place which is only designated for feeding (AOR = 2.6, p < 0.05).

On the other hand, children of mothers who rarely allow their child to practice self‐feeding or do not promote feeding autonomy had 70% lower odds (AOR = 0.3, p < 0.05) of being on track in problem‐solving skills compared to children of those mothers who always encouraged self‐feeding (Table 3).

Discussion

This section interprets the study's key findings in the context of their implication to ECD and existing evidence. Promoting ECD aligns with global priorities outlined in the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals and the African Union's Agenda 2063 (African Union 2025; UN 2023; UNICEF 2021). Consequently, the current study investigated the problem‐solving skill development of young children and its association with RF, one of the key components of the WHO nurturing care framework (WHO 2018).

Based on the ASQ‐3 criteria (Squires and Bricker 2009), approximately 40.4% of children in the current study were found to be at risk of not achieving their problem‐solving potential. These are children who may need learning activities, monitoring and further assessment by professionals (WHO 2018). The current finding aligns with Ethiopia's ECD policy framework statement, which states that more than half of children under five in the country face a heightened risk of poor developmental outcomes (Ministry of Health 2020).

In addition, the current prevalence of risk of delayed problem‐solving skill in the study area is comparable with global reports keep including the Lancet series, which estimated that 250 million children (~43%) under five in LMICs are at risk of failing to reaching their full development potential (Lu et al. 2016; USAID 2021; World Bank 2023). Multicounty studies in Africa also reported higher rates of developmental delays among young children, with problem‐solving delays reaching 90% as measured by the ECD Index in some regions of the continent (Bliznashka et al. 2021; Ewerling et al. 2020).

The findings of the current study further demonstrated significant and positive correlations between the development of problem‐solving abilities and improvements in both motor and language skills, underlining the interconnected nature of these developmental domains. Cognitive development, which refers to the growth and maturation of mental processes including problem solving, serves as the foundation for the acquisition of essential skills including language, social interaction, and motor coordination (Adolph and Hoch 2019).

Children from larger households had significantly higher odds of being on track in problem‐solving skills, indicating that perhaps having more family members around the household may support the development of early problem‐solving skills. Frequent interactions with more people in larger families may enhance problem‐solving through communication, interactive play, and improved dietary support (Callanan et al. 2020; Cicirelli 1976).

In resource‐limited settings, despite the concern of resource sharing in larger households, the presence of more family members may support RF practices by providing additional caregivers such as older siblings or extended family who might contribute to meal preparation, assist with child feeding, or offer emotional and social interaction during feeding which in turn may enhance ECD (Bentley et al. 2011; Pérez‐Escamilla et al. 2017).

While several studies report negative associations between larger family size and ECD domains due to increased economic strain (Guan et al. 2025; Islam and Khan 2022; Magtoto 2017; Symeonides et al. 2021), other evidence suggests that larger families may confer developmental advantages through enhanced social interaction and peer learning among siblings (Afiq and Zafar 2025; Marsh et al. 2019; Reynolds‐Salmon et al. 2024).

Children can benefit from RF environments, where their internal signals of hunger and satiety are recognized and met with prompt, emotionally supportive and developmentally appropriate responses (Liu and Chang 2023; McIsaac et al. 2022). “Young children's growth and development are shaped by both what they are fed and how they are fed”(Black et al. 2022).

In the current study, children whose mothers usually fed them in varied and stimulating settings were more likely to be on track for problem‐solving development than children who were always fed in a fixed, unstimulating location. In this context, feeding in varied environments refers to exposing children with stimulation and pleasurable mealtime experience. These findings suggest that some flexibility in feeding location, coupled with positive engagement, may be beneficial for children's problem‐solving skills in limited resource areas.

In the study area, more than half of the mothers did not always feed their children in a place designated feeding. In many rural Sidama households, infant and young children are commonly carried in their mother's arm while she walks outdoors. Thus child feeding commonly takes place in varied and non‐structured environments rather than designated feeding areas. These feeding contexts may expose children to richer sensory, social, and verbal stimulation, which becomes increasingly influential for cognitive development (McCormick et al. 2020; Ronfani et al. 2015; Yang et al. 2021). Children actively engaged with their surroundings during feeding through observation, exploration, and interaction have been linked with higher cognitive and problem‐solving abilities (Trapp and Ziegler 2019).

Feeding children in diverse feeding environments could provide more opportunities for exploration, observation, and stimulation, as well as potentially better intake, all of which may contribute to improved ECD outcomes. Given the limited home stimulation in the study setting, allowing children to explore their external environment may contribute to better dietary intake, which could, in turn, improve problem‐solving skills. Especially during early life, adequate nutritional intake has essential roles for proper brain development which in turn increases the child's ability to explore their environment and interact with their mothers (Prado and Dewey 2014; Pelto et al. 1999; Yousafzai et al. 2013).

Children of mothers who rarely allow their child to practice self‐feeding or who do not encourage feeding autonomy have a lower chance of developing problem‐solving skills compared to those children whose mothers strongly encourage feeding autonomy. Optimal IYCF depends not only on what is fed, but also on how, when, where, and by whom the child is fed (Pelto et al. 2003). WHO joined UNICEF to recommend active feeding which was subsequently broadened to RF with the recognition that controlling feeding behavior could have adverse effects on children's development (Birch 1998; Pelto et al. 2003).

Various studies and reports also supported the importance of feeding autonomy as one of the important RF practices to improve child nutritional status and thereby their ECD. WHO recommends that RF should involve encouraging self‐regulation and independent feeding (Engle et al. 2000; WHO 2003). Mothers' control over feeding can override the child's hunger and satiety cues, hindering feeding autonomy and ECD (Schalka et al. 2013). Parents may justify controlled feeding due to concerns about intake, appetite, food refusal or child size (Moore et al. 2006), or due to time constraints requiring efficiency. Misinterpreting food refusal as poor appetite rather than as a child seeking autonomy can also create stressful mealtimes, leading to frustration, weakened internal cue recognition, and reduced communication of needs (Black and Aboud 2011).

Feeding autonomy is grounded in the concept of responsive caregiving, a culturally adaptable approach that encourages children to eat independently while responding to their physiological and developmental cues, thereby fostering self‐regulation (Kowalski et al. 2023; Pérez‐Escamilla et al. 2021). Beyond feeding practices, encouraging autonomous eating and self‐regulation has been linked to broader cognitive, emotional, and social development in infants and young children (Zhang et al. 2023).

Additional evidence indicates that feeding autonomy positively influences cognitive development in young children. Responsive feeding practices that encourage children to eat independently in response to physiological cues support self‐regulation, which extends beyond eating to broader cognitive, emotional, and social development (Kowalski et al. 2023; Pérez‐Escamilla et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2023).

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

While existing Ethiopian research has largely emphasized factors contributing to undernutrition, this study contributes to the literature by assessing the relationship between problem‐solving skills, an ECD domain, and RF practices. This study adds to the limited body of literature on ECD in Ethiopia by shifting the focus from what children are fed to the equally important consideration of how they are fed. The study is further strengthened by the collection of primary, community‐based data from mother–child dyads. A key limitation of this cross‐sectional study is the lack of follow‐up data, precluding assessment of sustained associations between RF practices and ECD outcomes. The lack of urban‐rural comparison in ECD outcomes and RF practice could be another limitation of the study. The narrow confidence intervals (CIs) could also be considered as a limitation of the study. The significance of the odds ratio despite the CIs being close to one may reflect the large sample size of the study. Furthermore, the presence of ethnic diversity and various child care practices across Ethiopia may restrict the extrapolation of these results to other settings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the role of nurturing care, particularly RF, in optimizing one of the ECD domains, problem‐solving skills. The large number of children at risk for problem‐solving delays indicates the need for nurturing young children through RF and other integrated approaches to mitigate potential long‐term consequences.

A nurturing and interactive feeding environment might provide opportunities for exploration, critical thinking, and adaptive learning, all of which are essential for fostering ECD. The current study emphasized the association between RF practices and problem‐solving skills of young children in Ethiopia. Feeding autonomy and mealtimes characterized by varied and stimulating environments were significant predictors of improved problem‐solving skills of young children in resource‐limited settings. Additionally, a larger family size was significantly linked to better problem‐solving skills, highlighting the supportive role of family members in fostering early cognitive development.

Recommendations

Feeding autonomy and supportive feeding environments through the provision of developmentally appropriate stimulations, particularly in resource‐limited settings may represent important correlates of problem‐solving skills in young children. Encouraging age‐appropriate self‐feeding may contribute to the development of problem‐solving skills by promoting independence, enhancing hand‐eye coordination, and facilitating trial‐and‐error learning. In addition to talking and playing with the child during feeding, by varying the feeding environment either indoors or outdoors in their compound, mothers may engage their child in ways that can improve their intake and support their problem‐solving skills.

Considering the family structure and integrating family‐level characteristics, such as sibling engagement during feeding and caregiving also may offer culturally appropriate and cost‐effective strategies to enhance problem‐solving skills in young children. Further longitudinal or intervention‐based studies are needed to clarify the relations between RF practices and ECD domains.
