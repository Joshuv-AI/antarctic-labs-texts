Unusual presentation of transthyretin (ATTR) cardiac amyloidosis: A case report of pruritus as a possible initial symptom and challenging diagnosis

Published: 2023

ABSTRACT

BACKGROUND

Amyloidosis is a complex disorder often referred to as “the great imitator” due to its nonspecific clinical presentation. 1 The disease is characterized by the accumulation of misfolded proteins in various organs throughout the body. 2 While any organ in the body can be affected by amyloidosis, the heart is the most commonly affected organ (70%), followed by the kidney, liver, gastrointestinal system, and soft tissue, which are involved in 60%, 20%, 15%, and 10% of cases, respectively. 3 , 4 Cardiac amyloidosis (CA) can lead to heart failure, cardiac arrhythmias, sudden cardiac death, and deposition of proteins in coronary arteries. 5 The severity of cardiac dysfunction is the most crucial predictor regarding the patient's prognosis. 6 However, diagnosing CA is one of the most significant dilemmas among clinicians, as the cardiac manifestations of amyloidosis can also be seen in more prevalent heart failure etiologies, such as ischemic cardiomyopathy and aortic stenosis. 5 Although the accumulation of proteins in soft tissues, including the skin, is not usual, but can present a range of symptoms, including pruritus. This symptom is typically localized to a single site; however, in some cases, it can affect multiple areas. 7 In this report, we present a case of CA in which the patient initially presented with pruritus. Later, during the workup, the detection of ascites fluid raised a series of questions, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of CA. The case underscores the importance of considering amyloidosis in patients presenting with nonspecific symptoms, particularly those affecting the skin, and highlights the need for increased awareness of the disease among clinicians.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 70‐year‐old man presented with a mild generalized pruritus that had been ongoing for 1 month without any associated skin lesions. The symptom was not alleviated or exacerbated by daily activities such as showering or sun exposure. The patient denied experiencing any constitutional symptoms, including fever, weight loss, night sweating, gastrointestinal symptoms, jaundice, or urine or stool color alterations. The patient's vital signs were within normal limits during the physical examination and no skin lesions, thyromegaly, splenomegaly, liver disease‐related signs, or jaundice were observed. The patient had a history of hypertension for the past 20 years, which was being treated with a daily dose of 25 mg losartan and was well controlled. The patient's family history was negative for any significant diseases, including hematologic, renal, thyroid, cardiac, or skin diseases or malignancies. No other household members were experiencing pruritus at that time. The patient had no history of smoking, alcohol consumption, or drug abuse.

Initial screening tests were performed, including complete blood count (CBC), liver and thyroid function tests, serum creatinine and urea, stool examination, fasting blood sugar, ferritin, coagulation profile, alkaline phosphatase (AlkP), and gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT). Except for a reduction in mean corpuscular volume (MCV) (70 fL) with normal hemoglobin levels (13 mg/dL), and slightly elevated AlkP levels (403 U/L), all other tests were normal. An abdominopelvic sonography was then performed, revealing no pathologic findings. Subsequently, a magnetic‐resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was performed, revealing a mild dilation of the main pancreatic duct and common biliary duct, beginning at the level of the ampulla of Vater, with a normal intrahepatic biliary tree. Endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) was also performed, which did not reveal any pathologic findings.

Further blood tests, including antimitochondrial antibody (AMA), antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti‐smooth muscle antibody (ASMA), hepatitis B virus surface antigen (HBsAg), anti‐HCV Ab, and anti‐HIV Ab, were negative. A serum protein electrophoresis showed a reduction in albumin, along with elevated levels of β1, β2, and Ɣ, with a normal level of total protein and albumin to globulins ratio of 1.01. In order to rule out autoimmune hepatitis, a liver core needle biopsy was performed, which revealed chronic active hepatitis without significant fibrosis, with a histological activity index (HAI) of 6, which was not specific enough to provide an exact diagnosis.

Three months after the initial visit, an abdominopelvic CT scan with IV contrast showed ascites fluid in the abdomen and pelvis, along with an abnormal cardiothoracic ratio, dilated inferior vena cava (IVC), and hepatic veins. Another abdominal ultrasound was performed, showing significant systolic reflux flow into the hepatic vein, suggesting that congestive heart failure (CHF) caused by tricuspid valve (TV) regurgitation was the probable reason for the formation of the ascites fluid.

Based on the presented clinical findings, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was conducted and showed biatrial enlargement, severe left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy with a LV ejection fraction of 50%, mild right ventricular (RV) dilation with mild systolic dysfunction, and moderate to severe TV regurgitation. An electrocardiogram (ECG) also depicted a right bundle branch block with low voltage in limb leads, which suggested restrictive cardiomyopathy (Figure 1). To further evaluate the condition, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) was performed, revealing biatrial enlargement with atrial wall and interatrial septum (IAS) hypertrophy, LV hypertrophy with wall thickening, reduced LV global longitudinal strain (−8%), and mild RV hypertrophy with moderate systolic dysfunction and severe TV regurgitation. In the gadolinium study, a subendocardial to transmural enhancement (reverse myocardial nulling) was observed in the wall of all four chambers, strongly suggesting CA. In order to ascertain our hypothesis, an abdominal wall fat pad biopsy was obtained, and a Congo red staining was performed on the sample, showing apple‐green birefringence when viewed under polarized illumination (Figure 2). A technetium‐99 m pyrophosphatase (Tc99m‐PYP) study was conducted to investigate further, showing significant diffuse PYP activity throughout LV and RV myocardial regions, which was more prominent in the LV apex and septum, with a quantitative visual score of 3. The Tc99m‐PYP scan findings indicated a myocardial PYP uptake greater than rib uptake and a heart‐to‐contralateral lung PYP uptake ratio of 1.52, suggestive of transthyretin amyloidosis. Finally, an endoscopy was conducted, revealing no esophageal varices. Ultimately, given the subtype diagnosis, our intended course of treatment involved the administration of diuretics as the general management due to the heart failure symptoms, alongside tafamidis. However, the patient expressed reluctance to comply with the suggested treatment and did not consent for further therapeutic plans.

DISCUSSION

Amyloidosis is a pathologic condition characterized by the accumulation of misfolded proteins in various tissues. Various protein components with different shapes and functions can produce amyloid fibrils in amyloidosis. These protein aggregates known as amyloid fibrils, exhibit a distinctive β‐pleated sheet structure that stains positively with Congo red and displays green birefringence under polarized light. 8 Two significant amyloidosis subtypes are recognized, namely, light chain amyloidosis (AL) and transthyretin (ATTR) amyloidosis. While AL amyloidosis exhibits cardiac involvement in about 70% of cases, ATTR amyloidosis involves the heart in approximately 10% of cases. ATTR amyloidosis includes both hereditary forms (ATTRm) and acquired wild‐type ATTR (ATTRwt), also known as senile amyloidosis. 9 Amyloid fibril deposits can accumulate in various organs, with the heart being one of the most commonly affected. CA can significantly impair cardiac function due to the infiltration of amyloid fibrils into cardiac tissue and the direct toxicity of circulating light chains on myocardial cells. 10 The diagnosis of AL amyloidosis requires positive Congo red staining on tissue biopsy, whereas ATTR amyloidosis can be diagnosed without a biopsy. 11 Until recently, CA was considered a rare condition that could only be diagnosed by autopsy. However, significant advances have been made in diagnosing CA, revealing a higher prevalence of the disease than previously believed, with an incidence of 1.38–3.69 per 100,000 person‐years and a prevalence of 14.85 per 100,000 person‐years between 2004 and 2018. 12 , 13

Despite the recent advancements, diagnosing CA remains a challenge for clinicians due to the wide range of cardiac manifestations that can be observed in other cardiovascular diseases. Common cardiac symptoms of CA include exercise intolerance, fatigue, dyspnea, syncope, edema of the lower extremities, chest pain, and atrial fibrillation. 14 The prevalence of amyloidosis extracardiac events (AECEs) as an initial manifestation is higher than that of amyloidosis cardiac events (ACEs), with a frequency of 56% across all amyloidosis types. It has been observed that skin and mucosal manifestations are more frequently exhibited in the AL subtype, whereas they are rarely seen in the ATTRwt subtype. 15 Our current case, however, is intriguing as it presented with pruritus and was subsequently diagnosed with the ATTR subtype. The patient exhibited no significant skin lesions that could provide a definitive diagnosis. Therefore, we approached the patient's complaint of generalized pruritus and initial laboratory findings only revealed a mild rise in AlkP levels, which could occur in various situations, including infiltrative liver diseases, such as amyloidosis. Given the lack of clues, we continued to manage the patient's pruritus with periodic reevaluations to identify the underlying cause of pruritus. While systemic amyloidosis rarely involves the skin, it often presents with pruritus, petechiae, ecchymosis, and non‐healing ulcers. However, pruritus is not a typical dermatological manifestation of the ATTR subtype. 16 The unexpected presentation of pruritus, in this case, highlights the significance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for amyloidosis, particularly in cases that present with atypical features. The precise underlying mechanisms responsible for pruritus in the context of CA remain a subject of incomplete elucidation. However, it is postulated that the etiology is closely linked to various mechanisms such as: (a) the deposition of amyloid fibrils within neural and vascular structures, engendering nerve irritation and eliciting the release of pro‐inflammatory biomarkers, thus inciting pruritic sensations, (b) the potential for mast cell activation consequent to the presence of amyloid deposits, resulting in the discharge of histamine and various other mediatory compounds with pruritogenic properties, and (c) the propensity for amyloid deposits to sensitize nerve endings in the skin, rendering them more amenable to stimuli that might otherwise fail to provoke pruritic responses. 7 , 17 , 18 , 19 Within the framework of these proposed pathways, we hypothesized that the observed pruritus in our patient may have arisen either just from the activity of inflammatory mediators without producing skin lesions or from a pruritic phenomenon that lacks overtly discernible skin lesions. But, we still need more basic science evidences and observations.

Echocardiography is a common imaging modality used during the diagnostic evaluation of CA, given that most patients with CA develop CHF. However, echocardiography has a sensitivity of only 60% for detecting CA. 20 CMR imaging has a unique role in evaluating CA, with the late‐gadolinium enhancement (LGE) technique being the cornerstone of the diagnosis. 21 Obtaining ECG can also be helpful, with the most common finding being low‐voltage waves in limb leads. 22 Bone scintigraphy is a suitable technique for identifying transthyretin accumulation in tissues. This method has been widely acknowledged as an acceptable way of detecting CA while aiding in the differentiation between ATTR and AL. Regarding the management of CA, major advancements have been made in the last decade, making the condition more controllable than it used to be. The current approach to management involves supportive treatment for affected organs and tailored therapeutic regimens based on the subtype of amyloidosis. For AL amyloidosis, the standard treatment protocol includes chemotherapy utilizing bortezomib, cyclophosphamide, and dexamethasone, as noted by previous literature. 25 Until recently, organ transplantation was the only treatment option for the ATTR subtype. However, a recent study by Li et al. demonstrated that tafamidis exhibited significant efficacy in terms of all‐cause mortality in ATTR patients when compared to a placebo. 26

CONCLUSION

Herein, we presented a rare and compelling case of ATTR subtype CA with pruritus as the initial presentation. The diagnostic challenges of CA are well known to clinicians, owing to the multitude of clinical manifestations that can mimic other, more prevalent cardiac diseases. Early and accurate diagnosis, including identification of the specific amyloidosis subtype, is crucial for properly managing the disease, given the potential for serious complications such as CHF and cardiac arrhythmias, since these adverse outcomes can significantly impact patients' quality of life and survival. Thus, increasing clinicians' awareness of CA, mainly when presenting with atypical symptoms, is of great importance. Our case underscores the importance of considering CA as a differential diagnosis in patients with unusual clinical features and highlights the need for further research to improve the early recognition and management of this complex disease.
