A sensitive and specific non-invasive urine biomarker panel for prostate cancer detection
Authors: Menglang Yuan, Marcio Covas Moschovas, Kandarp Joshi, Yohei Sanada, Roshane A Perera, Bongyong Lee, Rudramani Pokhrel, Jiri Polivka, Alexandra Miller, Ernest K Amankwah, Ignacio Gonzalez-Gomez, Naren Nimmagadda, Ezra Baraban, Anant Jaiswal, Cuncong Zhong, Inoel Rivera, Guru P Sonpavde, Chetan Bettegowda, Vipul Patel, Christian P Pavlovich, Ranjan J Perera

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundProstate cancer (PCa) is one of the leading causes of cancer death in men. While prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing is widely used for screening, its diagnostic accuracy is limited, often failing to distinguish between benign and malignant prostate conditions, underscoring the need for novel biomarkers with improved diagnostic performance. This study aimed to identify and validate a panel of urinary RNA biomarkers with improved diagnostic accuracy for PCa.MethodsRNA-sequencing analysis of exfoliated cells in urine specimens identified 50 candidate RNAs. After initial qPCR testing in pooled urine, three biomarkers (TTC3, H4C5, EPCAM) with optimal specificity and sensitivity were selected as a biomarker panel. Diagnostic performance was evaluated in a case–control study divided into development (n = 243 participants) and validation (n = 646 participants) datasets. Biomarker expression was confirmed in tissue specimens, and the oncogenic function of TTC3 was assessed in vitro and in vivo.FindingsThe three-biomarker urine panel robustly identified PCa with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.94–0.98) compared to 0.83 (95% CI: 0.77–0.88) for urinary prostate cancer antigen 3 (PCA3) RNA in the development dataset and 0.92 (95% CI: 0.89–0.94) compared to 0.76 (95% CI: 0.72–0.80) for PCA3 in the validation dataset. Urine biomarkers were nearly eliminated post-prostatectomy and were confirmed to originate from prostate tissue at both the RNA and protein levels. The panel maintained high diagnostic accuracy of PSA-negative PCa cases and distinguished PCa from benign prostate conditions (BPH, prostatitis). Functional studies demonstrated that TTC3 depletion significantly suppressed in vitro and in vivo tumour growth.InterpretationThis urine-based biomarker panel offers a promising sensitive and specific noninvasive diagnostic for PCa with the potential to form the basis for laboratory-developed and in vitro diagnostic assays.FundingThis study was supported by the International Prostate Cancer Foundation, JHU SKCCC (grant number P30CA006973), and Bankhead-Coley Cancer Research Program (grant number 24B16) to R. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Prostate cancer is the most common malignancy in men, and about one in eight men will be diagnosed with prostate cancer in their lifetime. Prostate cancer detection has a long history, with PSA widely used for decades despite its notably poor diagnostic performance. New biomarkers, like PCA3 RNA (an FDA-approved test), have provided some support for clinical decision-making, but no currently available biomarker is flawless. The demand for reliable prostate cancer biomarkers, especially those that can be measured in urine without requiring prostate massage, remains a critical clinical need.

Added value of this study

We analysed the performance of a 3-marker panel (TTC3, H4C5, EPCAM) in a case–control study split into development and validation datasets. We demonstrated that the three-marker panel robustly identified prostate cancer compared to urinary PCA3 RNA with high sensitivity and specificity. Urine biomarkers were nearly eliminated post-prostatectomy and were confirmed to originate from prostate tissue. The panel maintained high diagnostic accuracy of PSA-negative cases and distinguished prostate cancer from benign prostate conditions (hyperplasia or prostatitis). Our findings pave the way for similar advances in molecular urology and have practical implications for the development of clinically noninvasive biomarkers for prostate cancer.

Implications of all the available evidence

We developed a robust, noninvasive urine liquid biopsy biomarker assay to detect prostate cancer. The biomarker panel can detect prostate cancer even when PSA is in the normal range and distinguish prostate cancer from benign prostate conditions (hyperplasia or prostatitis), which is crucial for improving early diagnosis, minimising unnecessary biopsies in benign cases, and reducing prostate cancer mortality. The RNA biomarkers may provide new insights into prostate cancer tumorigenesis and progression.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most common cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths in men, with over 250,000 PCa diagnoses annually in the United States alone.1 Available treatment options (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, androgen deprivation therapy) have improved the overall survival of men with PCa.2 Nevertheless, the incidence of advanced PCa increased by ∼41% in men aged ≥45 years between 2011 and 2018.3 Biomarkers are used to detect PCa in clinical practice, with prostate-specific antigen (PSA) the most commonly used serum biomarker for diagnosis and monitoring.4 However, while sensitive, it is not very specific, so prostate biopsies are needed to confirm the diagnosis, which in many cases are negative and can result in unintended complications. Further, sensitive detection can lead to overtreatment of generally indolent low-grade PCa. Despite the potential of PSA for PCa screening, the results from two prospective screening trials conducted in the US and Europe were conflicting, and at best the implications were that large numbers of individuals needed to be screened to prevent a PCa-related death.5,6 PCa screening rates decreased when the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) downgraded PSA screening as a recommendation in 2012, but uptake increased again after the panel upgraded its recommendations in 2018, reducing the subsequent incidence of metastatic PCa.7 Nevertheless, older men are less likely to benefit from PCa screening,8 and clinical decision-making requires additional reliable diagnostics for PCa detection and monitoring.9

An ideal biomarker for clinical use should 1) be non-invasive for safety and ease of collection; 2) be highly sensitive, specific, and have high positive and negative predictive values; and 3) improve clinical decision-making. Urine, as a non-invasive and easily accessible biofluid, is a potentially valuable substrate for biomarker testing, especially for PCa.10 Demonstrating this benefit, the US FDA approved a quantitative RNA expression assay for prostate cancer antigen 3 (PCA3, Progensa) in urine in 2012, which helps determine the need for repeat biopsy in men with a prior negative biopsy result. PCA3 is a PCa-specific biomarker expressed in urine following prostate massage in men with PCa,11,12 and, at a cut-point of 35, discriminates PCa with higher specificity (55.7%) while maintaining sensitivity (68%). However, the test produces many false negatives, with 32% of PCa and 26% of aggressive PCa missed at that cut-point.13 Several other urine biomarkers with improved specificity have been marketed, including SelectMDx (DLX1, HOXC6),14 ExoDx Prostate IntelliScore (EPI) (SPDEF, ERG, PCA3),15 and the Michigan Prostate Score (PCA3, PSA, TMPRSS2-ERG).16 The National Comprehensive Cancer Network Guidelines endorse these tests, but results are mixed.17

The major clinical symptoms of PCa overlap with those of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and prostatitis, making them difficult to distinguish by symptoms alone. Serum PSA is also elevated in many pathophysiological conditions of the urinary tract, contributing to its limited specificity. A high proportion of patients with elevated PSA do not develop PCa, and up to 15% of men 62 years and older with normal PSA are expected to have PCa,18 which makes it difficult to rely solely on PSA for clinical decision-making. While the Prostate Health Index (PHI) test combines serum total PSA and its derivatives [free PSA, [−2]pro-PSA (p2PSA)] to discriminate PCa from benign or insignificant disease,19 alpha-blockers and especially 5-alpha reductase inhibitors given for BPH affect levels of PSA and/or its derivatives,20 potentially limiting the value of the PHI in men on pharmacotherapy for BPH. Another secondary serum biomarker, the 4Kscore, combines total, free, and intact PSA and human kallikrein 2 (hK2) to discriminate men with no cancer or pathologically indolent disease from aggressive PCa.21 However, like PHI, its results can be affected by BPH pharmacotherapy and by DRE,22 and it is much more expensive than PSA or PHI serum testing. The need thus persists for novel PCa-specific biomarkers as alternatives to serum PSA-based biomarkers for accurate diagnosis, to reduce unnecessary biopsies, and for prognostication and monitoring of all men at risk.

We previously discovered differentially expressed mRNAs, lncRNAs, and metabolites in urine samples from patients with PCa and healthy individuals by deep sequencing and mass spectrometry and reported PCa-specific changes.23, 24, 25 Here, we extend our observations to identify a sensitive and specific PCa urine-enriched biomarker panel formed from three RNAs (TTC3, H4C5, EPCAM) that robustly identifies men with PCa. We also used ELISA to measure EpCAM protein as an additional readout in this study. Our analysis shows that this panel outperforms the urinary marker PCA3, distinguishing PCa from benign prostate disease regardless of PSA levels. Our assay offers a clinical advantage by eliminating the need for prostate massage or examination prior to sample collection, such that voided urine can be easily collected in a medical clinic.

Methods

Study design and participants

Clinical datasets

Supplementary Fig. S1 shows the study design and workflow conducted in five phases: (a) urine biomarker discovery, (b) assay development and validation, (c) tissue-based validation, (d) assay performance evaluation, and (e) functional studies.

All urine specimens were collected from participants attending AdventHealth Global Robotics Institute and Johns Hopkins All Children's Hospital, FL or Johns Hopkins Bayview Medical Center and Johns Hopkins Hospital, MD between 2022 and 2025. The study compared cases with controls across three datasets, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. S1: (i) assay development; urine specimens from 243 individuals split into healthy controls (n = 107) or cases (n = 136); (ii) assay validation; urine specimens from 646 individuals split into healthy controls (n = 162) or cases (n = 484); and (iii) performance evaluation; urine specimens from 229 individuals split into healthy controls (n = 72), BPH (n = 76), prostatitis (n = 7), or cases (n = 74). Clinicopathological and demographic data was collected from the clinical records or prostate biopsy histopathology reports, and preoperative PSA, biopsy, and Gleason scores (GS) were recorded.

All participants were prospectively enrolled and sequentially and chronologically allocated to either the development (first ∼25% enrolled) or validation (remaining cases) dataset at enrolment, which had the practical advantage of allowing assay development in the first phase of the study and validation in the second phase of the study, whilst ensuring independence of the datasets in a real-world diagnostic setting. Despite the median age and suspicious DRE percentage varied in the development and validation datasets, these differences reflect the nature of the development cohort, which included a higher proportion of younger individuals undergoing screening without suspected disease. To explore individual biomarker changes and confirm biomarker tissue of origin, a subset of cases (n = 98 in the development phase and n = 409 in the validation phase) also provided post-prostatectomy samples.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for PCa cases were patients with histologically-confirmed prostate cancer based on systematic and/or MRI-targeted prostate biopsy performed after urine collection. Inclusion criteria for healthy controls were all men without a prior diagnosis of PCa, who had a negative result on prostate biopsy following urine collection, and whose gland volume on MRI was under 50 cc with no evidence of PCa as determined by normal PSA and/or digital rectal examination or healthy individuals attending urology clinic for other (non-prostate-related) reasons with no major lower urinary tract symptoms. Inclusion criteria for BPH were men without a prior diagnosis of PCa, who had a negative result on prostate biopsy, and whose gland volume on MRI was 50 cc or greater. Individuals with a history of other solid tumours were excluded from the study.

Although no randomisation was applied, all specimens were collected based on uniform eligibility criteria and standardised protocols. All participants underwent biopsy based on comparable clinical criteria. Matching between cases and controls was not performed, as the primary objective was biomarker discovery and validation rather than risk factor analysis. The overall case-to-control ratio was 1.3:1 in the development phase and 3:1 in the validation phase.

Sample collection

Fifty millilitres of voided urine specimens were collected from the donors at first and midstream morning urination. The urine was immediately mixed with urine preservation solution (Norgen Biotek, Thorold, ON, Canada) and kept at room temperature for up to 14 days for further analysis. All urine specimens were centrifuged, exfoliated cells were used for total RNA extraction, and urine-free cells were subjected to ELISA.

Forty-five pairs of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples from PCa and adjacent normal tissue were used and, of these FFPE tissues, 26 pairs of tissues were used for RNA extraction, ten pairs of tissues were used for immunohistochemistry (HRP/DAB visualisation), and nine pairs of tissues were used for RNA fluorescence staining. For FFPE RNA extraction, twenty consecutive 18-μm sections were freshly cut from each tissue block on a HistoCore BIOCUT microtome (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), and RNA was extracted with the RNeasy FFPE kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). To assess the tissue specificity of our biomarker panel, we also evaluated tissue expression of the identified biomarkers in men with PCa or benign prostate disease using immunohistochemistry and a prostate disease spectrum tissue microarray (TMA, PR821) obtained from Tissue Array (Derwood, MD; 39 PCa, 22 BPH, 5 chronic prostatitis, and 14 normal).

Ethical approval

The Human Research Ethics Committee and IRB of the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine approved the study protocol and human sample collection (Reference No. 237998). All subjects provided written informed consent.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR of exfoliated cells

Total RNA was extracted from exfoliated cells in ∼50 ml urine using QIAzol lysis reagent and the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Eluted RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA and analysed by real-time qPCR. Briefly, total RNA was treated with DNase I (Qiagen) and then eluted with 30 μl of DNase/RNase-free water. During the biomarker discovery and development phase, 10 μl (∼100 ng) of total RNA was used for reverse transcription using the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The 50 μl pre-amplification reaction contained 25 μl of pre-amplification Master Mix, 24 μl of cDNA, and one μl of pooled primers (final primer concentration 10 nM). We then performed initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min and 12–14 cycles of cDNA pre-amplification: 95 °C for 15 s and 58 °C for 4 min. Gene expression was quantified using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). During the urine-based validation phase, 27.5 μl (∼275 ng) of total RNA was used for reverse transcription using the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems). qPCR reaction was mixed with fluorogenic probe-primer combinations specific for the target gene and TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). The TaqMan probe was labelled with the fluorescent reporter dye FAM at the 5′ end and with the fluorescent quencher dye MGB at the 3′ end. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed with QuantStudio 5 or 7 (Applied Biosystems). Each gene was tested in quadruplicate, and threshold cycle (Ct) values were averaged. Ct values of each target gene were normalised to the reference gene ACTB using the 2−ΔCT method. The reference gene ACTB was consistently detected, while non-detection of the target gene was rare. In cases where Ct values for specific target genes were undetectable by qPCR, the assay was repeated. If non-detection persisted upon repeat, the target was classified as negative. No other missing data were imputed. The primer sequences are listed in the Supplementary Table S1.

Immunohistochemistry

FFPE tissue blocks from the prostate disease spectrum tissue microarray or individual PCa and adjacent normal tissue specimens were cut as 5 μm sections. IHC staining was carried out using the rabbit-specific HRP/DAB (ABC) Detection IHC Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK; ab64261; RRID:AB_2810213). Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and endogenous peroxidase was blocked with hydrogen peroxide. After blocking with protein block and incubating with primary antibodies targeting EPCAM (ab223582, Abcam; 1/500 dilution; RRID:AB_2762366), TTC3 (ab80061, Abcam; 1/200 dilution; RRID:AB_1604032), or H4C5 (HPA 042201, Sigma–Aldrich, St Louis, MO; 1/100 dilution; RRID:AB_2677898) at 4 °C overnight, biotinylated goat anti-polyvalent, streptavidin peroxidase, and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution were sequentially applied onto the sections. After washing, sections were counterstained with Gill's haematoxylin III (Sigma–Aldrich) and mounted with EcoMount. Whole-slide images were obtained at 40 × magnification using the Aperio VERSA system (Leica Microsystems).

Positive IHC stains was defined as brown colour according to the manufacturer's demonstrative slides. Tissue sections were scored semi-quantitatively in five separate fields at × 400 magnification according to: (1) the number of positively stained cells (score 0, <5%; score 1, 6%–25%; score 2, 26%–50%; score 3, 51%–75%; and score 4, 76%–100%); and (2) scoring the staining intensity (score 0, absent; score 1, weak; score 2, moderate; score 3, strong). The final score was the product of (1) and (2) for individual slides. For simplicity, the final staining score was expressed as follows: negative staining as 0 for absent (−) and 1+ for weak (+); 2+ for moderate (++); and 3+ for strong (+++) staining.

Statistical analysis

The sample size of patients with PCa and controls was estimated using Power Analysis and Sample Size (PASS) Statistical Software v15. We used the 2−ΔCT values of each gene to generate a classification score for each subject. An algorithm combining the 2−ΔCT values of all genes discriminated the two classes of samples (i.e., PCa vs normal or non-cancerous control). Biomarker levels in urine samples from PCa cases and controls were compared using independent sample t-tests and between before and after prostatectomy or in PCa and adjacent normal tissue specimens using paired t-tests. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess diagnostic performance characteristics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), as well as respective areas under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The Youden index (J) was used to define the optimal cut-point (c). J = maxc (sensitivity(c) + specificity (c)-1) and is the point maximising the Youden function, which is the difference between the true positive rate and false positive rate over all possible cut-point values. The biomarkers diagnosed each specimen based on a predetermined cut-point in the classification score. The biomarker-determined diagnosis was compared with the actual pathological diagnosis. Spearman's correlation was used to calculate the correlation between the Gleason score and the percentage of Ki-67-positive cells. The performance of the biomarker panel was estimated and subjected to ROC analysis by multiple logistic regression.

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance (p-values) was calculated for independent samples (Mann–Whitney U-test) or paired samples (paired t-test) in GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) or SPSS 17.0 (IBM Statistics, Armonk, NJ). ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; NS, not significant.

Results

Discovery of urinary biomarkers for prostate cancer

Our previous study reported transcriptional profiles of urinary cells from patients with PCa and normal individuals.23 Here, we conducted further, in-depth transcript analysis using the urine biomarker discovery workflow shown in Fig. 1a. Based on differential gene expression (DGE) analysis, we initially identified 815 (418 upregulated, 397 downregulated) PCa-specific genes in urine from men with PCa compared with normal healthy urine. To confirm that the RNA originated from the prostate, we examined RNA expression in TCGA-PRAD database. Among 418 upregulated genes, 95% (397/418) were significantly upregulated compared to normal healthy prostate controls in the TCGA-PRAD dataset.

We prioritised the top 50 genes (adj. p-value <0.05 and logFC >2) that were highly upregulated in both primary and metastatic PCa and determined the expression levels of these 50 candidates in urinary exfoliated cells by qPCR analysis using pooled RNA derived from five PCa and five normal urine specimens (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Table S2). Nine genes with increased expression in PCa cell lines were further characterised in 20 independent PCa and 20 control urine specimens (Fig. 1c). Combining the properties of aberrant expression and diagnostic value (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Fig. S2), we selected three top-performing candidates: tetratricopeptide repeat domain 3 (TTC3), H4 clustered histone 5 (H4C5), and epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EPCAM) for further analysis. The TTC3 gene is located at 21q22.2, and its protein product functions as an E3 ubiquitin protein ligase, mediating the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of phosphorylated Akt in the nucleus. TTC3 plays a role in neuronal differentiation inhibition and asymmetric cell division in cancer cells.26,27 The H4C5 gene is located at 6p22.1 and is a core component of the nucleosome, where it plays a role in modulating chromatin structure. The EPCAM gene is located at 2p21, and its encoded protein is highly overexpressed in many human epithelial cancers.28 The EpCAM protein is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein initiated by regulated intramembrane proteolysis. It differentially regulates oncogenic signalling pathways, varying by cancer type.29 Together, these biomarker discovery findings support the original hypothesis that urine-enriched RNAs might offer diagnostic potential in PCa and present preliminary results supporting these candidate biomarkers for the robust identification of patients with PCa.

The controls in the development dataset [median (IQR) age, 35 (27–38); suspicious DRE, 2.8%] were younger with less suspicious DRE than participants in the validation dataset [median (IQR) age, 57 (33–63); suspicious DRE, 37%]. Before combining the identified biomarker candidates, we first investigated the expression of the three individual biomarkers in the development dataset. Expression levels of TTC3, H4C5, and EPCAM RNA were significantly higher in PCa than normal urine (Fig. 2a). Moreover, the expression of each biomarker diminished to low or undetectable levels in post-prostatectomy urine specimens compared with corresponding pre-prostatectomy samples (Fig. 2b). The non-coding RNA PCA3 and EpCAM protein were quantified as positive controls, since PCA3 and EpCAM have been previously shown to be overexpressed in PCa, and an ELISA was readily available for EpCAM quantification.30 As expected, urinary PCA3 expression in exfoliated cells and EpCAM protein levels in the supernatant were significantly higher in urine from men with PCa than normal and significantly decreased post-prostatectomy (Supplementary Fig. S3a and b). In these same patients with PCa who underwent blood testing, serum tPSA also significantly decreased post-prostatectomy (Supplementary Fig. S3c). Consistent with a previously published urine exosome assay,15 SPDEF transcript levels in exfoliated cells in urine from patients with PCa were substantially higher than normal controls and lower in post-prostatectomy than pre-prostatectomy urine specimens (Supplementary Fig. S3d). Subsequently, we performed nonparametric ROC analyses for PCa diagnosis for each biomarker alone and combined. Based on the logistic regression analysis, the combined panel of three biomarkers (AUC, 0.96; sensitivity, 94%; specificity, 86%) outperformed any single RNA biomarker (Table 2) and demonstrated good diagnostic performance in classifying PCa and normal (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table S3). A larger proportion of 131 patients with PCa were positive for the three-marker panel than were positive for PCA3 [110 (84.0%) vs 95 (72.5%)] (Fig. 2d), particularly in 14 patients with serum PSA-negative [11 (78.6%) vs 7 (50.0%)] (Fig. 2e), where PSA negativity was defined as a serum PSA level <4.0 ng/ml at the time of urine collection prior to diagnosis. These individuals were newly diagnosed based on prostate biopsy despite low PSA levels. Furthermore, the panel indicated a diagnosis of PCa regardless of risk classification (Fig. 2f).

Consistent with the results from the development study, the expression of TTC3, H4C5, and EPCAM RNA were significantly higher in pre-prostatectomy PCa than normal urine in the validation dataset (Fig. 2g) and were reduced considerably in post-prostatectomy PCa samples (Fig. 2h). ROC analysis confirmed the strong diagnostic performance of the three-biomarker panel, with an AUC of 0.92, sensitivity of 91%, and specificity of 84%, effectively distinguishing patients with PCa from healthy controls in the validation dataset (Fig. 2i, Table 2). A higher proportion of 484 patients tested positive for PCa with the three-marker panel than with PCA3 [429 (88.6%) vs 275 (56.8%)] (Fig. 2j), particularly among 77 patients with serum PSA-negative disease [66 (85.7%) vs 42 (54.5%)] (Fig. 2k). Additionally, the panel reliably identified PCa across all Gleason risk classifications (Fig. 2l). Together, these results independently validate the three-biomarker panel for the robust identification of patients with PCa.

Tissue-based validation of the three-biomarker panel

Having demonstrated that the three-biomarker panel accurately discriminates patients with PCa from healthy individuals, we next sought to determine the origin of the three biomarkers in prostatic tissue. We isolated RNA from 26 pairs of PCa and adjacent normal FFPE tissues and performed qPCR analysis. As in the urine analyses, most PCa tissue expressed higher levels of TTC3, H4C5, and EPCAM than matched adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 3a). In ROC analyses, the three-marker panel had an AUC of 0.973, sensitivity of 96%, and specificity of 92% for PCa, parallelling the positive control PCA3 (Fig. 3b).

Histopathological analysis of the tissue sections confirmed the abnormal glandular architecture, with disturbance of normal epithelial–stromal relationships, in PCa across different Gleason grades (Fig. 3c). In these patients with PCa, positive staining of tumour cells with AMACR (P504S, used clinically) indicated a diagnosis of PCa. P504S was diffusely expressed in the cytoplasm of malignant glands but not in any tissue from normal controls (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Expression of the proliferation marker Ki-67 increased with the Gleason score and was rarely seen in normal tissue (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. S4b and c). Representative immunofluorescence staining of the three biomarkers is presented in Fig. 3d. PCa expressed higher levels of TTC3, H4C5, and EPCAM than adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 3e). Collectively, this tissue-based validation highlights that the origin of these urinary three-biomarkers is malignant prostatic epithelium, demonstrating their equally robust performance in PCa tissues.

Enhanced specificity of the biomarker panel for prostate cancer

BPH, prostatitis, and PCa can all increase circulating PSA but have very different clinical implications. No current biomarker accurately distinguishes BPH from PCa. To assess the specificity of our biomarker panel for PCa, we collected urine specimens from patients with benign prostate disease or healthy individuals (Supplementary Table S4). Urinary levels of the three biomarkers varied between BPH, prostatitis, and control and were higher in patients with PCa than in benign disease and control groups (Supplementary Fig. S5a). The median values of the positive marker PCA3 (RNA) and EpCAM (protein) were higher in patients with PCa than in healthy controls, as expected (p < 0.001). They were also increased in patients with BPH (Supplementary Fig. S5b and c). Furthermore, the diagnostic performance of the three-marker panel was higher than that of PCA3 (AUC, 0.89 vs 0.68) when applied to urine specimens from patients with PCa compared with those with BPH, prostatitis, or no prostatic disease (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table S5). The biomarker panel demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance in distinguishing patients with PCa from those with BPH (AUC, 0.87 vs 0.63) compared with PCA3 (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Table S5).

To corroborate the reliable diagnostic performance of PCa in urine, we quantified biomarker expression in tissue samples from patients with PCa and benign controls using immunohistochemistry (Supplementary Table S6). Almost all of the 56 benign disease cases were negative (IRS = 0 or 1+) for biomarker expression (Fig. 4c): 50 (89.3%) cases were negative for EpCAM; 45 (80.4%) were negative for TTC3; and 54 (96.4%) were negative for H4C5. In 48 patients with PCa, the majority of cases were positive (IRS = 2+ or 3+) for biomarker expression, although the proportion of positive cells and staining intensity varied (Fig. 4c). Tumour EpCAM staining was moderate in 21 (43.8%) cases and strong in 20 (41.7%) cases, with strong expression mainly confined to neoplastic epithelial cells (membrane and cytoplasmic staining). Tumour TTC3 staining was moderate in 26 (54.2%) cases and strong in 12 (25.0%) cases, and strong TTC3 expression (primarily cytoplasmic and/or nuclear) was seen in neoplastic glands and stroma. Tumour H4C5 staining was moderate in 15 (31.3%) cases and strong in four (8.3%) cases, and strong H4C5 expression (primarily cytoplasmic and/or nuclear) was again seen in neoplastic glands and stroma (Fig. 4c and d, Supplementary Fig. S5d).

When patients with PCa were divided into low- and high-grade disease, there was a positive correlation between the biomarker panel and Gleason score (r = 0.332, p = 0.021; Supplementary Fig. S5e). Furthermore, the three-biomarker panel had greater AUC, sensitivity, and specificity values in tissue samples from patients with PCa compared with non-cancerous or BPH-only controls (Fig. 4e and f, Supplementary Table S7). All metrics increased when the three biomarkers were combined, highlighting the cancer-specificity of the biomarker panel for the accurate detection of patients with prostate cancer (PCa).

Stability of TTC3, H4C5, and EPCAM RNA

Small changes in mRNA sequence and structure have been shown to alter mRNA stability, subsequently influencing the mRNA decay rate and abundance.31,32 Thus, we attempted to identify ultra-stable local structural elements in our three biomarkers to investigate the stability of TTC3, H4C5, and EPCAM RNAs. By in silico analysis, we identified twelve ultra-stable (p < 0.01) local structures in TTC3, one in H4C5, and two in EPCAM mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S6a). Notably, EPCAM and H4C5 mRNAs contain 400 and 240 bp ultra-stable local structures at their 5′ ends, respectively. It has been shown that the degradation of some mRNAs begins from the 5′ and that formation of a stable 5′ structure protects the mRNA and slows degradation.33 For TTC3, which is much longer than H4C5 and EPCAM mRNAs, most of its ultra-stable local structures were found at its 3′ end (Supplementary Fig. S6b), agreeing with previous findings that the folding or stem-loop structures at 3′ ends tend to enhance mRNA stability and have longer half-lives.34,35 By treating PCa cells with actinomycin D to inhibit transcription, we found that TTC3, H4C5, and EPCAM have high stability (Supplementary Fig. S6c). Remarkably, TTC3 RNA demonstrated a longer half-life than the other two RNAs. This stability is a desirable property for a reliable biomarker that must be measured in labile biological samples exposed to variable environmental conditions.

In vitro and in vivo functional characterisation of TTC3 in prostate cancer cells

To investigate whether the biomarkers might be functional in PCa, we first determined the biological roles of TTC3 as an exemplar in PCa cell lines. By qPCR analysis, we found that TTC3 expression levels in PC3 and LNCaP PCa cells were higher than in normal human prostate epithelial cells (Fig. 5a). We next used two individual siRNAs targeting TTC3 in PCa cells and found that both efficiently inhibited TTC3 expression compared with controls (Fig. 5b). MTS assays revealed that TTC3 knockdown significantly inhibited PC3 and LNCaP cell viability (Fig. 5c). Given that TTC3 siRNA#2 suppressed PCa proliferation more than siRNA#1, we applied this siRNA in further experiments. Transwell assays demonstrated that silencing TTC3 markedly decreased the invasion of PCa cells (Fig. 5d).

To further test whether TTC3 knockdown suppressed tumorigenicity in mice, we injected PC3 control or TTC3-knockdown (shRNA) cells subcutaneously to establish xenografts. In vivo analysis showed that silencing TTC3 in PCa cells caused dramatic reductions in tumour volume in nude mice (Fig. 5e). After euthanizing the mice, cells expressing TTC3 shRNA produced smaller tumours than controls, halving the tumour weight (Fig. 5f). Furthermore, lower TTC3 expression was associated with reduced in vivo proliferation as measured by Ki-67 expression and increased apoptosis by TUNEL (Fig. 5g and h). Together, these results suggest that TTC3 can significantly promote PCa cell growth in vitro and in vivo.

Downstream molecular alterations mediated by TTC3

To identify targets regulated by TTC3, we performed global gene expression profiling analysis in TTC3-silenced PCa cells. After silencing TTC3, 1133 genes were significantly downregulated two-fold (Fig. 6a). Using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA),36 we detected significant enrichment of various cancer-related pathways in the genes regulated by TTC3 (p < 0.05, Fig. 6b). KEGG pathway-related GSEA plots with high enrichment scores are presented in Supplementary Fig. S7a. Through an integrated analysis of RNA-seq data and two independent cancer gene databases, we identified 211 genes involved in tumour progression, including 157 candidate cancer drivers, 23 oncogenes, 17 tumour suppressors, 10 cancer genes, and four genes classified as NA (Fig. 6c). Of these, 18 genes were enriched in multiple pathways and have been previously reported to be associated with PCa progression (Fig. 6d). Using qPCR analysis, we validated the downregulation of these target genes and observed significantly decreased transcript levels of MYCL, PDGFB, PTX3, MUC1, BIRC3, TGFA, TRPM2, AR, and EGR1 in TTC3-depleted PCa cells (Fig. 6e). Collectively, these findings suggest that TTC3 enhances the tumorigenic potential of PCa cells.

Discussion

Molecular profiling has proven effective in personalising PCa management, enabling risk stratification and predicting treatment responses.37 Noninvasive tracking of molecular biomarkers, such as through liquid biopsies using urine, holds promise for improving PCa diagnosis and outcomes.37,38 Here, we present a non-invasive, highly sensitive, and specific biomarker panel capable of accurately diagnosing prostate cancer (PCa), even when PSA levels are normal, and distinguishing patients with PCa from those with other benign prostate conditions. Our biomarker panel and discovery approach offer several advantages. First, the biomarker panel is based on urine specimens, which is superior to blood for several reasons: it is specific for urinary tract conditions, provides a stable matrix for analysis, and allows for repeatable and noninvasive samples for frequent monitoring. Second, our panel identifies patients with PCa even when their PSA is normal, outperforming other clinically available assays and reducing the likelihood of false negatives. Third, the panel appears to discriminate well between benign and malignant prostate disease, which is encouraging for reducing false negatives if applied for screening. Fourth, we confirm that the biomarkers in the panel are expressed in malignant prostate epithelium, as demonstrated through both tissue analysis and the measurement of biomarkers pre- and post-prostatectomy, with at least one of these being oncogenic. Fifth, in silico analysis suggests that the biomarkers are likely to be stable, which is a prerequisite for successful clinical implementation in the real world, where sample processing is often difficult to control in practice. These characteristics suggest that the panel might be useful both for early detection and monitoring of established disease, and the target population now needs to be established through further prospective studies with associated clinical follow-up.

Analysis of urine and tissue specimens revealed high expression of TTC3, H4C5, and EPCAM in PCa compared with normal and benign controls. While TTC3 and H4C5 are not currently classified as prognostic genes in PCa, our findings demonstrate their levels sharply decrease after tumour resection, suggesting potential as biomarkers for recurrence monitoring when combined with EPCAM. This finding requires confirmation in larger studies and additional experimental modelling to extend our conclusions. Furthermore, although we observed that the mRNA expression levels and stability of TTC3, H4C5, and EPCAM varied drastically across cell lines, the reasons for this remain unclear. Future studies should investigate potential transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms in these cell lines that may explain these observations.

While the ExoDx urine-based assay has shown potential for predicting high-risk PCa, its specificity was low (34%) and it was tested on patients already suspected of having PCa.15 In contrast, our panel utilised urine specimens from healthy controls and patients with PCa, achieving a sensitivity of 94% and a specificity of 86% in the development dataset and a sensitivity of 91% and a specificity of 84% in the independent validation dataset, respectively. Similarly, our urinary panel may prove useful and superior to PCA3 (AUC 0.89 vs 0.68) for discriminating patients with cancer from those with benign prostate disease. Furthermore, our panel robustly identified patients with GS ≤7 and PSA <4 ng/ml. These findings validate the potential of the panel as a noninvasive test for accurate PCa diagnosis. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recently provided recommendations on PCa risk assessment,39 and we propose that our panel would be useful in surveillance protocols to ultimately validate its performance.

Our urine-based biomarker panel presents a promising alternative to PSA and existing assays for the accurate detection of PCa. While the detection of RNA biomarkers in urine is challenging due to the limited number of exfoliated cells and low RNA yields, the three-marker panel overcame these limitations and exhibited strong diagnostic performance. The panel demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity for improving the early diagnosis and management of PCa. This RNA biomarker panel demonstrates strong diagnostic performance in pre-biopsy urine samples and may serve as the foundation for laboratory-developed and future in vitro diagnostic assays. While its utility for clinical decision-making was not assessed here, its accuracy suggests potential value in guiding future studies aimed at improving patient selection and addressing limitations of PSA-based screening, especially in diagnostically ambiguous cases. Although the development and validation datasets differed in age and proportion of suspicious DREs, our urine-based biomarkers demonstrated consistent performance across both datasets, suggesting robustness to variation in these factors and potential generalisability to a broader patient population. Ongoing large prospective validation and direct comparison with other assays are warranted to establish the clinical applicability of this method.
