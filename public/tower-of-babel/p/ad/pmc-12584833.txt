Perspectives on the impact of regulatory measures on national COVID-19 vaccination programs: a qualitative study from six EU member states
Authors: E.D. van Vliet, C. Buhl, R. Jacobsen, A. Andersen, A.B. Almarsdóttir, F. Dermiki-Gkana, C. Oikonomou, E. Deligianni, C. Kontogiorgis, M. Kos, N. Čebron Lipovec, I. Ribeiro-Vaz, A. M. Silva, P. Ferreira, E. Poplavska, S. Abtahi, I. Hegger, T. Leonardo Alves
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe European Medicines Agency (EMA) issued regulatory recommendations and communications in 2021 about the risk of thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS) associated with COVID-19 adenoviral vector vaccines Vaxzevria and Jcovden. Little is known about how such measures impacted on national vaccination strategies and how they influenced decision-making processes of policy-makers and experts. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of regulatory actions for Vaxzevria and Jcovden on national vaccination strategies following the EMA's 2021 review on safety measures on TTS.MethodsA grey literature review was performed to gather information on EMA's actions as well as changes to national vaccination strategies in Denmark, Greece, Latvia, the Netherlands, Portugal and Slovenia. Semi-structured interviews were held with experts who had either advisory or decision roles in national vaccination programmes to contextualise the results of the grey literature review and discuss their experiences. Interviews were coded and analysed on national levels.ResultsEMA made various adaptations to product information and released safety warnings and other regulatory communications for both vaccines relating to TTS risk. Countries varied widely in changes made to national vaccination policies. Interviews revealed that experts relied mainly on their professional networks and information sources, other than the EMA, to inform their recommendations and/or decision-making.ConclusionEMA's regulatory actions were not the main source informing adaptations to national vaccination strategies. National decision-makers country reverted to their preferred sources. The use of varying sources explains some of the variations in the vaccination strategies.

Background

The adenoviral vector vaccines Vaxzevria (AstraZeneca) and Jcovden (Janssen-Cilag) were developed to immunise the population against SARS-CoV-2. Vaxzevria (formerly known as COVID-19 Vaccine AstraZeneca) obtained conditional marketing authorisation in January 2021 and Jcovden (formerly known as COVID-19 Vaccine Janssen) in March 2021 by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) (European Medicines Agency, 2023a, 2023b). Shortly after the introduction of these vaccines, reports of thrombotic events were received and later a new adverse drug reaction (ADR) was identified: thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS) (Greinacher et al., 2021; Pavord et al., 2021; Pottegård et al., 2021; See et al., 2021). In response to the initial reports, the EMA assessed safety signals and published recommendations about TTS risk and clinical care, updated the product information and released Direct Healthcare Professional Communications (DHPC) throughout 2021 using their pre-established methods for monitoring vaccine safety (European Medicines Agency, 2021n, 2021p, 2021q, 2021r, 2021s, 2021t, 2021u). Member States’ authorities also reacted to these developments and national vaccination strategies were altered as new information on TTS risk emerged over time (Durand et al., 2023; European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, 2021). Yet, there is little understanding of how decisions were taken during this period (Schippers & Rus, 2021). Therefore, TTS provides an important case to study the use of European regulatory actions and risk communications in the national vaccination policy decision-making process during a health crisis. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of regulatory actions for Vaxzevria and Jcovden on national vaccination strategies following EMA's 2021 safety measures.

Methods

The study applied a mixed methods approach using a grey regulatory literature review and semi-structured interviews which are described in the sections below. This study is part of a larger study and its full protocol and summary can be found in the HMA-EMA Catalogue of Real-World Data (Buhl et al., 2024; HMA-EMA Catalogue of Real-World Data, 2022; van Vliet et al., 2024). The study was performed in six European Union (EU) member states: Denmark, Greece, Latvia, The Netherlands, Portugal and Slovenia. These countries have different geographical locations in Europe and varying healthcare systems.

Literature review

To reconstruct the events that took place in 2021, a grey literature review was conducted. It aimed to gather all relevant data on Vaxzevria and Jcovden related to TTS as well as changes to vaccination strategies that were published in 2021. Each national research team gathered information from their government institutions and their repositories. Information from the EMA was collected from its website. This included DHPCs, safety updates, changes to the product information, meeting notes from the Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee (PRAC), published news items and other recommendations on TTS risk. A standard format was devised to prepare national overviews and timelines, to map and assess developments in national COVID-19 vaccination policies and any changes ensuing from the communications about the TTS risk. All relevant data were gathered into the template and analysed to reconstruct events and visual timelines.

Interviews

To provide context to the events, semi-structured interviews were conducted among experts and decision-makers in each country. Interviewees were identified using purposive sampling and snowballing. All interviewees were involved either in advising or guiding decisions which related to the use of either vaccine. To structure and harmonise the interviews, an interview guide was developed (see Supplemental Material 1). The interview guide was pilot tested in the Netherlands. Findings from the pilot testing were incorporated into the guide. Once a general version of the final guide was agreed upon, national teams adapted it to their settings and translated into their native languages according to a translation protocol (see Supplemental Material 2). First, baseline information was obtained about interviewees’ background and role and workplace during 2021. The interview covered four themes relating to the information received and/or acted upon during the pandemic: (1) the content of the risk information, (2) the channel through which it was received, (3) the source and (4) the format of the risk information. Lastly, participants were invited to reflect on the overall decision-making process. Each interview was recorded, and the audio recordings were transcribed verbatim. National teams coded and analysed the interviews using a predefined code set based on topics from the interview guide. The code set was created and discussed with all researchers to reflect national differences and settings. Results were shared anonymously and summarised in English using an established format largely based on the interview guide.

Ethical considerations

In each country, the study plan was presented to the local ethical board for approval. Approvals were waived in Denmark, Latvia and the Netherlands. In Greece, Portugal and Slovenia, permission was granted. All included interviewees provided informed consent for their participation.

Results

Regulatory review – EMA procedures and actions in 2021

Vaxzevria

The EMA granted Vaxzevria (International Non-proprietary Name (INN): COVID-19 Vaccine (ChAdOx1-S [recombinant])) conditional market authorisation on the 29th of January 2021. In total, the EMA released three DHPCs, seven safety updates and made four changes to the Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) and package leaflet (PL) for Vaxzevria. In total, there were 14 communication outputs about the associated TTS risk. The three DHPCs were released between March and June 2021. These discussed the risk of thrombocytopenia and coagulation disorders, the link between Vaxzevria and thrombosis in combination with thrombocytopenia, and provided updated information about the risk of TTS (European Medicines Agency, 2021n, 2021q, 2021r). Seven safety updates were issued between March and September 2021. The first safety update stated that ‘Vaxzevria could possibly be associated with very rare cases of specific embolic and thrombotic events in combination with low levels of blood platelets (thrombocytopenia)’ (European Medicines Agency, 2021m). The second update confirmed the link between Vaxzevria and TTS (European Medicines Agency, 2021i). The following updates included recommendations on diagnostics and follow-up vaccinations (European Medicines Agency, 2021h, 2021l). The fifth update stated that the PRAC continued to monitor TTS and clinical care recommendations for suspected TTS (European Medicines Agency, 2021k). In the sixth update in July, latest reports of TTS in EudraVigilance were mentioned (European Medicines Agency, 2021j). The final update in September highlighted changes to the product information and recent study outcomes (European Medicines Agency, 2021g).

On four occasions, changes were made to the SmPC and PL for Vaxzevria. The first two changes consisted of a warning and recommendations on embolic and thrombotic events followed by an update of this information. The third change added sections about the frequency of TTS as an ADR, and previous occurrence of TTS became a contraindication. The final update changed some sections of the product information including new research outcomes from studies (European Medicines Agency, 2021u).

Jcovden

Jcovden (INN: COVID-19 vaccine (Ad26.COV2-S [recombinant])) received conditional market authorisation on the 11th of March 2021. The EMA released two DHPCs, six safety updates and altered the SmPC and PL at three times. These added up to 11 communication outputs about the associated TTS risk. The first DHPC announced the link between Jcovden and TTS (European Medicines Agency, 2021p). The second DHPC alerted healthcare professionals on the signs and symptoms of TTS (European Medicines Agency, 2021o). The first safety update reported that the PRAC had started an assessment of reports on TTS after vaccinations with Jcovden, but they could not yet establish causality (European Medicines Agency, 2021c). Roughly a week later the second safety update identified TTS as a very rare side effect (European Medicines Agency, 2021f). The next update gave recommendations on diagnostics (European Medicines Agency, 2021b). The fourth update confirmed the monitoring of the PRAC of TTS cases and highlighted the updates made to the product information (European Medicines Agency, 2021e). In July, the fifth update confirmed the suspected TTS cases from EudraVigilance (European Medicines Agency, 2021d). The final safety update stated the conclusion of the PRAC that the product information was to be updated and that the statement on TTS occurring mainly in women was to be removed due to new scientific evidence (European Medicines Agency, 2021a). The first change to the SmPC and PL was the addition of TTS as an ADR. The second change consisted of a recommendation on diagnostics. The final change related to the usage of Jcovden as a booster vaccine, which was then contraindicated if a patient had had TTS after an initial Jcovden vaccination. This was a preventive measure as there were no cases reported of TTS associated with a Jcovden booster by then (European Medicines Agency, 2021t).

National vaccination policies

The national vaccination programmes were analysed per country and visual timelines were developed (see Supplemental Material 3). Overall, the usage of Vaxzevria started in February 2021 across all included countries. When the first reports of thrombotic events appeared in March, Denmark, Latvia, the Netherlands, Portugal and Slovenia paused their Vaxzevria vaccinations, but not Greece. With the exception of Denmark, all countries resumed vaccination albeit often adapting it to different target groups and/or under stricter conditions. Denmark continued the pause of Vaxzevria and later suspended its use. It became available upon request for a few more months and was later permanently discontinued. Later on, the Netherlands paused the usage of Vaxzevria again, making it only available upon request and finally discontinued its use. In Greece, Latvia, Portugal and Slovenia, Vaxzevria remained available albeit with mandatory informed consent, with a warning about its risks or only for very specific target groups.

In April 2021, the vaccinations with Jcovden started across the countries, except in Denmark. In Denmark, Jcovden was withheld, then became available only upon request and its use was discontinued in August 2021. In Greece, the Netherlands, Portugal and Slovenia, Jcovden was often subjected to restrictions or made available only upon request. Latvia made no changes to the administration of Jcovden.

Reflections on decision-making processes

In total, 21 people were interviewed who were either advising or involved in the decision-making processes of national vaccination programmes. Nine participants in Denmark, one in Greece, three in Latvia, five in the Netherlands, one in Portugal and two in Slovenia. Reflections obtained during the interviews are discussed below, per country.

Denmark

Danish experts recalled receiving their information via email from national authorities. They considered the information from these institutions to be timely and often preceding the information from the EMA. Other channels referred to were public press conferences and press releases in the mainstream media. Scientific information was often obtained via informal peer contact.

Danish experts referred to national authorities as their source of information. They also mentioned informal contacts with these authorities.

Content informing decision-making included EMA documentation such as PRAC assessment reports, safety updates and EMA's Lines-To-Take (LTT) shared with the European Medicines Regulatory Network. A study with data from the Danish and Norwegian health registries was also used.

As to the format, EMA information was often perceived as intricate, complex and lengthy, difficult to grasp and hard to translate.

When invited to reflect about this period, experts stated that a study based on the Danish and Norwegian health registries had played a key role in the decision-making. Having quick access to such information and being able to conduct pharmacovigilance studies on these registries proved to be pivotal for the entire vaccination strategy. However, some experts felt that there were also some political influences in choices made, such as that of making available discontinued vaccines upon request, even against expert recommendations.

Greece

In Greece, the interviewed expert referred to international organisations as the main information channel. These included the EMA, the Unites States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) and the World Health Organisation (WHO). The information was proactively sought online. Sources were mainly scientific literature, international organisations and vaccine manufacturers. The content of EMA documentation was considered to be adequate and timely. The format was well-structured and readable. Overall, decision-making in Greece followed the information flow once available, with a greater role for epidemiological and safety data, as well as choices being made in other countries. Decision-makers did notice the effect of mainstream media on the public, which then also affected the decision-making processes. For instance, if decision-making was delayed, the public's trust decreased. In general, such influence was mostly noticeable for Vaxzevria and barely for Jcovden.

Latvia

For Latvian experts, the main channels were the EMA, the national Drug Regulatory Agency (DRA) and informal, international peer networks. They considered the information to be timely. In terms of the content, documentation from the EMA was perceived to be thoroughly researched and therefore reliable, however, some criticism was noted as to the use of general phrasing such as ‘benefit-risk balance is positive’. The format was considered acceptable and useful. The most impactful information for decision-making relating to the vaccination campaign were (inter)national informal exchanges between peers. Decisions had to be made quickly and Latvia at that time had both a relatively small stock of mRNA vaccines and a large stock of Vaxzevria, making decisions on TTS risks difficult. The negative impact of the emergence of TTS was strongly felt for Vaxzevria, but barely for Jcovden which proved to be very popular in Latvia.

Netherlands

Dutch decision-makers considered the flow of information overwhelming and recalled receiving information from all directions. The main channels were (inter)national peer networks, and the national drug regulatory authority (DRA) which acted as a linking pin between the EMA and the Dutch government. The main source of information was informal networks. (Inter)national experts were contacted directly to obtain the latest information. Experts at the DRA considered the EMA to be timely and could not see how the EMA and/or PRAC could have operated more swiftly. Experts outside the DRA did consider the EMA to be slow. The immediate political and public pressure prompted quick decisions. These experts felt the need to act and could not wait for official outputs from the EMA. Nonetheless, EMA information was still considered to be very important and often used as a rationale for decisions, providing authoritative weight. Experts were critical of the format used in EMA documents and stated the need for more visual representations. Documentation from the EMA consists of large bodies of text making it difficult to quickly assess the main outcomes. Output styles such as an infographic would have been appreciated. There was a strong need for epidemiological data to make choices for the vaccination strategy, but some experts reflected that the provision of such information is not the task of the EMA. There was also a call for more openness about the rationale for choices. General terminology such as ‘based on scientific results’ was not considered adequate. Experts wanted more openness on uncertainties. As to the information impact, experts relied heavily on their (inter)national network. They felt pressured to respond as quickly as possible to each development. At times they were surprised by sudden outputs from the EMA and would have preferred to have been informed in advance. There was a need for some preparation time between the updates of new information and public dissemination.

Portugal

The Portuguese expert received information mainly via meetings where representatives of the EMA and/or national DRA were present. Other sources used were official publications from the EMA, national DRA, WHO and scientific literature. The EMA's LTT were considered to be very suitable. Information was first presented in meetings where the news about TTS was provided orally. Overall, the information from the EMA was considered to be clear, reliable and adequate. The expert mentioned that public opinion and vaccine hesitancy were important factors when making decisions.

Slovenia

For Slovenian experts, (inter)national media was cited as the first channel of TTS risk information. This resulted in proactive searches for more information online, at regulatory authorities websites and via peers. These channels were considered adequate, but information was not always timely. The main sources referred to were national authorities, authorities from neighbouring countries and (inter)national peer networks. The format of EMA information was considered lengthy and bureaucratic, difficult to find and navigate. Live videoconferences by the EMA were viewed more positively. The content of EMA information was very relevant for decision-making but often contained general statements. More detailed (background) information was considered necessary, therefore additional information sources were sought. Altogether, many information sources were vital to the whole decision-making process. Not just EMA documentation, but also case reports, and the FDA and other European DRAs provided useful information. It was also mentioned that decisions were influenced by political and public pressure.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of regulatory actions on national vaccination programmes, using the risk of TTS associated with adenoviral vector COVID-19 vaccines as a case study. The grey literature review of (inter)national regulatory actions showcased diverse timelines and responses from countries, which were then compared to the output from the EMA. The EMA publishes its information in the same manner, which is applicable to all member states. Yet, as can be observed in the timelines, countries reacted to it differently. As to Vaxzevria, all included countries made TTS-related adaptations to their national vaccination programmes even before EMA published its first safety update on TTS. Over the course of 2021 countries adopted very different approaches in their vaccination programmes. Some, such as Denmark, applied very strict requirements to the use of these vaccines (such as discontinuation or having it available only upon request) whereas others, such as Greece, adjusted target groups and made little or no changes. Overall, the reactions from Member States seemed more aligned with EMAs output for Jcovden than for Vaxzevria.

The interviews were conducted to explore the rationale behind these responses. The results showed that experts and decision-makers in national vaccination programmes were influenced by multiple and varying sources of information. Most countries reported the use of informal (social) and peer networks to stay up to date. Information from the EMA was considered reliable and authoritative, but not always meeting their needs. The public and political pressure prompted fast decisions. This resulted in experts seeking additional (applicable) information from their networks, other organisations (such as DRAs, the ECDC, the WHO and the FDA), and evidence from nationwide healthcare registries. The variation observed in the vaccination programmes can therefore be partially explained by the diversity in information input, i.e. what source of information was accessed, considered relevant and/or suitable to needs at a given time.

The rationale behind the observed variability across vaccination strategies is multifactorial. Besides the various information sources used, other aspects most likely played an influential role such as decisions taken by other Member States, COVID-19 vaccine availability (mRNA or adenoviral vector vaccines), COVID-19 infection and hospitalisation rates, as well as national operational and logistical aspects (Cavaleri et al., 2021).

Improvements could be made to the risk information to better meet target audience needs. During a pandemic, speed of reaction is essential. Experts and decision-makers felt pressured to make decisions quickly and needed information. This is somewhat reflected in their search for other sources and in the changes to their programmes which are not aligned with EMA's risk information timing. The EMA made remarkable efforts to accelerate safety review procedures with the TTS signalling (such as shortening processes into days which would normally take months) (Caplanusi et al., 2024; Durand et al., 2023). However, this was a learn-by-doing process. That might also partially explain the differences in Member States’ responses to Vaxzevria risk information when compared to Jcovden. Another suggestion for improvement would be to increase transparency about the rationale underlying certain choices. Experts and decision-makers referred to the use of vague or general text with insufficient information. Having access to the data underpinning certain recommendations can aid decision-makers in determining what is relevant for their (national) situation. Given that not all involved experts had a regulatory background, EMA documentation was considered lengthy, difficult to interpret and in need of more visual aids. EMA has user-tested their COVID-19 (risk) information and also concluded that such testing to be necessary and that the use of visual-supported information was beneficial (Castro et al., 2023). When timing is key, as during a pandemic, target groups rapidly seek information to match their needs. If the retrieved risk information and recommendations from the EMA do not prove to be timely, transparent or sufficiently applicable, experts and decision-makers will turn to other sources on which they will base their decisions.

The complexity of risk communication and ADRs is not a new phenomenon, but the COVID-19 pandemic did emphasise the need for a structured approach to decision-making surrounding ADRs. Frameworks and (new) data sources can aid decision-makers in managing diverging opinions and needs from stakeholders during a crisis (Chen et al., 2020; Hammad & Davies, 2025).

Limitations

The main limitation of our study is recall bias, which might have affected the interviews. These were implemented once the vaccination campaign was near completion and decisions thereof had already been made. It might be difficult for respondents to remember either the accurate timing of events, the temporal order, or changes to decisions about vaccination policies. We also faced difficulties in recruiting participants who were willing and able to disclose information on the decision-making process, as many were bound by confidentiality agreements or felt under scrutiny and/or review. This resulted in a small sample size of interviewees and therefore limits their representativeness and our results’ generalisability.

Some decision-makers and experts who were interviewed in an official capacity demanded access beforehand to the script, to obtain clearance. While this might have led to less spontaneous responses, we do not expect that it would have affected the content of the feedback being provided.

A very complex vaccination strategy made data collection and interpretation particularly difficult for Greece. The lack of harmonised sources of information on the strategy, the timelines and the subsequent changes made the presentation of this data into visuals very intricate. Moreover, additional aspects might have also impacted national vaccination policies, such as political decisions and negative media coverage. The latter was the case in Slovenia, where two deaths after vaccination with Jcovden were heavily reported and strongly shaped the public and professional opinion on adenoviral vector vaccines. This manuscript focused on TTS, which is associated with adenoviral vector vaccines. As other COVID-19 vaccines were also available in stock in some countries that might also have influenced certain choices within the vaccination strategy.

It remains challenging to disentangle and ascertain how regulatory actions affected national vaccine strategies, as these were probably multifactorial and influenced by other relevant aspects such as politics, public sentiment and availability of other vaccines.

Conclusion

Documentation from the EMA was not the first information source for Member States’ adaptations to their national vaccination programmes for adenoviral vector COVID-19 vaccines. The included countries showed wide variations in their vaccination strategies. Time pressure prompted decision-makers and experts to seek other accessible information sources that better met their needs, namely professional networks and other (inter)national agencies. The use of alternative sources of information might explain some of the variation observed in vaccination strategies.
