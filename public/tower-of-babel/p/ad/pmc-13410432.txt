Integrated Visible Light and Oxidant Protection Through Multifunctional Sunscreens
Authors: Beatriz Peñín, Raúl Losantos, Ignacio Funes‐Ardoiz, Diego Sampedro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh‐energy visible radiation (HEVR) induces adverse effects on the skin, yet a limited number of commercial compounds have been approved for use in humans. As most conventional filters are ineffective in this region, there is an urgent need to develop new and more effective components for sunscreen formulations. Here, we have designed and prepared a new family of HEVR photoprotectors with enhanced properties. These compounds feature tunable absorption, solubility, and improved photostability in solution and in formulation. Furthermore, they also show a significant antioxidant capacity, a complementary and desired property for cosmetic products, as it prevents long‐term damage. Finally, sunscreen preparations, including the best‐performing compounds, were benchmarked against standard formulations and commercial products, resulting in better photoprotection when compared to the state‐of‐the‐art HEVR sunscreens.Keywords: antioxidant, high‐energy visible radiation, photoprotection, sunscreen, UV

Introduction

Exposure to sun radiation has beneficial biological effects on human health, such as the synthesis of vitamin D. However, it also has damaging outcomes, especially concerning the skin. Since the 1930s, when the U.S. Public Health Service began to warn about the correlation between the appearance of melanoma and premature skin aging with long exposure to ultraviolet (UV) radiation, scientists have started to develop different technologies to protect human skin [1, 2]. Despite the number of awareness campaigns informing of the risks of long sun exposure, melanoma skin cancer incidence is increasing yearly, causing ca. 60k deaths from detected 330k new cases in 2022 [3, 4]. Currently, sunscreens are the most effective method to filter the UVA (315–400 nm) and UVB (280–315 nm) radiation [5], with formulations containing organic (chemical) or inorganic (physical) photoprotectors, and their effectiveness is quantified through the Solar Protection Factor (SPF) (Figure 1A) [6, 7, 8]. Inorganic filters are less frequently used in Europe, not only because their particle size must be large enough to prevent permeation of the skin barrier, but also because they have been demonstrated to have negative effects in marine organisms [9]. On the contrary, organic photoprotectors are the main component in many EU and FDA‐approved sunscreens, although they are also subject to safety concerns, acting as endocrine disruptors or toxins in corals [10, 11, 12]. Both chemical and physical filters may accomplish some basic requirements to be used in sunscreen formulations, including strong UV absorbance (chemical) or reflectance (physical), high photostability under sun exposure, lack of emission, and chemical stability (Figure 1A). Sunscreens are one of the main marketed products in the cosmetic industry. To illustrate, a widely used UV‐A filter, avobenzone, reached a market size that stood at USD 350 million in 2024, with a production of hundreds of tons per year, as well as octinoxate (UVB filter) with tens of tons of production per year [10, 13, 14]. However, the use of octocrylene for sunscreen formulations has decreased (USD 300 million in 2024; 1.5K tons of production/year) due to concerns about its potential to harm marine life, particularly coral reefs [15] (Figure 1B).

Furthermore, sun radiation is capable of generating free radical species like reactive oxygen species (ROS) [16]. Therefore, cosmetic products typically include compounds with antioxidant properties to prevent sunlight damage through the scavenging of light‐generated free radicals [8, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. For example, vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is widely used in cosmetic products because of its high antioxidant capacity, which operates via an electron donation process that can neutralize oxidative stress [22]. Thus, cosmetic formulations typically add to their composition both photoprotective and antioxidant compounds. This implies an increase in cost and complexity due to potential interactions between components when formulating these products for general use. Ideally, a multipurpose ingredient featuring solar light absorption and antioxidant capacity could have a clear impact on the design and preparation of new commercial products. This would minimize the required amount of sun filters, reducing environmental and health issues and lowering costs.

Photoprotection has typically focused on the UV region of the solar spectrum because of its higher energy and short‐term effects (Figure 1B). However, recent studies have demonstrated that high‐energy visible radiation (HEVR, above 380nm) has detrimental effects on the human skin: high‐intensity blue light penetrates more deeply into the skin than UVA‐B rays do, generating free radicals (ROS), causing skin aging, hyperpigmentation (melasma), oxidative stress, immunomodulation, and DNA damage [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Sunlight, electronic devices, and indoor lighting (LED) [28, 29, 30] are the main blue light sources to which humans are exposed. In this sense, potential damage from this type of light could be relevant, considering the modern lifestyle. Nowadays, most of the HEVR sunscreens are based on inorganic filters such as TiO2, ZnO, or Fe2O3 [31], despite their potential safety risks as they reach the bloodstream and can accumulate in important organs, causing toxic effects [32]. Surprisingly, there are very few approved organic sunscreens for commercial use [33] that protect human skin from HEVR: in the EU, the only approved compounds are bis‐ (diethylaminohydroxybenzoyl benzoyl) piperazine (BDBP) [34], methoxy‐propylamino cyclohexenylidene ethoxyethylcyanoacetate (MCE) [35], and phenylene bis‐diphenyltriazine (TriAsorB) [36]. While in the USA, the FDA has not approved any blue light protection sunscreen to date [37]. Thus, it is obviously relevant to expand the available library of compounds covering this region of the solar spectrum by preparing new HEVR filters to cope with the potentially damaging effects of sunlight beyond UV [38, 39, 40] (Figure 1B).

Aminocyclohexenimines emerged in recent years as potential candidates for sun protection, as they have structural similarities to mycosporine‐lime amino acids, natural UV filters [41, 42, 43]. The synthesis and photoprotection mechanism have been the major research developments in the recent studies, both computationally [44, 45, 46, 47] and experimentally [48, 49, 50, 51]. They fulfill all the requirements to be employed as photoprotectors: strong UV absorption, high (photo)stability, lack of emission, and non‐toxicity [52]. However, until now, these outstanding properties were limited to UVA/B protecting agents, leaving the relevant HEVR uncovered. (Figure 1C) Herein, we have designed a new generation of compounds in which we have tuned the properties to shift the absorption to the HEVR region. At the same time, these compounds have been prepared to feature antioxidant activity and compatibility with standard cosmetic formulations, which provides a dual mode of action and might allow for simplifying the final sun cream [52].

First, we envisioned a new set of compounds aiming for a red shift in the UV–vis spectrum via chemical modification of the chromophore through substitution of the pendant aryl group on the general structure (Figure 1D, blue circles) while keeping similar optical properties and photoprotection mechanism [41]. The set of designed compounds was tested through Time‐Dependent Density Functional Theory (TD‐DFT) methods (see Supporting Information for further details) using their absorption properties as a benchmark [53, 54, 55, 56, 57]. We included electron‐withdrawing substituents in the aromatic ring, such as nitro‐, cyano‐, fluoro‐, and trifluoromethyl groups, obtaining promising results. A donor substituent like an amino group was also considered, with a successful red shift of the absorption wavelength. (Figure 1D) It is important to note that only symmetric substitution in both N‐sites was considered to increase the synthetic and practical feasibility of the process, aiming for commercial applications. We also tested the substitution of the phenyl ring for heterocycles, where a bathochromic shift toward the red was observed for the maximum absorbance (see Figures S2 and S3, and Tables S1 and S2).

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of Photoprotectors

We then aimed for the synthesis of the most promising compounds following a simple and scalable synthetic procedure [41]. p‐toluensulfonic acid catalyzed the double condensation of 1,3‐cyclohexanedione with different amines (Figure 2A), using a Dean‐Stark to remove water along the condensation reaction. We efficiently synthesized and isolated the new photoprotectors 1–8 with moderate to high yields (26‐85%) on a multigram scale (≈2g). In the case of amines with para substitution, both electron‐withdrawing substituents, such as ‐NO2, ‐F, and ‐CN (1, 2, 8), and electron‐donating groups, such as the dimethylamino substituent (5), resulted in good yields. The incorporation of heterocycles, like pyridine (6), is also possible. Also, electron‐withdrawing substituents at the ortho position (3, 7) reduced the yield to 26 and 30%, while a substituent at the meta position (4) provided a good yield (66%).

Once the sun filters were synthesized, we delved into the study of their photoprotective properties. First, we experimentally measured the UV–vis spectra to evaluate the potential use of the resulting compounds as photoprotectors in the relevant region. All of them showed high absorption values, with molar absorption coefficients (ε) in the range of 19 988–14 836 (L·mol−1·cm−1), and specific extinction coefficient of 1% solution (E1,1) (industry standard that normalizes the molar absorption by the molar mass and refers to the absorbance of a solution containing 1 g of a substance in 100 mL of solvent) between 421 and 265, which are values in the range of commercial compounds in the same region of the spectrum. The new filters extensively covered the UVA and UVB regions, and some of them exhibited very relevant absorption in the visible region of the spectrum, as intended by design (Figure 2B). Most of the synthesized compounds show a tail band absorbing well beyond 400 nm. Especially relevant are 1, 5, and 6, where their λmax are located at 384, 374, and 387 nm, respectively, covering most of the HEVR radiation.

The next step focused on the evaluation of photostability, which is one of the most important requirements for any photoprotector. To do so, we prepared solutions in methanol of the different photoprotectors followed by exposure for 1h in a solar light simulator with a typical outcome of 1 SUN (1kW/m2) (see Supporting Information for further details), monitoring the degradation profile by UV–vis spectroscopy. As a representative example, the photostability test for 1 is shown in Figure 3A (see Figures S5–S13 for additional tests). After 60 min of irradiation, 1 photodegraded only 12%, whereas 5 showed 13% and 6 10% photodegradation (Figure 2C). Also, the other 5 photoprotectors resulted in high photostability (1%–12% degradation) for all the filters except 7 (34%). Solar spectrum ranges from 250 nm up to 1000 nm, but the irradiation intensity varies at the different wavelengths (Figure S1). While UV radiation is the most energetic sunlight, it is also partially filtered by the atmosphere, and only a portion reaches the Earth's surface. For the sake of comparison, we measured a widely used UVA commercial photoprotector, such as avobenzone (AVO), with a maximum absorbance centered on 357 nm (Figure S13). Sunlight intensity is much higher at 384 nm (absorption maximum of 1) than at 357 nm (absorption maximum of AVO). Even so, the new family of compounds far exceeds the photostability of avobenzone (99%–87% recovery vs 81% AVO recovery), except for 7 with a 66%. Moreover, long‐term effects of irradiation were tested, and 1 and AVO were irradiated for 24h with the solar simulator (SS). Photoprotector 1 yielded a 21% recovery after 24 h, but avobenzone achieved 10% recovery after only 6 h, even considering its different absorption. These results confirmed the extremely high photostability of the compounds even if their absorption has been significantly red‐shifted. Looking into potential degradation pathways, we compared the photostability in the absence of oxygen for compound 1 and avobenzone for 1h of SS exposure (see Figures S14 and S15). Avobenzone presented higher stability (96% recovery) compared to the photostability under realistic conditions, while 1 obtained a similar degradation (80% recovery), suggesting a non‐oxidative degradation pathway. The potential hydrolysis of the HEVR filters was also evaluated. For this purpose, 1 was dissolved in D2O:MeOD, and the decomposition was monitored by NMR. Both at room temperature and upon heating at 50°C, only minimal hydrolysis (15%) was observed (see Supporting Information for further details). These experiments account for the significant stability of these compounds.

Antioxidant Capacity

The addition of different active species in formulations can influence several properties, including stability and SPF, and the quantities of these compounds are strictly regulated [58]. So, the incorporation of multifunctional compounds onto sunscreen formulations can reduce the complexity and potential interaction between components and reduce costs at the same time [59]. For example, the use of photoprotectors with good antioxidant properties can be an option to avoid the addition of vitamin C or other antioxidants to the formulation. Thus, we tested the antioxidant capacity of the new compounds using a standard reported protocol [60, 61]. The DPPH assay consists of evaluating the scavenging activity of the 2,2‐diphenyl‐1‐picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical by the different test compounds. To do this, we prepared different samples, including the HEVR screening lead candidates 1, 5, and 6, a reference antioxidant such as vitamin C (ascorbic acid), commercial photoprotectors such as avobenzone and octinoxate, and finally a control sample with methanol (see Supporting Information).

In Figure 2D, the measured antioxidant capacity is represented from the resulting UV–vis spectra (complete UV–vis spectra can be found in Figures S26–S31). It can be observed that 1, 5, and 6 yield results from 21% to 73% of DPPH, while commercial sunscreens such as avobenzone or octinoxate only captured 1% radicals. Variations in the new compounds are thus significant.

To explain this difference, we hypothesized that photoprotectors with an oxidable nitrogen atom (5 and 6) obtain moderate to high values of antioxidant capacity, while for 1, whose nitrogen substituent is already oxidized (nitro group), the antioxidant capacity is considerably lower (Figure 2D). In these tests, vitamin C, a typical antioxidant, under the same conditions resulted in 100% scavenging activity as expected. Interestingly, 5 and 6 can be considered as good antioxidants, and this property adds to the excellent results found as sunlight filters, having a dual function inside a specific formulation.

Photoprotective Properties in Sunscreen Formulations

Next, we aimed to test the new compounds as potential sunscreens in cosmetic formulations. To do so, we measured the transmittance of a series of seven different oil‐in‐water (o/w) formulations, which were prepared following the simplest industrial standard [62], including different active species to cover the whole UVA+UVB radiation. However, there are strict regulations concerning the maximum concentration of each photoprotector allowed, which is usually around 5%–10% w/w per photoprotector, depending on their toxicity levels [10]. Thus, we incorporated the lowest amount for each of the novel filters tested: 5% w/w, together with avobenzone (AVO) and octinoxate (Oct) as state‐of‐the‐art UV light photoprotectors, two of the most common UV‐A/B filters integrated in industrial sunscreens (Figure 3A).

We first aimed to identify the general behavior of the HEVR‐synthesized filters in cosmetic formulations. Thus, F2, F3, and F4 contain only the newly developed HEVR filters and are compared with a UVA+B sunscreen as the reference formulation (F1), treated as a control sample. Next, we generated formulations F5, F6, and F7 as combinations of the new synthesized filters together with standard UVA/B filters, aiming for the widest possible spectrum coverage with the combination of novel filters and commercial photoprotectors in a more realistic sun cream. All formulations were emulsified using 20% NeoPCL (commercial mixture of emulgents and oily additives to create the base of the cream) and 80% water with 2 glycerol drops and measured following the industrial standard [62]. Finally, F8 [63] and F9 [64] are commercial sun creams specifically designed to provide HEVR protection and used directly as benchmarks (see Supporting Information for F8 and F9 detailed composition).

Analyzing these results, the three single photoprotectors in F2, F3, and F4 formulations can filter the blue light and part of the UVA range (300–400 nm), showing a 28%–46% transmittance (Figure 3B). As expected, this moderate photoprotection along the UV–vis spectra covers an extensive region of the solar spectrum, including UVB, UVA, and blue light. However, long‐range protection requires the combination with stronger absorbers in the UB region, where the light is more damaging, in order to achieve a high solar photoprotection factor (F5‐‐F7). In the F5 spectrum, the interaction between filter 1 and the avobenzone and octinoxate causes a minor decrease in the HEVR filtration. Comparing F2 and F5, the presence of UV filters reduces the blue light absorption from a 46% transmittance to 62% at 420 nm. However, the F5 blue light protection is still higher than F9 in the whole blue light visible range (400–500nm), and also higher than F8 in the 410–470 nm range, notably improving the absorption profile.

This effect is also clear in F6, where the interaction between commercial compounds and 5 implies a minor influence on the HEVR absorption, with only a 10% transmittance variation. Again, F6 has a stronger blue light absorption compared to the commercial F8 and F9, enhancing the HEVR protection. In this case, visible light absorption is considerably higher than both commercial products in the complete visible light sun‐spectra (until 500nm).

Finally, in F7, the mixture of 6 with avobenzone and octinoxate attained the lowest % transmittance variation compared to F4. An 8% transmittance decrease was observed. In the same way, F7 showed stronger photoprotection in the visible range compared to F8 and F9. The best blue light coverage was observed in F7 in the 400 to 430 nm range. On the other hand, a more sustained absorption along the visible spectra with high photoprotection levels was observed for F6. Overall, the newly synthesized compounds exhibit excellent performance, even surpassing the current industrial standards for blue‐light photoprotection.

As the prepared formulations proved to be more effective in protecting the HEVR range, we aimed for a deeper understanding of the factors controlling this process. The composition of the commercial creams (F8 and F9) does not precisely detail the amount of each photoprotector they contain. Therefore, we performed a direct comparison between our results and the recently published data by Bacqueville et al., who developed the phenylene bis‐diphenyltriazine (TriAsorB) HEVR photoprotector [36]. In that study, the transmittance of a sunscreen formulation containing 5% w/w TriAsorB was measured in a comparable formulation to F2‐F4. After the corresponding data processing, they stated that TriAsorB exhibited an absorbance of 0.3 units at 400. Therefore, at the same wavelength, our novel sunscreens displayed a 17%, 53%, and 70% higher absorbance for formulations F2, F3, and F4, respectively (see Figure S19). Overall, these three newly synthesized HEVR photoprotectors are capable of reducing the amount of HEVR that reaches human skin compared to one of the most prominent HEVR filters currently available on the market.

The photostability of the new sunscreen formulations was also evaluated (Figure 3C). F1 and F4‐‐F9 formulations were irradiated under an SS source for ∼1.5h, and the photodegradation was monitored by absolute irradiance measurements (Figures S20–S25). The photodegradation of reference F1 was also measured to evaluate the photodegradation of the UV filters avobenzone and octinoxate in the absence of our newly synthesized blue light photoprotectors. In this manner, the benefits or potential drawbacks of combining our filters with commercial ones can be tested. Avobenzone and octinoxate presented high degradation in all sunscreen formulations (isolated and in combination with HEVR photoprotectors). Nevertheless, some slight stabilization was observed when the UV filters were combined with the synthesized filters. To illustrate, a 26% photostabilization of avobenzone was observed in F6 (AVO+Oct+5). Moreover, HEVR photoprotectors exhibited a strong photostability (6%–15% photodegradation) after 1.5h SS exposure. Hence, the incorporation of the newly synthesized photoprotective molecules in formulations not only allows for HEVR protection but also contributes to enhancing the photostability of other components, suggesting an increased and extended protection against solar radiation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have designed and synthesized a new family of sunscreens that show outstanding properties for HEVR wavelength photoprotection. These compounds feature excellent photostability, good antioxidant capacity, and remarkable blue‐light photoprotection. These properties are comparable and even outperform state‐of‐the‐art chemistry in the market. These new filters effectively meet the primary requirements of photoprotectors: strong absorption in the relevant part of the solar spectrum, high photostability, and can be formulated together with commercial UV filters, plus notable antioxidant properties. We anticipate that these compounds will complement the limited number of HEVR organic filters that are, at this moment, approved for use by the European Union or the FDA, as they showed better photochemical properties and photoprotection capability.
