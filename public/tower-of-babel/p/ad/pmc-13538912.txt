Causal effects of multiple sclerosis therapies in left-truncated registry data
Authors: Dagmawi Chilot Haile, Ibrahima Diouf, Serkan Ozakbas, Dana Horakova, Eva Kubala Havrdova, Francesco Patti, Sara Eichau, Raed Alroughani, Alessandra Lugaresi, Valentina Tomassini, Alexandre Prat, Marc Girard, Murat Terzi, Bassem Yamout, Samia J Khoury, Pierre Grammond, Yolanda Blanco, Vahid Shaygannejad, Matteo Foschi, Andrea Surcinelli, Stefano Neri, Bianca Weinstock-Guttman, Julie Prevost, Maria Pia Amato, Michael Barnett, Oliver Gerlach, Nevin John, Allan G Kermode, Marzena Fabis-Pedrini, William M Carroll, Anneke van der Walt, Helmut Butzkueven, Vincent van Pesch, Aysun Soysal, Riadh Gouider, Saloua Mrabet, Daniele Spitaleri, Elisabetta Cartechini, Davide Maimone, Radek Ampapa

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Left-truncation is an unrecorded interval between multiple sclerosis (MS) onset and initial data in observational studies. This delay may bias estimates of disease-modifying therapy (DMT) effectiveness, especially when determined by patient or disease characteristics.Objectives:To examine whether causal effect estimates of DMTs over the full disease course can be reliably derived from left-truncated registry data.Methods:We analysed data from MSBase (144 centres, 41 countries) to assess the impact of left-truncation on causal treatment effect estimates. Cox marginal structural models (MSMs) estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for relapses, disability worsening and improvement, considering left-truncation at random and not-at-random. Fixed-time truncation and multivariable adjustment were applied to remediate bias.Results:The study included 5588 patients tracked from true MS onset. The null model, without left-truncation, estimated the DMT effect on relapse risk (HR = 0.64; 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.54–0.77). Left-truncation inflated this estimate. Shorter random truncation (1 year) produced greater bias (HR = 0.34), decreasing with longer durations (3-year HR = 0.48). Truncation not-at-random biased relapse estimates (HR = 0.37). Disability outcomes were less sensitive.Conclusion:MSMs can reliably estimate DMT effectiveness in left-truncated MS registry data, although accuracy depends on truncation mechanism and duration. Both random and not-at-random truncation impact relapse estimates. Disability outcomes appear less sensitive. Fixed-time truncation and covariate adjustment mitigated bias.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, left-truncation, disease-modifying therapies, marginal structural modelling, causal inference

Introduction

Many areas of research in neurology have seen the rise of observational data to guide clinical practice. Increasingly, specialty registries are being utilised to uncover causal relationships between risk factors or therapies and outcomes. Observational studies investigating long-term effectiveness of therapies often face left-truncation bias.1,2 Left-truncation occurs when patients enter a study after a variable time from disease onset, leading to an unobserved interval between symptom onset and data capture.3,4 If the timing of study entry is informed by patient characteristics, this could introduce systematic bias into estimates of treatment effectiveness. Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an example of a disease with a chronic course, susceptible to such bias. In MS, left-truncation may distort estimates of treatment effectiveness on relapse risk or disability.

Methods of causal inference, such as marginal structural models (MSMs), are becoming more commonly employed to address confounding in observational MS studies.5–9 However, their performance in the presence of left-truncation remains unclear. This simulation study evaluated the impact of left-truncation, both random and not-at-random, on the estimates of disease-modifying therapy (DMT) effectiveness in relapsing MS. It explored design solutions to counter this bias. We hypothesised that MSMs can produce reliable effect estimates in MS despite left-truncation.

Methods

Data source and study population

The study utilised the MSBase registry, a global clinical database of MS. 10 Data were extracted in August 2023 from 144 centres in 41 countries.

Eligible patients were diagnosed with clinically isolated syndrome or relapse-onset MS, with at least 1 year of follow-up, a minimum of three disability scores (with at least 1 per year) and a dataset including demographics, clinical, DMT and relapse history. Disability was quantified using the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS). A Neurostatus-certified rater was required at each MSBase site. 11

Two populations were included. The full cohort (30,838 patients) met the inclusion criteria, regardless of time from disease onset to registry entry, used to establish consistency of methods by replicating findings from previous analysis 6 and establishing associations between patient characteristics and observed left-truncated time. A subset (5588 patients) followed from MS onset, defined as entering the registry within 3 months of first symptoms, was used for all analytical aspects.

Study design

This study was designed to evaluate the causal effect of DMTs on clinical outcomes in MS, with a primary focus on assessing the impact of left-truncation on these estimates. We employed a Cox MSM, hypothesising that it yields reliable, unbiased estimates even in the presence of left-truncation.

Follow-up time was segmented into 6-month bins. Patients were permitted to transition between treated and untreated states without censoring or re-baselining. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), recalculated at each bin, created a pseudo-population in which treatment allocation was independent of measured confounders.

First, replicated the results of previously published Cox MSM 6 using an expanded study sample included the full cohort and the subset without left-truncation. The latter served as the reference for subsequent analyses of left-truncated data.

Exposure

To evaluate changing DMT status over time, we classified 6-month bins as ‘Treated’ (treated with any DMT for ⩾75% of the bin), ‘Not treated’ (<16.4%) or ‘Partially treated’ (16.4%–75%).

Outcomes

Outcomes were relapses, disability worsening and improvement within 6-month bins. Disability worsening was defined as an EDSS increase of ⩾1 point (1.5 if baseline EDSS was 0, 0.5 if baseline EDSS was ⩾6), confirmed 12 months. Disability improvement was a decrease of ⩾1 point (1.5 if baseline EDSS was 1.5, 0.5 if baseline EDSS was ⩾ 6.5), also confirmed 12 months.

Statistical analysis

Cox proportional hazards MSM were developed using R (v4.4.0) with ipw and rms packages to compare the outcomes between treated and untreated states. For each scenario, the hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated. Covariate balance and weight stability were assessed via standardised mean differences and distribution of weights.

Weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Covariates included fixed (sex), time-varying (treatment status, prior treatment, pregnancy, disease duration, disability score, relapses, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) activity, MS course) and stabilising (birth date, disease duration at first visit, MS onset date).

We first applied this method to the full cohort to replicate prior 2021 findings, then to the subset of patients with registry entry within 3 months of MS onset, considered left-truncation free and serving as a reference for the subsequent experiments.

Left-truncation at random

We first assessed the impact of random delay from MS onset to first clinic visit on the estimated treatment effect size. This assumes the time from MS onset to first visit (the truncation time) was not systematically related to patient characteristics or outcomes. Truncation time for each patient was randomly sampled from normal distributions with means of 1, 2 or 3 years and a standard deviation of one-quarter of the mean (Figure 1).

A schematic diagram of left-truncation mechanisms, illustrating random and not-at-random truncation.

Data before the truncation date were discarded. The first clinic visit after this date was considered the beginning of follow-up. Patients who were truncated after their last visit were excluded. We generated 50 iterations with one seed and five additional iterations with different seeds to assess robustness.

MSM estimated the average treatment effect among the treated for each truncated dataset. Point estimates of the effect size for DMT (β) were averaged across iterations truncated at 1, 2 or 3 years and compared to the null model.

Left-truncation not-at-random

To investigate where timing of the first clinical visit was systematically associated with patient characteristics, we applied left-truncation not-at-random. Individual left-truncation time was assigned based on observed associations between patient characteristics and time from MS onset to first registry entry. A mixed-effects regression model was fitted on the full cohort, estimating time to first registry entry from age, baseline EDSS, sex and pre-baseline relapses, with country-level random intercept. Based on this model, individual left-truncated time was predicted once for patient in a subset followed from MS onset (Figure 1). MSM was applied to this left-truncated group, and resulting estimates were compared with the null model.

Next, we explored interventions to minimise bias from left-truncation not-at-random by testing two remedies. First, all patients’ data were left-truncated at a fixed time of 2 or 3 years from MS onset. Second, patient characteristics strongly associated with truncation, together with selected variables associated with treatment assignment, were included as adjustment variables in the Cox MSM to account for confounding related to truncation. An intervention was deemed satisfactory if it normalised biased point and interval estimates with those from the null model.

Left-truncation with misspecified IPTW models

To simulate real-world scenarios where treatment determinants are unobserved or inadequately accounted for, we deliberately misspecified IPTW models by excluding critical covariates (EDSS, recent relapses, recent MRI activity and prior DMT use) and including highly correlated covariates (pregnancy status and duration). These misspecifications were first applied to the non-truncated Cox MSM and then combined with left-truncation not-at-random to assess its impact and examine how they influenced causal treatment estimates over time. This provided insights into the sensitivity of Cox MSM to model misspecification and left-truncation. We applied fixed-time truncation and multivariable adjustment to remediate bias introduced by misspecified IPTW in left-truncated data.

Results

Baseline characteristics of cohorts

The inclusion/exclusion of patients has resulted in 30,838 patients irrespective of time to first registry entry and a subset of 5588 with entry within 3 months of MS onset. The majority of patients were female, with relapsing-remitting MS, a mean onset age of 30–32 years and mild disability (EDSS = 0–3.5) (Table 1). At the initial visit, most were untreated, followed by patients on interferon beta.

Reference model (no left-truncation)

To establish consistency with previous work, we repeated Cox MSM in the full cohort of 30,838 patients. 6 Treatment effect estimates were consistent: relapses HR = 0.52 (95% CI = 0.44–0.62), confirmed disability worsening HR = 0.83 (95% CI = 0.72–0.95) and confirmed disability improvement HR = 1.2 (95% CI = 0.92–1.5). Applying the validated method to 5588 patients with complete data from MS onset, yielded HR = 0.65 (95% CI = 0.54–0.77) for relapses, HR = 0.87 (95% CI = 0.6–1.3) for disability worsening and HR = 1.2 (95% CI = 1–1.5) for disability improvement, all within the 95% CI of previously published estimates (relapses: HR = 0.51–0.54 (95% CI = 0.38–0.68), disability worsening: HR = 0.59–0.74 (95% CI = 39–88), disability improvement: HR = 1.26–1.36 (95% CI = 1.02–1.80)). 6 The alignment of these estimates with the full cohort and prior analyses provides reassurance regarding the robustness of the approach, despite the early-entry nature of this reference group.

We further applied a stricter treatment classification that excluded partially treated bins, which served as the reference model. HRs in the full cohort were 0.79 (95% CI = 0.7–0.9) for relapses, 0.82 (95% CI = 0.74–0.91) for disability worsening and 1.2 (95% CI = 1.0–1.4) for disability improvement, respectively, while in the subset, they were 0.64 (95% CI = 0.54–0.77), 0.76 (95% CI = 0.55–1.1) and 1.2 (95% CI = 0.91–1.5) (Supplementary Table 2).

Truncation at random

We compared the causal estimates in the cohort of 5558 patients before versus after left-truncation. Figure 2 shows the average treatment effects among the treated for the untruncated dataset and datasets truncated at random with mean truncation times of 1, 2 or 3 years (averaged over 50 iterations). Shorter truncation intervals introduced bias for relapses, with 1-year HR of 0.34 (95% CI = 0.23–0.51) versus reference HR of 0.64 (95% CI = 0.54–0.77), which diminished with longer truncation duration (3-year HR = 0.48, 95% CI = 0.35–0.66). In contrast, HRs for disability worsening and improvement remained consistent across truncation durations, suggesting minimal bias (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 1).

Left-truncation not-at-random

Left-truncation not-at-random was compared in 5588 patients before versus after left-truncation. Truncated time was predicted from a multivariable model of time from first MS symptom to registry entry, conditional on patient characteristics, most closely associated with recent relapses (β = −0.32) and EDSS (β = 0.12). Compared to reference relapse estimates (HR = 0.64, 95% CI = 0.54–0.77), left-truncated not-at-random estimates were inflated (HR = 0.37, 95% CI = 0.25–0.56). A similar pattern was observed for disability worsening (HR = 0.57 (95% CI = 0.29–1.1) vs. reference HR = 0.76 (95% CI = 0.55–1.1)), although CI overlapped more, while HRs for improvement remained stable (HR = 1.1 (95% CI = 0.76–7.5) vs. reference HR = 1.2 (95% CI = 0.91–1.5)). Both fixed-time truncation and multivariable adjustment reduced bias, with fixed truncation at 2 or 3 years being most effective (Figure 3 and Table 2).

Green curves show the estimates after applying these remedies.

Left-truncation with misspecified IPTW models

To test MSM robustness, IPTW models were misspecified in left-truncated not-at-random data. Omitting key confounders, EDSS and relapses had a negligible impact, while omitting prior DMT use produced substantial bias. Excluding brain MRI activity resulted in a negligible change, and including closely correlated variables did not introduce substantial bias (Table 3). Fixed-time truncation and multivariable adjustment in the outcome model mitigated biases from left-truncation and model misspecifications (Table 4).

Bias and 95% coverage across left-truncation scenarios

Bias, relative bias and 95% coverage, relative to the reference, were evaluated across truncation scenarios. Relapse outcomes were most affected, 1- and 2-year random truncation yielded relative biases of −0.44 and −0.43 and coverage of 16% and 12%, respectively, while truncation not-at-random produced a relative bias of −0.42 with 0% coverage (single iteration). Biases for disability worsening and improvement were smaller, with coverage generally near 100% (Table 2).

Discussion

This study showed how informed left-truncation, common in registries of chronic neurological conditions such as MS, affects causal estimates of treatment effects. Our results indicate that average treatment effects among the treated MS patients can be effectively estimated over the disease trajectory using left-truncated data. The reliability depends on the nature of left-truncation, outcomes assessed and misspecification of balancing models. Effective strategies to reduce the bias introduced by informed left-truncation are consistent left-truncation at 2 or 3 years from MS onset and a multivariable adjustment of the outcome MSM with variables omitted from the balancing model. Interestingly, the lack of balancing for baseline MRI variables does not bias treatment effects estimates. Clinically, MS immunotherapies markedly reduce the risk of relapses and disability worsening and increase the likelihood of disability improvement.

In left-truncation at random, where delayed entry is independent of patient characteristics, bias arises mainly from information loss, not selection. To simulate this, truncation times were randomly sampled from a normal distribution, isolating the impact of missing early data while assuming registry entry unrelated to outcomes or baseline risk. Treatment effect estimates for relapse outcomes were biased when short truncation windows (mean 1 year) were applied. This bias diminished with longer truncation periods (2 and 3 years).

The sharp attenuation in HR at 1-year truncation at random likely stems from excluding early follow-up, which includes the presenting MS attack when relapse risk is highest. 12 In parallel, many are yet to commence their first DMT, or DMTs have not reached full effect. 13 Since we focused on patients followed from onset, their early disease trajectory may differ due to evolving diagnostic criteria, treatment initiation dynamics and natural progression. Newer MS criteria allow earlier diagnosis, 14 enriching cohorts with patients at earlier stages and higher relapse risk. These patients may also seek care following a relapse, overrepresenting active disease early. Excluding the earliest period after MS onset lowers relapse risk estimates. Although DMTs are most effective early, their benefits accrue over time and require a latency period.13,15,16 Removing early follow-up excludes initial relapses before treatment effects manifest, underestimating HR. In addition, relapse rate declines naturally, so left-truncation captures later, more stable phases of MS, further reducing HR.

Unlike the estimates of treatment effect on relapses, those for disability worsening and improvement were robust to bias from truncation at random. This may reflect the more gradual, cumulative nature of disability worsening compared to acute, transient changes of relapses. Disability worsening in MS typically occurs over extended periods, involving neurodegeneration that unfolds over months or years. 17 This is especially true for relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS), where short-term disability fluctuations, such as temporary worsening following a relapse, may resolve, making long-term disability trajectories less sensitive to early follow-up exclusions. 18

In specialised registries with longitudinal data, patient enrolment is often delayed, influenced by disease severity, characteristics and healthcare access.19,20 Entry may be delayed among patients with milder symptoms or slower progression, while those with highly active disease often seek specialist care earlier, facilitating enrolment. To assess the impact of this informed delay on treatment effect estimates, we applied a left-truncation not-at-random framework in patients followed from onset. This resulted in a stronger estimated treatment effect on relapse. Similarly, the estimated hazard for disability worsening was lower, although confidence intervals overlapped. In contrast, the HR for disability improvement remained consistent, suggesting it may be less influenced by entry timing.

The observed inflated effects of therapy on relapses and disability worsening following truncation not-at-random may reflect differences in disease activity between early and delayed entrants. Patients with higher initial relapse activity and lower disability were more likely to be included in the registry earlier. Their disease phenotype would predispose them to a superior response to the available therapies than among patients with less relapses and higher disability. Selectively including more complete early follow-up information among these patients in the registry data would likely lead to inflation of the estimated treatment effects. From a clinical perspective, such overestimation due to left-truncation not-at-random may lead to overly optimistic expectations of treatment effectiveness, particularly in early disease stages. This may influence therapeutic decision-making, including escalation or persistence with therapy.15,21,22 Patients with lower inflammatory activity or later presentation may derive more modest benefit than suggested by such estimates.

To mitigate bias from left-truncation not-at-random, we implemented fixed-time truncation and multivariable adjustment. Fixed-time truncation involved left-truncating follow-up at a predetermined 2 or 3 years from MS onset for all patients, avoiding variability by patient characteristics. This mitigated the impact of informed delay on treatment estimates. In addition, covariates strongly associated with left-truncation and treatment assignment were included in the Cox MSM to address residual confounding not captured by the IPTW model. This provides an additional adjustment layer, improving the outcome model and reducing bias, although less efficiently than fixed left-truncation. These findings have important implications for registry design and data collection practices. While variability in time from symptom onset to registry entry is an inherent feature of real-world datasets such as MSBase, efforts to minimise this variability, through earlier enrolment where feasible or analytically standardised entry criteria, may improve the validity of causal estimates. In addition, systematic capture of pre-enrolment clinical variables is critical, as these factors influence both timing of entry and treatment allocation. In the absence of such data, differences in timing of entry into the registry may not be adequately accounted for, which could increase the risk of biased estimates.

We investigated the impact of IPTW model misspecification on treatment effect estimates and found that its influence depends on which variables are omitted. Excluding treatment history produced substantial bias, consistent with prior findings that ignoring pre-baseline exposure distorts causal inference. 23 Omitting MRI variables had little impact, likely because their predictive value for treatment or outcomes was minor compared to clinical predictors.24–26 While combining misspecification with left-truncation not-at-random increased bias, it was less than the structural bias from informed registry entry timing. Sensitivity analyses indicate that recent relapse history has a moderate influence on estimates, whereas MRI, EDSS and pregnancy variables appear less critical, with minimal impact on results.

This study has several limitations. First, MSMs assume no unmeasured confounding. Although our IPTW model included a comprehensive set of covariates, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Reassuringly, adjusting outcome models with additional covariates did not substantially alter estimates, and the findings remained consistent with previous analyses, external studies and clinical experience, suggesting that residual confounding is unlikely to materially affect results. In addition, the reference group may reflect patients who presented early following an attack and therefore have higher inflammatory activity. The robustness of this approach is evidenced by its comparison with previously published estimates in both the full cohort and early-entry subgroup, and by consistency with estimates observed in the present full cohort irrespective of time from onset to registry entry. Because the truncation scenarios were applied within the same underlying cohort, differences in estimates reflect the impact of truncation rather than differences in baseline population characteristics.

Second, in our exposure classification, periods on immunosuppressants were categorised as untreated, and low- and high-efficacy DMTs were pooled, potentially underestimating treatment effects. We addressed this by defining treated time as 6-month intervals with ⩾75% DMT exposure, ensuring the treated group reflects sustained treatment. However, the observed truncation effects cannot be assumed to apply uniformly across drug classes. Third, missing data, particularly baseline MRI variables, could bias results; however, exclusion of MRI information from IPTW had minimal impact, suggesting treatment estimates were largely unaffected because the exposure models sufficiently accounted for clinical variables. Finally, although left-truncation was addressed using multiple scenarios, including both random and informed truncation, some uncertainty remains about how well these sampled patterns represent real-world registry entry. Consistent estimates across truncation scenarios and bias adjustments support the generalisability of our findings.

Conclusion

Causal effects of DMTs for MS can be estimated reliably in observational data from specialised registries despite the presence of left-truncation of longitudinally acquired data. The bias introduced by informed delay of enrolment of patients into registries (left-truncation not-at-random) can inflate treatment effects on relapses and disability worsening in MS. Simple remedies, such as consistent left-truncation of registry data at a fixed time, with or without covariate adjustment for likely determinants of the left-truncation not-at-random, substantially reduce bias. Furthermore, MSM are robust to most misspecifications of the models of treatment allocation in MS, even without MRI data. MSM in combination with consistent left-truncation and covariate adjustment of the outcome model is suitable for the evaluation of causal treatment effects in MS registries.
