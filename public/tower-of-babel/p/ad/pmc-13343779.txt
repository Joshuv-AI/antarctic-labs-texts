An exceptional localization of esophageal cancer: metastasis to the internal auditory canal
Authors: Thomas Renders, Younes Steffens, Anne Doyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetastasis to the internal auditory canal (IAC) is rare, representing less than 1% of lesions in this region. We report the case of a 76-year-old man with a history of esophageal adenocarcinoma treated with chemoradiotherapy, who presented with progressive dizziness and unilateral hearing loss. Days later, he developed acute left peripheral facial paralysis. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed an enhancing IAC lesion associated with two cerebellar nodules. Vestibular testing confirmed a complete unilateral deficit. Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging showed hypermetabolic activity in all lesions, and a stereotactic biopsy of the largest cerebellar nodule confirmed the diagnosis of metastatic adenocarcinoma consistent with the esophageal primary. Despite supportive care, the patient died within one month. This case highlights the need to consider metastatic disease in atypical audiovestibular presentations in patients with a cancer history.

Introduction

Metastasis to the IAC represents 0.3%–0.7% of lesions in this region1. Esophageal cancer rarely metastasizes to the brain, and IAC involvement is exceptional. We present a rapidly progressive case of IAC and cerebellar metastases from esophageal adenocarcinoma, documented through multimodal imaging from first symptoms to death due to leptomeningeal dissemination.

Case report

A 76-year-old male presented with three weeks of progressive dizziness. His history included esophageal adenocarcinoma (cT3N1M0) treated with chemoradiotherapy (5-fluorouracil and oxaliplatin) completed 24 months before symptom onset, with no recurrence on thoracoabdominal imaging two months earlier.

He reported worsening left hearing loss. Videonystagmoscopy revealed only a few ocular saccadic intrusions. Audiometry confirmed complete left-sided deafness. Video head impulse test showed a markedly reduced lateral canal gain with corrective saccades.

He returned before completion of investigations with sudden left facial paralysis (House–Brackmann V) and headache. Gadolinium-enhanced Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) showed a 13 × 6 mm enhancing IAC lesion near cranial nerves VII–VIII (Fig. 1). Initial interpretation suggested vestibular schwannoma.

Gadolinium-enhanced MRI showing a 13 × 6 mm enhancing nodule in the left internal auditory canal.

Multidisciplinary review identified two additional cerebellar nodules (Fig. 2), raising suspicion of metastatic disease.

Axial MRI showing cerebellar metastatic lesions in T2-weighted (A) and T1-weighted (B) images.

Videonystagmography revealed a 100% left vestibular deficit. Lumbar puncture identified atypical cells but was non-diagnostic. FDG-PET demonstrated hypermetabolic activity in all three lesions (Fig. 3). Methionine-PET confirmed and better delineated these lesions (Fig. 4).

FDG-PET showing hypermetabolic activity in the IAC lesion and cerebellar nodules.

C11-methionine PET-CT showing the main lesion in the internal auditory canal (A) and the two other cerebellar lesions (B and C).

Stereotactic biopsy of the largest cerebellar lesion revealed CK7-positive, CK20-negative adenocarcinoma consistent with the esophageal primary (Fig. 5). No extracranial disease was detected.

Cerebellar metastasis stained with hematoxylin and eosin (A) and CK7-positive immunohistochemical staining (B).

A palliative management plan was initiated, including corticosteroids, analgesia, and supportive care. The patient’s neurological status rapidly deteriorated, and he died less than one month later.

Discussion

IAC metastasis is rare and often associated with widespread disease. Primary origins include lung, breast, skin and gastrointestinal tumors, with adenocarcinoma representing the most frequent histological subtype [1].

Clinical manifestations are variable; hearing loss is the most common initial symptom, followed by facial paralysis, vertigo, and tinnitus [1].

Metastatic spread to the temporal bone may occur hematogenously, through leptomeningeal dissemination, or by direct extension [2]. In this case, multifocal lesions and cerebrospinal fluid abnormalities strongly support leptomeningeal spread.

Published cases illustrate the heterogeneity of metastatic routes. Imauchi et al. described cervical esophageal carcinoma metastasizing to the temporal bone [3], whereas Han et al. reported gastric carcinoma spreading to the petrous apex [4]. Adams et al. described leptomeningeal involvement with bilateral auditory nerve lesions [5]. Our case differs by its rapid, multifocal leptomeningeal dissemination.

MRI is the reference imaging modality for IAC lesions, but PET imaging provides valuable metabolic information. Methionine PET, although less widely available, has shown higher sensitivity and specificity than FDG-PET for intracranial metastases [6].

Brain metastases from esophageal cancer remain uncommon, with reported incidences of 1.4% to 3.8% according to different studies [7].

The onset of symptoms is variable, with a median interval between diagnosis of the primary tumor and diagnosis of metastasis of approximately 5.6 months (0.0—36.5) [8].

Prognosis is extremely poor; median survival is below four months, with an overall 1-year survival rate of 18% [7]. At the stage of leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, a systematic literature review reports survival times ranging from 1 to 7 months [9].

Conclusion

Esophageal adenocarcinoma metastasis to the IAC is exceptionally rare. Rapid progression of audiovestibular symptoms should prompt thorough neuroimaging. In this case, PET imaging complemented MRI and confirmed the diagnosis. The fulminant evolution highlights the aggressiveness of leptomeningeal dissemination.
