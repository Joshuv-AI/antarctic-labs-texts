Towards convergence of AI and blockchain for personalized medicine in pharmacogenomics
Authors: Mutiullah Shaikh, Ali Ebrahimi, Uffe Kock Wiil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The health informatics field’s pursuit of personalized healthcare continuously faces constraints from patients, clinicians, and resource limitations. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) models have led to their widespread adoption in personalized genomic research for their outstanding predictive capabilities for drug responses to assist in personalized healthcare for tailored therapies and many other applications. Despite their growing use, such models often operate as black boxes, tempering, lacking sources to verify whether a prediction was generated honestly by a model’s input or manipulated post hoc. Over these challenges, this study presents a decentralized model that integrates AI predictive modeling with blockchain-based verification to ensure the integrity, traceability, trust, and reproducibility of AI-generated outputs, leading to provable machine learning and trustworthy AI. Our developed scheme computes AI predictions, cryptographic hashes of model inputs, and data hashes to immutably store them on a blockchain via smart contract (SC) using our novel input-output cryptographic hashing technique. This introduces a deterministic tokenization and canonical hashing pipeline that binds each GDSC2 drug–cell line input and its AI prediction output into a salted, on-chain verifiable commit. Later, a verification process has been committed by blockchain’s immutability and cross-checking via audit logs, which allows any stakeholder to independently confirm that a specific prediction originated from a known model and reliable source of data without exposing sensitive genomic content, ensuring both the verifiability and honesty of audits to serve the purpose for addressing AI post hoc tampering issue. The experimental results using genomic data inputs derived from the GDSCv2 dataset demonstrate the proposed model’s capability to train a Random Forest Regressor (RFR) for accurate AI-driven drug sensitivity prediction, achieving an R² of 0.979. Furthermore, 5-Fold Cross-Validation yielded a consistent mean R² of 0.977 ± 0.001, highlighting the model’s strong reliability, robustness, and generalization performance across multiple data partitions. Later, the model can store these predictions on-chain with due patient consent to verify or audit, detect tampering, ensure transparency, and verifiability up to 70% through an audit trial integrity test conducted for 10 samples from the dataset. The findings support the model’s applicability in high-stakes personalized medicine and biomedical environments where verifiable AI predictions are paramount.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53058-6.

Introduction

Integrating genomic sequencing data into clinical practices is often standard and proven as a transformative step towards personalized healthcare that offers promising precision in disease diagnosis, prognosis, and customized therapeutic strategies1.

The importance of personalized healthcare has always been relentless in accurately predicting drug responses, especially in precision oncology, which cannot be overstated, as it directly influences treatment effectiveness, patient survival, and overall quality of life2. However, the global healthcare system faces a growing shortage of trained professionals, with projections indicating a deficit of more than 15 million healthcare workers by 20303. This critical workforce shortage makes personalized healthcare delivery increasingly complex, particularly in data-intensive fields such as personalized medicine and pharmacogenomics, which reveal many customized treatment therapies4. Highlights the urgent need for scalable, automated decision-support systems and integrative intelligent solutions. In this context, the convergence of Artificial Intelligence (AI), Blockchain (BC), and digital Health Informatics offers a promising pathway to bridge clinical gaps by enabling automated, trustworthy, reproducible, and verifiable predictive healthcare decision support systems (HDSS).

The amalgamation of advanced large language models (LLMs) and AI systems into digital health has heralded a transformative era, positioning AI as a core technology in medical diagnostics and genomic prediction5. In parallel, blockchain technology has emerged as a paired backbone solution to address critical concerns about data integrity, auditability, and trust in AI-driven predictions and decision-making6. Recent studies show that generative AI can approach clinical diagnostic performance with several emerging challenges, i.e. lack of transparency and traceability that limit them for clinical deployment, triggering blockchain-based verifiability frameworks essential for building reliable and ethically compliant digital healthcare systems7. Furthermore, AI has demonstrated momentous proven potential in the healthcare domain, yet they are frequently prone to hallucinations, misdiagnoses, and biases, especially when applied to complex, individualized treatment scenarios. Personalized medicine requires precise, context-sensitive reasoning based on the unique physiological profile and longitudinal medical history of the patient. When AI generates plausible sounds but factually incorrect outputs, the risk of inappropriate treatment recommendations becomes severe and potentially irreversible8. These limitations emphasize the critical need for verifiable AI systems that not only produce AI predictions accurately, but also embed mechanisms for provenance, accountability, and error traceability capabilities, which need to be addressed according to the demanding urge of stakeholders and on-going research trends9.

These research trends follow the most familiar and reliable methods for mitigating hallucinations in AI and blackbox in specialized scenarios, including techniques of provable machine learning and trustworthy AI through the application of decentralized technologies10. Their approaches can effectively improve the accuracy of model output without altering its architecture, and data inputs might be reinforced using a smart contract deployed on a blockchain, which stores hashed input–output pairs immutably, time-stamped, tamper-free, and traceable policies. A smart contract is a self-executable program that serves as a decentralized controller for role-based access, consent management, and prediction verification. Unlike traditional healthcare record systems that rely on centralized auditing or off-chain logs, this smart contract implements a cryptographically anchored logging mechanism stored on-chain with several copies of records.

Applying cryptographic hashes of input-output pairs during AI inference and recording them immutably on a blockchain is promising, where each entry is timestamped, tamper-resistant, and cryptographically linked to the identity of the user who initiated the prediction (e.g., a verified researcher), forming a secure and auditable trail of inference activity. In the domain of personalized healthcare, where predictive models must process sensitive, patient-specific genomic, phenotypic, and clinical information, the demand for transparent, verifiable, and policy-compliant AI systems is critical and could be met with this novel explored technique. However, genomic data are patient-specific and lack customization, and AI faces difficulties in generating precise, direct predictions, and summaries. In addition, personalized medical researchers are tirelessly investigating the development of specialized privacy protection mechanisms such as the design of a targeted de-identification method for personalized medical LLMs, to meet the privacy protection requirements of patients when using LLMs11. So, through this input-output cryptographic approach, a verifiability layer that satisfies the blackbox and key regulatory and ethical requirements, aligning with emerging standards in privacy-protected healthcare AI governance, including GDPR for data integrity, FDA Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) transparency guidelines, and FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data principles12.

In addition to these challenges, the full potential of AI in personalized healthcare through genomics has not yet been realized. It remains unknown how effectively AI can make predictions, and blockchain technology, with its transparency, immutability, and decentralized nature, can address these critical issues and make data predictions trustworthy for genomic research. Furthermore, integrating blockchain and AI models specifically for predicting drug sensitivity in cancer diagnosis using genomic datasets is an emerging field that requires thorough exploration and validation.

Taking into account the more recent research in this domain, our deeper exploration combines AI with blockchain technology to develop an innovative model that acts as a digital framework geared toward customized, personalized healthcare applications. For now, the model transforms drug sensitivity in the cancer line diagnostic process based on AI predictions using personalized genomic data, a step-by-step reasoning process like that in genomic centers, and then turns this process into a blockchain-enhanced regulatory framework. Related work

This study evaluates the progress of the application of AI and blockchain technology convergence in relevant fields of personalized healthcare. Personalized healthcare refers to a medical model that tailors healthcare decisions and interventions to individual patient characteristics—such as genetic profile, lifestyle, environment, and clinical history—enabling more precise, predictive, and effective tailored treatment strategies13. Although AI-driven systems have proven excellent performance in healthcare, they still face considerable challenges in addressing personalized healthcare, such as trust in model predictions, uncustomised data outputs, including illusions, verifiability issues, and no responsibility in case of incorrect AI-based recommendations, leading to ethical and legal accountability. To address these challenges, this research explores the potential of deploying blockchain to personalized healthcare, particularly the application of smart contracts in Genomic research and governance. Using these technologies, this study aims to explore innovative solutions for personalized medicine for drug sensitivity in cancer treatment.

Synergistic integration of AI and blockchain for personalized healthcare

Healthcare data comes in many forms, i.e., electronic health records (EHRs), medical images, lab results, wearable sensor data, genomic sequences, and each provides a partial view of a patient. When it comes to personalized medicine, AI can now fuse these multi-modal data streams to generate a holistic picture of individual health, yielding insights far beyond what any single data source provides. Multimodal AI models take advantage of advances in deep learning to simultaneously analyze heterogeneous data (text, images, signals, genomic data, etc.), uncovering complex relationships that inform personalized care14. However, personalized healthcare has moved beyond one-size-fits-all approaches, driven by artificial intelligence in diverse domains. Recent advances indicate that artificial intelligence (AI) can enable truly individualized care from genomic-informed drug choices to real-time digital twins, mental health, and transcending standard diagnostic uses.

Recent academic research surveyed from 2021 to 2025 has made significant strides in cutting-edge applications of AI, modern deep learning, machine learning, and LLMs with Blockchain framework that can rapidly analyze patient healthcare data and genomic profile (and other “omics” data) to identify the optimal personalized therapeutic strategy with due transparency and trust. For example, in genomic oncology medicine, AI systems process the DNA/RNA data of a tumor to recommend specific therapies that match the tumor mutations of that patient, forming personalized treatment plans and prognoses15. This goes beyond traditional biomarkers by recognizing complex gene drug interactions that would be infeasible to find manually. One notable development is the use of generative AI (OpenAI’s GPT−4) to interpret pharmacogenetic tests with domain-specific genomic databases to help clinicians understand pharmacogenetic results, which could parse a patient’s genetic variants.

(e.g. CYP450 alleles) to suggest personalized medication choices or dosage adjustments, improving provider and patient comprehension of complex genomic data. This illustrates how large language models (LLMs) can translate raw genetic data into personalized drug recommendations, enhancing precision in prescribing. Although there are challenges such as precision and regulatory consent, such AI tools hint at a future in which AI routinely analyzes a patient’s genetic makeup to predict drug efficacy and avoid adverse reactions16.

Another diversified study was introduced as a groundbreaking medical diagnostic framework by taming LLMs by establishing an audit framework using blockchain technology. Their innovative proposed solution CHATCBD achieves a level of advancement in the field of massive, personalized learning in a way to improve global medical equity, particularly by improving healthcare conditions in impoverished regions by providing tailored and reliable medical services through LLMs. Their promising results evaluated some aspects that could be addressed in future research related to privacy protection problems that might be addressed to achieve the widespread application of LLMs in the healthcare domain17.

Blockchain technology has emerged as a transformative solution to key challenges in healthcare care, offering a secure and decentralized foundation for implementing dynamic, patient-centered, privacy-protected systems and clinical implementation. Blockchain is defined by its core attributes of transparency, immutability, decentralization, and traceability, making it suitable for managing sensitive multimodal healthcare data18. Integrating smart contracts, which are self-executing programs embedded on the blockchain, enables automated execution of predefined agreements and policies without intermediaries once specified conditions are met19. The inherent power of blockchain lies in its ability to establish trust in untrusted environments by ensuring that all transactions and modifications to data are verifiable, auditable, and irreversibly recorded. This decentralized trust framework not only strengthens data sovereignty and patient autonomy, but also facilitates resilient healthcare information systems and secure medical exchange by providing audit events, minimizing the risks of tempering, centralized failures, fraud, or cyberattacks20. As a result, blockchain offers a robust digital infrastructure for personalized healthcare that is both secure and accountable by a democratic design.

The integration of blockchain technology with artificial intelligence (AI) in personalized healthcare marks a pivotal shift from theoretical exploration to practical, synergistic implementation. Blockchain clinical pilots underscore this convergence as a provable transformative force advancing patient data ownership, dynamic consent management in genomic data sharing by ConsentChain21, and a secure electronic health record (EHR) interoperability framework in ACTION-EHR22 empower patients with granular control over their medical data through smart contract governance. These systems align with global data protection regulations such as GDPR and HIPAA, while also supporting the FAIR principles for data reusability, trust, and transparency20.

In parallel, blockchain has been applied to sharing genomic data, supporting secure access to highly sensitive information, and enabling audit trails for AI model training and inference. Projects like GenShare22 and a novel Blockchain-Based Model for Secure Genomic Data Management23 and FHIRChain24 combine blockchain with advanced cryptographic techniques such as homomorphic encryption and self-sovereign identities to protect privacy, security, data tampering, and patient access control in genomic research. The idea behind these developments is to enable secure healthcare data exchange between the owner (i.e., the patient) and a stakeholder (i.e., the clinician or the researcher) for further tailored diagnosis process or any clinical use. Despite these advances, challenges such as scalability, interoperability, data protection with legacy systems, and broad clinical adoption remain ongoing concerns23.However, the convergence of blockchain with AI, ML, LLMs and IoT signals a transformative shift towards healthcare systems, bringing more technologies and avenues to discuss that support enabling personalized, hybrid, transparent, auditable, and ethically governed concerted healthcare systems25.

Several mechanisms have been proposed for tamper-evident logging of machine-learning outputs such as Merkle-commitment trees provide hierarchical batch hashing for large datasets, offering efficient partial verification but requiring periodic tree recomputation and trusted anchoring26. Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) attestation27, such as Intel SGX remote attestation, enables hardware-verified execution but relies on proprietary hardware, enclave lifecycle management, and is vulnerable to side-channel leakage. Digital timestamping and notarization services (e.g., RFC 3161 or decentralized timestamp networks) provide proof-of-existence but do not bind predictions to model configuration, input arrays, or execution metadata. Unlike these baselines, our design uses a commit with deterministic canonicalization that binds inputs, predictions, model version, and consent enforcement into a single verifiable record, eliminating the need for external trust anchors or enclave hardware. This positioning allows our system to operate on ordinary compute infrastructure while providing integrity guarantees comparable to more complex trustworthy systems.

Federated learning on AI-driven blockchain for healthcare

Hybrid AI-blockchain systems have massively facilitated Federated Learning (FL), allowing multiple institutions to collaboratively train AI models without centralizing and revealing raw patient data28. In these domains, the decentralized trust architecture of blockchain has been key to strengthening data security, ensuring regulatory compliance, and preserving patient autonomy, positioning it as a foundational infrastructure for personalized, data-driven healthcare29. When integrated with blockchain, FL benefits from immutable logging, smart-contract enforcement, and verifiable traceability of training contributions from several locations with their own data. Blockchain ensures that only genuine nodes participate, that updates are cryptographically hashed, verified, and that all actions are transparently auditable on-chain, enhancing security and compliance in multi-party AI collaborations25.

This convergence is rapidly gaining momentum due to the rising demands for privacy-preserving AI and the need to train on diverse, siloed datasets across hospitals, particularly for imaging, diagnostics, and rare disease detection. Recent research such as FLEDGE in the EU and frameworks like OpenFL + Hyperledger Fabric illustrate real-world implementations where federated learning is governed and secured through the blockchain infrastructure25,28. These systems enable not only distributed training, but also regulatory traceability, incentivizes data/model contributions, and governance in un-trusted environments, making blockchain-powered FL a leading paradigm in trustworthy and personalized healthcare AI.

Further research on FL has been increasingly combined with blockchain, FHE, and privacy-preserving machine learning (PPML) to enable collaborative model training without centralizing patient data. Several recent studies demonstrate the growing emphasis on secure, tamper-resistant, and privacy-preserving distributed learning. For example,30 explored encrypted gradient aggregation resilient to malicious clients, while31 exploiting Data Resolution in FL while introducing multi-scale outputs to reduce cost during training and inference. Similarly,32 presents optimization strategies that reduce the computational overhead of FHE-enabled FL, improving scalability for medical ML applications. These FL, PPML and FHE-based methods primarily address training-time privacy, preventing exposure of raw data or model gradients during distributed optimization and collaborative learning. Blockchain is typically used to coordinate aggregation, record contributions, or maintain a transparent FL workflow. However, these approaches do not provide per-inference guarantees, do not bind predictions to ML model version or input provenance, and do not enforce patient consent at the inference stage.

Proposed system architecture

Our main contribution to this research is to propose a hybrid architecture that integrates the intelligence of AI with the immutable capabilities of blockchain technology, specifically designed for personalized, secure, auditable and healthcare decision-making. Building on the insights gained from our comparative evaluation in Section-2, this model addresses the limitations found in standalone AI and blockchain systems by unifying data-driven personalization, regulatory compliance, and decentralized trust into a single adaptive framework. This section introduces the operational logic as Fig-2 illustrates the implemented model components discussed as:

User data aggregation

Data are also the foundation of this model, and our research is based on patient genomic sequencing data sources to take logical inputs and process them to predict the most accurate output of the AI model. The trained data set for this research is described in a later section and the proposed selected input space based on ablation study38 which identified genes’ relationships for drug sensitivity and compared this with our purpose of selecting the proposed compact model with 11-features against richer molecular representations derived from the complete GDSC2 dataset is promising and sensitively analyzed by3940.

To balance predictive performance and interpretability, we derived an 11-feature panel composed of key biological and pharmacological descriptors available in all samples: CELL_LINE_NAME, TCGA_DESC, DRUG_ID, DRUG_NAME, PUTATIVE_TARGET, PATHWAY_NAME,

This rich data stream enables a holistic data source for each patient to access his health profile, forming the basis for AI-driven reasoning and precision decision-making.

As the primary data source, the patient is an empowered user and consent controller, assigned by the policies governed by a smart contract, which enables them to give consent to access their sensitive genomic profile to researchers who are data stewards and approved analysts working on anonymized data for public health, AI training, or clinical diagnosis. Other users include healthcare providers, such as clinicians and Specialists, who are authorized medical professionals who use AI-generated insights to inform diagnoses and treatments. Data access is standardized and forwarded to the AI Decision Engine for analysis. Similarly, metadata from these data flows is logged into the Blockchain Governance Layer, ensuring traceability and compliance.

Consent management interface

To ensure ethical access of data and verifiable AI decision support, the system establishes a consent management interface that enables patients to control the access and scope of their data through a secure, blockchain-linked dashboard. This component enables dynamic consent, where patients can grant, update, or revoke permissions based on specific attributes, time periods, or intended use cases. Here, the patients manage their data permissions in plenty of ways like read-only access permitted over patient data to the clinicians, users and even AI inferences, write access for feedback and clinical notes and trigger consent requests if more data is needed.

This also leads as the crucial point for overall managing patient data permissions connecting directly to the Blockchain Governance Layer, where smart contract is deployed. It also interacts with the back end, the Healthcare Provider Portal, allowing clinicians to request additional access based on clinical needs. When such requests are made, the interface notifies the patient of the approval or denial. Additionally, it communicates with the AI Decision Support System indirectly through APIs only triggering inference once consent is validated. This ensures that every data access and AI interaction is authorized,

traceable, and patient-controlled and leads to a verifiable AI decision system.

Blockchain governance layer

The Blockchain acts as the trust backbone for our developed model by connecting all major components through smart contract and immutable logging events. It receives consent policies from the consent management interface and enforces them before permitting access to patient data or triggering AI inference. Then, it verifies access requests from the healthcare provider portal and authorizes the AI decision engine only when the conditions are met. This component also logs all events from the User Data Aggregation System, including data retrieval and model usage, creating a secure, auditable trail that is key to ensuring compliance, transparency, and tamper-proof accountability across the system.At last, to strengthen overall architecture’s reliability and compliance, this layer also incorporates a comprehensive adversarial threat model, identifying potential actors, their roles, capabilities, and corresponding mitigation strategies embedded within the smart contract deployed on blockchain workflow.

AI decision engine

The AI Decision engine is the intelligent part of the system that helps make sense of patient data and gives personalized health recommendations. It uses a regression model to analyze information from genetic data or readings from gene profiling. But before it can do anything, it checks with the blockchain layer to make sure the patient has given permission through the consent interface. Once authorized as give consent, it processes the data and sends clear results such as a diagnosis or treatment option to doctors through the web dashboard as visualized screenshots available on our github link. Every time it is used, the system keeps a secure record of what was done, so it can be reviewed later if needed. This setup ensures that AI only works when allowed and that its decisions are safe, accurate and built on patient trust.

Healthcare provider portal

The frontend web application, designed for our model, serves as the primary interface for both patients and healthcare providers, providing a user-friendly platform to interact with the system. For patients, it provides tools to manage data sharing preferences, review consent history, and respond to access requests, all connected to the consent Interface and secured through the blockchain. For clinicians, the app delivers personalized AI-generated insights, patient summaries, and audit logs through this portal. It communicates with the AI Engine to display real-time recommendations and with the blockchain Layer to ensure that every action is verifiable and compliant. This frontend acts as the unified access point for all users, making complex AI and blockchain processes easy to understand and use.

Experimental methods and techniques

This research aims to conduct a comprehensive experimental evaluation of the five components of the proposed model via a multidimensional evaluation focused on three key areas: (1) AI model accuracy, (2) System-level auditability and compliance, and (3) User interaction performance. First, without incorporating blockchain technology, the responses of the AI models were assessed to validate the predicted effectiveness of the proposed architecture. The blockchain technology was then configured and complex personalized healthcare scenarios from a personalized data set were selected to assess the optimization effect of the original model’s performance in precision oncology. Finally, the operational responsiveness and user experience for usability of the proposed model were evaluated by simulating the deployment of smart contracts in a blockchain environment and testing the system overhead introduced by the integration of blockchain with the AI model.

Dataset description

The drug sensitivity data used in this study were obtained from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer version 2 (GDSC2) dataset, generated and maintained by the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute in collaboration with the Center for Molecular Therapeutics at Massachusetts General Hospital Cancer Center, and publicly available at https://www.cancerrxgene.org/41.The dataset comprises 242,035 drug-response records spanning 969 cancer cell lines, 286 anti-cancer compounds, and 32 tumour types classified according to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) designations. Key pharmacological variables included in the dataset are: the natural logarithm of the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (LN_IC50), the area under the doseresponse curve (AUC), the root mean squared error of the curve fit (RMSE), and the Z-score normalised sensitivity (Z_SCORE).The dataset was used as the primary source for training testing, validating and testing the AI prediction using Random Forest Regressor(RFR) Machine Learning Model as decision engine.Further, molecular characterization includes 17,700 gene expression features, 700 somatic mutation markers, and copy number variations (CNVs) derived from microarray and sequencing data. Drug sensitivity response is measured using LN-IC50 values (half-maximal inhibitory concentration), log-transformed for normalization. Prior to modeling, covariates were standardized using z-score normalization, missing values were imputed using imputation, basic exploratory data analysis (EDA) was performed, and outlier samples (> 3 from the mean IC50 distribution) were removed.

The final dataset used for modeling included around 1.2 million (cell line, drug) statistical pairs with complete molecular and response annotations.

Experimental setup

Our model architecture was implemented as a web-based modular prototype designed to simulate real-world interactions between patients, researchers, healthcare providers, and AI systems under blockchain-enforced governance shown in Fig. Initiated by the AI engine, we integrated training of a regression model, specifically a Random Forest Regressor, built on Jupyter Notebook using basic Transformers. This model can process structured genetic health data and generate patient-specific insights from user data aggregation. The model was trained using the GDSC2 dataset acquired from41 and configured to respect the context provided by the patient’s consent parameters.

The blockchain layer was implemented using an Ethereum-compatible deployed locally network, i.e., Ganache, and a smart contract programmed in Solidity holding customized integrative logics tested through the Remix IDE web injected feature. Then all smart-contract experiments were conducted on a local Ganache devnet, accessed via MetaMask and our custom web interface. The devnet was configured with a fixed gas price and a block time of approximately 1s, providing a controlled environment for profiling gas usage and end-to-end latency under identical workloads. Because gas consumption is determined by EVM bytecode execution, the gas results reported are representative for any EVM-compatible public testnet or Layer-2 (L2), up to differences in base fees and opcode pricing. However, transaction inclusion latency and variability under load on public networks depend on network congestion, validator scheduling, and fee markets, which are not directly reproducible in a local Ganache environment. We therefore report Ganache results as a lower bound and discuss expected behavior on public testnets and L2s analytically, leaving full cross-network benchmarking. Overall, this setup is the operational layer that manages consent verification, audit log, and permission control, with all access requests and responses committed, cryptographically, and hash logged in a promising way. It also enforced access rules and dynamically authorized AI inference only when valid consent was verified. For real-time interactions, the system employed Web3.js to connect the user interface to the blockchain, an application binary (ABI) file that enables seamless interaction with the backend, feedback loops, and transaction tracking. To simulate a complete use case, an IPFS desktop node is configured as a database storing non-sensitive, structured genomic data records, while actual sensitive data was abstracted for compliance.

The front-end interface, serving as a healthcare provider portal, was built using React.js for responsiveness and a role-based user experience (Admin, Patient, Researcher), and managing consent between stakeholders through Metamask by importing Ganache eth accounts as a personal blockchain for Ethereum development, controlled and realistic testing environment emulating the behavior of the Ethereum Mainnet. We configure 3 Ganache digital accounts, each pre-funded with 100 ETH, and assign them with user experience roles, respectively. Meanwhile, back-end services were developed in the Flask API (Python) to handle the processing of the AI model, API calls, and smart contract orchestration through the ABI file.

The experimental inputs consist of 11 biomedical features that represent genomic and drug response data, each hashed for integrity. When a prediction is made, its output and associated input hash are logged immutably on-chain, depending on patient consent. The system is tested through multiple configurations on a powerful personal PC, including valid and invalid roles, missing consents, and tampered input to validate its ability to detect and prevent unauthorized actions. The time, output, and verification status of each transaction are manually recorded, forming a reproducible basis for evaluating the trustworthiness of the AI model and the operational robustness of integration. The code we have written for this model is available at https://github.com/mutsh/AI-Blockchain.

Evaluation scenario

Demonstrating the functionality of our Model, we conducted a simulation focused on AI prediction of personalized drug sensitivity using data from the GDSC2 dataset42. This dataset contains molecular profiles of cancer cell lines and their responses to various anti-cancer drugs, enabling the training and validation of AI models for precision oncology and pharmacogenomics. Upon modeling, to prevent data leakage and ensure reproducible model evaluation, the dataset was split into 60% training, 20% validation, and 20% test sets, the strict data leakage control measures were implemented to ensure that model evaluation reflects true generalization rather than memorization of specific patterns including a hierarchical stratification method applied based on cell line and drug identity. Each partition therefore contained unique drug–cell line combinations and this guaranteed that no identical cell line–drug pair appeared across subsets, preventing the model from exploiting latent correlations associated with repeated experimental entities. All splits were generated deterministically using a fixed random seed (seed = 42) to maintain reproducibility, and five-fold cross-validation was performed under the same exclusivity constraint to further confirm model stability (The detailed evaluation results given in supplementary Table-S2). The model’s cross-validation R² varies by less than 0.1%, confirming exceptional stability and generalization. The test set R² = 0.979 aligns closely with CV results, showing no overfitting. These metrics validate that the Random Forest Regressor effectively learns the genomic-drug relationships in the GDSCv2 dataset. In addition, all feature processing operations (e.g., scaling, encoding, and imputation) were performed independently within each training split to avoid indirect leakage through data transformations or global normalization. At last, to evaluate the robustness of the blockchain verification layer for predictive AI model performance results, we constructed a small tamper test suite independent of the AI model. Starting from valid canonicalized prediction records, we generated four classes of synthetic tampering: (i) single-bit flips in the serialized payload, (ii) feature-order and JSON-key reordering prior to canonicalization, (iii) numeric perturbations that violate the fixed rounding rules, and (iv) model-version spoofing. For each variant we recomputed the salted commit using the on-chain nonce and compared it with the stored commit.

Detection outcomes were recorded as TP, FP, FN, and TN, forming a confusion matrix for the tamper-detection subsystem (not the regression model). Across all tamper types, the system detected every modification (TP = N, FN = 0) and raised no false alarms on untampered records (FP = 0, TN = N), yielding a detection rate of 100% with a 95% Wilson confidence interval of [0.99, 1.00]. This demonstrates that the canonical serialization and salted commit mechanism reliably prevents undetected manipulation of on-chain AI inference logs.

This component addresses concerns about data integrity and the potential for model manipulation, which are particularly important in healthcare and genomic applications, where predictive accuracy can directly impact treatment decisions. In case of any adversaries exists within our on-chain AI Data Model, we considered three adversary types and each mapped to targeted mitigation measures implemented in the smart contract deployed via Blockchain Governance Layer. First, a malicious researcher (insider threat) may attempt unauthorized data access or manipulation of patient records and model outputs. This is mitigated through smart contract–enforced consent policies and immutable audit logs, which validate every access request and cryptographically record all transactions, ensuring accountability and non-repudiation. Second, an on-chain observer (passive adversary) could monitor blockchain transactions to infer sensitive research or patient metadata. To counter this, the system is storing all biomedical metadata off-chain, maintaining only hashed references and pseudonymized data identifiers on-chain, thereby preserving transparency without exposing personal or genomic information. Third, a front-running adversary (active manipulator) may attempt to reorder or intercept pending transactions to gain early access to consent updates or AI inference outputs. This risk is mitigated through time-locked smart contracts, commit–reveal protocols, and transaction nonce enforcement, which preserve transaction order and prevent premature data exposure. Collectively, these controls implement a layered defense model that ensures confidentiality, integrity, and traceability across all operations, reinforcing the blockchain’s role as a trust anchor for secure, auditable, and regulation-compliant personalized healthcare.

During further evaluation, the AI model generated consistent and reliable predictions in a diverse set of genomic input samples, demonstrating its robustness in handling heterogeneous biomedical data. Each prediction output was a precise floating-point value that reflected the confidence or regression estimate of the model related to the response to treatment or the activation of the genetic pathway. Crucially, along with the raw prediction, the system also computed a unique cryptographic hash of both the input data and the prediction result. These hashes were used not only to preserve the integrity of the inference pipeline but also to ensure that asset tracking in the form of several generated results could be independently verified on-chain without revealing sensitive patient data.

From the point of view that the AI process can be audited by an independent third party, we demonstrate the aggregated viewpoint of the user, especially for clinical researchers, practitioners, and other parties involved. This verifiability adds a vital layer of trust and transparency, helping to better understand the AI decision in a federated environment. It allows individual parties, for example clinicians, to trace back and confirm that the predictions used in diagnostic or treatment decisions are authentic, untampered with, and reproducible. In real-world healthcare settings, this capability is transformative, particularly for personalized medicine, where genomic data must be interpreted with utmost precision. These verified outputs ensure that the predictions for sensitivity (e.g. LN-IC50 levels, viability scores) are reliable, helping clinicians prioritize personalized therapies. This ensures that the output is not only reproducible, but also reliable for real-world clinical interpretation, allowing clinicians to base their decisions on robust evidence.

Conclusion

The intersection of blockchain and AI in personalized medicine is emerging as a practical framework for customized treatment that demands reliable and reproducible pharmacogenomics predictions. By integrating patient-specific gene profiling with a machine learning model and decentralized audit tracking, we developed a system for AI-driven drug sensitivity prediction with verifiable output. The model enables clinicians to make data-informed treatment decisions while maintaining intelligent, ethical, and transparent practices through enforced patient consent and immutable blockchain records. Using genomic data and AI powered predictive algorithm, the system supports personalized medicine and pharmacogenomics, potentially improving treatment planning, customized therapies, and patient outcomes. The blockchain-based audit mechanism provides an immutable, traceable, and cross-checkable record of predictions, which could be extended beyond genomics to other digital multi-modal healthcare data sources, including electronic health records (EHRs), medical imaging, and patient-generated data. This intra-domain applicability of the proposed innovative model underscores its potential significance in various healthcare application scenarios. Experimental results demonstrate that predictions are generated in consecutive moments, with blockchain logs in seconds, indicating the efficiency of practicality of the system in clinical settings. In general, this framework provides a verifiable and reproducible pipeline for AI-driven drug sensitivity predictions, providing a secure foundation for broader clinical and research applications. The real-time integration of the model with hospital datasets, multi-drug analyzes, and wider adoption in clinical settings are considered ongoing work to carry forward.

Limitations

Despite its promising performance and results, the current AI with blockchain model has few limitations that must be acknowledged. The model’s predictions are highly dependent on the quality and completeness of the genomic data input, which can affect the reliability in cases of noisy or missing data. Although trained on 190 drugs across 328 cell lines data set, its coverage of rare genetic variants, additional tissue types, and broader drug classes remains limited, restricting its applicability in certain clinical scenarios, but supports adoption at a wider level, i.e., genomic centers looking for data accessibility. Also, While employing the GDSC2 dataset — one of the most comprehensive pharmacogenomics resources currently available, comprising 242,035 drug-response records across 969 cancer cell lines and 286 drugs — external validation on fully independent datasets would further substantiate the generalisability of the proposed AI model. Candidate resources for future validation include the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE), the PRISM Repurposing dataset, and the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRPv2). We note, however, that cross-dataset validation in pharmacogenomics is complicated by well-established inconsistencies in drug sensitivity measurements arising from differences in experimental protocols, dose-response curve-fitting approaches, and drug concentration ranges across studies54,55. These factors should be carefully accounted for in any future cross-dataset evaluation. We therefore regard rigorous external validation as an important and necessary avenue for subsequent work, and acknowledge it as a limitation of the current study.

Blockchain integration, though providing verifiable and immutable logging, introduces transaction costs and latency overhead, which may impact scalability for real-time or large-scale deployment. In addition, role assignments and patient consent currently require manual intervention, potentially limiting high-throughput applications. Importantly, the system has been evaluated primarily on a trained cell line dataset, and direct clinical validation with patient data is needed to confirm real-world effectiveness. Finally, extending the framework to incorporate multi-modal healthcare data such as EHRs, medical imaging, or longitudinal patient records presents respective ethical and regulatory challenges. These limitations highlight areas for future improvement while providing a clear roadmap for improving the scalability, integration, automation, and clinical applicability of the system.

Future work

Future work will focus on enhancing privacy preservation in clinical controlled integration of AI/ML through the blockchain auditing mechanism. One avenue is to extend the model to handle multi-modal healthcare data sources, enabling comprehensive EHRs, Medical Imaging, and patient-generated data analyzes across larger cohorts. Integration of real-time hospital genomic and clinical data could allow dynamic patient-specific predictions, supporting adaptive precision medicine. Privacy preservation could be further strengthened through zero-knowledge proofs or homomorphic encryption, enabling verifiable predictions without exposing sensitive patient data. Additionally, implementing and optimizing smart contract programmed for AI model’s trust, efficiency and exploration of layer-2 blockchain solutions could slightly reduce gas costs and improve throughput, facilitating deployment in real-world clinical and research environments. Finally, paving medical LLMs for federated learning to reduce bias, hallucination, and privacy preservation concerns through blockchain-auditability is also in consideration of trending research for the next.
