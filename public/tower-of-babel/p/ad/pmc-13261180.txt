Editorial commentary: From prevalence to practice – implementing familial hypercholesterolemia screening in high-risk clinics using an implementation science framework
Authors: Khalid Al-Rasadi, Anurag Mehta
Published: 2026

The study by Toor et al., published in this issue of the American Journal of Preventive Cardiology, provides powerful real-world validation of a critical implementation principle: targeted, opportunistic screening for familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) in high-risk settings is a highly effective strategy for case-finding [1]. The finding that approximately 1 in 3 to 4 patients in Qatari diabetes and cardiology clinics meet phenotypic criteria for FH is more than an epidemiological observation; it is an urgent call for systematic, integrated clinical action. This alarmingly high clinic-based prevalence must be understood within the broader regional context established by the landmark Gulf FH Registry. A multinational study, the first of its kind in the Arabian Gulf region, revealed an estimated adult FH prevalence of 0.9% (1:112) in the general population, which is about three-fold higher than global estimates [2,3]. The evidence of high yield in specialty clinics against a background of elevated regional prevalence powerfully demonstrates that the burden of undetected FH is substantial and that high-risk clinics represent a strategic starting point for systematic case-finding.

The pivotal challenge and the greatest opportunity to save lives lies in sustainably translating this detection capability into routine clinical practice. Fortunately, the recent International Atherosclerosis Society (IAS) guidance, also published in the Journal, provides a robust implementation science framework specifically designed to bridge this gap [4]. This editorial synthesizes the findings of Toor et al. and the Gulf FH Registry through the lens of implementation science, offering a practical pathway from clinical insight to population health impact.

The "Where" and "How" of efficient detection: Aligning evidence with strategy

Toor et al. have expertly identified the "where": specialist cardiology and diabetes clinics are high-yield venues for FH detection, a conclusion reinforced by the stark contrast with lower prevalence found in broader acute coronary syndrome cohorts like Gulf COAST [5]. This aligns perfectly with the IAS core implementation strategy advocating for "opportunistic detection by various healthcare professionals... in tertiary, secondary, and primary care settings [4]." The Gulf FH Registry, while revealing relatively high general prevalence, also exposed a critical "care gap" in management: among patients with definite/probable FH, only 12% and 3% achieved 2016 LDL-C targets for high and very-high ASCVD risk, respectively [3]. This profound treatment gap, even among identified patients, validates that opportunistic screening focused on enriched populations can identify significant disease burden that universal screening might miss due to resource constraints, while simultaneously highlighting that detection alone is insufficient without an integrated care pathway.

Furthermore, the authors' methodological rigor in comparing DLCN, SB, and FAMCAT tools directly addresses the "how." Their pragmatic decision to use a general population prevalence threshold for FAMCAT, rather than recalibrating for a high-risk cohort, prioritizes the tool's use as a consistent, comparable case-finder—a key consideration for health systems selecting and standardizing screening tools. This operational decision exemplifies the tailoring of evidence-based tools to local contexts, a cornerstone of implementation science.

Beyond phenotypic suspicion: Navigating the implementation pathway

The acknowledged key limitation, the lack of genetic FH confirmation, points to the next critical phase in the implementation pathway outlined by Sarkies and Jones et al., a model adopted by the IAS [4,6]. This model prescribes three stages: 1) assessing local barriers/enablers, 2) tailoring strategies, and 3) monitoring and sustaining improvements.

1. Assess Local Barriers and Enablers: The Qatari context presents unique enablers (high consanguinity facilitating cascade screening; centralized healthcare system) and barriers (need for genetic testing infrastructure, potential gaps in systematic follow-up). The Gulf FH Registry underscores regional enablers and barriers, confirming the role of high consanguinity rates and revealing systemic challenges: underutilization of high-intensity statins (62% of definite/probable FH patients), ezetimibe (33%), and PCSK9 inhibitors (1.4%), alongside poor LDL-C target attainment [3].

Critically, a structured assessment using frameworks like the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) [7] must specifically investigate a profound clinician-level barrier already quantified in the region: a severe deficit in physician awareness and knowledge. A Saudi Arabian study revealed that 93% of physicians had poor knowledge of FH, with major gaps in understanding its prevalence, inheritance, cardiovascular risk, diagnostic criteria (awareness of DLCN criteria was only 16%), cascade screening (26% awareness), and modern treatment options like PCSK9 inhibitors (23% awareness).⁸ This evidence transforms "lack of awareness" from a hypothetical barrier into a measured, urgent priority for tailored educational intervention. The phenotypic prevalence established by Toor et al. provides the foundational evidence of a "care gap." The next step is to expand this assessment to understand specific barriers at patient, clinician, and system levels; including the quantified knowledge gaps among non-lipid specialists, patient concerns about genetic diagnosis, and reimbursement for cascade testing.

2. Tailor Implementation Strategies: The IAS guidance offers a menu of evidence-based strategies [4]. For Qatar and similar settings, priority strategies emerging from Toor et al.'s work, the Gulf FH Registry, and the physician knowledge assessment should include:

•Central Coordination & Clinical Decision Support: Implementing EHR-embedded tools (like FAMCAT or DLCN prompts) within identified high-risk clinics, linked to a central FH registry. This addresses the "busy clinic" barrier and compensates for knowledge gaps by providing point-of-care guidance, integrating screening into workflow, and tracking a population with high established ASCVD prevalence (33% with premature CAD among definite/probable FH patients in the Gulf registry) [3].

3. Monitor, Evaluate, and Sustain: Implementation requires continuous evaluation. The success of embedding FH screening into clinics should be measured using frameworks like RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) [10]. Key metrics include: reach (percentage of eligible clinic patients screened), effectiveness (proportion of phenotypic cases genetically confirmed, LDL-C reduction achieved—addressing the treatment gap highlighted by the Gulf FH Registry), adoption (number of clinicians using the tool), implementation (fidelity to screening protocol and participation in education programs), and maintenance (program sustainability over time). The MyCode Community Health Initiative in the US provides an exemplar for evaluating cascade testing implementation strategies [11].

Clinical and policy implications: A call for systems change

The synergy between Toor et al.'s findings, Gulf FH Registry data, quantified physician knowledge gaps, and the IAS implementation roadmap creates a compelling, multi-layered case for systems change. For clinicians in the Middle East and beyond, the imperative is to act on the high pre-test probability in their high-risk patients. For health system leaders and policymakers, the mandate is to build supportive infrastructure:

1.Formalize Screening Protocols: Mandate or incentivize structured FH case-finding tools in diabetes and cardiology clinics, leveraging their high yield.

2.Fund Genetic Integration: Allocate resources to pair phenotypic screening with affordable genetic testing, moving from suspicion to precision diagnosis.

3.Invest in Physician Education and Support Cascade Screening: Develop, fund, and mandate comprehensive educational programs to close critical knowledge gaps among healthcare providers. Concurrently, develop and fund culturally appropriate family communication and testing programs, ensuring treatment pathways deliver intensive lipid-lowering therapy currently underused in the region.

Conclusion

Toor et al. have illuminated a hidden pool of high-risk individuals within specialty clinics. The Gulf FH Registry has quantified substantial population-level burden and consequential treatment gaps. The physician knowledge assessment has diagnosed a critical breakdown in the first step of the care cascade: clinician awareness. The IAS guidance provides the blueprint for systematic intervention. The next chapter for Qatar, and a model for the region, is to operationalize this blueprint, moving beyond documenting the problem to deliberately engineering clinical pathways. This engineering must include robust mechanisms to build clinician competency, transforming high-risk clinics from sites of missed opportunity into engines of systematic detection and care. By doing so, healthcare systems can convert the striking statistic of "1 in 3" from a measure of disease burden into a testament of effective prevention, ensuring detection becomes the first step in a lifelong journey of risk reduction for entire families.
