Exploring patient experiences of pain fatigue and physical activity in syndromic heritable thoracic aortic disease using mixed methods
Authors: Gry Velvin, Heidi Johansen, Katrine Bostrøm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe combination of chronic pain, fatigue, physical activity restrictions, and a potentially life-threatening disease poses significant challenge for individuals with syndromic Heritable Thoracic Aortic Disease (sHTAD). The primary aim of this study is to explore the interplay between chronic pain, fatigue, and physical activity in adults with sHTAD, how these factors affect daily life management. The intention is to draw on both patient-reported outcomes and the lived experiences of individuals with sHTAD to provide a comprehensive understanding of their challenges and coping strategies. A mixed-methods triangulation approach was employed, combining qualitative focus group interviews (n = 36), and a quantitative questionnaire (n = 52) including validated instruments measuring pain, fatigue, sleep problems, physical activity and multi-organ symptoms. The study primarily emphasizes the qualitative component to gain a deeper understanding of the participants` lived experiences. An overarching theme “Patients` Experiences and Coping” and three subthemes emerged from the triangulated data: (1) Health Challenges in Daily Life. 2) Balancing Chronic Pain and Fatigue with Physical Activity and Restrictions. 3) Interaction and Communication with Healthcare Providers. Quantitative analysis revealed that 79% of participants reported experienced chronic pain, while 58% reported fatigue. Both conditions were strongly associated to sleep disturbances and multi-organ involvement, with fatigue also associated with low physical activity and anxiety. The qualitative data supported these findings, with participants describing pain and fatigue as “energy drainers” that profoundly impacted family life and hindered work participation. They reported that managing the interplay of pain, fatigue, anxiety, and physical restrictions due to having a life-threatening disease as sHTAD was particularly challenging. Addressing pain and fatigue in the healthcare settings can be especially difficult in this population, as these symptoms may be overshadowed by concerns about life-threatening complications. Given the importance of physical activity in mitigating pain and fatigue, recognition and interventions aimed at breaking this vicious cycle are essential. The triangulation of quantitative and qualitative data highlights the profound impact of chronic pain and fatigue on individuals with sHTAD in association with the broader health complications. Introduction

Syndromic hereditary thoracic aortic disease (sHTAD)

Heritable Thoracic Aortic Disease (HTAD) encompasses a broad spectrum of genetic disorders primarily associated with aortic pathology, most notably aneurysms and dissections (1, 2). HTAD is classified as non-syndromic (nsHTAD) when confined to the aorta, and syndromic (sHTAD) when accompanied by extra-aortic features. Genetic testing is essential for confirming the etiological diagnosis of sHTAD (1–5). Six genes (FBN1, TGFBR1, TGFBR2, SMAD3, TGFB2, and COL3A1) are strongly or definitively associated with sHTAD1. The most prevalent forms of sHTAD are Marfan syndrome (MFS), Loeys-Dietz syndrome (LDS,) and vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS). These conditions are characterized by systemic manifestation affecting multiple organ systems, including the cardiovascular, craniofacial, ocular, and cutaneous features1–5. The most serious complications in sHTAD are related to the risk of aortic and other large artery aneurysm and dissection, necessitating life-long medical management1,4,5. Life-threatening complications may require emergency interventions, with an increased risk of morbidity and mortality1,6. Standard care involves regular cardiovascular monitoring, pharmacological blood pressure control, and, when indicated, prophylactic surgery1,7,8. During the diagnostic process, patients are frequently advised to implement lifestyle modifications, including restrictions on physical activity. Specifically, they are generally advised to avoid contact sport, limit physical exertion, and strictly monitor their blood pressure due to the heightened risk of aortic dissection9–16.

Research also indicates that physical activity can be associated with anxiety in patients with sHTAD, potentially resulting in a sedentary lifestyle12–15. This presents a significant concern, given that physical activity is known to prevent and alleviate both pain and fatigue and plays a vital role in overall health maintenance9,10,17–23. The complexity of sHTAD also impact various domains of daily life, including family dynamics21,24, education, work-life balance, and leisure activities21,24–26, ultimately affecting quality of life (QoL)27–33.

Chronic pain and fatigue in sHTAD

In recent years, chronic pain and fatigue have been increasingly recognized as significant factors influencing varies aspects of the lives for individuals with genetic disorders18–21,24,32,34–37. However, research focusing specifically on these symptoms in patients with LDS and vEDS remains limited. To date, only one small-scale primary study has investigated pain and fatigue in these populations19.

Both chronic pain and fatigue are multifactorial conditions encompassing physical, psychosocial, and social dimensions38,39. Chronic pain is defined as “an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage, or described in terms of such damage, lasting more than 12 weeks”40. Fatigue is typically described as “an overwhelming sense of tiredness, lack of energy, and feelings of mental and/or physical exhaustion”41. These symptoms appear to be particularly prevalent in individuals with sHTAD18–21,35.

In a study of the Norwegian MFS population, 64% of participants reported chronic pain42, while international prevalence rates from 47%43 to 91,5%44. In the only identified study addressing pain and fatigue in LDS and vEDS, 85% of individuals with LDS and 67% with vEDS reported chronic pain19. Similarly, severe fatigue was reported in 42% of Norwegian MFS patients20, while international estimates range from 37%37 to 88,5%44. In the same study of LDS and vEDS, 58% of individuals with LDS and 42% with vEDS reported experiencing severe fatigue19.

The exact pathophysiological mechanisms underlying pain and fatigue in sHTAD are not yet fully understood. Chronic pain has been associated with structural abnormalities such as scoliosis, dural ectasia, vertebral body deformities, and sacral erosions, as well as psychological distress and anxiety18,37. However, many studies report no clearly identifiable cause. Fatigue has been linked to sleep disturbances, multiorgan burden, cardiovascular issues, orthostatic intolerance, and anxiety35. Studies on MFS14,18,21,34,35,46, and one study of LDS and vEDS19 have identified significant associations between chronic pain, fatigue, and reduced physical activity, indicating that these symptoms may mutually reinforce each other`s negative effects13,14. Despite their prevalence and impact on daily life, chronic pain and fatigue have historically been underrecognized and undertreated in sHTAD populations18,34,35. Moreover, studies report that many patients express dissatisfaction with the medical care they receive for these symptoms (11,19,21,27,35). To adequately acknowledge the impact that pain and fatigue may exert on patients with sHTAD, it can be imperative that healthcare and social services professionals adopt a proactive, multidisciplinary and evidence-informed approach to identifying, understanding, and addressing these challenges within clinical and support settings.

The rationale for this study

The research on chronic pain, fatigue, and physical activity in sHTAD is fragmented and remains relatively underexplored. There is a notable lack of studies employing qualitative or mixed methods design to explore these symptoms in depth within this patient group18,19,21,35,37. Some studies have examined pain and fatigue in individuals with MFS; however, only one study has addressed these issues in relation to LDS and vEDS. This highlights a significant gap in the literature and lack of knowledge, particularly concerning LDS and vEDS, but also regarding MFS.

This study is part of a larger research project exploring health burden, psychosocial aspect, QoL and physical functioning in individuals with sHTAD. Several reviews27,35,47 and original research articles13,14,19,25,31,48,49 have been published as a result of this research project. Through this scientific work, we identified a significant need for more comprehensive insight and analysis of the patients’ experiences concerning the complex interplay between chronic pain, fatigue, and physical restrictions in the context of living with a potentially life-threatening condition such as sHTAD. There is a particular need to advance knowledge regarding pain, fatigue and physical challenges in individuals with LDS and vEDS, although such insight is also needed for those with MFS. Recognizing these challenges is crucial for adequately addressing the specific needs of this patient group.

The objectives and research questions

By employing a mixed-methods approach to examine the interplay between chronic pain, fatigue, and physical activity in individuals with sHTAD, this study aims to elucidate how these factors mutually influence each other and impact the management of daily life.

Research questions:

What is the prevalence of pain and fatigue among individuals with sHTAD, and how do these patients perceive the impact of these symptoms on their daily life?

Materials and methods

Study approval and ethics

The research project was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics (Health Region South-East) (2017/745). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki, the Norwegian Health Research Act, and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). The research adhered to established reporting guidelines: the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ)50, the STROBE Statement for observational studies51, and the GRAMMS framework for mixed methods reporting52. These frameworks ensure methodological rigor, transparency, reproducibility and the meaningful integration of qualitative and quantitative findings. All participants provided written informed consent. Strict confidentiality protocols were followed to protect participants ‘identities, including anonymization of transcripts, secure data storage, and limited access to identifiable information.

Patient involvement in the study

To enhance the clinical relevance of the study, a reference group consisting of three members from the national patient organization was established. The members of the reference group were included for their experiential knowledge as participants, not for conducting formal research The group was actively involved throughout the research process, contributing to the development of research questions, interview guides, and the questionnaires, as well as participating in data analysis and dissemination of results. Their involvement was essential to ensure that the study addressed patient-relevant concerns and perspectives.

Mixed methods design

This study employed a mixed method design53,54, with a primary emphasis on the qualitative component to gain in-depth insight into how individuals with sHTAD navigate chronic pain, fatigue, and physical restriction, often requiring significant adaptation and coping strategies. The quantitative data complemented and contextualized the qualitative findings by proving an overview of groups characteristics.

A cross-sectional sample of adults diagnosed with LDS, vEDS and MFS was utilized. Findings from both components were synthesized to provide a comprehensive overview aligned with the study`s objectives.

Qualitative component

Focus groups interviews were conducted to elicit in-depth insights into participants experiences, facilitating dynamic discussions and allowing for the sharing of personal perceptions in an interactive group setting55,56. Each session was conducted by pairs of experienced moderators and co-moderators, who were trained in both sHTAD and interview techniques. All moderators possessed clinical experiences with sHTAD patients and had either Master`s degree or a Ph. D. Both moderators and co-moderators undertook a specialized in-house training course designed for these specific interviews, in addition to being adept in the requisite interview techniques. No clinical relationships existed between the moderators and the participating patients.

Participants

The inclusion criteria: Adults (> 18 years) with confirmed diagnoses of MFS, LDS and vEDS who were registered at the TRS National Resource Centre for Rare Disorders in Norway were eligible to participate. The exclusion criteria included uncertain diagnoses, an inability to speak or understand Norwegian, and severe psychological conditions that could hinder participation.

Sample strategy

A hybrid strategy combing convenience and purposive sampling was employed to ensure participant diversity and enhance the study`s trustworthiness. Participants were recruited through two main channels:

(i) A workshop held in October 2017, where 22 of 24 adults (11 with LDS, 11 with vEDS) consented to participate (two individuals declined to participate, one due to time constraints, and the other because she/he did not wish to take part) (ii) A purposive sample of 70 adults with MFS recruited from the Norwegian Marfan Foundation meeting in April 2018, from which the first 14 volunteers were included. An overview of the selection process and participants characteristics is presented in Supplementary File 1.

Interview guide

A semi-structured interview guide with open-ended questions was developed based on clinical experience, input from the reference group, and relevant literature18,20,44–46. The guide was pilot tested by the reference group to ensure that it elicited meaningful responses regarding experiences of pain and fatigue in the context of living with sHTAD. Each session lasted between 90 and125 minutes, was audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim by two researchers (GV, HJ). To maintain confidentiality, transcripts were anonymized and color-coded using pseudonyms.

Analysis

Data were analysed using Inductive Systematic Text Condensation (ISTC)57, involving the following steps: (i) initial overview reading (ii) identification and coding of meaning units (iii) condensation into themes and categories, and (iv) synthesis into overarching themes (see Supplementary File 2). The analysis focused on credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability57. Two researchers (GV, HJ) independently conducted the analysis, resolving discrepancies in collaboration with the reference group and a third researcher (KB) until consensus was reached.

Quantitative component

The quantitative component utilized a study-specific questionnaire developed based on insights from the qualitative findings, relevant literature, and feedback from both professional experts and the reference group. The questionnaire included several standardized, validated instruments and was pilot tested by members of the patient organization to ensure clarity and relevance.

Participants

In January 2018, a questionnaire was distributed to 70 patients with molecularly confirmed LDS and vEDS, registered at the TRS National Resource Centre. Each participant received written information, a consent form, the questionnaire, and a prepaid return envelope. Patients with MFS were not included in this study, due to that a separate quantitative study on this population had recently been conducted 58.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire included items on demographics variables (age, gender, residence, family history, education, work participation, and disability status), and clinical variables (aneurysm and surgeries). Sleep problems were assessed through a binary yes/no item. Physical activity was assessed using questions from the Norwegian HUNT study59. Pain was measured using the Brief Pain Inventory [60] and the Standard Nordic Questionnaire61. Fatigue was assessed using the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS)62, where scores above 4 indicate clinically significant fatigue. Psychological distress was measured using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)63, with scores above 8 indicating significant symptoms. These instruments have demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties [60,61,64] and are validated for use in both the Norwegian General Population (NGP) and other patient groups64–67.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated, including frequencies, proportions, ranges, and means (± SD), normality checks were conducted. Bivariate logistic regression was used to examine associations between chronic pain and demographic/clinical variables, while bivariate linear regression was used to explore associations between mean FSS score and independent variables. Due to the limited sample size, multivariate analyses were not conducted. Data were analysed using SPSS Statistics for Windows Version 25, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Mixed method integration

Integration of qualitative and quantitative findings was achieved using a joint display approach68, which involved comparing and synthesizing results from both components to identify areas of convergences, divergences and complementarity. A visual table was created to align qualitative themes with quantitative findings, enabling a more comprehensive understanding. The integration process involved iterative analysis by the research team, along with feedback from the reference group to ensure accuracy and relevance. The triangulation of qualitive and quantitative data is described in Supplementary File 3: Triangulation Analysis. This includes the identified themes supporting quantitative data, interpretations, and quotations from the data material, and the separate ISTC analysis for the qualitative data.

A translated version of the two semi structured interview guides used in the focus groups are included in Supplementary File 4, and a translated version of the questionnaire from the quantitative part is included in Supplementary File 5.

The overarching theme “Patients` experiences & coping” encapsulates the essential aspects of underlying the sub-themes identifies in this study. At its core, this theme emphasizes the interconnectedness between how patients perceive their condition and the strategies they employ to manage daily life challenges. Central to all sub-themes is the lived experiences of patients where coping implicitly emerges in their daily management and interpretation of their circumstances.

Health challenges in daily life

Both the quantitative and qualitative data indicated that chronic pain and fatigue have comprehensive impact beyond the medical perspective, incorporating social, emotional, and function dimension for people with sHTAD. In the quantitative analyses, 79% of participants reported chronic pain, and 58% experienced severe fatigue. Severe fatigue was also significantly associated with cardiovascular symptoms (p = 0.001), anxiety (p = 0.001), and chronic pain (p = 0.002) and low physical activity level (p = 0.014) (See Table 3).

Despite that more than 40% reported higher education, only 27% were employed full-time, while 66% received disability benefits. The average age of workforce exit was 41 years. Fatigue (p = 0.035) was significantly associated with less work participation.

The participants in the focus groups echoed these findings, describing pain and fatigue as ongoing challenges, especially when combined with concerns about managing a life-threatening condition such as sHTAD. Nevertheless, many participants claimed that pain and fatigue were the most difficult symptoms to manage in daily life. They emphasized that while the life-threatening symptoms, such as cardiovascular complications may evoke immediate concern, these symptoms are often managed in a clinical setting and treated with interventions that offer a sense of control. In contrast they described that pain and fatigue, are persistent, everyday experiences that permeate all aspects of life, making them harder to alleviate through medical interventions. They described these symptoms as chronic, unpredictable, and often resistant to conventional treatment, leading to ongoing physical and psychological discomfort.

“Even though I ‘often anxious that my aorta might rupture or something else serious might happen, I try to forget about in daily life. But the pain and fatigue are so limiting, and won`t leave me alone” (Female with LDS).

Particularly fatigue was described as especially challenging to manage in the workforce. They emphasized that pain sometimes can be mitigated through medication or adjusted to physical activities, while fatigue often lacks clear triggers and could persist despite rest, making it difficult to anticipate or alleviate. They also described that in contrast to pain, which may fluctuate in intensity, fatigue often create a constant sense of exhaustion that impair cognitive function, focus and productivity. Some stated that the incomprehensibility and inexplicability of fatigue can make it harder for employers and colleagues to understand, making it more difficult get accommodation and support. Participants that experiences fatigue and cognitive symptoms described that they are hesitant to disclose these issues to their employer, often due to stigma or concern about career progression. The combination of mental and physical exhaustion, along with the unpredictability of fatigue was emphasized as particularly difficult to manage in the workplace.

“I really don`t know how I`m going to manage working. I want so badly, but sometimes I can`t get out of my bed, and other days my mind is so foggy that I can`t function at work. I don`t understand why, and neither does anyone else” (Female with MFS).

Balancing chronic pain and fatigue with physical activity and restrictions

In the quantitative analyses 88,5% (n = 45) reported that they had been recommended to restrict their physical activity because of risks associated with the diagnosis, and 77% had modified their habits. Higher physical activity level was associated with lower score of fatigue (p = 0.033) and anxiety (p = 0.021), not chronic pain.

Most of the participants in the focus groups confirmed that they had received recommendations for physical restrictions to limit both the intensity and types of physical activities. However, many found it challenging to manage these recommendations in daily life. Some also reported that they had received conflicting advice from different professionals, which created confusion and uncertainty.

“Yes, I`ve received different recommendations from different doctors, maybe they don`t really know? I really want to stay in good physical shape, prevent fatigue and pain, but it`s so hard to know how to do that” (Male with LDS).

Most participants were concerned with physical activity and its importance for maintain fitness, with several also believing that it helped alleviate pain and fatigue. However, a challenge was the experience of anxieties related to physical activity, particularly related to blood pressure regulation and understanding what constitutes overly intensive static exercise. They tried to identify appropriate and health-promoting activities, but the fear of exacerbating their condition or triggering a life threating event leads to uncertainty about how safely engage in physical activity. This situation was described as even more intricate because chronic pain and fatigue not only reduced their functional capacity but also diminished their motivation to remain physically active. They highlighted a paradox; activities that could potentially alleviate these symptoms might be risky, creating a sense of helplessness in managing the underlying condition and the associated pain and fatigue. For some this resulted in prolonged inactivity. Others described it as a vicious cycle in which physical restriction, pain, and fatigue reinforce each other, further impairing physical function in daily life. The psychological burden and complexity of managing an invisible yet life-threatening condition were emphasized by many. They felt alone with the difficult task of balancing their desire to improve or maintain physical health through activity with the constant need to adhere to medical advice that emphasised caution.

“Yes, I`ve been given advice about physical restrictions, such as not exercising too hard, avoiding heavy lifting, and keep my blood pressure down. But I find it hard to put this into practice. I get nervous about exercising, often checking my pulse and pay attention to symptoms, which makes me anxious. So, it`s actually often easier to just skip exercising to avoid those worries, despite that activity might help with both pain and fatigue” (Male with vEDS).

Interaction and communication with healthcare professionals

In the quantitative analysis, 87% of the participants reported having annual monitoring in specialized healthcare services for cardiovascular symptoms and over 70% were also followed by their general practitioner for their sHTAD. Few had reviewed multidisciplinary rehabilitation programs, and one-third expressed unmet rehabilitation needs (advice on physical activity and exercise related to potential cardiovascular issues, pain and fatigue, as well as work-related rehabilitation and recovery after cardiovascular surgery). None described that they received regular follow-up for musculoskeletal issues, and few had contact with physiotherapist. These results were also confirmed by the focus group interviews. Most participants expressed satisfaction with their cardiovascular monitoring they received, with some also having regular follow-up with an ophthalmologist, particularly those with MFS. Some had tried physiotherapy, but most expressed dissatisfaction because the physiotherapist`s lack of knowledge about their disease and how to manage the physical restrictions. Despite that many reported chronic pain and fatigues, none reported receiving any form consultations of monitoring for these symptoms. Participants described this as an under-communicated and under-recognized issue in the contact with healthcare professionals, often overshadowed by more severe symptoms of the disease.

“It`s not easy to talk about; fatigue and chronic pain are so vague and unclear, so I understand that the professionals don`t always get it. I wonder if I could get some help managing the pain, dealing with fatigue, and getting advice on how to be more active and safely exercise. I suppose I should just ask during my next hospital appointment” (Female with LDS).

The participants in the focus groups felt that discussing fatigue could easily be perceived as a lack of initiative or motivation, and pain as expression of complaining or whining. Limited understanding and disregard from others affected both their self-esteem and identity negatively. Others chose to conceal their symptoms rather than disclose them, despite feeling increasingly exhausted. The participants emphasized the importance of being recognized for these symptoms, as they had significant impacts on daily life and could be detrimental to health due to reduced physical activity.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to use a mixed-methods approach to explore how patients with sHTAD experience the interplay between chronic pain, fatigue, and physical activity restriction while living with a potentially life-threatening condition. Our findings confirm that the prevalence of pain and fatigue is substantial among individuals with sHTAD. Moreover, our results indicate that most individuals experience challenges related to navigate activity restrictions and modify their physical activity accordingly. The interplay of chronic pain and fatigue appears to intensify these challenges already posed by the underlying medical condition. Addressing these issues in clinical settings may be especially difficult, as symptoms such as pain and fatigue are often overshadowed by the focus on preventing life-threatening complications. However, it sems crucial that these symptoms are acknowledged and manged effectively, as inactivity may lead to serious health consequences for individuals with sHTAD.

Health challenges in daily life

Our findings highlight the profound impact of chronic pain and fatigue on various aspects of daily life, consistent with studies of other chronic conditions and genetic conditions69–71. These symptoms can create a vicious cycle, affecting physical, emotional, cognitive, and social functioning, which adds to the complexity of daily life management. In line with previous research69–74, our study found that chronic pain and fatigue compromise individuals` ability to maintain daily routines, fulfil social roles, and participate in work life.

Participants described how the unpredictability of symptoms, along with the emotional burden of living with a life-threatening condition, led to exhaustion, helplessness, and heightened psychological distress. Many expressed ongoing worries about their health and fear of physical activity. This anxiety not only depleted their energy but also intensified feelings of vulnerability and reduced self-efficacy.

Despite living with a potentially life-threatening disease, many participants stated that chronic pain and, particularly fatigue were the most difficult symptoms to manage in daily life. Fatigue was described as severely impairing cognitive functions such as concentration, memory, and problem-soling, contributing to reduced function in both social and professional settings. These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that fatigue is a key factor contributing to diminished work participation20,21,25,26. Several participants in our study described that they were hesitant to disclose these issues to their employer, often due to stigma or concern about career progression. This lack of disclosure may prevent appropriate accommodation and contribute to underrecognition of the impact of job performance. For some this man disrupt career trajectories or even job loss. This may represent a key contributing factor to the high rates of early disability pension observed among individuals with sHTAD25,26.

Balancing chronic pain and fatigue with physical activity and restrictions

Living with a serious aortic condition, such as sHTAD entails adhering to various physical restrictions aimed at preventing life-threatening cardiovascular events, including aortic dissection or rupture9,10,12. In one study of people with LDS and vEDS, participants reported having received advice regarding as many as 76 different physical restrictions14. Our findings support that patients are frequently advised to limit physical activity, but many report difficulties in interpreting and implementing these recommendations in daily life. Several also described lack of tailored guidance and uncertainty about what constitutes health-promoting activities. This, combined with anxiety related to physical exertion, chronic pain and, fatigue, may contribute to inactivity and a sedentary lifestyle. Moreover, psychological stress may intensify the perception of pain and fatigue, contributing to a self-reinforcing cycle that makes symptom management increasingly difficult 71,73–76. Although physical activity is widely recognized as an effective intervention for managing musculoskeletal pain and fatigue22,23, individuals with sHTAD face a complex dilemma: how to reconcile activity restrictions with the potential benefits of physical activity. Pain and inactivity may form a self-perpetuating cycle. When physical activity is avoided, muscle weakness, joint stiffness, and overall physical deterioration may worsen, which in turn can increase the intensity and frequency of pain and fatigue episodes [22,76]. This is especially concerning in individuals with connective tissue disorder, where pain may be stem from joint instability, hypermobility or recurrent injuries77.

Our results suggest that the complexity of managing a potentially life-threating condition, together with chronic pain, fatigue, and anxiety related to physical activity, makes it particularly challenging to identify suitable forms of activity that relieve symptoms without compromising safety. Addressing these challenges requires a nuanced approach that considers both the physical restrictions necessary for cardiovascular safety, and the importance of pain and fatigue management.

Interaction and communication with healthcare professionals

Most participants reported attending annual cardiovascular monitoring with specialized healthcare services. However, few had received clinical assessment or follow-up care related to musculoskeletal issues, pain, and fatigue. Our findings indicate that communicating about pain and fatigue with healthcare professionals may be particularly difficult for individuals with life-threatening conditions such as sHTAD. Clinical consultations often prioritize the prevention of acute cardiovascular complication, which may lead both patients and providers to overlook or minimize the importance of pain and fatigue.

Patients may be reluctant to raise these concerns, fearing that such symptoms will be viewed as secondary or insignificant in the context of a potentially life-threatening diagnosis. This pattern is consistent with findings from studies on cancer [70,78] and rheumatoid arthritis [69], where patients reported hesitancy to discuss pain or fatigue due to the fear of appearing weak, complaining, or burdensome to the healthcare system. In cancer care, patients often refrain from reporting these symptoms out of concern that doing so might delay or alter their treatment, due to the belief that fatigue is inevitable and untreatable, or simply a reluctance to be seen as complaining70.

Communicating barriers, combined with a limited expertise among healthcare professionals in supporting symptoms management 35,69,70,79, may contribute to the underrecognition and undertreatment of pain and fatigue. This can leave patients feeling that these symptoms are their sole responsibility to manage and accept, despite their significant impact on functioning and QoL.

Clinical implications and direction for further research

Health professionals may play a critical role in addressing these challenges faced by individuals with sHTAD by recognizing that, although not life-threatening, chronic pain and fatigue significantly impact patient`s QoL. Pain and fatigue present unique challenges for this group, primarily due to anxiety related to physical activity and difficulties in translating the advice of physical restrictions into practice, coupled with the uncertainty regarding the scientific basis for the recommendations, as no international guideline currently exists. This uncertainty can lead to inactivity, triggering a vicious cycle of increased pain, fatigue and further inactivity. Given the importance of physical activity in mitigating pain and fatigue 80,81, recognitions and interventions aimed at breaking this cycle are essential. By explicitly asking about these symptoms during consultations, clinicians can encourage patients to share their experiences without feeling that their concerns are secondary. Creating an environment where patients feel comfortable discussion all aspects of their health, including symptoms like pain and fatigue, and how to interpret advice of physical restrictions, is essential for comprehensive care. Collaboration with multidisciplinary teams can be key to addressing the complex needs of these patients, ensuring that both physical and psychosocial aspects are considered. Educating patients on coping strategies, pacing, and lifestyle adjustments may help them to better manage pain and fatigue in their daily lives.

Further research is needed to assess the long-term effectiveness of tailored interventions for managing pain and fatigue in this population group. Additionally, developing clear guidelines on how to balance physical activity with safety concerns remain critical. Further studies could also investigate the role of psychological interventions in reducing the anxiety associated with physical restrictions and improving QoL for individuals with sHTAD.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the inclusion of participants with medically confirmed diagnoses, from all health regions in Norway, ensuring a broad and representative sample. In the questionnaire component, we utilized measurement instruments validated for the Norwegian population, and the focus groups interviews were conducted by experienced professionals and researchers. The results from both components demonstrated convergent validity, complementing each other, and the results might contribute to insight to a field that has been scarcely studied. Another key strength is the adherence to established reporting guidelines, including COREQ for qualitative studies, STROBE for quantitative research, and GRAMMS for mixed methods research. By following these frameworks, the study ensures methodological rigor, transparency, and comprehensive integration of qualitative and quantitative findings. This enhances the reliability and reproducibility of the research, facilitating comparison with similar studies and contributing to best practices in mixed method research.

However, integration of qualitative and quantitative data can be complex. Ensuring rigor and validity across both components can be challenging, and there is a risk of bias during this process. For example, the conceptual understanding of chronic pain, fatigue, and physical activity may differ between standardized questionnaire responses and the more nuanced accounts provided in the qualitative findings. Therefore, reconciling potentially differing results between the qualitative and quantitative data may complicate the overall conclusions and limit the dept of conclusions. The relatively small sample size in the quantitative phase also reduces the statistical power of the study, particularly in the absence of a priori power calculation, limiting the generalizability of the quantitative findings. A further limitation concerns the selection process, as all participants were registered at a national resource centre. This may have led to the inclusion of individuals with particularly complex medical needs. At the same time, it could also reflect a sample of resourceful and motivated individuals who actively sought registration and chose to participate in research. Moreover, since the study only included participants from Norway, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Similar studies conducted in other countries may yield different results due to differences in healthcare systems, cultural contexts, or patient experiences.

Conclusions

Our mixed methods study revealed that pain, fatigue, and the difficulty of balancing physical activity poses considerable challenges for patients with MFS, LDS and vEDS. The high prevalence of pain and fatigue, and their profound impact on daily life, work performance, family dynamics, and QoL are particularly striking. Managing pain and fatigue, along with a potentially life-threatening aortic condition with physical restrictions, makes it difficult for these patients. Despite the ongoing uncertainty regarding the medical and pathological mechanisms behind pain and fatigue, thorough evaluation and continuous monitoring of these symptoms are crucial. Many patients expressed a strong need for more personalized diagnostic information, greater psychosocial support, and tailored guidance in identifying suitable health-promoting activities. A more comprehensive understanding of the complex interaction between physical limitations, symptom management, and the psychological burden of living with a potentially life-threatening condition is needed. Further research and knowledge are essential for healthcare professionals, patients, and caregivers to develop the necessary health related competence to address pain and fatigue in individuals with sHTAD.
