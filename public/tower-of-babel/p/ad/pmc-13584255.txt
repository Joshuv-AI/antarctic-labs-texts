Dynamic shifts in cutaneous bacterial and fungal communities throughout human aging: a pilot study
Authors: Tingting Li, Xin Ge, Ganglian Chen, Zhijian Huang, Yuying Li, Zhe Wan, Ruoyu Li, Ruojun Wang, Wenjie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe skin microbiome plays an important role in aging, yet most aging biomarkers predominantly focus on gut bacteria, overlooking the skin microbial communities, especially its fungal component.ObjectivesTo comprehensively profile the skin bacterial and fungal microbiome across age, sex, and anatomical sites (sun-exposed forehead vs. non-sun-exposed back) and develop an integrated microbial model for age prediction.MethodsA total of 160 skin swabs from 80 healthy individuals stratified into four age groups (centered at 10, 30, 50, 70 years) were conducted by DNA sequencing for microbial analysis. An age-predictive model was built using a random forest classifier trained on bacterial and fungal composition data.ResultsWe found clear age- and sex-specific differences in the skin microbiome. Fungal diversity was significantly higher in females, while bacterial diversity decreased markedly around age 30 in both sexes. Malassezia dominated fungal communities; its abundance peaked at 30 years, declining with age, especially on female foreheads. Age-dependent shifts occurred in dominant Malassezia species (e.g., M. globosa in children, M. arunalokei in the elderly). Bacterial communities shifted from diverse childhood profiles (e.g., Pseudomonas, Streptococcus) to Cutibacterium dominance in young adulthood, which declined in older individuals. Correlation analysis revealed stronger age-microbe associations in males. Finally, we developed a predictive model using four key microbial markers—Lactarius (fungus), Chryseobacterium, Gordonia, and Psychrobacter—that showed good performance in age-group classification (AUC = 0.97).ConclusionCollectively, these findings reveal distinct age- and sex-related patterns in the skin microbiome, highlight the importance of including fungi in microbiome studies, and demonstrate the potential of microbial profiles as candidate age-associated signatures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05355-3.

Introduction

The human microbiome plays a pivotal role in both health and aging, offering unprecedented potential as a predictive marker for the aging process [1]. Recent studies found that gut microbial diversity changes with advancing age [2, 3], with specific bacterial taxa, such as Escherichia coli, Bifidobacterium, Lachnospiraceae, and Clostridiaceae, emerging as promising biomarkers for predicting age [4, 5]. Despite these compelling findings, investigations into the role of the skin microbiome in aging remain relatively limited.

The skin microbiome is not merely a passive bystander—it is integral to maintaining cutaneous homeostasis and overall skin health, as highlighted by numerous studies [6, 7]. Its composition is shaped by a multitude of intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including age, sex, anatomical location, genetic background, and environmental exposures [8–10]. Our prior studies have demonstrated that imbalances in the skin microbiome are closely linked to several dermatological conditions. Specifically, fungal dysbiosis—particularly involving Malassezia species—has been associated with the development of seborrheic dermatitis and atopic dermatitis [11–13].

Recent studies have placed growing emphasis on the link between the skin microbiome and the aging process [14]. One study found that the skin bacterial microbiome outperformed that of the gut and oral cavity in predicting age [15]. Compared with younger individuals, elderly subjects demonstrated increased skin bacterial diversity and a decreased abundance of lipid-dependent bacteria such as Cutibacterium spp [16, 17].

Despite these promising insights, the fungal aspect of the skin microbiome remains an underexplored frontier. To date, only three studies have explored the link between age and the cutaneous fungal microbiome. One study employed traditional culture-based methods to investigate Malassezia species [18], while the other two, although providing valuable insights, were limited by small sample sizes and lacked integrated bacterial-fungal analyses designed to develop robust, age-predictive models [19, 20].

In this study, we comprehensively profile the fungal and bacterial microbiome of healthy individuals across four age groups and both sexes. We also developed an integrated microbial model to predict age group. To account for environmental influences on skin aging, we analyzed microbial communities from both sun-exposed (forehead) and non–sun-exposed (upper back) skin sites. These findings aim to deepen our understanding of the role of the skin microbiome in the aging process and lay the groundwork for future microbial interventions to promote skin health.

Methods

Participants

We recruited 80 healthy volunteers from North China between April and June 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the authors’ institution, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Detailed demographic characteristics and sample information are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Subjects with active skin disorders or systemic diseases (including autoimmune diseases, malignant tumors and diabetes) were excluded from the study. The study population comprised elderly individuals undergoing natural aging, and stringent inclusion criteria were applied to minimize confounding factors and ensure sample consistency: (1) no clinical signs of premature or pathological aging; (2) no dermatological conditions at the skin sampling site; (3) no history of excessive sun exposure or cosmetic dermatologic procedures at the sampling site; (4) no use of topical or systemic antibiotics or antifungal agents within three months prior to sampling; and (5) no cleansing or washing of the sampling site within 24 h before specimen collection.

Sample collection

Participants refrained from washing their face and body for 24 h prior to sampling. Skin swab samples were collected from the forehead and upper back using sterile cotton swabs pre-moistened with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Each sampling site was swabbed ten times with consistent pressure. Swab tips were detached and placed into tubes containing 1.5 mL PBS. Samples were stored at − 20 °C until DNA extraction.

DNA extraction

Mechanical disruption of samples was performed using 5-mm stainless steel beads in a TissueLyser system (QIAGEN, Germany) at 30 Hz for 10 min. Genomic DNA was subsequently extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). DNA samples were stored at − 80 °C prior to downstream analyses.

Gene amplification, library preparation and sequencing

The fungal ITS1–5 rDNA region and the bacterial 16 S rRNA V3–V4 fragments were amplified using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Amplicons were separated on 2% agarose gels, excised, and later purified with the QIAGEN Gel Extraction Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). Sequencing libraries were then constructed with the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free kit and quantified using both Qubit fluorometry and quantitative PCR (qPCR). Finally, paired-end sequencing was carried out on the NovaSeq 6000 platform at Novogene (Beijing, China).

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw sequencing data were first demultiplexed according to sample-specific barcode sequences and PCR primers. After removal of barcode and primer sequences, paired-end reads were merged using FLASH (v1.2.11) [21]. Residual primer sequences were further trimmed using Cutadapt. The merged reads were subjected to quality control using fastp (v0.23.1) to obtain high-quality clean reads [22]. Low-quality reads and reads containing ambiguous bases were removed during preprocessing. Chimeric sequences were subsequently identified by comparison against the SILVA database (for bacterial 16 S rRNA sequences) and the UNITE database (for fungal ITS sequences) and were removed to generate effective tags.

Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were inferred from effective tags using the DADA2 plugin implemented in QIIME 2 software (Version 202202). DADA2 was used for sequence denoising, error correction, and ASV generation.

Taxonomic assignment was performed in QIIME 2. Bacterial 16 S rRNA sequences were annotated against the SILVA database (release 138.1), whereas fungal ITS sequences were assigned using the UNITE database (version 9.0).

The average sequencing depth was approximately 50,000 reads per sample for both bacterial 16 S rRNA and fungal ITS sequencing datasets.

Machine learning-based age prediction model

To develop an age-group classification model, we adopted a two-step analytical framework: microbial features were first screened using a random forest classifier, and the selected features were then entered into a multivariable logistic regression model for age-group classification.

Specifically, the dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%) using stratified sampling via the caret package (version 6.0–94) [23]. The random forest model was implemented using the randomForest package (version 4.7–1.1) [24] in R software (version 4.3.0) [25], and features were selected based on Mean Decrease Gini scores. To ensure computational reproducibility, the random seed was fixed at 123. A multivariable logistic regression model was subsequently constructed based on the selected microbial features.

Model performance was evaluated through Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and confusion matrices using the pROC package (version 1.18.5) [26]. For age-group classification, we employed a multiclass ROC approach, reporting Area Under the Curve (AUC) values with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to assess the model’s discriminative performance.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R software (version 4.3.0) [25]. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Depending on the data distribution and study design, differences between groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 80 participants were recruited, and 160 microbial samples were collected. Participants were stratified into four age cohorts (10, 30, 50, and 70 years ± 3 years), with 20 participants included in each group. These age groups were selected to represent key physiological stages across the human lifespan, including childhood, young adulthood, middle age, and elderly age. For each individual, microbial samples were collected from the forehead (sun-exposed area) and the back (non-sun-exposed area). The gender distribution was balanced across the groups.

Sex and age differences in microbial diversity

There was no significant variation in the Shannon or Simpson diversity of bacterial communities between the sexes. In contrast, fungal diversity was consistently higher in females, as indicated by both diversity indices (Fig. 1A-B). This sex-related distinction was further supported by Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) analysis, which showed a tendency toward sex-associated distribution patterns in fungal communities, whereas bacterial communities exhibited no obvious separation (Fig. 1C-D). These findings suggest that bacterial community diversity does not differ substantially between sexes, whereas fungal community diversity appears to be relatively higher in females.

Overall, bacterial community diversity appears to be more sensitive to age-related physiological and environmental changes, whereas fungal community diversity is more strongly influenced by sex. This discrepancy may reflect distinct colonization strategies and physiological dependencies, such as lipid utilization, between bacteria and fungi.

Age-related microbiome composition changes

Fungal communities

To explore the skin microbiome associated with physiological aging, we analyzed the composition of fungal and bacterial communities on both sun-exposed (forehead) and non-sun-exposed (back) skin sites in healthy individuals across different physiological age groups.

In the analysis of the fungal microbiome, Malassezia was identified as the predominant genus across all age groups (Fig. 3A–D). Malassezia, a lipid-dependent microorganism, shows peak relative abundance in young adults around the age of 30, followed by a gradual decline with increasing age. By analyzing age-related trends in individual fungal genera across different sexes and anatomical sites (Fig. 3E-F), we observed that the relative abundance of Malassezia on the forehead decreased with increasing age specifically in females, a pattern not observed in males. In males, especially in children, the genera Issatchenkia and Lactarius were significantly more abundant compared to females but decreased to nearly undetectable levels in adulthood. Conversely, female children exhibited a relative enrichment of Alternaria. Overall, Alternaria, Blumeria, and Candida were more prevalent in females than in males. Comparative analyses of other genera are provided in Supplementary Figure S1 and S2, which demonstrates broadly consistent distribution patterns across different anatomical sites.

Bacterial communities

We further analyzed the genus-level composition of the skin bacterial microbiome across healthy individuals of different physiological age groups. As shown in Fig. 4A–D, we observed that the composition of the skin bacterial communities in childhood differed significantly from that in young and middle adulthood. Specifically, the pediatric skin microbiome exhibited greater diversity, with Pseudomonas as the predominant genus, followed by Streptococcus. However, the relative abundance of these genera declined sharply in adulthood, shifting toward a dominance of Cutibacterium species. With age, Cutibacterium abundance declined, while other genera generally showed an upward trend.

Similarly to the fungal community, we observed a consistent age-related trend in bacterial communities across different anatomical sites, regardless of sex (Fig. 4E-F). Some bacterial genera were more abundant in females than in males, such as Chryseobacterium, while others showed the opposite trend, such as Gordonia. Staphylococcus exhibited an age-dependent sex pattern—being more abundant in females during childhood and shifting to higher levels in males with increasing age. To provide a broader context, the relative abundances of the top 15 bacterial genera across age groups are presented in Supplementary Figures S3 and S4.

Age-dependent alterations revealed by LEfSe analysis

To investigate species-level changes in fungal and bacterial communities associated with age, Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was employed. The resulting Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) scores, calculated through linear regression, quantify the contribution of individual species to the differences observed across age groups. Using an LDA threshold of 4.0, we identified distinct colonization patterns.

For the fungal microbiome, LEfSe analysis revealed that Malassezia globosa predominated in children, particularly females (Fig. 5A–B). In young and middle-aged adults, Malassezia sympodialis and Malassezia restricta were the dominant species (Fig. 5B, D), while Malassezia arunalokei was more abundant in elderly individuals, especially on the back (Fig. 5B–D), highlighting a dynamic, age-dependent shift in fungal composition. These findings highlight dynamic, age-dependent shifts in fungal colonization at the species levels.

As bacterial communities did not display distinct age-related patterns at the species level, we focused our analysis on genus-level differences. LEfSe analysis revealed that in individuals in their 30s, Cutibacterium was the dominant genus in females at both the forehead and back sites (Fig. 5A–B), while males showed enrichment of both Cutibacterium and Lawsonella (Fig. 5C–D). These findings highlight coordinated, age- and sex-dependent shifts in the skin bacterial microbiome at the genus level.

Age-group prediction model performance

Subsequently, we performed Spearman correlation analysis between age and the relative abundance of the top 30 most abundant bacterial and fugal genera across all participants. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, a total of 14 bacterial genera showed significant correlations with age. Among them, Corynebacterium and Cutibacterium showed significant positive correlations with age in male skin. In contrast, genera such as Pseudomonas, Streptococcus, and Stenotrophomonas showed significant negative correlations, suggesting a decline with increasing age. Interestingly, these age-associated shift was more prominent in males: 12 bacterial genera were correlated with age in males (2 positively and 10 negatively), whereas only 6 genera showed correlations with age in females (3 positively and 3 negatively).

For the fungal community (Fig. 6B), genera such as Malassezia exhibited age-related enrichment in male skin, while Issatchenkia and Lactarius were negatively correlated with age. However, only three fungal genera exhibited significant age-related correlations in female subjects. Collectively, these findings highlight a significant and diverse relationship between skin microbial genera and chronological age.

To evaluate the association between skin microbial composition and age groups, we constructed a microbiome-based age-group classification model using microbial abundance profiles. Four key genera—Lactarius (coefficient = -2.055084), Chryseobacterium (-3.455774), Gordonia (-2.208921), and Psychrobacter (2.714412)—were identified as major contributors in the model (Table 1). The model demonstrated strong classification performance, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.97 achieved in the test set (Fig. 6C), indicating high discriminatory ability for age-associated microbial patterns. These findings suggest that skin microbiome profiles may provide candidate microbial signatures associated with age groups.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the skin microbiome across different physiological age groups, sexes, and anatomical sites, highlighting distinct patterns in bacterial and fungal community dynamics. Genus- and species-level analyses revealed age-specific colonization patterns for both bacteria and fungi, with notable site- and sex-dependent differences. Importantly, we developed a microbiome-based age-group classification model that demonstrated high accuracy (AUC = 0.97), highlighting several key microbial genera as potential age-related biomarkers.

Consistent with previous studies [16, 27–30], we found that bacterial diversity was particularly sensitive to age-related changes, exhibiting a marked shift and significant decline in young adults. Further analysis at the genus level revealed that this reduction in diversity was primarily driven by the enrichment of Cutibacterium in the skin of this age group. This microbial shift may be linked to increased sebum production during young adulthood, as Cutibacterium thrives in lipid-rich environments. Notably, this feature was highly consistent across both sexes and various anatomical sites, suggesting it may represent a common characteristic of the skin bacterial microbiome’s maturation and stabilization following puberty.

Previous studies investigating age-related changes in the skin fungal microbiome remain scarce, often focusing on a narrow age range and lacking systematic comparisons between sexes and between sun-exposed and non–sun-exposed anatomical sites [19, 20]. Our study provides a more comprehensive and fine-grained analysis by stratifying participants into four age groups (10, 30, 50, and 70 years) and simultaneously considering both sex and anatomical site. This multi-factorial design enabled us to uncover several novel insights into the dynamics of the skin fungal microbiome across the human lifespan.

Notably, we found that fungal diversity was strongly associated with sex, with females consistently exhibiting higher diversity than males. At the genus level, lipid-dependent Malassezia species were significantly enriched in the skin of 30-year-old males. However, this enrichment was not observed in females; the abundance of Malassezia on the female forehead showed a decreasing trend with age. We speculate that this sex-specific divergence may be partially explained by greater attention to facial hygiene among females [31, 32] and by hormonal differences, particularly the higher androgen levels typically found in young males [33–35]. Overall, these findings indicate that, compared to bacteria, fungi may be more susceptible to both extrinsic (e.g., hygiene practices) and intrinsic (e.g., hormone levels) factors shaping the skin ecosystem.

At the species level, an intriguing finding was the age-associated shift in dominant Malassezia species. For example, Malassezia globosa predominantly colonized the skin of female children, while Malassezia restricta was significantly enriched in middle-aged individuals and Malassezia arunalokei mainly colonized the skin of elderly individuals. Notably, these age-related colonization patterns were especially pronounced in non–sun-exposed areas (back). Previous studies have suggested that ultraviolet A (UVA) and ultraviolet B (UVB) exposure may influence the growth of Malassezia species [36], and that certain Malassezia species can produce pigments and fluorochromes with potential ultraviolet (UV)-filtering properties [37, 38]. However, differences between forehead and back skin likely reflect multiple physiological and environmental factors beyond sun exposure alone, including variations in sebum production, skin hydration, temperature, and local microenvironment. Taken together, our findings suggest that anatomical-site-specific factors may contribute to age-related alterations in Malassezia composition.

A key highlight of this study is the development of the first age group-prediction model based on an integrated analysis of both bacterial and fungal components of the skin microbiome. Among the key genera identified, Lactarius, Chryseobacterium, Gordonia, and Psychrobacter are age-associated and may represent candidate microbial markers for further investigation. Notably, Lactarius, a genus of Basidiomycota fungi known for secreting milky latex when damaged [39], showed a marked decline with age. While antioxidation is a key mechanism in preventing skin aging and certain species of Lactarius are known to contain antioxidant compounds [40–42], direct evidence linking these compounds to skin aging remains limited. Our findings suggest a possible association between Lactarius-related metabolites and age-associated skin microbial changes, although further mechanistic studies are required. Chryseobacterium is a Gram-negative bacterium found in soil, water, and healthy skin, though it can act as an opportunistic pathogen under certain conditions [43–45]. Some species of Chryseobacterium can produce keratinolytic enzymes capable of degrading keratin without compromising the skin barrier [46, 47], suggesting a possible association with skin physiological status. Its decreasing abundance with age observed in our study may relate to the age-related decline in skin hydration, given its preference for moist environments [48]. With the improvement of sequencing technologies, two other low-abundance bacterial genera, Gordonia and Psychrobacter, were included in our model, suggesting their potential involvement in skin aging [49–52]. Overall, our predictive model incorporates one fungal and three bacterial genera, among which the fungal genus Lactarius and the bacterial genus Chryseobacterium may be of particular interest for future studies on skin aging. Further exploration of these genera or their metabolites may not only deepen our understanding of the skin microbiome’s contribution to the skin aging process but also provide a basis for future mechanistic or translational studies.

However, given the relatively small sample size and the lack of external validation, further studies with larger and more diverse cohorts are warranted to validate the robustness and generalizability of the predictive model developed in this study. In addition, potential heterogeneity within the pediatric group, including developmental and physiological variation, should be considered when interpreting the findings. Furthermore, the causal relationships and functional significance of the identified microbial associations remain unclear. Future studies incorporating multi-omics approaches and functional validation are needed to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of host–microbiome interactions during the skin aging process.
