Effortful Control but Not Negative Affectivity Predicts Intensity of Restricted Repetitive Behaviors in Toddlers With Neurodevelopmental Conditions
Authors: Suzanne L Macari, Grace K Bell, Catherine Bianco, Sarah Hamilton, Mariana Torres-Viso, Kelly K Powell, Angelina Vernetti, Leah AL Wang, Chelsea Morgan, Megan Lyons, Julian R Hinz, Thomas V Fernandez, Katarzyna Chawarska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRestricted repetitive behaviors (RRBs) are prevalent across neurodevelopmental conditions. It has been hypothesized that greater severity of RRBs reflects elevated distress or diminished regulatory capacity. To test these hypotheses, prospective associations between early negative affectivity (NA) and effortful control (EC) and later RRB severity were examined in a transdiagnostic sample of toddlers with and without autism.MethodParticipants were 238 toddlers with neurodevelopmental conditions (autism: n = 165; non-autism: n = 73). NA and EC were assessed at time 1 (T1) (mean [SD] age =24.4 [5.2] months) using the Toddler Behavior Assessment Questionnaire or Early Childhood Behavior Questionnaire. At time 2 (T2) (mean [SD] age = 42.2 [7.3] months), severity of RRBs was assessed using the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) and Autism Diagnostic Interview–Revised (ADI-R). Multivariate regression models tested whether T1 NA and EC predicted time 2 RRBs, controlling for age, sex, nonverbal developmental quotient, T1 RRBs, and visit year.ResultsHigher EC at T1 predicted lower ADI-R RRB scores at T2 (p < .001) but was not associated with ADOS RRB scores (p = .621). Higher EC was associated with ADI-R lower intensity of compulsive adherence to nonfunctional routines or rituals (p = .001), stereotyped repetitive motor mannerisms (p < .001), and preoccupation with parts of objects or their nonfunctional elements (p = .002), but not circumscribed interests (p = .266). T1 NA was not associated with T2 RRBs on either measure.ConclusionRegulatory and affective traits show differential longitudinal associations with RRBs, highlighting a potential role for EC in their emergence or maintenance. Interpretation of null findings for NA is limited by unmeasured early environmental influences on the development of anxiety.Diversity & Inclusion StatementWe worked to ensure sex and gender balance in the recruitment of human participants. Restricted and repetitive behaviors (RRBs) represent a highly heterogeneous group of behaviors and include unusual sensory interest, stereotyped movements, and restricted interests and activities.1, 2, 3 RRBs represent one of the core symptom domains in autism spectrum disorder (autism),4 but are also manifest in other neurodevelopmental conditions and in children developing typically.5, 6, 7, 8 In some children, RRBs are mild and do not interfere with daily activities; however, in other children, the intensity of RRBs affects the ability to function adaptively. Introspective accounts by autistic children and adults indicate that, in some cases, performing stereotyped behaviors can be experienced as calming or enjoyable9,10 or as helpful to increase focus, to alleviate boredom, to disengage from stressful events,11, 12, 13, 14 or to cope with anxiety and reduce uncertainty.15 Consequently, it has been hypothesized that RRBs may serve regulatory functions,16 though empirical evidence remains limited.17, 18, 19, 20

Regulation of behavior and attention is subserved by processes referred to as executive functions (EFs) in older children or effortful control (EC) in younger children. EF skills include working memory, cognitive flexibility, inhibitory control, planning, problem solving, emotional regulation, and attentional control.21 Atypical development of these regulatory functions is thought to interfere with learning and purposeful execution of complex action plans on both motor and cognitive levels and is observed in young children with multiple neurodevelopmental conditions.22 Robust evidence suggests associations between weaker EF skills and higher intensity of RRBs in older children and adolescents, regardless of diagnosis.23, 24, 25 Much less is known about the association between executive functioning and RRBs in younger children. In a longitudinal study of autistic preschool-age children, EF impairment was not associated with Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) RRB scores measured 2 years later,26 but given the very small sample size, significance of the finding remains to be clarified.

Similar to EFs, numerous studies report positive associations between intensity of RRBs and anxiety symptoms in autism3,27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 and in other neurodevelopmental conditions.33 The associations with anxiety are generally small to moderate across RRB subtypes, and the directionality of the effect remains unclear. Most prospective studies investigated RRBs as predictors of later anxiety symptoms34, 35, 36, 37 or examined their bidirectional associations,38, 39, 40 with inconsistent findings owing to varying ages, measures, and analytical approaches.

Although anxiety symptoms and executive dysfunction are typically not diagnosed in infants and toddlers, their temperamental precursors, negative affectivity (NA) and EC, can be measured reliably and are relatively stable into school age.41, 42, 43 Temperament refers to biologically based patterns of reactivity and regulation of attention, emotion, and behavior that are relatively stable across early childhood.44, 45, 46 NA encompasses emotional behaviors such as sadness, frustration, shyness, and soothability; elevated levels of NA in early childhood is a well-established risk factor for development of later anxiety symptoms in the general population.47, 48, 49 EC involves the ability to regulate attention, including orienting, sustained attention, and inhibition of distractors as well as flexibly shifting attention between tasks,50, 51, 52 and is considered the early childhood analog of executive function.53, 54, 55, 56, 57

Several studies examined links between EC or NA and RRBs in young children. In typically developing preschool-age children, lower EC was concurrently associated with higher RRB scores on the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS).58 To our knowledge, there have not been any studies that prospectively examined EC and RRBs in very young children with autism or other neurodevelopmental conditions. Prospective links between early childhood NA and later RRBs have been examined in infants with a family history of autism or prematurity. In toddlers with and without a family history of autism, higher levels of shyness and perceptual sensitivity, which are components of NA, measured at 24 months were associated with higher RRB SRS scores at 36 months.59 Similarly, perceptual sensitivity measured at 24 months correlated positively with ADOS RRB scores at 36 months in children born very preterm.60 Lastly, in 18-month-old younger siblings of children with autism, intensity of escape during a negative emotion induction task was associated with higher RRB scores at 24 months.61 It is unclear, however, if the findings from children with a family history of autism generalize to clinical samples of children with autism and other neurodevelopmental conditions. Moreover, none of the studies controlled for initial RRB levels or regulatory functions, so the extent to which these NA features uniquely contribute to RRBs over time has yet to be determined.

Taken together, evidence in older children points to concurrent associations between RRBs and both elevated anxiety symptoms and EF vulnerabilities. Only a few studies examined ties between developmental precursors of EFs and anxiety and the emergence of RRBs in early childhood. These studies are suggestive of prospective links between elevated NA indices and higher RRB scores later on; the evidence regarding EC and RRBs is extremely limited. The existing studies are characterized by small samples, enrollment of infants with elevated probability of developing developmental concerns, and great variability in both predictor and outcome measures. Even though both NA and EC indices are thought to be related to RRBs, their contribution is usually considered separately. Lastly, despite reports that RRBs are present across neurodevelopmental conditions, transdiagnostic research on RRBs is rare.

The current study fills these empirical gaps by using a large well-characterized transdiagnostic sample of toddlers with and without autism and examining joint prospective links between NA and EC in toddlerhood and RRBs at preschool age. We quantify RRBs using both observational and parent interview measures. We hypothesize that toddlers with a higher propensity to experience distress and a lower ability to regulate attention and behavior will have higher RRB scores in preschool years. In addition to controlling for developmental quotient (DQ), sex, and age, the analyses consider potential contribution of changes in referral and diagnostic practices as well as measurement tools over the recruitment period.

Method

Participants

The study was approved by the Human Investigations Committee of the Yale School of Medicine, and informed written consent was obtained from all parents before testing. Participants were 238 toddlers who took part in a prospective study of social and emotional development and were recruited between February 2000 and October 2018 for their time 1 (T1) visit (at a mean age of approximately 2 years). Toddlers were included if, after their parents or medical/educational professionals expressed concern about their development, they were subsequently diagnosed with autism or another developmental condition and retained the diagnosis over the 2 assessment points. The exclusion criteria were a known genetic syndrome associated with neurodevelopmental conditions, gestational age less than 32 weeks, suspected or diagnosed hearing loss or visual impairment, history of head trauma with loss of consciousness, and nonfebrile seizure disorders. The participants were categorized as American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, or White based on the National Institutes of Health Policy on Reporting Race and Ethnicity Data. In this study, 76% of parents reported their child’s race as White, 3% as Black, 6% as Asian, and 8% as multiracial. Information was not available for 6% of participants. Among the participants, 13% were identified as Hispanic and 59% as non-Hispanic; ethnicity information on 28% of the participants was not available.

The assessment battery was administered by PhD-level clinicians with established reliability for the administration of measures. The battery at T1 (mean [SD] age = 24 [5] months) and time 2 (T2) (mean [SD] age = 42 [7] months) consisted of the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL),62 a standardized assessment of developmental motor, verbal, and cognitive skills (yielding verbal DQ and nonverbal DQ); ADOS, ADOS-Generic,63 or ADOS-2nd edition,64 capturing social affect and RRB symptoms; and Vineland Adaptive Behavior Scales (VABS),65, 66, 67 a measure of adaptive functioning in the socialization, communication, daily living, and motor domains. At T2, the Autism Diagnostic Interview–Revised (ADI-R)68 was administered in addition to the MSEL, ADOS, and VABS.

The clinical best estimate diagnoses were made by multidisciplinary teams experienced in early diagnosis of neurodevelopmental conditions lead by PhD- or MD-level clinicians. The classification was based on results of developmental, language, and diagnostic assessments; review of medical and developmental history; DSM-5 criteria; and clinical judgment. Diagnoses assigned before 2013 were based on DSM-IV criteria and included autism, pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified, and Asperger syndrome (57% of cases). The clinical best estimate diagnoses were assigned in 2013 and later were based on DSM-5 criteria (43% of cases). All these diagnostic categories were converted to an autism label. Based on the available data at T1 or T2, of 239 toddlers, 165 were diagnosed with autism and 73 were diagnosed with other, nonautistic developmental or emotional conditions (non-autism). The latter group included children with global delays (n = 17), language delays (n = 18), and a variety of developmental concerns including elevated attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder and anxiety symptoms, motor delays, emotion regulation issues, social and communication concerns, disruptive behaviors, and unspecified neurodevelopmental conditions (n = 38).

Temperament Measures

Parents provided information at T1 regarding their child’s temperament using either the Early Childhood Behavior Questionnaire (ECBQ)44 (n = 102) or its earlier version, the Toddler Behavior Assessment Questionnaire-Supplemental (TBAQ-S) (Goldsmith & Rothbart, 1999, unpublished data) (n = 136). The ECBQ and TBAQ-S measures consist of 18 and 13 scales, respectively, all rated on a 7-point Likert scale. The composite scores for NA and EC were computed based only on the scales that overlapped between the 2 versions of the instrument. NA was based on the Discomfort, Sadness, Perceptual Sensitivity, Shyness/Social Fearfulness, Soothability, and Anger/Frustration scales, and EC was based on the Inhibitory Control, Attention Shifting, Attention Focusing, and Low-intensity Pleasure scales. See the online Supplement for further details. Measure version (ECBQ, TBAQ-S) was included in the analyses as a covariate.

RRB Measures

At T2, RRBs were measured using the ADOS-Generic or ADOS-2nd edition and ADI-R. The ADOS is a standardized observational assessment measure consisting of play probes aimed to elicit social and repetitive behaviors relevant to the diagnosis of autism. At T1, RRBs were based on Toddler Module (45%) and Module 1 (55%), and at T2, RRBs were based on Modules 1 (50%) and 2 (50%). The ADOS provides raw and calibrated algorithm scores for social affect and RRB domains. The RRB domain scores are based on items capturing sensory interests (item D1), motor stereotypies (item D2/D3), and restricted interests and repetitive behaviors (item D5), as well as unusual intonation or stereotyped speech (item A3/A4). To harmonize the scores across ADOS versions and modules, we retained the higher score on the D2 and D3 items of the ADOS Toddler Module as the motor stereotypy measure. Following standard practice, scores on these items were summed and converted into ADOS RRB calibrated scores spanning 0 to 10, with a higher score indicating greater symptom severity.

The ADI-R is a standardized semistructured parent interview capturing social, communication, and repetitive behaviors relevant to diagnosis of autism. The restricted, repetitive, and stereotyped patterns of behavior domain scores encompass 4 categories of symptoms: C1, unusual preoccupation and circumscribed patterns of interest; C2, compulsive adherence to nonfunctional routines and rituals; C3, stereotyped, repetitive motor mannerisms; C4, preoccupation with parts of objects or nonfunctional elements of materials. Higher RRB scores on the ADI-R scales indicate greater frequency and interference levels. The primary outcome measure consisted of a sum of the C1 to C4 items. In the secondary analysis, we examined associations between the NA and EC scores and each of the C1 to C4 domain scores of the ADI-R. Higher RRB scores on the ADOS and ADI-R indicate greater frequency and interference levels of the symptoms. For the sake of brevity, we refer to the RRB scores as capturing intensity of the targeted behaviors.

Statistical Analysis

In the preliminary analyses, dichotomized (0/1) occurrence of RRBs based on the ADOS and ADI-R at T1 and T2 were compared between the groups using χ2 analysis. The presence of RRBs based on the ADOS and ADI-R was indexed by the RRB score greater than 0 on either instrument. Group differences in RRB scores were analyzed using analysis of variance. Group differences on the NA and EC temperament scores were examined using group × version general linear models. Raw associations between the dependent and independent variables were examined using Pearson’s r correlation coefficients. In the primary analysis, the contributions of the T1 NA and EC temperament scores to the T2 ADOS and ADI-R RRB scores were examined using multivariate linear regression analysis. Whenever indicated, variables known to be associated with intensity of RRBs, such as chronological age, sex, and nonverbal DQ,69 were included a priori as covariates in addition to the visit year and the version of the temperament questionnaire (source). In a follow-up analysis, we examined links between NA and EC scores and the ADI-R RRB subdomain C1 to C4 scores using analogous multiple regression models as above. With a sample size of 238 and 8 predictors in the multivariate regression analysis, we have a power of 0.80 to detect f = 0.26 (small) or larger effects. Data processing and analysis were performed using SAS 9.4 software.70

Results

Preliminary Analysis

Phenotypic Presentation of Toddlers in Autism and Non-Anonutism Groups

Girls comprised 20% (n = 33) of the autism group and 32% (n = 23) of the non-autism sample (χ21 = 3.72, p = .054). Similar to the autism group, the non-autism group exhibited a wide range of cognitive, verbal, social, and regulatory skills (Table 1, Figure 1A, B). The autism group had significantly greater delays at both time points in all developmental skills indexed by the MSEL (verbal and nonverbal cognitive abilities), as well as in adaptive social and communication skills as evidenced by scores on the VABS. In addition, toddlers in the autism group showed more significant autism-related behaviors on both the ADOS and the ADI-R compared with the non-autism group. The effect sizes for these differences displayed in Figure 1 demonstrate that the non-autism group represents children with various neurodevelopmental conditions and does not consist solely of children with the broader autism phenotype.

RRBs in Autism and Non-Autism Groups

Based on the ADOS at T1, the prevalence of RRBs was 98% in the autism group and 63% in the non-autism group (χ21 = 56.82, p < .001) (Figure 1). At T2, these proportions were 98% and 67% (χ21 = 45.21, p < .001). The frequency estimates based on the ADI-R at T2 were 99% and 77% in the autism and non-autism groups, respectively (χ21 = 33.57, p < .001). There was high agreement between the ADI-R and the ADOS classification regarding presence vs absence of RRBs, as 82% of children were noted to have some form of RRBs by both instruments (χ21 = 1.72, p = .19). The intensity of RRB symptoms based on the ADOS RRB Calibrated Severity Scores was higher in the autism vs non-autism group at T1 (F1,237 = 132.22, p < .001) and at T2 (F1,23 = 144.67, p < .001), as well as at T2 based on the ADI-R (F1,237 = 74.84, p < .001). The ADOS RRB Calibrated Severity Scores and ADI-R RRB scores were significantly correlated (r238 = 0.38, p < .001). Taken together, the RRBs were highly prevalent in both groups at both time points; however, they were more prevalent and severe in the autism group. At T2, there was high agreement between the parent report and direct observation on the overall presence of RRBs, but the consistency regarding the intensity rating was moderate.

Group Differences in Temperament Scores

A group (2) × measure version (2) general linear model analysis on NA scores indicated that after controlling for the effects of age, visit year, sex, and nonverbal DQ, there was no significant effect of group (F1,230 = 0.27, p = .602), measure version (F1,230 = 1.05, p = .306), or their interaction (F1,230 = 0.01, p = .913). An analogous analysis of the EC scores indicated a significant effect of group (F1,230 = 12.10, p = .001), but no significant effects of the measure version (F1,230 = 0.12, p = .712) or their interaction (F1,230 = 0.03, p = .868) (Figure 2A, B). Thus, the autism and non-autism groups had comparable scores on the NA scale, but the autism group had lower EC scores than the non-autism group, indicating a greater degree of vulnerability in this domain. Discussion

This study examined prospective links between temperament and restricted and repetitive behaviors in toddlers with a range of neurodevelopmental conditions including autism. As previously reported, repetitive behaviors were common in toddlers both with autism and with other neurodevelopmental conditions, highlighting their transdiagnostic nature.5,8,69,71,72 By definition, all children with ASD displayed some form of RRBs, and among children with other conditions, more than 60% of the sample displayed RRBs—an estimate consistent with other studies in similar populations.5,69 EC skills were significantly lower in the autism group than in the non-autism group,73 but the NA scores were comparable in the 2 groups.73,74 We hypothesized that toddlers, who are more prone to distress in response to everyday challenges, and children who have lower ability to regulate their attention and behavior would have higher levels of RRBs by the time they reach preschool age. Our study offers partial confirmation of the hypotheses. Although lower temperament-based EC indeed predicted higher levels of RRBs later on, there was no evidence of a unique contribution of NA to later RRBs. Specifically, after controlling the effects of NA along with the covariates, which included nonverbal DQ and ADOS RRB level at T1, only early EC skills predicted RRB scores at T2. Specifically, lower EC was associated prospectively with higher global RRB scores on the parent interview measure (ADI-R), and when the subtypes of RRBs were considered, lower EC predicted higher scores on compulsive adherence to nonfunctional routines or rituals, stereotyped repetitive motor mannerisms, and preoccupations with parts of objects or nonfunctional elements. There were no significant effects with regard to unusual preoccupations and circumscribed interests, perhaps because these behaviors are known to develop or intensify later in development. These results suggest that early EC vulnerabilities are associated with a broad range of repetitive behaviors in young children. The study extends prior reports of concurrent relations between EFs and RRBs in older children23 and motivates further investigations into mechanistic links between precursors of executive dysfunction in toddlerhood and intensity of RRB symptoms in neurodevelopmental conditions.

Given that temperamental characteristics are considered to be biologically based, stable, and measurable in early infancy,41, 42, 43 our data suggest that intensity of RRBs is, at least in part, related to underdeveloped EC skills. We hypothesize that toddlers with lower ability to regulate their attention and behavior may have difficulties selecting stimuli or motor actions that are relevant to a task or struggle to inhibit stimuli or motor actions that are prepotent but no longer relevant. In addition, lower EC skills may impact reward value learning, making predictions, and planning. In this context, repetitive behaviors may, on one hand, be related to difficulties inhibiting irrelevant or repetitive thoughts and behaviors, but on the other hand, serve to stabilize the otherwise less predictable stream of experiences, further reinforcing and solidifying repetitive behavioral patterns. This hypothesis would be best tested by examining whether preventive strengthening of early EC skills may lessen frequency or intensity of repetitive behaviors in children with neurodevelopmental conditions including autism.

Concurrent links between RRBs and anxiety symptoms in autism have been shown in school-age children,3,28,30, 31, 32 and emerging evidence suggests such links in toddlers carrying risk factors for neurodevelopmental conditions.59,60 Here we examined, to our knowledge for the first time, prospective links between temperamental precursors of anxiety symptoms and various forms of RRBs in toddlers with neurodevelopmental conditions. We hypothesized that toddlers who are prone to distress may be more likely to engage in repetitive behaviors, presumably to self-soothe or to distract from stressful situations. When NA was considered in isolation, there was a modest association with later compulsions and adherence to routines, which weakened when NA and EC were considered jointly in the analytical models. Nonetheless, this is an intriguing finding given that links between anxiety and insistence on sameness, routines, and rituals were previously reported in older children.30,38,75 No links between early NA and other forms of RRBs, including motor stereotypies, unusual interests, and preoccupation with parts of objects, were detected. Given that symptoms of anxiety develop throughout early childhood as a function of not only early temperamental vulnerabilities but also other factors such as attentional biases and exposures,76, 77, 78, 79, 80 the effects observed here warrant further investigation when these additional factors can be taken under consideration. Taken together, our results do not support the hypothesis that an early temperamental propensity toward distress predicts intensity of later RRBs.

The RRBs were captured using 2 key sources of data, direct observation and parent interview. The agreement between these 2 sources regarding the presence of RRBs was very high, with more than 80% of preschool-age children rated as having RRBs on both instruments. The agreement regarding the intensity of RRBs was moderate and consistent with prior studies.81,82 This is understandable given that parent report is based on an extended time frame and triggering contexts that may be harder to replicate during a clinical assessment. Not surprisingly then, the effects of temperament on intensity of RRBs were observed only when parent interview data were considered. Although it is plausible that this is due to potential shared method variance—both of the measures are based on parent report—there are several mitigating factors. The temperament and RRB instruments were administered, on average, 18 months apart; tap into distinct developmental domains; and crucially differ in how information is extracted (parent report questionnaire vs structured interview). However, the effect of method observed here highlights the need for development of multimodal assessment tools for clinically important outcomes, such as RRBs.

We considered broadly defined constructs of NA and EC and global RRB intensity scores. Future studies should examine contributions of specific subscales within these temperament constructs to the intensity of specific subtypes of RRBs. Our sample included toddlers who displayed developmental concerns not only at the age of 2 but also 1 to 2 years later. The sample did not include toddlers with transient developmental delays or those with risk factors for developmental conditions but without clear clinical symptoms. Children with known genetic syndromes, children with extreme prematurity, and children with major neurological/sensory impairments are also not well represented. These exclusions may affect generalizability of the present findings and prospective links between temperament and RRBs in such samples. Whereas EC is robustly associated with later executive functioning,83 NA is only 1 of many factors contributing to the emergence of anxiety symptoms in later childhood.76, 77, 78, 79, 80 Additional factors such as parental practices, attentional biases, and early exposure to trauma may need to be present to facilitate development of anxiety symptoms in preschool-age children.79,80 Measurement of multimodal precursors of anxiety in toddlerhood may yield a different pattern of results. Lastly, the nature of the reciprocal association between NA and EC and their joint role in emergence of psychopathology are ongoing topics of discussion,77,84, 85, 86 and future studies are needed to disentangle this association in larger, more balanced samples enriched for children with specific executive functioning and anxiety-related vulnerabilities.

The study demonstrates that in toddlers with neurodevelopmental conditions, lower EC but not NA predicts higher levels of repetitive behaviors. This effect is robust and present after controlling for known contributors to RRB intensity, such as nonverbal DQ, initial levels of RRBs, and sex. These findings motivate mechanistic investigations into shared pathophysiology underlying development of EC and repetitive behaviors in transdiagnostic samples. The study also offers support for clinical studies exploring whether targeting precursors of executive dysfunction in toddlers with neurodevelopmental conditions may lead to lowering the interference level of RRBs in later childhood.75 Such studies may identify specific skill areas that would help modulate expression of RRBs in young children.
