Associations between various lipid indices and coronary collateral circulation in patients with acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tianfeng Zhang, Chenghua Wang, Zhenghui Wang, Long Zhao, Hao Xia, Yiming Wang, Chunyang Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to evaluate the association between multiple lipid indices and coronary collateral circulation (CCC) in patients diagnosed with acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).MethodsThis was a cross-sectional retrospective study involving 421 patients with STEMI who underwent coronary angiography between January 2022 and December 2024. Participants were categorized into a poor CCC group (Rentrop grade 0–1) and a good CCC group (Rentrop grade 2–3) according to Rentrop grading criteria. The following lipid parameters were evaluated as both continuous and categorical variables: total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C), lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], apolipoprotein B (ApoB), apolipoprotein A-I (ApoA-I), non-HDL-C/HDL-C, ApoB/ApoA-I, atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), and lipoprotein composite index (LCI). The associations between these lipid indices and CCC status were assessed using multivariate logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsMultivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that higher HDL-C quartiles were significantly associated with reduced odds of poor CCC (odds ratio [OR]: 0.544, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.351–0.771, P < 0.05), whereas elevated LDL-C (OR: 29.299, 95% CI: 3.562–240.976, P < 0.05), non-HDL-C (OR: 50.140, 95% CI: 5.408–464.834, P < 0.01), and non-HDL-C/HDL-C (OR: 4.510, 95% CI: 1.186–25.368, P < 0.05) quartiles were significantly associated with increased odds of poor CCC. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that LDL-C (cutoff: 3.265, AUC: 0.647, 95% CI: 0.573–0.721, P < 0.001), non-HDL-C (cutoff: 2.735, AUC: 0.752, 95% CI: 0.688–0.816, P < 0.001), and non-HDL-C/HDL-C (cutoff: 2.393, AUC: 0.686, 95% CI: 0.611–0.761, P < 0.001) exhibited favorable predictive performance for poor CCC. Stratification analysis showed that the highest prevalence of poor CCC was observed in patients with concurrently elevated levels of LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and non-HDL-C/HDL-C.ConclusionSeveral lipid indices—including LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and the non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio—are significantly associated with impaired CCC in patients with STEMI. Notably, non-HDL-C exhibits the strongest association with CCC dyscrasia and therefore warrants early clinical attention.

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a severe cardiovascular event posing a major public health burden [1]. By 2030, the annual incidence of AMI in China is projected to exceed 23 million cases—a rise consistently associated with ongoing urbanization and the high population-level prevalence of behavioral exposures, including smoking, physical inactivity, and unhealthy dietary patterns [2]. Although primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the established standard of care for patients with acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), myocardial reperfusion and left ventricular functional recovery following PCI often remain suboptimal [3,4]. Coronary collateral circulation (CCC) refers to a network of small arterial anastomoses that form between different coronary artery territories, providing an alternative route for blood flow to ischemic myocardium distal to occluded or severely stenosed vessels [5]. The development of well-developed CCC serves as a crucial endogenous protective mechanism in patients with acute total occlusion (ATO), mitigating myocardial ischemia and significantly improving prognosis in STEMI [6,7]. However, there is considerable interindividual variability in CCC development, and the determinants influencing its formation are not fully elucidated [8].

Accumulating evidence robustly links impaired CCC formation with dysregulated lipid metabolism—including altered lipoprotein composition, oxidative modification, and inflammatory lipid signaling—not merely as a correlative finding but as a potential mechanistic contributor to collateral insufficiency [9,10]. Prior studies investigating lipid-CCC associations have largely relied on comparisons of isolated lipid parameters between patients stratified by CCC status, often in cohorts with heterogeneous etiologies, comorbidities, or reperfusion statuses [[11], [12], [13]]. Critically, no study to date has systematically evaluated or directly compared the discriminatory capacity of multiple composite lipid indices for predicting CCC status among patients with STEMI. This gap is clinically urgent: accurate preprocedural assessment of CCC could improve prediction of adverse outcomes, inform revascularization decisions—including timing and completeness of multivessel PCI—and help select candidates most likely to benefit from adjunctive therapies targeting collateral growth. In this study, we assessed four pathophysiologically informed composite lipid parameters—the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), the non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (non-HDL-C/HDL-C), the apolipoprotein B to apolipoprotein A1 ratio (ApoB/ApoA1), and the lipid combination index (LCI)—for their independent associations with CCC grade and their comparative discriminatory performance in distinguishing good versus poor CCC among a rigorously defined cohort of STEMI patients presenting with ATO. Identification of a readily measurable, biologically interpretable, and clinically actionable lipid-based biomarker would address a pivotal unmet need in interventional cardiology. Importantly, because these indices reflect dynamic, modifiable facets of lipid metabolism—responsive to lifestyle interventions, statin therapy, PCSK9 inhibition, and emerging lipid-targeting agents—our findings support the rational development of mechanism-guided strategies to enhance coronary collateral growth, thereby improving long-term ischemic resilience and clinical outcomes in patients with obstructive coronary artery disease.

Materials and methods

Study population (Fig. 1)

This was a single-center, observational, retrospective study enrolling 744 patients diagnosed with STEMI who presented with ATO in at least one major coronary artery and subsequently underwent PCI. All cases were confirmed by coronary angiography at The Affiliated People's Hospital of Jiangsu University between January 2022 and December 2024. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they experienced ischemic chest pain lasting ≥30 min but ≤12 h, accompanied by ST-segment elevation of ≥0.1 mV in two or more limb leads or ≥0.2 mV in two or more contiguous precordial leads on electrocardiogram, or new-onset left bundle branch block [14].

Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) incomplete baseline data; (2) history of acute myocardial infarction; (3) lipid-lowering therapy prior to hospitalization; (4) angiographically confirmed left main coronary artery occlusion; (5) non-culprit vessel occlusion identified during coronary angiography; and (6) type 2 myocardial infarction. After applying the eligibility criteria, 421 patients with STEMI and ATO were ultimately included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated People's Hospital of Jiangsu University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee. Clinical and laboratory collection

Basic clinical information, including age, sex, weight, height, waist circumference (WC), socioeconomic status, history of hypertension, history of diabetes, smoking status, alcohol consumption, medication use, and angiographic findings, was retrospectively collected from the electronic medical records of The Affiliated People's Hospital of Jiangsu University. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a digital scale (Tefal PP-TZ01) while participants wore light clothing and were barefoot. Height and WC were measured with non-elastic tape measures accurate to 0.1 cm. Participants stood upright with feet together during height measurement. Waist circumference was recorded at the midpoint between the lower margin of the rib cage and the iliac crest [15]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m2). Blood pressure (BP), including systolic and diastolic values, was measured twice at a 10-min interval, and the median value was used for analysis. Measurements were performed only after participants had abstained from caffeine, physical activity, and smoking for at least 30 min prior to assessment [16]. A calibrated digital sphygmomanometer (Yuwell YE900) was used for BP measurement.

Venous blood samples were collected from each participant after an 8-h fast to measure fasting blood glucose (FBS), creatinine, urea, uric acid, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and a complete lipid profile, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], apolipoprotein B (ApoB), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), and small dense low-density lipoprotein (sdLDL). Fasting blood glucose was measured using the glucose oxidase method on a Beckman Coulter autoanalyzer (AU5821). All other biochemical parameters were quantified using enzymatic assays on the same instrument.

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [17]. The AIP was computed as follows: AIP = log(TG (mmol/L)/HDL-C (mmol/L)) [18]. Non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) was derived by subtracting HDL-C from total cholesterol: non-HDL-C = TC (mmol/L) − HDL-C (mmol/L). The LCI was calculated using the formula: LCI = [LDL-C (mmol/L)/HDL-C (mmol/L)] × TG (mmol/L) × TC (mmol/L) [19]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index was calculated as Ln[TG (mg/dL) × FBS (mg/dL)]/2 [20]. Hypertension was defined as a prior diagnosis of hypertension, current use of antihypertensive medications, or a new in-hospital diagnosis based on the following criteria: repeated office measurements showing systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg [21]. Diabetes was diagnosed according to American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria, defined as fasting blood glucose (FBS) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), 2-h plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) [22]. Smoking status was assessed through a structured interview and classified into three categories: current smoker (regular smoking within the past month), former smoker (cessation more than one month prior), and never smoker.

Coronary angiography(CA) and grading of coronary callaterals

Baseline coronary angiography was performed using the Judkins technique via either the transradial or transfemoral approach, according to operator preference. To evaluate the presence and extent of CCC, angiographic images were independently reviewed by two senior interventional cardiologists in a blinded fashion. Collateral vessel grading was conducted using the Rentrop classification [23]: Grade 0 (no visible collaterals), Grade 1 (filling of side branches without opacification of the epicardial segment), Grade 2 (partial opacification of the epicardial artery with delayed filling), and Grade 3 (complete opacification of the epicardial artery beyond the occlusion). Based on this system, patients were categorized into two groups: a poor CCC group (Rentrop grades 0–1) and a good CCC group (Rentrop grades 2–3).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software (version 26.0; Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR, 25th–75th percentile). For between-group comparisons, the independent samples t-test was used when both groups exhibited normal distribution; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages and were compared using the χ2 test. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to calculate correlation coefficients for each lipid index. The association between lipid indices and CCC was assessed using both continuous and categorical representations, with categorical variables classified into four quantiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify potential factors associated with poor CCC in patients with STEMI, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. To adjust for potential confounding effects, two logistic regression models were fitted: Model 1—univariate logistic regression; Model 2—multivariate logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, BMI, WC, socioeconomic status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, eGFR, uric acid, AST, and ALT. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine the optimal cut-off values and area under the curve (AUC) of each lipid index for predicting CCC severity. Cut-off values were identified based on the maximum Youden index. LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and the non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio were categorized into low, medium, and high groups according to tertiles for inconsistency analysis. Consistency was defined as belonging to the same tertile category across variables, while inconsistency indicated membership in different tertile categories. All possible combinations of the three variables yielded 27 distinct patterns, and their associations with CCC were analyzed using a multivariate logistic regression model with adjustment for sex, history of hypertension, history of diabetes, smoking status, eGFR, uric acid, AST, and ALT. Variance inflation factor (VIF) was computed for each predictor to assess multicollinearity, and variables with VIF >5 were excluded to minimize overfitting and enhance model stability. Statistical significance was determined at a two-tailed P < 0.05.

Results

Correlation analysis of each lipid index (Table 1)

Spearman correlation analysis revealed that TC, LDL-C, Apo-B, sdLDL, and non-HDL-C were strongly positively correlated with each other. Similarly, TG, non-HDL-C/HDL-C, AIP, and LCI exhibited strong positive correlations among themselves. TC, LDL-C, and Apo-B showed high intercorrelation, as did LDL-C and TC. Furthermore, HDL-C was strongly positively correlated with LDL-C, Apo-B, sdLDL, ApoB/ApoA-1, and non-HDL-C/HDL-C. Additional clusters of strong positive correlations were observed among TC, LDL-C, Apo-B, sdLDL, non-HDL-C, and LCI; between LDL-C, Apo-B, and sdLDL; and between Lp(a) and AIP. Notably, Lp(a) showed no significant correlation with any other lipid indices.

Baseline characteristics (Table 2)

The final analysis included 421 patients with a mean age of 61.26 ± 12.11 years, of whom 357 (84.80%) were male. According to the Rentrop scoring system, patients were classified into a poor CCC group (Rentrop grade 0–1, n = 261) and a good CCC group (Rentrop grade 2–3, n = 160). Baseline characteristics, including demographic data, medical history, laboratory parameters, and angiographic findings, are summarized in Table 2. Compared with the poor CCC group, the good CCC group had a longer time from symptom onset to presentation [4 (2, 7) vs. 3 (2, 5), P < 0.05]. Regarding laboratory measurements, patients in the poor CCC group exhibited significantly higher levels of AST, ALT, TC, LDL-C, Apo-B, ApoB/ApoA-1, and non-HDL-C, as well as lower ALT/AST ratios, than those in the good CCC group (all P < 0.05). Meanwhile, each lipid index was categorized into four groups based on the quartile method: Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4. Fig. 2 illustrates the prevalence of poor and good CCC across the quartiles of each lipid index. Furthermore, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between each lipid index and CCC, with results presented in Table 3. For LDL-C, the proportion of poor CCC was significantly higher in Q3 and Q4 than in Q1 (P < 0.05). For TC, the proportion of poor CCC was significantly lower in Q3 and Q4 relative to Q1 (P < 0.05). For non-HDL-C, the proportion of poor CCC was significantly elevated in Q2, Q3, and Q4 compared to Q1 (P < 0.05). Discussion

AMI and its subsequent complications—including cardiac rupture, heart failure, and malignant arrhythmias—have become leading causes of death and disability in China [24]. CCC refers to an anastomotic network that connects the perfusion territory of one epicardial coronary artery to another. Its development provides an alternative blood supply to ischemic and hypoxic myocardium resulting from coronary artery occlusion [5]. During CCC formation, two distinct processes—arteriogenesis and angiogenesis—are involved. Arteriogenesis involves the remodeling of preexisting arterial vessels through anatomical increases in lumen diameter and wall thickness, whereas angiogenesis is defined as the sprouting and growth of new capillaries from preexisting microvessels [25]. These processes are tightly regulated by a variety of biomechanical and biochemical factors, including perfusion pressure, wall shear stress, systemic hypoxia, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and endothelial function. The development of CCC can prevent or reduce ischemic myocardial necrosis, and in optimal cases, well-developed collaterals can maintain normal myocardial function in regions supplied by stenotic or occluded coronary arteries [26]. CCC is therefore considered a key prognostic indicator in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD). A meta-analysis of 12 independent studies evaluated the impact of CCC on all-cause mortality in patients with stable or acute CAD and found significantly lower mortality in the good CCC group compared to the poor CCC group, indicating that robust collateralization is strongly associated with improved clinical outcomes [27]. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify modifiable factors associated with CCC status in this patient population. Several underlying mechanisms contributing to poor CCC in AMI patients have been identified, among which disturbed lipid metabolism plays a central role and is regarded as a hallmark of impaired vascular remodeling [28,29]. Hypertriglyceridemia is consistently associated with AMI, a relationship mediated in part by excessive release of free fatty acids and subsequent stimulation of proinflammatory cytokines, fibrinogen, and coagulation factors [30]. Previous studies have shown that conditions characterized by clusters of metabolic abnormalities—including hypertriglyceridemia (e.g., metabolic syndrome, overweight, or obesity)—exhibit a stronger association with endothelial dysfunction and impaired neovascularization [29,31]. In recent years, several novel lipid-related indices TG with other metabolic parameters (such as HDL-C and glucose) have been proposed to better stratify coronary collateral status. The TyG index has been suggested as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance [32]. Elevated TyG index is closely correlated with increased arterial stiffness and microvascular damage [33], both of which are linked to reduced coronary perfusion, diminished shear stress, and impaired arteriogenesis [34]. The TG/HDL-C ratio and AIP reflect overall atherogenic lipid profiles and the severity of insulin resistance [35]. In a retrospective study of 2540 patients with CAD, Zhu Boda et al. used logistic regression to show that an AIP >0.14 was independently associated with chronic total occlusion (CTO) lesions (OR = 1.540). Moreover, elevated AIP was associated with higher odds of post-revascularization cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality following CTO intervention [36]. Chronic exposure to high levels of TC and LDL-C leads to functional and structural vascular abnormalities, including endothelial dysfunction, subendothelial lipid deposition, plaque progression, and compromised collateral vessel development [37,38]. Hypercholesterolemia reduces endothelial nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability and suppresses expression and activity of endothelial NO synthase (eNOS), which are critical for endothelial progenitor cell (EPC) migration and vascular repair [12]. Lp(a) exhibits atherothrombogenic properties by inhibiting the fibrinolytic system and promoting thrombus formation. Lp(a) accumulates in the arterial wall, transports cholesterol, and binds oxidized phospholipids. Elevated circulating Lp(a) inhibits transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) activity, attenuates synthesis and/or release of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and decreases endothelium-derived NO production, thereby impairing angiogenesis [39]. In patients with AMI, elevated serum Lp(a) levels are closely associated with poor CCC [40]. Transgenic mouse models overexpressing Lp(a) exhibit markedly reduced recovery of blood flow after hindlimb ischemia in a dose-dependent manner [41]. The relationship between serum HDL-C levels and CCC remains controversial. In patients with stable CAD, one study reported that low HDL-C predicted poor CCC [42], but this finding has not been consistently replicated in other studies [43]. The non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio is increasingly recognized as a composite marker reflecting multiple metabolic disturbances. Its relevance in assessing atherosclerotic burden is particularly evident in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), especially regarding complication development. Studies have shown that this ratio is positively correlated with urinary microalbumin excretion in patients with T2DM, indicating a stronger association between elevated ratio values and diabetic nephropathy [44]. Furthermore, even after optimizing glycated hemoglobin and blood pressure control, continued monitoring of the non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio was associated with a significantly lower odds of major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with T2DM patients [45]. The ApoB/ApoA1 ratio reflects the balance between pro-atherogenic and anti-atherogenic lipoproteins. Walldius et al. [46] analyzed data from the AMORIS cohort (N = 120,000) and directly compared the ApoB/ApoA1 ratio with other lipid ratios—such as LDL-C/HDL-C and non-HDL-C/HDL-C—in a subgroup of 10,000 patients. Their results demonstrated that the ApoB/ApoA1 ratio had a stronger association with coronary artery stenosis severity and plaque instability than other lipid measures. This superiority may stem from the fact that ApoB represents all atherogenic particles containing apolipoprotein B (including VLDL and IDL), while ApoA1 serves as a major component of anti-atherogenic HDL. Additionally, in patients with acute coronary syndromes, the ApoB/ApoA1 ratio is independently associated with multivessel disease, plaque rupture, and coronary thrombosis [47]. The LCI was first introduced by Wu et al. to provide a more comprehensive assessment of residual cardiovascular disease burden, particularly in patients who have achieved target LDL-C levels. The LCI was first introduced by Wu et al. to provide a more comprehensive assessment of residual cardiovascular disease burden, particularly in patients who have achieved target LDL-C levels [19]. In a cross-sectional study, Si et al. [48] found that combining retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4) with LCI improved diagnostic accuracy for acute coronary syndromes. Their findings confirmed that the RBP4–LCI composite is a novel independent predictor whose association strength with adverse cardiovascular outcomes is comparable to that of established clinical conditions such as hypertension and diabetes. Similarly, Yin Boying et al. [49] demonstrated that LCI is a robust indicator of dyslipidemia-related coronary heart disease incidence, independent of whether conventional lipid markers—such as LDL-C and TC—are within normal ranges.

Given the importance of CCC in patients with ATO and the established value of lipid indices in AMI, we extended prior research by exploring, for the first time, the association between various lipid indices and CCC in AMI patients with ATO. We found a significant association between elevated levels of non-HDL-C, non-HDL-C/HDL-C, and LDL-C and the presence of poor CCC in these patients. The odds of having poor CCC were significantly higher among individuals in the highest tertile of these lipid parameters. However, the relationship between non-HDL-C/HDL-C and poor CCC was nonlinear. One potential explanation for the observed association lies in the counterbalancing effects of lipoprotein components. HDL-C is widely recognized for its atheroprotective properties, whereas atherogenic cholesterol contained in non-HDL-C fractions promotes pathological vascular changes. Therefore, a low non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio—below a specific threshold—reflects a lipid profile marked by relatively higher levels of protective HDL-C and/or lower levels of atherogenic lipoproteins. This favorable lipid equilibrium may fail to create a sufficiently pro-atherogenic environment to be strongly associated with impaired coronary collateral circulation (CCC) development. This may explain the lack of significant association in the Q2 and Q3 groups, while a significant effect was observed in the Q4 group. Notably, 200 patients (47.8%) in this study exhibited discordant distributions across the three lipid indices. To explore this inconsistency, we categorized each of the three lipid indices—non-HDL-C, non-HDL-C/HDL-C, and LDL-C—into low, medium, and high tertiles. Patients whose values for all three indices fell within the same tertile category (e.g., all low) were classified as "concordant," while those with values spanning different tertiles were labeled "discordant." All possible combinations of concordance and discordance were generated and analyzed. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to assess the associations between these patterns and poor CCC. Our results revealed that concurrent elevation of non-HDL-C, non-HDL-C/HDL-C, and LDL-C was the strongest predictor of poor CCC in AMI patients with ATO.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design inherently limits our ability to establish temporal sequence or infer causality from the observed associations. Second, although we adjusted for a range of known confounders, residual confounding may persist due to unmeasured or imperfectly assessed variables—such as dietary patterns, physical activity levels, and genetic predisposition—potentially influencing the results. Third, despite meticulous collection of clinical data, the possibility of information bias, including recall bias and misclassification, cannot be fully excluded. Fourth, the single-center setting and narrow inclusion criteria may restrict the generalizability of our findings to broader or more diverse populations across different healthcare contexts. Finally, while statistically significant associations were identified, the moderate sample size may have constrained statistical power for more granular subgroup analyses. Therefore, future prospective, multicenter longitudinal studies with larger cohorts and repeated assessments of both lipid profiles and coronary collateral circulation (CCC) are needed to confirm these findings and further investigate potential causal mechanisms.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study investigated the associations between various lipid indices and CCC in patients with AMI, using both continuous and categorical variable approaches. The results showed that LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and the non-HDL-C/HDL-C ratio had the strongest associations with impaired CCC, suggesting that these lipid parameters may warrant early clinical attention as potential indicators of poor collateral development. However, it should be noted that these findings were derived from a post hoc analysis of cross-sectional data, and therefore require confirmation in future prospective studies.
