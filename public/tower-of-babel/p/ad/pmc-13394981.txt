Phase II Trial of Nivolumab in Advanced Solid Tumors Based on Genomic Profiling: BELIEVE Trial (NCCH1901) Subcohort
Authors: Masashi Kanai, Kuniko Sunami, Tatsunori Shimoi, Satoshi Nishiwaki, Ichiro Kinoshita, Tetsuhiro Yoshinami, Chigusa Morizane, Yusuke Sato, Toshio Kubo, Mamoru Ito, Hiroshi Nishihara, Toru Mukohara, Hidekazu Shirota, Ryo Ko, Taro Shibata, Manabu Muto, Noboru Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTComprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) tests can identify putative biomarkers for immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), including tumor mutational burden (TMB); however, their clinical utility remains uncertain. In this study, the efficacy and safety of nivolumab treatment based on the CGP results were investigated using a subcohort from the BELIEVE trial (NCCH1901; jRCTs031190104). This trial aims to improve drug accessibility for patients with advanced solid tumors harboring actionable genetic variants through off‐label drug administration. Patients for whom off‐label nivolumab treatment was proposed based on CGP results were eligible. Nivolumab was administered until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. The primary endpoint was the best objective response rate (ORR) in patients with measurable disease. Secondary endpoints included progression‐free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), disease control rate (DCR), and safety. Among 60 enrolled patients, 56 patients who received study treatment were included in the analysis. TMB‐High was the most frequent biomarker prompting nivolumab treatment (47/56, 83.9%), followed by CD274 (PD‐L1) amplification (6/56, 10.7%) and CDK12 inactivating variants (3/56, 5.4%). Among 50 patients with measurable disease, the ORR was 16.0% (95% confidence interval [CI], 7.2–29.1) and the DCR was 40.0% (95% CI, 26.4–54.8). Median PFS and OS were 2.7 months (95% CI, 2.2–4.3) and 7.2 months (95% CI, 4.4–9.0), respectively. Although a subset of patients achieved durable responses, nivolumab treatment guided by CGP results demonstrated limited efficacy. The predictive value of TMB‐High may differ across tumor types, and integration with other clinicogenomic factors is warranted to improve its predictive accuracy.Keywords: biomarkers, genomic profiling, immune checkpoint inhibitors, molecular tumor board, TMB

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the treatment of diverse malignancies by stimulating antitumor immune responses [1, 2]. Since a subset of patients shows a durable response, predictive biomarkers for ICI response have been extensively investigated. First, programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) expression has been adopted as a predictive biomarker of ICIs in several tumor types such as non‐small cell lung cancer and breast cancer, whereas its predictive accuracy differs across tumor types, and some patients show robust responses despite low PD‐L1 expression levels [3, 4, 5]. Next, microsatellite instability‐high (MSI‐High) status has been established as a predictive biomarker of ICI response across diverse tumor types, leading to the tumor‐agnostic approval of the anti‐PD‐1 antibody, pembrolizumab [6, 7, 8, 9]. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) has also emerged as a tumor‐agnostic biomarker based on the results of the KEYNOTE‐158 trial [10], and pembrolizumab has now gained the tumor‐agnostic approval for TMB‐High tumors. In addition, other genomic variants, such as CD274 (PD‐L1) amplification and CDK12 inactivating variants, have been reported to predict ICI response in retrospective studies [11, 12]. However, the clinical utility of these genomic variants remains unclear and requires validation in independent cohorts that include diverse tumor types.

The BELIEVE trial (NCCH1901; jRCTs031190104) was launched as a nationwide, multi‐cohort platform study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of off‐label use of the matched agents proposed by the molecular tumor board based on the results of comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) tests [13]. In the present study, we investigated the efficacy and safety of off‐label nivolumab treatment based on CGP results using a subcohort of the BELIEVE trial.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Considerations

This study examined data from the nivolumab subcohort of the BELIEVE trial (NCCH1901; jRCTs031190104), a multi‐cohort, platform study conducted under a single master protocol [13]. The trial aims to improve drug accessibility for patients with actionable genetic variants through off‐label drug administration [13]. The trial was conducted in accordance with the Clinical Trials Act (Act No. 16 of April 14, 2017) and the Patient‐Proposed Health Services scheme (PPHS) in Japan [14]. The study protocol and informed consent documents were approved by the certified review board of the National Cancer Center Hospital (NCCH) and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare as a PPHS. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Patient Eligibility

Patients were eligible for inclusion in the nivolumab subcohort if they had histologically confirmed solid tumors, including cancers of unknown primary origin, with unresectable, progressive, metastatic, or locally advanced disease that was not amenable to, or had become refractory to, standard therapy. Eligible patients were required to have at least one evaluable tumor lesion on contrast‐enhanced CT or MRI, with or without measurable disease. The indication for nivolumab treatment was judged by the molecular tumor board at each participating institution. TMB cutoff values were not prespecified in the protocol, and the incorporation of additional biomarkers, such as CD274 amplification and CDK12 variants, was also determined by the independent molecular tumor board based on the latest evidence; no exclusion criteria regarding genomic variants were prespecified. Patients were enrolled if nivolumab was not approved for their disease at the time of enrollment and if no clinical trials or advanced medical care programs were available. Additional eligibility criteria included an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) of 0 or 1, adequate organ function, and provision of written informed consent.

Intervention

Nivolumab was administered intravenously at a dose of 240 mg every 2 weeks or 480 mg every 4 weeks. Nivolumab was provided by Ono Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd. Dose modification was not permitted; treatment interruptions or discontinuation followed prespecified criteria based on toxicity. Concomitant administration of other anticancer drugs was not allowed. Treatment was continued until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, withdrawal of consent, or the physician's decision.

Assessments and Endpoints

Tumor response was assessed radiologically every 8 weeks according to RECIST v1.1 criteria in patients with measurable lesions. The primary endpoint was the best objective response rate (ORR) at 16 weeks assessed by treating physicians in patients with measurable disease. Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), progression‐free survival (PFS), disease control rate (DCR), and safety. Adverse events were graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Statistical Analysis

As the primary analysis, a Bayesian analysis was conducted using a prior distribution with an expected objective response rate (ORR) of 30% [Beta (0.6, 1.4)], which was set to a common clinically meaningful value across all drug cohorts in the BELIEVE trial. The maximum sample size was set at 50 patients with measurable lesions, which was expected to allow for a reasonable level of exploratory analysis while minimizing the influence of the prior distribution. Interim monitoring was implemented such that early termination of the cohort would be considered if the posterior probability exceeded 95% that the response rate was above 60% or below 20%. The Clopper‐Pearson method was also used to estimate the frequentist 95% confidence intervals (Cis) for the response rate. ORR, PFS, and OS were analyzed in the full analysis set (FAS) of patients with measurable lesions. Safety was analyzed in all patients who received at least one dose of treatment (FAS).

Results

Patient Characteristics

The CONSORT diagram is shown in Figure 1, and patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. A total of 60 patients were enrolled between April 2020 and August 2021, with data cutoff in June 2025. Among the 56 patients who received nivolumab treatment, 50 patients with measurable lesions comprised the response‐evaluable FAS. Among seven patients without target lesions, six patients received nivolumab treatment and were included in the FAS. In total, 56 patients constituting the FAS were evaluated for safety. The median age was 59.0 years (range, 19–83). Among them, 40 patients (71.4%) were female and 27 (48.2%) had ECOG PS 0. Metastatic disease was present in 54 patients (96.4%). Regarding treatment history for recurrent/metastatic disease, 53 patients (94.6%) had received prior anticancer drug therapy, whereas three patients (5.4%) were chemotherapy‐naïve. Prior radiation therapy had been administered to 29 patients (51.8%). The most frequent tumor type was cervix uteri (n = 11, 19.6%), followed by ovary/fallopian tube (n = 8, 14.2%). Endocrine organs and rectum accounted for four patients each (7.1%), while breast, colon, liver/intrahepatic bile duct, and prostate cancer accounted for three patients each (5.4%). The remaining 17 patients (30.4%) had other tumor types (Table S1).

Regarding genomic biomarkers prompting off‐label nivolumab treatment, TMB‐High, including intermediate TMB, was the most frequent (n = 47, 83.9%). Other genomic variants included CD274 (PD‐L1) amplification (n = 6, 10.7%) and CDK12 inactivating variants (n = 3, 5.4%).

Efficacy

Among the response evaluable cohort, the mean of the Bayesian posterior distribution was 16.5% [Beta (8.6, 43.4)] and the ORR and the DCR were 16.0% (95% CI, 7.2–29.1) and 40.0% (95% CI, 26.4–54.8), respectively (Table 2). The patients with TMB‐High (n = 43) showed an ORR of 16.3% and a DCR of 41.9%. Among five patients with PD‐L1 amplification, one achieved a PR, while two patients with CDK12 inactivating variants showed no responses (Table 2). The median PFS was 2.7 months (95% CI, 2.2–4.3), with the 6 month and 1‐year PFS rates being 26.0% (95% CI, 14.9–38.6) and 22.0% (95% CI, 11.8–34.2), respectively. The median OS was 7.2 months (95% CI, 4.4–9.0), with the 6‐month and 1‐year OS rates being 58.0% (95% CI, 43.2–70.2) and 36.0% (95% CI, 23.1–49.1), respectively (Figure 2). Safety

In the FAS of 56 patients, grade 3 adverse events (AEs) were observed in 28.6% (95% CI, 17.3%–42.2%) (Table 3). No grade 4 or 5 AEs were observed. Grade 3 immune‐related AEs (irAEs) included aspartate aminotransferase (AST) elevation, abdominal infection, adrenal insufficiency, gamma‐glutamyltransferase (γ‐GTP) elevation, and myositis (n = 1 each, 1.8%). No unexpected signals were observed (Table 3).

Discussion

In our current study, off‐label nivolumab treatment based on CGP results showed an ORR of 16.0% and a DCR of 40.0%. The prespecified expected ORR of 30% was not achieved. Median PFS was limited to 2.7 months, and most patients experienced early progression except for a subset of durable responders. The safety results were comparable to those of previous reports, and no unexpected signals were observed. The most common biomarker prompting nivolumab treatment was TMB‐High, which could theoretically increase sensitivity to ICIs through high expression of neoantigens [16, 17, 18]. In a retrospective analysis of 1662 advanced cancer patients who underwent next‐generation sequencing (NGS), the higher TMB group showed better clinical outcomes when treated with ICIs [17]. In contrast, TMB cutoff values varied markedly between cancer types. The predictive value of TMB‐High (≥ 10 mut/Mb) for ICIs response was investigated using the cohort enrolled in the KEYNOTE‐158 trial [10], in which pembrolizumab achieved an ORR of 29% in 102 patients with TMB‐High tumors, whereas the ORR in the non‐TMB‐High group was 6% [10]. These results led to the tumor‐agnostic approval of pembrolizumab for TMB‐High tumors. However, during the enrollment period of this study (April 2020 to August 2021), pembrolizumab had not yet been approved in Japan for TMB‐High tumors, and off‐label nivolumab treatment under this trial was an alternative treatment option. Following the publication of the KEYNOTE‐158 trial results, other groups reported a positive association between TMB‐High and ICI response [19, 20, 21]. For example, Duvivier et al. conducted a basket trial investigating pembrolizumab in patients with TMB‐High tumor and reported an ORR of 11% and a DCR of 31% in the colorectal cancer cohort, and an ORR of 26% and a DCR of 45% in the non‐colorectal cancer cohort [20]. Similarly, Alva et al. reported an ORR of 21% and a DCR of 37% DCR in breast cancer patients with TMB‐High tumor [19]. Since the prespecified null hypothesis of 15% DCR was rejected in both trials, the results were interpreted in a positive manner. However, these DCRs were comparable to the 40% DCR observed in our current study. In contrast, other studies have failed to validate the predictive performance of TMB‐High. McGrail et al. analyzed the association between TMB‐High and ICI response using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data [22]. They reported that TMB‐High predicted ICI response in a limited number of cancer types, such as melanoma, lung cancer, and bladder cancer, all of which were classified as ICI‐responsive tumors. In these tumor types, neoantigen load strongly correlated with CD8‐positive T‐cell infiltration. However, in other cancers, increased neoantigen production did not necessarily translate into enhanced CD8 T‐cell infiltration, suggesting that the predictive value of TMB‐High is tumor type–dependent. More recently, Yamaguchi et al. reported a negative association between TMB‐High and ICI response in colorectal cancer using data from the Center for Cancer Genomics and Advanced Therapeutics (C‐CAT) repository in Japan [23]. A total of 77 colorectal cancer patients harboring TMB‐High with microsatellite‐stable (MSS) subtype were analyzed. Patients treated with pembrolizumab showed significantly shorter time to treatment failure (TTF) and OS compared with those treated with other conventional anticancer agents such as trifluridine/tipiracil or regorafenib. These findings suggest that the primary reason for the discrepancy between the KEYNOTE‐158 trial and our current study is mainly attributable to tumor heterogeneity among the enrolled patients, rather than differences between pembrolizumab and nivolumab.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. First, the TMB score was not routinely collected, and the cut‐off value for TMB‐High was not prespecified because this trial was designed before the tumor‐agnostic approval of pembrolizumab for TMB‐H tumor, which established the TMB‐H cut‐off value of 10 mut/Mb. Consequently, the TMB‐High status was determined by an independent molecular tumor board. Second, information on microsatellite instability (MSI) was not routinely collected. Although MSI‐High tumors may have been present among durable responders in our current study, patients with known MSI‐High were unlikely to enroll because pembrolizumab was already available as standard of care for MSI‐High tumors in a tumor‐agnostic manner during the study period. Consistent with this assumption, among 45 cases for which MSI status data were available through C‐CAT database, all were identified as microsatellite stable (MSS). Third, the sample size was limited. In particular, it was not feasible to discuss the predictive value of PD‐L1 amplification (n = 6) or CDK12 inactivating variants (n = 3) from our current results.

In summary, off‐label nivolumab treatment based on CGP results showed limited efficacy. Although TMB‐High was the most common biomarker prompting nivolumab treatment in this study, its predictive value is tumor type–dependent and could be affected by other immunological characteristics [24, 25]. Harmonization of TMB with other biomarkers is currently investigated to improve its predictive value in guiding ICI treatment.
