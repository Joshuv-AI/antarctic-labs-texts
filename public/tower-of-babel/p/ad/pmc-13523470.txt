Antifungal Activity of a Postbiotic Cell-free Supernatant of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum from Pulicaria jaubertii against Aspergillus fumigatus and Candida albicans
Authors: Faisal Mohammed Abdul AL-Nihmi, Emad Abdu Ali Alserkal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing prevalence of antifungal resistance necessitates the exploration of novel therapeutic agents from natural reservoirs. Bacteria derived from medicinal plants serve as an underexploited repository of biologically active secondary metabolites. This study investigated the chemical profile and antifungal potential of the cell-free supernatant (CFS) derived from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum (L. plantarum) strain isolated from the medicinal plant Pulicaria jaubertii (P. jaubertii) (Asteraceae) in Yemen.MethodsL. plantarum was isolated from surface-sterilized leaves of P. jaubertii and cultured to obtain CFSs. The chemical composition of the CFS was characterized using Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. Antifungal efficacy was evaluated against clinically significant isolates of Aspergillus fumigatus (A. fumigatus) and Candida albicans (C. albicans) using agar incorporation and agar well-diffusion methods, respectively.ResultsFT-IR spectroscopy indicated that the CFS is a complex mixture containing functional groups of organic acids, peptides, and carbohydrates. It exhibited dose-dependent antifungal activity, achieving 71.9% and 90.0% growth inhibition against A. fumigatus and C. albicans, respectively, at 30% (v/v). Notably, a sharp increase in efficacy occurred between 20 and 25% (v/v). We hypothesize that this threshold effect may reflect a multi-mechanistic mode of action, potentially driven by the interplay between the acidic and peptidic constituents.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the potential of L. plantarum CFS as a promising, sustainable source of multicomponent antifungal agents. This study highlights the value of integrating plant biodiversity with microbial chemistry to develop postbiotic interventions that address critical fungal challenges.

Background

Fungal pathogens constitute a significant and escalating challenge to global public health, with their clinical impact magnified by rising incidence rates and associated morbidity and mortality [1]. While numerous fungal species exist as commensals, certain pathogens can instigate severe, life-threatening invasive diseases, particularly in immunologically vulnerable hosts, such as organ transplant recipients and individuals with HIV/AIDS [2]. A. fumigatus and C. albicans rank among the most prevalent and clinically consequential opportunistic mycoses. Invasive aspergillosis represents a leading cause of infectious mortality in hematological patients [3], whereas C. albicans remains a predominant etiological agent of candidiasis, ranging from mucosal colonization to life-threatening candidemia [4].

The current pharmacopoeia of antifungal agents is limited and often beset by considerable limitations, including host toxicity, unfavourable pharmacokinetic interactions, and a narrowing spectrum of activity [5]. This predicament is exacerbated by the rapid emergence of antifungal resistance, propelled by the selective pressure of drug misuse [6].

Medicinal plants have perennially served as invaluable reservoirs for bioactive lead compounds [7]. P. jaubertii, an aromatic plant endemic to Yemen and a member of the Asteraceae family, has a documented history in ethnomedicinal practices for treating inflammatory states and infections [8]. While the phytochemical profile of P. jaubertii is well-documented [8].

In addition to plant-derived compounds, probiotic systems, particularly CFSs, have garnered scientific interest [9]. L. plantarum is a notable probiotic species that secretes a plethora of metabolic byproducts, including organic acids, bacteriocins, and bioactive peptides [10]. These metabolites, which collectively constitutes the CFS, offer distinct advantages over live probiotic administration, including enhanced stability and the elimination of microbial translocation risks in susceptible hosts [11].

Given the ethnobotanical significance of P. jaubertii and the established antimicrobial properties of L. plantarum, this study aimed to investigate the leaves of P. jaubertii to screen for the presence of L. plantarum and subsequently isolate and characterize its CFS. The principal objective was to evaluate the inhibitory activity of this postbiotic preparation against clinically relevant isolates of A. fumigatus and C. albicans, thereby exploring its potential as a novel source of antifungal agents.

Methods

Plant material and authentication

Fresh, asymptomatic leaf samples of P. jaubertii were aseptically collected from privately owned land in Rada’a, Al-Bayda Governorate, Yemen, during the spring season. Permission to access the land and collect plant material was obtained from the land owner prior to sampling. The collection of plant material complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation. The plant species was taxonomically identified and authenticated by Assoc. Prof. Dr. Abdulwareth Abdulqader Al-Moneify, Associate Professor of Botany and Microbiology at Albaydha University, Yemen. A voucher specimen was deposited in the herbarium of the Department of Biology, Albaydha University, under reference number AU-BIO-PJ-01.

Bacterial isolation and CFS preparation

To isolate plant-derived bacteria, the leaf samples were subjected to a rigorous surface sterilization protocol to eliminate epiphytic microbiota, adapted from previously described methods [12]. Initially, the leaves were washed thoroughly with sterile distilled water, followed by treatment with 5% (v/v) Tween 20 for 5 min under vigorous vortexing, and subsequently rinsed multiple times. The samples were then immersed in 70% (v/v) ethanol for 1 min, followed by five consecutive rinses with sterile distilled water. Afterwards, the leaves were submerged in 2% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 10 min. To ensure the complete removal of the sterilizing agents, the samples were finally rinsed ten times with sterile distilled water. To validate the efficacy of the surface sterilization process, 100 μL aliquots of the final rinse water were aseptically plated in triplicate onto MRS agar plates as a negative control. Following inoculation, the culture plates were maintained in an anaerobic atmosphere at 37 °C for 48 h, utilizing a BBL Gas Pak jar system. From the MRS plates exhibiting quantifiable growth, a random selection of 10 to 20 discrete colonies was extracted for subsequent refinement. To ensure culture purity, the isolated L. plantarum strains were initially inoculated into 5 mL of Oxoid MRS broth, followed by successive streak-plating procedures on solid MRS agar [13]. The bacterial isolates were putatively identified based on their morphological traits, Gram staining reactions, and catalase activity [14].

For CFS production, the identified L. plantarum strain was inoculated into fresh MRS broth and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 18 h to reach the late logarithmic growth phase [15]. The pH of the freshly prepared sterilized CFS was measured and consistently ranged between 3.8 and 4.0. To ensure optimal preservation of bioactive metabolites and to strictly preclude any potential degradation associated with freeze thaw cycles, the sterilized CFS was prepared freshly and utilized immediately for all downstream antifungal assays, without undergoing any prior storage.

Fungal isolation and identification

Clinical isolates of A. fumigatus and C. albicans were obtained from archived culture collections at a hospital in Rada’a, Yemen. A. fumigatus samples originated from endoscopy-guided sinus aspiration [16], whereas C. albicans was isolated from oropharyngeal swabs [17]. Identification was performed via a polyphasic approach. C. albicans was identified by colony morphology, germ tube tests, and chlamydospore formation [18, 19]. A. fumigatus was identified by macroscopic and microscopic features via lactophenol cotton blue staining [20].

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopic analysis

The chemical characterization of the L. plantarum CFS was conducted at King Saud University utilizing an FT-IR spectrometer (Nicolet Nexus 670) equipped with a diamond attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory. To ensure analytical precision, the ATR crystal was rigorously decontaminated with 70% ethanol and spectroscopic-grade methanol prior to each measurement, followed by the acquisition of a background spectrum. A 20 µL aliquot of the CFS was applied to the crystal and allowed to air-dry completely at room temperature, forming a thin film to eliminate water interference. Spectra were recorded across the mid-infrared range (4000 to 650 cm⁻1), with each spectrum representing an average of 32 consecutive scans at a resolution of 4 cm⁻1 to optimize the signal-to-noise ratio. Unfermented MRS broth was analyzed under identical conditions to serve as a control, allowing for the differentiation of bacterial metabolites from the culture medium. Spectral data collection and baseline corrections were executed using OMNIC software.

Assessment of antifungal activity

Activity against A. fumigatus

The antifungal activity against the filamentous fungus was evaluated via the agar incorporation method, widely known as agar dilution method [21]. To achieve final concentrations ranging from 5 to 30% (v/v), the CFS was incorporated into sterilized, molten Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA). Prior to the addition of the CFS, the molten SDA was allowed to cool to a temperature of 40–45 °C to prevent the thermal degradation of any potential heat-sensitive bioactive compounds. The mixtures were prepared to maintain a constant final volume of 15 mL per plate. The precise volumetric proportions of CFS and SDA used to attain each target concentration are detailed in Table 1. A 5 mm mycelial plug of A. fumigatus was placed at the center of all test and control plates. SDA supplemented with amphotericin B (2 µg/mL) served as the positive control. All experimental assays were performed using independent biological replicates (n = 3). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h. The colony growth was determined by measuring two perpendicular diameters of the mycelium and calculating the average. All measurements were performed by an investigator blinded to the treatment allocations. Activity against C. albicans

The activity against the yeast was determined via an agar well diffusion assay [22]. To prepare the inocula, the yeast cell suspensions were adjusted to match the turbidity of a 0.5 McFarland standard (optical density of 0.12–0.15 at 530 nm), which corresponds to a concentration of 5 × 10⁶ colony-forming units (CFU)/mL. Subsequently, a sterile cotton swab was dipped into the standardized suspension and swabbed evenly across the surface of SDA plates to achieve a confluent lawn [23]. The wells were filled with 50 µL of CFS at various concentrations (5–30% v/v). Fluconazole (25 µg/mL) was used as the positive control. All the assays were performed as three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Following incubation at 37 °C for 48 h, the diameter of the inhibition zone was measured. Specifically, the zones were quantified by taking the average of two perpendicular measurements. These measurements were also conducted in a blinded manner.

Statistical analysis

Data from three independent replicates (n = 3) are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To evaluate the dose-dependent inhibitory effects of CFS and identify significant differences between treatment groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. This was followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test for rigorous pairwise comparisons and to establish statistical subsets (p < 0.05). Furthermore, to validate the robustness of the findings and quantify the magnitude of the observed effect regardless of the sample size, Omega-squared (ω2) was calculated as an effect size estimator. All statistical procedures and graphical illustrations were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA) [24].

The analysis yielded an exceptionally large effect size (ω2 = 0.99), indicating that approximately 99% of the variance in antifungal activity is attributable to the CFS concentration. Discussion

This investigation demonstrated that the CFS of a plant-derived L. plantarum strain, isolated from P. jaubertii, possesses potent antifungal activity. Rigorous statistical analysis substantiated a highly significant, dose-dependent treatment effect against both targeted fungi. However, it should be explicitly acknowledged as a limitation that, due to the unavailability of molecular sequencing facilities in our region, the identification of this bacterial isolate relied exclusively on comprehensive phenotypic and biochemical profiling. While these tests strongly align with the characteristics of L. plantarum, the exact taxonomic assignment remains presumptive pending future molecular confirmation.

Chemical profiling via FT-IR spectroscopy provides a foundational understanding of this bioactivity. The prominent and broad absorption band at 3244.27 cm⁻1, assigned to O–H stretching vibration suggests the presence of organic acids, such as lactic and acetic acids, which are primary metabolites of L. plantarum [25]. Based on previous studies, the antifungal action of such organic acids may involve the penetration of their undissociated forms into the fungal cell, which likely leads to intracellular acidification and the subsequent collapse of transmembrane proton gradients. Once inside the neutral cytoplasm, they dissociate, potentially leading to intracellular acidification, disruption of the proton motive force, and subsequent metabolic failure [15]. Concurrently, the Amide I (1624.06 cm⁻1) and Amide II (1581.63 cm⁻1) bands indicate the presence of proteinaceous compounds, pointing to the potential secretion of bioactive peptides or bacteriocin-like inhibitory substances (BLISs) [26, 27]. This aligns with extensive literature highlighting L. plantarum as a robust producer of broad-spectrum antifungal compounds. For instance, previous studies have characterized specific plantaricins and cyclic dipeptides from L. plantarum that exhibit potent inhibitory effects not only against clinical pathogens like Candida spp. but also against a wide array of phytopathogenic fungi such as Fusarium oxysporum, Botrytis Cinerea, and Aspergillus flavus [28]. The presence of these compounds in our CFS likely contributes to the observed efficacy.

If present, established literature suggests that such compounds might disrupt fungal membrane integrity. Furthermore, the C–O stretching vibration at 1072.42 cm⁻1 suggests the presence of carbohydrates (potentially exopolysaccharides), which may play an adjunctive role by interfering with fungal adhesion.

Collectively, the FT-IR profile hints at the presence of multiple bioactive groups, suggesting a likely multimechanistic action. As noted in existing literature, such synergistic effects may reduce the likelihood of fungal resistance [29]. However, it is crucial to acknowledge the limitations of our experimental scope. Direct mechanistic assays such as evaluations of membrane integrity, intracellular leakage, or protease treatment of the CFS to confirm proteinaceous activity were not performed in this study.

Furthermore, the fresh CFS utilized in our bioassays exhibited a highly acidic pH (ranging between 3.8 and 4.0). Because pH-neutralized or acid-matched controls were not included in the experimental design, we cannot definitively disentangle the presumed targeted effects of the peptide components from the broad, non-specific antimicrobial impact of the acidic environment. Consequently, while a synergistic, multimechanistic action is hypothesized based on the FT-IR data, the substantial (and potentially primary) contribution of organic acids alone to the observed fungal inhibition cannot be ruled out.

A pivotal finding of this study is the differential susceptibility profile between the tested strains. Compared with C. albicans (which showed initial inhibition at 10% CFS), A. fumigatus exhibited growth inhibition at a lower concentration (5%). This disparity is likely attributable to fundamental differences in cellular ultrastructure. Aligning with broader literature on microbial antagonism, filamentous fungi often exhibit immediate sensitivity to L. plantarum metabolites; extracellular acidification can rapidly impair hyphal tip growth and spore germination. This phenomenon is similarly observed in the successful biocontrol of severe agricultural phytopathogens, such as Penicillium spp., using L. plantarum isolates [30]. In contrast, the complex cell wall of C. albicans, rich in β-glucans and chitin, likely requires a higher critical concentration of the CFS to be effectively compromised [31, 32].

However, a pronounced increase in efficacy was observed between the 20% and 25% concentrations. At these levels, we hypothesize that the combined action of acids and potential peptides likely overwhelms fungal defense systems. As suggested by previous studies [33], such a hypothesized multimechanistic assault may reduce the propensity for resistance emergence. Although Tukey’s post hoc analysis indicated a statistically significant difference between the highest tested concentration (30% CFS) and the fluconazole positive control, the biological and clinical relevance of achieving 90.0% relative inhibition efficacy is highly noteworthy. Rather than claiming absolute statistical equivalence, the substantial magnitude of this inhibitory effect supported by a robust statistical effect size underscores the potent dose-dependent activity of the L. plantarum CFS. These findings echo recent global paradigms advocating for the repurposing of L. plantarum postbiotics not merely as clinical therapeutic alternatives against resilient human pathogens, but also as versatile, eco-friendly biocontrol agents against diverse agricultural fungal infections [34].

Conclusions

This study suggests that the CFS of plant-derived L. plantarum may represent a promising multicomponent antifungal agent. Its efficacy, derived from a synergistic blend of bioactive metabolites, and its nature as a “postbiotic” circumvent many challenges of live probiotic therapy. These attributes highlight its potential as a sustainable strategic alternative in the global effort to combat antifungal resistance.
