Embedding standardized nursing terminology in electronic health records: the Lazio regional experience
Authors: Maurizio Zega, Annalisa Bononati, Valentina Vanzi, Andrea Urbani, Ivano Giuliani, Ivano Giuliani, Gennaro Scialò, Stefano Severini, Annalisa Testa
Published: 2026

Digital transformation is reshaping healthcare systems across Europe, with major investments directed toward interoperability, data standardization, and the implementation of electronic health records (EHRs). In the European Union, these priorities are now framed more explicitly by the European Health Data Space (EHDS), which identifies interoperability, data quality, and structured electronic health information as enabling conditions for cross-border care and health system improvement.1 In this context, the World Health Organization (WHO) has consistently emphasized the importance of standardized terminology and semantic interoperability as essential conditions for improving the quality, safety, and continuity of care. Shared and structured clinical language supports accurate documentation, facilitates communication among professionals, and enables the generation of reliable and comparable health data across settings and systems. It is also fundamental for strengthening decision-making, supporting health policy development, and improving the evaluation of healthcare outcomes. Within this perspective, the WHO Family of International Classifications (WHO-FIC) offers a reference framework for the harmonization of health information and clinical documentation, highlighting the importance of integrating professional terminologies into interoperable health information systems.2

Yet one critical component of healthcare remains insufficiently represented in digital infrastructures: nursing care.3 This gap is not marginal. Nursing care constitutes a substantial and continuous component of health service delivery, within both hospital and community settings, and contributes directly to patient surveillance, clinical risk management, continuity of care, and outcomes.4, 5, 6, 7, 8 Standardized nursing terminologies (SNTs) offer a concrete response to this problem.5 Since the 1970s, they have evolved from classification systems focused mainly on nursing diagnoses into broader frameworks capable of structuring diagnoses, interventions, and outcomes.5 Their relevance has grown steadily in parallel with the expansion of digital health systems. By providing a shared and person-centered language for documenting the nursing process, SNTs improve the transparency, comparability, and usability of nursing documentation and create the conditions for nursing data to become visible, measurable, and actionable within healthcare systems.5

However, nursing data in many EHRs remain poorly structured or only partially integrated. This invisibility limits their use in outcome evaluation, case-mix interpretation, and reimbursement systems that rely almost exclusively on medical diagnoses. It also obscures the independent contribution of nursing care, despite growing evidence that standardized nursing data predict length of stay, intra-hospital transfers, mortality, and patient complexity. Nursing diagnoses and interventions reflect care intensity and constitute meaningful clinical-organizational indicators. Their absence is therefore a structural, not technical, limitation.9

The Lazio Region, home to more than 5.7 million residents, offers one of the first large-scale examples of how nursing data can be structurally embedded within a regional digital health ecosystem. Developed under Italy's National Recovery and Resilience Plan, the regional EHR aims to unify digital infrastructures across all emergency hospitals, integrating interoperability services, a Clinical Data Repository, terminology services, and policy management systems. Its most innovative feature is the inclusion, from the outset, of a dedicated nursing section based on standardized nursing language (NANDA-I),10 enabling structured representation of nursing assessment, diagnoses, and care planning across a workforce of more than 45,000 nurses.

This design is operationalized through the Professional Assessment Instrument (PAI),11 developed within the Centre of Excellence for Nursing Scholarship (CECRI) of the Board of Nursing (OPI) of Rome and made available to the Regional Health Service. The PAI supports standardized assessment, diagnosis, intervention planning, and outcome evaluation, and incorporates a validated decision-support component that enhances standardization while preserving clinical judgment. Workflow tools such as dashboards and worklists further support multidisciplinary coordination.

The regional EHR project in Lazio represents more than a technological upgrade. It reflects a shared vision, grounded in scientific evidence and sustained by the joint commitment of political, governmental, institutional, and healthcare stakeholders, that recognizes digital health as a systemic transformation rather than a purely technical intervention. Its most important lesson is that nursing traceability should not be considered optional in large-scale EHR implementation. By embedding SNTs into a regional digital infrastructure, the Lazio experience offers a concrete example of how nursing care can become more visible, measurable, and actionable within health systems. This has implications not only for documentation quality, but also for patient safety, clinical governance, interprofessional collaboration, and secondary use of health data, facilitating value-based healthcare and supporting knowledge generation. As European health systems continue to invest in digital transformation, the inclusion of SNTs in national EHRs should be seen as a strategic priority. The Lazio experience demonstrates that large-scale implementation is feasible. The challenge now is ensuring it does not remain an isolated example.
