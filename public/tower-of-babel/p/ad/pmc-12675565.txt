The critical role of LRG1 in pathological fibrosis
Authors: Tianxiang Yang, Heping Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pathological fibrosis is a chronic process characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), which disrupts tissue architecture and function and accelerates disease progression in organs such as the lungs, liver, heart, kidneys, skin, and eyes. Recent studies have identified leucine-rich α-2 glycoprotein-1 (LRG1), a secreted glycoprotein known for modulating angiogenesis and immune responses, as a key player in the pathogenesis of fibrotic disorders. LRG1 expression is up-regulated by multiple pro-fibrotic mediators, including transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), and has been shown to regulate fibroblast differentiation, myofibroblast activation, and ECM production. Through interactions with the TGF-β signaling pathway and other cascades, LRG1 plays a crucial role in pathological fibrosis. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which LRG1 drives fibrogenic responses will pave the way for novel therapeutic strategies targeting pathological fibrosis. This review explores the emerging functions of LRG1 in fibrosis of different organs and discusses therapeutic approaches aimed at mitigating fibrosis through LRG1 inhibition.

Introduction

Fibrosis is a pathological process characterized by the overexpression of extracellular matrix (ECM) components, most notably collagen, which alters the normal architecture and function of the tissues. This process takes the form of an inappropriate adaptive reaction to persistent inflammation or injury in various organs, such as the liver [1], lung [2], kidney [3], heart [4], skin [5], and the eye [6]. Under normal conditions, tissue injury-initiated repair process involves a controlled inflammatory response, fibroblast activation and proliferation, followed by tissue remodeling [7]. However, in pathological fibrosis, persistent inflammation and fibroblast activation results in the overproduction of the ECM and deposition of scar tissues.

Several key mediators and molecular pathways are implicated in fibrotic processes, with transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) playing a central role. TGF-β drives fibroblast activation, promotes ECM production, and suppresses matrix degradation through complex signaling cascades. Other factors include oxidative stress, cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that contribute to the fibrotic microenvironment [8, 9]. The accumulation of scar tissue impairs the function of affected organs, contributing to severe morbidity and mortality.

Recent evidence suggests that the Leucine-rich α−2 glycoprotein 1 (LRG1), a secreted glycoprotein and a member of the leucine-rich repeat (LRR) family of proteins, emerges as an important molecule critically involved in inflammation-driven fibrosis. Despite being discovered decades ago, the role of LRG1 in physiology has not been reported, and LRG1-deficient mice display no apparent phenotypic defects [10]. It is believed that LRG1 is mainly involved in human diseases, including pathological angiogenesis, inflammation, wound healing, and fibrosis. Identified eleven years ago as a critical regulator of pathological angiogenesis [10], LRG1 quickly became a significant focus in angiogenesis research. Its core mechanism involves modulation of the TGF-β signaling pathway [10], which fine-tunes cell-to-cell interactions within the tissue microenvironment and plays a pivotal role in vascular development and pathological angiogenesis. In addition, LRG1 exerts pleiotropic functions in immune regulation. LRG1 has a high binding affinity to cytochrome c (Cyt c) [11, 12]. When mitochondria-released Cyt c binds apoptotic protease-activating factor-1 (Apaf-1) and activates caspases, the intrinsic apoptotic pathway is triggered [13]. The LRG1–Cyt c interaction blocks Cyt c from associating with Apaf-1, thereby protecting lymphocytes and other cells from Cyt c-induced apoptosis [11, 12]. On the other hand, the expression of LRG1 is rapidly upregulated during inflammation and infections [14, 15]. LRG1 is critically involved in the wound healing response by supporting hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) expression [16], promoting the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [17], and regulating matrix metalloproteinases [18].

Angiogenesis and fibrosis are interconnected processes of the wound healing response [19], and LRG1 is known to be critically involved in both pathogenic angiogenesis and fibrosis. This review discusses the molecular structure of LRG1, followed by its regulation and expression in physiological conditions. Importantly, we examined the existing literature regarding the role of LRG1 in fibrosis and explored the therapeutic and biomarker potential of LRG1 for organ fibrosis.

The molecular structure of LRG1

LRG1 was first isolated from human serum in 1977 [20], and its amino acid sequence was determined later in 1985 [21]. The crystal structure of LRG1 was almost completely solved in 2023 (amino acids S39-Q347 were determined) [22]. LRG1 consists of a single polypeptide chain of 347 amino acids, featuring eight leucine-rich repeats, an α-helix structure in the C-terminal region (CT), and three β-sheets in the N-terminal region (NT) [22] (Fig. 1). The LRR motifs, consisting of 19–29 amino acids, serve as platforms for protein–protein interactions, including the plasma LRG1-Cyt c interaction [11], facilitating diverse biological activities. These repeats form a horseshoe-shaped solenoid structure with a concave surface allowing for high-affinity binding to various ligands, including cationic proteins such as TGF-β [12]. LRG1 protein has four N-linked and one O-linked glycosylation site, and two disulfide bonds (Fig. 1). These glycosylation changes result in differences in molecular weight, with the deglycosylated protein ranges from 34 to 36 kDa and the glycosylated form ranging between 55 and 60 kDa [23, 24]. The glycosylation pattern of LRG1 varies in different tissues and cells and is often associated with its biological function. For example, compared with serum LRG1, neutrophil-derived LRG1 has a unique glycosylation profile [23], and the molecular weight range of LRG1 expressed by CD11b+F4/80− neutrophils is also wider than that of CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages [24]. In addition, the glycosylation patterns of LRG1 in the serum of patients with pancreatic cancer [25] and colorectal cancer [26] are rich in mannose, fucose and sialic acid, suggesting that these glycan modifications may affect the function of LRG1 in tumors. The mechanism of LRG1 glycosylation/deglycosylation and the impact of differential levels of glycosylation on its function remain elusive.

The expression of LRG1

Histological studies of LRG1 often show diffuse extracellular staining in organs with low vascular exclusion, attributed to the blood-borne nature of LRG1 and the limited reliability of available antibodies. However, in specific tissues such as the testis, LRG1 may further integrate into the ECM, the structural scaffold composed of collagen, glycoproteins, and proteoglycans, rather than merely being diffusely distributed in the extracellular space (the general fluid-filled compartment outside cells). This is evidenced by its accumulation around seminiferous tubules, likely due to its diffusion from blood vessels and subsequent binding to ECM components [27]. LRG1 is predominantly found in plasma at a concentration of approximately 10–50 µg/ml [28]. Most LRG1 presents as a monomer, though higher molecular weight multimers also exist, with glycosylation patterns varying across tissues [23–26]. It is primarily synthesized by hepatocytes and neutrophils [29], making these cells the main producers of LRG1 under normal physiological conditions. However, LRG1 expression has been observed at lower levels in various other cell types and tissues, such as alveolar epithelial cells in the lung [30, 31], renal tubular epithelial cells [32], and interstitial cells of the heart [33]. Interestingly, fibroblasts can also be a source of LRG1 [34], suggesting a potential tissue-specific role of this molecule. During late embryonic and early postnatal development stages, LRG1 expression is upregulated in adipose tissue, particularly as adipocytes accumulate lipids [35]. Both white and brown adipocytes secrete significantly higher levels of LRG1 compared to the liver, despite similar transcription levels in both tissues [36, 37]. Under physiological conditions, endothelial cells generally exhibit low or undetectable levels of LRG1. Exceptions have been reported in the kidney, where LRG1 has been detected in endothelial cells by in situ hybridization and immunohistochemistry [38]. There have also been reports of LRG1-positive endothelial cells in the lung [30] and brain [39], although the observations are yet to be confirmed by others.

In summary, while LRG1 exists in the serum and is expressed in multiple cells and tissues in healthy individuals, its physiological function and regulatory processes are largely undefined. Experiments using knockout animals have shown that the absence of LRG1 causes no phenotypic abnormalities, suggesting its redundant role under physiologic conditions [10, 27, 40]. Increasing interest has been placed on understanding the regulation of LRG1 expression and defining the role of LRG1 under pathological conditions over the last few years, since such an understanding would reveal novel targets for therapy and biomarkers for a wide variety of diseases.

Regulation of LRG1 expression in disease conditions

The expression of LRG1 is subject to complex regulations influenced by inflammatory stimuli, transcriptional networks, and cellular microenvironment (Fig. 2). The interleukin-6 (IL-6)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway emerges as a principal driver of LRG1 transcription [41–43]. This pathway is particularly relevant to inflammation-driven pathologies [41]. Indeed, a recent study showed that deleting the STAT3 binding site on the LRG1 promoter completely suppressed its transcriptional activation [43]. In addition to IL-6, cytokines such as IL-1β, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-17, IL-4, IL-10, and IL-33 can also regulate LRG1 expression across various types of cells, including endothelial cells [44], bronchial epithelial cells [31], hepatoma cells [14], and macrophages [45]. In addition, Fos-related antigen 1 (FRA-1) [46] and the FAK/ERK-ELK signaling axis [47] have also been implicated in regulating LRG1 expression. The transcription factor ELK1 was shown to initiate LRG1 transcription in a mechanical strain model [47], and ELK4 could activate LRG1 transcription by cooperating with the Sp1/Sp3 complex [48]. The peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor beta/delta (PPARβ/δ) controls LRG1 transcription by binding to regulatory regions of the LRG1 gene in dermal fibroblasts [34]. Furthermore, hypoxia can induce LRG1 expression, suggesting potential HIF-1α binding elements within the LRG1 promoter [49]. In ovarian cancer cells, the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) TUG1 has been demonstrated to promote the transcription of LRG1 [50]. At the post-transcriptional level, LRG1 expression is modulated by a suite of microRNAs (miRNAs), including miR-335 [51], miR-497 [52], miR-150-5p [53], and miR-24-3p [54], particularly in pathological conditions such as cancer (Fig. 2). Multiple independent studies have demonstrated that LRG1 can also be incorporated into extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes, across diverse pathological conditions. Proteomic analyses of EVs from colorectal cancer patients identified LRG1 as part of the vesicle cargo, with elevated EV-LRG1 levels correlating with tumor presence and recurrence [55]. In non-small cell lung cancer, LRG1 was found to be enriched in exosomes derived from tumor cells and blood, where exosomal LRG1 contributed to pro-angiogenic signaling in the tumor microenvironment [56]. Outside of oncology, high levels of LRG1 have been detected in exosomes from gestational diabetes mellitus in pregnant mothers [57] and in exosomes released from diabetic kidneys [58], confirming that LRG1 is also associated with the vesicles rather than merely in soluble form.

Although the molecular mechanism controlling LRG1 gene expression, protein synthesis, intracellular trafficking, and the release in free- or vesicle form are complicated and incompletely understood, its expression and production are generally increased in pathological conditions, particularly those related to pathological fibrosis.

LRG1 expression in pathological fibrosis

Elevated levels of LRG1 have been detected in a variety of fibrotic conditions, including fibrosis of the lung [30, 59], kidney [60], liver [61], skin [47], ocular tissue [62], and the joint [63] (Fig. 3). LRG1 has been considered not only as a biomarker of fibrosis but also a pro-fibrotic factor. Mechanistically, LRG1 can be viewed as an “adjustment amplifier” linking diverse stimuli to fibrosis. IL-6/STAT3, mechanical strain (FAK-ERK-ELK1) and hypoxia (HIF-1α) each raise LRG1, which then biases signaling toward canonical TGF-β/Smad2/3 or non-canonical TGF-β/MAPK pathways, driving fibroblast activation and ECM deposition [30, 41–43, 47, 49, 64–66] (Fig. 4). Conversely, PPARβ/δ limits LRG1 and restrains TGF-β-dependent fibrosis [34]. Importantly, cytokines can also act without LRG1-dependent pathways in pathological situations, so conditional knockouts and multi-omics are still needed to isolate LRG1’s unique contribution within the broader pro-fibrotic network. LRG1 in ocular fibrosis

Ocular fibrosis is a complex biological process contributing to the pathogenesis of numerous sight-threatening conditions, such as corneal and conjunctival scarring, open-angle glaucoma, failure of glaucoma filtration surgery, secondary cataract, subretinal fibrosis in neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), and proliferative vitreoretinopathy [6, 67]. Ocular fibrosis involves inflammation, fibroblast activation and ECM accumulation. It disrupts the normal microenvironment of the eye, leading to cellular degeneration and ultimately the loss of visual function.

We previously showed that inflammation, including infiltrating macrophages and the complement system, critically contributes to subretinal fibrosis in nAMD [68]. Mechanistically, macrophages can promote subretinal fibrosis through a process called macrophage-to-myofibroblast transition (MMT), which can be induced by TGF-β or complement C3a [69] and requires the macrophage metalloelastase 12 (MMP12) [70]. Complement fragment C5a, on the other hand, can promote macular fibrosis by inducing EMT in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells [68]. The vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) also plays a critical role in subretinal fibrosis by modulating macrophage recruitment and polarization [71]. Additionally, our team demonstrated that aging accelerates subretinal fibrosis through increased circulating fibrocytes and enhanced profibrotic activity of bone marrow-derived macrophages [72].

TGF-β is a key regulator of ocular fibrosis by inducing fibroblast activity, enhancing the ECM deposition and accumulation, and facilitating the EMT [67, 73], which is mediated by the Smad-dependent (canonical) pathway. For instance, the TGF-β/Smad2/3 pathway is linked with fibrotic changes in human lens epithelial cells [74–76], corneal tissue [77], and RPE cells [78]. TNF-α can enhance corneal [79] and RPE/choroid region [80, 81] fibrosis by inducing the TGF-β/Smad pathway. A connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), a fibrogenic cytokine, acts as a downstream mediator of TGF-β/Smad-induced fibrosis, both facilitating EMT and ECM production in human RPE cells [82]. Interferon-gamma (IFNγ), on the other hand, can suppress TGF-β/Smad-mediated collagen production by inducing expression of Smad7 in human subconjunctival fibroblasts [83].

TGF-β can regulate ocular fibrosis through Smad-independent (non-canonical) pathways, including the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway. The signaling pathways of TGF-β consist of three MAPK subfamilies: extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERKs), c-Jun N-terminal kinases (JNKs), and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases (p38s) (Fig. 4) [67]. ERK activation is involved in TGF-β-mediated EMT [65] and the fibrotic response in human RPE cells [84]. Furthermore, studies have shown that JNK and p38 pathways are also involved in the migration of corneal epithelial [85] and endothelial cells [86], and collagen I induction in human RPE cells [87]. In addition, the RhoA/Rho-kinase pathway [88] and its downstream myocardin-related transcription factor-A/serum response factor (MRTF-A/SRF) pathway (Fig. 4) [89, 90] are other Smad-independent pathways involved in TGF-β-induced ocular fibrosis. Importantly, it should be noted that both Smad-dependent and non-Smad pathways can act simultaneously. For instance, the phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway was reported to be a mediator of TGF-β-induced collagen I expression in human RPE cells through both Smad-dependent and Smad-independent pathways [91].

LRG1 was found to be associated with retinal pathogenic neovascularization in 2013 [10]. Its expression was nearly undetectable in healthy retinas but became significantly upregulated in experimental models of retinal vascular diseases, such as choroidal neovascularization (CNV) and oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) [10]. LRG1 expression was elevated in both endothelial cells and myofibroblasts of choroidal neovascular membranes in nAMD patients [92]. Elevated levels of LRG1 have also been detected in the plasma and vitreous of PDR patients [93, 94]. However, LRG1 did not significantly enhance the predictive accuracy of a risk model for PDR [95]. A transcriptional study revealed that senescent myeloid cells in laser-induced CNV displayed an upregulated angiogenic transcriptional profile, with significantly higher LRG1 expression compared to young cells [96]. As noted earlier, aging can promote retinal fibrosis [72], and abnormal angiogenesis also exacerbates fibrosis [97], suggesting the likely role of LRG1 in ocular fibrosis. Indeed, a study of the corneal alkali burn mouse model has shown that LRG1 exacerbates corneal fibrosis by inducing neutrophil infiltration and activating the IL-6/Stat3 signaling pathway [62]. LRG1 can also activate NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4), driving EMT in human RPE cells [98] (Fig. 3). It is unclear whether LRG1 mediates ocular fibrosis through the canonical and non-canonical TGF signaling pathway. Nevertheless, compelling evidence suggests that LRG1 has the potential to be a biomarker for vascular proliferative eye diseases [92–95].

LRG1 in pulmonary fibrosis

Pulmonary fibrosis is characterized by the excessive deposition of ECM proteins in lung tissue, resulting in scarring, alveolar wall thickening, and disruption of normal lung architecture. Such pathological remodeling causes reduced elasticity, impairs gas exchange, and eventually leads to progressive respiratory dysfunction that may culminate in respiratory failure. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is the most common and severe form of this condition, with undetermined etiology. In addition, pulmonary fibrosis can arise as a secondary effect of autoimmune diseases, environmental exposures (asbestos and silica), radiation, or drug reactions [2, 99]. TGF-β is a key regulator of pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis through the promotion of fibroblast activation and ECM deposition under conditions of increased oxygen tension, by both Smad-dependent [100, 101] and Smad-independent [102] signaling pathways. Chronic damage in the alveolar epithelial cells decreases the regeneration capacity of these cells; the dysfunctional cells then secrete pro-fibrotic factors like TGF-β and interleukin-13 (IL-13), which in turn activate fibroblasts and induce ECM [103, 104]. The alveolar epithelial cells can undergo EMT through TGF-β [105, 106] and Wnt/β-catenin pathway (Fig. 4) [107]. The alternatively activated M2 macrophages also secrete pro-fibrotic cytokines like TGF-β and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) that further recruit and activate fibroblasts and perpetuate fibrosis [108, 109].

LRG1 has been identified as a potential biomarker for active tuberculosis, lung fibrosis, asthma, and allergic rhinitis [65]. A clinical study demonstrated that serum LRG1 levels were significantly higher in tuberculosis patients compared to healthy controls [110]. Additionally, allergen-induced inhibition in allergic airway disorders may lower LRG1 and TGF-β receptor II levels in affected patients [111]. Lung tissue from elastase-treated mice or patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease had higher levels of LRG1 [112]. Increased LRG1 in dysfunctional endothelial cells exacerbates alveolar destruction and accelerates the disease by causing maladaptive vascular changes [112]. Goblet cells, a subset of mucin-producing bronchial epithelial cells that normally do not express LRG1, secreted LRG1 in the mouse model of ovalbumin-induced asthma [31]. Furthermore, expression of LRG1 requires IL-13-driven transdifferentiation of normal bronchial epithelial cells into goblet cells, a process called goblet cell metaplasia. Locally secreted LRG1 from epithelial cells may participate in asthma progression by triggering TGF-β-induced subepithelial fibrosis [31]. In a mouse model of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, LRG1 promoted lung fibrosis by regulating the phosphorylation of TGF-β and Smad2, leading to the activation of fibroblasts independent of Endoglin (ENG) [30]. The ENG is an accessory receptor that enhances vasodilation by stimulating nitric oxide production in endothelial cells via the Smad2 pathway [113] (Fig. 3). The authors further showed that LRG1 did not enhance TGF-β-Smad1/5/8 signaling in fibroblasts in the presence of ENG [30], though LRG1 drives pathological angiogenesis with the assistance of ENG [10]. This suggests that although LRG1 regulates both angiogenesis and fibrosis via TGF-β, distinct TGF-β/Smad pathways are involved in these processes. In a mouse model of bleomycin-induced systemic sclerosis, LRG1 knockout significantly reduced lung fibrosis, highlighting the role of LRG1 in promoting collagen synthesis and fibrotic changes [59].

LRG1 in cardiac fibrosis

Cardiac fibrosis is a pathological condition caused by an excessive accumulation of ECM components in the myocardium. It is a hallmark of numerous cardiac disorders and contributes significantly to both diastolic and systolic dysfunction. This maladaptive remodeling process can be seen in aging, metabolic disorders such as diabetes and obesity, conditions such as myocardial infarction, and pressure overload caused by hypertension [4]. TGF-β is the master regulator of cardiac fibrosis, promoting fibroblast activation and the synthesis of ECM proteins, including types I and III collagen [114, 115], through both Smad-dependent [116] and Smad-independent pathways [117, 118]. It can also stop the extracellular matrix from breaking down by downregulating matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and upregulating tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) [4, 115]. Furthermore, by inducing fibroblast activation and inflammatory cascades, cytokines such as IL-1β [119], IL-6 [120], and TNF-α [121] have been shown to enhance the fibrotic response. Other contributing factors include PDGF [115, 122] and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [115, 123].

LRG1 could be a serum biomarker for early-onset myocardial infarction, especially when used with other biomarkers like plasma brain natriuretic peptide [124]. A new study has found that plasma LRG1 is a useful biomarker for predicting cardiac fibrosis in ST-elevation myocardial infarction [125]. However, other research has shown that LRG1 might help protect against cardiac fibrosis. For instance, a lack of LRG1 made myocardial fibrosis worse in a mouse model of myocardial infarction by blocking the Smad1/5/8 pathway [24] (Fig. 3). In the mouse model of transverse aortic constriction, the fibrotic heart had much less LRG1 expression. This was also seen in human fibrotic hearts [33]. It has been shown that LRG1 stopped cardiac fibrosis by blocking the TGF-β1 signaling [33]. The results highlight the complexity of the role of LRG1 in organ fibrosis and the urgent need for further investigations.

LRG1 in renal fibrosis

Renal fibrosis is a progressive and irreversible condition affecting renal function due to excessive ECM deposition. It is a hallmark of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and a significant contributor to the development of end-stage renal disease [3]. Prolonged injury and inflammation are the initial triggers of fibrogenesis, due to the release of profibrotic cytokines such as TGF-β and the infiltration of macrophages [126, 127] and T cells [128, 129]. The TGF-β/Smad pathway is essential for renal fibrosis because it stimulates the production of extracellular matrix and myofibroblast activation [3, 130]. Tubular epithelial cells’ expression of intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) [133, 134] and macrophage-derived MMPs [131, 132] can trigger EMT, a critical early stage of TGF-β-driven fibrosis. In addition to EMT, endothelial–mesenchymal transition (EndoMT) has a major impact on renal fibrosis [135, 136].

In the healthy kidney, LRG1 is expressed in glomerular endothelial cells and the tubulointerstitium [38]. In pathological conditions, LRG1 expression is upregulated in glomerular endothelial cells [137], as well as in experimental diabetic kidney disease (DKD) [38, 138, 139]. LRG1 may promote renal pathological angiogenesis, partly through the p38 and TGF-β/Smad1/5/8 pathway (Figs. 3 and 4) [38, 137, 138]. The plasma level of LRG1 has been proposed as an early marker of DKD progression [140] and CKD, such as lupus nephritis [141]. The urinary LRG1 has the potential as a biomarker for end-stage kidney disease [142] and kidney injury [143]. In addition to endothelial cells, tubular epithelial cells also express LRG1 [60, 144]. Hong et al. have shown that LRG1 derived from human renal tubular epithelial cells (HK-2) enhanced renal fibrosis by activating the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway in fibroblasts [144]. Another recent in vitro study has shown that LRG1 expressed by glomerular endothelial cells enhanced fibrosis by promoting the expression of the plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1, a profibrotic marker) through the TGF-β signaling pathway [139]. Interestingly, LRG1 has also been shown to have a protective role in renal fibrosis. Liu et al. have shown that overexpression of LRG1 in HK-2 cells reduced TGF-β1-induced fibronectin secretion and LRG1 inhibited fibronectin secretion in TGF-β1-treated HK-2 cells. They further demonstrated that LRG1 mitigated renal fibrosis by promoting angiogenesis, and suppressing inflammatory and pro-fibrotic cytokine secretion [60]. Their study highlights the interconnected relationship between pathological angiogenesis and fibrosis. However, it remains hard to understand why HK2-derived LRG1 exhibits opposing roles in fibrosis, for example, promoting fibrosis by activating fibroblasts [60] and reducing fibrosis by inhibiting fibronectin secretion [144]. The role of LRG1 in renal fibrosis may depend on specific experimental conditions and the microenvironment.

LRG1 in skin fibrosis

Skin fibrosis is a pathological condition with the accumulation of ECM, particularly collagen, in the dermis. This process leads to thickened, stiffened skin with reduced elasticity and impaired function. Skin fibrosis is a hallmark of several disorders, including systemic sclerosis, chronic radiation-induced skin injury, and keloids and hypertrophic scars. Like fibrosis in other organs, TGF-β is also critical in skin fibrosis. The canonical TGF-β/Smad pathway activates fibroblasts and exacerbates ECM production [5, 145]. Meanwhile, the interaction between TGF-β and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways not only enhances ECM synthesis but also establishes a feedback loop that amplifies fibrotic responses by stabilizing Smad proteins [146]. Also, prolonged inflammation in the skin favoring fibroblast activation and myofibroblast differentiation [145].

The elevated levels of LRG1 in the serum may be a potential biomarker for systemic sclerosis [147]. A recent study discovered that the application of LRG1 to human foreskin fibroblasts resulted in increased production of type I collagen. The authors also reported that LRG1 inhibited ECM degradation via the TGF-β/Smad2/3 pathway. Additionally, LRG1 can elevate TGF-β1 levels, initiating a positive feedback loop that intensifies fibrotic reactions. Notably, LRG1 expression was significantly reduced in the skin of elderly humans, suggesting its potential role in maintaining skin health and combating skin aging [66]. Researchers examined human dermal fibroblasts and discovered that LRG1 synthesis significantly increased when subjected to mechanical strain, independent of TGF-β1 or inflammatory stimuli. The FAK/ERK/ELK1 signaling pathway regulates this process (Fig. 3). ELK1 is directly associated with the LRG1 promoter to enhance its activity. Moreover, LRG1 serves as a mediator connecting biomechanical force to pathological angiogenesis, therefore facilitating the advancement of fibrosis in hypertrophic scars [47]. A study utilizing a fibroblast-specific PPARβ/δ knockout mice demonstrated that the absence of PPARβ/δ exacerbates fibrosis by stimulating fibroblasts and enhancing collagen deposition via LRG1-dependent regulation of the TGF-β/Smad3 signaling pathway (Figs. 3 and 4). This indicates that LRG1 provides protection against cutaneous fibrosis [34]. LRG1 safeguards against cutaneous fibrosis in a manner analogous to its protection against cardiac fibrosis [33]. Both diseases are associated with a deficiency of Pparβ/δ, indicating that LRG1's capacity to inhibit fibrosis may be connected to the PPARβ/δ signaling pathway.

LRG1 in cancer-related fibrosis

Fibrosis is a hallmark of cancer progression, characterized by excessive ECM deposition, chronic inflammation, and stromal remodeling. It is a critical component of the tumor microenvironment, driven by activated fibroblasts, particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts. Fibrosis in cancer is a ‘double-edged sword’, it can restrain cancer progression in its early stages, but becomes a promoter of tumor growth, metastasis, and treatment resistance in advanced phases [148]. TGF-β also has dual roles, acting as a tumor suppressor in the early phases and a tumor promoter in advanced stages [149]. Both canonical and non-canonical TGF-β signaling pathways are implicated in cancer progression [149–152].

LRG1 in the blood and urine has been proposed as a biomarker for tumor diagnosis [153–158]. LRG1 can regulate angiogenesis and tumor cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis [42, 159–162]. EMT is a critical step for tumor metastasis. LRG1 can promote EMT in cancer cells via the HIF-1α/VEGF-A pathway [163] or the MAPK/p38 signaling pathway (Fig. 3) [164]. In addition, the nuclear paraspeckle assembly transcript 1 (NEAT1) has been shown to enhance EMT in retinoblastoma cells through the miR-24-3p/LRG1 axis [54]. LRG1 appears to promote tumor progression through multiple mechanisms.

LRG1 in other tissue fibrosis

Higher levels of LRG1 have been observed in other fibrotic conditions, such as liver fibrosis in fatty liver disease, and cirrhosis [61] and arthrofibrosis (joint fibrosis) [63], whereby fibrosis results from sustained or recurrent chronic inflammation and dysregulated wound healing. The molecular pathways driven by LRG1-mediated fibrosis warrant further investigation.

LRG1 as a therapeutic target

LRG1 is thought to be a promising therapeutic target for pathological angiogenesis and fibrosis. Both mRNA and protein of LRG1 have been targeted for therapy in various studies. Gu et al. successfully utilized miR-497 to target LRG1 in mice, activating the TGF-β/Smad pathway to enhance bone formation and mitigate fibrosis-like pathological remodeling in osteoporosis [52]. In another study, the authors used adeno-associated virus 5 (AAV5)-shRNA to silence LRG1 gene, resulting in a significant reduction in load-induced (mechanical forces applied to the wound surface) hypertrophic scars in mice [47].

ET is a recently developed peptide (Glu-Thr-Trp-Thr-Asp-Trp-Gln-Trp-Trp-Thr-Pro-Trp) that has a high affinity for LRG1. By combining ET with lenalidomide, Fan et al. discovered a novel proteolysis-targeted chimera known as ETTAC-2, which enlists the E3 ubiquitin ligase Cereblon to break down LRG1 [165]. By blocking the TGF-β–Smad3 pathway and lowering fibrosis markers, ETTAC-2 efficiently broke down LRG1 in fibrotic HK-2 renal cells in vitro [165]. Using a unilateral ureteral obstruction mouse model, ETTAC-2 treatment improved kidney histology, reduced extracellular matrix deposition, enhanced renal function, and decreased LRG1 expression in vivo [165]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. showed that ETTAC-2 significantly decreased pathological neovascularization in a mouse model of alkali burn-induced CNV by efficiently depleting LRG1 and blocking the TGF-β–Smad1/5/9 signaling pathway [166]. Additionally, the authors created a novel drug delivery system by taking advantage of the upregulation of LRG1 in renal fibrosis [167]. The ET peptide functionalized their engineered nanocarrier, DENNM, guaranteeing targeted delivery to kidneys with fibrosis. The controlled release of nintedanib, which inhibits the TGF-β–Smad2/3 signaling pathway and decreases extracellular matrix deposition to mitigate fibrosis, was made possible by caspase-3 activation after it was internalized by damaged renal cells [167].

A few antibodies have been developed to neutralize or block the function of the LRG1 protein. Magacizumab, a humanized lgG4 monoclonal antibody targeting LRG1 and derived from the parent antibody 15C4, was first reported in 2020 by Kallenberg et al. [168]. Magacizumab has high binding affinity and specificity for human LRG1. It effectively inhibited pathological angiogenesis and vascular leakage in the mouse model of laser-induced choroidal neovascularization (CNV) [168]. Magacizumab has achieved a good therapeutic effect in tumors, including the mouse models of melanoma, Lewis lung carcinoma, and colorectal cancer [169]. When Magacizumab was conjugated with monomethyl auristatin E, an anti-mitotic cytotoxic agent, it effectively targeted LRG1 in the extracellular tumor microenvironment, exhibited comparable efficacy to traditional chemotherapeutics, but with fewer side effects [170]. The Fab fragment of Magacizumab named MagaFab has also been developed, and it offers several advantages, including a reduced risk of inflammatory reactions, a lower molecular weight enabling delivery at higher molar doses, and adaptability for development as bispecific antibodies [168]. MagaFab exhibited a slightly lower affinity for human LRG1 compared to Magacizumab; however, it still achieved comparable inhibition of pathological angiogenesis and reduction of vascular leakage in the mouse model of CNV [64, 168]. The structural basis of how MagaFab recognizes human LRG1 has provided valuable insights to support the development of optimized antibodies against LRG1 [171]. Overall, Magacizumab represents a promising therapeutic option for LRG1-targeted angiogenesis-related diseases, particularly for patients who are refractory to VEGF inhibitors.

E4C12 is another LRG1-neutralizing antibody, which can effectively inhibit LRG1-induced macrophage polarization to the M1 phenotype and reduce inflammatory cytokine release [172]. In vivo E4C12 treatment significantly decreased atherosclerotic plaque size, necrotic core areas, and macrophage infiltration in an atherosclerosis mouse model, resembling the effects of genetic LRG1 knockout [172].

Furthermore, targeting LRG1 to reduce fibrosis has also been applied in cell engineering. Liu et al. engineered chimeric antigen receptor-triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2-macrophages (CAR-TREM2-Ms) and delivered them via hydrogel microporous microneedles to modulate the scar microenvironment. Single-cell transcriptome sequencing analysis and in vitro experiments revealed that CAR-TREM2-Ms effectively targeted and downregulated LRG1 expression in endothelial cells to mitigate fibrosis and scar formation [166].

Conclusion

Across organs, LRG1 enhances the canonical TGF-β/Smad2/3 cascade and thereby amplifies fibroblast activation, myofibroblast differentiation and ECM accumulation in the lung [30, 59], kidney [60], liver [61], skin [47], eye [62] and joint [63] to promote pathological fibrosis (Figs. 3, 4). It can also interface with non-canonical TGF-β cascades, the TGF-β/MAPK in pulmonary and renal fibroblasts [30, 60] and the FAK/ERK–ELK1 axis in mechanically strained dermal fibroblasts [47], revealing a shared signaling backbone (TGF-β pathway) that is tuned by biomechanical or inflammatory cues within tissue microenvironment (Figs. 3, 4). In addition to fibroblasts, endothelial cells (of the lung, kidney, and liver) [30, 60, 61], tubular or bronchial epithelial cells [31, 60], and infiltrating neutrophils [62] can serve as dominant sources or targets of LRG1, associating pathological angiogenesis, epithelial plasticity and immune cell recruitment to fibrosis. Nonetheless, targeting LRG1 has achieved anti-fibrotic effects, particularly in cardiac [24, 33] and dermal fibrosis [34] and is attributed to the downregulation of TGF-β through the PPARβ/δ pathway [33, 34].

Summary and perspectives

Fibrosis is a hallmark of many chronic diseases. Despite differences in etiology, fibrotic diseases share some common cellular and molecular mechanisms. The TGF-β signaling pathway is known to be a master regulator of fibrosis. Accordingly, targeting TGF-β or its receptors, including ALK1 and ENG, has been explored extensively for managing fibrosis. However, TGF-β is a versatile cytokine integral to development, immune regulation, ECM homeostasis, and tissue repair; its inhibition may lead to adverse effects, posing challenges to its long-term therapeutic application. Therefore, identifying alternative therapeutic targets for fibrosis has become necessary.

LRG1 has emerged as a potential therapeutic target for angiogenesis- and fibrosis-related diseases, especially for patients who do not respond to anti-VEGF treatment. Over the past decade, studies have uncovered pathways such as the TGF-β/Smad1/5/8 signaling cascade in LRG1-mediated pathologies. Given that TGF-β is an essential mediator in fibrotic processes and that pathological angiogenesis contributes to fibrosis by creating a favorable microenvironment, the role of LRG1 in fibrosis has garnered increasing attention. Higher plasma levels of LRG1 have been reported in several fibrotic diseases, such as PDR [94], myocardial infarction [125], lupus nephritis [141], and systemic sclerosis [147]. Patients with neovascular AMD had higher levels LRG1 in aqueous humor compared to non-AMD controls [92]. Since LRG1 is dispensable for development and homeostasis [10] targeting LRG1 would avoid the side effects associated with directly blocking the TGF-β pathway.

The results reviewed here point to a predominantly pro-fibrotic role for LRG1, yet several species- and tissue-specific discrepancies remain unresolved. For instance, LRG1 drives fibroblast activation to promote fibrosis in murine lung and kidney models [30, 59], but myeloid cell-derived LRG1 appears to have an anti-fibrotic role in pressure-overload cardiac fibrosis [24]. Such divergence may reflect tissue-specific roles of LRG1. Myeloid-derived LRG1 binds to TGF-β receptor 2 (TGF-βR II)/Alk1 complex to activate the downstream Smad1/5/8 pathway to inhibit fibrosis [24], while lung fibroblasts LRG1 binds to TGF-βR II/Alk5 complex to have pro-fibrotic function [30]. Across species, promoter architecture (such as STAT3 binding site) and glycosylation patterns also diverge between mouse and human LRG1, potentially altering the expression level and function of LRG1 [41–43]. Current studies rely on acute injury models or over-expression systems, leaving chronic, low-grade fibrosis and large-animal data lacking. Additionally, most mechanistic studies employ global LRG1 knockout or systemic antibody blockade in a single inbred strain (C57BL/6) and a single sex (male), leaving strain and sex effects unexplored. Therefore, conditional cell type-specific LRG1 knockouts, longitudinal human biopsies and single-cell multi-omics will be essential to resolving these context-dependent discrepancies and to de-risk LRG1-targeted therapies for clinical translation.

Although therapeutic strategies targeting LRG1 have advanced, current studies remain confined to the pre-clinical stage, with limited human pharmacokinetic and long-term safety information. Magacizumab, a humanized anti-LRG1 monoclonal antibody, and its Fab fragment, MagaFab, have shown great potential as therapeutic agents for reducing pathogenic angiogenesis in animal models of tumors [169, 170] and nAMD [168]. Their immunogenicity and tissue penetration abilities in human patients require clarification. Silencing LRG1 using miRNA or shRNA is another approach but may present greater technical challenges due to their short half-lives in vivo. LRG1-targeted drug delivery vehicles (such as peptide ET) have shown the effect of reducing neovascularization and fibrosis in in vitro and in vivo studies [165–167], but long-term evaluation of their duration of action, biological toxicity, and immunogenicity is still lacking.

Next, priority directions could be: (i) developing organ-specific delivery vehicles, for instance, conjugating LRG1 antibodies and kidney-homing peptides to target renal fibrosis specifically; (ii) developing small molecule drugs targeting LRG1, such as aptamers, to improve the ability of penetrating membrane structures to reach the lesion area, such as through the retina to reach the subretinal area to treat nAMD fibrosis; (iii) combination regimens with anti-VEGF, anti-integrin or senolytic drugs to tackle multifactorial fibrotic niches and enhance fibrosis-reducing efficacy; (iv) optimizing existing targeted drugs, such as Magacizumab, based on the resolved LRG1 structure to improve drug stability, safety and long-term efficacy, and reduce the need for administrations; (v) integrating single-cell transcriptomics, EV-proteomics and imaging, developing gene-editing approaches that achieve cell-type-specific LRG1 silencing.

Overall, targeting LRG1 for fibrosis requires careful consideration. Fibrosis in different tissues is related to different mechanisms, and LRG1 may play different roles. A comprehensive understanding of the role of LRG1 in organ fibrosis is essential for developing effective and safe LRG1-targeted therapies for pathological fibrosis.
