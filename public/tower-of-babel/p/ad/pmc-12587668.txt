Dutch participatory surveillance framework for evaluating evolutionary changes on SARS-CoV-2 affecting rapid diagnostic test sensitivity in 2022 − 2023
Authors: Eva Kozanli, Wanda Han, Tara Smit, Jordy de Bakker, Mansoer Elahi, Ryanne Jaarsma, Gesa Carstens, Albert Jan van Hoek, Dirk Eggink
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs) for SARS-CoV-2 have been pivotal for diagnostics, and shaping policies regarding self-isolation. In case of decreased sensitivity of RDTs to novel virus variants, viral spread can increase. In order to monitor for reduced sensitivity of RDTs we used a collection of SARS-CoV-2 positive samples from RDT negative patients. Infectieradar, a national participatory surveillance that registers respiratory symptoms and investigates the causative pathogen(s), is used here as a framework to study false-negative RDT results and possible relation of the emergence of new virus variants.MethodsParticipants reported weekly on RDT use and symptoms linked to Acute Respiratory Illness (ARI). Each week, all RDT-positive samples and a subset of 200 symptomatic, participants with RDT negative samples were invited to send in nose throat swabs (NTS). SARS-CoV-2 Ct-values were determined using RT-PCR on RDT-positive and RDT-negative NTS samples and compared using unpaired T-tests. Sequencing was performed on all eligible samples to compare the proportion of mutations in the N encoding gene and investigate the clustering patterns of genome sequences through analyses. NTS samples of participants with discordant RT-PCR and RDT results were also analyzed using RDTs by professionals in the laboratory. Between October 2022 and October 2023, our study had 16,893 participants and we collected 1,757 self-test-positive/NTS PCR-positive samples (RDT+/PCR+) and 359 self-test-negative/NTS PCR positive samples (RDT-/PCR+), which is 4.3% of RDT-negative samples.ResultsWe observed overall higher Ct-values in the RDT-negative group, but saw no changes in viral loads throughout our sampling period. Few and relatively small differences in prevalence of amino acid substitutions were observed when we compared the RDT-negative group and to RDT-positive group. No specific clusters within the phylogenetic tree were observed from the RDT-negative sequences, which suggested there were no distinctive genetic properties of RDT-negative specimens. This was further confirmed by laboratory analyses.ConclusionsEvaluating RDT performance in the Dutch population and in-depth analysis of false-negative RDT specimens, led to no evidence for SARS-CoV-2 evolution affecting RDT sensitivity of the tests used. The participatory surveillance program Infectieradar is a powerful tool for our national surveillance of acute respiratory illnesses, as well as for research purposes. Background

Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs) for SARS-CoV-2 have played a significant role in controlling the COVID-19 pandemic and continue to be useful for mitigating individual risk, surveillance and research. Since the start of the pandemic, self-isolation has been based on SARS-CoV-2 testing, for which reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was the diagnostic standard [1]. Later, when SARS-CoV-2 self-tests (RDTs) became available, they were sold and distributed widely, allowing convenient and less expensive self-testing at home, allowing for the downscaling of centralized test facilities [2]. Although the pandemic has been declared over, the sensitivity and specificity of RDTs are important to monitor routinely to ensure that they function appropriately even when new variants of the virus occur.

The wide use of RDTs brings about subsequent behavior when individuals test positive, such as isolation or other preventive action. Undetectable virus, leading to false-negative outcomes of these RDTs, could therefore result in increased virus spread. Furthermore, these false-negative self-tests could potentially be the result of genetic drift and selective pressure on the virus, such as host-adaptation and immunity-escape. The majority of RDTs detect the nucleocapsid (N) protein of SARS-CoV-2. This N-protein is involved in multiple stages of the virus life cycle and is therefore abundant in SARS-CoV-2, making it a sensitive target for such RDTs [3]. A potential risk is that substitutions in the N-protein, or even changes in other regions of the genome, can weaken or abolish binding to antibodies used in the RDT and thereby affect its sensitivity [3, 4].

Routine monitoring of the sensitivity of RDTs is therefore important, both during a pandemic and continuously afterwards. Low sensitivity may cause false-negatives, and an underestimation of the virus, leading to a false sense of safety, and continuous spread of the virus with subsequent implications for those infected. Monitoring this sensitivity requires confirming RDT negative tests by a gold standard, like RT-PCR.

It is known that sensitivity of RDTs is reduced in cases with low viral load, like in an asymptomatic population. Other contributions to lower sensitivity might be discovered from false-negative testing in symptomatic participants [5]. As self-testing partially replaced the PCR-testing for detection of SARS-CoV-2, selective pressure may have resulted viral strains that were more difficult to detect. Viruses continue to evolve over time and factors driving this are an important subject for study. The undetected SARS-CoV-2 in RDTs could be caused by biological and evolutionary changes in the virus, resulting in undetected circulation. As sensitivity of RDTs can depend on many factors, we focus on some of the viral characteristics to clarify some of these factors.

Earlier studies have performed epitope mapping to evaluate sites that were likely bound by antibodies and deep mutational scanning to look at less conserved sites of the N-protein, which could both inform us on potential risks of SARS-CoV-2 variant mutations for the efficacy of RDTs [6, 7]. These sites can be monitored through sequencing data to keep track of RDT sensitivity and monitoring is the responsibility of the RDT producers. Moreover, studies have only found indirect evidence that SARS-CoV-2 variants affected the performance of specific RDTs [8, 9].

In our surveillance program, Infectieradar [10, 11], we have asked participants to perform an RDT when experiencing respiratory symptoms. Every week, participants receive questionnaires and when they report a positive test result, they are then asked to send in a self-sampled, combined nose/throat swab (NTS) to confirm SARS-CoV-2 infection and determine viral load using RT-PCR, as well as determine the virus variant as part of the national SARS-CoV-2 genomic surveillance. A random subset of approximately 200 participants per week who report a negative RDT after indicating respiratory symptoms, are also asked to send in an NTS to identify circulating respiratory pathogens. Among this second group, SARS-CoV-2 is also sporadically detected, despite the reported negative RDT. This phenomenon is often missed in studies and raises questions about RDT sensitivity.

In this study, we investigate the impact of evolutionary changes of SARS-CoV-2 on the sensitivity of SARS-CoV-2 RDTs, using the surveillance program Infectieradar. The construction of Infectieradar allows us to investigate SARS-CoV-2 RDT sensitivity using PCR results from NTS samples. We provide a single brand of RDTs to participants, which makes self-testing accessible and uniform. As the focus of many studies has been primarily directed towards SARS-CoV-2 positive cases, our framework that monitored ARI might gather information on those cases in our blind spot: false-negative RDT cases. We analyze these false-negative RDT cases to evaluate the use of a participatory surveillance framework for ARI for monitoring and elaborate on its possible uses in the future. Whole genome sequencing using an extensive set of RDT-negative samples and comparing those to RDT-positive samples allows for thoroughly studying biological and evolutionary features of SARS-CoV-2 that could affect RDT performance and evaluating the current self-testing method.

Methods

To monitor false-negative RDTs per SARS-CoV-2 variant, we explored the RDT-negatives per Ct-value and time in the pandemic, compared nucleocapsid mutations between positives and negatives, as well as phylogenetic clustering.

Setup and sample collection

The details of the Infectieradar study and participation of individuals are thoroughly described by Smit et al. [10]. This study from the National Institute for Public Health and the Environment of the Netherlands (RIVM) is part of the InfluenzaNet network which studies acute respiratory illness (ARI) in various countries [10, 12]. Participants were followed through questionnaires, filled out weekly, concerning symptoms indicating acute (i.e. respiratory diseases such as the common cold, COVID-19 or ARI, but also gastrointestinal complaints) and other infections (supplementary methods). Infectieradar recruited participants among the general population, independently of healthcare seeking behavior.

Questionnaires were sent out weekly to 16,893 participants, in which they report their health and respiratory symptoms (supplementary methods). Participants were asked to do a SARS-CoV-2 RDT when they experienced respiratory symptoms. As of October 2022, this study also contains a self-swab-component for nose throat swab (NTS), allowing laboratory RT-PCR based diagnostics for a broad panel of respiratory pathogens. A sampling kit was delivered to all participants upon inclusion. The kit contained NTS, a vial containing Virus Transport Media (Xebios Diagnostics), rapid SARS-CoV-2 antigen RDTs (MP Biomedicals), and appropriate return envelopes, suitable for medical diagnostic materials. Instructions including a link to an instructional video were provided and sampling kits were replaced in case of expiration [10]. RDTs of one brand were included to allow for proper comparison of results as well as testing methodology. The target (nucleocapsid protein) and detection method (colorimetric detection) of this brand is used by most SARS-CoV-2 RDTs, and thus representative for common SARS-CoV-2 RDTs. Participants reporting a positive RDT were asked to send in an NTS. Out of the group that reported respiratory symptoms but a SARS-CoV-2-negative RDT, a weekly subset of 200 participants was randomly selected and also sent an NTS. A ticketing-system was used to establish random sampling, which was explained in the paper of Smit et al. [10]. Only participants that fulfilled inclusion criteria were invited to this system. For declined invitations the system generated a new ticket to invite another participant using random sampling to ensure enough samples were collected per week. However, some participants with either a positive or negative RDT sent in their samples despite not being invited, their samples were included as well. Samples received at RIVM were stored at −80 degrees Celsius after performing molecular diagnostics.

RT-PCR

Firstly, SARS-CoV-2 viral load was determined for participants’ NTS samples through RT-PCR at the National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (RIVM), Bilthoven, The Netherlands as described [1]. RNA extraction was performed using MagNApure 96 (MP96) with total nucleic acid kit small volume (Roche). Five µL of the purified nucleic acid was used in TaqMan® Fast Virus 1-Step Master Mix (Thermo Fisher) in the Roche LC480 II thermal cycler. RT-PCR targeted the E-gene and RdRP-gene using specific primers and probe and equine arteritis virus (EAV) as controls. Cycle threshold (Ct)-values lower than 40 were considered SARS-CoV-2 positive.

PCR results were analyzed separately for RDT+/PCR + and RDT-/PCR + group. Their frequencies and proportions were determined for each month to specify the contribution of the falsely negative group over time. Ct-values in each group are compared using Student’s unpaired T-test. Ct-values of the RDT-/PCR + group over the time of this study were compared using simple linear regression and visualized in R (v. 4.3.1).

Whole genome sequencing

Samples with a Ct-value below 32 were subjected to amplicon-based sequencing, following the NanoPore protocol “PCR tiling of COVID-19 virus (Version: PTC_9096_v109_revE_06FEB2020)” derived from the previously described ARTIC v3 protocol [13, 14]. Whole genome sequencing was performed on an Oxford Nanopore R9.4.1 flow cell. Genome assembly was performed with our in-house analysis pipeline [15]. Sequences were only considered with a coverage of >50x depth-of-coverage and with less than 5% ambiguity.

Nucleocapsid substitution analysis and phylogeny

SARS-CoV-2 whole genomes were used to analyze presence of nucleocapsid substitutions. Sequences were then analyzed using NextClade (Web 2.14.1) for variant determination and to examine nucleocapsid substitutions [16]. These substitutions were used in an analysis to compare their proportions in RDT+/PCR + and RDT-/PCR + participants which was assessed through Chi-squared tests. Visualization was performed using Python (v.3.10) and the seaborn package.

To compare sequences based on their RDT result, we aligned the curated sequences to a reference sequence (EPI ISL: 12954045) of a descendent omicron variant BA.1. By comparing against a newer ancestral reference, we were able to capture small differences more easily. We used this comparison to identify clustering between RDT-negative samples, which would be an indication for genomic regions that are possibly responsible for diminished detection by RDT. We downsampled sequences per ISO week and based on a positive RDT result to get an equal representation between RDT+/PCR + and RDT-/PCR + samples over time. The evaluated alignment was used to generate a phylogeny based on a maximum likelihood approach in IQtree (v. 1.6.12). GTR + F + R2 Model was used according to the Bayesian Information Criterion based on the embedded ModelFinder function [17, 18]. The resulting tree was rooted in Figtree (v. 1.4.4) on our BA.1 reference sequence and the phylogeny was visualized using R (v. 4.3.1) and the ggplot2 package [19].

RDT performance testing

RDT results were reported by each participant as described above. In case of discrepancies between the reported RDT result and RT-PCR results of the NTS, we tested the NTS sample (with Ct-values below 21) for reactivity in the RDT. RDT lysis buffer was mixed in a 1:1 ratio with NTS sample and 50 µl was added to the RDT cassette. After 15 min incubation at room temperature, results were scored by three independent assessors.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Between October 2022 and October 2023, this study collected 528,350 questionnaires that were filled out every week from 16,893 different participants (55.1% female). In 99,044 of these questionnaires (18.7%), participants reported ARI symptoms. In this period, we received 7,274 NTS for follow-up with diagnostics, of which 75.1% of the samples were coupled to a reported RDT result. All sampled participants in this study were older than 16 years (median age = 53 years, 64.7% female).

Diagnostic PCR showed low Ct-values in RDT positive and negative samples

Characteristics of the NTS samples were compared to RDT results. We analyzed RDT and NTS results of 1,757 self-test-positive/NTS PCR positive samples (RDT+/PCR+) and 359 self-test-negative/NTS PCR positive samples (RDT-/PCR+). Out of all RDT-negative samples, these 359 samples (4.3%) were SARS-CoV-2 positive in PCR and could therefore be classified as false-negative samples. The median time between sampling for RDT and sampling for the NTS was 0 days (IQR: −1–0 days). Figure 1 shows that the share of RDT-/PCR + within all SARS-CoV-2 PCR-positive participants did not significantly differ over time of our study, indicative for a lack of increase in circulation of selection of novel variants that possibly “escape” detection by RDTs.

To compare NTS samples from RDT+/PCR + and RDT-/PCR + participants, Fig. 2 shows the distribution of Ct-values within the RDT-/PCR + and RDT+/PCR + group. The median Ct-value of the RDT-negative group was significantly higher than that of the RDT + group. This indicated a lower viral load for the RDT-negative group (median Ct-value RDT-positive: 21.0, RDT-negative: 25.3, p < 0.01). The Ct-values of the RDT-/PCR + group varied greatly (IQR: 21.4–30.7) indicating that based on PCR both low and high viral load samples tested falsely negative in RDT. The samples with highest viral load in our RDT-negative group were used in our laboratory-based RDT validation described below.

Figure 3 shows no correlation between the period of sampling and the Ct-values from the NTS for the RDT-negative group (R2 = 0.0002), again indicative of absence of increase of circulation of novel SARS-CoV-2 variants that are not detected by RDTs.

Nucleocapsid substitutions are similar in RDT positive and negative groups

SARS-CoV-2 positive NTS samples were sequenced to identify virus variants. This allowed characterization of weekly trends that could be compared to nationwide sequencing programs. The comparison (Fig. 4) showed that samples within the study were representative of viruses observed in the general population. Firstly, we compared the nucleocapsid substitutions in our sequences, to identify presence of, or over-representation of specific changes in the N protein that could explain the lack of detection of N protein by the RDT. Figure 5 shows one unique substitution in the RDT-/PCR + group for N: A182S. This substitution only occurred in two samples in the RDT-negative group (p < 0.05, Chi-Squared test). In addition, three substitutions were identified with significantly higher proportions in RDT-negative compared to RDT-positive samples. These three substitutions are the deletion in GER30-32 (11.2% versus 7.4%) substitution S33F (11.2% versus 7.1%) as well as Q229K in the nucleocapsid (0.8% versus 0.06%) (Chi-Squared Test, and for all: P < 0.05). However, the observed differences in proportions of these mutations and corresponding amino acid changes in the N protein within the RDT-negative compared to the RDT-positive group were small and did not increase over our period of observation. The fact that they were detected in both the RDT-positive and RDT-negative group, shows that the substitution does not significantly affect the detection of the variant by the RDTs, as further elaborated on in our laboratory-based RDT validation.

Laboratory-based RDT duplicate generated positive test results

To account for the effect of methodology on falsely negative self-tests, we reproduced RDTs using the NTS material of RDT-negative participants that had an NTS with a high viral load. Samples from the RDT-/PCR + group showing the lowest Ct-values (highest viral load) were selected (Ct-value < 21; n = 75). In addition, two samples containing a virus with Q229K were included, despite low viral load which is around the limit of detection of the RDT. All samples tested did result in a clear positive self-test, as assessed by three assessors, confirming our PCR and sequencing approaches. The NPS sample containing the unique N: A182S substitution as well as samples containing substitution S33F or deletion in GER30-32 that were overrepresented in the RDT-negative group also resulted in a positive RDT in the laboratory validation. The two samples containing Q229K showed a weak positive result, corresponding to the low viral load of the sample.

Discussion

Continuous monitoring of SARS-CoV-2 RDTs performance remains important for their use in self-testing to inform self-isolation decision making. We show that routine collection of paired NTS and RDT in a participatory surveillance system provides a valuable way to monitor the impact of virus evolution on the functioning of RDTs. By analyzing 7,274 NTS samples of symptomatic participants with ARI, we were able to compare PCR and sequence results in depth for a SARS-CoV-2 RDT-positive and -negative group. Even during times of high genetic diversity of the virus and implemented policies which advised self-testing as grounds for self-isolation, we found no indication of altered RDT sensitivity due to evolutionary changes in SARS-CoV-2. We did not find an increase in false-negative-RDTs among our population cohort. Also, we did not see selection of substitutions in the nucleocapsid, nor did we find clustering when analyzing the entire genome profile in RDT-negative isolates. This leads us to conclude that these RDTs can properly detect currently circulating variants and that there is no evidence that genetic drift affected SARS-CoV-2 self-test performance.

We found higher Ct-values through RT-PCR in the paired NTS samples of participants with a negative self-test results. This indicates that low viral loads contribute to false-negative self-testing and confirms challenges of sensitivity of RDTs, as described in previous studies conducted elsewhere [3, 20]. However, high viral loads were also observed in the PCR results for a considerable number of samples of RDT-negative participants, which is not consistent with sensitivity as the only contributor to false-negative testing. Others have found very high sensitivity of RDTs performed by professionals in samples with low Ct-values (93.6% for Ct-value < 25), but this leaves about 50% of our false-negative results unexplained [21].

Due to mutations within the viral genome, molecular tests constantly need to be evaluated and possibly updated. Likewise, changes in the N protein can lead to diminished or even abolished recognition by RDTs.

We compared viral sequences from self-test negative samples with self-test positive samples and found some substitutions that occur in slightly higher proportion in self-test negative samples. However, three of these four substitutions are also found in self-test positive samples, indicating that detection is still feasible. Our laboratory validation confirmed that these substitution variations can be observed using self-tests. It is therefore unlikely that these substitutions affect the self-test sensitivity to variants significantly. In literature we do not find an association of any of these substitutions with RDT methods and these were also not found in deep mutational scanning studies [6]. Other studies have mentioned more RDT false-negative results in context of newer variants, but this can be linked to an overall lower viral load which can be attributed to the combination of these variants and an increased immunity in the population [3, 9, 21, 22].

Our suggestion is that self-sampling methodology of RDT users contributes to false-negative RDT results in which case suboptimal self-sampling changes RDT sensitivity compared to PCR, which has been stated before [23]. We think difficulties in reading a weak signal on the RDT may also play a role. Although the latter might be less relevant, according to an earlier study that revealed similar results for professional and laymen readouts of self-tests [24]. The PCR + NTS samples of participants (with Ct-values lower than 21) that reported a negative RDT result, were found RDT-positive in the laboratory, providing some evidence that discordant results between PCR and RDT results are possibly due to suboptimal sampling of the sample used for self-testing and/or execution of the RDT by participants.

There are some limitations to our study. Sampling for self-testing by RDTs and NTS for PCR testing might not have been taken simultaneously for each participant, possibly presenting different stages of disease. However, we observed that for most cases, they were taken within the same day or the next day. Furthermore, the time between symptom onset and testing might lead to reduced viral load at the time of sampling, as it is thought that the peak of viral loads coincides with symptom onset. However, our RDT and PCR results are coupled and therefore comparison between different variants in their sensitivity to the RDT is still strong. Tropism of the virus also plays a role in this comparison since for RDTs only the nose was sampled, while for PCR nose and throat were sampled, which can affect viral load in the tested sample. There is currently no evidence of SARS-CoV-2 variants with a specific site of replication. However, this is something to keep in mind when comparing results of RDT and PCR in the future.

The study only included a symptomatic population, we can therefore not state anything about the usage of RDTs in asymptomatic individuals. Like most studies, we struggle with any possible biases in participation which we tried to reduce with the accessibility of sampling and questionnaires. Although samples were not stratified for participant specific characteristics, our ticketing-system ensured maximum randomness for sample selection within our cohort. Furthermore, we have no reason to assume that any biases in our study population would have an effect on presence of specific SARS-COV-2 variants, and their subsequent sensitivity in RDTs.

During this study, the use of RDTs was encouraged by providing all participants with tests and resupplying when necessary. This led to the use of RDTs from a single brand, of which extensive in-house laboratory validation had previously occurred. Quality tests comparing various SARS-CoV-2 variants have been done to test performance of this test and put this into context with other RDTs [25]. The conclusion of our study can therefore be used as a reference to pursue RDT sensitivity studies for the evolving SARS-CoV-2 virus, but not as a conclusion for the sensitivity of all available RDTs on the market, although many brands rely on similar or even identical reagents in their tests. When compared to national surveillance, our dataset appears to be representative, regarding observed variants. Our diagnostic PCR is robust as it is extensively checked and updated based on national surveillance data, leading to consistency in our measured Ct-values. The above qualities provide a strong argument for the Infectieradar program, as a scalable surveillance framework that provides a nearly real-time dataset. Due to the high and constant engagement of participants in questionnaires, we reached them shortly after symptom onset. The large sample size of this study led to a representative group of false-negatives and their NPS samples helped us verify their results in the laboratory. Participant-based sampling makes Infectieradar accessible for participants and cost-efficient.

Understanding the sensitivity and specificity of RDTs is essential when interpreting surveillance data. New variants and developing immunity levels in the population seemed to lead to more asymptomatic cases. Updating these results is therefore necessary if used in future scenarios. Since Infectieradar is a broad surveillance program with sampling based on ARI symptoms it facilitates monitoring multiple respiratory viruses at once. This overarching initiative can result in more cost-effective monitoring. Not only does this study about monitoring RDT performance inform future policymaking, but it also provides opportunities for research and evaluation by utilizing the programs longitudinal and scalable character. This way, Infectieradar can contribute to pandemic preparedness.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our analyses show that, up to 2023, there have not been evolutionary changes in SARS-CoV-2 that contributed to false-negative results by RDTs used in this study. The study emphasizes that our participatory surveillance system, with active sampling based on symptoms, contributes to both the national disease surveillance as well as the monitoring and evaluation of RDTs.
