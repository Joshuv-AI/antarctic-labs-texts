Feasibility and safety of right heart catheterization via median cubital vein in patients with pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Yuxing Lai, Lingyun He, Han Cai, Wei Zhou, Jinzi Su, Pingping Wu, Zhoufei Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the feasibility and safety of right heart catheterization (RHC) via the cubital vein.MethodsThis retrospective-prospective cohort study included 93 patients (January 2020–June 2023) in the retrospective phase, with 48 undergoing cubital vein RHC (R-CV) and 45 femoral vein RHC (R-FV). A prospective phase (July 2023–October 2024) enrolled 60 patients, each undergoing both approaches. Subgroup analysis divided patients by right ventricular end-diastolic diameter (≥ 3.80 cm vs. <3.80 cm).ResultsR-CV showed fewer complications (8.89% vs. 0%, p = 0.028) and shorter fluoroscopy time (178.27 ± 43.27 s vs. 210.76 ± 54.80 s, p = 0.002) than R-FV. In patients with right ventricular dilation (≥ 3.80 cm), cubital vein RHC reduced fluoroscopy time compared to femoral vein RHC (p < 0.001).ConclusionCubital vein RHC is safe, efficient, and especially advantageous for patients with enlarged right ventricles, warranting wider adoption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05604-3.Keywords: Right heart catheterization, Pulmonary hypertension, Cubital vein

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a severe clinical syndrome characterized by progressively increased pulmonary vascular resistance. With the increasing availability and development of echocardiography, the detection rate of PH has risen, with a global prevalence of approximately 1%, and it may reach 10% in individuals over the age of 65 [1, 2]. The 5-year survival rate for patients with idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (IPAH) is only 34%, which is lower than many malignant cancers [3]. Recent advances in the understanding of its pathophysiology [4–6] and the development of therapeutic modalities such as pharmacological and device-based treatments, have significantly improved the prognosis of PH patients [7, 8]. However, timely diagnosis remains crucial for initiating effective treatment.

Currently, right heart catheterization (RHC) is considered the gold standard for diagnosing PH, with a mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) greater than 20 mmHg at rest and sea level [9, 10]. RHC not only aids in the classification of PH [11] and guides subtype-specific treatment and prevention strategies to improve prognosis [12], but it also evaluates surgical feasibility in congenital heart disease-related PH, assesses cardiac function and hemodynamics, and provides reference for surgical risk evaluation [13]. Additionally, RHC allows for acute vascular response testing and pulmonary arteriography, which can guide drug therapy and localize pulmonary vascular lesions [9].

The most commonly used vascular access for RHC is through central veins such as the femoral, internal jugular, and subclavian veins. Although the femoral vein approach is widely used, it is associated with risks of complications such as hematoma at the puncture site, deep vein thrombosis, pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, and even rare retroperitoneal hematoma [14]. Post-procedural immobilization for several hours increases discomfort and complications related to bed rest. On the other hand, the subclavian or internal jugular routes may lead to severe complications like pneumothorax, hemothorax, air embolism, and arterial injury [15]. Patient-specific differences often lead to increased difficulty with deep vein puncture [16]. The widespread use of bedside ultrasound has improved the safety and feasibility of deep vein punctures [17], but it has also raised the skill and learning curve for operators. As of now, there are no clear guidelines on which route should be preferred for RHC.

As a diagnostic procedure, RHC may not be widely accepted if associated with high surgical risks, which is one of the main barriers to its clinical adoption. The cubital vein, as a peripheral superficial vein, is commonly used for intravenous infusion. Its fixed anatomical location, ease of puncture, and compression for hemostasis offer potential advantages such as simplicity, fewer complications, and higher patient comfort. Our team has reported successful experiences using the cubital vein for adrenal vein blood sampling and temporary pacemaker implantation [18, 19]. Therefore, we aimed to explore the feasibility of performing RHC through the cubital vein. We conducted a clinical study that combines both retrospective and prospective cohorts. Our objective is to compare the feasibility and safety of RHC via the cubital vein with the femoral vein, improving the technique to make RHC more accessible and widely implemented.

Patients and methods

Study design

This study employed a combined design of a retrospective cohort and a prospective self-controlled cohort. Initially, a retrospective analysis of historical case data was performed to explore the differences in procedural indicators and safety between the cubital vein and femoral vein approaches, providing preliminary hypotheses and trend evidence for the study. To further control potential selection bias and confounding factors from the retrospective analysis (such as the impact of baseline differences in patient conditions on the choice of access route), we established a prospective self-controlled cohort. In this cohort, each patient underwent RHC via both approaches sequentially, thereby directly comparing the two routes while minimizing individual variation.The subjects were included from the First Hospital of Fujian Medical University. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (approval number: LY2024-082-01). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients participating in the prospective study.

Inclusion criteria: ① Age ≥ 18 years but ≤ 80 years; ② Echocardiography indicated PH or clinically suspected pulmonary hypertension. ③ All patients signed informed consent and received right cardiac catheterization.

Exclusion criteria: ① Acute coronary syndrome, cerebrovascular accident, severe peripheral vascular disease, and history of major surgical procedures within 1 month; ② End stage malignant tumor; ③ Serious blood system diseases; ④ The patient is pregnant, breastfeeding, or planning to conceive; ⑤ Severe allergy to iodinated contrast agents; ⑥ Severe renal insufficiency, serum creatinine > 176 µ mol/L; ⑦ Currently participating in other clinical or drug studies; ⑧ Unwilling or unable to sign an informed consent form.

A total of 153 patients were included. 93 patients were divided into two groups (R-CV group, n = 48; R-FV group, n = 45). 60 patients received RHC procedure via median cubital vein and femoral vein. Baseline data

① Age, gender, height, weight; ② Record baseline serum biochemical parameters, including creatinine, glomerular filtration rate, uric acid, total cholesterol, triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, glycated hemoglobin; ③ Record baseline echocardiograms of left atrial internal diameter, left ventricular end-diastolic internal diameter, interventricular septal thickness, left ventricular ejection fraction, right ventricular end-diastolic internal diameter, pulmonary arterial systolic pressure.

RHC parameters: mean pulmonary artery pressure, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, right ventricular pressure, right atrial pressure, vena cava pressure, and fluoroscopy time. Safety parameters include the presence or absence of complications such as hematoma (> 3 cm) at the puncture site, deep vein thrombosis, arteriovenous fistula, pseudoaneurysm.

Grouping method and subgroup analysis

Based on the route of RHC implementation (cubital vein OR femoral vein), the retrospective study was categorized into R-CV and R-FV groups, and baseline dates and RHC parameters were compared between the two groups. Similarly, the prospective study was categorized into P-CV and P-FV groups and baseline information and RHC parameters were compared between the two groups. All patients in the retrospective and prospective studies were uniformly included in the analysis and divided into FV ≥ 3.80 cm, FV < 3.80 cm, CV ≥ 3.80 cm, and CV < 3.80 cm groups based on the right ventricular end-diastolic internal diameter in the echocardiograms, using 3.80 as the cutoff point. The differences in RHC parameters between the groups were compared separately.

Right heart catheterization procedure

All operations were performed by two experienced operators. The RHC procedure was performed via the femoral vein by: ① ultrasound-guided puncture of the femoral vein in the operating room after disinfection and local anesthesia; ② Insertion of a guidewire after the puncture needle entered the femoral vein, removal of the puncture needle, and insertion of a 7 F sheath along the guidewire (Fig. 2A); ③Use of a 0.014-inch guidewire to guide a Swan-Ganz (Edwards Lifesciences LLC, CA, USA) catheter under fluoroscopic X-ray vision. CA, USA) catheter. ④ The Swan-Ganz catheter was sequentially passed through the femoral vein - inferior vena cava - right atrium - right ventricle to reach the pulmonary artery. ⑤ The Swan-Ganz catheter balloon was inflated with 1.5 ml, and the pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, pulmonary artery pressure, right ventricular pressure, right atrial pressure, inferior vena cava pressure, and oxygen saturation of each chamber were measured sequentially. ⑥ The Swan-Ganz catheter was withdrawn and a pigtail catheter was delivered to the pulmonary artery for pulmonary arteriography.

Statistic analysis

All data were statistically analyzed by SPSS 25.0, and the distribution of data was analyzed by Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed measures were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s), and t-test was used for comparison between groups.Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (quartile) M(Qi-Qu), and non-parametric test method (Mann-Whitney U test) was used for comparison between groups. Categorical variables were tested using the x2 test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Furthermore, fluoroscopy time was significantly decreased in the CV ≥ 3.80 cm group compared to the FV ≥ 3.80 cm group (192.96 ± 45.48 s vs. 229.28 ± 49.90 s, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

RHC is an essential part of the diagnosis and classification of pulmonary arterial hypertension. However, the complications of deep vein puncture and the difficulty of operation have restricted the widespread implementation of RHC. To solve this problem, we performed RHC through cubital vein access to explore a more efficient and simple method. In this study, through retrospective and prospective studies, we found that compared with the traditional femoral vein route, the cubital vein route for RHC significantly reduced puncture-related complications and shortened operative fluoroscopy time. At the same time there was no significant difference between the two modalities in obtaining right heart catheterization parameters.

A meta-analysis by Florian G[15] found that the complication rate of peripheral venous access, including the cubital vein, was significantly lower than that of the femoral vein pathway (0.3% versus 1.1%; p = 0.04), whereas the jugular vein pathway had the highest complication rate (2%), which was why we did not choose the jugular vein pathway. The data for this meta-analysis came from different countries. Therefore, it must be considered that the method of puncture of the peripheral vein and whether it is guided by ultrasound determines the success rate of puncture and the incidence of complications. So, we further explored the potential advantages of cubital vein access as the main venous route for RHC through a controlled study based on the improvement of the cubital vein puncture process.

Preoperatively, we established a cubital vein indwelling needle in the ward and used the guidewire-sheath exchange technique during surgery to place the sheath quickly, reducing steps and improving efficiency. The right cubital vein consisted of the cephalic vein, the median cubital vein, and basilic vein. The cephalic vein was superficially located and well exposed and was preferred. The basilic vein was thick and straight, but deeper in location, as a second choice. If the patient was obese or had tortuous veins, and multiple punctures were unsuccessful in creating a cubital vein needle, we used ultrasound guidance to create a cubital vein needle. With the increasing proficiency in ultrasound application and venipuncture techniques, all patients in this study successfully had an indwelling needle placed in one of these veins preoperatively, demonstrating the technical feasibility of the cubital vein approach. Theoretically, the cubital vein’s superficial position allows for easy compression for hemostasis, making it particularly suitable for patients undergoing anticoagulation therapy (e.g., warfarin or novel oral anticoagulants), and it has the potential for outpatient procedures. However, further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate its safety.

We compared patients with RHC via cubital vein and RHC via femoral vein in a retrospective study and found that fluoroscopy time was significantly shorter in the cubital vein group, which was related to the anatomical and physiological structure of the right heart. The catheter introduced through the cubital vein enters the right atrium at a more favorable angle aligned with the natural direction of blood flow (Fig. 1), facilitating smoother passage through the tricuspid and pulmonary valves, thus significantly reducing the time required to traverse these two structures. Notably, the reduction in fluoroscopy time decreases the exposure of both the patient and the operator to radiation. In contrast, the catheter introduced via the femoral vein often requires guidewire assistance or may need to coil within the right atrium and right ventricle to adjust its direction, which increases the irritation and potential damage to the myocardium. Our center has even observed cases of elevated troponin levels post-RHC. This difficulty is particularly pronounced in patients with significant right ventricular enlargement, where the challenge of crossing the pulmonary valve is further increased, and the relative lack of support for the catheter leads to a prolonged time in reaching the distal branches of the pulmonary artery. These factors collectively contribute to an increased overall fluoroscopy time with the femoral vein approach.The curvature of the cubital vein pathway through the right atrium into the pulmonary artery was visually smaller than the femoral vein pathway under Digital Subtraction Angiography(DSA) fluoroscopy (Fig. 1). This suggests that through the cubital vein, the Swan-Ganz catheter is more easily guided by the guidewire into the right ventricle. Obviously, the shorter fluoroscopy time reduced intraoperative radiation damage to both the patient and the operator. In contrast, the femoral vein pathway right heart catheterization into the pulmonary artery relied on the guidance of the guidewire or the catheter coiled into a circle in the right atrium, thus increasing to a certain extent the stimulation and damage to the myocardium by the guidewire and catheter, and we found cases of elevated troponin after RHC.

In this study, postoperative complications associated with the cubital vein approach were significantly reduced. This may be attributed to several factors, including the superficial location of the cubital vein, which facilitates compression for hemostasis and reduces the risk of hematoma; the absence of deep fascial penetration, which lowers the risk of injury to adjacent arteries and nerves; the ability for patients to avoid bed rest postoperatively, thereby reducing immobilization-related complications; the improved accuracy of puncture under ultrasound guidance; and the shorter procedure time. These factors collectively demonstrate that the cubital vein approach reduces complications such as hematoma (> 3 cm) at the puncture site, deep vein thrombosis, arteriovenous fistula, and pseudoaneurysm, offering superior safety.Considering postoperative care, the cubital vein path did not require bed braking, which was especially suitable for patients with advanced age and poor basal conditions.

Previous studies showed [20] that pulmonary hypertension can lead to enlargement of the right ventricle, and the right ventricle was subjected to excessive pressure load for a long period of time, which also led to an increase in pulmonary artery pressure, forming a vicious circle and causing progressive deterioration of cardiac function. When moderate-to-severe pulmonary hypertension was combined with right ventricular enlargement, patients were often accompanied by reduced oxygen saturation and severe heart failure, with a poor prognosis [21]. It was difficult to perform RHC in these patients, with high risk of complications, and some patients even required multiple RHCs during the course of treatment. Therefore, when preoperative echocardiographic screening detected moderate-to-severe pulmonary arterial hypertension combined with a significantly enlarged right ventricular diameter, how to complete the RHC quickly and safely had been a problem that needed to be addressed. Based on the above, we performed subgroup analysis using an echocardiographic right ventricular diameter of 3.80 cm as the cutoff point, and found that pulmonary artery pressure, right ventricular pressure, and fluoroscopy time were significantly increased in patients with right ventricular diameters of ≥ 3.80 cm compared with those with diameters of < 3.80 cm, and that there was no significant difference between cubital vein and femoral vein in terms of the parameters of the right heart catheterization obtained when RHC was performed by the cubital vein and femoral vein as the access route. This suggested that RHC in PAH patients with significant right ventricular enlargement required more time, regardless of the route used. Second, in patients with right ventricular diameters ≥ 3.80 cm, we found that RHC with cubital vein access significantly reduced fluoroscopy time compared with femoral vein access. Our study demonstrates the feasibility and advantages of the cubital vein route over the femoral vein route for RHC in patients with significant right ventricular enlargement and provides a new option for RHC in patients with severe pulmonary hypertension.

With the continuous advancement of medical technology, it has been found that the femoral artery route has more complications. In contrast, the radial artery route has fewer complications and higher patient comfort, and has become the most important vascular access for left heart catheterization. The current guidelines in the United States and Europe consider it feasible to routinely perform left heart catheterization through the radial artery pathway [22, 23]. Of course, arterial and venous routes cannot be generalized, and further validation of the cubital venous route as the preferred vascular access for RHC is needed.

We found that the cubital vein route for RHC was not inferior to the central venous route in RHC and it is consistent with lower complications and fluoroscopy time. Therefore, we believed that routine right heart catheterization via the cubital vein route was feasible and should be preferred, especially in patients with large right ventricular diameters. This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, the retrospective nature of part of the analysis may introduce potential bias. Specifically, high-risk patients may have been preferentially assigned to the cubital vein approach, as some patients with New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class IV heart failure, who are unable to lie flat, could not undergo the femoral vein approach.however, the cubital vein approach remains feasible in a semi-recumbent position. Additionally, the choice of vascular access and specific catheter selection were based on the operator’s preference, which may have introduced further selection bias. Formal inter-observer variability analysis was not conducted.Therefore, in order to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results, future multicenter, large cohort studies are necessary to further validate the findings of this study.

Conclusion

The results significantly showed the safety of the antecubital vein compared to the femoral vein access site, with fewer complications and shorter fluoroscopy time. In addition, the anatomical course was found to be more feasible in patients with enlarged right ventricles, as fluoroscopy indicated easier advancement of the Swan-Ganz catheter through the dilated right ventricle into the pulmonary arteries. The simplified procedural workflow is more amenable to clinical adoption and is expected to become the preferred vascular access route for RHC.
