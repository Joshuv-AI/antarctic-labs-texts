Deep learning-based classification of gh-secreting pituitary neuroendocrine tumors granulation patterns using H&E-stained histopathology
Authors: Zeming Yan, Yi Lin, Qingjun Cen, Wanming Hu, Zongming Wang, Zize Feng, Jing Guo, Xiaobing Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate classification of granulation patterns in growth hormone-secreting pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (GH-PitNETs) is clinically essential but traditionally dependent on labor-intensive immunohistochemistry (IHC). To address this, we developed the Cross-Attention Pituitary Neuroendocrine Tumor Network (CA-PITNET), a deep learning model for automated subtyping directly from routine H&E-stained whole-slide images (WSIs). Utilizing 95 GH-PitNETs WSIs (54,610 patches) and 36 nonfunctioning PitNET WSIs (39,590 patches), the model was evaluated on two tasks: distinguishing GH-PitNETs from nonfunctioning PitNETs (Task I) and differentiating densely from sparsely granulated GH-PitNETs (Task II). Following staining normalization and rigorous validation, CA-PITNET demonstrated exceptional diagnostic accuracy. For Task I, CA-PITNET achieved patient-level AUCs of 0.948 (95% CI: 0.854–1.000; internal validation, n = 48) and 0.948 (95% CI: 0.837–1.000; external test, n = 33), with accuracy of 0.938 and 0.909, respectively. For Task II, patient-level AUCs were 0.935 (95% CI: 0.890–1.000; internal validation, n = 38) and 0.875 (95% CI: 0.670–1.000; external test, n = 25), with accuracy of 0.895 and 0.840, respectively. Generated heatmaps precisely localized diagnostic regions, highlighting diagnostically relevant regions consistent with pathologist-reviewed areas. This model is designed to augment the pathological workflow by improving the subtyping efficiency of GH-PitNETs, serving as a valuable adjunct to the standard IHC-based classification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02393-w.

Introduction

Pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs) are one of the most common intracranial tumors with an incidence of approximately 1 case per 1000 in the general population [1]. Growth hormone (GH)-secreting PitNETs comprise approximately 8–16% of pituitary neuroendocrine tumors (PitNETs) and are associated with substantial morbidity and increased mortality [2–3]. Acromegaly, which results from chronic GH hypersecretion, is characterized by enlargement of the hands, feet, forehead, jaw, and nose [4–6]. Moreover, more than 50% of patients with acromegaly develop cardiovascular and respiratory complications. Additionally, the incidence of secondary neoplasms is elevated in patients with acromegaly compared to the general population [7–9]. GH-PitNETs are generally categorized as densely granulated (DGGH) and sparsely granulated (SGGH) subtypes [10]. SGGH adenoma mainly presents invasive growth and is not sensitive to conventional drugs, while DGGH adenoma presents non-invasive growth and has a better responsiveness to conventional drugs such as octreotide, and then the patient may have a good prognosis [11]. Therefore, distinguishing SGGH and DGGH adenomas is importance when considering the therapeutic strategy.

SGGH and DGGH adenomas are pathologically distinguished primarily by cytokeratin (CK) immunohistochemistry, with CAM5.2 (targeting CK8/18) being a key marker. The presence of fibrous bodies is the most characteristic histological hallmark of SGGH. Immunohistochemically, fibrous bodies appear as discrete, perinuclear spherical inclusions (typically 2–5 μm in diameter) that exhibit strong, dot-like CAM5.2 positivity. In contrast, DGGH adenomas typically demonstrate strong, diffuse cytoplasmic CAM5.2 staining (often described as a perinuclear pattern due to its distribution around the nucleus, but morphologically distinct from the discrete inclusions of SGGH). SGGH adenomas, aside from the occasional fibrous bodies, generally show weak, focal, or negative CAM5.2 staining in the background cytoplasm. Thus, the pattern of CAM5.2 immunoreactivity—discrete spherical inclusions versus diffuse cytoplasmic staining—serves as a critical diagnostic criterion [12]. Nevertheless, making a pathologic diagnosis is cumbersome and challenging due to the subtle morphological differences among GH- PitNETs, which require comprehensive image scanning and multiple immunohistochemical markers. It requires high demand for clinical experience of neuropathologists. Additionally, the high economic cost is another limiting factor. With the rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI), increasing studies are attempting to apply deep learning algorithm for the classification and diagnosis of various tumours, including ovarian, breast, lung, gastric, and colorectal cancers [13–19]. Morphological characteristics captured in the routine H&E staining can also reflect the underlying molecular or genetic information. Some pathologists have attempted to predict tumor subtypes based on HE-stained pathological images using statistical methods [20–21]. Deep learning algorithms have been shown to assess tumour heterogeneity of colorectal cancer without gene expression profiling, based on H&E images [22–24]. Previous studies tried to predict the granulation patterns of GH-PitNETs based on preoperative magnetic resonance radiomics. However, the prediction of GH-PitNETs subtypes directly from H&E-stained images represents a more efficient and clinically valuable approach.

Based on our previously developed MSSA-DIS framework [25], this study presents a novel and effective two-stage multi-instance learning classification framework for WSI analysis of PitNETs. This method simultaneously fuses information between different instances at various scales and aggregates them into a fixed-size vector required by the network’s continuous layers for regression. We demonstrated the efficacy of this model by distinguishing GH secreting PitNETs from nonfunctioning ones. Moreover, this model also displays satisfactory performance in the classification of GH-PitNETs subtypes.

Materials and methods

Study populations

From January 2018 to December 2023, 106 patients with pathologically confirmed PitNETs (according to the 2022 WHO classification) treated at SYSUCC were enrolled as the training and internal validation cohorts. Additionally, eligible patients from the First and Third Affiliated Hospitals of Sun Yat-sen University constituted two independent external validation sets (25 patients). The patient sample information included in this study is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Data processing strategy

WSIs of PitNETs typically comprise billions of pixels, necessitating division into smaller, manageable patches for computational analysis. However, not all patches contain diagnostically relevant information, as many represent background areas, tissue folds, or regions affected by staining artifacts that commonly arise during H&E preparation due to inconsistent reagent concentrations or temperature fluctuations. To address these challenges and select the most representative patches for accurate classification, we designed a two-stage whole slide multi-instance learning classification framework (Fig. 1). WSIs were fully scanned and divided into non-overlapping patches (1024 × 1024 pixels), with blank regions filtered by RGB thresholding. First, a bag filtering network was designed to filter effective instances. Then, inspired by the clinical workflow of pathologists, who typically examine histopathological slides at various magnification levels, we designed a multiscale attention network incorporating multi-resolution inputs. For each region of interest, multi-scale image patches were extracted at low, medium, and high magnifications. These patches were then processed using a cross-attention-based feature aggregation mechanism, which dynamically fuses nuclear morphological features with tissue-level architectural patterns, thereby emulating the integrative diagnostic reasoning employed by pathologists.

Feature saliency-guided patch selection

Guided by pathologists’ diagnostic approach, we developed a selection network that identifies key diagnostic regions using discriminative confidence scores, prioritizing morphologically specific patches. By linking prediction confidence to histopathological discriminability, our method automatically highlights salient morphological features.

Specifically, confidence scores were computed as the softmax prediction probability output of the ConvNeXt-based selection network for each patch. Patches were ranked in descending order of confidence, and those falling below a task-specific threshold were discarded as non-informative. The threshold was empirically determined on the training set to ensure a minimum of 300 diagnostically representative patches were retained per WSI, balancing computational efficiency with diagnostic coverage.

Based on the previously developed MSSA-DIS framework, this study constructed a multi-scale analysis system for PitNET classification. CA-PITNET employs MSAN to extract diagnostically relevant morphological features and Cross-Attention Attention-Based Multiple Instance Learning module (CA-AbMIL) to integrate cellular-level features with tissue architecture (Fig. 2). Key features like nuclear membrane irregularity (high-power) dynamically guide tissue feature fusion, replicating the pathological diagnostic paradigm linking cellular abnormalities to tissue structural changes.

Implementation details

The computational framework was implemented using PyTorch 1.12.1 with CUDA 11.3 acceleration. WSIs were processed into 1024 × 1024 pixel patches, followed by automated filtering to exclude non-informative regions (background/noise) through RGB thresholding. For model input, patches were resized to 224 × 224 pixels with standard data augmentation (random cropping/flipping) and normalization.

The network architecture utilized a ConvNeXt backbone initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights. The MSAN underwent parameter optimization using 16,356 training patches for Task I and 10,637 training patches for Task II, ensuring robust feature extraction across diagnostic tasks. The CA-AbMIL module was subsequently trained with frozen MSAN weights to maintain feature stability while optimizing attention-based aggregation of multi-scale features.

Model optimization employed AdamW (weight decay=1e-4) with cosine annealing learning rate scheduling (5e-5→5e-8 over 100 epochs). Training was conducted on NVIDIA Tesla A800 GPU with batch size 16, ensuring diagnostic relevance by maintaining ≥ 300 representative patches per WSI through instance selection.

To contextualize CA-PITNET’s performance, we compared against seven established MIL-based WSI classification methods. Embedding + max and Embedding+mean are non-attention MIL baselines that aggregate patch-level features via max- or mean-pooling respectively, without learned instance weighting. AbMIL introduces a gated attention mechanism that assigns learned scalar weights to each patch, enabling the model to prioritize diagnostically relevant instances. TransMIL replaces the attention aggregator with a Transformer encoder, modeling long-range spatial dependencies across patches via self-attention. DTFD-MIL employs a double-tier pseudo-bag distillation strategy that subdivides bags into pseudo-bags during training, improving generalization on small datasets. SA-AbMIL extends AbMIL with spatial attention, incorporating positional relationships between patches. CLAM-SB and CLAM-MB use attention-based clustering with instance-level supervision constraints, with single-branch (SB) and multi-branch (MB) variants targeting binary and multi-class settings respectively. All comparator models were re-implemented from publicly available code repositories using consistent training protocols: identical ConvNeXt feature extractor, AdamW optimizer, patch preprocessing pipeline, and train/validation/test splits as CA-PITNET, ensuring a fair comparison.

Heat map generation

To visualize diagnostically relevant histopathological features, we generated attention heatmaps using gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM). This technique highlights morphological regions that most influenced the model’s predictions, validated against pathologist-annotated regions of interest. Heatmap intensities correlate with the model’s decision confidence, providing interpretable localization of discriminative features.

Statistical analysis

For assessing model performance, we employed accuracy as the evaluation metric on both datasets, with all accuracies calculated using a threshold of 0.5. where TP represents true positives, TN represents true negatives, FP represents false positives, and FN represents false negatives.

The primary performance metric for model comparison was the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), selected for its threshold-independence and robustness to class imbalance. Secondary metrics included accuracy, sensitivity, precision, and F1 score, all calculated at a fixed classification threshold of 0.5. This threshold was adopted as the standard operating point consistent with prior MIL-based WSI classification studies; sensitivity and specificity values reported in this manuscript are derived under this threshold.

Patch-level predictions were aggregated per patient via mean probability averaging, and the 0.5 threshold was applied to the aggregated score for final patient-level classification. 95% confidence intervals for patient-level AUC were estimated using stratified bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

The patient cohort stratification and analytical workflow are detailed in Fig. 3. Key demographic and clinicopathological characteristics of the study population, including age distribution, gender ratio, tumor diameter, Knosp grading, histopathological subtypes, and immunohistochemical profiles are comprehensively summarized in Supplementary Table S1. This supplementary dataset preserves essential baseline parameters while maintaining focus on core analytical findings in the main text.

Briefly, two tasks were designed in this study. In Task I, the model was designed to distinguish GH-secreting from non-functioning pituitary adenomas. Task II focused on classifying the DGGH and SGGH subtypes of GH-PitNETs. All WSIs underwent staining normalization and were divided into training, validation and external validation sets.

For Task I, 75 samples were utilized for training, comprising 58 GH-secreting and 17 non-functioning ones; 48 samples were designated as the validation set (37 GH-secreting, 11 non-functioning), and 33 samples constituted the independent external test set (25 GH-secreting and 8 non-functioning). For Task II, 57 samples were used for training (29 DGGH, 28 SGGH), 38 samples formed the internal validation set (20 DGGH, 18 SGGH), and 25 samples constituted the independent external test set (9 DGGH, 16 SGGH).

All dataset partitioning was performed strictly at the patient level, ensuring that no patches derived from the same patient appeared across training, validation, and test sets, thereby eliminating any risk of data leakage.

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve and normalized confusion matrix for all models are presented in Figs. 4 and 5. The CA-PITNET method exhibited high sensitivity in both datasets, achieving 0.996 [0.988–0.998] in the test dataset, underscoring its proficiency in accurately identifying positive cases. Performance of CA-PITNET and comparison with the other models in Task II

Task II presented considerable challenges for classification due to the high morphological similarity between patches from DGGH and SGGH subtypes. As illustrated in Table 3, the CA-PITNET methodology exhibited markedly superior performance compared to alternative approaches. In the internal validation cohort, CA-PITNET attained an accuracy of 0.951 and an AUC of 0.985 [0.967–0.988]. Furthermore, the method maintained robust outcomes in the independent external validation set, achieving an accuracy of 0.922 and an AUC of 0.928 [0.903–0.935].

Similarly, the sensitivity of the CA-PITNET method was notably high in both sets, with a sensitivity of 0.939 in the validation set and a sensitivity of 0.877 in the test set. The F1 score was consistently strong, ranging between 0.914 and 0.953 across both sets. The CA-PITNET (CE) model also demonstrated robust performance in Task II, with an AUC of 0.944 and an accuracy of 0.843 in the test set.

Patient-level evaluation was additionally conducted for Task II following the same aggregation and bootstrap procedure described in Sect. 3.2. As presented in Table 4, CA-PITNET achieved patient-level accuracy of 0.895, sensitivity of 0.850, specificity of 0.944, F1 score of 0.895, and AUC of 0.935 (95% CI: 0.890–1.000) in the internal validation set (n = 38), and accuracy of 0.840, sensitivity of 0.778, specificity of 0.875, F1 score of 0.778, and AUC of 0.875 (95% CI: 0.670–1.000) in the external test set (n = 25), substantially outperforming all comparator methods. Notably, the external test set comprised 9 DGGH and 16 SGGH cases, with SGGH constituting the majority class (64%), effectively reversing the training class distribution. The SGGH-specific specificity of 0.875 (14/16 correctly identified) under this inverted distribution directly demonstrates that model performance is not driven by majority-class bias. Patient-level confusion matrices for both sets are illustrated in Fig. 6. The wider confidence intervals observed in the external test set reflect the inherent statistical uncertainty of small-cohort evaluation—a recognized limitation in rare tumor subtype research—and the lower CI bound (0.670) nonetheless remains comparable to the best-performing alternative method (DTFD: AUC 0.680) at its point estimate.

To evaluate the influence of various components on the efficacy of the CA-PITNET model, we performed an ablation study for both Task I (Table 5) and Task II (Table 6).

In Task I, the MSAN + CA-AbMIL framework achieved exceptional performance in both internal validation (AUC 0.997, accuracy 0.992) and external testing (AUC 0.996, accuracy 0.974), demonstrating robust generalizability. Notably, the model exhibited near-perfect sensitivity (0.996) and specificity (0.953) in external testing, critical for clinical deployment where false negatives carry high risk. The comprehensive integration of multi-scale attention (MS), spatial attention (SA), and cross-attention pooling (CA-AbMIL) further optimized precision (0.953 vs. 0.941 in CA-AbMIL alone) while maintaining balanced F1 scores (> 0.97), underscoring its diagnostic reliability for subtle histopathological distinctions.

To further contextualize the multi-scale contributions, we note that the cross-attention mechanism in CA-AbMIL dynamically weights feature contributions from each resolution level based on input content, rather than applying fixed scale priorities. In Task II, where the discriminative signal (cytoplasmic granulation density and fibrous body presence) operates at the subcellular level, high-resolution features capturing nuclear contour and cytoplasmic texture are expected to carry greater discriminative weight—consistent with the nuclear-predominant attention patterns observed in Sect. 3.6. However, intermediate- and low-resolution features contribute complementary tissue architectural context: intermediate magnification captures the regional consistency of granulation across tumor cell clusters, while low magnification preserves global tissue topology. The ablation results in Table 6 confirm that multi-scale integration provides net performance benefit despite the dominance of high-resolution cues. We acknowledge that a formal per-scale ablation—training with single-resolution inputs only—would provide more granular attribution of scale-specific contributions, and we identify this as a direction for future work.

Visual interpretation for the results of Task I and II

To enhance interpretability and validate the model’s decision-making process, we visualized the attention distribution of selected patches in Task I (GH-secreting vs. non-functioning PitNETs) and Task II (DGGH vs. SGGH subtyping), as shown in Fig. 7. This focus suggests that while the definitive diagnostic feature for SGGH is the cytoplasmic fibrous body, the model likely identifies secondary nuclear alterations associated with these subtypes, such as distinctive chromatin clumping patterns and nuclear contour irregularities. These nuclear-level features may serve as reliable surrogate morphological markers that correlate with the granulation status, especially given that fibrous bodies—the hallmark of SGGH—can be subtle and challenging to resolve on standard H&E stains without CAM5.2 confirmation. This focus is particularly evident in patches with dense cellular areas, where attention maps highlighted nuclei with varying morphology and chromatin texture. This is biologically plausible, as prior studies have reported that cytoplasmic granulation density in GH-PitNETs co-varies with nuclear size, chromatin distribution, and cellular packing density on H&E. In Task I, nuclear atypia and cellular density differences between GH-secreting and nonfunctioning adenomas provide a more direct discriminative signal, aligning with the model’s high performance (AUC 0.997).

We acknowledge, however, that direct validation of whether the model detects fibrous bodies or cytoplasmic granulation patterns on H&E—rather than nuclear surrogates—remains to be established, ideally through co-registration with serial CAM5.2-stained sections. This constitutes a limitation of the current interpretability analysis and is prioritized for future investigation.

Discussion

Artificial intelligence (AI) has revolutionized pathological diagnosis [26–27] across multiple tumor types, including breast, lung, and colorectal cancers, where deep learning models demonstrate remarkable accuracy in tumor classification based on histopathological features. However, the application of AI in PitNETs [28–30] remains nascent. Current diagnostic workflows for GH-PitNETs rely on immunohistochemistry (IHC) for CK-staining [31] to distinguish DGGH and SGGH subtypes [32–33], a process that typically requires 48–72 h and specialized expertise. While a full IHC panel remains essential for confirming tumor lineage and hormone expression per WHO criteria, CA-PITNET serves as a highly efficient ancillary tool to streamline the identification of granulation patterns. This approach holds significant potential to augment preliminary diagnostics, reduce turnaround times for subtyping, and alleviate the operational burden on histopathology laboratories. It is worth noting that although CA-PITNET can accurately predict the particle phenotype, in clinical practice, IHC staining of PIT1, SF-1, TPIT, GH, and other pituitary hormones is still required to complete the final lineage diagnosis.

While recent studies have explored AI-based Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) radiomics for pituitary adenomas characterization, our study is the first to establish an end-to-end deep learning framework capable of automating both functional classification (GH-secreting vs. non-functioning) and granular subtype diagnosis (DGGH vs. SGGH) directly from routine H&E-stained WSIs, achieving AUCs of 0.996–0.997 and 0.928–0.985, respectively.

We present a two-stage, whole-slide image (WSI) multi-instance learning classification framework based on instance selection. The proposed instance selection network for multi-instance learning adaptively filters patches that represent the image-level features of the slide. Furthermore, our CA-AbMIL pooling method incorporates a cross-attention mechanism, which not only embeds information about the existence of similar instances into the instance feature vectors but also fuses feature vectors from different fields of view for better weighted average pooling. The experimental results on two datasets demonstrate the superiority of our approach, which is primarily utilized for the pathological classification of pituitary adenomas. In the future, we plan to extend our method to the subtyping classification of other tissue tumors. Additionally, our goal is to enhance the interpretability of the MIL method in its results.

The success of our CA-PITNET model hinges on its ability to address two fundamental challenges unique to pituitary tumor pathology. First, the morphological distinction between DGGH and SGGH relies on subtle cytoplasmic granulation patterns rather than overt nuclear atypia or architectural disruption. While transformer-based models excel in global contextual modeling, their computational intensity and reliance on large-scale pretraining limit their applicability to fine-grained cytomorphological tasks requiring high spatial fidelity. To balance computational efficiency and localized feature extraction, we selected ConvNeXt-a modern Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) variant optimized for hierarchical feature learning-as the backbone architecture. This design leverages ConvNeXt’s inherent capacity to preserve spatial granularity, critical for resolving cytoplasmic texture variations that define GH-PitNETs subtypes.

The MSAN emulates the hierarchical evaluation protocol of histopathological diagnosis by integrating cytomorphological details at multiple resolutions. High-resolution feature maps capture nuclear contour irregularities enabling granular analysis akin to pathologists’ focused scrutiny of cellular textures. Intermediate scales assess the regional consistency of granulation patterns across tumor cells, while low-resolution features preserve architectural context through strategic downsampling of high-resolution data. This design ensures a correlation between fine-grained cellular discriminators and tissue-wide structural patterns, despite the inherent limitation of low-resolution scales in resolving tumor border details due to micron-level fidelity constraints. Spatial attention mechanisms further prioritize diagnostically salient regions while suppressing artifacts [34], aligning with systematic evaluation workflows that iteratively link cellular morphology to architectural organization.

CA-AbMIL addresses a key diagnostic challenge—differentiating true sparse granulation from staining artifacts. By adaptively aggregating features from regions rich in diagnostic information, the model reduces the impact of cytoplasmic vacuolation artifacts that may mimic SGGH morphology. This hierarchical multiscale design aligns with WHO [35–36] guidelines for systematic tumor evaluation, where pathologists iteratively correlate cellular details with tissue architecture to confirm subtype diagnoses.

Second, the clinical demand for rapid diagnostic workflows necessitates computational architectures that prioritize both efficiency and interpretability in resource-constrained settings. Whole-slide imaging (WSI) inherently produces spatially complex data where diagnostically irrelevant regions—such as staining artifacts, tissue folds, or acellular zones-interfere with automated analysis. To reconcile computational tractability with diagnostic precision, we designed a hierarchical filtering strategy: first, a lightweight backbone network identifies regions of low morphological certainty through entropy-driven uncertainty mapping; second, spatial coherence validation enforces topological consistency within tumor microenvironments using histopathology-defined criteria. This dual-filtering paradigm significantly reduces computational resource demands while maintaining diagnostic fidelity, achieving specificity improvements that align with pathologists’ selective focus on tumor-enriched areas. Notably, the streamlined architecture demonstrates compatibility with entry-level clinical hardware, circumventing reliance on specialized immunohistochemistry infrastructure-a critical advantage for global health equity in neuropathology services.

In our analysis of visual interpretability, a key observation is that the model pays much more attention to the morphology of the cell nucleus than to the characteristics of the cytoplasm. From a pathological perspective, DGGH and SGGH are defined based on the distribution pattern of their cytokeratin. The characteristic of SGGH is the presence of paranuclear eosinophilic structures - fibrous bodies. However, in pituitary pathology, it is well known that SGGH cells often exhibit more eosinophilic manifestations, with nuclear pleomorphism and eccentric nuclei. In DGGH tumors, tumor cell nuclei are typically centrally located, oval-shaped, and exhibit prominent nucleoli. The preference of CA-PITNET for the nuclear region indicates that it has learned to utilize these nuclear features as an alternative marker for granular structures. This is particularly beneficial for screening based on H&E staining, as in conventional staining, fibroblasts are usually very weak or difficult to distinguish from the background cytoplasm. The model is able to avoid the “weakness” of cytoplasmic features by leveraging high-fidelity nuclear details, highlighting its robustness as a preliminary diagnostic aid.

Despite these advances, our study has limitations that warrant consideration. The scarcity of SGGH specimens constrained model generalizability, as evidenced by the 5.7% AUC drop in external versus internal validation. Multicenter collaborations are urgently needed to expand rare subtype cohorts. Furthermore, while our model focuses on GH-PitNETs, the broader category of PitNETs includes prolactinomas [37] and corticotroph adenomas [38–39], each defined by distinct transcription factors and biomarkers. Extending this framework will require addressing heterogeneous molecular targets and staining protocols. Notably, while the method demonstrates promise for pituitary pathology, its generalizability to tumors with heterogeneous microenvironments [40–42] (e.g., infiltrative gliomas or metastatic carcinomas) remains to be validated. Furthermore, while our attention maps align with regions of interest identified by pathologists, future studies should involve spatial validation using CAM5.2-stained serial sections. Directly overlaying CA-PITNET attention maps with cytokeratin-positive fibrous bodies would definitively confirm whether the model is capturing primary granulation features or secondary nuclear changes, further strengthening its biological interpretability. Finally, real-time clinical integration demands further computational optimization. Future research should prioritize architectural innovations to enhance both efficiency and interpretability across diverse tumor ecosystems.

Conclusion

In the present study, we established a CA-PITNET model, which could effectively distinguish GH-PitNETs from nonfunctioning adenomas. More important, this model is capable of distinguishing two subtypes of GH-PitNETs, with favourable AUC, high sensitivity, and high PPV in the independent internal and external test sets. The superior performance of the CA-PITNET model implicates a potential application in aiding the diagnosis of GH-PitNETs.
