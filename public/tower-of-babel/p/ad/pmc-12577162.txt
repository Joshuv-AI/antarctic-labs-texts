The effects of a YouTube prenatal program on social support, environmental health behavior, and content satisfaction: a quasi-experimental research design
Authors: Geum Hee Jeong, Hyun Kyoung Kim, Hye Young Min
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnant women in the post-COVID-19 era are obtaining information on maternal and fetal health, postpartum care, and newborn health during pregnancy via non-face-to-face prenatal education interventions. Social media is extensively utilized for seeking information, making it an appropriate medium for delivering knowledge. There is a scarcity of YouTube-based interventions specifically designed to educate pregnant women despite the high demand for environmental health education to safeguard maternal health from environmental risk factors. This non-randomized study aimed to investigate the effects of an environmental health program utilizing YouTube videos on social support, environmental behavior, and satisfaction among Korean pregnant women, based on Swanson’s knowledge-empathy-caring theory.MethodsThis study employed a non-equivalent control group pre-post-test design according to the transparent reporting of evaluations with non-randomized designs reporting guideline. The experimental program consisted of prenatal education delivered through Zoom conferences and seven researcher-developed YouTube videos over a 4-week period. The control group received face-to-face prenatal education for 4 weeks in the public health centers. A total of 72 pregnant women participated, with 38 in the experimental group and 34 in the control group. The program was conducted between March and November 2022 in two cities in South Korea.ResultsThe YouTube-based approach was effective in improving content satisfaction (t=-4.27 p <.001) and personal environmental health behavior (t=-2.48, p =.015). However, it was ineffective in enhancing social support (U = 559.00, p =.323) and community environmental behavior (t=-0.51, p =.609).DiscussionPrenatal education via YouTube requires the incorporation of empathy as a strategy to elicit emotional responses from pregnant women. The YouTube video intervention could promote personal environmental health behavior, but it did not effectively support pregnant women socially. YouTube can serve as a valuable resource for prenatal education by enhancing personal environmental health behaviors, as well as improving content satisfaction among expectant mothers. Maternal healthcare providers can utilize social media as a means to deliver environmental knowledge, elicit satisfactory emotions, and encourage healthy behaviors.Trial registrationThe study was registered on February 18, 2022, under the registration number KCT0007006 in the Korean Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS), which can be retrieved from https://cris.nih.go.kr/cris/search/detailSearch.do?seq=23860&status=5&seq_group=21307&search_page=M).Keywords: Environmental health, Pregnant women, Prenatal education, Satisfaction, Social support

Background

Social media encompasses online electronic communication platforms that facilitate the sharing of information, ideas, and personal messages. Examples of social media include YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, Twitter, TikTok, and LinkedIn [1]. Among these, YouTube is the most popular due to its ability to rapidly upload videos in an open platform manner without review [2]. The platform’s advantages include easy accessibility for users seeking information, a vast array of knowledge, advice, social support, and interaction opportunities. However, a downside is that the information on YouTube can often be unreliable, fake news is prevalent, and misinformation spreads rapidly [3].

YouTube is a popular source of information for patients, as 75% of them use the platform to acquire health knowledge, engage with others, and make healthcare decisions, including diagnosis, treatment, and management [4]. As the general population’s health literacy improves, so does their ability to select and access accurate information, making YouTube a highly utilized resource [3]. Health-related YouTube videos can enhance users’ knowledge and alter their health beliefs by offering expert demonstrations and training, which is why they are frequently employed by governments and healthcare organizations [4].

The use of non-face-to-face online media, such as YouTube, for seeking information has increased during COVID-19, particularly among pregnant women. This is due to heightened social isolation and a reluctance to go out, as the risk to maternal and fetal health is greater during the pandemic. As the number of in-person childbirth classes has decreased, pregnant women have turned to the internet for information, making it more likely for them to find information on YouTube [5]. Studies that have analyzed the content and quality of pregnancy-related YouTube videos have focused on topics such as obstetric epidural anesthesia [6], postpartum sexuality [1], and placenta accreta [7]. The information on epidural techniques and placenta accreta was found to be inaccurate. However, the information about postpartum sexuality was deemed highly useful and impactful when provided by health professionals. Although there are limited studies analyzing YouTube videos for pregnant women, videos from health professionals have proven to be an effective intervention for providing information, suggesting that pregnant women may benefit from prenatal education.

Environmental health behaviors are particularly important during pregnancy, as maternal exposures can affect both mothers and fetuses. Chemicals, heavy metals, electromagnetic waves, and pesticides have been linked to adverse outcomes such as preterm birth, low birthweight, spontaneous abortion, impaired fertility, and long-term reproductive problems. Fetal and child consequences further include restricted growth, delayed psychomotor and cognitive development, and reduced reproductive health [8]. These findings underscore the need to strengthen environmental health behaviors during pregnancy. However, despite the growing importance of digital health education, studies applying YouTube interventions specifically to promote environmental health behaviors among pregnant women remain scarce.

Providing informational interventions to pregnant women through social media may offer social support via interaction [3] and potentially increase satisfaction with education [9]. Swanson’s Empathy, Knowledge, and Caring (EKC) Model [10] is a middle-range nursing theory that emphasizes the integration of knowledge-based and empathetic interventions in nursing practice. It has been recognized as particularly relevant for women of reproductive age [11], making it a suitable theoretical framework for this study. Grounded in this model, the present research aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a YouTube-based educational intervention and to provide a foundation for its active incorporation into prenatal education. Specifically, it was hypothesized that applying the EKC-Model through YouTube would strengthen social support, thereby promoting both personal and community environmental health behaviors. Furthermore, the program was expected to improve participants’ satisfaction with antenatal education (Fig. 1).

Conceptual framework of the YouTube-based antenatal education program grounded in Swanson’s Empathy, Knowledge, and Caring Model

Therefore, this study aimed to create and implement YouTube videos as prenatal education for pregnant women, and to assess whether knowledge gained from these environmental YouTube videos can enhance social support, environmental health behaviors, and content satisfaction, in accordance with Swanson’s EKC-Model [10]. The specific research objectives are as follows: (1) to evaluate the impact of an environmental YouTube-based prenatal education intervention on social support for pregnant women; (2) to assess the influence of an environmental YouTube-based prenatal education intervention on environmental health behaviors for pregnant women; and (3) to determine the effect of an environmental YouTube-based prenatal education intervention on content satisfaction for pregnant women.

The hypotheses of this study were as follows:

The experimental group participating in the environmental prenatal education YouTube program will exhibit higher levels of social support than the control group.

Methods

Study design

This study employs a non-equivalent control group, non-synchronized design to evaluate the effectiveness of a prenatal environmental health program for pregnant women, utilizing YouTube as a platform. This study adhered to the TREND (Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Non-randomized Designs) reporting guidelines.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pregnant women over the age of 20 and before 36 gestational weeks, (2) pregnant women who wished to participate in the antenatal education program at the public health center, and (3) pregnant women who attended all 4 weeks of pregnancy education. The exclusion criteria included: (1) pregnant women with pre-existing or current diseases, (2) pregnant women who did not understand Korean, and (3) pregnant women who did not possess a smart device to watch YouTube videos or attend Zoom meetings. Participants who did not complete all seven YouTube videos were considered drop-outs.

Allocation

Participants were selected using a purposive sampling method from individuals attending antenatal education programs at public health centers in C City, Gangwon Province, and G City, Chungcheong Province. Neither randomization nor blinding was performed. The researcher met with the directors of the public health centers and maternal centers to explain the study’s purpose, data collection period, research methods, and to obtain permission. Pregnant women attending the antenatal education program at the health center were asked for written consent to participate in the study. The purpose and methodology of the study were explained to them before the program began. Trained research assistants provided the study description and written consent form, allowing participants ample time to understand the study objectives and procedures. Questionnaires were distributed to those who voluntarily agreed to participate.

Intervention

The creation of the YouTube video adhered to the ADDIE (analysis, design, development, implementation, evaluation) process [12]. This process was documented in a protocol, and the experimental study protocol was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service of the National Institutes of Health, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (KCT0007006).

Analysis phase

The researchers conducted a systematic review to search for YouTube videos related to women’s health, specifically focusing on environmental health. They found no such applications on YouTube. To locate relevant articles, they searched databases such as PubMed, Cochrane, CINAHL Complete, Embase, ERIC, and RISS, using the keywords “women’s health” and “YouTube.” The only pregnancy-related YouTube topics discovered were epidural anesthesia in obstetrics [6], sexuality in childbirth [1], and placenta accreta [7]. The researchers selected YouTube topics based on an internet-based systematic review of maternal environmental health and a study of maternal environmental health needs. They prioritized topics such as environmental hormones, chemicals, heavy metals, nutrition, fine dust, and emission methods, based on meetings among researchers and previous studies [13].

Design phase

The researchers created the content for the YouTube video education by developing a storyboard and producing an initial PowerPoint slide draft, which was divided into seven subtopics. These subtopics included endocrine disruptors and pregnancy, chemical exposure and pregnancy, heavy metal exposure and pregnancy, nutrition and pregnancy, fine dust exposure and pregnancy, environmental toxin release during pregnancy, and environmental toxin release during postpartum. To validate the content, the advisory team consulted with two professors of women’s health nursing and one midwife. They were asked two questions regarding the appropriateness and comprehensibility of the educational content, with responses given on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (very inappropriate and very difficult to understand) to 5 (very appropriate and very easy to understand). All responses were between 4 and 5 points, and the content was subsequently adopted.

Development phase

The researchers converted the PPT into a video lecture. Each video covered one of the seven topics and ranged in length from 6 min 35 s to 12 min 24 s, with a total duration of 1 h 22 min 35 s. The training materials included lectures, images, and animations. The videos were uploaded to a YouTube channel and made accessible exclusively to pregnant women who shared their YouTube addresses. One video was provided in the first week, followed by two videos each in weeks 2–4, resulting in a total of seven videos over the 4-week period (Table 1).

Implementation phase

The researcher recruited the experimental group from a public health center in City C, Gangwon Province, and provided four childbirth education sessions and YouTube videos through small group education via online Zoom meetings. The control group was recruited from a public health center in City G, Chungcheong Province, and was provided with four sessions of general in-person prenatal group education for pregnant women participating in childbirth education. The experimental group received the prenatal education program through videoconference in a Zoom room, totaling 8 h over 4 weeks, with 2 h per week, led by researchers and midwives in March, April, September, and October 2022. The program’s content included understanding pregnancy, pregnancy health management, understanding childbirth, pain control during childbirth, environmental risk factors during pregnancy, prenatal environmental health behavior, postnatal health management, breastfeeding, and baby care. Questions and answers were included after each session, and YouTube videos were made available through KakaoTalk (a social media platform widely used in Korea) following each session. A research assistant checked whether participants had watched the videos and, if not, encouraged them to participate via phone and text. For the control group, a face-to-face antenatal education program was delivered by a researcher for 2 h per week over 4 sessions in April, June, August, and October 2022, totaling 8 h in the health center training room. The control group program’s content consisted of prenatal healthcare on the first day, childbirth healthcare on the second day, postpartum healthcare on the third day, and newborn healthcare on the fourth day. The time was the same as the experimental group, and the control group was provided with YouTube videos after completing the education.

Evaluation phase

Pre-test: Prior to the first session, both the experimental and control groups were surveyed either online or in person. The pre-survey included a questionnaire that inquired about the participants’ general characteristics, obstetric features, social support, and environmental health behaviors, taking approximately 10–15 min to complete.

Post-test: The post-test took place in week 4, immediately after the conclusion of the antenatal education program. The content and methodology of the questionnaire were identical to those of the pre-test, but with the inclusion of questions regarding satisfaction with the educational content.

Assessments

Data collection took place from March 30 to November 2, 2022. The experimental group was assigned to a public health center in C City, Gangwon Province, while the control group was assigned to a public health center in G City, Chungcheong Province. Both the control and experimental groups underwent a pre-test at the beginning of their first prenatal education session and a post-test at the conclusion of their fourth session. For the experimental group, researchers conducted childbirth classes via a Zoom conference and provided YouTube videos for each session. In contrast, the control group’s childbirth classes were conducted in person. Upon completion of the post-test, the control group was provided with the YouTube video links. All participants received a gift at the end of the study.

Outcome measurements

Social support

The Perceived Social Support through Others Scale-8 (PSO-8) was utilized in this study [14]. The PSO-8 comprises three factors: 4 items related to the quality of care provided, 2 items addressing women’s personal attitudes, and 4 items concerning the experience of stress during labor. There are a total of 8 questions, with each question being answered on a 5-point Likert scale that ranges from 1 “not at all” to 5 “very much.” The overall score ranges from 8 to 40, with higher scores signifying greater social support. The internal consistency reliability, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.91 for the original instrument and 0.95 for this study.

Environmental health behaviors

The environmental health behavior measure for women was adopted from a study by Kim & Kim [15]. This tool comprises 17 personal environmental health behaviors and 16 community environmental health behaviors. Personal environmental health behavior is divided into four factors: 7 lifestyle, 4 personal hygiene, 3 diet, and 3 dust-related behaviors. Community environmental health behaviors are also divided into four factors, with 5 reduction, 5 involvement, 3 recycling, and 3 reuse behaviors. The scale employs a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 “not at all” to 5 “very much.” Total personal health behavior scores range from 17 to 85, while total community health behavior scores range from 16 to 80, with higher scores indicating higher levels of environmental health behavior. The internal consistency reliability, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.90 for personal environmental health behavior and 0.91 for community environmental health behavior in the original instruments. In this study, the values were 0.85 and 0.87, respectively.

Content satisfaction

Content satisfaction was assessed using the e-learning satisfaction scale tool, which was designed to measure satisfaction in online education [16]. This tool comprises three factors: content (8 items), interface (5 items), and communication (4 items). For this study, only content satisfaction was considered. The scale utilizes a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 5 (“very much”). Total scores can range from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater content satisfaction. The internal consistency reliability, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.93 for the original instrument and 0.95 for this study.

Sample size

The number of participants was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.4 [17]. A two-tailed independent t-test was specified with a significance level of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.90, and an allocation ratio of 1:1. The target effect size was set to d = 0.80, which corresponds to a medium-to-large magnitude and was adopted from a prior experimental study involving a YouTube application [1]. Based on these assumptions, the required total sample size was estimated at 70 (35 per group). To accommodate an anticipated 20% dropout rate, the recruitment target was set at 84 participants (42 per group). Ultimately, in the control group, 8 participants did not attend the training, resulting in 34 participants included in the analysis. In the experimental group, 4 participants were excluded due to insufficient responses to the questionnaire, leaving 38 participants for the final analysis (Fig. 2).

Ethical consideration

This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the researcher’s university (KNU-IRB-2021-113). After obtaining permission from the directors of the health center and the maternal and child health center, the researcher personally conducted the survey. To safeguard the rights of the participants, the study’s purpose and methodology, the potential benefits and drawbacks of participation, privacy and confidentiality, and the option to refuse or withdraw were explained prior to data collection, and consent was obtained. Participants in the face-to-face training were interviewed in person at the health center training room, following quarantine measures and signing the instructions and consent form. Those participating in the Zoom meetings confirmed their intention to participate via text and phone, and completed the survey using an online Naver form.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analyzed using the SPSS/WIN 26.0 program. The general characteristics, obstetric characteristics, social support, satisfaction, and environmental health behaviors of the subjects were analyzed by frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. To test for homogeneity between the control and experimental groups in terms of general characteristics, obstetric characteristics, social support, and environmental health behaviors, we conducted the t-test, Mann-Whitney test, and chi-square test after assessing normality. The normality of the dependent variable was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the differences in post-hoc scores between the control and experimental groups were examined using the t-test and the Mann-Whitney test.

Results

Homogeneity between the two groups

No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding age, gestational age, number of children, occupation, illness, social support, personal environmental health behaviors, and community environmental health behaviors. Additionally, no statistically significant differences were found between the two groups in the personal health behavior subscale of lifestyle, personal hygiene, diet, and dust management, as well as the community health behavior subscale of waste reduction, community involvement, recycling, and reusing (Table 2).

Effects on social support, satisfaction, and environmental behaviors

The Shapiro-Wilk test for normality was conducted on the dependent variable, social support, which did not meet the criteria for normality. Consequently, the Mann-Whitney test was performed. However, personal environmental health behavior, community environmental health behavior, and content satisfaction met the normality criterion; therefore, the t-test was used for those variables.

Intervention effects

Hypothesis 1, stating that “the experimental group participating in the environmental prenatal education YouTube program will exhibit higher social support than the control group,” was not supported. The mean social support score for the control group was 32.53 ± 5.31, while that for the experimental group was 31.00 ± 6.63. This difference was not statistically significant (U = 559.00, p =.323).

Hypothesis 2, according to which “the experimental group participating in the environmental prenatal education YouTube program will exhibit higher levels of environmental health behavior than the control group,” was supported for personal health behaviors but not for community health behaviors. The total score for personal environmental health behavior was 55.85 ± 6.48 in the control group and 60.37 ± 8.64 in the experimental group, which was statistically significant (t=−2.48, p =.015). In the sub-scale, lifestyle was statistically significantly higher in the control group at 22.68 ± 3.96 and in the experimental group at 24.55 ± 3.90 (t=−2.02, p =.047). Dust was also statistically significantly higher in the control group at 10.59 ± 1.50 and in the experimental group at 11.79 ± 1.83 (t=−3.02, p =.004). However, there was no statistically significant difference in personal goods (t=−1.68, p =.096) and diet (t=−0.60, p =.548). The total score for community environmental health behavior was 54.18 ± 5.58 in the control group and 55.11 ± 9.09 in the experimental group, which was not statistically significant (t=−0.51, p =.609). Additionally, the sub-scales of reduction (t=−0.72, p =.469), involvement (t=−0.44, p =.660), recycling (t=−1.32, p =.189), and reuse (t = 1.06, p =.290) were not statistically significant.

Hypothesis 3, stating that “the experimental group participating in the environmental prenatal education YouTube program will exhibit greater satisfaction than the control group,” was supported. Content satisfaction (t=−4.27 p <.001) was statistically significantly higher in the experimental group, with a mean of 31.84 ± 5.26, compared to the control group’s mean of 25.35 ± 5.62 (Table 3).

Discussion

This study developed a YouTube video education program based on the EKC-Model and applied it to pregnant women to test its effect on environmental health behavior and satisfaction [10]. Pregnant women who viewed the YouTube videos and received internet-based prenatal education exhibited higher levels of personal environmental health behaviors and content satisfaction compared to the control group, which only received face-to-face education. However, there was no difference in social support and community environmental health behaviors between the two groups. Consequently, the prenatal educational intervention using YouTube videos was partially verified for its impact on knowledge and behavior changes. This empirical study provided partial support for the knowledge-empathy-caring theory, as knowledge provision through YouTube altered health behavior and appealed to emotions through empathy-promoting activities.

The results demonstrated that the impact of social support was due to the tailored YouTube videos, as well as the research team’s feedback, advice, and support, which evoked empathy in comparison to pregnant women who received standard prenatal education. The study found no difference in social support between pregnant women who received standard prenatal education and those who received in-person lectures, YouTube videos, or advice from the research team. In this study, efforts to promote social support through planned empathy activities, watching YouTube videos, encouragement, and internet-based lectures emphasizing the importance of environmental health were not effective. Social support can be divided into informational and emotional support, and in this study, informational support was predominant, which may account for the lack of empathy. Informational support includes advice, guidance, and information, while emotional support encompasses love, concern, and sympathy [18]. In this study, minimal emotional support was provided, which hindered the reciprocal human interaction between participants and providers. Social media offers the advantage of providing social support, advice, and interaction [3], so encouragement and counseling from the research team will be necessary when implementing future YouTube interventions. Considering the increased use of YouTube as a means to cope with loneliness and depression during COVID-19 [19], YouTube has the potential to be a valuable intervention for social support effects. In addition to providing women’s health information, YouTube videos should be emotionally relevant, addressing grief and depression [20]. As YouTube can be customized, provides social support, is highly accessible, and has extensive penetration and diffusion [1], it could be utilized in prenatal education to offer both information and social support to improve emotional health, such as loneliness and depression.

Personal environmental health behavior refers to actions that prevent exposure to environmental toxins and protect both the mother and fetus from environmental health problems [21]. Among these personal health behaviors, effects were observed in the subdomains of lifestyle and dust, but not in personal care and diet. Using the same tools, environmental health interventions employing cartoon comics [22] and an internet-based prenatal classroom intervention [23] showed no impact on personal health behaviors. However, an in-person prenatal classroom lecture [24] did have an effect on personal health behaviors. Consequently, interventions that combined YouTube and lectures proved more effective than those that solely provided cartoon comics [22] or face-to-face lectures [24]. In this study, the effectiveness of the customized YouTube and lecture intervention was higher because the fine dust education was repeated in both the first and third sessions, and the risk factors for pregnancy were taught in the third session. Therefore, it would be more effective to apply a combination of expert-led lectures and YouTube content rather than providing lectures alone.

It is preferable for healthcare professionals to be the providers of information in videos, and for these videos to be relatively lengthy. Although longer videos may decrease attention span, they have been shown to increase credibility, usefulness, and content quality [25]. The concept of knowledge and empathy-based nursing supports the idea that personal behaviors can be influenced through social media, as it posits that direct knowledge provision can be synchronized with behavior change by affecting individual beliefs [1].

Community environmental health behaviors can be characterized as altruistic actions that prevent harmful environmental impacts and alternative behaviors that result in lifestyle changes that are less burdensome on the environment [21]. Using the same tool, an internet-based prenatal classroom intervention with environmental health content did not change community behavior among pregnant women [23], while a cartoon comic book intervention [22] and an in-person prenatal classroom [24] led to increased community behavior. In this study, it is possible that the content on community health behaviors was not effective because it was only included in a small portion of the face-to-face lectures during the third session and was not emphasized. YouTube viewing involves a complex individual cognitive process of exploring, filtering, and integrating health knowledge [4] and is not a collective activity, so it does not appear to have resulted in changes in altruistic beliefs that synchronize community action. As a function of social media, YouTube alone is not sufficient to elicit empathy through interaction, requiring interactive efforts from health professionals and strategies to engage audiences [26]. Personal health behavior involves protecting mothers and fetuses from environmental toxins, while community environmental health behavior represents the altruistic act of creating a sustainable environment for the community [21]. A study on Facebook found that when altruistic messages are posted on social media, users focus on them more than selfish messages [27]. A study on Instagram also found that users were able to escape negative emotions, develop gratitude, altruistic attitudes, and empathy, which synchronized with donation behavior [28]. Therefore, there is potential for social media to be used to inspire altruistic community behavior.

In this study, the YouTube intervention proved effective in enhancing content satisfaction. Since YouTube is frequently criticized for its prevalence of fake news, misinformation, low usefulness, unreliability, and poor content quality [29], it is essential to fact-check and involve experts to address these issues. The rapid spread of rumors, stigmas, and conspiracy theories on the platform necessitates such measures. During the COVID-19 pandemic, YouTube searches related to pregnancy significantly increased, covering topics such as infertility, preterm labor, low birth weight, stillbirth, breastfeeding, vaccine side effects, treatments, and malformations [30]. This trend suggests that pregnant women are seeking accurate information and are more susceptible to health problems due to misinformation [3]. Consequently, it is crucial for health-related YouTube videos to be uploaded by experts to ensure the accuracy of the information provided. Fortunately, health-related YouTube videos have become more accurate, allowing health experts to disseminate information quickly and effectively.

This study had several limitations. One limitation of the study is the dropout rate, with four participants leaving the experimental group and eight leaving the control group. This indicates a higher dropout rate in the in-person training group, suggesting lower participation in this intervention. However, the study did not analyze the reasons for these dropouts, which limits understanding of factors influencing participant retention. Conducted as a quasi-experimental study at two public health centers in South Korea, and the findings are not generalizable. Additionally, the study did not employ double-blind methods or random sampling. Future research should aim to confirm the effectiveness of interventions while incorporating culturally diverse populations and diversifying educational approaches, such as online media, mobile applications, counseling, and mentoring. Expanding the study to include a broader, regionally diverse sample of pregnant women would enhance its applicability across different cultural contexts.

High-quality content is essential for satisfactory health information, which in turn improves the digital literacy of the general public in navigating and selecting information [31]. However, YouTube videos posted by experts often focus on complex disease mechanisms that may be difficult for the general public to understand and may not align with their interests. Therefore, it is necessary to present more comprehensible, realistic, and practical content [25]. Moreover, YouTube videos produced by academic institutions such as government agencies, universities, and hospitals tend to have higher usability and content quality compared to those created by individual experts [26]. Thus, it is recommended that professional organizations take the lead in producing these videos.

Consequently, the incorporation of digital video platforms into antenatal education programs should be actively promoted. Such an approach can enhance nursing care for environmental health in a society where many pregnant women obtain information through social media. From a practical perspective, professional organizations and public health authorities should take the lead in producing high-quality, evidence-based video content that is both accessible and engaging. Integrating videos into antenatal education programs can broaden the reach of nursing care, particularly in digital environments where pregnant women frequently seek health information. Policymakers and healthcare providers are therefore encouraged to support the use of video-based social media platforms in prenatal education strategies, thereby strengthening environmental health promotion and addressing the evolving needs of expectant mothers in contemporary society.
