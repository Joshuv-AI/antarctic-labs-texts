Effect of the atherogenic index of plasma on the expression of platelet CD36 and CD62P in patients with acute ischemic stroke: a retrospective study from Tianjin, China
Authors: Yusen Cai, Miaomiao Wei, Yumeng Gu, Lin Wang, Xin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) is closely linked to the incidence of acute ischemic stroke (AIS). This study aimed to investigate the effect of AIP on the expression levels of platelet surface markers CD36 and CD62P in AIS patients, and further analyze the correlations among these three indicators.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 441 AIS patients who underwent platelet flow cytometry and were admitted between January 2024 and June 2025. Participants were stratified by AIP tertiles. Platelet CD36 and CD62P expression levels were detected via flow cytometry. Multivariate adjusted regression models and linear regression analyses were performed to explore the relationships between AIP and platelet CD36/CD62P expression. Stratified analyses were conducted by potential risk factors to examine subgroup differences. A generalized additive model (GAM) with smooth curve fitting was used to investigate the association pattern between CD36 and CD62P. Simple mediation analysis via the bootstrap method was applied to explore the potential mediating effect of CD36 in the relationship between AIP and CD62P.ResultsAIP, whether considered as a continuous or a categorical variable, was significantly correlated with platelet CD62P levels. For each 1-unit increase in AIP, CD62P levels rose by 1.62 (β = 1.62, 95% CI: 0.41–2.82, P = 0.009). When AIP was divided into tertiles, the highest tertile (Q3) showed a significant increase in CD62P levels compared to the lowest (Q1), with a significant dose-response trend (P < 0.05). AIP also showed a significant positive correlation with CD36 (β = 2.30, 95% CI: 0.43–4.16, P = 0.016), with significant differences in CD36 levels in both middle (Q2) and highest tertiles (Q3) compared to Q1. The GAM with smooth curve fitting showed that higher CD36 expression led to increased platelet CD62P levels (95% CI:11.19–12.87), confirming a significant nonlinear relationship (P = 0.0017). A mediation model indicated that CD36 partially mediates the effect of AIP on CD62P.ConclusionIn AIS patients, high AIP levels are linked to increased platelet markers CD36 and CD62P, as well as dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis. AIP has the potential to serve as a significant biomarker for assessing platelet activity.Keywords: Acute ischemic stroke, Atherogenic index of plasma, Platelet activation, CD36, CD62P

Introduction

Globally, approximately 7 million individuals succumb to stroke annually, rendering it the second leading cause of mortality worldwide and also the foremost cause of disability. Among these incidents, ischemic stroke constitutes 70% of stroke cases. Its principal pathological mechanism involves platelet activation and thrombus formation instigated by the rupture of atherosclerotic plaques, subsequently resulting in the acute occlusion of cerebral blood vessels [1–3].

Dyslipidemia is a significant risk factor for acute ischemic stroke(AIS) [4]. The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), as a novel indicator for assessing lipid metabolism, offers a more sensitive reflection of lipid metabolism disorders and the potential risk of atherosclerosis [5]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that AIP is not only closely associated with the development of carotid plaques but also serves as a predictor of the occurrence of AIS [6, 7]. Furthermore, persistent elevation of AIP contributes to an increased risk of AIS, and dynamic monitoring of AIP levels facilitates the formulation of more effective lipid-lowering strategies [8].

Platelet activation plays a crucial role in thrombus formation during AIS, with Cluster of Differentiation 36 (CD36) and Cluster of Differentiation 62P (CD62P) being key molecules in this process. CD36, part of the class B scavenger receptor family, interacts with Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) and High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL) [9]. CD62P, a membrane protein stored in platelet granules, moves to the surface of platelets upon activation, facilitating platelet adhesion to endothelial cells and leukocytes, and is a vital marker for thrombus formation and inflammation [10].

Lipid-lowering and antiplatelet therapies are key treatments for AIS. Previous studies have focused on the impact of a single AIP measurement on stroke outcomes, but the connection between plasma atherogenicity and platelet reactivity remains uncertain. This study investigated the relationship between AIP and platelet markers CD36 and CD62P, examining their interrelations.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study included a cohort of 441 patients diagnosed with AIS and who underwent flow cytometry analysis upon admission to the Stroke Center at the Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University between January 2024 and June 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Diagnosis of new-onset cerebral infarction confirmed by head computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). (2) Patients were admitted to the hospital within 72 h of stroke onset. (3) Age between 18 and 85 years. (4) Completion of fasting blood lipid testing after admission. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Presence of severe hepatic and renal insufficiency, malignant tumors, or hematological disorders. (2) History of trauma, surgical procedures, or hemorrhagic conditions within the preceding two weeks. (3) Prior administration of antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy before hospital admission. (4) Incomplete or missing essential information. The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University (Ethics Approval No. KY2023K138).

Grouping method

Fasting blood lipid levels were collected after an 8-hour fast and submitted for testing upon patient admission and AIP was calculated via the formula AIP = lg (TG/HDL). Patients were stratified into three tertiles according to AIP distribution: Q1 (low tertile): AIP < −0.05, n = 146; Q2 (middle tertile): −0.05 ≤ AIP < 0.198, n = 147;Q3 (high tertile): AIP ≥ 0.198, n = 148.

Collection of baseline data and biochemical indicators

Baseline data (age, sex), medical history (hypertension, diabetes mellitus(DM), coronary heart disease(CHD), Ischemic stroke(IS), smoking history, alcohol consumption history), and biochemical indicators (blood lipid profile, blood count, coagulation tests) were collected, Medication history (anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, hypolipidemic drugs, antihypertensive drugs) were collected.

Detection of platelet CD36 and CD62P expression

Within 2 h of admission, 2 mL of venous blood was collected from each patient into a tube with 3.8% sodium citrate. The sample was mixed and processed immediately via flow cytometry for the detection of CD62P and CD36 expression. The process involved the following steps: (1) Adding fluorescently labeled antibodies to 50 µL of blood and incubating for 15 min in the dark; (2) Fixing with 1% paraformaldehyde, centrifuging, and discarding the supernatant; (3) Resuspending the cells in PBS; (4) Analyzing CD62P and CD36 expression with a BD FACS Verse flow cytometer and FlowJo 10.8.1 software (Fig. 1).

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.3.1. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed by one-way ANOVA, while skewed data were presented as median (Q1-Q3) and analyzed by Kruskal-Wallis H test. Categorical data were expressed as frequency and percentage and compared using the chi-square test. For indicators showing a significant intergroup difference (P < 0.05), post-hoc tests were conducted to pinpoint specific differences: for normally distributed continuous variables, the SNK-q test was used to control Type I error, with the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test as an optional exploratory tool. For non-normally distributed variables, the Dunn test with Bonferroni correction was applied to minimize false positives. For categorical variables, the chi-square test with Bonferroni correction was used, setting the significance level at α’≈0.017.Multivariate regression explored the link between AIP tertiles and CD36/CD62P expression through three hierarchical adjusted models, with variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis for multicollinearity (VIF < 10 indicating no severe multicollinearity). A trend test examined CD62P changes with rising AIP, and linear regression identified factors influencing the correlation between AIP and platelet CD36/CD62P expression. Stratified analyses by gender, age, smoking and other risk factors assessed CD36/CD62P expression in subgroups. A generalized additive model (GAM) with smooth curve fitting examined the link between CD36 and CD62P. A mediation analysis employing the bootstrap method with 1,000 resamplings was conducted to investigate whether CD36 mediates the effect of AIP on CD62P, while controlling for age and gender. The average causal mediation effect (ACME) and its proportion of the total effect were reported. All tests were two-tailed, with significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Initially, 659 individuals were enrolled in the study. After excluding 150 on antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, 34 with missing data, 25 with severe hepatic/renal issues or other severe conditions, and 9 with recent trauma or surgery, 441 patients diagnosed with AIS were included in the final analysis. These patients were categorized into three distinct groups based on tertiles of the AIP (Fig. 2).

Flow diagram of patient inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study cohort had a mean age of 70.7 ± 11.4 years, 61.9% of the participants were male. As the AIP increased, so did age, male percentage, SBP, platelet markers CD36 and CD62P, and Intracranial atherosclerotic Stenosis (ICAS) prevalence. No significant differences were observed in the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, or coronary heart disease. Post-hoc analysis showed that Q1 was younger than Q2 and Q3, with lower SBP. Q3 had higher male proportion and ICAS prevalence than Q1 and Q2. CD36 expression was significantly higher in Q3 compared to Q1, while CD62P expression was also higher in Q3 than in Q1 (Table 1).

A linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between AIP and CD36 expression, revealing a significant positive correlation (β = 2.30, 95% CI: 0.43–4.16, P = 0.016), particularly among males and individuals over the age of 65. No significant interactions were identified between the stratified variables and AIP (Table 3). In relation to AIP and CD62P, a 1-unit increase in AIP was associated with a significant increase in CD62P levels by 1.62 (95% CI: 0.41–2.82, P = 0.009), although the interaction effect was not statistically significant. Notable effects were observed in males, individuals over 65, and patients with diabetes, a history of alcohol consumption, or coronary heart disease(Table 4). Forest plots were utilized to illustrate the effect sizes and heterogeneity within subgroups.

Discussion

Increased platelet reactivity is crucial in the development of occlusive arterial thrombosis. Increased platelet activity and thrombosis risk are related to conditions such as dyslipidemia-related atherosclerosis, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [11]. AIS involves atherosclerosis, thrombosis, and poor cerebral blood flow, with platelet activation being key to thrombosis and the progression of vascular atherosclerosis [4]. The preliminary results of this study indicate that elevated levels of AIP are significantly correlated with increased expression of CD36 and elevated levels of CD62P on the platelet surface, even after controlling for potential confounding variables. This suggests that AIP may serve as a potential predictor of these platelet activation markers. Considering that high AIP levels are associated with dyslipidemia and may further increase the risk of platelet activation and atherosclerosis, it is advisable to implement intensive lipid-lowering and antiplatelet therapies for AIS patients exhibiting high AIP levels.

Individuals at high risk for dyslipidemia frequently exhibit elevated plasma lipid peroxides, predominantly transported by LDL. These oxidized lipoproteins have the potential to activate platelets [11, 12]. Notably, Small Dense Low-Density Lipoprotein (sdLDL) is particularly susceptible to oxidation [13]. In patients with CHD, there is a positive correlation between sdLDL levels and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) levels, indicating a possible interaction between plasma sdLDL and PCSK9 levels in the context of atherosclerosis [14]. Oxidative stress induced by vascular endothelial injury has the potential to influence the expression and secretion of PCSK9. The interaction between PCSK9 and sdLDL may further exacerbate the pathological progression of atherosclerosis by intensifying lipid oxidation modifications [15].AIP serves as a robust indicator of lipid metabolism disorders and is associated with cholesterol esterification, remnant lipoproteinemia, insulin resistance, and sdLDL levels [16–18]. However, research exploring the relationship between AIP levels and platelet activation remains sparse. A registry study involving patients who underwent coronary stent implantation found no direct correlation between AIP and high platelet reactivity [17]. Our study, however, demonstrated that elevated AIP levels are associated not only with increased levels of lipids (such as TC, TG, LDL, and VLDL) and decreased HDL levels but also with heightened platelet CD62P levels. The dyslipidemia resulting from elevated AIP may lead to vascular endothelial injury and directly facilitate platelet activation.

Atherosclerotic diseases are now a leading cause of death worldwide [19]. Despite optimal pharmacological intervention, ICAS remains a significant risk factor for stroke recurrence [20]. Higher AIP levels are linked to severe coronary stenosis and an increased risk of total occlusion and poor collateral circulation [21]. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that AIP is closely associated with the rapid progression of coronary atherosclerosis, an association independent of traditional risk factors including LDL and hypertension [22]. Elevated AIP levels are also linked to increased carotid intima-media thickness and a higher prevalence of carotid plaques [23]. In the present study, the proportion of ICAS cases increased with rising AIP levels, accompanied by upregulated expression of the platelet surface markers CD36 and CD62P. Furthermore, platelet activation acts as a mechanistic bridge linking dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis [24].

CD36 on platelets recognizes lipoprotein-associated patterns, linking plasma lipid stress to platelet activation and thrombosis. Our GAM analysis corroborated that CD62P levels demonstrate a significant nonlinear escalation in response to increased CD36 levels. Subsequent examination employing a basic mediation model indicated that CD36 serves as a partial mediator in the pathway linking AIP to CD62P. These results contribute to a deeper understanding of the mechanism underlying the relationship between elevated AIP levels and platelet activation.

CD36, in conjunction with Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and TLR6, initiates signaling pathways essential for platelet activation. The activation of CD36 induces Src family kinases, which subsequently activate critical molecules such as Syk, Vav-1, PLCγ2, ERK5, and JNK [11, 25–27]. The presence of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) enhances CD36 expression on platelets [28–30]. Thereby contributing to endothelial dysfunction and the progression of atherosclerotic plaque. Platelets exposed to ox-LDL have the capacity to adhere to endothelial cells in regions susceptible to atherosclerosis [31]. The presence of CD62P on platelets facilitates the recruitment of leukocytes through its interaction with PSGL-1. Alterations in platelet receptors and signaling pathways expedite atherothrombotic events [32]. CD36-mediated fatty acid uptake induces stiffness in aortic endothelial cells, which markedly promotes vascular dysfunction and atherosclerosis induced by a high-fat diet.

Research has demonstrated that PCSK9 contributes to elevated plasma LDL levels by degrading LDL receptors and can also interact with platelet CD36 to activate downstream pathways, thereby facilitating platelet activation and thrombosis [33]. In recent years, multiple studies have indicated that PCSK9 inhibitors can further reduce LDL levels and are effective for the secondary prevention of cardio-cerebrovascular events. In the FOURIER trial’s stroke subgroup analysis, combining statins with evolocumab lowered the relative risk of major events by 15% (HR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.72–1.00) [34]. Furthermore, a multicenter prospective cohort study conducted in China confirmed that, compared with statins alone and/or ezetimibe, combination therapy with PCSK9 inhibitors significantly decreased the 1-month stroke recurrence rate in patients with symptomatic Intracranial Atherosclerotic Stenosis (sICAS) (5.6% vs. 2.2%; HR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.11–0.99) [35]. Additionally, compared with statin monotherapy, 6 months of combination therapy with evolocumab was associated with the regression of intracranial atherosclerotic plaques [36].

The identification of high-risk populations enables the implementation of targeted interventions [37, 38]. Our stratified analysis showed elevated CD36 and CD62P levels in males over the age of 65, particularly those with diabetes, though interaction effects were not statistically significant. A related study found higher platelet activation markers in type 2 diabetes patients compared with the general population [28]. Additionally, hyperlipidemia promotes CD36 translocation to the cell membrane [39, 40]. Decreased androgen levels in males over the age of 65 may contribute to increased platelet activation [41].

This study is subject to several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design may introduce selection bias. Secondly, the absence of measurements for ox-LDL and PCSK9 constrains the investigation of the relationship between AIP and platelet activation. Although no significant findings were observed, the potential influence of lipid-lowering medications cannot be entirely discounted. Additionally, the study did not include ADP-activated platelet flow cytometry for assessing platelet hyperreactivity.

Conclusions

The preliminary findings of this study suggest that in patients with AIS, elevated AIP levels are associated with increased expression of platelet CD36 and CD62P. Furthermore, CD36 appears to partially mediate the pathway through which AIP induces elevated CD62P expression, indicating that higher AIP levels are frequently linked to platelet activation.
