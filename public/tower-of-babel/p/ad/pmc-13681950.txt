Functional near-infrared spectroscopy as a tool for monitoring treatment-related neurofunctional changes in posttraumatic stress disorder and trauma-related symptomatology: a narrative review
Authors: Michela Balconi, Elena Gobbi, Ilaria Pagnoni, Elena Campana, Marco Sandrini, Rosa Manenti, Maria Cotelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPosttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is characterized by substantial heterogeneity in symptom presentation, illness trajectory, and treatment response, highlighting the need for objective approaches capable of monitoring treatment-related neurofunctional changes beyond conventional symptom-based outcomes. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is an increasingly used non-invasive neuroimaging technique that enables repeated assessment of cortical hemodynamics in clinically feasible settings. Although fNIRS has been widely applied to characterize neurofunctional alterations associated with PTSD, its potential role in monitoring longitudinal treatment-related brain changes has not been critically synthesized.MethodsA narrative review was conducted using a structured search strategy across PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, PsycINFO, and the Cochrane Library to identify studies published up to 30 January 2026. Studies were eligible if they included adults with PTSD or clinically significant trauma-related symptomatology and employed repeated NIRS/fNIRS assessments to evaluate neurofunctional changes associated with pharmacological or non-pharmacological therapeutic interventions.ResultsFour studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. Across the included studies, the non-pharmacological interventions consisted of trauma-focused psychotherapy, attention-based cognitive training, and equine-assisted services. No eligible longitudinal studies evaluating pharmacological treatments using repeated NIRS/fNIRS assessments were identified. Across studies, treatment was consistently associated with modulation of prefrontal cortical hemodynamic activity, although the direction and magnitude of neurofunctional changes varied according to intervention type, experimental paradigm, and methodological approach. Overall, the available evidence supports the feasibility of repeated fNIRS assessment while highlighting substantial methodological heterogeneity and the limited maturity of the current evidence base.ConclusionsCurrent evidence suggests that fNIRS represents a promising and clinically feasible approach for monitoring treatment-related neurofunctional changes in PTSD and trauma-related symptomatology, particularly during non-pharmacological interventions targeting prefrontal regulatory processes. Rather than replacing established neuroimaging techniques such as functional magnetic resonance imaging, fNIRS should be considered a complementary tool that is particularly suitable for repeated assessments in real-world clinical settings. Future research should prioritize adequately powered longitudinal studies, standardized acquisition and analytical protocols, multimodal neuroimaging approaches, and the inclusion of pharmacological treatment trials to determine the potential of fNIRS as a candidate longitudinal neurofunctional biomarker of treatment response.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08430-1.

Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a complex psychiatric condition characterized by re-experiencing of the traumatic event, avoidance of trauma-related cues, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and hyperarousal following exposure to traumatic events [1]. However, PTSD is associated with substantial interindividual variability in symptom presentation, illness trajectory, and response to treatment [2–4]. Although exposure to potentially traumatic events is common, only a subset of exposed individuals develops persistent PTSD. This marked interindividual variability indicates that vulnerability and resilience to trauma are influenced by complex interactions among psychological, biological, and contextual factors [2, 4–8]. This considerable heterogeneity presents significant challenges for both clinical management and research, underscoring the need to move beyond purely symptom-based frameworks toward a more mechanistic understanding of treatment response and to identify objective neurobiological markers capable of complementing conventional clinical assessment and supporting individualized therapeutic strategies [9, 10].

Converging evidence indicates that the emotional experience of trauma can exert long-term effects on cognitive function. Accordingly, PTSD is associated with disturbances in cognitive and emotional regulation processes, including impairments in attention, executive control, and emotion-cognition interactions [11, 12]. At the neural level, these disturbances are thought to reflect dysregulation within fronto-limbic circuitry, accompanied by widespread alterations in large-scale functional connectivity and neuroplasticity-related mechanisms [3, 13, 14]. Recent neuroimaging studies have further demonstrated that successful treatment is accompanied by functional reorganization of cortical and fronto-limbic networks, suggesting that treatment response is associated with dynamic neurobiological processes rather than exclusively with symptom reduction [15, 16]. Collectively, these findings suggest that PTSD is characterized by measurable neurofunctional alterations, which may evolve dynamically in response to therapeutic interventions, underscoring the potential value of neurofunctional markers for elucidating treatment-related mechanisms and objectively monitoring neurobiological recovery beyond changes detected by symptom-based clinical measures.

Evidence suggests that both pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions are effective in the treatment of PTSD, underscoring the need for an individualized treatment approach in which psychological interventions, pharmacological therapies, or their combination may be considered according to patients’ clinical characteristics, symptom severity, preferences, and response to treatment [17–22]. Among pharmacological approaches, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are approved by different international agencies to treat PTSD, and these drugs are generally combined with psychotherapy to improve treatment outcomes [23–25]. Trauma-focused psychotherapies, including cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy (CPT), and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) [26–28], have received the strongest empirical support for the treatment of PTSD [17, 29–31]. Nevertheless, treatment response remains highly variable, with a substantial proportion of patients experiencing incomplete remission or delayed access to effective care [5, 9, 17, 29]. This variability underscores the need for tools capable of capturing treatment-related neurobiological changes beyond symptomatic improvement alone [5]. In both clinical practice and research settings, treatment response is predominantly assessed using self-report measures or clinician-administered scales, which, although indispensable, may fail to capture subtle, early, or mechanistically relevant neurofunctional changes associated with therapeutic processes. Within this context, there has been growing interest in neuroimaging methodologies capable of providing enhanced characterization of neural processes and more sensitive indices of treatment-related brain changes, particularly within longitudinal and clinically feasible frameworks [15, 32, 33]. Consequently, increasing attention has been devoted to the identification of neuroimaging biomarkers that not only characterize disease-related abnormalities but also provide objective measures of treatment-induced neurofunctional changes. Such biomarkers may contribute to precision psychiatry by facilitating treatment monitoring, improving prognostic stratification, and supporting individualized therapeutic decision-making [34–37].

Among the currently available neuroimaging techniques, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has substantially advanced the understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying PTSD by consistently demonstrating alterations within fronto-limbic circuits involved in emotional regulation, fear extinction, and executive control [15, 16]. However, despite its excellent spatial resolution and ability to investigate both cortical and subcortical structures, fMRI presents practical limitations for repeated clinical assessments. These include high costs, limited accessibility, long acquisition times, high susceptibility to motion artifacts, contraindications and patient burden (such as implanted metallic devices, claustrophobia, etc.), difficulty translating group-level findings to individual patients, reducing its utility as a standalone diagnostic or prognostic biomarker, limited test-retest reliability for several task-based paradigms and some resting-state connectivity measures [38–41], and relatively low ecological feasibility. These considerations have stimulated growing interest in complementary neuroimaging approaches capable of monitoring treatment-related neurofunctional changes under more naturalistic conditions (Fig. 1). In this context, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) has emerged as a promising technique because of its portability, relatively low cost, good tolerability, and suitability for repeated evaluations, making it particularly attractive for psychiatric research and clinical practice [38, 39, 42–55].

In PTSD research, NIRS/fNIRS has been primarily applied to characterize disorder-related cortical alterations, with a predominant focus on prefrontal regions implicated in emotion regulation, cognitive control, and top-down modulation of limbic responses, thereby providing neurofunctional evidence that complements current neurobiological models of PTSD [56–58]. Across task-based paradigms, individuals with PTSD or subthreshold posttraumatic stress symptoms have shown atypical prefrontal hemodynamic responses during emotionally salient, trauma-related, or cognitively demanding tasks [14, 56–63]. These findings are broadly consistent with evidence from functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), which has consistently demonstrated dysfunction within prefrontal networks involved in emotional regulation and executive control, supporting the convergent validity of fNIRS as a cortical neuroimaging technique while highlighting its complementary role in longitudinal clinical investigations [15, 39, 42].

Although previous studies have demonstrated the ability of NIRS/fNIRS to detect PTSD-related cortical dysfunction and, in some cases, to identify neurofunctional features associated with symptom severity and clinical status [56, 62, 64], considerably less attention has been devoted to understanding whether these cortical alterations change in response to therapeutic interventions. Consequently, the potential role of fNIRS as a longitudinal biomarker of treatment-related neurofunctional modulation remains insufficiently characterized. Addressing this knowledge gap is particularly relevant because objective measures of treatment-induced brain changes could complement conventional clinical outcomes, improve the understanding of mechanisms underlying recovery, and ultimately support more personalized approaches to treatment monitoring.

Given the widespread use of pharmacological and psychological treatments for PTSD, there is a clear theoretical rationale for the adoption of longitudinal neuroimaging approaches to investigate treatment-related neurofunctional modulation. In this context, NIRS/fNIRS may represent a feasible method for monitoring cortical changes associated with therapeutic interventions, particularly within prefrontal systems implicated in emotion regulation, cognitive processing, and stress responsivity [56, 58]. Compared with conventional neuroimaging modalities, its portability, tolerability, and suitability for repeated assessments make fNIRS particularly attractive for longitudinal studies aimed at tracking dynamic neurofunctional changes during treatment, especially in real-world clinical settings [38, 65].

To date, however, the available evidence remains fragmented, with substantial methodological heterogeneity regarding treatment modalities, experimental paradigms, fNIRS acquisition protocols, and neurofunctional outcome measures. A critical synthesis of this emerging literature is therefore warranted to clarify the current state of evidence, identify methodological challenges, and define priorities for future research.

Accordingly, the present narrative review aims to critically examine the current evidence on the application of NIRS and fNIRS for monitoring the effects of pharmacological or nonpharmacological interventions in adult individuals with PTSD or clinically significant trauma-related symptomatology. Rather than focusing on the diagnostic utility of fNIRS, this review specifically addresses its potential role as a longitudinal tool for monitoring treatment-related neurofunctional changes. Furthermore, we discuss methodological strengths, limitations, and sources of heterogeneity across studies, compare the available evidence with findings from the broader neuroimaging literature, and outline future directions for the integration of NIRS/fNIRS into longitudinal and translational research aimed at developing objective neurofunctional biomarkers of treatment response within precision psychiatry.

Methods

Search strategy

A literature search was conducted to critically synthesize the available evidence on the application of near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) for monitoring treatment-related neurofunctional changes in posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and trauma-related symptomatology. Given the limited number of available longitudinal studies and the substantial methodological heterogeneity across clinical populations, intervention types, neuroimaging protocols, and outcome measures, a narrative review was considered the most appropriate methodological approach to provide an integrative and critical appraisal of the current evidence rather than a quantitative synthesis [66, 67]. Nevertheless, to enhance methodological transparency and reproducibility, the literature search and study selection followed a structured approach based on predefined eligibility criteria and are reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement where applicable.

Studies were identified through systematic searches in five electronic databases: PubMed (MEDLINE), Web of Science, Scopus, PsycINFO, and the Cochrane Library. The following search strategy was used: ((near-infrared spectroscopy OR NIRS) AND (Post traumatic stress disorder OR Posttraumatic stress disorder OR PTSD OR trauma-related symptomatology) AND (treatment)) (see Supplementary Material S1).

The last literature search was conducted on 30 January 2026 (Fig. 2).

Study selection

Study selection was conducted using Rayyan, a web-based platform designed to facilitate literature screening and study selection [69, 70].

After duplicate removal, titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers (EG and EC) according to the predefined eligibility criteria.

Potentially eligible articles subsequently underwent full-text review, and the reference lists of all included studies were manually screened to identify additional relevant publications. Disagreements at any stage of the selection process were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (MC) until consensus was achieved.

Although PRISMA guidelines were adopted to improve transparency in the reporting of the study selection process, the present work should be considered a narrative review because its primary objective was to provide a critical qualitative synthesis of an emerging and methodologically heterogeneous body of literature rather than to perform a systematic quantitative comparison.

The complete study selection procedure is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 2) [68].

Results

A total of 76 records were initially identified through database searches and subsequently managed using Rayyan. Following the removal of 20 duplicate records, 56 articles underwent title and abstract screening. No additional records were identified through manual screening of the reference lists of the included studies. Of these, 48 records were excluded because they did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. The remaining eight articles were assessed through full-text review.

Following refinement of the eligibility criteria during the revision process to improve the methodological consistency of the review, four studies fulfilled all inclusion criteria and were included in the final qualitative synthesis. The remaining four studies were excluded because the investigated populations did not correspond to the predefined eligibility criteria, specifically as they did not include adult participants with PTSD or clinically significant trauma-related symptomatology as the primary clinical condition under investigation or because NIRS/fNIRS was not used to longitudinally assess treatment-related neurofunctional changes in PTSD or trauma-related symptomatology. The study selection process is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 2).

Characteristics and main findings of the included studies

Four studies were included in the final qualitative synthesis [71–74]. Two studies adopted a single-arm uncontrolled design [72, 73], whereas two were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [71, 74]. Two investigations evaluated trauma-focused psychotherapeutic interventions based on eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) [73, 74], while the remaining two studies investigated non-pharmacological interventions including attention-based cognitive training [71] and equine-assisted services (EAS) [72].

Despite differences in intervention modality and study design, all investigations employed repeated NIRS/fNIRS assessments to evaluate treatment-related cortical hemodynamic changes, primarily within prefrontal regions involved in emotion regulation, executive functioning, attentional control, and cognitive processing. Neurofunctional outcomes mainly relied on changes in oxygenated hemoglobin (oxy-Hb), whereas deoxygenated hemoglobin (deoxy-Hb) was evaluated in only a subset of studies. Importantly, no eligible longitudinal studies investigating pharmacological treatment effects using repeated NIRS/fNIRS assessments were identified. The principal characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Among the included studies, two investigated treatment-related neurofunctional changes associated with EMDR-based interventions [73, 74]. Ohtani et al. [73] conducted a single-arm uncontrolled study in patients with PTSD undergoing weekly EMDR sessions. Bilateral prefrontal cortical activity was repeatedly assessed using multichannel NIRS during trauma-related recall tasks performed with and without concurrent eye movements. Trauma-related recall elicited increased bilateral prefrontal oxy-Hb concentrations, whereas the addition of eye movements significantly attenuated this response. Across treatment sessions, a progressive reduction in prefrontal oxy-Hb activation during trauma recall was observed, and the magnitude of this reduction significantly correlated with improvements in PTSD symptom severity assessed using the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS). No significant longitudinal changes were observed for deoxy-Hb [73]. Similarly, Rimini et al. [74] investigated the neurofunctional effects of eye movements during a single EMDR session in individuals presenting with traumatic memories. Bilateral prefrontal hemodynamic activity was recorded during the desensitization phase of EMDR. Participants receiving standard EMDR with eye movements demonstrated significant modulation of oxy-Hb and deoxy-Hb responses together with alterations in low-frequency hemodynamic oscillations, whereas participants treated without eye movements exhibited a different pattern of prefrontal activation. Two included studies evaluated non-pharmacological behavioral interventions targeting trauma-related symptomatology through distinct therapeutic approaches [71, 72]. Eli et al. [71] conducted a parallel RCT evaluating attention-based cognitive training in bereaved (Shidu) parents presenting with trauma-related symptomatology. Participants were randomly assigned to Attention Bias Modification (ABM), Attention Control Training (ACT), or Attention Control Geometric training (ACG). fNIRS recordings obtained during a dot-probe task demonstrated that participants receiving ACT showed significant modulation of bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) activity relative to baseline, accompanied by sustained reductions in PTSD intrusion symptoms. In contrast, ABM produced no significant neurofunctional or behavioral effects [71]. Lanning et al. [72] performed a single-arm uncontrolled study investigating equine-assisted services (EAS) in military veterans with PTSD. Bilateral prefrontal cortical activity was monitored using fNIRS during equine-related and non-equine activities. Although no significant treatment-related changes were observed in oxy-Hb or total hemoglobin concentrations, equine-related activities elicited greater deoxy-Hb responses than meditation or processing activities. These neurofunctional findings were observed alongside significant improvements in anxiety, psychological flexibility, and affective outcomes; no formal association analyses between hemodynamic changes and psychological measures were reported.

Overall, the available evidence indicates that NIRS/fNIRS is capable of detecting treatment-related neurofunctional changes associated with non-pharmacological interventions for PTSD and trauma-related symptomatology. Across studies, treatment was consistently accompanied by modulation of prefrontal cortical hemodynamics, although the direction and magnitude of these changes varied according to intervention type, experimental paradigm, and neuroimaging methodology. Collectively, these findings support the feasibility of repeated NIRS/fNIRS assessment while simultaneously highlighting the substantial methodological heterogeneity that currently limits direct comparison across studies.

Discussion

This narrative review critically synthesized the current evidence regarding the application of NIRS/fNIRS for monitoring treatment-related neurofunctional changes in adults with PTSD and trauma-related symptomatology. Overall, although the available evidence remains limited, the findings consistently support the feasibility of repeated NIRS/fNIRS assessments for detecting longitudinal cortical hemodynamic changes associated with non-pharmacological interventions. At the same time, the review highlights the early developmental stage of this field, which is currently characterized by a limited number of studies and considerable methodological heterogeneity.

A particularly relevant finding emerging from this review is that, after applying rigorous eligibility criteria, only four longitudinal studies specifically addressed treatment-related neurofunctional changes in PTSD or trauma-related symptomatology using repeated NIRS/fNIRS assessments. This observation underscores that longitudinal neurofunctional monitoring in PTSD remains an underdeveloped area of investigation rather than reflecting limitations of the present review.

Another important finding is the complete absence of eligible longitudinal studies investigating pharmacological interventions using NIRS/fNIRS. Because pharmacotherapy remains one of the cornerstones of evidence-based PTSD treatment, this gap represents a significant unmet need within the current literature. The integration of NIRS/fNIRS into pharmacological clinical trials could provide valuable mechanistic information regarding cortical responses to treatment that cannot be captured through symptom-based clinical measures alone, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of treatment response [16, 35].

Despite differences in intervention modality, study design, and neuroimaging protocols, all included investigations targeted prefrontal cortical regions involved in emotion regulation, executive functioning, attentional control, and top-down modulation of limbic activity. Across studies, treatment was consistently associated with measurable modulation of prefrontal hemodynamic responses, although the direction and magnitude of these neurofunctional changes varied according to the therapeutic approach and experimental paradigm. These findings collectively suggest that fNIRS is sufficiently sensitive to detect dynamic cortical changes occurring during psychological interventions, supporting its potential role as a longitudinal neurofunctional monitoring tool rather than solely as a cross-sectional assessment technique.

Among the interventions examined, the strongest and most consistent evidence was observed for trauma-focused psychotherapies based on EMDR [73, 74], in which longitudinal reductions in prefrontal oxy-Hb activation during trauma recall were associated with improvements in PTSD symptom severity. Conversely, behavioral interventions such as attention-based cognitive training [71] and equine-assisted services [72] demonstrated more heterogeneous neurofunctional patterns, likely reflecting differences in therapeutic mechanisms, treatment duration, and cognitive processes engaged during intervention.

Taken together, these findings indicate that the principal value of fNIRS may lie in its ability to capture treatment-related cortical plasticity over time rather than to provide a static characterization of PTSD-related dysfunction. This distinction is particularly relevant because most previous neuroimaging studies have focused on identifying disorder-related abnormalities, whereas relatively few investigations have examined how cortical activity evolves throughout therapeutic interventions [34]. Consequently, repeated fNIRS has the potential to complement conventional clinical outcome measures by providing objective information regarding neurofunctional changes occurring during treatment. In addition, the portability, relatively low cost, and ease of administration of fNIRS make repeated assessments feasible in routine clinical settings, enabling the characterization of neurofunctional trajectories with a temporal granularity that is difficult to achieve using other MRI-based techniques. Moreover, longitudinal changes in cortical activation may emerge before overt clinical improvements become detectable, raising the possibility that fNIRS-derived measures could serve as early indicators of treatment response and support timely adjustments of therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, its greater ecological flexibility also allows neural responses to be assessed during more naturalistic behavioral paradigms and potentially within therapeutic contexts, providing information that may better reflect patients’ everyday functioning.

In this perspective, fNIRS could contribute not only to documenting treatment-induced neuroplasticity but also to informing personalized intervention strategies by identifying individual patterns of neurofunctional adaptation.

The present findings should be interpreted within the broader framework of contemporary neuroimaging research on PTSD. Over the past two decades, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has substantially advanced the understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying PTSD, consistently demonstrating abnormalities within distributed fronto-limbic networks involved in emotional regulation, fear processing, salience detection, and executive control [15, 16]. In particular, altered activity and functional connectivity involving the dorsolateral and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, hippocampus, and insula have been repeatedly associated with impaired emotion regulation and deficient top-down control over trauma-related emotional responses.

Although fNIRS cannot directly assess subcortical structures such as the amygdala or hippocampus because of its limited penetration depth, the present findings are remarkably consistent with the broader fMRI literature. Across all included studies, treatment-related neurofunctional changes were observed within prefrontal cortical regions known to play a central role in cognitive control, attentional regulation, and modulation of limbic activity. Consequently, rather than providing alternative evidence to fMRI, the available fNIRS studies appear to complement existing neuroimaging findings by offering longitudinal information on cortical functional changes occurring during treatment [42].

This complementary role is particularly relevant because the respective strengths of fMRI and fNIRS differ substantially. Functional MRI remains the reference standard for whole-brain functional mapping owing to its excellent spatial resolution and ability to investigate both cortical and subcortical networks. Conversely, fNIRS offers several methodological advantages that are particularly valuable for longitudinal psychiatric research, including portability, relatively low cost, greater ecological validity, and the possibility of repeated measurements in naturalistic clinical environments [38, 39, 42, 65]. Rather than competing technologies, the two techniques should therefore be considered complementary, with fMRI primarily contributing to the characterization of large-scale neural circuits and fNIRS providing a practical approach for repeated monitoring of cortical neurofunctional changes throughout therapeutic interventions.

An additional aspect emerging from the present review concerns the neurobiological interpretation of treatment-related prefrontal modulation. Although the included interventions differed substantially in their therapeutic mechanisms, all were associated with changes involving cortical regions supporting executive functioning, attentional control, and emotion regulation. These observations are consistent with current models proposing that successful PTSD treatment is accompanied by progressive restoration of prefrontal regulatory processes and improved top-down modulation of trauma-related emotional responses [15, 16]. From this perspective, the observed hemodynamic changes should not simply be interpreted as isolated variations in cortical activation but rather as indirect markers of functional neuroplasticity accompanying clinical recovery.

The EMDR studies included in the present review provide particularly compelling evidence supporting this interpretation [73, 74]. Longitudinal reductions in prefrontal oxy-Hb activation during trauma recall were associated with significant improvements in PTSD symptoms, suggesting that repeated therapeutic exposure may progressively reduce the cognitive and emotional demands associated with trauma processing. Although these findings require replication in larger samples, they are broadly consistent with previous neuroimaging studies reporting normalization of prefrontal activity and functional connectivity following successful psychotherapy.

Conversely, the more heterogeneous findings observed following attention-based cognitive training and equine-assisted interventions likely reflect differences in therapeutic targets rather than inconsistencies of the neuroimaging technique itself [71, 72]. Whereas EMDR directly addresses trauma-related memory processing, attention-based interventions primarily modify cognitive control and attentional bias, and equine-assisted services engage complex emotional, interpersonal, and sensorimotor processes. Consequently, differences in the direction and magnitude of cortical hemodynamic changes across interventions are biologically plausible and further emphasize the importance of interpreting fNIRS findings within the specific neurocognitive mechanisms engaged by each therapeutic approach rather than expecting uniform patterns of cortical activation across all treatments.

Beyond their neurobiological interpretation, the present findings also raise important questions regarding the role of NIRS/fNIRS-derived measures as potential biomarkers of treatment response. Most neuroimaging studies conducted in PTSD to date have primarily focused on identifying neural correlates of disease severity or on predicting clinical outcomes before treatment. In contrast, the studies included in the present review adopted a longitudinal perspective, aiming to characterize neurofunctional changes occurring throughout therapeutic interventions. This distinction is clinically relevant because biomarkers associated with baseline vulnerability or prognosis (trait biomarkers) are conceptually different from biomarkers reflecting dynamic neurobiological changes occurring during treatment (state biomarkers) [34].

From this perspective, the available evidence suggests that fNIRS may be particularly well suited to monitoring state-dependent neurofunctional changes rather than identifying stable diagnostic signatures of PTSD. The longitudinal modulation of prefrontal hemodynamic activity observed across the included studies indicates that cortical activation patterns are not static but evolve in parallel with therapeutic processes, supporting the hypothesis that repeated fNIRS assessments may provide objective information regarding treatment engagement and neurofunctional recovery. Although the current evidence remains insufficient to establish fNIRS-derived measures as validated biomarkers of treatment response, these findings support their potential role as candidate longitudinal neurofunctional biomarkers requiring further validation in larger prospective studies [43, 47, 75].

This distinction also has important implications for precision psychiatry. Current PTSD treatment selection still relies predominantly on clinical symptoms and patient history, whereas objective biological indicators capable of monitoring treatment-related neurofunctional changes remain largely unavailable. In this context, repeated fNIRS assessments could complement conventional clinical evaluations by providing additional information on cortical adaptations occurring during treatment, potentially facilitating the early identification of responders and non-responders and supporting more individualized therapeutic strategies [35, 75].

An additional methodological consideration emerging from the present review concerns the interpretation of different fNIRS-derived hemodynamic measures. Most included studies primarily relied on oxygenated hemoglobin (oxy-Hb), whereas deoxygenated hemoglobin (deoxy-Hb) was evaluated less consistently. This predominance of oxy-Hb is consistent with the broader fNIRS literature, in which oxy-Hb generally demonstrates greater sensitivity to task-related cortical activation because of its larger signal amplitude and higher signal-to-noise ratio [38, 50, 65]. Nevertheless, the limited evidence currently available does not support considering oxy-Hb and deoxy-Hb as competing measures. Rather, these chromophores provide complementary physiological information regarding cerebral oxygen delivery, oxygen extraction, and neurovascular coupling. Consequently, future longitudinal studies should ideally report both measures to facilitate comparison across investigations and improve understanding of treatment-related neurovascular mechanisms.

The present review also highlights substantial methodological heterogeneity across the available literature. Considerable variability was observed with respect to intervention protocols, duration of treatment, timing of neuroimaging assessments, experimental paradigms, cortical regions investigated, acquisition systems, and analytical strategies. Such heterogeneity currently precludes direct comparison across studies and limits the possibility of identifying consistent neurofunctional signatures associated with specific therapeutic interventions. Importantly, differences in neurofunctional findings are likely to reflect not only methodological variability but also genuine differences in the neurocognitive mechanisms engaged by distinct interventions, reinforcing the need for greater methodological standardization without overlooking the biological complexity of PTSD treatment.

Collectively, these observations indicate that the principal challenge for future research is no longer to demonstrate the technical feasibility of fNIRS, which has already been established across numerous psychiatric applications, but rather to determine how longitudinal neurofunctional measurements can be standardized, validated, and integrated into clinically meaningful models of treatment monitoring. Achieving this objective will require adequately powered longitudinal studies, harmonized acquisition and analysis protocols, multimodal neuroimaging approaches combining fNIRS with complementary techniques such as fMRI or electroencephalography, and closer integration between neurofunctional measures and clinically relevant outcomes.

Despite the promising findings summarized in this review, several limitations of the current evidence should be acknowledged. First, the number of available longitudinal studies remains very limited, reflecting the early developmental stage of this research field. Consequently, the conclusions that can be drawn regarding treatment-related neurofunctional changes remain necessarily preliminary. Second, the included studies were characterized by substantial methodological heterogeneity with respect to clinical populations, intervention protocols, treatment duration, neuroimaging paradigms, cortical regions investigated, acquisition systems, and analytical procedures. Such variability limits direct comparison across studies and currently precludes the identification of robust neurofunctional signatures associated with specific therapeutic interventions.

Additional limitations concern the relatively small sample sizes of most studies, which reduce statistical power and may contribute to inconsistent findings across investigations. Furthermore, differences in medication status, symptom severity, treatment history, and clinical comorbidities were not consistently controlled or reported, introducing potential sources of variability that may influence cortical hemodynamic responses independently of the interventions under investigation. Future studies should therefore adopt more homogeneous clinical samples and standardized reporting procedures to improve comparability and reproducibility.

At the same time, these limitations should not obscure the principal contribution of the available evidence. Across different therapeutic approaches, repeated NIRS/fNIRS assessments consistently demonstrated the ability to detect treatment-related modulation of prefrontal cortical activity, supporting the feasibility of longitudinal neurofunctional monitoring in PTSD and trauma-related symptomatology. Although the direction and magnitude of these changes varied across studies, such variability likely reflects both methodological diversity and genuine differences in the neurocognitive mechanisms engaged by distinct interventions.

Future research should therefore move beyond proof-of-concept studies and focus on the development of standardized longitudinal protocols that allow direct comparison across laboratories and clinical settings. Harmonization of acquisition parameters, preprocessing pipelines, cognitive paradigms, and outcome measures will be essential to improve reproducibility and facilitate evidence synthesis. Particular attention should also be devoted to multicenter studies with adequately powered samples, repeated follow-up assessments, and the integration of neurofunctional findings with standardized clinical outcomes.

An additional priority will be the integration of fNIRS with complementary neuroimaging and psychophysiological techniques. Multimodal approaches combining fNIRS with functional magnetic resonance imaging, electroencephalography, autonomic measures, or digital behavioral phenotyping may provide a more comprehensive characterization of treatment-related brain changes by simultaneously capturing cortical activation, large-scale network organization, and physiological responses to therapy. Such multimodal frameworks are likely to represent an important direction for translational PTSD research over the coming years [38, 39, 42].

Finally, although current evidence remains insufficient to support the routine clinical implementation of fNIRS as a biomarker of treatment response, the present review suggests that the technique possesses several characteristics required for future clinical translation, including non-invasiveness, portability, repeatability, and compatibility with ecologically valid therapeutic settings. Continued methodological refinement, together with larger prospective longitudinal studies, will be necessary to determine whether fNIRS-derived measures can ultimately contribute to personalized treatment monitoring within precision psychiatry.

Conclusions

This narrative review provides the first critical synthesis specifically focused on the application of NIRS/fNIRS for monitoring treatment-related neurofunctional changes in PTSD and trauma-related symptomatology. Although only a limited number of longitudinal studies currently meet rigorous methodological criteria, the available evidence consistently supports the feasibility of repeated NIRS/fNIRS assessments for detecting cortical neurofunctional changes associated with non-pharmacological therapeutic interventions.

Importantly, the review also identifies significant gaps in the existing literature, including the complete absence of longitudinal studies evaluating pharmacological treatments, the marked methodological heterogeneity across available investigations, and the lack of standardized acquisition and analytical protocols. Addressing these limitations should represent a priority for future research.

Rather than replacing established neuroimaging techniques such as fMRI, fNIRS should be considered a complementary approach that is particularly well suited for repeated longitudinal assessments performed in clinically realistic settings. Its portability, tolerability, and feasibility make it a promising candidate for monitoring dynamic treatment-related cortical changes throughout the therapeutic process.

Overall, while the current evidence remains preliminary, the findings of this review support continued investigation of fNIRS as a candidate longitudinal neurofunctional biomarker capable of complementing conventional clinical outcome measures. Future multimodal and adequately powered longitudinal studies will be essential to determine its clinical utility and to facilitate the integration of objective neurofunctional monitoring into personalized treatment strategies for PTSD and trauma-related disorders.
