Potential of ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW as Early and Sensitive Indicators of Chronic Heart Failure Status and Medication Adherence in a Patient With Dementia: A Case Report
Authors: Junko Shida, Hiromi Misawa, Tomomi Suzuki, Nozomi Aoki, Mai Miura, Itsuro Kazama, Masahiro Wanezaki, Ryosuke Watanabe, Chisaki Uno, Yumi Matsuda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn an elderly patient with chronic heart failure (CHF) and dementia, the extracellular fluid volume (ECW)/intracellular fluid volume (ICW) and ECW/total body water (TBW) ratios measured by bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) may serve as early and sensitive indicators of CHF status and effective markers for assessing medication adherence.Keywords: bioimpedance analysis (BIA), chronic heart failure, dementia, ECW/total body water (TBW), extracellular fluid volume (ECW)/intracellular fluid volume (ICW)

Introduction

The number of chronic heart failure (CHF) patients in Japan is estimated to be approximately 1.2 million [1], and projections suggest that this figure will reach approximately 1.3 million by 2030 [2]. The incidence of CHF increases with age, and in Japan, a superaged society, the rapid rise in elderly CHF patients has become a significant social issue [3]. On the other hand, since 2020, the treatment of CHF in Japan has undergone a paradigm shift with the introduction of four‐drug combination therapy—angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI), sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2 inhibitor), β‐blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA)—collectively known as the “Fantastic Four,” which has demonstrated promising outcomes, including improved survival [4].

Despite remarkable advancements in pharmacological therapy, dementia remains a significant patient‐related factor that can hinder treatment. The prevalence of dementia among elderly patients with CHF is approximately 20% on average [5], reaching two to four times that of healthy individuals [6, 7], and its impact on medication adherence is a growing concern. Core symptoms of dementia, such as memory impairment and decreased comprehension and judgment, reduce patients' self‐care ability and quality of life [8], consequently influencing the pathophysiology and prognosis of CHF. In fact, the 30‐day readmission rate for CHF patients with dementia is significantly higher than that for those without dementia [9], and studies have suggested that decreased medication adherence due to memory impairment contributes to increased readmission and mortality rates [10]. Particularly in Japan, approximately 20% of elderly individuals with dementia live alone [11], and this proportion is expected to increase further. Given this trend, the decline in medication adherence is anticipated to become an increasingly critical barrier to effective CHF treatment.

Strategies to improve medication adherence in patients with dementia include optimizing the number of medications, simplifying administration methods, and adopting approaches that facilitate management by both the patient and caregivers [12]. However, even when these strategies are combined with support services such as home nursing care, directly monitoring medication intake remains challenging, particularly for patients living alone. Consequently, the assessment of medication adherence often relies on indirect and potentially unreliable information, such as patient self‐reports or verification of empty medication containers. As a result, when symptoms fail to improve or worsen, it becomes difficult to determine whether the poor therapeutic response is due to inadequate medication efficacy despite proper adherence or whether nonadherence is the primary cause. This uncertainty poses a significant barrier to treatment decisions, including medication selection and dose adjustments.

Thus, maintaining medication adherence in elderly CHF patients with dementia—especially those living alone—is inherently challenging, and evaluating drug efficacy is equally difficult. Given this context, ensuring and improving medication adherence during the fluctuating course of CHF is crucial not only for preventing hospital readmissions and maintaining quality of life but also from a healthcare economics perspective.

As mentioned above, assessing medication adherence is essential in the management of CHF, and body weight is another key objective indicator that is frequently emphasized. According to the acute and CHF management guidelines, rapid weight gain is considered a sign of worsening CHF, warranting an increase in diuretic dosage [13]. Particularly in home care settings, where immediately accessible objective indicators are limited, weight‐based assessments tend to be heavily relied upon [14]. However, recent studies have highlighted the limitations of using body weight as the sole indicator of fluid status [15]. Body weight does not always accurately reflect fluid overload or dehydration [16] and is particularly susceptible to fluctuations due to edema and changes in nutritional status [17]. Consequently, an overreliance on weight‐based assessments may hinder precise evaluations of fluid volume, potentially delaying appropriate care [14].

To address these challenges, we actively utilize bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) for body composition monitoring. BIA is considered more accurate than body weight in quantitatively assessing a patient's fluid status at the bedside [14, 15]. Previous studies have reported that measuring the extracellular fluid volume (ECW), total body water (TBW), and edema index via BIA is beneficial for fluid monitoring and clinical risk stratification in CHF patients [18, 19]. On the basis of these findings, regular body composition monitoring via BIA—particularly ECW assessment—has been suggested as a necessary component of home‐based disease management for CHF patients [14].

In this study, we conducted regular monitoring of the ECW in elderly patients with chronic CHF living alone with dementia. This approach allowed us to assess fluid status, which is difficult to evaluate on the basis of only body weight changes, and to explore the potential of ECW monitoring for evaluating medication adherence, drug efficacy, and CHF status.

As previously mentioned, accumulating evidence suggests that changes in the ECW reflect the pathophysiology of CHF [14, 15, 18, 19]. However, case reports assessing body composition monitoring in CHF patients with dementia remain scarce, and few studies have explored its relationship with medication adherence and drug efficacy. Notably, current CHF guidelines lack sufficient descriptions of the management of cognitive impairment and dementia, highlighting the need for the development of preventive strategies and intervention methods [8]. Therefore, this study provides novel insights into the management of chronic CHF by supporting the utility of the ECW as an objective indicator. Additionally, these findings suggest the potential application of ECW monitoring in assessing medication adherence in patients with dementia and as a tool for evaluating CHF status prior to blood tests.

Objective

This study aimed to evaluate the usefulness of body composition monitoring via BIA for assessing medication adherence, treatment efficacy, and CHF status in an elderly patient with dementia living alone at home.

Methodology

Body composition analysis was conducted using the TORAY Medical MLT‐600 N [20], which employs multifrequency bioelectrical impedance (2.5–350 kHz) for precise analysis of body composition and fluid distribution [20]. Compared to the high‐performance medical‐grade InBody970 [21], the MLT‐600 N showed smaller measurement errors [14], confirming its accuracy and reliability.

Patient Information

The patient was a man in his 80s with a medical history of hypertension, atrial fibrillation, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), renal impairment, and mild dementia. He lived alone in a traditional Japanese‐style house without family support. He was independent in basic activities of daily living (ADL) but required assistance with instrumental activities of daily living (IADL).

Case History: Clinical Findings and Timeline

In May 2023, he was diagnosed with atrial fibrillation and HFrEF (left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) 30%–40%, Clinical Scenario: CS1). After hospitalization, he transitioned to home‐based care. Subsequently, due to limited disease understanding and cognitive decline, he discontinued outpatient follow‐up and sought medical attention only when symptoms worsened. His NT‐proBNP levels remained around 1000 pg/mL. He frequently discarded prescribed medications based on his own judgment, citing reasons such as “no effect” or “not feeling unwell,” indicating poor medication adherence. Cognitive function was assessed as a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) of 1 and a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of 21.

Regarding lifestyle, he consumed meals irregularly two to three times per day with cooking support from a home helper. He had a history of alcohol consumption spanning over 60 years and was currently drinking 500 mL of beer daily. He had smoked 20–30 cigarettes per day for 60 years and continued to smoke approximately 8 cigarettes daily. His physical activity was extremely limited, spending most of his time seated, except for brief weekly outings by taxi. He showed no sustained engagement in cardiac rehabilitation.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations: Diagnostic Assessment

In September 2024, he presented with dyspnea and bilateral leg edema (Figure 1). NT‐proBNP had risen to 3886 pg/mL. His blood pressure was 120–130/80 mmHg, heart rate 60 bpm, and SpO2 96%–98%. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed hypokinesis of the anterior wall and septum, with an LVEF of 35%. Mild regurgitation was noted in the aortic and mitral valves, with no moderate or severe stenosis or regurgitation. Tricuspid regurgitation was also mild. The right atrium and right ventricle were moderately enlarged, and the inferior vena cava (IVC) measured 25 mm with no respiratory variation. The New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification was Class III, and no significant changes in renal function were observed.

Treatment: Therapeutic Intervention

Multiple medications, including carvedilol 20 mg, were continuously prescribed (Figure 1). To manage hypertension, sacubitril/valsartan (ARNI) was titrated up to 400 mg.

Home‐visit nursing care began in September 2024. Initially, visits were conducted daily to directly confirm medication intake after breakfast. One month later, as symptoms improved, visits were reduced to twice weekly. During visits, nurses assessed medication adherence by checking for medication bags and remaining pills, and they organized medications for the next scheduled visit. Outpatient visits were once a month, and home care services were utilized three times per week.

Conclusion and Results: Follow‐Up and Outcomes

After the initiation of home nursing care, dyspnea at rest and bilateral leg edema resolved, and NT‐proBNP decreased to 1179 pg/mL (Figure 1). LVEF measured by TTE remained at 30%–40%. In addition, cardiac function was periodically assessed using focused cardiac ultrasound (FOCUS), which showed no left ventricular enlargement, severely reduced systolic function, a small amount of pericardial effusion, no right ventricular enlargement, and an IVC of 16 mm with preserved respiratory variation. The NYHA classification remained Class III.

Despite the up‐titration of ARNI to 400 mg, blood pressure consistently remained elevated at 170–180/80 mmHg, but no recurrence of heart failure symptoms was observed. The attending physician and visiting nurses adjusted medications cautiously, considering the patient's cognitive status and history of discontinuing treatment.

Body composition and fluid balance were monitored over time. After ARNI dose escalation, the patient's body weight remained stable with minor fluctuations. Both ECW and intracellular water (ICW) showed corresponding changes. Notably, the ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW ratios demonstrated a consistent downward trend throughout the observation period.

Discussion

Effectiveness of ECW‐Based Monitoring in CHF Management

Despite the limitation of involving a single patient, this study yielded two key findings regarding body composition measurements in a home‐based HFrEF patient with dementia:

Temporary dehydration and intracellular fluid shifts could be more accurately evaluated via the ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW ratios to assess fluid balance.

The patient had minimal subjective symptoms and physical findings indicative of CHF, and neither blood pressure reduction nor improvement in the ejection fraction was observed after ARNI dose escalation. Additionally, given his prior history of poor adherence, there was a suspicion of recurring medication nonadherence. However, the reduction in NT‐proBNP, a biomarker unaffected by neprilysin inhibition [22], combined with the absence of renal function deterioration, suggests an improvement in CHF status. Notably, ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW were the only indicators correlated with NT‐proBNP reduction, highlighting their potential as early and sensitive markers reflecting CHF status in patients with dementia who lack clear symptoms or physical signs.

Thus, body composition monitoring enables a more precise assessment of fluid volume and distribution changes, aiding in the estimation of medication adherence, treatment efficacy, and CHF status. These findings reaffirm that while body weight remains a key indicator for pharmacologic adjustments in CHF management [13], it is not always a reliable parameter in home‐based CHF patients [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

Usefulness and Mechanism of Evaluating the Therapeutic Effects of ARNI With a Focus on ECW

We have previously reported that ECW serves as a reliable and essential objective parameter for monitoring CHF in home care settings [14]. In the present case, in addition to the evaluation of absolute values such as body weight and ECW, the potential significance of continuously assessing changes in ratio‐based indices—such as ECW/ICW, which have been rarely reported as objective markers in heart failure patients—was suggested.

First, examining the trajectories of body weight and ECW independently, both showed moderate fluctuations over time, though the timing of changes did not always coincide. Notably, there was a period when ECW increased despite a reduction in body weight (Figure 1, December 3), which could be interpreted as an early sign of heart failure exacerbation [14]. Thus, while absolute values are helpful for intuitive interpretation, they have limitations in capturing subtle or time‐sensitive changes.

On the other hand, both ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW exhibited a consistent and gradual decline throughout the observation period (Figure 1), suggesting an improvement in fluid distribution. The ECW/ICW ratio reflects the osmotic balance between extracellular and intracellular compartments and is considered helpful for identifying fluid shifts and electrolyte imbalances [22]. It has been reported to be particularly associated with changes in Na+ and K+ levels, as well as serum osmolality (Osm), thereby contributing to the early detection of dehydration, edema, and electrolyte disturbances. Previous studies involving dialysis patients have also indicated the utility of ECW/ICW in assessing prognosis and nutritional status [22].

A decrease in ECW/ICW within the appropriate physiological range—indicating a reduction in ECW and a relative increase in ICW—is theoretically associated with improvements in muscle strength and metabolic function, and is therefore considered a favorable condition [22]. In the present case, although the ECW/TBW ratio remained above 40%, suggesting ongoing extracellular fluid excess, it showed a gradual improvement over time.

Corresponding to these changes in fluid ratios, NT‐proBNP levels decreased by approximately 70%, from 3886 to 1179 pg/mL, indicating a clinically significant response. While NT‐proBNP is known to be influenced by renal function [23], no notable changes in renal function were observed in this case, allowing the reduction in NT‐proBNP to be interpreted as a reflection of decreased myocardial strain [23, 24]. Previous studies have demonstrated that ECW may serve as a predictor of BNP levels [14] and that BNP and NT‐proBNP are highly correlated [24], findings that are consistent with those observed in this case.

Furthermore, prior reports indicate that a ≥ 30% reduction in NT‐proBNP within 4–8 weeks of initiating ARNI therapy is associated with approximately a 60% reduction in the risk of cardiovascular death or heart failure hospitalization [25, 26]. In this case, a reduction exceeding 70% was observed, indicating a robust therapeutic response well beyond the established benchmark.

These findings suggest that changes in fluid balance, which are difficult to capture using only absolute values such as body weight and ECW, may be more accurately assessed using ratio‐based indicators like ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW. Particularly in the home care setting, where measurement conditions may be constrained, combining these composition‐based monitoring indices with other clinical information may contribute to the early detection of heart failure exacerbations and treatment responses.

Mechanism of ARNI and Its Role in Reducing the ECW/ICW and ECW

Following the dose increase of ARNI, the patient did not exhibit a significant reduction in blood pressure, yet the ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW ratios steadily declined. Given this trend, along with the observed decrease in NT‐proBNP and stable body weight, it was inferred that the patient maintained good medication adherence and that the vasodilatory and diuretic effects of ARNIs were achieved, albeit without a direct impact on blood pressure fluctuations.

The mechanism underlying the decrease in the ECW and increase in the ICW with ARNI can first be attributed to the activation of the natriuretic peptide (NP) system [25]. Sacubitril, a neprilysin inhibitor, prevents the degradation of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), and C‐type natriuretic peptide (CNP), thereby increasing natriuresis and vasodilation [25]. This promotes sodium excretion and likely contributes to the reduction in ECW. Additionally, valsartan, an angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB), inhibits aldosterone secretion mediated by angiotensin II [25]. A decrease in aldosterone levels suppresses renal sodium reabsorption, leading to increased sodium and water excretion [25], which further supports ECW reduction.

Furthermore, alterations in transport mechanisms, such as Na+/K+‐ATPase activity and Na+/H+ exchange [26], may decrease the intracellular osmotic pressure, promote water movement into cells, and increase the ICW. As the excess intravascular volume decreased, water was redistributed intracellularly, leading to an increase in the ICW. These physiological responses provide a theoretical explanation for the observed decreases in the ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW ratios, which are consistent with the expected pharmacological effects of ARNIs.

Through these processes, the cardiac workload was alleviated, as evidenced by the significant reduction in NT‐proBNP levels. These findings suggest that the patient maintained adherence to ARNI therapy, achieved therapeutic effects, and consequently experienced an improvement in CHF status.

Notably, a reduction in the ECW/ICW can also result from improved muscle strength through appropriate nutrition and exercise [14, 24]. However, given the patient's cognitive impairment and reluctance toward cardiac rehabilitation, he had not engaged in aerobic or resistance training. If an exercise regimen tailored to cardiac function and nutritional status could be implemented, further improvements might be achievable.

Factors Contributing to the Lack of ARNI‐Induced Blood Pressure Reduction

Despite maintaining good medication adherence and achieving the vasodilatory and diuretic effects of ARNIs, which led to CHF improvement, the lack of a significant reduction in blood pressure may be attributed to two primary factors:

The patient's blood pressure was measured at his home, a traditional Japanese house, during an observation period when the average temperature fell below 0°C [27]. Notably, Japanese homes generally have lower airtightness than international standards do [28], making them particularly cold in winter, which has been associated with adverse health effects [29]. The patient's home was no exception—while the primary living area was maintained above 20°C, hallways and bathrooms dropped below 10°C, creating large temperature fluctuations. This environmental factor likely triggers sympathetic nervous system activation, leading to peripheral vasoconstriction [30].

Additionally, the patient had renal dysfunction and was presumed to have age‐related arterial stiffness, contributing to decreased vascular compliance [31]. When cold exposure and habitual smoking are considered, the combined effects of NE‐induced vasoconstriction likely counteract the vasodilatory properties of ARNIs. This interplay provides a plausible explanation for why, although ARNIs effectively reduce cardiac preload and promote natriuresis, they fail to significantly lower blood pressure. Given these considerations, the addition of an SGLT2 inhibitor, which exerts its effects primarily through natriuresis, could enhance blood pressure reduction and further improve CHF status, compensating for the limitations observed in this case.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study has three primary limitations.

First, it is based on a single case with a limited observation period, which necessitates caution in interpreting the findings as they remain speculative in nature.

Second, medication adherence in this study was evaluated indirectly through confirmation of remaining medication packets, without employing remote monitoring or direct observation methods. Therefore, interpretations should be made with this limitation in mind.

Third, it cannot be ruled out that lifestyle‐related environmental factors—such as cold exposure, alcohol consumption, or smoking—may have influenced the results.

Fluid assessment in heart failure is inherently complex and requires an integrative evaluation using multiple indicators.

Moving forward, larger‐scale and multi‐center studies are needed to verify the clinical utility and reliability of ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW ratios as assessment tools for heart failure and medication adherence. Establishing standardized monitoring protocols is also desirable.

Conclusion

This case suggests that the ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW ratios, measured via BIA, may serve as objective indicators to evaluate the therapeutic effectiveness of ARNI treatment and medication adherence.

These indices also hold potential as predictive markers for NT‐proBNP levels. They may provide a sensitive and early means to detect heart failure status in patients with both dementia and heart failure, who often present with minimal symptoms or physical findings.

Monitoring ECW/ICW is relatively easy to implement in clinical settings such as home visits and outpatient care. From a clinical standpoint, incorporating these indicators into home care monitoring protocols may enable earlier detection of fluid imbalance or signs of non‐adherence to medication, thereby facilitating more timely treatment decisions.

In the future, integrating BIA‐derived indices into remote monitoring systems and combining them with existing heart failure management tools may contribute to a more comprehensive risk assessment and therapeutic optimization, particularly for elderly patients with cognitive decline.
