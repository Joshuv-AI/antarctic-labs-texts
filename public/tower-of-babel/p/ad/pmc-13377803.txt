The impact of facilitating patient involvement on decisional conflict among patients undergoing surgical treatment for lung cancer: the serial mediating roles of hope level and social support
Authors: Na Ren, Fengyan Ma, Mengbai Tian, Xiaoran Qu, Mufei Sun, Guochao Zhang, Yun Che, Ruifeng Xu, Yimin Qi, Mingyu Wang, Xinyu Zhang, Zhe Song, Liang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the development of new technologies, surgical treatment options for lung cancer have become increasingly diverse, providing patients with more choices before surgery, which may lead to hesitation, uncertainty, and decisional conflict. Decisional conflict can affect psychological well-being and may reduce treatment adherence. Facilitating patient involvement has been proposed as a potential strategy to reduce decisional conflict; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.AimThis study aimed to examine the association between facilitating patient involvement and decisional conflict among patients undergoing surgical treatment for lung cancer and to explore the mediating and serial mediating roles of hope level and social support.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 321 patients undergoing surgical treatment for lung cancer using a convenience sampling method. Data were collected using a self-developed sociodemographic questionnaire, the Facilitating Patient Involvement Scale, the Herth Hope Index, the Perceived Social Support Scale, and the Decisional Conflict Scale. Mediation and serial mediation analyses were performed using Model 6 of the PROCESS macro (version 4.1) for SPSS, with 5,000 bootstrap resamples to estimate indirect effects and their 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe median decisional conflict score was 25.00 (IQR:6.25–28.13). Overall, Overall, 51.4% of patients had decisional conflict, and 13.7% reported high levels of conflict. Facilitating patient involvement (r = − 0.394), hope level (r = − 0.504), and social support (r = − 0.677) were all negatively correlated with decisional conflict (all P < 0.05). Facilitating patient involvement was associated with decisional conflict through both direct and indirect pathways. The total effect was − 0.744, with an indirect effect of − 0.337, accounting for 45.3% of the total effect. The serial mediation model involving hope level and social support was statistically significant (indirect effect = − 0.069).ConclusionFacilitating patient involvement was associated with lower levels of decisional conflict through both direct and indirect pathways, including indirect pathways involving hope level and social support. These findings provide new perspectives for the development of multi-level decision-support systems. Clinical nursing practice may benefit from greater attention to the integration of psychological and social resources to better address decisional challenges among patients undergoing treatment for lung cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16195-9.Keywords: Lung cancer, Facilitating patient involvement, Hope level, Social support, Decisional conflict, Serial mediation

Background

Lung cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related death worldwide [1, 2]. Its incidence and mortality rates remain high both domestically and internationally, posing a major threat to public health [3]. Surgical resection remains the preferred treatment for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [4]. However, with advances in clinical medical technology, treatment options have become increasingly diverse. In addition to conventional video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), minimally invasive approaches such as thermal ablation and hybrid procedures combining ablation with surgery have been increasingly applied in clinical practice [5, 6]. Among these, thermal ablation is not only suitable for patients with small pulmonary nodules but also provides an important alternative treatment option for patients who are elderly, have impaired cardiopulmonary function, or are unsuitable for conventional surgical treatment [5].

In clinical practice, the selection of treatment strategies is a complex process jointly influenced by tumor characteristics, patients’ baseline conditions (such as age, cardiopulmonary function, and comorbidities), and personal preferences [5, 7]. While the increasing diversity of treatment options offers patients greater opportunities for treatment, it also substantially increases the complexity of decision-making. When multiple treatment options are clinically applicable, patients are often required to carefully weigh long-term efficacy, perioperative risks, and postoperative recovery across different approaches. In addition, each treatment modality differs in economic cost, time consumption, and potential side effects, while patients’ values and preferences also vary considerably [8]. As a result, the decision-making process becomes highly individualized. Under such psychological pressure, patients are often required to engage in complex trade-off evaluations, and this uncertainty may contribute to decisional conflict, manifested as hesitation, psychological distress, and doubt regarding treatment decisions [9].

The concept of decisional conflict was first proposed by Janis and refers to the psychological uncertainty individuals experience when making choices involving risk ambiguity, and value-based trade-offs [10, 11]. When patients’ decisional needs are not adequately met, their quality of life may decline and may be accompanied by negative emotions such as anxiety, depression, and regret [12, 13]. In addition, decisional conflict may delay the treatment process, hinder the effective delivery of medical and nursing care, and adversely affect clinical outcomes [14]. Although previous studies have explored factors associated with decisional conflict among patients with lung cancer, the underlying psychosocial mechanisms remain insufficiently understood and warrant further investigation.

In recent years, facilitating patient involvement, as a core component of the shared decision-making (SDM) model, has received increasing attention. It refers to the extent to which patients perceive that healthcare professionals encourage or support their participation in treatment decisions [15]. Accumulating evidence indicates that greater facilitation by healthcare professionals enhances patients’ disease-related knowledge and understanding of treatment options, which may help reduce decisional uncertainty during treatment decision-making [16, 17]. Therefore, we hypothesized that facilitating patient involvement may be associated with lower levels of preoperative decisional conflict among patients undergoing surgical treatment for lung cancer. In addition to this direct association, psychological and social factors may also play mediating roles in this relationship.

Hope level refers to an individual’s belief that their current situation can improve and is considered a positive psychological resource [18]. It may motivate patients to cope with difficulties and alleviate negative emotions [19]. Previous studies have shown that patients with higher levels of hope are more likely to actively engage with healthcare professionals during treatment and nursing care [20]. When patients are willing to actively seek information about treatment options and maintain a positive outlook, their level of decisional conflict tends to be lower. In addition, facilitating patient involvement may enable patients to perceive encouragement and support from healthcare professionals, thereby enhancing their level of hope. Therefore, we hypothesized that facilitating patient involvement may be associated with decisional conflict through hope level.

Social support, defined as the emotional and informational assistance individuals receive from family, friends, and social networks, is considered an important external resource for alleviating psychological burden and decisional stress among patients with cancer [21, 22]. A strong support system can provide emotional, informational, and financial assistance [23], enabling patients to cope with lung cancer and surgery more effectively, clarify their decision preferences, and improve consistency in medical decision-making, thereby reducing decisional conflict [24]. Meanwhile, facilitating patient involvement reflects the support and assistance provided by healthcare professionals and has been regarded as a form of institutional support in previous studies [25]. Therefore, we hypothesized that facilitating patient involvement may be associated with patients’ decisional conflict through social support.

According to Positive Psychological Resources Theory, hope is regarded as an important internal psychological resource that may enhance individuals’ coping ability and promote active engagement with external support systems [26]. Patients with higher levels of hope often demonstrate greater psychological resilience and more proactive coping strategies. Such patients may be more likely to actively mobilize social resources by seeking emotional and informational support, communicating effectively with healthcare professionals and family members, and maintaining social interactions throughout treatment, thereby enhancing their perceived social support [27]. In addition, previous empirical studies have confirmed a significant association between these two variables [27]. Therefore, we hypothesized that hope level and social support may operate sequentially and exert a serial mediating effect in the relationship between facilitating patient involvement and decisional conflict.

At present, research examining the relationships among facilitating patient involvement, hope level, social support, and decisional conflict remains limited, and the underlying mechanisms of decisional conflict among patients undergoing surgical treatment for lung cancer are not well understood. Therefore, this study proposed four hypotheses and constructed a theoretical model to provide a more comprehensive understanding of patients’ decisional conflict and to inform strategies for improving their physical and psychological well-being and quality of life.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was a cross-sectional study conducted in the Department of Thoracic Surgery at the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, Beijing, China, between September 2023 and January 2024. A convenience sampling method was used to recruit patients with lung cancer scheduled for elective treatment. Patients undergoing therapeutic procedures (including surgery, ablation, or combined approaches) were included, while those undergoing diagnostic procedures (e.g., puncture biopsy) were excluded.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Diagnosis of lung tumor; (3) Awareness of their diagnosis and ability to understand and respond to the questionnaire, either independently or with assistance; (4) Scheduled for elective surgical treatment; (5) Provision of informed consent and voluntary participation in the study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Refusal to participate in the study; (2) Severe dysfunction of other vital organs (e.g., liver or kidney); (3) Presence of communication barriers, hearing impairment, or cognitive impairment.

Sample size estimation

Sample size estimation was based on Kendall’s method [28], which recommends a sample size of 5–10 times the number of variables. In this study, 16 independent variables were included, resulting in a required sample size of 80–160 participants. Considering a potential questionnaire attrition rate of 20%, the estimated sample size ranged from 96 to 192. A total of 324 questionnaires were distributed, of which 321 were returned, yielding a valid response rate of 99.1%. Three patients who did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, all of whom underwent needle biopsy rather than surgical treatment.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the National Cancer Center/Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (No. 22/081-3282). Participants were informed about the purpose and significance of the study prior to the survey. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was ensured.

Survey instruments

Demographic and clinical characteristics questionnaire

The research team developed a self-designed questionnaire based on a literature review. It included information on sex, age, marital status, education level, occupation, place of residence, number of children, living status, monthly per capita household income, payment method, chronic disease, cancer history, disease duration, chemotherapy history, and surgical method.

Facilitation of patient involvement scale

The Facilitation of Patient Involvement Scale (FPI) was developed by Martin [15] to assess the extent to which patients perceive that healthcare professionals encourage or facilitate their participation in treatment decision-making. The scale is unidimensional and consists of 9 items, rated on a 6-point Likert scale. The Chinese version was translated and revised by Wu Qing [29], demonstrating good reliability and validity, with a content validity index of 0.931 and a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.885. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.855.

Herth hope index

The Herth Hope Index (HHI), developed by Herth [30], is widely used to assess hope levels among patients with cancer. It comprises 12 items across three dimensions: positive attitude, positive action, and positive relationships. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of hope. Total scores range from 12 to 48, with scores of 12–23 indicating low hope, 24–35 moderate hope, and 36–48 high hope. The Chinese version was translated by Zhao Haiping [31], with a test–retest reliability of 0.92 and a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.850. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.796.

Perceived social support scale

The Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS) was developed by Zimet et al. [32]. and subsequently translated and adapted for the Chinese population by Jiang Qianjin [33]. It includes 12 items across three dimensions: family support, friend support, and other support. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of perceived social support. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.970.

Decisional conflict scale

The Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS), developed by O’Connor in 1995 [34], is one of the most widely used instruments for assessing decisional conflict. It consists of 16 items across five dimensions: support, uncertainty, informed, values clarity, and effective decision-making. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from “yes,” “probably yes,” “unsure,” “probably no,” to “no,” scored from 0 to 4, respectively. The total score is calculated by averaging the item scores and multiplying by 25, yielding a range of 0–100. Scores < 25 indicate no decisional conflict, scores of 25.0–37.5 indicate a moderate level of decisional conflict, and scores > 37.5 indicate a high level of decisional conflict. Higher scores reflect greater decisional conflict and lower decision quality. The Chinese version has been validated by Lam et al. [35]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.977.

Data collection methods

Data were collected through face-to-face distribution of electronic questionnaire QR codes. Patients were surveyed on the day of hospital admission. To ensure ethical and scientific standards, all surveys were conducted anonymously, and participation did not affect patients’ subsequent treatment. All data were used solely for academic research purposes. Participants were informed about the study in person, and questionnaires were distributed after informed consent was obtained.

To ensure data consistency, trained research staff provided standardized guidance on the questionnaire’s content. Patients predominantly self-administered the survey; however, for those unable to write due to physical or literacy constraints, caregivers assisted by recording the patients’ dictated responses. To maintain data authenticity, research staff supervised these sessions to ensure that caregivers functioned solely as recorders and did not influence or interpret the patients’ answers.

To enhance data quality and completeness, the electronic questionnaire was designed with mandatory response settings, requiring all items to be completed before submission. In addition, technical measures, such as IP address restrictions, were implemented to prevent duplicate responses. These procedures may have contributed to the high response rate and data completeness observed in this study. After data collection, two researchers cross-checked the dataset and supplemented clinical information using medical records from the hospital information system (HIS) to ensure accuracy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0 and R version 4.3.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that continuous variables were not normally distributed; therefore, data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Differences between two groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to examine the associations among decisional conflict, facilitating patient involvement, hope level, and social support.

For mediation analysis, a serial mediation model was applied using Model 6 of the SPSS PROCESS macro (version 4.1) developed by Hayes (2013). Facilitating patient involvement was entered as the independent variable (X), decisional conflict as the dependent variable (Y), and hope level (M1) and social support (M2) as mediators. Education level, place of residence, living status, number of children, monthly per capita household income, and disease duration were included as covariates. The model examined the mediating roles of hope level and social support as sequential mediators in the indirect relationship between facilitating patient involvement and decisional conflict. Statistical significance was determined using two-tailed tests with P < 0.05. In addition, bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated based on 5,000 bootstrap resamples to further verify the mediation effects.

To further examine the robustness of the findings and address potential heterogeneity across treatment types, a sensitivity analysis was also conducted among patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, the largest subgroup in the study sample, using the same serial mediation model and covariate adjustments as in the primary analysis.

Results

Common method bias test

Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias in the collected data. 6 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, with the largest factor accounting for only 38.8% of the variance (< 40%). This indicates that there was no significant common method bias in this study.

Multiple linear regression analysis of relationships among study variables

Before conducting the mediation analysis, diagnostic tests were performed to ensure the validity of the regression model. The Durbin–Watson statistic was approximately 2, indicating no autocorrelation of residuals. In addition, all variance inflation factors (VIFs) were below 10, suggesting the absence of multicollinearity. A bias-corrected bootstrap method (5,000 resamples) was used to test a serial mediation model, with facilitating patient involvement as the independent variable, decisional conflict as the dependent variable, and hope level and social support as mediators, while controlling for education level, place of residence, living status, number of children, monthly per capita household income, and disease duration. These covariates were selected based on variables that showed statistically significant differences in the univariate analysis.

Categorical variables were coded and entered into the regression models as follows: education level (primary school or below = 1, junior to senior high school = 2, associate degree or above = 3); place of residence (rural = 1, town = 2, urban = 3); number of children (0 = 1, 1 = 2, ≥ 2 = 3); living status (yes = 1, no = 2); monthly per capita household income (< 4000 RMB = 1, 4000–9999 RMB = 2, ≥ 10000 RMB = 3); and disease duration (≤ 6 months = 1, > 6 months = 2). Other variables were entered using their original values.

Table 3 presents the results of the stepwise regression analysis. When facilitating patient involvement, hope level, and social support were entered simultaneously into the model, all three variables were negatively associated with decisional conflict: facilitating patient involvement (β = −0.212, P < 0.001), hope level (β = −0.212, P < 0.001), and social support (β = −0.322, P < 0.001).

Serial mediation analysis of the relationships among study variables

Further examination of the mediation pathways is presented in Table 4; Fig. 2. When the mediators were included in the model, the total effect of facilitating patient involvement on decisional conflict of − 0.744 (P < 0.001) decreased to a direct effect of − 0.408 (P < 0.001), indicating partial mediation. Bootstrap analysis with 5,000 resamples showed that the indirect effects were statistically significant. Three indirect pathways were identified: the pathway through hope level (indirect effect = − 0.146, 95% CI [− 0.235, − 0.065]), the pathway through social support (indirect effect = − 0.122, 95% CI [− 0.205, − 0.046]), and the serial mediation pathway involving both hope level and social support (indirect effect = − 0.069, 95% CI [− 0.139, − 0.026]). All confidence intervals excluded zero, indicating statistical significance. These findings suggest that hope level and social support function as both independent and serial mediators in the relationship between facilitating patient involvement and decisional conflict.

Sensitivity analysis

To further examine the robustness of the findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted among patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, the largest subgroup in the study sample, using the same serial mediation model and adjusting for the same covariates as in the primary analysis. The results showed that the total indirect effect remained statistically significant (indirect effect = − 0.237, 95% CI [− 0.371, − 0.116]). Specifically, the indirect pathway through hope level was statistically significant (indirect effect = − 0.116, 95% CI [− 0.213, − 0.031]), and the serial mediation pathway involving hope level and social support was also statistically significant (indirect effect = − 0.050, 95% CI [− 0.118, − 0.012]). However, the indirect pathway through social support alone was not statistically significant (indirect effect = − 0.071, 95% CI [− 0.153, 0.016]). Overall, the sensitivity analysis results were generally consistent with the main analysis and provided additional support for the robustness of the findings (Table 5).

Discussion

This study constructed and examined a serial mediation model involving facilitating patient involvement, hope level, social support, and decisional conflict, systematically exploring the association between facilitating patient involvement and decisional conflict among patients undergoing lung cancer treatment. The findings were generally consistent with the proposed hypotheses. The results indicated that facilitating patient involvement was negatively associated with decisional conflict and was also indirectly associated through the independent and serial mediating roles of hope level and social support. These findings provide a theoretical basis for understanding the psychosocial associations between facilitating patient involvement and decisional conflict and may offer implications for optimizing preoperative shared decision-making among patients with lung cancer.

Association between facilitating patient involvement and decisional conflict

This study found that facilitating patient involvement was negatively associated with decisional conflict (supporting H1), which is consistent with previous clinical studies [36, 37]. When patients perceived greater encouragement and support from healthcare professionals to participate in treatment decision-making, they tended to have a more comprehensive understanding of the diagnostic and treatment process, surgical risks, prognosis, and alternative options, and consequently reported lower levels of decisional conflict [38, 39].

In addition, when healthcare professionals actively and thoroughly explained lung cancer-related knowledge and treatment plans, patients felt valued and respected. This enhanced their cooperation with treatment and increased their willingness to proactively learn about their disease and treatment details, thereby reducing confusion and hesitation during decision-making [40]. These findings suggest that healthcare professionals should establish a patient-centered communication model, integrate patients’ preferences and characteristics, and guide them to fully understand their condition and available treatment options [41]. Encouraging patients to ask questions and addressing their concerns can help ensure information symmetry. Explaining treatment plans in plain language and promoting patient participation through shared decision-making may further reduce decisional conflict.

Hope level as a mediator

This study supported Hypothesis 2, indicating that hope level played a significant mediating role in the association between facilitating patient involvement and decisional conflict. Beyond the mere provision of information, facilitating patient involvement may promote patients’ positive expectations regarding treatment outcomes through emotional support. When patients perceive encouragement from healthcare professionals, they may experience higher levels of hope and greater self-efficacy when facing treatment decisions [30]. This hope-oriented positive mindset may help patients shift from emotional resistance to more rational evaluation, enabling them to assess the risks and benefits of treatment options in a calmer and more objective manner, thereby potentially alleviating decisional conflict through enhanced psychological coping mechanisms. Therefore, nursing interventions should not only focus on information provision but also consider strategies such as goal setting to enhance patients’ sense of hope and better support their potential mediating role.

Social support as a mediator

This study supported Hypothesis 3, indicating that social support played a significant mediating role in the association between facilitating patient involvement and decisional conflict. This finding suggests that facilitating patient involvement may not only be directly associated with lower levels of decisional conflict but may also relate to decisional conflict indirectly through external support resources.

Previous studies have shown that patients who receive sufficient encouragement and opportunities to participate in decision-making often perceive higher levels of professional support and humanistic care during the treatment process [42]. Social support may function as an important supportive resource in this context. On the one hand, strong support systems, including healthcare professionals and family members, may provide timely information clarification and feedback, thereby helping to reduce confusion arising from cognitive differences or information asymmetry [42]. On the other hand, multidimensional emotional support may help patients maintain emotional stability and clearer thinking when facing complex medical decisions [43]. Such psychological security and informational support derived from external resources may, in turn, be associated with lower levels of decisional conflict.

Based on these findings, healthcare professionals may pay greater attention to the assessment and enhancement of patients’ social support systems. In addition to strengthening professional support within the healthcare team, integrating support from family members and peers may also be beneficial. For example, peer-support groups and online communication platforms (e.g., WeChat groups) may provide opportunities for emotional support and experience sharing. A multidimensional support network involving healthcare professionals, family members, and peers may help enhance patients’ confidence during decision-making and may be associated with lower levels of decisional conflict.

Serial mediation of hope level and social support

Notably, this study identified a statistically significant serial mediating pathway involving hope level and social support in the association between facilitating patient involvement and decisional conflict, supporting Hypothesis 4. This finding suggests a potential dynamic interaction between individual psychological resources and external supportive environments.

First, facilitating patient involvement may represent not only the provision of information but also respect for patients’ autonomy by healthcare professionals. Such positive interaction experiences may be associated with higher levels of hope among patients. Second, hope level, as an internal motivational resource, may influence patients’ support-seeking behaviors. Patients with higher levels of hope often demonstrate stronger coping motivation and may be more likely to actively seek emotional and informational support from healthcare professionals, family members, and peers [44, 45]. This mobilization of social resources driven by hope may contribute to a synergistic relationship between psychological resources and social support. Ultimately, when patients possess both sufficient psychological resources (higher hope) and strong external support (social support), they may approach treatment decision-making with greater confidence and emotional stability, which may be associated with lower levels of decisional conflict [23].

Therefore, clinical practice may benefit from an integrated intervention approach focusing on participation, hope, and support. Healthcare professionals may not only pay attention to patients’ experiences of participating in decision-making through patient-centered, detailed communication but also apply positive psychological strategies, such as sharing successful rehabilitation experiences, to enhance patients’ sense of hope. In addition, encouraging family members and peer support systems to provide continuous support may help establish a more comprehensive supportive network and better address patients’ decisional difficulties.

Study limitations

This study had several limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional design, causal relationships among facilitating patient involvement, hope level, social support, and decisional conflict could not be established. Second, participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital in Beijing, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Third, the study included patients undergoing different thoracic interventions, including surgery, thermal ablation, and hybrid procedures, which represent distinct clinical decision contexts. Differences in treatment types may therefore have introduced heterogeneity into the study population. Future studies may further include larger numbers of patients undergoing thermal ablation and hybrid procedures to better examine potential differences across treatment types. Fourth, data were collected through face-to-face administration. Although participation was voluntary and anonymous, the presence of research staff may still have contributed to social desirability or acquiescence bias. Finally, the sample size was relatively limited, and future studies with larger samples and longitudinal designs are needed to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this study showed that facilitating patient involvement was associated with lower levels of decisional conflict through both direct and indirect pathways, including the indirect pathway involving hope level and social support. These findings provide a multi-level theoretical framework for the development of decision-support interventions. By integrating psychological and social resources, clinical nursing strategies may help better address the decisional challenges faced by patients undergoing treatment for lung cancer.
