Cost of a Healthy Diet: A Population-Representative Comparison of 3 Diet Cost Methods in Canada
Authors: Gabriella Luongo, Valerie Tarasuk, Leah E Cahill, Mohammad Hajizadeh, Yanqing Yi, Catherine L Mah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferent food price sources and dietary assessment tools may impact the estimation of diet costs and hamper our understanding of the relationship between diet costs and dietary intakes.ObjectivesWe aimed to investigate the effect of 3 diet cost derivation methods, with increasing numbers of food prices and geographic specificity, holding consistent the dietary assessment tool, on the estimation of diet costs overall and by food group.MethodsWe matched 24-h dietary recall data from the 2015 Canadian Community Health Survey-Nutrition (CCHS-N) to food price data from 3 Canadian Consumer Price Index (CPI) food price lists; national short list, national long list, and provincial long list. We compared the daily ($/day) and energy-adjusted ($/2000 kcal) diet costs overall and by food groups for the overall population (4+), children (4–18), and adults (19+).ResultsThe proportion of dietary intakes (grams) that were covered by CPI prices significantly increased from the national short list to the national long list but did not significantly differ from the national long list to the provincial long list. The national short list resulted in the highest daily and energy-adjusted diet costs overall. No difference in diet costs was noted between the national and provincial long lists. Diet costs for 4 food groups—additions, sweets, fruits, and vegetables, which were poorly covered by the national short list—significantly differed using the national and provincial long lists. All 3 diet cost methods were significantly correlated with energy intakes; however, a strong/very strong correlation was detected for children, and a weak/moderate correlation for adults.ConclusionsThe choice of food price data may introduce bias in the diet cost estimate, as well as limiting our understanding of how individuals allocate their diet costs. Refinement of diet cost estimation methodology and measures can strengthen future studies of how consumers allocate their purchases to their diets.Keywords: diet cost, diet quality, dietary intake, dietary adequacy, food prices

Introduction

Few populations today meet food-based dietary guideline recommendations for a healthier diet [1,2]. The total expenditure toward food or allocated toward food purchases within a household budget, and the nutritional quality of expenditures, has received growing attention as an important research question in population dietary adequacy [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Diet costs are a derived measure that operationalizes household budget expenditure on food through dietary assessment, which estimates the amount of money an individual would need to spend to consume their reported dietary intakes [9,10]. Prevailing diet cost literature suggests that individuals’ food selection behaviors are made within the confines of a cost allocation within a household budget, as well as daily energy requirements (i.e., 2000 kcal/d) [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. On average, nutrient-poor foods are energy-dense and have lower energy costs (i.e., dollars per kilocalorie), whereas nutrient-rich foods are energy-poor and have higher energy costs [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. For example, by food groups, on average, fruits and vegetables have higher energy costs, whereas sugars, fats, and oils have lower energy costs [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. The diet cost literature also shows that more nutritious diets tend to be associated with higher diet costs, whereas less nutritious diets tend to be associated with lower diet costs [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

However, a debate has also been raised that suggests that more nutritious diets do not necessarily cost more. Several studies have specifically examined the question of food costs, namely, how diets reflect food selections constrained by the price, variety, and diversity of commodities and grocery products available in the modern food supply [21,22]. For example, linear programming studies have identified that it is feasible to consume a low-cost nutritious diet [23,24]. In the United States, social policy programs such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program rely on the model of the US Thrifty Food Plan, which uses linear programming models to create a list of groceries that represents a healthy yet affordable diet [25].

Other studies have interrogated diet cost assumptions, suggesting a spurious relationship between costs and energy composition of foods [[26], [27], [28]], whereas energy costs do not appear to be positively associated with unit costs (dollars per gram) or serving costs (dollars per serving) [27,29]. For example, when foods in the United States and United Kingdom were compared by energy costs, more nutritious foods such as fruits and vegetables appeared to be the most expensive [27,29]. However, by unit price or serving price, the costs of fruits and vegetables were among the lowest, whereas the costs of foods high in sodium and sugar were among the highest [27,29]. Yet, when we examine consumer purchasing and consumption patterns at the individual level, more nutritious diets tend to be more expensive both by daily (dollars per day) and energy-adjusted (dollars per kilocalorie) diet costs [9].

Two studies to date have compared different diet cost estimation methods to examine their relative impacts at the individual level [30,31]. Monsivais et al. [30] compared the following 3 diet cost methods in a sample of university faculty and staff (aged 25–65 y): (1) 4-d diet diary with receipt data; (2) 4-d diet diary with supermarket prices; and (3) food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) with supermarket prices. Aaron et al. [31] estimated diet costs in 2 ways in a population of low-income Californian women: (1) 3 24-h dietary recalls with receipt data; and (2) 1 FFQ with store food prices. Although Monsivais et al. [30] did not find any significant differences in the diet cost estimate among the 3 diet cost methods for both daily and energy-adjusted diet costs, sex differences were present; women had a significantly higher energy-adjusted (per 2000 kcal) diet cost than men, but no difference was noted for daily diet cost by sex. Additionally, Aaron et al. [31] identified that although there were no significant differences in the daily diet cost measure between the 2 methods, method 1 (24-h dietary recall and receipt data) provided a higher energy-adjusted diet cost than method 2 (FFQ and supermarket prices).

However, from a methodological perspective, heterogeneity exists in these estimations that may hamper our understanding of how individuals allocate their diet costs [9]. At the individual level, diet costs are typically estimated by multiplying the quantity of food consumed (in grams) by the unit price of that item (in dollars per gram), summed across all foods consumed by an individual. In these 2 comparative studies, both the dietary assessment tool and food price source varied, therefore limiting the potential to identify whether differences in diet cost estimates arose from the dietary assessment or the food price and cost estimation side of the equation.

For example, the availability of food price data relative to the types of foods reported from dietary assessment tools can influence diet cost estimates among food groups. The heterogeneity is apparent in Canadian data where not all dietary intake data can be reasonably matched to food price data, and the differences in the food items that can be matched to food prices varied by food group, energy, and nutrients, thereby introducing systematic error in the diet cost estimation and its relationship to dietary intakes [32]. The Canadian study examined the feasibility and measurement error of using food supply data to measure diet costs, showing that the Canadian Consumer Price Index (CPI) 2015 national short food price list provided excellent coverage of food prices for certain food groups (i.e., fats and oils, processed meats, breakfast cereals, and baby food), but poorer price coverage for others, including those of particular interest to assessing the nutritional quality of diets (i.e., baked products, alcoholic beverages, and snacks) [32]. Energy-adjusted nutrients and diet costs are commonly used metrics in the diet cost literature. Yet, the Canadian analysis identified that only 72.9% of total energy intakes were accounted for by the available food prices [32]. This suggests costs for certain food groups may contribute differentially to the estimation of diet costs, depending on how the larger variety and coverage of prices available for those groups are used in cost estimation.

The prevailing diet cost literature suggests that individuals make food purchasing and consumption decisions within the theoretical confines of diet costs and caloric requirements[[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Therefore it is hypothesized that in the face of diminishing food budgets, individuals will first allocate their diet costs to less expensive sources of energy, which includes energy-dense and nutrient-poor foods, to meet their caloric requirements within their budgetary constraints [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Features of cost estimation such as food price source, dietary intake assessment method, number and specificity of food prices to match to dietary intakes, and considerations for regional variation may bias the derivation of diet costs (under- or overestimate) [6,[33], [34], [35], [36]].

To date no research has isolated the cost estimation in diet cost methodology, by varying cost estimation methods systematically while maintaining a consistent dietary assessment. A recent policy transition in national statistics in Canada has presented a unique opportunity to examine this question. The aforementioned Canadian study examining the feasibility and measurement error of using food supply data to measure diet costs identified that on average the majority (76.3%) of grams of intake reported by respondents of the 2015 Canadian Community Health Survey-Nutrition (CCHS-N) had an appropriate match to a food price available in the 2015 Canadian CPI food price list. Several commonly consumed foods (see Table 1) reported in the 2015 CCHS-N had prices available (i.e., coverage) in the 2015 CPI price list (quadrant 1), generally, widely available staple food groups such as milk and dairy products or grain foods.

Other commonly consumed food groups, however, did not have high CPI price coverage (quadrant 2), specifically: fruits, vegetables, sweets, and additions. This is problematic because these food groups are popular among Canadians, but food prices to assign to these food items do not exist, which may exacerbate possible systematic error when estimating diet costs. Those food items that are not well covered by the CPI but are commonly consumed have the greatest risk of biasing the diet cost estimate. Foods in quadrant 2 are commonly consumed in Canada and have consistently been identified as dietary components that contribute to the nutritional adequacy of diets (e.g., higher fruits and vegetables intakes are linked to reduced noncommunicable disease risk, whereas sweets intake as a marker of elevated free and total sugar intake can indicate elevated risk)[[37], [38], [39]].

The CPI has recently undergone a major digital transition in the estimation of its food price aggregate, from a sampling design and retail audits to retailer-provided scanner data. The availability of scanner data beginning in 2017 has presented the CPI with a significantly larger food supply data set of prices for modeling [[40], [41], [42]]. Additionally, from 2017 onward, new public-use price lists have been made available for commonly consumed foods encompassing greater variety and diversity in the food supply, in particular for certain food groups such as vegetables, as well as the availability of these monthly prices at both the national and provincial level as opposed to only national prices [[40], [41], [42]].

The purpose of this study was to investigate if the greater number and types of food prices, as well as their geographic specificity—characteristics that make them more reflective of the diversity of “real-world” food selections in those food groups made among the population—affect the CPI coverage of commonly consumed foods by Canadians and the estimation of Canadians’ diet costs. We specifically focused on how the method of diet cost derivation affects the diet cost estimate overall and for those foods previously shown to be poorly covered by food supply data in quadrant 2 (additions, fruits, vegetables, and sweets), and whether the findings by different diet cost derivation methods are the same between children and adults. The results will uncover how diet cost methods affect the costing estimate of commonly consumed food groups and may provide further insight into measuring the effects of household food spending decisions on the type, quantity, and adequacy of foods purchased and consumed.

Methods

Data sources

Two sources of Statistics Canada data were utilized for this study. We matched dietary intake data from the 2015 CCHS-N to food price data available in the CPI food price lists in 2015 and 2017 [[40], [41], [42], [43]]. The 2015 CCHS-N is Canada’s most recent nationally representative dietary assessment survey administered to 20,487 Canadians aged ≥1 y across the 10 provinces [44]. All respondents completed a 24-h dietary recall comprising type and quantity of foods consumed in the previous 24 h (midnight to midnight) using the Automated Multiple-Pass Method [43]. The 2015 CCHS-N excluded individuals living in the territories, on reserves and other Indigenous settlements, full-time members of the Canadian Forces, and institutionalized populations [43]. For the purposes of this study, a single recall was used to estimate mean intakes [45], and the analysis was restricted to those aged ≥4 y [46]. Further details on the sociodemographic composition of the 2015 CCHS-N respondents have been previously published [[47], [48], [49], [50]].

The Canadian CPI collects and monitors monthly prices of consumer goods in 8 aggregate categories, including food for the 10 provinces and 3 urban centers in Canada’s 3 territories (1 urban center per territory) [51]. In addition to indices estimating the changes in the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services and their relative components, the CPI also publishes lists of average retail prices (inclusive of tax) for select food items collected from representative stores [51].

In total, 3 food price lists published through the Statistics Canada CPI program were used for this study: (1) 2015 national short list; (2) 2017 national long list adjusted to 2015 prices (herein referred to as the 2015 national long list); and (3) 2017 provincial long list adjusted to 2015 prices (herein referred to as the 2015 provincial long list) [40]. For the 2015 national short list, in 2015, prices were collected at the national monthly level for 49 indicator food items through field collection or scanner data, and the average price of each product was weighted by strata-level census population to represent the average Canadian retail price for that product [51]. The 2015 national short list was the only food price list available that collected data at the same time point (i.e., month and year) as the 2015 CCHS-N, thereby reflecting food and beverage choices made in 2015. Beginning in 2017, Statistics Canada created an expanded, second CPI price list that published the prices of 106 food items at the national and provincial monthly level, derived from estimates using scanner data exclusively [41]. Prices in the price list were weighted based on a market share estimator based on the total units of each food product sold by geography [41]. Since 2017 was the first year of food price collection for this expanded list, we created the national long and provincial short lists using the 2017 data. As the dietary intake data for this study were reflective of food and beverage choices made in 2015, all prices in the 2017 CPI price list (national and provincial) were further adjusted to 2015 prices for the purposes of this study based on the CPI index for food, considering food product category and geography (i.e., national or provincial) [52].

For all 3 food price lists, the price of each food item was converted to dollars per gram. In instances in which the price was expressed per quantity of grams or kilograms, a simple conversion to 1 g was computed. In instances in which the conversion was not direct (i.e., dollars per milliliter or dollars per unit), we identified the most commonly consumed item in the 2015 CCHS-N (nationally or provincially depending on the food price list) that matched the CPI price list item and used the Canadian Nutrient File (CNF) food code for the 2015 CCHS-N item to adjust the price to dollars per gram [53]. To convert dollars per unit to dollars per gram, we used the middle size that was available in the CNF to conduct the conversion [53].

Food price matching

Following previous diet cost methodology, we matched food prices from the 3 CPI price lists to 150 available Bureau of Nutritional Sciences (BNS) food codes representing the food sources of intakes in reported intakes in CCHS-N data [32]. Reported food items with any missing nutrient, gram weight, or energy data were excluded (6.6% of food items removed for those aged ≥4 y, 7.2% for children, and 6.3% for adults), consistent with previous diet cost methodology. Similarly, all water (BNS51C) intake was removed because the price of water could not be reliably estimated. For compliance with Statistics Canada disclosure requirements, respondents with zero energy or gram weight intakes were also excluded (<0.1% of respondents were removed). No exclusions were made based on dietary intakes or diet costs, as there is no evidence on the relationships in error, between diet costs and dietary intakes, that could be used to do so.

BNS food codes were then matched to monthly prices from the 3 CPI food price lists using a previously developed systematic matching process to crosswalk between CPI food items, BNS food codes, and food groups [32]. For all respondents, food prices could be matched precisely to the CCHS-N month of respondent survey completion because the 2015 CCHS-N data collection occurred over a calendar year.

3.All remaining unmatched food items were considered nonmatches. The nonmatches were priced using 3 data sets to establish a crosswalk: (1) The CPI food component, which describes food groupings used to compute the monthly index [52]; (2) CPI food price lists: national short, national long, and provincial long [40,54]; and (3) the unmatched BNS food codes [55]. We first assigned each of the unmatched BNS food codes to the most disaggregated food group category available in the CPI food component (e.g., fruit, fruit preparations and nuts; fresh fruits; apples). Then we assigned food prices in the CPI price lists to each of the food groups in the CPI food component. Finally, we computed a price for the unmatched BNS food code, based on the matched food group from the CPI food component, and a mean of all relevant price(s) available in the CPI price list within the given food group. For example, in the national short list, there was no price available for “BNSD40E: Grapes.” This was resolved by first matching grapes to the most relevant disaggregated food group in the CPI food component, in this case “fresh fruits.” Prices of apples, bananas, grapefruits and oranges were available in the national short price list and matched to the CPI food component “fresh fruits” food group. Finally, the BNSD40E: Grapes national short list price was computed by taking an average of those 4 fruit prices.

Finally, the BNSD40E: Grapes national short list price was computed by taking an average of those 4 fruit prices.

For the national short list and national long list, where more information was needed to facilitate a match to the CPI food group (e.g., BNSD40L: Other fruits), we used the most commonly consumed (in grams) food item reported in that BNS group from the CCHS-N national estimate to identify the specific representative product matchable to CPI. For the provincial long list, the most consumed item (in grams) in each BNS food code per province was used to match to the price list because intakes varied within each BNS food code by province and in certain BNS food codes, multiple prices were available in the CPI price list (e.g., meats, fruits, and vegetables). For example, because the national short list had >1 fruit price available that could be matched to BNSD40L: Other fruits, we needed more information to appropriately match BNSD40L to an appropriate price. For the national short list, the most commonly consumed food item in BNSD40L was blueberries, raw. The national short list did not have an exact or near exact match to blueberries, raw. Using the CPI food component, we matched BNSD40L to fresh fruits based on blueberries, raw being the most commonly consumed food item in BNSD40L. Within the fresh fruits category of the CPI food component, the national short list CPI provided food prices for apples, bananas, grapefruits, and oranges. Therefore, the price assigned to BNSD40L was the average unit price for apples, bananas, grapefruits, and oranges.

Diet cost derivation

For each individual, daily diet costs (dollars per day) were calculated by multiplying the quantity (in grams) of each food item by BNS food code reported in the dietary assessment tool by the matched unit price (dollars per gram) of that food item from the relevant CPI list and then summed across all items consumed. For the 2 national lists, diet costs were computed by matching the month of 2015 CCHS-N completion to the same month in the CPI price lists. For the provincial list, diet costs were computed by matching the month of 2015 CCHS-N completion and province of residency reported by the respondent to the same month and province in the respective CPI price list. Energy-adjusted diet costs were computed by the division of total daily diet cost by total calories consumed (excluding water) multiplied by 2000 to represent a 2000 calorie diet. Daily diet cost (dollars), energy-adjusted diet cost (dollars per 2000 kcal), and the cost by food group for each of the 2 diet cost metrics were computed for each respondent, using each of the 3 diet cost derivation methods.

Analyses

Four analyses were conducted. First, proportions and means were generated to examine the number of BNS food items that had a match (exact or near) with each of the 3 CPI food price lists. Second, daily and energy-adjusted diet costs were computed overall (4+) and among children (aged 4–18 y) and adults (aged 19+ y) as was the cost of each food group. Third, the correlation between each diet cost method was quantified among the population overall, and among children and adults separately for daily and energy-adjusted diet costs. Finally, the correlation between daily diet cost and total energy intake was quantified.

The analysis was conducted using STATA/MP statistical software. The master file of the 2015 CCHS-N accessed through the University of British Columbia and Atlantic Research Data Centres was used. Statistics Canada sampling weights were used ensure that the population was representative of the 33.3 million Canadians. Statistics Canada provided bootstrap replication weights (B 500), used to generate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) that took into account the multi-stage survey design of the 2015 CCHS-N [43]. As this was a descriptive study, nonoverlapping 95% CIs was used to denote significantly different results at alpha = 0.05 [56,57].

For all age groups, using the 2015 CPI national short list resulted in 76.29% (95% CI: 75.80, 76.78%) of reported dietary intakes covered by an exact or near match, based on 44.67% of BNS food codes matched. Using the 2017 CPI national long list accounted for a significantly greater proportion of CPI coverage (82.05%; 95% CI: 81.53, 82.57%) compared to the national short list. Similarly, use of the 2017 provincial long list resulted in 82.07% (95%CI: 81.54, 82.59%) of dietary intakes covered, based on 62.00% to 64.00% (depending on the province) of BNS food codes with an exact or near exact match. This pattern persisted for both children and adults, and the proportion of CPI coverage overall was significantly higher for children than adults.

Table 2 also shows the coverage for the 4 commonly consumed food groups of interest—additions, sweets, fruits, and vegetables. We found that sweets coverage did not significantly increase across the 3 diet cost methods. Additions, fruits, and vegetables, however, significantly increased in dietary intakes covered from the national short list to the national long list but did not significantly differ from the national long list to the provincial long list. This pattern persisted for both children and adults. Correspondingly, the proportion of BNS food codes matched to food prices increased for additions, sweets, fruits, and vegetables from the national short list to the national long list and remained approximately the same from the national long list to the provincial long list.

Diet cost overall and by food groups by food price method

The following results section highlights our findings on whether energy-adjusted diet cost varied by method, to respond to the diet cost literature, which is largely focused on energy costs and dietary intakes. Energy-adjusted and daily diet costs overall and for the 4 selected food groups are discussed below. For full detail of the outcomes of the analysis, Supplemental Table 3 includes the energy-adjusted diet costs for all food groups by all age groups, and Supplemental Table 4 includes the daily diet costs of all food groups by all age groups.

Overall dollar values for energy-adjusted and daily diet costs among Canadians aged ≥4 y were computed to be significantly higher using the national short list than the national and provincial long lists. There was no significant difference, however, in estimated diet costs between the national long list and the provincial long list. The magnitude of difference between diet costs, as computed by the 3 pricing methods, was substantial. Across the entire population, energy-adjusted diet costs were over $3.89 (30%) higher calculated with the national short list than the provincial long list, with energy-adjusted diet costs estimated at $16.70/2000 kcal (95% CI: 16.24, 17.15) using the 2015 national short list, $13.12 (95% CI: 12.86, 13.38) using the national long list, and $12.81 (95% CI: 12.55, 13.07) using the provincial long list for Canadians aged ≥4 y. Children’s energy-adjusted and daily diet costs were highest using the national long list and were significantly different from the national short list and the provincial long list which did not differ from one another. Adults followed the same pattern as for the overall population (4+) wherein the national short list yielded significantly higher diet costs than the national long list, and there was no significant difference between the national long list and the provincial long list.

Discussion

Summary of results

This analysis made use of Statistics Canada nationally representative 24-h dietary recall data from the 2015 CCHS-N and CPI food price data from 2015 and 2017 to compare 3 diet cost derivation methods. We paid particular attention to the overall diet costs, including estimates of diet cost adjusted for energy intake, and the cost of 4 food groups (additions, sweets, fruits, and vegetables) that are commonly consumed by Canadians, but for which the original 2015 national short list had poor availability of food price data.

Overall, the method of diet cost derivation significantly affected the daily and energy-adjusted diet cost estimate overall and cost by food group. As compared to the national short list, the national long list and provincial long list resulted in a significantly higher proportion of intakes (grams) covered with an exact or near exact food price match for total diets, as well as by all 4 food groups of interest.

By diet cost method, the national short list resulted in the most expensive overall diet cost estimate by daily and energy-adjusted diet cost. The national long list and provincial short list did not significantly differ in daily or energy-adjusted diet costs for the overall diet. Additions, fruits, and vegetables had significantly higher diet costs using the national long list and provincial long list as compared to the national short list. Only additions also demonstrated a significant difference using the provincial long list as compared to the national long list. For sweets, daily and energy-adjusted costs were significantly higher in the national short list as compared to the provincial long list.

Between the 3 methods, the national long list and the provincial long list were the most strongly correlated, and the strongest correlation between diet cost method and total energy was noted between the national long list and the total energy.

Differences in diet costs overall and by food groups were also noted between children and adults. Specifically, for children, the national long list provided the highest energy-adjusted and daily diet cost estimate, whereas the national short/provincial long lists provided the lowest estimate. Conversely, for adults, the national short list provided the highest diet cost whereas the national long/provincial long provided the lowest estimate. At the same time, children displayed the strongest correlation with all diet cost methods perhaps suggesting that their food selection represents more energy dense nonstaple foods. For example, among the most popularly consumed foods (i.e., those in quadrant 1 and 2), vegetable, meat, and nonalcoholic beverages were more commonly consumed among adults, whereas milk and dairy products and grains were more commonly consumed among children.

Diet cost derivation and food selection behaviors

Unlike prior work by Monsivais et al. [30] and Aaron et al. [31] comparing diet cost methods, this analysis maintained a consistent dietary assessment tool to disentangle the specific impact of the diet cost method used on the estimation of diet costs, here, by focusing on the quality of food price data used for cost estimation. We found that the diet cost method used had an impact on both daily and energy-adjusted diet costs overall and for both children and adults. Further, likely due to the consistent data collection approach employed by Statistics Canada, all 3 diet cost measures were highly correlated with one another. However, similar to Monsivais et al. [30], we found a weak-to-moderate correlation between all 3 daily diet cost methods and energy intakes overall and a stronger correlation for children than for adults [30].

The 3 methods of diet cost derivation provided increasing levels of food item specificity and geographic coverage. The national short list represented the least specific source of price data as the prices were representative of a short list of food items at the national level. The national long list increased the type and number of food prices collected. Finally, the provincial long list retained the same number of food items but included an additional layer of provincial specific pricing. Overall, increasing the number of prices appeared to have the largest impact on changing the overall diet cost estimate as compared to providing provincial specific prices. At the population level and for adults, there was a significant difference between the national short list and national long list but no difference between the national long list and provincial long list. For children on the other hand, the provincial long list provided a nonsignificant difference in diet costs from the national short list; however, these estimates of diet cost were significantly different from the national long list.

When examining overall diet costs, the greater specificity in prices (i.e., increased price coverage) provided in the national long list resulted in a net lower overall daily diet and energy adjusted diet cost. However, for some commonly consumed food items in the diet, the change in diet cost derivation method resulted in increased diet costs. For instance, for daily and energy-adjusted diet costs, we found that increasing the variety of prices for fruits, vegetables, milk and dairy products, and grains provided a significantly greater diet cost estimate. Conversely, increasing the number of meat product prices and sweets appeared to reduce diet costs. As much as the number of food prices (i.e., variety of foods) appear to have an impact on the diet cost estimate so might the type of the foods included in the price list and the time point for which they were collected, which has a nutritional significance where such breadth of coverage does not account for “true” variation in reported dietary intakes reflective of how individuals use the food supply.

The 3 CPI price lists allowed us to examine this variation in detail by food group within diets. For example, nonalcoholic beverages are a high proportion of the reported dietary intakes of adults, relative to children, and therefore their diet costs. Our findings demonstrated that where nonalcoholic beverages were double the cost in the national short list, they contributed significantly to a higher diet cost; whereas the foods that were more highly consumed among children, such as nonstaple energy-dense foods (e.g., milk and dairy products) contributed more to diet cost differences, as compared to adults, between the national short and long lists. Hence, the price of a food group is based on the number of food prices available to match to food items and the type of food products represented within a food price list. The relative price of a food group can therefore vary within a given diet cost data set (i.e., if the food price coverage differs between food groups) and across data sets (i.e., if food price coverage for a given food group varies between food price lists).

The dollar value of diet costs differences captured in our study is substantial. For example, nonalcoholic beverages showed a $3.40 per day reduction from the national short list to the national long list despite a difference of only 2 food items between the lists (short list with 7 items and long list with 5 items); arguably, nonalcoholic beverages were mostly responsible for the changes in food prices between diet cost methods. In fact, when looking at food prices included in the national long list, much of the product differentiation in fruit juices, soft drinks, and tomato juices are no longer available in the long list, and the package size for fruit juices increased. Typically, in the food supply, larger package sizes result in lower unit prices for products. Additionally, for processed meats, across all 3 diet cost methods the same 2 food prices (wieners and bacon) were used despite a significant reduction in the diet cost between the national short and national long lists. The package size of wieners decreased from a standard 450 g package size to 400 g, which was representative of an average dollars per gram of many package sizes and included an adjustment of prices from 2017 to 2015, which was likely imperfect.

Overall, the cost of a diet is dependent both on the diversity in diet for more expensive or less expensive food prices as well as the quantity consumed. For fruits and vegetables, Canadians appear to have higher “true” reported food diversity, which may have resulted in increased diet cost in the national long list estimate. Similarly, the reduction in the number of nonalcoholic beverage prices in the national long list may have artificially reduced the price of these items thereby widening the gap between less expensive and more expensive sources of foods. Therefore, within a given price database the availability of prices in a given food group relative to other food groups can skew our understanding of purchasing behaviors and differences in diet costs between nutritious and less nutritious food groups.

Unlike the national short list, which prioritized lower cost staple food items, the variety of foods represented in the national long list provided greater product price differentiation that may be reflective of increasing caloric intakes alongside increased diet costs. For example, overall energy-adjusted diet costs differed in price the most for nonalcoholic beverages (price decrease), vegetables (price increase), milk and dairy products (price increase), and fruits (price increase) between the national short list and national long list. Even though milk and dairy products and nonalcoholic beverages were well covered in the national short list and were popularly consumed among Canadians, changes in the structure of the food price list between the national short list and national long list contributed to a decrease in the number of prices for nonalcoholic beverages and an increase in the number of prices for milk and dairy products, which significantly changed the prices of these 2 food groups. Conversely, for fruits and vegetables that were popularly consumed but poorly covered by the national short list, the greater product differentiation in the national long list resulted in a significant increase in price. The relative change in the number of prices between the 3 diet cost methods and the quality of prices all contributed to the observation that Canadians tend to consume a high variety diet of nonstaple foods.

Government sources of dietary intake and diet cost data

Although this is the first Canadian analysis to make use of government collected dietary intake and food price data, numerous American diet cost studies have made use of US intake and price data [3,33,59,60]. Similar to the 2015 CCHS-N, the NHANES in the United States uses 24-h dietary recalls to capture the type and quantity of foods consumed by Americans and is commonly used by diet cost studies [3,33,[59], [60], [61]]. To complement the NHANES, in 2001–2002 the Center for Nutrition Policy and Promotion (CNPP) food price database was created [62]. The CNPP and CPI food price list differ in 4 important ways: (1) the CNPP was a purposefully built food price data set for foods reported in the 2001–2002 NHANES with each item in the price database linked to the NHANES data set whereas the CPI price list is a separate source of food price data unattached to the 2015 CCHS-N; (2) the CPI price list accounts for seasonal variation in the estimates such that the monthly estimates are possible to match to the month of 2015 CCHS-N completion; (3) the CNPP price list provides prices in “as purchased” quantities, whereas a limitation in the Canadian data is the lack of complementary metadata to be able to convert all food items in the 2015 CCHS-N to as purchased quantities; and perhaps most importantly, (4) the way in which food price data collection differs—the CNPP database uses Nielsen scanner data from a representative group of American shoppers with prices representing consumer purchases based on shopper sociodemographic characteristics whereas the CPI price list objectively measures prices from all Canadian retail outlets that report prices and weights the prices based on the number of units sold for each food product (note per the limitations below that the CPI program has now transitioned to use of scanner data) [62,63].

Further work with the CNPP database has aimed to match purchases with nutrient information to examine the dietary quality of the food supply [64]; a main limitation of this approach is an assumption that purchased quantities can stand as a proxy for consumed quantities within diets, when taking into account household characteristics. Despite this, based on the results of the present analysis, the CNPP may provide a more robust data source than the CPI for measuring dietary selection behaviors given that items in the CNPP are matched specifically to NHANES dietary intakes. Moreover, the relatively high product differentiation across foods groups in the CNPP database reduces the likelihood of misclassification, which may hinder our understanding of dietary choices in relation to diet costs.

Our analysis was a first attempt to quantify the different impacts of diet cost derivation methods on food groups that are commonly consumed by Canadians and are thereby important food prices to capture to reduce systematic error. Based on the 4 food groups of interest—additions, sweets, fruits, and vegetables—we found that the diet cost method did in fact impact price coverage differentially and consequently altered the diet cost estimate for that food group. With a constant dietary assessment tool used, the differing diet cost estimates may be enough to influence the noted association between diet costs and dietary adequacy. Future research should attempt to quantify how these different methods influence the relationship between diet costs and dietary adequacy and how relative changes in food prices over time may exacerbate or minimize these differences.

The results shed light on the importance of appropriate dietary assessment tools to capture diet costs and also the idea that greater dietary diversity may result in overall increased prices for foods and food groups. Our study may have also corroborated previous work that highlighted the spurious relationship between energy cost and energy density [[26], [27], [28]]. Prior to the introduction of the CPI national and provincial long lists, the national short list would have been the only source of diet costs. The national short list comprises energy-dense staple foods that are important commodities to track in the food supply [32]. Therefore, the national short list, along with other sources of food supply price lists that prioritize monitoring of energy-dense staple foods, may actually over-emphasize the costs of these foods as they occur in diets, with implications for the understanding of dietary choice behaviors at the population level. Overestimation of diet costs appear to be greater in the national short list because of the excess coverage of quadrant 1 foods relative to the diverse and more expensive foods that Canadians are consuming in quadrant 2. However, the national short list does include a greater variety of nonalcoholic beverages, which compose a high proportion of adult diet costs, and as such may be a driver of the relatively higher diet cost as computed using the national short list as compared to the national and provincial long lists.

Moreover, our analysis suggests that diet costs are sensitive to both the type and number of food prices within and between food groups. Ideally, food prices are collected at the same disaggregated level for each food item (or some equivalent food category like the BNS groups) reported in a dietary intake assessment tool. However, dietary intake data and food price data are often collected separately in national statistical surveillance programs, without a diet cost study in mind. Diet cost researchers should be attentive to how the type and number of food prices may differentially impact the diet cost estimate of food groups and overall diet and how those differences in diet cost may impact the relationship observed between diet cost and dietary intake. Critical appraisal as well as quantification and reporting of relative coverage of the food prices in relation to the dietary intakes of the population in the nutrition literature will assist in strengthening the reproducibility of diet cost analyses and support the development of methodology to ascertain how food price data sets may best be selected for varied research questions dealing with different populations of interest. For example, in the present analysis, although the national long list provided significantly better food price coverage overall than the national short list, the national short list provided the greatest coverage of nonalcoholic beverages, which comprised a large part of adult Canadians’ diets.

The 3 diet cost methods increased in the number of prices and/or geographic coverage of the prices from the national short list to the provincial long list. The national short list comprised the shortest list of food items at the most aggregated geographic level (i.e., nationally) as compared to the national long list (more food items) and the provincial long list (same food items as the national long list but with provincial pricing). Therefore, objectively, the provincial long list would provide the highest quality price estimate but also require the most resources to compute (i.e., the most prices possible at the highest level of geographic specificity), whereas the national short list would provide the lowest quality estimate but required the least number of resources. Based on our analysis, we concluded that the national long list provided the most appropriate diet cost estimate for a population-representative national analysis given that a longer list (objectively more accurate) provided a significant change in the diet cost estimate but provincially specific prices did not change the diet cost estimate.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first Canadian population-representative study to make use of national dietary intake data alongside national food price data to estimate diet costs and to compare and contrast diet cost methods to refine our methodological understanding of how the method of diet cost derivation affects the relationship between diet costs and dietary intakes. Unlike previous work, this analysis used consistent dietary assessments for the intake data to isolate the effects of diet costs. However, there are 7 main limitations.

First, our pricing strategy made use of the dietary intakes at the aggregated BNS food code level thereby reducing our availability to detect price differences within food codes. However, given the even greater aggregation of food prices available, it would not have been feasible to reliably match each individual food item reported in the 2015 CCHS-N to food prices. Second, we priced the foods consumed based on the dietary intake provided, which represents the quantity of foods consumed (not purchased). For certain foods and food groups, a larger quantity of that product is purchased than consumed due to refuse from waste or cooking (i.e., meat). However, we did not have access to any appropriate government metadata or data to transform consumed quantities to as-purchased quantities within the data sources used. Hence, these differences may bias the results of diet cost estimates. Third, this analysis assumes that all food items were purchased at retail locations because the prices reflect retail prices; this is also consistent with practice in most diet cost literature. The 2015 CCHS-N does not provide information on the location of food purchase, and the CPI price list only reflects the price of foods purchased at retail. Although in Canada, about 70 cents of every food dollar is typically spent at retail, the prices of foods purchased at nonretail locations may be different than those purchased at retail thereby biasing the diet cost estimate [65]. For example, one diet cost study among American adults aged ≥20 y found that although there was no significant difference between the mean per capita daily diet cost of foods consumed at home and away from home, foods consumed away from home had significantly lower diet quality as indicated by the 2015 Healthy Eating Index score [59]. This finding is contrary to the diet cost literature showing more nutritious diets are associated with higher diet costs [9]. Fourth, between the national short list and the other 2 diet cost methods, the government’s method of data collection changed from a combination of retail prices and scanner data to exclusively scanner data, which allowed for the collection of a larger variety of prices; however, the differences between scanner and in-person retail data are unknown. In addition, the package size of some standard products (i.e., orange juice) between the lists increased, which may have decreased the total cost of nonalcoholic beverages. Fifth, we used the CNF to convert products not measured in grams in the CPI price list to a dollars per gram value, which may have resulted in the over- or underestimation of the true cost of that food item. For example, the price of milk products in the CPI is based on dollars per liter. To convert the price to dollars per gram, we identified the food code of the most consumed (in grams) milk product in the CCHS-N nationally (for the national short and long list) or for each province (for the provincial long list) and identified the conversion factor in the CNF for that food code to convert the price from dollars per liter to dollars per gram for the diet cost computation. Sixth, we excluded a minority of food items with unavailable nutrient, gram weight, or energy data, which could have biased the diet cost estimate. For example, respondents may have reported intakes of a food item with available gram weight data (which would have been used to derive diet costs in this analysis) but might have been missing other nutrient data, and therefore that food item would have been excluded. Although the nutrient data were not used in this analysis, given the end goal of this work was to examine the relationship between diet costs and dietary intakes and therefore complete nutrient data, future analyses could examine the impact of excluded food items. Here, because the main goal of this analysis was comparing the 3 diet cost methods, and <10% of food items were removed, an imputation was not conducted. Future sensitivity analyses may be considered to understand if there was a difference in diet cost estimate as those foods with missing food items may systematically influence the diet cost estimation and differences in costs between the 3 methods compared. Finally, although data from a single 24-h dietary recall are adequate to estimate mean intakes, within-individual random errors may bias the results of the correlation analysis between energy intake and diet cost.

Conclusion

Our analysis demonstrates that the method of diet cost selected matters and is an important consideration when identifying and reducing bias in diet cost research. Overall, greater variety in prices decreased the diet cost estimate. Attention should be paid when conducting diet cost studies to ensure that food prices that are matched to dietary intake data appropriately capture the breadth of foods reported to minimize bias by food group and for the overall diet. In this way, we can refine the methodological underpinnings of diet cost estimation and more accurately examine how consumers might make food purchasing decisions.
