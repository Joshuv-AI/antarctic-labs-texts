Nonconforming gender expression and adolescent indoor tanning
Authors: Ruili Li, Ciren Zhuoma, Huihui Ren, Xiaowei Ren, Xiaozhen Li, Qiguo Lian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGender nonconformity (GNC) describes the degree of misalignment between individual’s gender expression and gender norms and is often linked to negative health outcomes. We aimed to investigate the association between GNC and adolescent indoor tanning, the most preventable cause of skin cancer, among U.S. high school students.MethodsThis study examined cross-sectional data from the 2017 state Youth Risk Behavior Survey in Maryland. Participants included 25,432 female and 25,060 male students in grades 9 through 12. The primary outcome was the prevalence of self-reported 12-month history of indoor tanning before survey. The GNC was measured by self-perceived gender expression (from “very feminine” to “very masculine”) and sex (“female” or “male”). The logistic regression analysis for weighted survey data was performed to estimate the association between GNC and adolescent indoor tanning.Results Among 50,492 high school students in Maryland, the prevalence of indoor tanning was 6.8(95% CI, 6.4-7.3), and varied by demographic characteristics and sexual orientation. Among male students, indoor tanning was significantly associated with moderate GNC (AOR, 1.55; 95% CI, 1.13-2.13; P=0.007) and high GNC (AOR, 4.92; 95% CI, 4.04-5.99; P<0.001). However, among female students, indoor tanning was not associated with moderate and high GNC in the adjusted models. Furthermore, indoor tanning was also significantly associated with transgender identity, the AOR was 7.16 (95% CI, 5.89-8.72; P<0.001) compared with cisgender female students, and 9.31(7.44-11.64; P<0.001) compared with cisgender male students.ConclusionsGender nonconforming adolescent males and transgender adolescents report higher risk of indoor tanning than gender conforming adolescent males and cisgender adolescents respectively. Skin cancer prevention efforts targeted at gender minorities might reduce indoor tanning, a preventable risk factor for skin cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25033-9.Keywords: Adolescent, Indoor tanning, Gender nonconformity, Epidemiology

Introduction

Skin cancer is the most common cancer and a major public health concern in the USA and worldwide. In the USA, during 2012–2015, 5.8 million American treated for any skin cancer annually at a cost of $8.0 billion, the annual number of patients and annual costs rose to 6.1 million and $8.9 billion during 2016–2018 respectively [1]. Although fair skin and family history of skin cancer are two important independent risk factors for skin cancer [2], intentional or intentional exposure to ultraviolet radiation, either from outdoor sun exposure [3] or indoor tanning [4], is the most preventable cause. Research estimated that for an American a substantial portion (25%) of the lifetime ultraviolet exposure occurs before age 18 years [5, 6].

In 2009, the World Health Organization officially classified artificial tanning devices as carcinogenic to humans [7], the US Food and Drug Administration in 2014 required that all indoor tanning devices must carry a warning label that people under 18 should not use them [8], and since 2012 many U.S. states have promulgated indoor tanning laws to prohibit minors from using commercial indoor tanning devices [9]. During the last decade, significant progress has been made in reducing indoor tanning among U.S. adolescents, the prevalence of indoor tanning has declined from 13.3% to 5.6% [10]. Despite continuing decline of skin cancer rates due to efforts in addressing skin cancer risk factors, the U.S. Surgeon General call for actions to reduce its risk, including identifying at-risk populations [11].

Because of body dissatisfaction, sexual minorities are more likely to use indoor tanning devices, which in turn, increases the probability of developing skin cancer [12, 13]. Gender expression (e.g., style of hair or dress, voice, or behavior) can shape human health risk behaviors [14] through interpersonal interactions. Gender nonconformity (GNC) describes the degree of misalignment between individual’s gender expression and gender norms [15]. The terminology associated with sex, gender, gender expression, gender identity, gender nonconformity, and sexual minority has been previously published in JAMA Pediatrics, 2018 [14]. GNC associated elevated social stress from prejudice and discrimination links to many negative health outcomes, including mental distress, substance use, body dissatisfaction and disordered eating [14, 16]. According to minority stress framework [17], adolescent indoor tanning could be an indirect coping response and/or a direct form of GNC.

While it is clear that sexual minority adolescents experience increased indoor tanning compared with heterosexual peers, no study as we know has assessed indoor tanning by GNC. To address the research gap, we extracted data from Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS) and examined the association between GNC and indoor tanning among U.S. adolescents independent of sexual orientation.

Methods

Survey

To monitor health risk behaviors among youth, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) developed school-based cross-sectional YRBS among representative samples of high school students in grades 9–12 [18]. Since 1991, national, state, and large urban school district YRBS have been conducted biennially. The survey is administered in the classroom during one class period. Students complete their responses directly on a computer-scannable questionnaire. It’s anonymous and voluntary to participate the survey for students, and local procedures are used to obtain parental permission. CDC’s Institutional Review Board approved the national YRBS, and state and school district local institutional review boards approved their respective YRBSs. Detailed information about the complete copy of the YRBS methodology has been described elsewhere [19].

We chose Maryland for our study because it is the only state with the most recent data (2017) on both GNC and indoor tanning. The overall response rate in Maryland in 2017 was 77% and unweighted sample size was 51,087 [18]. Of these participants, 595 (1.2%) were excluded from the analysis because of missing information about sex, resulting a sample size of 50,492.

Measures

Outcome

Student respondents were asked in 2017 YRBS, “During the past 12 months, how many times did you use an indoor tanning device such as a sunlamp, sunbed, or tanning booth? (Do not count getting a spray-on tan).” Response options were “0 times”, “1 or 2 times”, “3 to 9 times”, “10 to 19 times”, “20 to 39 times”, and “40 or more times”. Indoor tanning was defined as having ever used an indoor tanning device at least once during the last 12 months before survey.

Exposure

Using a validated measure within school contexts [20], YRBS accesses perceived gender expression with the following statement and question: [14, 21, 22] “A person’s appearance, style, dress, or the way they walk or talk may affect how people describe them. How do you think people at school would describe you?” Response options are a 7-point scale: “very feminine”, “mostly feminine”, “somewhat feminine”, “equally feminine and masculine”, “somewhat masculine”, “mostly masculine”, and “very masculine”.

Based on the responses to the perceived gender expression and sex (“What is your sex?”), we created a 7-point continuous GNC score to indicate the degree of nonconformity to gender norms, ranging from 1 = very gender conforming to 7 = very gender nonconforming. According to a previous study [14] on YRBS, we also generated a 3-level GNC: (1) low GNC (GNC score :1–3), (2) moderate GNC (GNC score:4), and (3) high GNC (GNC score :5–7).

YRBS assesses the transgender identity among high school students a single-item measure developed by CDC survey methodologists: [23] “Some people describe themselves as transgender when their sex at birth does not match the way they think or feel about their gender. Are you transgender?” Response options are “No, I am not transgender”, “Yes, I am transgender”, “I am not sure if I am transgender”; and “I do not know what this question is asking”. Based on the responses to the question and sex, we categorized the respondents into three groups: [23] (1) cisgender female students (female, not transgender), (2) cisgender male students (male, not transgender), and (3) transgender students. Students who responded with unsure or unknown were excluded from outcome comparisons.

Covariates

The control variables were sexual orientation and other demographic characteristics. YRBS assesses sexual orientation with the question: “Which of the following best describes you?” Response options were “heterosexual (straight)”, “lesbian or gay”, “bisexual”, and “not sure”. Demographic characteristics assessed included sex (female or male), race/ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic/Latino, or all other races), and grade (9, 10, 11, or 12).

Statistical analysis

This study had no prespecified statistical analysis plan to be followed through. We analyzed the data using a suite of svy commands in Stata/SE statistical software (version 15.1; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) to account for the complex sampling design of YRBS. The statistical significance cutoff threshold in our study was 0.05 (two-tailed).

The prevalence estimates with 95% confidential intervals (CIs) were calculated using Taylor series linearization. Differences in the distribution of GNC and indoor tanning by demographic characteristics and sexual orientation were tested using chi-squared statistics. Because both GNC and indoor tanning varies by demographic characteristics and sexual orientation and because association between GNC and indoor tanning tend to vary by sex, we estimated sex-specific association between GNC and indoor tanning, after adjusting for race/ethnicity, grade, and sexual orientation.

In initial analyses of association, we examined both linear and quadratic associations between GNC score (a continuous variable) and indoor tanning using logistic regression models for female and male students respectively. In subsequent categorical analyses, we calculated the sex-specific adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% CIs of the associations between GNC level (an ordinal variable) and indoor tanning using logistic regression models.

Besides, considering that transgender youths often share similar adverse experiences with cisgender peers with moderate or high levels of GNC [14], we also estimated the direction and strength of the association between transgender identity and indoor tanning.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Among the total population, the weighted percentage (unweighted number) of students was 49.0% (95% CI, 47.9%−50.0%) (n = 25,432) female and 51.0% (95% CI, 50.0%−52.1%) (n = 25,060) male. For sexual orientation, 82.5% (95% CI, 81.9%−83.1%) (n = 39,706) of students identified as heterosexual, 3.6% (95% CI, 3.4%−3.8%) (n = 1,635) identified as lesbian or gay, 8.9% (95% CI, 8.5%−9.4%) (n = 4,109) identified as bisexual, and 5.0% (95% CI, 4.7%−5.2%) (n = 2,214) answered “not sure” to the question. For transgender identity, 49.2% (95% CI, 48.1%−50.3%) (n = 22,867) identified as cisgender female, 47.6% (95% CI,46.5%−48.7%) (n = 21,197) identified as cisgender male, and 3.2(3.0–3.5.0.5) (n = 1,423) identified as transgender. Additional information of the demographic characteristics can be found in S1 Table.

Discussion

Consistent with previous research based on YRBS data [14], we observed about 1 in 5 high school students in Maryland, USA disclosed either moderate (10.1%) or high (10.1%) levels of GNC, and the prevalence of GNC level varied by sex, race/ethnicity, grade, and sexual orientation [14, 24, 25]. Of particular note, compared with heterosexual students, a much higher prevalence of high GNC was reported among sexual minority students (lesbian or gay students:46.5%; bisexual students:13.1%; heterosexual students,7.0%), which echoed findings from a study based on 3 large urban school districts in 2015 YRBS [14]. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine associations between GNC and indoor tanning independent of sexual orientation in a diverse, population-based sample of high school students.

We observed a low prevalence of indoor tanning (6.8%) among US high school students in 2017, which surpassed the cancer objective C-20.3 (14.0%) for adolescents in Healthy People 2020. There is a substantial reduction (64%) in adolescent indoor tanning, the national prevalence in YRBS was 15.6% in 2009, 13.3% in 2011, 12.8% in 2013, and 7.3% in 2015, and 5.6% in 2017 [10, 26]. The decline could be attributed to several reasons such as joint actions to increase awareness the dangers of indoor tanning [8, 27], and state laws of indoor tanning restrictions for minors [9]. Despite a national trend of decline in indoor tanning has been observed, an estimated 900,000 high school students were exposed to indoor tanning in 2017 in United States [10]. It is noteworthy that in our study male students were more likely to report the experience of indoor tanning, while exiting studies suggested that prevalence of past-year indoor tanning was higher in female students compared to male adolescents [28, 29]. The inconsistent findings suggested the heterogeneity in the adolescent indoor tanning across different states. Research gaps still remain, including better understanding of motivations for tanning among adolescents [11].

There are both similarities and differences in the associations between GNC and indoor tanning among female and male students. Indoor tanning demonstrated a nonlinear increase with GNC score among both female and male students. The highest prevalence of indoor tanning was found among very masculine female students and mostly feminine male students. The lowest prevalence was found among mostly feminine female students and mostly masculine male students. Previous research [14] showed inconsistent results when examining the effect of GNC on mental distress, adolescents in the middle of the GNC spectrum experience significant distress due to discrimination and stigmatization. Specifically, they found the greatest prevalence of sadness and hopelessness occurred near the middle of the GNC score, and highlighted unique challenges faced by students in the middle of the GNC score [14]. In contrast, association between GNC and indoor tanning also varied by sex. Indoor tanning demonstrated a linear increase with GNC score in male students (but not in females). In categorical analyses, indoor tanning was more likely to occur among male students with moderate and high levels of GNC than among male students with low GNC. Among female students, however, indoor tanning was not associated with GNC level.

Previous studies [12, 13] highlighted that skin cancer prevention programs need to incorporate sex, sexual orientation, and race/ethnicity because an increased odds of skin cancer and indoor tanning occurs in sexual minority males, particularly males of color. A prevailing theory behind the disproportionate health risks in sexual minorities is the sexual minority stress model [17], which suggests that increased mental distress caused by stigma and discrimination against sexual minorities can lead to health risk behaviors, including indoor tanning possibly [30]. The potential coping mechanism for the social and minority stress has been supported by studies on the disparities in health risk behaviors, including substance use [31] and indoor tanning [13, 32], among sexual minority adolescents and adults. The increased body dissatisfaction in sexual minorities [33] makes this subpopulation as a whole particularly vulnerable to indoor tanning, although there may be different social norms and collective values for appearance in different subpopulations of sexual minorities.

Although sexual orientation and GNC are correlated, no analogous research has been done among broader nonconforming adolescents. Similar to sexual minority adolescents, gender nonconforming adolescents also experience increased stigma and discrimination, and mental distress and multiple health risk behaviors compared with cisgender peers [14, 34]. If social stress plays a similar role regarding GNC on indoor tanning, our findings are consistent to existing research suggesting that gender nonconforming female adolescents were evaluated as more acceptable than gender nonconforming male peers in our societies [14, 25, 35]. Part of the reason for that may be in Western social norms that masculine and heteronormative traits are still valued more, although masculinity norms are changing. For example, the term ‘sissy’ (gender nonconforming boy) is often a socially stigmatizing label, whilst ‘tomboy’ (gender nonconforming girl) is more acceptable [35].

Also, given that transgender adolescents often share similar adverse experiences with gender nonconforming cisgender peers [14], it is important to investigate whether transgender identity and GNC show a similar association with indoor tanning. In our study, both transgender identity and GNC demonstrated similar association with indoor tanning. transgender students had up to a 6-fold increased odds of reporting indoor tanning than cisgender female students, and the risk rose to 8-fold higher compared with cisgender male students.

Implications

Our findings have important implications for public health, clinical care and research. In public health practice, our study highlights health disparities by GNC in indoor tanning, a well-known behavioral risk factor for skin cancer [36]. Also, it’s essential to develop safe and supportive school settings for transgender and gender nonconforming students to buffer stresses of their nonconforming gender expression [14].

In clinical dermatology settings, clinicians may assess gender expression in their patient interview to identify possible indoor tanners and adolescents at high risk for skin cancer. A careful assessment of indoor tanning and social stressors for all adolescent patients is necessary, especially when patient demonstrates GNC. Furthermore, although there is few study, so far, on clinicians’ attitudes on gender minorities, a study [37] highlighted that health care providers held significant negative attitudes toward sexual minorities. Hence, recognizing and eliminating bias and stigma in clinical practice are important to improve health disparities in gender minorities, as well as in sexual minorities [30].

In skin cancer research, our study indicates risk reduction in gender minority adolescents is an area worthy of research. Further research addressing gender expression, skin cancer, and indoor tanning is warranted to better understand the links gender expression, social stressors and indoor tanning in the sexual minority stress framework. Also, just as with sexual orientation [15], it’s essential to routinely assess gender expression and identity in epidemiological surveys.

Limitations

Our study is subject to some limitations. First, our findings based YRBS data apply only to adolescents who attend high school, and gender minority adolescents are more likely to drop out of school or do not attend school [38]. Second, our findings from Maryland YRBS may not generalize directly to adolescents in other states without considering the differences of gender norms and state indoor tanning laws [39]. Third, students might be reluctant to disclose a stigmatized gender expression (high GNC level for boys in particular), which could introduce reporting bias. Fourth, we did not have the information on skin type that may predict indoor tanning, we controlled race/ethnicity as a proxy indicator instead. Fourth, the binary GNC classification, based on our data distribution, may not generalize directly to other studies with non-YRBS datasets [14, 40]. Last, given that the data are cross-sectional in design, we were unable to explore the causal effect of GNC on indoor tanning but give only an indication of association.

Conclusions

We report an elevated odds of indoor tanning, the most preventable cause of skin cancer, among gender nonconforming male adolescents and transgender adolescents. Targeted efforts for skin cancer prevention focused on the social stressors of gender expression may help reduce indoor tanning among gender minority adolescents.
