Mesenchymal stem cell therapy for liver transplantation: clinical progress and immunomodulatory properties
Authors: Fuli Wen, Guokai Yang, Saihua Yu, Haiyan Liu, Naishun Liao, Zhengfang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough liver transplantation (LT) is an effective strategy for end-stage liver diseases, the shortage of donor organs and the immune rejection hinder its widespread implementation in clinical practice. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) transplantation offers a promising approach for patients undergoing liver transplantation due to their immune regulatory capabilities, hepatic protection properties, and multidirectional differentiation potential. In this review, we summarize the potential applications of MSCs transplantation in various LT scenarios. MSCs transplantation has demonstrated effectiveness in alleviating hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury, enhancing the viability of liver grafts, preventing acute graft-versus-host disease, and promoting liver regeneration in split LT therapy. We also discuss the clinical progress, and explore the immunomodulatory functions of MSCs in response to both adaptive and innate immune responses. Furthermore, we emphasize the interactions between MSCs and different immune cells, including T cells, B cells, plasma cells, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, Kupffer cells, and neutrophils, to provide new insights into the immunomodulatory properties of MSCs in adoptive cell therapy.Keywords: Liver transplantation, Mesenchymal stem cells, Immunoregulation, Cell therapy

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) is an effective life-saving procedure for multiple end-stage liver diseases, including acute liver failure, decompensated cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [1, 2]. Despite the rapid development of LT techniques, there are still many problems and challenges, including donor shortage, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and immune rejection [3]. Consequently, most end-stage liver disease patients exceed the transplantation criteria or die while waiting for LT allocation [4]. To overcome these obstacles, several alternative methods have been effectively used to extend the clinical application of LT. For instance, marginal LT can partly compensate for the shortage of liver donors, although the poor outcomes associated with marginal LT (e.g., primary non-function) severely limit its broad application [5]. To reduce oxidative stress and hepatocyte apoptosis in ischemia-reperfusion injuries [6, 7], several corresponding measures, including shortening ischemic time, cold preservation, and the use of antioxidants have been employed [8–10]. However, extraneous ions or residues (e.g., potassium) derived from the preservative solution (e.g.,UV solution) can severely cause cardiac arrest in LT patients. Although several immunosuppressants have been effectively used to alleviate the immune rejection throughout the entire process of LT [11–13], the unmanageable dosages of immunosuppressants due to individual differences often leads to excessive use, causing additional liver damage to liver transplant recipients [14]. Therefore, novel methods or drugs are still needed to improve LT efficiency and the prognosis of liver transplant recipients.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are fibroblast-like multipotent cells characterized by the positive expression of CD105, CD73, and CD90, while they show negative expression of CD45, CD34, CD11b, and HLA-DR. MSCs also have the ability to differentiate into mesodermal tissues, such as osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondrocytes [15]. In addition to their stem cell properties, MSCs have been also shown to process both anti-inflammatory and tissue regeneration capabilities [16]17. Recent findings have demonstrated that MSCs transplantation can be effectively used to alleviate liver ischemia-reperfusion injury and inflammation, thus improving the therapeutic outcomes of marginal LT therapy [18, 19]. Therefore, MSCs transplantation provides a novel strategy for enhancing LT prognosis.

In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of the potential applications of MSCs transplantation in various LT conditions and the corresponding clinical advancements. Considering the pivotal role of MSCs in immunoregulation during LT therapy, we also explore the distinct immunomodulatory characteristics of MSCs transplantation in relation to the adaptive and innate immune systems.

MSCs transplantation for different LT conditions

According to the extensive literature over the last 20 years, there are four main benefit functions of MSCs once transplanted into the recipients: (1) reducing hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury; (2) improving liver graft viability; (3) preventing graft-versus-host disease; (4) promoting liver graft regeneration.

MSCs alleviate hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury

Ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury is an inevitable process that may lead to allograft dysfunction or even graft failure in LT therapy [20]. MSCs transplantation has been utilized to alleviate hepatic IR injury, and has demonstrated certain therapeutic outcomes (Table 1). For example, Xu et al. found that human umbilical cord MSCs could slow down IR injury in fatty liver by activating autophagy through upregulation of interferon gamma (IFN-γ) [21]. MSCs alleviated hepatic IR injury by modulating phenotypic transformation of Kupffer cells through inhibiting Drp-1 dependent mitochondrial fission [22]. Zheng et al. reported that MSCs transplantation could alleviate hepatocellular apoptosis in a hepatic IR model via PINK1-dependent mitophagy [23]. Moreover, MSCs also play an anti-inflammatory role in alleviating hepatic IR injury by inhibiting inflammasome activation and neutrophil recruitment [24, 25]. Over the past several years, substantial evidence has shown that the hepatoprotective functions of MSCs are closely related to their paracrine function. In fact, various MSCs-derived products, including conditioned medium and extracellular vesicles (e.g., exosomes), have been widely utilized to alleviate the hepatic IR injury [26–31]. Therefore, the MSCs therapy could mitigate hepatic IR injury through their paracrine function.

MSCs improve the viability of liver grafts

Due to the donor shortages, marginal donors, including livers from donors after cardiac death (DCD), are commonly used for LT therapy. However, marginal liver grafts are susceptible to ischemia, and conventional preservation methods (e.g., static cold storage) are insufficient for preserving DCD livers, resulting in an increased likelihood of postoperative complications and poor prognosis in LT therapy [32]. Therefore, enhancing the viability of marginal liver grafts is the only way to improve the therapeutic effectiveness of DCD liver transplantation. To replicate physiologic conditions, the normothermic machine perfusion (NMP) system has been effectively utilized to restore the hepatic function of DCD livers during organ preservation [33]. In particular, it has been demonstrated that the combined use of MSCs and NMP system can improve liver graft viability (Table 2). For example, Yang et al. found that the liver function, hepatic microcirculation, and the quality of DCD livers could be significantly enhanced following the combined use of bone marrow-derived MSCs and NMP system [34]. Through heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1) modification, Cao et al. also reported that the combination of HO-1-modificed MSCs perfusion and NMP system could reduce acute rejection of DCD liver by targeting NKT cell co-inhibitory receptors during dynamic liver preservation [32]. Using a similar method in a swine model, Sasajima et al. also found that MSCs could improve the viability of DCD liver grafts [35]. Moreover, Tian et al. demonstrated that MSC ameliorated liver injury and enhanced survival in a non-heart-beating (NHB) LT model by suppressing Kupffer cell apoptosis via TLR4-ERK1/2-caspase3 pathway [36]. Although the current studies have confirmed the protective effect of MSCs alone or in combination on liver graft viability, there is still a lack of comparative studies between MSCs and NMP system, as well as an understanding of the potential mechanism by which MSCs combined with the NMP system improve liver graft viability. Furthermore, the types of MSCs (including the tissue derivation and gene modification) and the duration of NMP vary. Therefore, relevant studies should be thoroughly evaluated before clinical application or trials.

MSCs prevent acute graft-versus-host disease

Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) is a well-known immune-mediated complication that occurs after allogeneic graft (stem cell or solid organ) transplantation [37]. Although reported data indicate that the incidence of GVHD in LT therapy is relatively low (0.5-2%), the mortality rate exceeds 85% [38], and there is no effective method to prevent GVHD after LT treatment. Considering the immunomodulatory effects and efficacy of MSCs therapy for acute GVHD following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, Xia et al. developed a rat model to evaluate the therapeutic effect of MSCs therapy on acute GVHD after liver transplantation [39]. They found that MSCs could prolong the survival time of recipients when administered from day 0 to day 6 after LT surgery, while administration of MSCs transplantation from day 8 to day 14 after LT surgery proved ineffective. Therefore, MSCs therapy has the potential to prevent acute GVHD after LT treatment. Various immunosuppressants (e.g., cyclosporine A, tacrolimus, methylprednisolone, and cyclophosphamide) are clinically used to prevent acute GVHD in LT patients. However, there are currently no comparative studies examining MSCs versus immunosuppressants, nor their combination in the context of acute GVHD after LT therapy. As a result, the clinical translational potential of MSCs transplantation for acute GVHD in LT patients still requires further investigation.

MSCs promote liver regeneration in spilt LT therapy

In order to alleviate the shortage of donor livers, split LT has been clinically utilized to achieve transplantation of one donor liver into two recipients [40]. Liver regeneration following partial LT surgery, including living donor LT and split LT, is crucial for successful organ transplantation [41]. Importantly, MSCs have been shown to promote liver regeneration after hepatectomy [42, 43]. For example, Yu et al. found that MSCs significantly enhanced hepatocyte proliferation and inhibited hepatocyte apoptosis after 30% small-size liver transplantation (SFSLT) [44]. Wang et al. discovered that MSCs significantly increased the expression of autophagy related proteins LC1 and Beclin-1 in liver tissue and protected autophagy vacuoles from degradation in 50% SFSLT rat model through ERK/mTOR signaling pathway [45]. Utilizing MSC conditioned medium, Du et al. also found that MSCs could reduce neutrophil and Kupffer infiltration in liver tissues, and promote liver function recovery in 50% SFSLT rats [46], suggesting that the paracrine function of MSCs plays a significant role in enhancing liver regeneration of spilt liver grafts. Given the completely different conditions in recipients who undergo spilt LT therapy, it is challenging to effectively evaluate the therapeutic effects of the same donor liver grafts on different recipients, not to mention the beneficial role of MSCs transplantation for spilt LT therapy in clinical practice. Aside from hepatocyte regeneration, hepatic vessel regeneration or reconstruction is another important process for successful spilt LT therapy. Therefore, the therapeutic effects of MSCs on hepatic vessel regeneration following spilt LT therapy require further investigation.

The clinical application of MSCs transplantation for LT therapy

Based on the protective role of MSCs in different LT conditions mentioned above, MSCs transplantation has been widely utilized in clinical practice (Table 3). Specifically, MSCs transplantation has been shown to reduce acute immunological rejection and decrease the infection and biliary complications following LT surgery. More importantly, a growing number of clinical studies have observed no significant side effects or MSC-related complications [47, 49, 51–53], indicating the safety of MSCs transplantation for LT therapy. However, a few clinical trials have shown that MSCs transplantation is ineffective in LT therapy. Therefore, the clinical benefits of MSCS transplantation for LT require further comprehensive analysis and evaluation.

MSCs transplantation for acute immune rejection

Immunological rejection is a typical characteristic following LT treatment [54, 55], and it tends to occur a high incidence when the immunosuppressive drugs are insufficient due to the individual differences in drug dosage. Shi et al. found that the serum levels of ALT, AST, and TBIL, as well as histopathological levels in patients with acute rejection (n = 13), were significantly reduced after a single peripheral intravenous injection of 1 × 106 cells/kg UC-MSCs [48]. Importantly, the contents of Treg/CD4%, TGF-β1 and PGE-2 in peripheral blood were significantly increased following MSCs transplantation. A case report suggested that acute rejection occurred in a LT patient despite the administration of conventional dual immunosuppressants (tacrolimus and methylprednisolone), and it was not alleviated after introducing mortemycopherol ester and three courses of methylprednisolone shock therapy. However, after peripheral intravenous injection of 12 doses of 1 × 106 cells/kg UC-MSCs, serum biochemical indices (ALT, AST, TBIL, ALP, and GGT) and histopathological levels were all improved [53], indicating that UC-MSCs could reduce acute rejection caused by steroid resistance.

MSCs transplantation for LT biliary complications

Biliary duct blockade is typically performed during LT operation, which can lead to IR injury in biliary tissues, resulting in biliary complications following LT treatment [56]. A phase I/II randomized controlled trial (n = 12) conducted by Zhang et al. found that the 1-year survival rate of LT patients with biliary complications was increased (100% vs. 72.9%), and the serum levels of TBIL, γGT and ALP of, as well as the number of interventional treatments were all reduced (33.3% vs. 64.3%) after six sessions of MSCs transplantation (1 × 106 cells/kg) [50]. ABO-incompatible is another factor that can contribute to LT biliary complications. Another phase I/II randomized controlled trial (n = 11) conducted by Zhang et al. found that the incidence of LT biliary complications was significantly decreased (0% vs. 45.5%) after UC-MSCs transplantation (1 × 106 cells/kg, 8 times) [49]. Therefore, the current clinical data suggest that MSCs therapy could reduce incidence of LT biliary complications. However, more clinical studies are still needed due to the diversity and complexity of LT biliary complications.

MSCs transplantation for LT infections

LT infections are often caused by the excessive suppression of entire immune system due to the repeated administration of immunosuppressive drugs in LT patients [57]. Zhang et al. demonstrated that MSCs transplantation could significantly reduce the incidence of septic shock (9.1% vs. 81.8%) and pulmonary infections (0% vs. 72.7%) in a randomized controlled trial (n = 11) [49]. Detry et al. also found that the incidence of LT infections were effectively decreased through the central vein injection of BM-MSCs (n = 10, 2.1 × 106 cells/kg) [52]. In addition to the overuse of postoperative immunosuppressants, other factors such as intraoperative hemorrhage and the underlying primary disease in recipients also contribute to LT infections. However, relevant clinical studies have yet to be reported. Nonetheless, current clinical trials indicate that MSCs transplantation could reduce LT infections.

The negative outcomes and challenges of MSCs transplantation for clinical LT therapy

Although most clinical trials have shown that MSCs transplantation is an effective strategy to reduce acute immunological rejection, and to address infections and biliary complications in LT patients, several clinical trials have also found no benefit effects of MSCs infusion in LT patients, and it may even be detrimental to their prognosis. A randomized controlled trial (n = 10) conducted by Casiraghi et al. found that after a single infusion of BMSCs (0.9–1.67 × 106 cells/kg), there were no significant differences in inflammatory factors and the histopathology between the control and MSCs groups [47], indicating that of MSCs transplantation provides no benefit for LT patients. Vandermeulen et al. found that BMSCs transplantation (n = 10) not only failed to improve liver function, but also significantly increased the probability of HLA -Class II mismatch between donors and recipients (29.6% vs. 6.9%) [51], thereby increasing the risk of immune rejection. These negative outcomes may be attributed to the low sample size, differing practice guidelines in various regions, and the diverse backgrounds of initial liver diseases. Hence, the potential benefits of MSCs transplantation in LT therapy should be further validated through larger prospective studies. More importantly, several issues remain unresolved before further clinical application of MSCs in LT therapy, including the need for robust biomarkers or methods to predict clinical outcomes, the heterogeneity of MSCs derived from different tissues or passage levels, and the measurable method for evaluating cell engraftment. More attention is now being focused on their immunomodulatory properties due to the fact that the degree or level of the inflammation in vivo affects the therapeutic outcomes of MSCs transplantation in LT therapy. Therefore, we summarized the immunomodulatory properties of MSCs in different inflammatory states.

Immunomodulatory properties of MSCs

One of the most attractive aspects of MSCs transplantation for LT therapy is their unique immunomodulatory properties on both adaptive and innate immune systems [58, 59]. In particular, MSCs have been found to exert both anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory effects when interacting with immune cells, including T cells, B cells, plasma cells (PCs), natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells (DCs), Kupffer cells (KCs), and neutrophilis, through both direct cell-to-cell contact and paracrine activity [58]. Therefore, the divergent immunoregulation of MSCs should be fully considered and monitored during adoptive cell transplantation in LT therapy.

The interactions between MSCs and innate immune cells

NK cells

Within the innate immune systems, hepatic NK cells play a key role in liver diseases [60]. Qu et al. found that BMSCs inhibited NK cell accumulation and activation by downregulating sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor type 5 (S1PR5) on NK cells in a polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (PolyI: C)-induced liver injury model [61]. In response to the regulation of MSCs, NK cells can secrete CXCL7 (the ligand of CXCR2) to enhance the recruitment of MSCs [62], suggesting the crosstalk between NK cells and MSCs. MSCs have been shown to inhibit NK cell proliferation and their cytotoxic activity through the secretion of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and TGF-β [63–65]. On the contrary, when the innate immune resistance is insufficient, MSCs can enhance the anti-infective response of NK cells. Indeed, it has been demonstrated that MSCs promote IFN-γ secretion from IL-12/IL-18-stimulated NK cells, improving defense against infections at injured sites and promoting tissue regeneration in a CCR2-dependent manner [66, 67]. Kupffer cells

Kupffer cells (KCs) are elf-renewing and resident phagocytes in liver tissues, serving as sentinels for liver homeostasis [68]. Tian et al. found that MSCs could inhibit KC apoptosis to improve liver graft survival through the regulation of TLR4-ERK1/2-Fas/FasL-caspase3 pathway [36]. Liang et al. proved that MSCs transplantation reduced M1 polarization of KCs in sepsis-induced mice [69]. Moreover, MSCs-derived extracellular vesicles have been shown to promote M2 polarization of KCs in traumatic hemorrhagic shock mice [70]. You et al. further illustrated the paracrine PGE2 and TNF-α play a crucial role in KC polarization to promote immune tolerance in LT therapy [71]. Additionally, Yang et al. discovered that older recipients could recruit more MSCs to injury sites due to increased inflammation induced by hyperfunction KCs [72], suggesting that the personalized-MSCs therapy should be considered owing to the individual crosstalk between MSCs and KCs in different subjects (Fig. 2).

Neutrophilis

Given the fact that excessive neutrophilis infiltration or recruitment is a typical characteristic of liver injuries, the inhibition of neutrophilis infiltration or recruitment in liver tissues was a key role of MSCs and MSC-derived extracellular vesicles in alleviating sepsis [73] or liver injuries [25, 74, 75]. However, multiple in vitro studies have shown that both MSCs and MSC derivatives enhance the antimicrobial functions of neutrophils and inhibit their apoptosis [76–78]. This discrepancy between the effects of MSCs on neutrophils in vivo and in vitro suggests the immunoregulatory role of MSCs on neutrophils (Fig. 3).

Dendritic cells

It is widely acknowledged that immature dendritic cells induce immune tolerance, while mature dendritic cells induce acute rejection [79]. Heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1) expressed BMSCs (HO-1/BMSCs) have been shown to significantly reduce acute rejection in liver transplantation by inhibiting DC maturation via ERK1/2/MAPK and p38/MAPK signaling [79]. Park et al. found that MSCs could markedly inhibit the differentiation of mouse bone marrow cells into dendritic cells induced by GM-CSF, as well as suppress the maturation of dendritic cells stimulated by LPS [80, 81]. Importantly, the inhibitory effect of MSCs on dendritic cell maturation is closely associated with the secretion of PGE2, IDO, and TGF-β [81, 82]. Moreover, MSC-derived extracellular vesicles and exosomes were also found to inhibit dendritic cell maturation by suppressing the expression of IL-6, CD83, CD38, and CD80 [83, 84]. In addition, MSCs have also been shown to induce the conversion of mature dendritic cells into regulatory dendritic cells by targeting Notch signaling pathway [85]. The interactions between MSCs and adaptive immune cells

T cells

MSCs transplantation has demonstrated a significant therapeutic effect on acute rejection of LT, which is closely related to the expansion of regulatory T (T-reg) cells in vivo [86]. In vitro studies have also shown that MSCs can promote the conversion of T cells into T-reg cells [87], and the expression of Foxp3 in T-reg cells is dependent on CD80 [88]. Additionally, it has been found that MSCs can modulate the balance between Th17 and T-reg cells in rats [89]. Through direct cell co-culture, Zhang et al. discovered that MSCs could induce the expression of IL-10 and TGF-β in T-reg cells, facilitate the conversion of T cells into an immune tolerance phenotype, and enhance the ability of T-reg cells to suppress T cell proliferation [90]. Furthermore, Court et al. further demonstrated that the mitochondrial transfer from MSCs to T cells plays a critical role in the promoting of Treg T-reg cell expansion and differentiation [91]. B cells

Recently, it has been discovered that MSCs transplantation can inhibit the proliferation and cytokine production of intrahepatic B cells by regulating the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and nuclear factor kappa B (NFκB) signaling pathways [92]. Che et al. also demonstrated that UC-MSCs can inhibit B cell proliferation and differentiation, as well as reduce IgM and IgG production of B cells after co-culturing with MSCs [93]. Moreover, MSCs have been found to promote B cells to differentiate into regulatory B (B-reg) cells [94]. Li et al. observed that MSCs enhance the generation of CD5+/IL-10+ B-reg cells through the IDO pathway [95]. Peng et al. also showed that MSCs increase the IL-10 production of CD5+ B-reg cells to help improve refractory chronic graft versus host disease [96]. Additionally, Chen et al. confirmed that MSCs enhance the differentiation of B cells into CD23+/CD43+ B-reg cells in intestinal inflammation [97]. Furthermore, MSCs were shown to inhibit plasma cell differentiation and antibody production when peripheral blood lymphocytes were stimulated with CpG [98]. The immunosuppression of MSCs requires inflammation

Previously, several clinical and basic studies have indicated that MSCs transplantation did not improve liver function during LT therapy, particularly when administered in conjunction with immunosuppressant treatment [47, 51, 99]. This prompts us to reveal the potential mechanism of the anti-inflammatory response and to seek novel strategies for enhancing the immunosuppression of MSCs transplantation for LT. It was demonstrated that the immunosuppression of MSCs on T cells was depend on IFN-γ and its receptor. Indeed, Ren et al. found that the secretion of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and the therapeutic outcome of MSCs transplantation for acute GVHD was ineffective when the IFN-γ receptor was deficient or blocked by its antibody [100]. Importantly, the IFN-γ priming has been effectively used to enhance the immunosuppression of MSCs [101–103]. Apart from IFN-γ, the priming of other pro-inflammatory factor including TNF-α, IL-1/β, IL-4, IL-6, IL-15, TLR3 and IL-17 have also been used to improve immunosuppression of MSCs [104–106]. Together, the blockade of pro-inflammatory factor receptors and the pro-inflammatory factor-primed approach indicate that inflammation plays a significant role in triggering the immunosuppressive response of MSCs.

Given the crucial role of immunoregulation in MSCs transplantation for LT, it is essential to consider the immunomodulatory factors secreted by MSCs, such as iNOS, IDO, PGE2, TWIST1, and TSG6. Various preclinical studies have utilized PGE2 [107], TWIST1 [108], TSG6 [108], and IDO [109] to assess the immunomodulatory potential of MSCs. Therefore, monitoring the levels of these immunomodulatory factors is crucial for a comprehensive evaluation of the effectiveness of MSCs transplantation in LT.

Conclusions and future directions

MSCs transplantation has emerged as a potential strategy for LT therapy due to their effectiveness in alleviating hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury, improving graft viability, preventing acute GVHD, and promoting liver regeneration, as well as their immunomodulatory functions. Current studies have indicated that the clinical application of MSCs in LT therapy is safe and does not lead to serious adverse events after adoptive cell transplantation. Nevertheless, the degree or level of inflammation in vivo directly impacts the immunomodulatory function of MSCs, resulting in varied therapeutic outcomes following MSCs transplantation in LT therapy. Notably, the immunosuppression provided by MSCs requires the elicitation of inflammation. Thus, a thorough evaluation of the immunomodulatory functions of MSCs is necessary prior to their further application of MSCs in LT therapy.

However, there are several unresolved issues that need to be addressed. Firstly, there is still a lack large-scale, multi-center clinical trials to confirm the therapeutic effects of MSCs transplantation on LT and to explore the immunomodulatory functions of MSCs in predicting their therapeutic efficiency. The heterogeneity in derivation of MSCs (such as tissue source, gender, age, etc.) leads to varying responses to inflammation, making it difficult to identify potential factors that affect the immunomodulatory functions of MSCs. Another challenge in this field is the significant discrepancies between the animal data and clinical data, as well as inconsistencies between in vitro and in vivo data. More importantly, the cell engraftment efficiency of MSCs transplantation (e.g., peripheral, portal vein injection) still needs further improvement [110], and related clinical trials are lacking. Among these challenges, the low cell engraftment efficiency poses a critical obstacle for MSCs to fully exert their immunomodulatory function in LT therapy. Enhancing immunomodulatory function and engraftment efficiency, coupled with rigorous clinical trials, will enable MSCs to offer a novel promising strategy for LT therapy.
