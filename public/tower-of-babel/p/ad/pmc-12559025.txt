Association of Carbohydrate Antigen 125 Levels and Right Ventricular Dysfunction in Patients With Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Leila Bigdelu, Amir Mohammad Hashem Asnaashari, Samira Seifi, Reza Basiri, Vafa Baradaran Rahimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients with right ventricular (RV) dysfunction have poor prognoses, and early detection of RV dysfunction is beneficial. Therefore, we aimed to determine the association between CA125 levels and RV dysfunction in patients with COPD.MethodsWe enrolled 40 patients with stable COPD underwent echocardiography and the serum CA125 levels were measured.ResultsThe mean CA125 level was higher in COPD patients with RV dysfunction (17.28 ± 27.35 U/mL) than without (10.94 ± 10.62 U/mL); however, it was not statistically significant (p = 0.147). Additionally, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion and pulmonary arterial pressure were not significantly different, while RV free wall strain (RVFWS) was decreased in the COPD patients with RV dysfunction when compared to the patients without RV dysfunction (p < 0.001). Moreover, the CA125 level was remarkably correlated with RVFWS (r = −0.497, p = 0.03) and FAC (r = 0.51, p = 0.025) in patients with RV dysfunction.ConclusionWe found that the CA125 level may not be beneficial in detecting RV dysfunction in stable COPD patients without exacerbation or hospitalization. However, further examinations with larger sample sizes are necessary to confirm our findings.Keywords: CA125, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, echocardiography, right ventricular dysfunction

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), a high‐prevalence, progressive, and inflammatory disorder, is identified as the third major reason for death globally [1]. COPD is associated with respiratory symptoms, airflow limitations, and frequent exacerbation episodes. Eventually, COPD patients are highly susceptible to developing right ventricular (RV) failure, pulmonary hypertension, and cor pulmonale, which may deteriorate its prognosis [2].

Transthoracic echocardiography is a well‐known method for diagnosing heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, and cor pulmonale in patients suffering from COPD [3, 4]. However, COPD patients have poor echo windows that may lead to false‐negative results. Therefore, investigating new markers to detect these comorbidities more easily, with lower costs, and without the need for echocardiography may be beneficial [5, 6].

Several tumor markers are extensively used to screen, diagnose, and monitor cancers; however, their role in recognizing other disorders is under evaluation. Carbohydrate Antigen 125 (CA125) is a high molecular weight glycoprotein usually synthesized in ovarian and endometrial cells and used to detect ovarian cancer [7, 8]. Recently, it has been emphasized that CA125 is stimulated in heart failure and suggested as a beneficial marker for diagnosing heart failure [9]. Previous investigations also emphesized that CA125 level is associated with the COPD severity [10].

Therefore, in the present examination, we aimed to determine the CA125 levels and echocardiographic parameters in patients with COPD with and without RV dysfunction.

Methods

Ethics Approval

The Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences approved the present investigation (confirmation code: IR.MUMS.fm.REC.1394.595). Additionally, all recruited patients received and signed written informed consent.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Patients over 60 years old with confirmed COPD according to respiratory‐function tests according to the criteria of the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) enrolled in the study [11]. The exclusion criteria were patients with COPD exacerbation or hospitalization during the last 3 months, nonmucinous ovarian cancer with high CA125 levels, the first trimester of pregnancy, pancreatitis, severe hepatic cirrhosis, severe hepatic necrosis, and hepatic or pancreatic or breast cancers or uterine leiomyomas.

Evaluation of Outcome

Demographic characteristics namely age, body mass index (BMI), gender, as well as duration of COPD were recorded. In addition, forced expiratory volume (FEV1), FEV1 predicted value, GOLD criteria category, and 6 min walk test (6MWT) results were also documented.

The COPD assessment test (CAT) questionare was also measured. CAT is a self‐administered questionnaire which assessed the quality of life and health status of patients suffering from COPD. It consists of eight questions scoring from 0 to 5 and the total score from 0 to 40. It determine the severity of cough, chest tightness, sputum, dyspnea, confidence, energy, activities, and sleep [12].

Echocardiography

All patients underwent transthoracic echocardiography using a Siemens ACUSON SC2000 by velocity vector imaging (VVI) software Syngo Workplace. One echocardiography specialist conducted all echocardiography. Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), velocity of the tricuspid annulus (S′), RV free wall strain (RVFWS), pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP), and fractional area change (FAC) were recorded. In addition, patients with FAC lower than 35% were considered COPD patients with RV dysfunction. Patients with FAC higher than 35% were assigned to COPD patients without RV dysfunction [13].

FAC was reckoned using the difference in RV end‐diastolic area and RV end‐systolic area divided by the RV end‐diastolic area obtained from the apical four‐chamber view. TAPSE was considered as the spacing between the RV end‐diastolic and end‐systolic at the tricuspid annulus from apical four‐chamber view. FAC and TAPSE values lower than 35% and 17 mm were considered abnormal. S′ velocity was measured using tissue doppler imaging along with noticing to underestimation of velocity. In this regard, we kept the annulus and the basal segment in line with the cursor of Doppler (Figure 1).

Velocity vector imaging of the right ventricular endocardium of a patient with mitral stenosis. (A) RV free wall strain (RVFWS), (B) velocity of the tricuspid annulus (S′), and (C) fractional area change (FAC).

Additionally, strain was estimated using Syngo Workplace software and offline analysis of recorded images. The endocardial and epicardial RV borders were manually delineated to make sure that the whole myocardial wall was covered. The software automatically traced and calculated the RVFWS for each patient (Figure 1). It has been suggested that RVFWS more than −19% is abnormal [14].

The longitudinal function of RV was evaluated by S′, RVFWS, and TAPSE and the the global RV function was determined using FAC.

CA125 Measurement

The serum levels of CA125 were measured in all patients using a chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay. The normal range of CA125 was considered < 35 U/mL, and the high CA125 levels were > 35 U/mL [15].

Sample Size Estimation

Based on a previous study, the sensitivity for CA125 in diagnosing heart failure in COPD patients was 0.86 [16]. According to the following formula (which p = 0.85, q = 0.15, z = 1.64, a = 0.1), the required sample size was 34. Considering the possible dropout rate of 15%, we enrolled 40 patients in our study.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS statistical program version 22 was utilized in order to data analysis (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois). Moreover, figures were drawn using GraphPad Prism 8.01 software (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). Parametric and nonparametric variables were reported using means and standard deviations (SD) and number (n) with a percentage (%), respectively. Moreover, the comparison between continuous parametric data was performed using independent t‐test. While, the Mann–Whitney U test was performed to compare nonparametric data. Finally, the difference between categorical variables was evaluated using χ 2 or Fisher's exact test. Additionally, correlation between continuous data was investigated using the Pearson for normally and the Spearman correlation coefficients for non‐normally distributed data. In the present study, the significance value was considered p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

We evaluated 40 patients suffering from COPD, including 32 (80%) males, and 8 (20%) females, with a mean age of 59.65 ± 8.63 years old. We found no remarkable differences in age (p = 0.801), gender (p = 0.408), and BMI (p = 0.27) between the two examinated groups. The FEV1 in COPD patients with RV dysfunction was lower than in patients without RV dysfunction, however, it was not statistically significant (p = 0.059). Furthermore, RV dysfunction group also had lower values of the predicted FEV1 value (p < 0.001) and 6MWT (p = 0.008) when comparing to the without RV dysfunction patients (represented in Table 1).

GOLD Criteria and CAT Test

We showed that 57% of the COPD patients without RV dysfunction possessed GOLD 1 and 43% had GOLD 2. In COPD patients with RV dysfunction, 31.5% had GOLD 1, 47.4% had GOLD 2, and 21.1% had GOLD 3 (Figure 2A).

The CAT score was notably greater in COPD patients with RV dysfunction (30.0 (11.0)) than COPD patients without RV dysfunction (6.5 (13.75), p < 0.001, Figure 2B).

CA125 Levels

The total mean CA125 level was 17.28 ± 27.35 U/mL, and the CA125 level in the COPD patients without RV dysfunction was 10.94 ± 10.62 U/mL and 17.28 ± 27.35 U/mL in the COPD patients with RV dysfunction (Figure 3A). No remarkable difference was found in the CA125 levels between the two studied groups (p = 0.147, Figure 3A).

(A) The CA125 levels and (B) normal and high CA125 levels in patients with COPD with and without right ventricular dysfunction. COPD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Data were presented as mean ± SD.

Additionally, 36 patients (90%) had normal CA125 levels, and 4 patients (10%) had high CA125 levels in the total population (Figure 3B). Moreover, 95% of patients without and 85% of patients with RV dysfunction had normal CA125 levels (Figure 3). Our results revealed no meaningfull differences in normal and high CA125 levels between the two studied groups (p = 0.605, Figure 3B).

Discussion

COPD is considered a progressive, inflammatory, and also debilitating lung disease manifested with obstructed airflow and respiratory symptoms [17, 18]. The risk of developing heart dysfunction, such as RV failure, is extensively greater in COPD patients. Additionally, COPD patients with RV dysfunction have a poor prognosis, and early detection may propagate survival and quality of life [19, 20].

Our investigation evaluated the CA125 levels and echocardiographic variables in COPD patients with and without RV dysfunction. We showed that the mean CA125 level was greater in COPD patients with RV dysfunction than in COPD patients without RV dysfunction; however, it was not statistically significant.

Yilmaz and colleagues demonstrated that the CA125 level was higher in hospitalized severe COPD patients with more than 10 years than in the normal group. Furthermore, the CA125 level in hospitalized severe COPD patients was independently associated with RV failure [16]. Similarly, Harshvardhan and colleagues noticed that the CA125 level was higher in moderate to severe COPD patients with RV failure than in normal RV COPD patients. The duration of COPD disease was also higher in patients with high CA125 than the normal CA125 levels [21]. Another study also supported that the CA125 level was higher in mild COPD patients living at high altitudes than in the healthy group [22]. The results of these examinations are contrary to our findings. However, we evaluated COPD patients without exacerbation or hospitalization and with good general and euvolemic conditions, which might be the reason for this contrast.

The longitudinal function of RV, which is evaluated by RVFWS and TAPSE, is less affected in patients suffering from COPD [23]. Therefore, in the present work, we used FAC in order to determination of RV dysfunction in COPD patients. In addition, echocardiography will specify mild RV dysfunction in these patients. Noteworthy, the mean FAC in COPD patients with RV failure was 27.53 ± 6.7, which is not a severe dysfunction [24]. Taken together, these reasons may be accountable for the CA125 levels in our investigation.

Rahimi‐Rad and colleagues demonstrated that the COPD patients with pulmonary hypertension had a more remarkable CA125 level when compared to the COPD patients without pulmonary hypertension. They also suggested the CA125 level as a biomarker for diagnosing COPD patients with pulmonary hypertension [25].

We realized that COPD patients with RV dysfunction had more high CA125 levels than COPD patients without RV dysfunction. However, the two studied groups observed no significant differences between normal and high CA125 levels. In accordance with our results, Barouchos and colleagues evaluated 52 patients with exacerbations of COPD with groups C and D of COPD severity. They supported that the CA125 level was not enhanced in the COPD patients with group C of severity (14.1 ± 5.4). It only increased in the patients with group D of COPD severity (53.8 ± 4.6) [26]. Similarly, another study also demonstrated that the CA125 level was notably propagated in very severe COPD patients when compared to moderate and severe COPD patients [27]. We excluded COPD patients with COPD exacerbation or hospitalization and only investigated COPD patients with good general and euvolemic conditions, which may be the reason for normal CA125 levels in our patients.

In echocardiographic evaluation, the TAPSE, strain, and PAP were not strikingly different between the two evaluated patients. In fact, TAPSE and S′ are longitudinal echocardiographic markers that are less affected by disorders due to increased pulmonary artery pressure. In contrast, COPD patients with RV dysfunction had reduced levels of FAC and RVFWS than in the COPD patients without RV dysfunction. Moreover, the CA125 level was firmly associated with RVFWS (r = −0.497, p = 0.03) in COPD patients with RV dysfunction. Yilmaz and colleagues reported that CA125 amounts were markedly associated with the forced expiratory volume in 1 s to forced vital capacity ratio [FEV1/FVC (r = −0.466, p = 0.002)], PAP (r = 0.550, p < 0.001), TAPSE (r = −0.496, p = 0.001), and RVFWS (r = −0.549, p = 0.002) in hospitalized severe COPD patients [16].

There are some limitations for the RVFWS assessment. Indeed, strain analysis may suffer from a number of technical limitations, such as the inter‐vendor variability, the dependency on the operator's experience, the quality of echocardiographic images, the frame rate setting, the loading conditions, and, finally, extrinsic mechanical factors, particularly anterior chest wall deformity [28, 29, 30]. Recent evidence indicates that RV mechanics may be impaired due to anterior chest wall deformity and/or sternal depression, even in the absence of any intrinsic myocardial dysfunction [30].

The present study also had some limitations. First, we evaluated a small number of patients and a single‐center study. In fact, we had a limited selection for included patients and the number of patients with the conditions we were looking for was small. Additionally, CA125 test was very expensive for us and we cannot afford more number of investigations. In fact, we want to have a new view to this association and it could be a new pioneer for further investigations. A larger sample size would be necessary to confirm or refuse this association and would provide a more robust basis for drawing conclusions. In the present study, we wanted to evaluate if CA125 marker is responsive in patients with less severe disease. Indeed, patients with recent exacerbations or hospitalizations have higher CA125 levels. We also excluded patients with other reasons that leads to increase the CA125 levels because these comorbidities may interfere with the raise of CA125 due to COPD. We only wanted to distinguish the CA125 level in stable and low‐risk COPD patients. However, future studies should also consider including patients with recent exacerbations or hospitalizations to better understand the role of CA125 in different clinical scenarios.

Conclusion

Although COPD patients with RV dysfunction had greater mean CA125 level when comparing patients without RV dysfunction, it was not statistically significant. Therefore, we concluded that the CA125 level might not be beneficial in detecting RV dysfunction in stable COPD patients without exacerbation or hospitalization. However, further investigations with larger sample sizes are needed to specify the role of CA125 levels in COPD patients with and without RV dysfunction.
