Bedside muscle ultrasonography to detect fasciculations and support diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis in a mechanically ventilated intensive care unit patient
Authors: Hiroyuki Kida, Taro Kanai, Masayo Morita, Takashi Hasegawa
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Diagnosing amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) in patients who require mechanical ventilation can be challenging, because standard neurophysiological testing may be logistically difficult and susceptible to electrical artifacts in the intensive care unit (ICU). We report a short case in which bedside muscle ultrasonography detected widespread fasciculations and supported timely escalation to definitive testing.

A 77-year-old man presented with impaired consciousness and severe hypercapnic respiratory failure (PaCO2 118 mm Hg) and was intubated and admitted to the ICU. Brain and chest computed tomography were unremarkable. After correction of severe hyponatremia, his consciousness improved. At the first extubation attempt, the patient was receiving pressure support of 5 cm H2O and positive end-expiratory pressure of 5 cm H2O. Extubation failed after about 4 hours because hypercapnia (PaCO2 72 mm Hg) and impaired consciousness recurred, necessitating reintubation. Over the preceding 2 months, he had developed progressive generalized weakness, marked muscle wasting, anorexia, and dyspnea without sensory symptoms. Neurological examination revealed prominent weakness and atrophy involving the face, neck, trunk, and limbs (left-predominant). Tendon reflexes were preserved despite atrophy, the snout reflex was positive, and abdominal reflexes were absent. Fasciculations were suspected visually in both deltoid and vastus medialis muscles but were not consistently reproducible. The subacute course of progressive weakness without sensory deficits together with unexplained hypercapnic respiratory failure raised suspicion for ALS.

Nerve conduction studies (NCS) and needle electromyography (nEMG) were considered necessary but were initially deferred because transporting a ventilated patient was high risk, and bedside testing was expected to be limited by ICU electrical noise. Therefore, bedside muscle ultrasonography was performed as a screening assessment. Bedside muscle ultrasonography was performed using Vivid S70N system (GE Healthcare) with a linear-array transducer (4.5–12.0 MHz). Gain and depth were adjusted at each site to optimize visualization of subtle spontaneous muscle movements. Each muscle was observed for approximately 10 seconds according to the previous research [1]. Irregular spontaneous twitches compatible with fasciculations diffusely across the muscle over ten times per 10 seconds were observed in the trapezius, deltoid, biceps brachii, first dorsal interosseous, vastus medialis, and tibialis anterior (Supplementary Video 1). Echogenicity was also mildly increased (Heckmatt grade 2) in most examined muscles. NCS and nEMG were subsequently performed and demonstrated widespread active and chronic neurogenic changes without sensory involvement, fulfilling the Awaji criteria and the Gold Coast criteria for definite ALS. Although diffuse fasciculations are not specific to ALS, several features argued against ICU-acquired weakness in this patient, including the 2-month pre-ICU progression of weakness and dyspnea, the absence of sensory symptoms or signs detected by NCS, and upper motor neuron signs.

The distinctive aspect of this case is that it provided immediate bedside evidence of diffuse lower motor neuron involvement in a mechanically ventilated ICU patient in whom electrodiagnostic testing was logistically difficult, thereby supporting prioritized definitive testing. Muscle ultrasonography can detect fasciculations more sensitively than visual inspection and has been shown to increase diagnostic sensitivity in ALS [2-5]. In ventilated ICU patients, ultrasonography can be performed rapidly at the bedside, does not require extensive repositioning, and is not affected by electrical artifacts from ICU devices. In our case, ultrasonography provided actionable evidence of widespread lower motor neuron involvement and supported the decision to pursue neurophysiological testing despite practical barriers.

In summary, bedside muscle ultrasonography may be a safe, practical, and artifact-free adjunct in ventilated ICU patients with otherwise unexplained hypercapnic respiratory failure and progressive weakness, helping clinicians prioritize definitive neurophysiological testing for timely diagnosis of ALS.

Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient. Ethical approval was not required for this case report.
