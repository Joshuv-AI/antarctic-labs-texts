Exploration and comparative visualization of metabolic networks in GEMs with MMINT
Authors: Juan P Molina Ortiz, Matthew J Morgan, Amy M Paten, Andrew C Warden, Philip Kilby
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryGenome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) are essential tools in systems and engineering biology, enabling the simulation of genome-encoded pathways, predicting metabolic fluxes and emergent phenotypes. GEM network visualization tools can facilitate the interpretation of the predictions derived from GEMs. Here, we present metabolic modeling interactive network tool (MMINT), a tool specifically designed for the exploration and comparison of GEM flux distribution networks. MMINT enables interactive toggling of source and target metabolites and reactions and highlights similarities and differences between sets of predicted fluxes. We demonstrate MMINT’s capabilities using the Pyrococcus furiosus GEM iPfu, showcasing its application in distinguishing the metabolic drivers of acetate- and ethanol-producing phenotypes. By providing an intuitive and responsive model exploration experience, MMINT addresses the need for a tool that simplifies the interpretation of GEM outputs and supports the identification of metabolic drivers of phenotypes.Subject areas: human metabolism, bioinformatics

Introduction

Genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) are instrumental tools for exploring and manipulating cellular metabolism, linking genes, proteins, and reactions through stoichiometric networks to predict metabolic phenotypes.1,2,3 Through the application of constraint-based analysis (CBA), such as flux balance analysis (FBA),4 GEMs can elucidate metabolic network adaptations in different cell types, organisms, and microbial communities under different environmental or genetic variations.5,6,7 By tracing flux distributions through a given metabolic network, GEM-based analyses can identify metabolic mechanisms underlying emergent phenotypes.5,8 The complexity and mathematical format of GEMs, however, can make interpretation and comparison of outputs challenging and often limit the mechanistic understanding GEMs strive to provide.

The rapid advancement in GEM-building pipelines9,10,11,12,13,14 and the availability of online databases with reliable reconstructions15,16,17,18,19 have enabled the application of these models across numerous research areas, including enzyme engineering, human health, and microbial ecology.1,6,7,20,21,22,23 Underpinning their utility is the ability to explore and compare flux distributions. For example, Schafer et al.19 compared metabolic flux distributions to explore pairwise interactions in plant leaf microbiota as a framework for informing the construction of synthetic microbial communities according to substrate. Therefore, there is a need for tools that can rapidly assess and compare outputs from automatically inferred GEMs or models built by third parties, enabling researchers to determine their suitability for downstream applications. This underscores the need for a tool specifically designed for model and flux exploration and metabolic phenotype comparison.

Visualization tools can be used to support the flux tracing process, addressing the complexity of interpreting GEM predictions and enabling comparison and examination of flux distributions. Yet, currently available platforms rely on preexisting, GEM-specific metabolic maps24,25,26 or on manual preselection of metabolites and reactions27,28 for them to be displayed as a network. Such prerequisites demand a comprehensive understanding of the network encoded in a GEM prior to visualization, which restricts discovery of novel mechanisms. Further, these tools focus mostly on individual CBA solution visualization without offering an intuitive and direct comparison of alternative flux distributions, which can guide the identification of the mechanisms that drive the emergence of divergent phenotypes.

To address these limitations, we introduce MMINT (metabolic modeling interactive network tool), a tool specifically designed for the exploration and comparison of bipartite (reaction- and metabolite-based) metabolic networks. MMINT leverages single-organism GEM networks (eukaryotic or prokaryotic), which can be combined with alternative CBA solutions, to create an interactive representation of flux distributions through the network. MMINT uniquely facilitates immediate comparative visualization of flux distributions when two solutions are provided, automatically highlighting their differences and similarities. Further, MMINT enables rapid and intuitive network exploration without prior network familiarity through network “decluttering” functions, including seamless toggling of source and target metabolites, delivering immediate visual feedback and enabling users to focus on specific pathways of interest. In doing so, these features further facilitate the identification of mechanistic drivers of phenotypes.

In this paper, we detail MMINT’s functionalities and applications using a preexisting GEM of Pyrococcus furiosus (iPfu),5 a thermophile archaeon and metabolic engineering platform. By comparing the predicted flux distributions of two engineering strategies derived from iPfu, we show how MMINT can contribute to model exploration and to the discovery of metabolic phenotypic drivers. The potential impact of MMINT extends beyond this example. By enabling the interactive visualization of metabolic flux and interrogation of the quality of newly built or third-party GEMs, MMINT can become an indispensable tool for researchers aiming to gain deeper insights into metabolic networks and their applications in synthetic biology and microbial ecology.

Results

Software and computational resources

The MMINT system is written in Java, using OpenJDK 22, and runs in a single thread on a local computer. MMINT requires the Java Run Time Environment (JavaRTE), which is available for all major operating systems. It also relies on the “dot” executable from the GraphViz package.29 GraphViz is an open-source graph visualization package, available for all major operating systems. The dot program is used for automatic graph layout to arrange edges (flux) and nodes (reactions and metabolites). The analyses and networks presented in this manuscript were performed on a Precision 7780 system with a 13th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i9-13950HX, 2,200 MHz, 24 Core(s), 32 Logical Processor. For reference, loading the large Yeast9 GEM (>4,000 reactions), a consensus GEM for S. cerevisiae S288C,30 and the extra-large GEM Human-GEM31 (>12,000 reactions) on the same processor takes <1.0 s of compute time. A supplementary visualization of the Human-GEM model (Figure S4) further demonstrates MMINT’s capability to handle large-scale GEMs. Based on these measurements, we have limited MMINT’s calculation time to 1 min, by default, to prevent performance issues on less powerful systems. However, this parameter can be extended through MMINT’s configuration file. For more details, refer to the README file in MMINT’s BitBucket repository: https://bitbucket.csiro.au/projects/MMINT/repos/mmint/browse.

Input data and storage

MMINT provides an intuitive interface and flexible input structure. It requires two types of inputs: a GEM, describing reactions stoichiometries and gene-protein-reaction associations in XML/SBML format and up to two CBA solution files. Solution files record the flux distribution as determined by CBAs and should include reaction identifiers in the first field/column and the corresponding flux in the second. Compatible formats include CSV, TSV, XLSX and TXT files. Importantly, FBA variations, such as parsimonious FBA (pFBA), geometric FBA, and loopless FBA, are fully compatible with MMINT. MMINT can also accept full flux distributions derived from flux variability analysis (FVA), e.g., using minimum and maximum metabolite production ranges as constraints to produce individual flux solutions, which can then be compared in MMINT.

All data are kept locally. Data privacy is ensured, as no data are sent to, or read from, remote servers. Once installed, MMINT can be used without access to the internet.

Design and network visualization

The MMINT software is composed of two interactive frames or “windows.” The control frame handles file loading and lists metabolites and reactions in a GEM and CBA solution. Meanwhile, the graph frame displays a graphic representation of the fluxes (edges) in up to two CBA solutions using nodes that represent metabolites and reactions in a GEM. Reactions are displayed in rectangles and metabolites in ellipses. Different colors and line style signify characteristics of the node; for example, gene-indicated reactions are displayed in rectangles with vertical double lines (see Figure S2 for legend of all variations of node characteristics). Importantly, MMINT does not display zero-flux reactions (non-active). Edges into a reaction indicate reactants, and edges out represent products of the reaction. Hovering the mouse over reactions, metabolites, and edges displays stoichiometric information, formulas, and flux quantities, respectively.

MMINT uses an automatic graph layout to organize network elements displayed in the graph frame. This means that modifications to the displayed network can be quickly incorporated, with no setup required; as soon as a GEM file and a CBA solution file are supplied, a graph is displayed.

Main functionalities

Full flux networks in CBA solutions can be highly complex, encompassing potentially hundreds or thousands of reactions and metabolites. Therefore, only a portion of the non-zero-flux reactions (active), and the corresponding metabolites, are shown by default in MMINT. The tool relies on different functions that allow refinement and further exploration of networks shown by default.

Media and product

The concepts of “media” and “product” define which elements of the network are displayed in the graph frame by default. Media are metabolites that are represented in the initial growth media registered in a CBA solution (products of active exchange reactions), while products include reactants of the biomass reaction and metabolic end products (reactants of active exchange reactions). “Intermediate” metabolites, linking media and product metabolites, are also displayed in the graph frame by default. Media and product elements are shown in the control frame, which can be selected or unselected to expand, simplify, or otherwise modify the displayed network in the graph frame. Similarly, Intermediate metabolites can be added to the media or product lists by right-clicking on the corresponding nodes on the graph frame and selecting “Add to –.” For additional details, see case study 2.

Comparison of metabolic network visualization tools

Several tools for the visualization of metabolic networks/fluxes in GEMs are currently available. However, most of them require a comprehensive understanding of the network encoded in a GEM. This includes tools such as Escher,24 MINERVA (molecular interaction network visualization),25,26 MetExplore,32 Pathway Tools,27 Fluxer,33 and CAVE.28 For instance, Escher relies on a preexisting metabolic map built for the GEM in question to display fluxes from a given CBA solution. MINERVA is designed for manually curated pathway maps and lacks support for dynamically importing and displaying FBA results for non-model organisms. CAVE and Pathway Tools rely on the manual selection of individual reactions to visualize them as a network.

Other tools where familiarity with the GEM to be analyzed is not a prerequisite are also readily available. For example, LMME34 (large metabolic model explorer) is available as an add-on for Vanted35 (visualization and analysis of networks containing experimental data) and can decompose GEMs into subsystems and relationships between the identified subsystems. However, it is not compatible with CBA solutions. Similarly, GEMtractor36 can decompose full GEMs into a metabolite-, reaction-, or enzyme-centric network files, compatible with tools for network visualization such as Cytoscape.37 Additionally, GEMtractor network files can be tailored based on CBA solutions. MMINT generates qualitatively equivalent outputs to GEMtractor’s decompositions (see method details); however, MMINT produces bipartite network structures for both metabolite- and reaction-centric views without relying on third-party software. Meanwhile, MetExplore has adapted Cytoscape directly as a plugin to display entire networks or automatically computed subnetworks from CBA solutions that are metabolite centric.32 Importantly, all these tools, other than Escher, lack a function to compare CBA solutions, and none of them enable dynamic network exploration, a key design feature of MMINT. While additional tools for metabolic network visualization exist, such as MetaboAnalyst,38 Vanted,35 and KEGG Mapper,39 these are not natively compatible with GEMs or CBA solutions.

Out of these alternatives, two options stand out: the broadly applied and cited Escher24 and the more recently published tool for GEM metabolic network visualization, CAVE.28 The main differences between these two tools and MMINT are shown in Figure 1.

To showcase MMINT’s capabilities, we introduce three distinct case studies centered around two previously published Pyrococcous furiosus GEM (iPfu) phenotypes.5 Briefly, the outcomes from two different engineering strategies were modeled through the application of select constraints for iPfu to mainly produce acetate in one instance (acetate phenotype) and ethanol in the other (ethanol phenotype) while medium conditions remained the same.5 Further details about iPfu and each phenotype can be found in Vailionis et al.5 and STAR Methods.

After loading a model and a solution, MMINT automatically identifies media and product metabolites, building a default network with non-zero-flux reactions. While such a network may already provide meaningful insights in terms of GEM structure and flux distributions, further refinement is possible to identify metabolites, reactions, and pathways of interest. Case 2: Solution comparison in the acetate and ethanol phenotypes

MMINT has been specifically designed to visualize and compare fluxes from two flux distribution solutions, which can aid in the identification of mechanistic drivers of phenotypes. Here, MMINT is used to highlight how reaction manipulations lead to different fluxes in terms of maltose degradation, end-metabolite production (acetate/ethanol), and sodium cycling in each of the two studied phenotypes. To generate the network visualizations shown in this section, we rely on several MMINT functionalities, including media and product selection, Hide/Unhide, and the Explode and Unexplode functions. Figure 3A shows that both phenotypes rely on a remarkably similar pathways but with notable differences, which are partially explained by reaction insertions and deletions.

Flux distribution comparison

(A) Differences in maltose degradation fluxes leading to the production of main end-metabolites between the acetate phenotype (light blue network elements) and the ethanol phenotype (purple network elements). Reactions and metabolites common to both phenotypes are shown in gold. A full display of the carbon fluxes in the acetate and ethanol phenotypes, including sections in common, is shown in Figure S1.

(B) Differences in sodium import fluxes (Sodium_e to Sodium_c) resulting from knocked and inserted reactions in the ethanol phenotype in relation to the acetate phenotype. While most of the reactions that drive this process are common to both phenotypes, metabolic differences are displayed (green boxes and numbers). A table summarizing main metabolic differences between the acetate and ethanol phenotype and the functions and reactions involved is included in the figure.

First, the acetate phenotype utilizes R07159 to metabolize GAP into 3PG, while the ethanol phenotype relies on R01058 GAPN (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) for the same metabolic conversion (Figure 3A, box 1). A crucial difference between these two reactions is that GAPN reduces NADP as intermediary, while R07159 in the acetate phenotype reduces ferredoxin instead. The acetate phenotype further relies on R00229 to transform acetyl-coenzyme A (CoA) into acetate, which relies on ATP. Meanwhile, R00229-G is responsible for this conversion in the ethanol phenotype. R00229-G uses GTP instead of ATP. These differences are highlighted in Figure 3A, box 2.

Finally, we can visualize the difference in fluxes being directed toward the production of ethanol in each phenotype. Most of the acetate produced in the acetate phenotype is exported into the extracellular space (Acetate_e), while all the acetate in the ethanol phenotype is further metabolized into ethanol, to then be exported (Ethanol_e). This demands increased activity in both AOR_acetaldehyde and R00746. AOR_acetaldehyde converts acetate into acetaldehyde while oxidizing ferredoxin and R00746 oxidizes NADPH to convert acetaldehyde into ethanol (Figure 3, box 3).

Another key metabolic difference between the acetate and the ethanol phenotypes that is partially explained by genetic modifications in the ethanol phenotype is how the system cycles sodium. The acetate phenotype relies on the oxidation of ferredoxin to import sodium through the mbh (membrane-bound hydrogenase) reaction, which is knocked out in the ethanol phenotype. As an alternative, the ethanol phenotype utilizes CODH (carbon monoxide dehydrogenase), an inserted sodium importer that converts intracellular CO into CO2 in the process (Figure 3A, box 4).

Case 3: Identifying mechanistic drivers in the acetate and ethanol phenotypes

Genetic and reaction manipulations are certainly influencing end-metabolite production differences between the acetate and ethanol phenotypes. However, Vailionis et al. trialled several similar phenotypes to improve ethanol production in iPfu, with most of them being unsuccessful.5 Therefore, we utilized MMINT to identify and show the underlying drivers that make this particular phenotype a successful one.

The solution exploration and comparison performed with MMINT in case 2 suggest that differences in NADP+/NADPH and ferredoxin cycling, derived from genetic manipulations, influence end-metabolite production in the acetate and ethanol phenotypes. Here, we combine the Add to Media/Product, Hide metabolite, and Explode/Unexplode functions in MMINT, to further understand cofactor cycling in both iPfu phenotypes. First, two key reactions involved in the cycling of ferredoxin (mbh, sodium importer, Fdred [reduced ferredoxin] to Fdox [oxidized ferredoxin]; R07159, GAP to 3PG, Fdox to Fdred) in the acetate phenotype are inactive in the ethanol phenotype (Figure 4A), suggesting a reduced reliance on ferredoxin in the latter. However, ferredoxin is crucial for metabolizing pyruvate and CoA into acetyl-CoA with R01196, a non-zero-flux reaction in both phenotypes. It is this need (ferredoxin cycling), and the lack of alternatives, that redirect fluxes from the knocked mbh toward AOR_acetaldehyde, increasing the production of acetaldehyde (Acetaldehyde_c), an intermediary between acetate and ethanol.

Neither the acetate nor the ethanol phenotypes are capable of exporting acetaldehyde; hence, to prevent its accumulation in the intracellular space, the metabolite is further converted into ethanol by R00746, which can be exported to the extracellular space. Yet, this step requires NADPH oxidation. A larger flux in the ethanol phenotype through AOR_acetaldehyde is therefore translated into an increased dependence on NADP/NADPH cycling (Figure 4B). This explains the redirection of fluxes through GAPN (R01058, NADP reduction) in the ethanol phenotype, over R07159 (ferredoxin reduction).

In summary, the phenotypic change between acetate and ethanol production is driven not only by the gene/reaction modifications to the iPfu network but also by the underlying shifts these introduce in terms of cofactors required to maintain redox balance. The network produced by MMINT suggests that acetate production in the corresponding phenotype results from its reliance on ferredoxin cycling. Modifications to the reactions in such phenotype result in flux being distributed through reactions that rely on both ferredoxin and NADP/NADPH cycling instead and lead to the production of ethanol.

Discussion

This work presents MMINT, a new tool designed to facilitate the exploration and comparison of metabolic flux networks, addressing a key challenge in the field of metabolic modeling. MMINT enables researchers to move beyond static representations of metabolic networks by providing an intuitive, interactive interface for visualizing and interrogating complex flux distributions. As demonstrated through the case studies using the P. furiosus GEM, MMINT can illuminate relevant metabolic pathways, highlight differences between phenotypic states, and uncover the underlying mechanistic drivers of these variations. Specifically, our analysis revealed the importance of cofactor balancing in driving the shift from acetate to ethanol production, demonstrating the power of MMINT to identify non-intuitive mechanistic metabolic principles, from which phenotypes emerge.

A key strength of MMINT lies in its capacity for rapid and intuitive network exploration, as users can dynamically refine visualizations without requiring prior network familiarity or predefined metabolic maps. Additionally, MMINT’s capability for immediate comparative visualization of alternative flux distributions distinctly facilitates the identification of metabolic mechanisms underlying phenotypic differences, streamlining analyses that are otherwise challenging using existing visualization tools.

Genome-scale metabolic modeling is widely used to understand molecular mechanisms underlying metabolic phenotypes. These insights can be leveraged to predict optimal engineering strategies and design microbial strains with desired properties. MMINT enables metabolic discovery by providing a stand-alone graphic user interface to explore flux distributions under alternative metabolic scenarios. MMINT is agnostic to the tool used for flux balance analysis and automatically constructs the full global network de novo without reference to an existing map. In contrast to existing tools (e.g., CAVE), MMINT provides novel functionality that allows users to explore and simplify the entire network quickly and easily without specifying a substrate or product. Currently no other visualization or design tools provide the flexibility to explore full CBA solutions and discover underlying metabolic mechanisms with the speed and clarity provided by MMINT.

While some visualization platforms integrate built-in FBA functionalities, embedding FBA functionality within a visualization tool significantly increases software complexity, often without delivering the full analytical options required for comprehensive CBA. Tools that do integrate FBA, such as Escher-FBA or Fluxer, support basic operations like modifying reaction bounds or performing simple knockouts but lack advanced capabilities such as gene-level knockouts or advanced CBA (e.g., FVA). These tools still rely on dedicated frameworks such as COBRApy to perform more sophisticated analyses, highlighting that stand-alone modeling environments remain essential for detailed model analysis. By requiring users to generate flux solution files externally, MMINT focuses on delivering a lightweight, user-friendly, and interactive interface for exploring precomputed flux distributions. This intentional design choice facilitates broader adoption and ensures seamless integration into existing research workflows.

The case studies presented here highlight the capabilities of MMINT to rapidly explore alternative flux distributions and identify key metabolites and reactions. For example, in the acetate phenotype, MMINT highlights how maltose import and metabolism lead to biomass production, facilitating the discovery of critical pathways by hiding common metabolites. This targeted visualization aids in understanding the specific biochemical routes utilized by different metabolic phenotypes. Case studies 2 and 3 also demonstrate how comparing flux distributions across metabolic phenotypes with MMINT identified underlying drivers such as cofactor cycling. MMINT visualizations highlight that phenotypic changes between acetate and ethanol production are driven by gene/reaction modifications and shifts in cofactor requirements for redox balance. MMINT suggests that acetate production relies on ferredoxin cycling, while engineering modifications shift fluxes to rely on NADP/NADPH cycling, leading to ethanol production. This suggests that cofactor-driven engineering strategies may successfully enhance ethanol production.

Similar cofactor-driven engineering strategies have been reported in the literature. For example, an alternative NADPH regeneration pathway has been engineered into Saccharomyces cerevisiae to improve ethanol production.40 Furthermore, phenolic acid production has been improved by systematically engineering cofactor supply and recycling.41 Thus, MMINT offers a powerful platform for dissecting and comparing metabolic networks, revealing mechanistic insights crucial for understanding and engineering metabolic phenotypes. The integration of cofactor-driven strategies further enhances the potential of metabolic engineering to optimize production processes and achieve desired phenotypic outcomes.

Limitations of the study

While MMINT is generally compatible with SBML/XML GEMs files, non-standardized formatting can lead to incorrect reaction and metabolite names or missing element details (e.g., metabolite formulas). Similarly, the use of non-conventional identifiers in GEMs may complicate user interpretability of the displayed network. Additionally, MMINT does not run CBA (e.g., FBA) natively. Instead, it leverages external, purpose-built modeling frameworks for CBA. This allows MMINT to remain lightweight and interoperable, thereby supporting broader adoption and maintaining ease of use. Future work will include exploring enhancements such as the incorporation of non-intensive FBA capabilities, or integrating MMINT with platforms such as COBRApy, to enable simulations to be performed directly but without compromising these advantages. Lastly, familiarizing with new software often demands time investment, and MMINT is not the exception. We recommend users to watch and follow the user video guides included in our online repository to quickly adapt to the different functionalities in MMINT.

Experimental model and study participant details

The Pyrococcus furiosus GEM (iPfu)

Vailionis et al.5 built iPfu from GenBank assembly ASM730v1, which corresponds to P. furiosus DSM 3638. The model was manually curated and calibrated based on experimental growth data with three carbon sources,44 achieving a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.95. The base iPfu model was later modified to resemble COM1, a P. furiosus strain amenable to genetic engineering. This process required removal of 15 metabolic reactions, with no reported impact to iPfu-derived growth prediction accuracy. The final characteristics of iPfu are shown in Table S1.

Modelling iPfu variants

OE-AdhF (acetate phenotype)

Following extensive testing, an iPfu variant, OE-AdhF, was selected by Vailionis et al. as a base model to explore potential engineering strategies aimed at optimising ethanol production in 14.6 mM maltose and 0.5 g/L yeast extract enriched media. OE-AdhF represents a variant where gene adhF, encoding an aldehyde dehydrogenase is over expressed, resulting in an increase in ethanol production (Table S2). Gene overexpression is not explicitly captured in conventional GEMs such as iPfu. Therefore, the base model was constrained to produce a predetermined quantity of ethanol. Specifically, the lower bound of the ethanol transport reaction (TP_ETOH) was set to 3.29 mmol/gDW/h (millimoles per gram of dry weight per hour), which was determined from empirical data and random flux sampling analysis.5

FBA predicts that optimising the OE-AdhF variant for biomass production in the specified media results in the synthesis of 3.29 mmol/gDW/h of ethanol, 46.09 mmol/gDW/h of acetate and 0.71 gDW/h of biomass (Figure S3A).

EO-AdhF-N/CO Δmbh (ethanol phenotype)

Aiming to identify engineering strategies that favour ethanol production over acetate, Vailionis et al. sampled a considerable combination of gene insertions and deletions for OE-AdhF. The variant that required the least modifications and produces the most promising predictions is OE-AdhF-N/CO Δmbh, which required two gene insertions and one deletion (Table S2). FBA predicts that this variant, henceforth referred as the ethanol phenotype, produces ethanol at a rate of 47.9 mmol/gDW/h, no acetate, and 0.8 gDW/h biomass (Figure S3B) when optimised for biomass production in the same media as OE-AdhF.

Method details

FBA in iPfu variants

To replicate the metabolic states highlighted in Figure S3, we utilised the SBML files b971180d_YM_closed_OEadhf (acetate phenotype) and b971180d_YM_GAPN_CODH_closed_OEadhf, which are part of5 data repository (Data S1). Model b971180d_YM_GAPN_CODH_closed_OEadhf was further constrained to represent the required ethanol phenotype (Table S2). To aid interpretability of the figures shown in the case studies, relevant metabolite identifiers in file b971180d_YM_GAPN_CODH_closed_OEadhf were replaced with their corresponding metabolite names (Datas S1 and S2). Compartment suffixes were also modified to represent metabolites in the extracellular space (_e) or intracellular space (_c). To model the flux distributions referenced in Case Studies, FBA4 was performed on the COBRAPy platform and the Gurobi solver43 with biomass set as optimisation objective.42 FBA solutions were converted to Pandas DataFrame objects and exported as Comma Separated Value (.csv) files (Datas S3 and S4). A detailed guide to replicate the networks shown in Case Studies (Figures 2, 3, and 4, respectively) can be found in our online repository (https://doi.org/10.25919/jfq3-rp68). Data S5 contains the files required to reproduce the flux distributions for the acetate and ethanol phenotypes.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Network topology in MMINT

We use a graph G(N,E) with nodes N = M U R, and edges E. M is the set of nodes representing metabolites (this includes media and product metabolites), and R is the set of nodes representing reactions. The set of edges in E is made of two types: edges in M x R from nodes representing the reactants to the reaction node itself; and edges in R x M from the reaction node to each of the products of the reaction.

Nodes with in-degree zero in G are called source or Media nodes i.e., metabolites available in the media. Nodes with out-degree zero are included in the Product nodes, and represent terminal metabolites produced by the organism during growth (for example CO2). Product nodes also include reactants of the biomass reaction.

Algorithm frameworks in MMINT

Max Flow algorithms

The Max Flow algorithm solves the classical Max Flow problem from graph theory (35), using an implementation of the Ford-Fulkerson algorithm (35). All edges in the graph have capacity equal to the flux through the associated reaction. As usual, with multi-Media and multi-Product instances, two dummy nodes S and T are added to G. Edges are added from S to all media nodes, and from all destination nodes to T. The maximum flow is then found between S and T.

The Simple Max Flow algorithm solves the same problem on the same graph as Max Flow but uses capacity one on all edges. This translates into a visually simpler solution that shows only one pathway linking media and product metabolites.

Shortest path and tree algorithms

The Shortest Path and Tree algorithms use the Dijkstra algorithm (36) to find the shortest path from every media node to every destination node. Tree algorithms grow a tree of pathways from the selected media or product metabolite(s). Source trees use the shortest path from each selected media node to all selected destination nodes. Destination trees use the shortest path from selected media nodes to each selected destination. A media tree for instance, grows a tree from selected media metabolite(s), and shows all the reactions and metabolites that this media has a part in producing. The product tree is the opposite: it grows the tree towards the selected metabolite(s), showing all the reactions and metabolites that contribute to producing the selected product.

Flood algorithms

Flood algorithms also use the Dijkstra algorithm with a limited depth D. The length of every edge is set to one. Every edge from G expanded by the Dijkstra algorithm is added to the display graph. If an edge returns to a previously seen node, the edge is marked as a cycle edge, and the destination node is not considered again. Similarly, a node is not considered if the distance to the node exceeds D. In MMINT, flood algorithms find all possible paths from or to a metabolite. Since there are many such paths, the number of edges in the path is limited using the “Level” counter on the graph frame. Click “+” to increase the number of edges displayed, or “-” to decrease it.
