Multi-scale modeling and sensitivity analysis for weak roadway areas: A case study of Xiaoyun coal mine
Authors: Zhichao Tian, Lianyu Ma, Yejiao Liu, Zhiwei Xiao, Yibo Zhang, Songzhe Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGround control in deep coal mine roadways faces significant challenges due to the presence of “weak zones” characterized by complex stress environments and structural incompatibilities. To differentiate the failure mechanisms of these zones and optimize support strategies, this study integrates theoretical analysis, multi-scale numerical simulation, and field monitoring, using the 6204 track roadway at Xiaoyun Coal Mine as a case study. A macro-meso coupled numerical model was constructed to simulate fracture evolution, and an orthogonal experiment utilizing Range Analysis and Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the sensitivity of support parameters. The results indicate that bolt spacing is the most critical factor controlling plastic zone propagation, significantly outweighing geometric parameters like bolt diameter or length. Based on these findings, a synergistic support strategy combining “dense high-strength bolting” and “deep-anchored cabling” was proposed. Numerical evaluations suggest that this scheme limits the plastic zone depth by 40% (to approximately 1.5 m), substantially mitigating confinement blind spots. Field monitoring indicates that the optimized support system successfully restrains the cumulative residual roof subsidence to approximately 31.9 mm and the sidewall convergence to approximately 22.8 mm, effectively promoting the long-term overall stability of the roadway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48033-0.Keywords: Deep roadway, Weak zones, Multi-scale modeling, Sensitivity analysis, Synergistic support optimizationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

As coal resource exploitation in China advances into depths exceeding 1,000 m, ground control in deep roadways faces unprecedented challenges, specifically the synergistic prevention and control of compound disasters involving rockbursts and roof collapses1–4. Against this complex engineering backdrop, domestic and international scholars have conducted extensive investigations. Through field measurements and numerical back-analysis, Kang and Jiang et al.2 systematically revealed the nonlinear expansion laws of the plastic zone. Based on the concept of effective anchorage layer thickness and superimposed beam theory, Yao et al.5 established a refined analytical mechanical model for roof anchorage, providing a quantitative basis for the influence of bolt spacing on reinforcement. Field studies by Kang and Gao1 further indicated that the peak stress of surrounding rock in deep mines increases exponentially with depth.

Regarding innovation in support technology, Xie and Wang6 proposed a collaborative control system of “external anchoring and internal destressing” for large-section coal roadways. Luo et al.7 validated the efficacy of active confinement using forepoling techniques. Meanwhile, advances in monitoring have provided data for precise control. Yuan8 reviewed the development of risk identification and early warning technologies for dynamic hazards. Liu and Xiao9 developed a dynamic feedback system for asymmetric support along gobs. Addressing extreme geological conditions, Gao et al.10 implemented a combined support system in ultra-thick coal seams, while Han et al.11 identified the critical impact of pillar width on mitigating rockburst risks. In terms of failure mechanisms, Zhao et al.12 utilized discrete element modeling to reconstruct macroscopic fracture patterns in deep coal mining. Further studies by Chen et al.13 successfully reduced the radius of broken rock zones using integrated modification technologies. Moreover, the theories of high pre-stressed bolting byKang1,2 and the excavation compensation methods proposed by He and others14–16 have become influential industry standards. The mechanical response of energy-absorbing support components has also been investigated in deep mines17,18, alongside research on the deformation of roadways in ultra-thick seams and layered rock models19,20.

Despite significant progress in theoretical and engineering domains, the instability of deep roadways fundamentally constitutes a multi-scale mechanical challenge, driven by the deterioration of rock mass structure, non-uniform stress redistribution, and support system interaction. This instability manifests macroscopically as large-scale deformation, originating mesoscopically from the initiation, propagation, and coalescence of micro-cracks. However, current support designs rely heavily on empirical methods, lacking quantitative frameworks to evaluate the sensitivity of key parameters—such as bolt spacing, diameter, and length—where minor perturbations can trigger nonlinear mechanical responses. Existing research remains limited in the systematic identification of “weak zones”, the differentiation of instability mechanisms, and parameter optimization based on sensitivity analysis. Specifically, an integrated framework connecting mesoscopic damage evolution with macroscopic parameter sensitivity is noticeably absent. Addressing this gap, this study classifies roadway weak zones and introduces an innovative multi-scale numerical simulation approach. By coupling macroscopic deformation with mesoscopic plastic zone evolution and utilizing orthogonal experiments, the sensitivity hierarchy and influence weights of critical support parameters are quantified. Using the 6204 track roadway in Xiaoyun Coal Mine as a case study, a comprehensive research paradigm merging theoretical analysis, multi-scale simulation, and field verification is established. This work provides a rigorous quantitative basis for ensuring the long-term stability of deep roadways under complex geological conditions.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 provides the theoretical failure analysis of weak zones; Sect. 3 describes the multi-scale numerical modeling and orthogonal experimental design; Sect. 4 discusses the sensitivity results and optimization scheme; Sect. 5 validates the effectiveness through field monitoring; and Sect. 6 summarizes the conclusions.

Theoretical analysis of weak zone failure mechanisms

Concept of weak zones

The “weak zone” is defined as the critical domain within a roadway’s support system where the damage threshold is first exceeded, resulting in large, asymmetric deformation. It characterizes the interface of incompatibility between the geostress environment, rock mass fabric, and support rigidity. Based on the underlying fracture mechanisms, weak zones are delineated as follows:

(1) Structural-Type: Characterized by failure along pre-existing geological weaknesses. Fractures predominantly form as bedding-parallel shears or complex tensile-shear zones caused by slip along joints, where the rock mass’s structural anisotropy dictates stability.

(2) Stress-Type: Localized at geometric discontinuities (e.g., wall-roof intersections) where high deviatoric stresses induce crushing–dilatant behavior. Damage typically presents as compressive-shear fracturing driven by stress redistribution that exceeds the rock mass yield criteria.

(3) Support-Mismatch-Type: Resulting from suboptimal support design, such as inadequate spacing or stiffness. This deficiency prevents effective stress transfer at the rock-support boundary, leading to localized tensile zones between bolts (inter-bolt failure) or shear failure around anchor plates due to insufficient confining pressure.

Failure characteristics of weak zones

(1) Sidewall-Controlled Weak Zones: Following excavation-induced stress redistribution, vertical stresses concentrate laterally. Intrinsic discontinuities (bedding planes, joints) facilitate conjugate shear fractures. As the rock mass deforms, slip along these planes culminates in X-shaped shear localization. Roof subsidence exacerbates this by imposing additional vertical loads, causing fragmentation, dilation, and lateral extrusion of the sidewall.

(2) Roof-Controlled Weak Zones: This mechanism is initiated by premature delamination and subsidence of weak roof strata, redirecting vertical stresses toward the sidewalls. The rigid floor acts as a boundary that induces a confinement effect, accumulating horizontal stress in the sidewalls. Even in robust sidewalls, mobilization of latent joints drives asymmetric damage, typically manifesting as tensile fracture zones near the roof interface and compression–shear zones near the floor.

(3) Floor–Sidewall Interaction Zones: A rigid roof establishes a stress arch, transmitting loads to boundaries. Failure originates in the weak floor via plastic yielding, inducing stress concentrations at sidewall bases. The resultant floor heave reduces the effective sidewall height, prompting roof sagging via a cantilever beam mechanism, which propels sidewalls outward and forms a deep-seated slip surface connecting the sidewall and floor.

Materials and methods

Geological parameters

The 6204 track roadway in Xiaoyun Coal Mine is situated at a depth of 800 m, penetrating through the No. 2 coal seam with an average thickness of 3.49 m. The immediate roof and floor consist of fine sandstone and mudstone, respectively, with the mechanical parameters of the rock mass provided in Table 1. The vertical in-situ stress is 20.35 MPa, and the horizontal in-situ stress is 26.46 MPa. The roadway was originally supported by a combined “bolt-shotcrete-mesh-cable” system. The roof bolts were cables with a specification of φ21.8 mm × 7300 mm, spaced at 1600 mm × 1600 mm, and pretensioned to 250 kN. Full-section ribbed steel bolts of φ22 mm × 2000 mm were installed with a spacing of 800 mm × 800 mm and a pretension of 80 kN. The shotcrete layer was C20 concrete with a thickness of 150 mm. Multi-scale numerical model setup

To accurately reconstruct the rock mass response following roadway excavation, this study adopts a macro–meso coupled multi-resolution numerical modeling strategy. The concept of “multi-scale” is implemented across two dimensions:

Physical Scale

The model spans from the far-field boundary (tens of meters) to the near-field surrounding rock of the roadway (meter scale).

Mechanical Behavior Scale

The model bridges the mechanical response across different resolutions.

Specifically, the macro-scale far-field region employs a coarse mesh (element size: 2 m) with linear elastic constitutive behavior to precisely simulate the distribution and stable transmission of the regional in-situ stress field. Conversely, the meso-scale near-field region—a critical 10 m × 10 m area surrounding the roadway—utilizes adaptive mesh refinement techniques to reduce element size to 0.25 m. Simulation at this scale focuses on capturing non-linear behaviors induced by excavation unloading and stress concentration, including the initiation and propagation of the plastic zone, shear dilatancy effects, and fracture evolution. The numerical simulations were conducted using FLAC3D (Version 6.0, Itasca Consulting Group, Official URL: https://www.itascacg.com/software/flac3d), incorporating a Mohr–Coulomb constitutive model for the rock mass. Based on the integrated Range and ANOVA evaluations, rock bolt spacing is identified as the sole “Class I: Highly Sensitive” parameter controlling global roadway stability, significantly outweighing component dimensions like length or diameter. Mechanistically, this dominance reflects the principle of “structural continuity over individual capacity” in deep, fractured soft rock: while high-strength bolts act as discrete point loads, their individual capacity cannot constrain volumetric expansion if wide spacing leaves “confinement blind zones” between them. System efficacy depends on forming a continuous “Compressive Reinforcement Arch”, which is achieved only when spacing is sufficiently reduced to ensure the overlap of compressive stress fields from adjacent bolts. In contrast, cable bolt quantity acts as a “Class II: Sensitive” factor specifically for the roof, providing essential deep-seated suspension, whereas other geometric parameters remain statistically “Class III: Insensitive”. Therefore, effective control relies not on over-dimensioning individual components, but on optimizing support density to establish a homogeneous confinement belt that fundamentally suppresses plastic dilation.

Therefore, effective control relies not on over-dimensioning individual components, but on optimizing support density to establish a homogeneous confinement belt that fundamentally suppresses plastic dilation. From a practical and economic perspective, this finding suggests that optimizing bolt spacing, rather than blindly increasing bolt dimensions, can lead to more efficient material usage and faster tunneling speeds, thereby balancing structural safety with economic feasibility.

Optimization design and simulation verification

Based on the results of the previous parameter sensitivity analysis, and to systematically validate the effectiveness of the support optimization strategy, this study designed three numerical simulation schemes with progressive comparative significance. These schemes were developed to conduct controlled variable analyses from two perspectives: “single-factor dominance” and “system synergy”. The specific settings for each scheme are as follows: Scheme I represents the conventional support condition, simulating the commonly used medium-to-low support density on-site, serving as the baseline for comparison with subsequent schemes. Scheme II is the single-factor optimized scheme, where only the highly sensitive parameter—bolt spacing—is adjusted to independently verify the dominant role of support density on surrounding rock stability. Scheme III is the synergistic support scheme, which further optimizes the anchor cable system based on statistically optimal combinations to establish a full-section, high-stiffness bearing structure characterized by “high-strength bolts combined with dense cables.” Considering the balance between on-site constructability and mechanical performance, the bolt spacing in Schemes I and II is uniformly set at 0.6 m to avoid potential construction interference associated with a 0.5 m spacing. Analysis of vertical stress distribution

Roadway excavation disrupts the original in-situ stress field, leading to its redistribution. The roof and floor generally enter a state of low stress or localized minor tension, primarily due to unloading rebound and bed separation. A comparison of vertical stress distributions under different support schemes reveals a pronounced influence of support parameters on stress concentration patterns. In Scheme I (conventional support), the maximum compressive stress in the deep sidewall reaches 29.10 MPa. The stress concentration zone is narrow with a steep gradient, indicating inadequate load-bearing capacity in shallow strata and consequent transfer of major loads to deeper regions. Under Scheme II (optimized bolt spacing), with increased bolt density, the peak compressive stress decreases to 27.30 MPa. Although stress levels are somewhat reduced, a distinct concentration area persists. Scheme III (synergistic support with high-tension cables) achieves a further reduction in maximum compressive stress to 25 MPa, representing a 14.1% decrease compared to Scheme I. Notably, the stress transition zone expands, suggesting optimized load transfer pathways and a more uniform distribution of stresses within the deeper surrounding rock, which enhances overall bearing capacity. Evolution and control of plastic failure zones

Based on fine-mesh numerical simulations, the evolution patterns and control mechanisms of the plastic zones in the surrounding rock under different support strategies were systematically revealed. Under Scheme I, the surrounding rock exhibited significant stress relaxation and extensive plastic propagation. The vertical development depth of the plastic zone in the roof reached 2.5 m, which not only indicated a relatively large failure range but, more critically, exceeded the effective anchorage range of conventional bolts. This resulted in the bolt ends being situated within the shear yield zone, readily triggering a chain instability characterized by “anchor failure and surrounding rock separation.” To address these issues, Scheme II improved the integrity of the shallow surrounding rock by increasing bolt density, slightly reducing the failure depth to 2 m. However, this strategy did not fundamentally alter the stress transfer path to deeper strata, leaving the risk of discontinuous plastic failure in the deep surrounding rock, with limited control effectiveness. In contrast, Scheme III demonstrated clear optimization effects. Through a synergistic support structure of “intensive shallow restraint + high-prestress deep suspension,” the depth of the plastic zones in both the roof and sidewalls was controlled within 1.5 m, representing a 40% reduction compared to the baseline scheme. Furthermore, this design ensured that the anchorage ends of the long cables were entirely located within the deep elastic stable zone, establishing a reliable deep load-bearing foundation. The results indicate that the optimized scheme not only significantly reduced the extent of the plastic zone quantitatively but also reconstructed the synergistic load-bearing system of “support-surrounding rock” in terms of mechanical mechanisms, effectively inhibiting the continued propagation of the plastic zone into deeper strata. Analysis of surrounding rock deformation results

To characterize the deformation behavior of the surrounding rock under varying support conditions, monitoring lines were installed normal to the roof and sidewalls, extending 6 m into the rock mass. The measured displacements exhibited a clear declining trend with increased support intensity. The maximum deformations were recorded under Scheme I, with roof subsidence reaching 68.3 mm and sidewall convergence measuring 45.2 mm. Scheme II yielded improved control, reducing these values to 50.7 mm and 37.1 mm, respectively. The most effective control was achieved under Scheme III, which further limited roof subsidence to 30.2 mm and sidewall convergence to 22.3 mm. Analysis of displacement distribution with depth indicated that at 5 m depth, the residual deformation under Scheme III was only 35.2% of that under Scheme I, highlighting its superior capacity to mobilize the load-bearing contribution of deeper rock zones. In summary, the integrated use of dense shallow bolting and deep high-prestress cables in Scheme III enhanced the overall stiffness of the support-surrounding rock structure. This resulted in a reduction of 55.8% in roof subsidence and 50.7% in sidewall convergence compared to Scheme I, demonstrating its optimal performance among the three schemes. From a techno-economic perspective, although Scheme III involves a higher density of support components—which necessitates a moderate increase in initial material investment—it successfully suppresses severe, large-scale non-linear deformation. This substantial reduction in anticipated long-term maintenance costs and the mitigation of costly production downtime make the optimized scheme highly economically viable and practically efficient. The excavation of roadways triggers stress redistribution within the surrounding rock, leading to subsequent progressive deformation. This deformation is effectively controlled by the combined bolt-cable support system. According to the field monitoring and numerical simulation comparison shown in Fig. 9, the surrounding rock under Support Scheme III exhibits typical full-cycle dynamic restraint characteristics. In the initial monitoring stage (the first 10 days), influenced by excavation unloading, the stress in the surrounding rock adjusted sharply, resulting in a continuous accumulation of deformation. At monitoring station 1#, the early roof subsidence reached approximately 5.0 to 9.5 mm, and the cumulative convergence of the two sidewalls reached around 2.1 to 4.2 mm. Station 2# demonstrated comparable early responses, indicating good synchronization and symmetry between the two monitored sections. During the transitional stage (days 15 to 25), as the high-stiffness support system gradually fully engaged and became load-bearing, the deformation rate of the surrounding rock began to significantly decrease. By day 20, the cumulative roof subsidence at station 1# progressed to approximately 20.0 mm, while the cumulative sidewall convergence steadily increased to roughly 13.8 mm. This decelerating trend in deformation increment indicates that the load-bearing framework, through the synergistic action of bolts and cables, had successfully intercepted further massive dilations in the deep weak zones. In the final monitoring stage (around day 30), the deformation curves distinctly flattened, revealing that the surrounding rock approached a rheologically stable state. At station 1#, the final cumulative roof subsidence safely plateaued at approximately 31.90 mm, and the cumulative sidewall convergence firmly capped at 22.85 mm. Station 2# showed highly consistent terminal behavior, with ultimate roof displacements settling near 31.50 mm and sidewall convergences stabilizing at approximately 22.50 mm. The close trajectory conformance and terminal agreement between the field measurements and the macroscopic numerical simulation predictions indicate that the mechanical equilibrium system established by Scheme III restrains plastic zone propagation efficiently and provides satisfactory long-term stability, fulfilling the safety requirements for deep mine production.

Discussion and limitations

While this study proposes a synergistic support strategy that successfully controls the deformation of weak roadway areas, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, FLAC3D is fundamentally a continuum-mechanics-based software, which possesses certain limitations in explicitly capturing macroscopic uncontinuous dynamic failure processes, such as sudden rock burst fragmentation or massive discrete roof collapse. Secondly, the geological and mechanical parameters applied in the numerical models were simplified based on equivalent rock mass theories. This simplification might overlook the intricate impacts of deeply concealed micro-faults and highly variable natural in-situ joints. In future studies, continuous-discontinuous coupling modeling methods (e.g., FDEM or 3DEC) should be integrated with real-time microseismic and stress monitoring data. Such integrations will allow for a dynamic updating of sensitivity variables, ultimately facilitating more precise stability evaluations and support optimization in deep complex geological environments.

Conclusion

This study conducted a comprehensive investigation into the stability control of weak zones in the deep roadway of Xiaoyun Coal Mine through an integrated approach involving theoretical analysis, multi-scale numerical simulation, sensitivity analysis, and field verification. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) Classification of Weak Zones and Mechanism of Synergistic Support. This study proposes a systematic classification of weak zones in deep roadways into three categories: structural, stress-induced, and support-mismatch, elucidating their corresponding failure characteristics. Building on this framework, a synergistic support mechanism was developed, combining “high-density bolts to form a continuous compressive arch in the shallow zone” with “high-tension cables providing deep suspension.” Analytical and numerical modeling suggest that this full-section, high-stiffness load-bearing structure can effectively mitigate the progressive propagation of the plastic zone and subsequent stress concentration.

(2) Dominant Sensitivity Law of Support Parameters. The relative sensitivity of key support parameters was quantified through multi-scale numerical simulations coupled with the ANOVA of orthogonal experiments. The results identified bolt spacing as the primary “highly sensitive” first-order parameter dictating global roadway stability. Its structural significance far exceeds that of individual component dimensions, such as bolt length or diameter. This finding supports the engineering design principle that “forming a continuous confinement structure” is more critical than merely “enhancing the ultimate capacity of individual supporting elements” in fractured soft rock masses.

(3) Efficacy and Field Validation of the Optimized Scheme. The optimized synergistic support scheme (high-density bolting + high-tension cables), developed based on the parametric sensitivity analysis, demonstrated a potential 40% reduction in plastic zone depth and a 14.1% decrease in peak vertical stress according to the computational models. Field applications conducted in the 6204 track roadway at Xiaoyun Coal Mine verified that this scheme induces a highly restrained dynamic response within the surrounding rock, characterized by “rapid early adjustment, a decelerating deformation rate, and final rheological stabilization.” Over a 30-day monitoring period, the ultimate cumulative rib convergence and roof subsidence were robustly contained at approximately 22.8 mm and 31.9 mm, respectively. The consistent trajectory and close numerical agreement between field measurements and simulated models indicate the favorable long-term stability and operational viability of the proposed support system.
