Association between social activities and cardiovascular diseases: evidence from the CHARLS database
Authors: Hongmiao Tao, Lihua Dong, Hongqiang Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between social activities and cardiovascular health has garnered widespread attention, but the specific associations of different types of social activities across various populations remain unclear. This study aimed to explore the correlation between different social activities and the likelihood of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including heart disease, stroke, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.MethodsThis study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to analyze the longitudinal associations between social activities and CVDs. Multivariable logistic regression models were applied to assess the link between different social activities and CVD outcomes, with stratified and sensitivity analyses conducted to verify the robustness of the results.ResultsA total of 38,684 observations were included, with an average follow-up period of five years. The analysis revealed that activities such as fitness and dancing were significantly associated with lower odds of stroke (OR = 0.75 [95% CI: 0.60, 0.94], p = 0.01). Conversely, playing mahjong, participating in social groups, and engaging in volunteer work were associated with higher odds of heart disease and dyslipidemia. Mahjong was linked to a higher likelihood of heart disease (OR = 1.10 [95% CI: 1.02, 1.19], p = 0.02), while social group participation was associated with an increased likelihood of dyslipidemia (OR = 1.59 [95% CI: 1.34, 1.89], p < 0.01). Stratified analyses revealed significant variations in these associations across different populations, such as higher odds of heart disease among women and drinkers who engaged in fitness and dancing.ConclusionThis study highlights the complex associations between social activities and cardiovascular health, emphasizing the need for personalized and context-specific health intervention strategies. Future research should further explore the multidimensional effects of social behaviors on cardiovascular health to provide more precise guidance for disease prevention and management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26872-w.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) represent a major public health challenge worldwide, leading to significant mortality and morbidity. In 2021, ischemic heart disease (IHD), stroke, and hypertension remained among the leading causes of death globally, accounting for 108.7, 87.4, and 16.3 deaths per 100,000 people, respectively [1]. With the global aging population and lifestyle changes, the prevalence and incidence of CVDs continue to rise, imposing a heavy burden on individuals, families, and societies [2]. Consequently, researchers are increasingly focusing on identifying effective prevention and intervention strategies [3, 4]. In addition to traditional biomedical factors, recent studies suggest that social factors, particularly social activities, may have a potential impact on cardiovascular health. Social activities are believed to reduce CVD risk through various mechanisms, such as enhancing social support, improving emotional well-being, and promoting physical activity [5]. However, findings on the relationship between social activities and CVDs remain inconsistent [6]. Therefore, it is of critical importance to explore the role of social activities in the prevention of CVDs.

Current research has preliminarily explored the complex relationship between social activities and CVD risk, with studies indicating that social activities can have both protective and risky effects [7]. Some studies have demonstrated that active social activities, such as interacting with friends, participating in community activities, and providing social support, can improve mental health and reduce stress [8]. For instance, a study found that frequent social interactions effectively alleviate psychological stress, which improves vascular function and reduces the occurrence of hypertension [9]. These findings highlight the potential protective role of social activities on cardiovascular health under certain conditions. Conversely, other studies have indicated that some forms of social activities are associated with unhealthy behaviors, such as prolonged sessions of playing mahjong or cards, which are often accompanied by sedentary behavior, smoking, and alcohol consumption, thereby increasing the risk of stroke and heart disease. A study on urban residents in China found a significant association between social activities like playing chess/cards/mahjong and the risk of hemorrhagic stroke (OR: 2.51; 95% CI: 1.05–6.01) [10]. This negative effect suggests that social activities are not universally health-promoting, and certain forms of social activities may even increase the burden on cardiovascular health. Although existing research has revealed the potential dual effects of social activities in CVD prevention, many studies have focused on single disease types and often failed to adequately adjust for demographic characteristics and chronic conditions as confounding factors [11]. Additionally, research on the impact of social activities in the context of multiple coexisting CVDs remains limited, leaving the burden of multimorbidity underexplored.

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study utilizes longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to distinguish the unique contributions of varying social activities on cardiovascular health. Unlike previous work that often operationalized social participation as a homogeneous construct, this analysis offers three key novelties: (1) we deconstruct social activities into eight specific activity types (e.g., volunteering vs. leisure activities) to isolate their distinct health impacts; (2) we examine associations with specific CVD subtypes (heart disease, stroke, hypertension, and dyslipidemia) rather than aggregate outcomes alone; and (3) we specifically investigate the impact of social activities on CVD multimorbidity. By employing multivariable logistic regression models with comprehensive adjustments and stratified analyses, this study aims to clarify the divergent effects of social activities and identify precise targets for intervention across different population subgroups.

Methods

Study population

This study utilized data from the CHARLS, collected between June 2011 and March 2012 across 28 provinces in China, covering 17,708 participants. The study design, rationale, and data collection methods of CHARLS have been detailed in previous studies [12]. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided informed consent. The study procedures adhered to the ethical standards of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration.

Although the primary target population of CHARLS is individuals aged 45 and older, the dataset includes household members, yielding a subset of younger participants. In this study, participants aged 40 years or older in 2011–2012 were selected. This inclusion criteria (≥ 40 years) was chosen to maximize the sample size and to capture the critical transition period (ages 40–44) for changes in social activities and early cardiovascular risk accumulation. Key variables extracted for analysis included demographic characteristics, health behaviors, chronic disease status, and social activities. The participant selection process is illustrated in Figure S1. From the initial dataset, we applied the following exclusion criteria sequentially: (1) 105 participants aged under 40 years were excluded; (2) 1,227 participants were excluded due to missing data on cardiovascular outcomes, including heart disease (n = 99), stroke (n = 213), hypertension (n = 361), and dyslipidemia (n = 554); (3) 16,175 participants were excluded due to missing information on social activities; and (4) 1,381 participants were excluded due to missing data on covariates, including 1,143 with missing demographic or lifestyle information and 238 with missing records on other chronic diseases. A complete-case analysis was conducted, requiring complete data across all five waves without imputation. Referring to previous population-based studies [13, 14], records with missing data were excluded to ensure data integrity and analytical validity. This exclusion approach, while reducing sample size, minimized potential bias from incomplete information and maintained consistency with established methodological standards in longitudinal cohort studies. After these exclusions, a final sample of 6,899 eligible participants was included in the longitudinal analysis. A longitudinal analysis was conducted using data from five survey waves, with follow-ups every two to three years, yielding approximately 38,684 observations from these participants.

Measurement of social activities and cardiovascular diseases

Social activities, the independent variables in this study, refer to the participants’ social behaviors within the past month, including interactions with friends or neighbors, participation in community leisure activities (e.g., playing Mahjong, chess), helping others, exercising or dancing, joining community or volunteer activities, and attending school or training courses [15]. Specifically, “community activities” encompassed participation in community-related organizations or clubs, while “volunteer activities” involved providing unpaid assistance through organized volunteer groups or community service programs. The detailed operational definitions and survey questions for all eight social activities are presented in Table S1. Participants were asked whether they engaged in these activities in the past month. Although frequency data were recorded, the distribution was highly skewed with low response rates for high-frequency categories in several activities. Consequently, to ensure statistical power and comparability across different activity types, social activities were operationalized as a binary variable (participation vs. non-participation). The dependent variables were cardiovascular diseases, including heart disease (e.g., myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure), stroke, hypertension, and dyslipidemia (e.g., elevated low-density lipoprotein, triglycerides, total cholesterol, or low high-density lipoprotein levels). The status of these chronic conditions was ascertained based on self-reported physician diagnoses (i.e., participants reported whether they had been diagnosed with the conditions by a doctor). This study aimed to examine the associations between social behaviors and the likelihood of cardiovascular diseases, considering the potential divergent effects of different activity types.

Covariates

Referring to previous large population literature [16, 17], this study included a range of demographic and health-related covariates. Covariates included age (specific age), sex (male, female), marital status (married, unmarried, divorced, widowed), residence (urban, rural), education level (no formal education, incomplete primary, private school graduate, primary school graduate, junior high school graduate, senior high school graduate, vocational school graduate, college graduate, undergraduate, master’s, doctoral graduate), retirement status (retired, not retired), smoking status (smoker, non-smoker), drinking behavior (drinker, non-drinker), and other non-cardiovascular chronic diseases (defined as the presence of diabetes, chronic lung diseases, or asthma). These covariates were used to control for confounding factors affecting cardiovascular disease, allowing for a more accurate analysis of the independent associations between social activities and health outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. Group comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using t-tests, while categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. Referring to previous literature, multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine the associations between social activities and cardiovascular diseases, with stepwise adjustments to assess the independent contribution of social activities [18, 19]. Referring to previous population-based literature [17, 20], this study applied three models to explore the relationship between social activities and cardiovascular disease risk. Model 1 included the independent variable (social activities) and the dependent variable (individual cardiovascular diseases) to preliminarily assess the direct association of each social activity with the likelihood of disease. Model 2 further adjusted for covariates, including sex, age, marital status, residence, education level, retirement status, smoking, drinking, and province, to account for the confounding effects of demographic characteristics. Model 3 added chronic disease variables to control for the influence of chronic conditions on cardiovascular disease, allowing for a more precise evaluation of the independent associations of social activities.

Additionally, based on Model 3, the study further analyzed the relationship between social activities and multiple cardiovascular diseases (i.e., two or more CVDs) to assess the potential protective or adverse associations of social activities under multiple disease burdens. The results included odds ratios (OR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and significance levels for each social activity, with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis

To validate the robustness of the results, three sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, the original logistic regression model was replaced with a linear regression model to examine the impact of different modeling methods. Second, analyses were restricted to samples aged ≥ 45 years. Since the standard target population of CHARLS and comparable literature typically focus on individuals aged 45 and above, this sensitivity analysis aimed to verify whether the inclusion of the 40–44 age group in the main model influenced the results. Lastly, the analysis was further refined for participants aged 45 to 85 to ensure the applicability of results within a specific age range. Fourth, to address potential reverse causality bias, whereby pre-existing cardiovascular disease may reduce social activity participation, we conducted analyses restricted to participants without any cardiovascular disease (heart disease, stroke, hypertension, or dyslipidemia) at their first observation. This approach ensures that observed associations reflect the prospective effect of social activities on CVD incidence rather than the impact of pre-existing disease on activity participation. These sensitivity analyses aimed to comprehensively evaluate whether the impact of social activities on CVD risk remained consistent across different models and age strata.

Results

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of 38,684 participant observations across five surveys. Participants included 6,899 in 2011, 7,355 in 2013, 7,077 in 2015, 8,695 in 2018, and 8,658 in 2020. The average age of participants was 61.84 ± 10.25 years, with a female predominance (53.19%). Most participants were married (81.37%), lived in urban areas (62.32%), and were predominantly employed (85.80%). Lifestyle data showed that 58.34% were non-smokers, while 41.66% were smokers; 66.36% did not consume alcohol, whereas 33.64% were drinkers. Health conditions included heart disease in 15.91% of participants, stroke in 4.80%, hypertension in 33.61%, and dyslipidemia in 17.52%. Social activities varied widely, with 32.73% visiting friends (Social Activity 1), and 17.5% engaging in leisure activities like Mahjong or chess (Social Activity 2), showing higher participation rates. Helping others (Social Activity 3) had a participation rate of 15.03%, while exercising or dancing (Social Activity 4) and participating in community activities (Social Activity 5) had rates of 6.35% and 2.11%, respectively. Volunteering, training courses, and other social activities had lower participation rates of 3.06%, 0.71%, and 1.39%, respectively. Overall, participants favored visiting friends and leisure activities, with lower engagement in volunteering and training.

Figure 1 and Table S2-5 illustrates the association between social activities and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk analyzed through three progressively adjusted models. After adjusting for covariates, the impact of social activities on various CVDs varied. For heart disease, leisure activities (Social Activity 2), exercising or dancing (Social Activity 4), and community participation (Social Activity 5) were significantly associated with increased risk, with ORs of 1.10 (95% CI: 1.02, 1.19), 1.12 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.25), and 1.27 (95% CI: 1.05, 1.54), respectively (p < 0.05). In stroke analysis, visiting friends (Social Activity 1) showed a protective effect, with an OR of 0.87 (95% CI: 0.79, 0.97). Helping others (Social Activity 3) and exercising or dancing (Social Activity 4) also had similar protective effects, with ORs of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.66, 0.93) and 0.75 (95% CI: 0.60, 0.94), respectively. No significant associations were found for hypertension with most social activities. In the dyslipidemia analysis, all social activities were significantly associated, particularly community activities (Social Activity 5) and volunteering (Social Activity 6), which showed higher risks with ORs of 1.59 (95% CI: 1.34, 1.89) and 1.65 (95% CI: 1.43, 1.90). Overall, Model 3 results indicate significant differences in how social activities affect CVDs, with activities like exercise and helping others showing protective effects, while community and leisure activities may increase disease risk.

Associations between social activities and cardiovascular disease (heart disease, stroke, hypertension, dyslipidemia)

Figure 2 and Table S6 examines the impact of social activities on multiple CVDs (i.e., two or more CVDs) through progressively adjusted models. Model 3 findings indicate that community participation (Social Activity 5) and volunteering (Social Activity 6) are significantly associated with multiple CVDs, with ORs of 1.29 (95% CI: 1.08, 1.54) and 1.28 (95% CI: 1.10, 1.49). These activities may involve higher social pressures or physical burdens, increasing disease risk. Other social activities, such as visiting friends, playing Mahjong, and exercising, showed no significant associations, suggesting limited or no impact on disease risk in the context of multiple CVDs.

Fig. 2Associations between social activities and co-morbidity (two or more diseases)

Associations between social activities and co-morbidity (two or more diseases)

Stratified analysis results reveal significant differences in the impact of various social activities on CVD risk across populations (Table 2 and Table S7-13). Visiting friends (Social Activity 1) significantly increased the risk of dyslipidemia in men (OR = 1.28 [95% CI: 1.17, 1.40], p < 0.01), suggesting a potentially adverse effect. Leisure activities (Social Activity 2) had a protective effect against stroke in men (OR = 0.85 [95% CI: 0.74, 0.97], p = 0.04). Helping others (Social Activity 3) notably protected against stroke, especially in non-retired individuals (OR = 0.78 [95% CI: 0.66, 0.93], p < 0.01). Exercising or dancing (Social Activity 4) showed protective effects against stroke (OR = 0.75 [95% CI: 0.60, 0.94], p = 0.01), but was associated with increased dyslipidemia risk in women (OR = 1.41 [95% CI: 1.16, 1.73], p < 0.01) and increased heart disease risk in drinkers (OR = 1.27 [95% CI: 1.03, 1.57], p = 0.03). Community activities (Social Activity 5) significantly increased the risk of heart disease (OR = 1.27 [95% CI: 1.05, 1.54], p = 0.01) and dyslipidemia (OR = 1.59 [95% CI: 1.34, 1.89], p < 0.01), particularly among non-drinkers. Volunteering (Social Activity 6) was significantly associated with increased dyslipidemia risk (OR = 1.65 [95% CI: 1.43, 1.90], p < 0.01), especially among male smokers. Educational and training activities (Social Activity 7) showed no significant effects on most diseases. Other social activities (Social Activity 8) significantly affected dyslipidemia, with higher risk observed among men, drinkers, and smokers (OR = 1.71 [95% CI: 1.23, 2.37], p < 0.01). Overall, social activities have differential impacts on CVDs across populations, with some activities showing protective effects while others, like community involvement and volunteering, may increase disease risk.

Sensitivity analysis (Figure S2-4) confirmed that substituting logistic regression with linear regression and analyzing samples aged ≥ 45 and between 45 and 85 yielded results consistent with the original findings, indicating that variations in analytical methods and age stratifications had minimal impact, further validating the robustness and consistency of the study’s conclusions. When restricting analyses to participants without any cardiovascular disease at their first observation (n = 4987), the associations between social activities and CVD risk remained largely consistent with the main findings (Figure S5). These results suggest that reverse causality is unlikely to fully explain the observed associations.

Discussion

This study explored the impact of different social activities on the risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including heart disease, stroke, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, over an average follow-up of five years using cross-sectional data from the CHARLS database. The findings indicate that activities such as playing Mahjong or chess, exercising, dancing, participating in community groups, and engaging in volunteer work were associated with an increased risk of heart disease and dyslipidemia. Conversely, visiting friends, helping others, and exercising or dancing showed significant protective effects against stroke. Notably, visiting friends was linked to an increased risk of dyslipidemia in men, while playing Mahjong or chess was protective against stroke in men. Engaging in helping others, exercising, and dancing significantly reduced stroke risk but were associated with an increased risk of dyslipidemia and heart disease in women and individuals who consume alcohol. Overall, the impact of social activities on CVDs varied by activity type and individual characteristics.

Our findings emphasize the benefits of positive social interactions and physical activities on cardiovascular health, consistent with existing literature that highlights the positive effects of social participation, such as enhanced social connections and reduced feelings of loneliness [5, 21]. Previous studies have shown that older adults participating in activities like dancing and exercising have a significantly lower risk of stroke, aligning with our findings that these activities reduce stroke risk (about 25% reduction) [22]. Additionally, helping others, such as providing neighborly assistance, is widely recognized for reducing psychological stress and improving life satisfaction [23, 24], which also demonstrated significant protective effects against stroke in this study (OR = 0.78 [95% CI: 0.66, 0.93], p < 0.01). In contrast, activities like playing Mahjong, community involvement, and volunteer work were linked to increased risks of heart disease and dyslipidemia. These adverse associations may be mechanistically explained by the behavioral context in which these activities occur. Specifically, prolonged sedentary time during Mahjong sessions can impair glucose metabolism and promote lipid accumulation [25]. Late-night participation disrupts circadian rhythms and may trigger sympathetic nervous system activation, elevating blood pressure and heart disease risk. Furthermore, the unhealthy dietary patterns commonly observed during social gatherings—characterized by high-fat, high-sugar, and sodium-rich foods—directly contribute to dyslipidemia and hypertension [26, 27]. While community activities and volunteering can enhance social participation, frequent involvement or heavy responsibilities may lead to social stress and psychological burden, exacerbating health risks [28]. These findings underscore the complexity of how different social activities, participation frequency, and individual coping mechanisms impact health.

The potential mechanisms underlying these differential effects likely involve complex interactions between psychological and physiological factors. Activities like exercising and helping others not only increase physical activity levels but also reduce stress through endorphin release, improve mood, and promote cardiovascular health [29, 30]. Conversely, individuals participating in community and volunteer activities may face increased social pressure, time conflicts, and responsibility burdens, particularly when poorly organized or lacking support, which can trigger stress responses, elevate cortisol levels, and subsequently increase the risk of hypertension, heart disease, and dyslipidemia [31, 32]. The differences in health effects of social activities may reflect multidimensional influences of psychological and physiological mechanisms.

Stratified analyses revealed significant variations in the impact of social activities on CVDs among different populations. Activities such as visiting friends, helping others, exercising, and dancing were protective against stroke, particularly among men and non-retired individuals, likely due to reduced psychological stress, improved vascular function, and enhanced metabolism [24]. These gender differences may be attributed to distinct patterns of social activities and physiological responses. Men tend to engage in more physically intensive social activities, which may contribute to improved endothelial function and reduced inflammatory markers, key mechanisms in stroke prevention [33]. Furthermore, non-retired individuals may benefit from the combined effects of occupational physical activity and social activities, creating a synergistic protective effect against cerebrovascular events [34]. However, in women and those who consume alcohol, these activities were associated with increased risks of heart disease and dyslipidemia, potentially due to higher exercise intensity, unhealthy dietary behaviors, and stress responses induced by alcohol consumption [35, 36]. The increased cardiovascular risk observed in women may reflect the “double burden” phenomenon, where social activities add to existing caregiving responsibilities, leading to elevated stress hormones and disrupted metabolic homeostasis [37]. Among alcohol consumers, the paradoxical associations are particularly concerning from a mechanistic standpoint. Alcohol consumption during social activities can potentiate triglyceride elevation, impair hepatic lipid metabolism, and induce oxidative stress on myocardial tissue [38]. The combination of alcohol intake with sedentary social activities creates a synergistic adverse metabolic environment. Playing Mahjong or chess showed protective effects against stroke in men, but the accompanying behaviors, such as prolonged sitting, late nights, and irregular eating, might increase the risk of heart disease and dyslipidemia. These findings align with emerging evidence on sedentary behavior patterns in leisure activities. While cognitive engagement in games like Mahjong may enhance cerebral blood flow and reduce stroke risk through improved mental stimulation [39], the extended sedentary periods (often 4–6 h per session) significantly reduce energy expenditure and promote insulin resistance, key pathogenic factors for dyslipidemia and cardiovascular disease [40]. The metabolic disruption is further compounded by irregular meal timing and consumption of high-calorie snacks during gameplay. Community and volunteer activities were also associated with increased risks of heart disease and dyslipidemia among non-drinkers, reflecting the negative impact of high social pressure and unhealthy diets. This counterintuitive finding may be explained by the organizational demands and social obligations inherent in formal community participation, which can generate chronic stress and cortisol elevation, particularly among individuals who perceive these activities as obligatory rather than voluntary [41]. Additionally, communal eating patterns during such activities may promote consumption of high-calorie, lipid-rich foods, contributing to adverse metabolic profiles [42].

Despite providing valuable insights, this study has some limitations. First, as an observational study, it cannot definitively establish causality. Since our analysis included participants with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions to maximize sample size and representativeness, the findings reflect associations rather than strict incidence risk. Consequently, the possibility of reverse causality—where poor physical health restricts social activity participation—cannot be entirely ruled out. Furthermore, the potential for unmeasured confounding remains, as factors such as dietary quality, medication adherence, family history of CVD, and psychosocial stress levels were not comprehensively captured in our dataset. These unmeasured variables may partially explain the observed associations and limit causal inference. Second, sample selection bias may affect the generalizability of our findings. Our complete-case analysis approach, while ensuring data quality, resulted in substantial exclusions due to missing data. This non-random missingness may be associated with physical functional impairment or severe cognitive decline, potentially underrepresenting the most vulnerable populations and underestimating the true associations between social activities and cardiovascular outcomes. Third, data on both social activities and chronic disease status relied on self-reports. While this is a standard approach in large-scale surveys, it may introduce information bias, such as recall bias or the underreporting of undiagnosed conditions. Furthermore, due to the skewed distribution of frequency data for certain activities, we operationalized social activities as a binary variable. While this ensured statistical robustness, it precluded the assessment of potential dose-response relationships between activity intensity and cardiovascular outcomes. Future research should explore the specific mechanisms of social activities, incorporating biomarkers and mental health assessments to better elucigate the pathways through which social behaviors influence CVDs. Additionally, interventional studies could help identify which social activities and participation frequencies are most beneficial for cardiovascular health, providing empirical support for public health policies.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complex impact of different social activities on cardiovascular disease risk, emphasizing the need for personalized and contextualized health interventions. The results indicate that positive social interactions, such as exercising, dancing, and helping others, provide significant protection against stroke, while activities like playing Mahjong, community involvement, and volunteering may increase the risk of heart disease and dyslipidemia under certain conditions. Based on these findings, we recommend targeted strategies for CVD prevention: (1) promoting physically active social activities while educating on healthy modifications during sedentary activities; (2) implementing structured breaks and healthier food options in community gatherings; and (3) tailoring activity recommendations based on individual risk profiles (sex, retirement status, alcohol consumption) through personalized healthcare counseling. These findings suggest that future research should delve deeper into the mechanisms by which social behaviors affect cardiovascular health through psychological, behavioral, and physiological pathways, and design diverse intervention strategies to offer more precise guidance for the prevention and management of CVDs.
