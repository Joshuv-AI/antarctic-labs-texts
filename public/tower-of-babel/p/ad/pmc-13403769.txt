Vaccination as a Treatment for Patients With CVD
Authors: Paul Allen Heidenreich
Published: 2026

In this issue of JACC: Advances, we hear from 3 experts on vaccination as a treatment for patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD). We learn of the growing randomized trial data on influenza vaccine benefit, the important role of the cardiology clinician in vaccination, and how these are incorporated into recent clinical guidance on vaccination from the ACC.

Vaccination represents one of the most underutilized preventive strategies in cardiovascular medicine. Despite mounting evidence that vaccines can reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, vaccination rates among cardiac patients remain suboptimal. Cardiologists and other cardiology clinicians are uniquely positioned to address this gap, serving as trusted advisors who can both recommend and facilitate vaccination for their high-risk patients.1

The evidence for vaccination initially was observational. In this issue, Vardeny2 notes that randomized trials are increasingly used to show vaccine effectiveness. The greatest evidence base is for influenza vaccine, with meta-analyses now clearly showing CVD benefit of vaccination.

In 2025, 2 mega-trials (of >460,000 patients aged ≥65 years), when combined, showed that high-dose influenza vaccine improves CVD outcomes compared with standard-dose influenza vaccine.3 These trials were remarkable in that they showed that such trials can be conducted on a large scale. A huge trial population is needed to show that a minimal cost/small benefit intervention works, is cost-effective, and should be implemented across the health system. Even though the per-patient benefit is small, the population impact is great given the large number of patients who can benefit from vaccination.

The benefits of vaccination extend beyond prevention of acute respiratory illness. We now have evidence of inflammation as the connection between viral infection and CVD as noted by Bhatt.4 Contemporary understanding of atherosclerosis recognizes inflammation as a central driver of CVD initiation and progression. Infections trigger acute inflammatory responses that can destabilize atherosclerotic plaques, increase thrombotic risk, and precipitate acute cardiovascular events.5 Beyond these acute effects, chronic low-grade inflammation from recurrent or persistent viral infections may accelerate progression of atherosclerosis. The surprising CVD benefits from the zoster vaccine are consistent with the idea that vaccination can suppress inflammation and in turn reduce CVD events.6,7 This anti-inflammatory mechanism likely contributes to the benefits of influenza vaccination as well.

The growing clinical trial database and our advancing knowledge of inflammation as the link between viral infection and cardiovascular events should convince cardiovascular clinicians that vaccination is an important part of cardiac care. Unfortunately, despite strong evidence supporting vaccination, implementation remains problematic, as outlined by Dr Nazir.8 National data consistently show that fewer than one-half of adults with CVD receive annual influenza vaccination, and pneumococcal and zoster vaccination rates are even lower. This represents a significant failure to translate evidence into practice by our health care system.

Cardiology clinicians can uniquely address this gap. Nazir8 emphasizes that a physician recommendation is a strong predictor of vaccine acceptance. Cardiac patients often view their cardiologist as their primary trusted health advisor. The cardiology clinic also provides a practical venue for vaccination. Many cardiac patients see their cardiology clinicians more frequently than they see their primary care clinicians, particularly during the period after acute events when vaccination benefits may be greatest. Clinic-based vaccination programs eliminate logistical barriers; they capitalize on teachable moments when cardiovascular risk is salient, allowing for high rates of vaccination. Several health systems have successfully implemented cardiology clinic–based vaccination programs, showing feasibility and dramatic improvements in vaccination rates. These programs typically involve standing orders, designated nursing staff, and electronic health record prompts to identify unvaccinated patients.

Recognizing both the evidence base and the opportunity for cardiologists to improve vaccination rates, Bhatt4 discusses the recently published comprehensive guidance on vaccination for cardiovascular patients by the ACC. The ACC guidance recommends annual influenza vaccination for all patients with CVD, with preference for high-dose or adjuvanted formulations in adults aged ≥65 years, consistent with the FLUNITY-HD findings noted by Vardeny.1 The ACC document also recommends vaccination for pneumococcal, COVID-19, respiratory syncytial virus, and zoster infection and provides guidance for dealing with vaccine hesitancy. Importantly, the guidance addresses practical implementation, encouraging cardiologists to directly offer vaccines in clinic settings, utilize standing orders, and incorporate vaccination status into routine clinical assessments.

The evidence supporting vaccination as a cardiovascular intervention is compelling and growing. Randomized trials have shown clear benefits in reducing cardiovascular events, and research has elucidated anti-inflammatory pathways underlying these benefits. Cardiology clinicians must seize this opportunity to improve outcomes for their patients by becoming active vaccine advocates and facilitators. By integrating vaccination into routine cardiovascular care, cardiology care teams can achieve meaningful reductions in cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, improving the length and quality of life for patients with heart disease.
