Characteristics of Patients with Myasthenia Gravis in the French Rare Disease Registry
Authors: Jean‐Philippe Camdessanche, Andoni Echaniz‐Laguna, Guilhem Solé, Cécile Blein, Mariana Ciumas, Claude Messiaen, Anne‐Sophie Jannot, Shahram Attarian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe French National Rare Diseases Registry (BNDMR) was established in 2007 to ensure access to optimal care standards for all patients with rare diseases in dedicated reference centres. The objective of this retrospective cohort study was to describe patients with myasthenia gravis (MG) in the BNDMR.MethodsAll patients aged ≥ 18 years in the BNDMR with a confirmed diagnosis of MG visiting a reference centre at least once between 2007 and 2021 (inclusive) were included. Diagnosis was defined through ORPHA:589 or ORPHA:391490 disease codes. Patients were followed for ≥ 12 months until 31 December 2022 (or until death). Data were collected on demographics and disease history. Mortality was estimated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Healthcare resource utilisation at the reference centre was documented.ResultsOverall, 3963 patients were analysed. Mean follow‐up duration was 6.1 ± 3.8 years. The median interval between diagnosis and inclusion was 3.8 months [IQR: 1.5–7.5] and the median age at symptom onset was 52.0 [IQR: 34.0–69.0] years. Survival probability was 82.7% at 10 years, and higher in women than men (p < 0.001; logrank test). The mean interval between visits was 4.1 months. The mean number of overnight or day hospitalisations per patient was 2.6 ± 3.4 and the mean number of physician consultations per patient per year was 1.7 ± 1.1.ConclusionThis national registry study provides reference data for patients in France with a confirmed diagnosis of MG. However, all patients with MG are still not managed in dedicated reference centres.Keywords: epidemiology, healthcare resource utilisation, mortality, myasthenia gravis, patient registry

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is a rare autoimmune disease in which auto‐antibodies targeting the neuromuscular junction impair synaptic transmission to voluntary muscles, causing muscle weakness [1]. As with all rare diseases, collecting national‐level data on the epidemiology, natural history and management is challenging due to difficulties in assembling large, representative samples. A meta‐analysis of available studies published in 2025 estimated the worldwide prevalence of MG at 173 cases per million [2]. Prevalence is higher in men than in women and increased with age [3]. In France, studies in the national administrative claims database (SNDS) have reported prevalence rates of 234 [4] and 342 [5] cases per million, the difference between the two estimates being probably due to the restrictiveness of the case identification criteria in each study. Database studies such as these, and others conducted in Germany [6], Italy [7], China [8] and the USA [9] can provide standardised data on large patient populations but also have limitations. Two important limitations are the lack of data on disease severity and treatment rationale, and imperfect coding quality. These limitations may be particularly problematic for rare diseases with which physicians are less familiar and for which a definitive diagnosis may take time to establish. A particular issue for MG may be identification of seronegative patients before a definitive diagnosis is made.

National patient registries, in which all diagnoses are confirmed, provide an attractive alternative. Such registries have existed for decades in Nordic countries, where they have provided comprehensive information on the epidemiology and burden of MG [10, 11, 12], and are also established in the United States [13] and Japan [14]. Subsequently, MG registries have been initiated in other European countries [15, 16], and the pooling of data from these registries to create a comprehensive European MG registry is under discussion [17, 18]. Large registries for rare diseases did not exist in France until 2007, when the Health Ministry established the French National Rare Diseases Registry (BNDMR), to ensure optimal care access for all patients with rare diseases [19, 20]. The BNDMR provides a robust data source for studies of a large cohort of patients with MG. Since all diagnoses are validated at reference centres before patient inclusion, data reliability is higher than in insurance claims databases. This retrospective cohort study aimed to describe the characteristics of patients with a confirmed diagnosis of MG in the BNDMR.

Methods

The BNDMR is managed by regional reference centres throughout the country designated by the French Health Ministry. In principle, care of all patients with rare diseases should be managed through these reference centres. All patients with these rare diseases are eligible for entry into the BNDMR. The disease is identified through the appropriate Orphanet ORPHA code and each patient is are assigned an identification number (IdMR) which can be used to track them if they receive care across different sites [21]. The IdMR can also be used to identify deaths in the French national statistics database (INSEE). A common data interface is used to collect the same information across all rare diseases. In the case of neuromuscular diseases, patient care is delivered in sixty reference centres, and organised through eight coordinating centres (FILNEMUS network).

All patients with MG in the BNDMR between 1 January 2007 and 31 December 2021 (the date of the most recent consolidated and validated dataset at the time of the analysis) were identified through the Orphanet ORPHA codes ORPHA:589 (acquired myasthenia; autoimmune myasthenia gravis) or ORPHA:391490 (adult‐onset myasthenia gravis). Patients were required to have a confirmed diagnosis from a neurologist in the reference centre, which requires the presence of fatigable and fluctuating muscle weakness confirmed by detection of antiAchR or Nati‐MuSK antibodies, electromyographic evidence of impairment of neuromuscular transmission, or response to pharmacological challenge [22]. Patients were required to have visited a reference centre at least once during the inclusion period for a consultation, hospitalisation, diagnostic work‐up or other MG‐related activity. Patients were excluded if they were not managed through the FILNEMUS network, if they were aged < 18 years (or if the first documented symptoms appeared before the age of 16 years), if the diagnosis of MG had not been confirmed, or if the patient did not want their personal data to be reused for research. Patients who died during the observation period were identified by linkage to the INSEE database.

The date of diagnosis of MG was documented if this was not the date of inclusion into the database, as well as the date of first symptoms. The patients were followed for a minimum of 12 months until 31 December 2022 (or until they died). Data were collected on demographics, diagnosis history and death. Mortality was estimated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. All interactions of patients with the reference centre (visits, hospitalisations or consultations) were documented.

Quantitative variables were described using mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range. Prevalence was estimated by taking the number of prevalent cases on 31 December 2021 divided by the French population at the same date. Regional prevalence rates were standardised for age and sex from the French population. Mortality was estimated using Kaplan–Meier estimates and survival between men and women compared with the log‐rank test. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the survival and survminer packages.

This study was approved by the BNDMR Scientific Committee (ORRP number: IRB00013741), complied with EU general data protection regulations, and was conducted in accordance with French MR‐004 reference methodology. Patients were informed about the re‐use of their data for research purposes and a detailed information sheet was published on the BNDMR website (www.bndmr.fr). Only patients not objecting to re‐use of their data for research purposes were enrolled in the study, and patient data were anonymised prior to the analyses reported here.

Results

During the inclusion period, 5165 patients with an ORPHA code for MG were identified in the BNDMR database, of whom 3963 patients met the eligibility criteria and constituted the analysis population. Of these 434 had died before 31 December 2022. The mean follow‐up duration of the analysis population was 6.1 ± 3.8 years (median: 5.7 years [IQR: 3.0–8.8]). At inclusion, 3357 patients (84.7%) had already been diagnosed with MG; the median interval between diagnosis and inclusion was 3.8 months (interquartile range: 1.5–7.5). The median age at symptom onset, documented for 2694 patients, was 52.0 [IQR: 34.0–69.0] years. The mean age at diagnosis, documented for 2882 patients (72.7%), was 51.2 ± 19.2 years.

Based on these data, the point prevalence of MG on 31 December 2021 was 52.2 cases/million [95% CI: 50.5–53.9]. A North‐West to South‐East gradient in prevalence was observed across the country, with the lowest prevalence rates being observed in Normandy (16.0 [11.7–21.9]) and Britanny (26.7 [21.0–33.8]), and the highest in Provence (128.5 [117.9–140.1]) and Corsica (141.5 [102.4–194.7]). The incidence in 2021 was estimated at 3.4 cases/million [95% CI: 3.0–4.0].

The mean age of patients at inclusion was 58.0 ± 18.0 years and 2208 patients were women (55.7%). In general, men were older than women at the time of inclusion (mean age: 62 ± 16 years in men and 55 ± 19 years in women). However, the gender distribution was age dependent, with women outnumbering men up to 65 years, and men being more numerous thereafter.

Discussion

This study describes a large cohort of patients with a confirmed diagnosis of MG and managed in a neuromuscular disease reference centre from the French national BNDMR rare diseases registry. One of the objectives of the national Rare Diseases Plan of 2007 was to facilitate rapid diagnosis after symptom onset and to avoid ‘roaming’ between physicians before the diagnosis is made. We observed that the median interval between diagnosis and inclusion in the registry was 3.8 months. Patients visit the reference centre on average every 4 months, but teleconsultations were only provided to a small minority of patients. Wider access to teleconsultations may help bring more patients with MG into the orbit of reference centres, particularly for those who live large distances away from a centre.

The estimated prevalence in the BNDMR (2 cases/million) is much lower than estimates from the two studies performed in the French national medico‐administrative claims database (SNDS) (342 and 234 cases per million) [4, 5] and this probably reflects the fact that many patients with MG are still not being followed in reference centres. For this reason, prevalence and incidence rates are underestimated but are expected to rise as the registry matures. Since the database was opened in 2007, patient numbers have been increasing by around 10 cases per million every 2 years, and recruitment shows no sign of saturation [23]. The situation is different to that in Nordic countries, where disease‐specific registries are linked to national healthcare administrative databases, ensuring more complete patient inclusion, and more accurate determination of prevalence rates (e.g., 186/million in Denmark, 209 in Finland, and 23.4 in Sweden) [11]. On the other hand, in Japan, where healthcare administrative databases do not provide universal coverage, the number of cases identified in these sources is lower than the number of patients in the Japanese national patient registry [24].

There is likely to be some severity bias in inclusion, patients with mild forms of rare diseases are less likely to be followed in reference centres [25]. It was nevertheless reassuring that the demographics of the study populations in terms of age, gender and regional distribution were similar between the BNDMR and SNDS studies [4]. Around half of the patients had early‐onset disease (onset at < 50 years), one quarter late‐onset disease (50–64 years), and one quarter very late onset disease (≥ 65 years); patients with juvenile‐onset disease were not included.

Five‐year survival was 92.2% in the present study, compared to 87.0% in the SNDS sample [4]; although comparisons between the two studies should be interpreted with caution, due to differences in definitions, this may indicate that patients managed in reference centres have a better outcome than those who are managed elsewhere. Overall survival (92.2% at 5 years and 82.7% at 10 years) was higher than reported in the Danish, Swedish and Finnish patient registries (84% at 5 years and 70% at 10 years) [11], or in a single centre registry from Israel (86% at 5 years and79% at 10 years) [26]. In contrast to the Nordic registries, we found mortality to be higher in men than in women, but this is probably artefactual since men with MG are typically older than women, and our analysis was not adjusted for age. In the Nordic registries, mortality is presented as standardised mortality rates (SMR) which are age‐adjusted [11]. In the French SNDS study [4], the SMR was also higher in women than in men, but again survival rates were higher than that reported in the Nordic registries.

French guidelines for management of MG specify that patients who are symptomatic or with poorly controlled disease should see their neurologist in the reference centre at least twice a year, and patients who are asymptomatic and well‐controlled should consult annually [22]. In the BNDMR, patients consulted their neurologists on average 1.7 times a year. Although clinical data on disease control is missing, this frequency would be consistent with these guidelines. Neurologist consultation frequency is lower than previously reported in the USA, where patients see their neurologist for their MG on average over three times a year [9]. In five European countries (France, Germany, Italy, Spain and the United Kingdom), the proportion of patients consulting neurologists was very similar across countries [27]. Over the follow‐up period of median duration of 9 years, 12.9% of patients were hospitalised overnight, although these patients were hospitalised on average 2.2 times a year. These would probably represent patients with poorly controlled disease. This number was identical to the mean number of MG‐related hospitalisations per patient·year in a recent hospital database study performed in England, although the proportion of patients who were hospitalised in the English study (36.8% over 10 years) was higher [28]. In the USA, the mean number of MG‐related hospitalisations per patient·year was somewhat higher (4.9% in 2019) [29].

A limitation of the study is that the BNDMR only collects data common to all rare diseases, so disease‐specific information such as antibody testing is missing. In the future, the participation of French FILEMUS reference centres in the proposed European MG database [17, 18] should provide a resource for collating disease‐specific information on patients with MG.

Future research in the BNDMR could usefully focus on how patients with MG are managed in France in order to describe the standard of care, and document any gap between care provided in expert reference centres and that available in everyday practice in France. Further research into mortality is also important, and this may help resolve the issue of whether patients with MG who receive the best standard of care have an increased mortality risk compared to the general population.

In conclusion, this national registry study provides reference data for a cohort of patients in France with a confirmed diagnosis of MG and showed that the characteristics of these patients were consistent with previous data from other sources.
