Smart Community Hypertension Management: A Narrative Review of Promises and Challenges
Authors: Manhong Yang, Long Wu, Yuzhen Zeng, Chengxia Zhao, Huiling Ye, Jianfei Wang, Ruotong Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertension control remains a significant challenge in primary care worldwide. Conventional community management, which relies on manual follow-ups and scattered records, often leads to inefficiencies and inequities. The emergence of mobile health, Internet of Things devices, and artificial intelligence offers transformative solutions. This narrative review synthesizes recent evidence on smart hypertension management in community settings. Here, we identified three dominant management approaches. One approach uses mobile health systems to support patient self-management. Another employs remote monitoring platforms to enable timely clinical interventions. A third applies artificial intelligence tools to automate follow-up processes. Evidence indicates these smart models can lead to clinically meaningful improvements. For instance, remote monitoring interventions have been associated with improved blood pressure control rates, in some cases by 15–25 percentage points. They also optimize resource allocation, particularly for underserved groups. However, key challenges persist, including the digital divide and data security concerns. This review synthesizes evidence demonstrating that smart management models can yield measurable, quantitative improvements in blood pressure control and resource efficiency. Future efforts must therefore be guided by this evidence, prioritizing large-scale trials to strengthen the findings and developing supportive policies on equity and data governance to ensure these solutions are effective, equitable, and secure.Keywords: hypertension, community health services, digital health, smart management, primary health care

Introduction

Hypertension is one of the most common chronic noncommunicable diseases and a major risk factor for serious cardiovascular events such as stroke, coronary heart disease, and heart failure. According to the Global Hypertension Report by the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately one-third of adults worldwide currently suffer from elevated blood pressure, and the majority do not achieve adequate control.1 In China, the number of adults with hypertension has reached 245 million, and awareness, treatment, and control rates remain suboptimal, with particularly poor control among rural populations and older adults.2

Hypertension typically has an insidious onset, a long disease course, serious complications, and multiple interacting risk factors. These characteristics mean that long-term management cannot rely on hospital outpatient care alone, and instead requires sustained involvement from community and primary healthcare institutions. Within this system, community health centers shoulder responsibilities such as hypertension screening, establishing patient records and follow-ups, health education, and comprehensive interventions. They play a pivotal role in the tiered diagnosis and treatment system and chronic disease management framework, serving as a crucial link between higher-level institutions and grassroots communities.

Currently, community hypertension management still relies primarily on manual follow-ups and paper-based records. Information remains scattered across various forms and files, with slow updates making it difficult to accurately reflect current blood pressure status.3 Also, patient education tends to consist of one-off, centralized lectures that lack specificity and fail to provide sustained behavioral interventions.4 Meanwhile, quality healthcare resources are concentrated in large cities and major hospitals, leaving primary care settings with relatively weak foundations in standardized diagnosis and treatment, individualized management, and information technology infrastructure.5 During the COVID-19 pandemic, these structural weaknesses became more pronounced, with a sharp decline in the frequency of hypertension assessments across many regions and a reduction in the number of patients commencing treatment.6 Collectively, these factors present substantial challenges to community hypertension management, particularly with respect to efficiency, quality, and equity.

Over the past decade, technologies such as Internet of Things (IoT) devices, big data analytics and artificial intelligence have advanced rapidly. Smart blood pressure monitors, wearable devices and mobile applications have progressively entered households, enabling patients to take multiple readings throughout daily life with results automatically uploaded to cloud platforms. Healthcare institutions can then collate and analyze this data, gaining clearer insights into blood pressure trends and alerting patients to schedule follow-up appointments or adjust treatment plans when necessary.7 Telephone follow-ups, video consultations, and online platforms are increasingly being used in hypertension management across certain regions. Studies indicate that these digital interventions hold promise for improving blood pressure control, enhancing treatment adherence and providing new tools for better resource allocation.8 In essence, smart technologies herald a paradigm shift in hypertension care, moving from reactive, visit-based care to proactive, continuous management. Their potential impact stems from their ability to fundamentally restructure care delivery: mobile applications empower patient self-management, connected devices enable seamless remote monitoring, and artificial intelligence unlocks data-driven personalization and efficiency. This convergence promises to enhance control rates, optimize resource use, and ultimately deliver more precise and preventive care in the community. Crucially, the most promising applications of these technologies are those designed to simplify, not complicate, the workflows for both patients and providers. Moreover, their success is often amplified when integrated with human touchpoints. For instance, technology can automate data collection and initial alerts, but the presence and guidance of a community nurse or health worker remain irreplaceable for providing tailored education, building trust, and offering the psychosocial support that fosters true patient comfort and long-term engagement. This human-in-the-loop approach ensures that technological efficiency translates into effective and compassionate care.

Despite these promising findings, the overall evidence base is still limited. Many studies with small sample sizes and short follow-up periods have focused on individual digital tools rather than comprehensive management models.9 There remains insufficient evidence regarding the performance of different smart management models within community and primary healthcare settings, particularly regarding their impact on equity, privacy protection and long-term sustainability. Consequently, addressing how to enhance monitoring efficiency and management quality while ensuring equitable access and data security represents a critical challenge for smart community hypertension management.

To address these gaps, we conducted a narrative review. This methodological choice was deliberate, as smart community hypertension management is an emerging and rapidly evolving field characterized by heterogeneous interventions and varying stages of evidence. A narrative review allows for the flexible and comprehensive synthesis needed to map this complex landscape, explore diverse conceptual models, and identify overarching themes and future directions, rather than focusing solely on quantifying the effect size of a single, standardized intervention.10 Our review synthesizes literature published between 2015 and 2024 on digital and smart interventions in community and primary care settings. We summarized the main management models, examined their effects on blood pressure control, patient self-management and service delivery, and identified common obstacles including the digital divide, data security and cost. This review aims to clarify the conceptual and technological basis of smart community hypertension management and to provide directions for future research, policy and primary care practice.

Challenges in Conventional Community Hypertension Management

In many countries and regions, community hypertension follow-up remains predominantly manual. Blood pressure readings, symptoms, and medication adherence are recorded on paper forms or entered into electronic documents in an unstructured manner.3 Due to the lack of standardization, information is often incomplete and difficult to retrieve. To assess a patient’s long-term blood pressure control, clinicians must sift through extensive records. This makes systematic analysis challenging, and the data is difficult to translate into actionable management decisions.

Follow-up content is similarly rudimentary. In practice, many community consultations focus primarily on current blood pressure readings and prescription adherence.9 Important lifestyle factors such as diet, exercise, sleep, and psychological stress are often inadequately recorded and monitored over time. Furthermore, health education is mostly delivered as one-time guidance, with insufficient subsequent personalized support. Research indicates that issues such as patients arbitrarily reducing or discontinuing medication, irregular self-monitoring of blood pressure, and inadequate understanding of target blood pressure levels and the importance of long-term control are prevalent, particularly among populations with lower health literacy.11

Currently, most of the high-quality medical resources and information systems are concentrated in large hospitals. However, the routine management of hypertensive patients is centered on primary healthcare institutions.12 Primary care facilities in rural and remote areas not only face shortages in healthcare personnel but also suffer from inadequate continuing education provision and weak information technology infrastructure.5 Despite national guidelines for chronic disease management, implementation intensity and management effectiveness vary significantly across regions, with some areas struggling to fully implement the required standards.13,14

Smart management has high expectations, yet its fairness issues cannot be overlooked.14 On one hand, many elderly patients experience declining vision, hearing, or fine motor skills, making it difficult for them to learn and operate new technologies. They also lack trust in smartphones and online services, often avoiding their use. Without assistance from family members or the community, they struggle to benefit from digital services. Although smart services may be feasible at the institutional level, their daily use often proves unsustainable due to high usage costs.15 Therefore, the possible marginalization of vulnerable groups during digital transformation should be prioritized when advancing smart community management.

Conceptual Framework and Technological Foundation of Smart Management

Objectives and Implementation Framework

The purpose of smart community management is not simply to upgrade equipment, but to use information technology to address the limitations of traditional care. It will improve blood pressure monitoring, data utilization, follow-up methods, and doctor-patient communication, thereby driving overall management enhancement.16

During routine blood pressure monitoring, smart blood pressure monitors and wearable devices enable patients to easily take measurements at home or during daily activities. This data is automatically transmitted via networks to community or regional platforms.17 Compared to relying only on clinic measurements, multiple home readings better reflect periodic blood pressure levels. Additionally, they facilitate the detection of easily overlooked abnormal indicators such as morning hypertension spikes and nocturnal blood pressure elevations.

During data processing, the platform aggregates blood pressure readings across different time periods. It not only generates intuitive trend charts but also flags individuals with persistently elevated blood pressure or significant fluctuations. The platform can also conduct risk assessments based on big data analysis, integrating patients’ blood pressure records, medication history, and complication data.16 These analytical insights provide reliable references for physicians to determine appropriate follow-up frequencies and prioritize interventions.

Regarding service delivery, smart management offers multiple communication channels including phone, video, and online platforms.17 Some patient concerns can be addressed remotely, with in-person visits required only when physical examinations or prescription adjustments are necessary. This proves particularly valuable for individuals with limited mobility or demanding work schedules.

Overall, smart management can be broadly divided into three interconnected stages. These are data collection, information analysis and risk assessment, and the joint development or adjustment of treatment plans by doctors and patients. As new measurement and follow-up data continuously enter the system, they provide the basis for subsequent assessments, forming a dynamic management cycle of iterative adjustments.

Key Technologies and Application Directions

The technological foundation for smart community hypertension management centers around the use of IoT devices, big data analytics, and artificial intelligence.18

Specifically, IoT devices have seen widespread adoption, with smart home blood pressure monitors gradually becoming commonplace across multiple countries and regions.19 After patients measure their blood pressure at home according to prescribed frequencies, the devices automatically record and upload the data, reducing instances of missed or incorrect entries while ensuring greater data integrity.

Big data analytics can precisely identify populations at high risk from vast follow-up datasets. The platform generates personalized blood pressure trend reports for specific groups or individuals, enabling clinicians to swiftly pinpoint patients with persistently uncontrolled blood pressure or irregular monitoring.16 In community settings, this screening approach allows limited follow-up time to be utilized more effectively for populations requiring intensive intervention.

Regarding the application of artificial intelligence, current efforts mainly focus on voice follow-up and automated questionnaire processing. Artificial intelligence can use speech recognition and natural language processing technologies to conduct standardized inquiries and automatically generate structured records.18 Artificial intelligence can also enable risk prediction and continuous blood pressure estimation through machine learning, allowing for more frequent and less intrusive monitoring. Although the relevant technologies are still evolving, they have demonstrated considerable potential in pilot projects.

Currently, three types of core practice pathways have emerged for the intelligent management of hypertension in communities. The first type centers on mobile applications and mini programs, primarily supporting patient self-management and health education. The second focuses on remote home blood pressure monitoring and IoT platforms, to improve the quality of blood pressure monitoring. The third type incorporates artificial intelligence for follow-up care and information processing, aiming to promote follow-up efficiency without significantly increasing labor costs. Subsequent chapters will provide further overviews based on these classifications.

Smart Management Models in Community Hypertension Care

In practice, digital health tools often work together. However, we can group the main smart management models into three types based on their main goal.

Mobile Network Systems for Patient Self-Management

This model uses mobile apps to help patients manage their own health. Its main goal is to give patients the tools and knowledge for self-care. With the widespread adoption of smartphones, management models based on mobile networks have become the most prevalent category in community smart healthcare practices.

Patients use mobile applications or mini programs to record or automatically synchronize blood pressure readings, set medication reminders, and receive health information and personalized recommendations pushed by the system.20,21

A randomized controlled trial in Chinese community populations further supports the feasibility of this model. Chen et al22 conducted an integrated intervention combining Internet with healthcare prevention among elderly hypertensive patients. Besides routine follow-ups, the intervention group received additional online education and communication modules, resulting in significantly higher knowledge awareness and blood pressure control rates compared to the control group. Cai et al23 provided an integrated intervention combining traditional Chinese medicine health management with Internet model. After three months of intervention, patients demonstrated significantly better symptom scores and quality of life compared to the control group, suggesting that this management model based on mobile networks could enhance community hypertension management to a certain extent and hold broader public health application value.

The primary advantage of the mobile network management model lies in its relatively straightforward deployment. Since most residents already own smartphones, community organizations need only provide necessary training and support. Additionally, this model offers flexible content delivery, enabling tiered distribution of different contents based on patient age, comorbidities, and educational level. However, this approach also faces certain challenges. Smartphone usage among elderly patients is limited, with some relying entirely on their children for device operation. On the other hand, complex interfaces or excessive steps can negatively impact user experience and reduce adherence rates. Also, overly frequent information pushes may provoke annoyance, leading patients to proactively disable notifications. Therefore, continuous adjustments to content design and push strategies are essential to ensure the application provides sufficient information without causing undue disruption.

Remote Monitoring Systems for Clinical Intervention

This model focuses on giving health care teams patient data so they can take timely action. It creates a connected system between the patient’s home and the clinic. Remote monitoring and intervention systems offer novel solutions for community hypertension management. These platforms utilize wearable devices for real-time patient blood pressure monitoring with automatic cloud data upload, enabling clinicians to conduct remote interventions.24 This model not only reduces patient hospital visit frequency but also improves healthcare service efficiency, a critical consideration for overburdened public health systems.

The remote monitoring and intervention model is based on smart blood pressure monitors and an IoT platform. Measurement activities are conducted at home and then connected to the community team via the platform. In short, patients use online home blood pressure monitors under the guidance of community doctors, taking measurements at home at the agreed frequency. The platform automatically organizes blood pressure curves and statistical metrics, flagging persistently elevated readings and significant fluctuations. Community physicians or nurses review alerts based on preset rules, follow up with patients exhibiting poor control or prolonged measurement gaps, and adjust medication or intensify lifestyle interventions as needed.

Several studies have confirmed that remote monitoring combined with primary care management can significantly improve blood pressure control. Huang et al25 and Duan et al26 both found that patients using remote monitoring systems experienced greater reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared to control groups, with blood pressure control rates increasing by 15 to 25 percentage points. A randomized controlled trial based on IoT for community management enrolled 459 patients. After 9 months of follow-up, the intervention group demonstrated more significant blood pressure reductions, with rural patients showing particularly pronounced benefits.17 It is worth noting that rural patients may derive greater benefits, highlighting the potential of this model in addressing the gap in healthcare between urban and rural areas.

For patients, remote monitoring reduces the time cost of traveling to and from medical institutions, allowing measurements to be integrated into daily life and facilitating the development of self-monitoring habits. As for community teams, this model supports adjusting follow-up strategies based on risk levels, enabling more time to be devoted to managing poorly controlled or high-risk patients and improving follow-up efficiency.14 However, the remote monitoring model imposes higher demands on equipment investment, network infrastructure, and system maintenance capabilities. Poorly designed alert strategies may generate excessive notifications, increasing physician workload. Procuring equipment and ensuring long-term operation require stable funding sources, posing a practical challenge for regions with limited economic resources.

Artificial Intelligence for System Efficiency

This model uses artificial intelligence to make health care work more efficient. It can work on its own to do tasks like follow-up calls, or it can be added to other systems to help analyze data. Models of artificial intelligence assistance primarily focus on two aspects, namely optimizing processes for follow-up and implementing novel methods for information collection.27

During follow-up, some community health service centers have adopted artificial intelligence voice systems to replace part of the manual telephone follow-up. The system automatically dials calls based on a preset questionnaire, inquiring about recent blood pressure readings, subjective symptoms, and medication adherence. Patients provide feedback via keypad input or brief responses.28 Subsequently, the system automatically generates structured records and flags anomalies, identifying these as abnormal cases requiring physician attention.

Studies indicate that artificial intelligence follow-ups achieve comparable call connection rates and information completeness to manual follow-ups, with slightly shorter call durations.29 This suggests that integrating artificial intelligence with manual verification may offer potential for labor savings.

In terms of data collection, some studies have employed machine learning methods such as support vector regression to construct noninvasive continuous blood pressure estimation models based on signals like pulse waves.30 While these techniques remain in the methodological validation phase, successful implementation could position them as complementary tools for high-frequency, low-interference monitoring in the future.31

These artificial intelligence tools show potential for improving workflow efficiency and enhancing data collection.32 However, most remain small-scale pilot studies, and further research and standardization are needed regarding clinical efficacy, model generalization capabilities, as well as ethical and privacy concerns.33

Advantages and Limitations of Smart Management Models

Advantages

Firstly, intelligent devices such as smartwatches and blood pressure monitors make it possible to swiftly track blood pressure fluctuations. This capability supports the early identification of abnormal readings, enabling patients to take prompt action and lower their risk of hypertension-related cardiovascular diseases.34 Studies have found that patients using these devices tend to achieve better control over their blood pressure and related symptoms, which not only improves their quality of life but may also help reduce long-term public health costs.31 Clinical evidence further supports the value of digital hypertension management applications. These tools have been shown to lower blood pressure effectively across a range of patients, with particularly marked benefits for older adults and those on medication.35

Secondly, by harnessing data analytics, smart management models enable a more personalized approach to care. Healthcare teams are leveraging big data and artificial intelligence to gain deeper insights from patient health information. This enables them to pinpoint individual factors influencing blood pressure and tailor treatment plans accordingly. For instance, patients can log their diet, exercise, and other habits via smartphone apps. Care teams then use this information to fine-tune treatments, which improves both adherence and outcomes.36 Simply put, once patients can make sense of their health data, they can make better decisions with their doctors and manage their condition more proactively. This is the core goal of modern public health work.

Another key feature of these models is their capacity for remote intervention, which lessens the need for frequent hospital visits. Via remote monitoring and virtual consultations, clinicians can keep abreast of a patient’s condition and deliver timely recommendations.37 This approach also promotes health equity by being particularly beneficial for older adults and residents of remote or underserved communities. It helps foster greater involvement and motivation in these groups.38

Also, compared to traditional manual follow-up and paper records, smart models significantly enhance efficiency and equity. In one 12-month study of a digital self-management program for hypertension, participants saw clinically meaningful drops in systolic blood pressure, pointing to sustained benefits over time.39

Ultimately, smart management models redefine hypertension care by integrating real-time monitoring, precision analysis, and remote support. This creates a continuous process where data guides decisions and actions, shifting the focus from reactive treatment to proactive prevention. This change empowers patients, optimizes resources, and reduces the disease burden, thereby establishing a viable model for broader application to other chronic conditions in public health.

Implementation Limitations

Despite numerous advantages, smart management models face significant limitations, such as accessibility, security, and long-term sustainability.

A primary obstacle is the digital divide, which disproportionately affects older adults. Many elderly patients struggle with the technological demands of these systems due to age-related physical changes, discomfort with new technologies, and sometimes a lack of confidence in using digital tools.40,41 This gap risks worsening health inequities and can limit the effectiveness of hypertension management for this large patient group. To address this, solutions must include intuitively designed devices, culturally sensitive training programs, and ongoing user support.

Beyond accessibility, patients and providers are rightly concerned about data security. Smart models depend on collecting large volumes of personal health data, which raises important privacy questions.42 If patients doubt the security of their data, their trust and willingness to participate will decline. Therefore, establishing confidence requires more than just technology. It demands strong data governance, clear and transparent privacy policies, and resilient security infrastructure.

Another critical challenge is economic sustainability. Implementing these systems involves significant costs, from the initial purchase of smart devices to their long-term maintenance and the technical support required to keep them running.43 These expenses can be prohibitive for lower-income communities and patients, creating a financial barrier to access. Although commercial platforms may eventually help reduce healthcare costs associated with hypertension, the high upfront investment remains a major hurdle for public health budgeting.44

To implement these models widely, we must balance usability, security, and cost. This will require new funding methods and partnerships that prove their value first. The final goal is a sustainable system that works for patients, providers, payers, and technology companies alike, making smart health management both practical and fair.

Future Directions and Strategies

The narrative approach adopted in this review has been instrumental in achieving its aim. By accommodating a wide spectrum of study designs and evidence types, including pilot feasibility studies and randomized controlled trials, it has provided the necessary breadth to construct a coherent framework of smart hypertension management. This methodological flexibility underscores a key finding: the field’s current strength lies not in definitive efficacy numbers for any single tool, but in the demonstrable convergence of multiple technological pathways toward a more proactive, data-driven care paradigm. The following recommendations for future research, policy, and practice are derived from this holistic understanding.

Technological Integration and Innovation

The application of 5G technology can effectively resolve data transmission delays common in traditional community hypertension management. By connecting smart medical devices like electronic blood pressure monitors to 5G networks, patient data can be transmitted in real time to community health providers. This allows medical staff to access patient health information immediately, identify abnormalities promptly, and intervene in a timely manner, enabling more precise management.45 The lower latency and high bandwidth of 5G also make it possible to conduct real-time remote consultations and provide instant clinical decision support, significantly improving the responsiveness of public health services.

Blockchain technology offers a promising solution to challenges of data security and privacy protection. Its distributed ledger allows encrypted data storage that is immutable and traceable.46 This approach not only strengthens security but also builds patient trust. A key feature of blockchain is its ability to enable secure data sharing across institutions without compromising patient control over their information, which is vital for integrated public health systems.

Edge computing brings a key advantage to hypertension management by processing data near its source, such as in a patient’s home. This local processing enables immediate alerts for dangerous blood pressure levels, significantly speeding up emergency response.47 Additionally, it strengthens data privacy and cuts down on the expenses associated with data transmission. These features are particularly valuable for implementing sustainable smart health solutions in communities with limited resources.

The value of artificial intelligence lies in its ability to derive deeper insights from patient data, supporting personalized health advice and early warnings.48 This means advanced algorithms can detect subtle patterns that signal future health deterioration, shifting care toward true prevention. At the same time, natural language processing makes digital tools more conversational and easier to use, thereby reducing adoption barriers and making technology more accessible to all patients.

Policy Support and Standardization

Hypertension management is widely regarded as an essential component of public health systems globally. As one example, China incorporated it as a key priority in its National Basic Public Health Service Program launched in 2009.49 To advance toward smart hypertension care, stronger policy support and standardization are now essential to keep pace with evolving public health goals.

Governments should strengthen policy frameworks to foster collaboration between medical institutions and technology companies. A particularly effective measure is to integrate validated digital health tools into multi-tiered medical insurance systems, which helps lower access barriers and expand coverage.50 It is also important that scientifically supported digital health services be formally recognized and reimbursed on par with traditional clinical care.

Strong privacy protection is equally critical to the success of digital health initiatives. China’s approach to digital hypertension management illustrates this well. The country has aligned its programs with national legislation such as the Personal Information Protection Law and the Data Security Law. Under this legal framework, programs must put key principles into practice, such as collecting only essential data, defining clear usage purposes, and applying strong security measures. They must also conduct strict reviews before sending any data across borders. Such measures are crucial for maintaining public trust and meeting regulatory obligations.

The establishment of uniform data standards and operational guidelines is another foundational element for effective smart management.51 Standardization makes data more comparable and useful. It also promotes collaboration and information sharing across different institutions. Furthermore, it advances the interoperability of electronic health records. Together, these gains contribute to a higher overall standard of public health management.

International examples provide helpful guidance for policy design.52 In Japan, CureApp HT has been approved as a digital therapeutic adjunct for essential hypertension, with ongoing post-market evidence collection. In September 2025, the US FDA cleared the Apple Watch hypertension notification feature, which is designed only for screening and alerting and is not classified as a treatment. Notably, no digital therapeutic has yet received FDA approval specifically for lowering blood pressure. These global experiences highlight that, while informative, each health system must still develop its own regulatory and payment frameworks based on local needs and contexts.

Finally, policy efforts should also focus on training healthcare workers, enhancing their understanding and ability to use new technologies effectively. This includes building not only technical skills, but also competencies in change management, workflow redesign, and a mindset of continuous improvement in the era of digital public health.

Multidisciplinary Collaboration and Public Education

Effective community hypertension management relies on collaboration across medical, technological, and managerial fields. By forming teams that include community doctors, IT engineers, and health managers, resources can be better integrated to drive innovation and provide comprehensive care.53

Digital health literacy is another crucial factor. A patient’s ability to understand and use digital tools directly affects the success of these interventions.54 Without clear and accessible support, even well-designed technologies may not be adopted. Effective approaches include providing training that is tailored to different age groups and cultural backgrounds, available in multiple languages, and supported by ongoing technical assistance. Involving family members in the training process can also help reinforce learning and improve long term adherence.

Diverse public education methods can significantly boost patient involvement. Online social platforms let users form support groups. In these groups people share health measurements and encourage each other. Special training helps older adults use smart devices. This training uses clear instructions and real examples. Regular workshops meet in person. They show how to use technology and provide health education. These workshops build local support networks. They also help people stay engaged over time. All these activities work together. They improve community health awareness. They also build self-management skills. This creates a strong base for sustainable smart health management.

Prioritized Research Agenda and Long-Term Vision

Current research on smart hypertension management relies heavily on small-scale pilot studies. These studies lack the scope of large clinical trials and comprehensive economic evaluations. Future investigations should address several critical areas to advance the field.

The priority involves conducting large multi-center randomized controlled trials. These studies should test various smart management approaches across diverse patient groups and healthcare environments. A trial duration extending beyond twelve months, ideally reaching 24 months or more, is necessary to document lasting behavioral changes and significant health outcomes. Tracking major cardiovascular events within these trials is particularly important, as these represent fundamental public health objectives.

Another critical area is the development of standardized evaluation frameworks. Such systems should integrate three key elements: clinical measurements, outcomes reported directly by patients, and health economic indicators. When researchers use consistent measurement approaches across different studies, it becomes possible to make meaningful comparisons and truly understand the comprehensive impact of smart management strategies.

Research must also address practical implementation and equity concerns. Studies should identify both barriers and success factors in real world settings, paying special attention to areas where resources are scarce. Developing strategies that overcome digital access barriers represents an urgent need. Ensuring equitable access to these technologies for disadvantaged groups is not only a technical goal but also a fundamental ethical obligation.

Looking ahead, new technologies promise to fundamentally reshape hypertension care. Technological development will evolve from simply tracking health data toward predicting risks and preventing adverse events. Healthcare services will simultaneously transition from standardized protocols to personalized interventions that adapt to individual patient needs. At the health system level, the focus will shift toward building connected ecosystems that enable smooth cooperation between medical institutions, technology companies, patients, families, and community resources. Together, these developments will establish a continuous healthcare experience centered on patients, creating the foundation for more effective public health outcomes.55

Final Remarks

Based on the synthesis of evidence from these studies, this review concludes that the three predominant smart management models, namely mobile health for self-management, remote monitoring for clinical intervention, and artificial intelligence for system efficiency, may improve hypertension control within community settings. Their effectiveness is consistently demonstrated through several key, quantitative indicators derived from the reviewed literature: improved blood pressure control rates, as remote monitoring systems are associated with increases of 15–25 percentage points compared to usual care, enhanced medication adherence, with interventions utilizing mobile health applications showing positive impacts on self-reported and objective measures, increased follow-up efficiency and coverage, where AI-driven tools such as automated voice follow-ups achieve comparable completion rates to manual calls while reducing staff time per contact, and enhanced patient engagement and self-management, facilitated by mobile health platforms that improve patient knowledge, self-monitoring behavior, and engagement with care plans.

The integration of these technologies enables a fundamental shift from reactive treatment to proactive, data-driven, and personalized management. However, as noted, their broader implementation faces significant challenges, including the digital divide, data security concerns, and questions of long-term cost-effectiveness. Future efforts must therefore not only focus on generating more robust evidence through large-scale trials but also on developing equitable strategies and supportive policies. By doing so, smart hypertension management can evolve into a standard, effective component of primary care, ultimately improving cardiovascular outcomes and advancing public health equity.
