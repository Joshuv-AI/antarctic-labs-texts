Healthcare workers’ perspectives on integrated oral and antenatal health care at public health facilities in southwestern nigeria: a qualitative study
Authors: Love Bukola Ayamolowo, Oluwabunmi Samuel Ibitoye, Ayodeji Omobolanle Esan, Folahanmi Tomiwa Akinsolu, George Uchenna Eleje, Abideen Olurotimi Salako, Ifeoma Eugenia Idigbe, Joanne Lusher, Oliver Chukwujekwu Ezechi, Moréniké Oluwátóyìn Foláyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWomen experience physiological and psychological changes that can affect oral health during pregnancy. This study evaluated the current practices, barriers, and facilitators of oral health care within antenatal care services and assess the feasibility and benefits of integrating oral healthcare into antenatal care frameworks, particularly in Southwest Nigeria.MethodsThis descriptive phenomenology study that recruited participants from Osun and Ekiti States healthcare facilities in Southwest Nigeria. Data were collected from healthcare workers between July and August 2023 via in-depth interviews. Transcripts were coded and analyzed using Atlas.ti version 8. The coding frame was structured according to the research questions, with new codes added iteratively to capture emergent themes. Insights were drawn out into a Microsoft word narrative document, linking findings back to transcripts to keep track of the weight of evidence behind specific points. The teams adhered to and employed procedures that ensured rigor.ResultsSixteen healthcare workers were interviewed. Three themes emerged from the interview. The first theme - current oral health practices at antenatal care services - shed light on the existing scope of antenatal care and, the limitations of oral health assessments and care within the antenatal care units. The second theme, which focused on the uptake of preventive oral care by pregnant women, identified a variety of facilitators and barriers to oral health care access by pregnant women. Facilitators include experiences of tooth pain or bleeding, while barriers were the lack of awareness, misconceptions, and financial constraints. The third theme explored the integration of oral healthcare into antenatal care, revealing perceived benefits and measures to improve access such as early detection, prompt referral, and preventive practices.ConclusionHealthcare providers in Southwestern Nigeria perceived that access to and uptake of preventive oral health care by pregnant women is poor possibly due to the absence of protocols for integrating oral health into antenatal care services. In addition, there are individual and structural level factors that limits the access to and uptake of oral health care by pregnant women. Many of these barriers can be addressed through oral health promotion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13308-1.

Introduction

Oral healthcare needs encompass a range of services and interventions necessary to enhance oral health, with due consideration to individual preferences, values, and social determinants of health [1–3]. Pregnant women are notably more vulnerable to oral health issues, such as gingivitis and caries, due to hormonal fluctuations and increased blood flow to the gums [4]. Suboptimal oral health during pregnancy can also negatively impact quality of life, with associated difficulties in eating, speaking and sleeping [5]. In addition, periodontitis, a common dental disease among pregnant women, is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes including preterm birth and low birth weight [6]: pregnant women with periodontitis face a 2–7 times increased risk of delivering premature and underweight babies [7]. Therefore, maintaining good oral health during pregnancy is vital for the wellbeing of the mother and baby.

The American College of Obstetrics and Gynecology recognizes that oral health constitutes a vital aspect of overall wellbeing during pregnancy. Practicing optimal maternal oral hygiene habits during the perinatal period assists in reducing the risk of early (pre-school) childhood caries [8]. Oral care during pregnancy poses negligible risk to mother and fetus [5]. Therefore, every expectant mother should undergo an oral risk assessment, receive guidance on optimal oral healthcare practices and receive referral for dental treatment where necessary [9].

Despite evidence linking the severity of maternal periodontal disease to adverse neonatal and maternal outcomes [6, 10], the utilization of oral healthcare services among pregnant women in Sub-Saharan Africa remains low [10]. A review conducted on the oral health behaviors of pregnant women in Nigeria indicates that the percentage of pregnant women who attend dental visits ranges between 4% and 63%; and dental care utilization is often prompted by the need to treat caries, and manage pain, swollen gums, and excessive salivation [11]. The reliance on out-of-pocket payments for oral healthcare in Nigeria [12], coupled with limited government funding and underfunded public health initiatives [13], creates significant barriers to access oral health care, including access by pregnant women [14]. High costs associated with private providers and the lack of affordable public options exacerbate these financial challenges [15]. However, little is understood about other individual and facility-level factors that also affect the uptake of oral health services by pregnant women in low-income countries like Nigeria. One hypothesis suggests that geographical location influences the availability, affordability, and awareness of oral health services by pregnant women [16].

Previous research with pregnant women in Nigeria collectively emphasizes the importance of addressing the oral healthcare needs of this population [11, 17] However, little is understood about the lived experiences and perceptions of this minority group. Healthcare workers in antenatal clinics, as the primary point of contact for pregnant women, are uniquely positioned to provide insights into their oral healthcare needs. While many health workers do not specialize in oral health care, they frequently encounter pregnant women with oral health-related symptoms or complaints during routine checkups that they can manage only when trained [18]. Their perspectives, therefore, are critical for understanding how oral healthcare can be effectively integrated into antenatal care, thereby contributing to a more holistic approach to maternal health. Moreover, multiple research had repeatedly raised the need to identify how to address the oral health needs of pregnant women through context-specific responses [16, 17, 19–23].

The present qualitative study therefore aimed to evaluate the current practices, barriers, and facilitators of oral health care within antenatal care services by exploring healthcare workers perspectives on integrating oral healthcare into antenatal care frameworks in public health facilities in Southwest Nigeria.

Methods

Study Design

This descriptive phenomenology study explored the oral healthcare practices of pregnant women in Southwestern Nigeria, focusing on the perspectives of healthcare providers regarding the uptake of preventive oral healthcare services in public health facilities, and addressing associated challenges. The study adopted an integrated care model, expanding on prior research conducted in Canada on the integration of oral health into antenatal care services [22, 23].

Setting

The study was conducted in Ekiti and Osun States in Southwestern Nigeria between July and August 2023. These States are predominantly Yoruba-speaking population with rich cultural traditions and practices that influence the uptake and use of healthcare and antenatal care [24]. Each State has three senatorial districts.

Participants

The participants comprised of healthcare workers working in health facilities that provided antenatal care, and who had a minimum of one-year working experience in these facilities. Eligible participants include midwives, dentists, and Obstetricians and Gynecologists. Participants had to also provide written informed consent to participate in the study. Participants who were not present in the office at the time of study conduct were excluded from the study.

Sample size

For the study, the aim was to recruit healthcare providers from the primary, secondary and tertiary health institutions: 12 midwives, six obstetricians and two dentists per local government. This ensured representation of key professional groups in the study — midwives and obstetricians for antenatal care, and dentists for oral health—while reflecting diverse resource settings and patient populations. The composition allows for in-depth, qualitative data sufficient to reach saturation. Two dentists per local government, though fewer in number, reflect the relatively smaller pool of oral health providers and still allow for capturing key insights on integrating oral care.

Sampling procedure

A four-stage sampling method was used to select participants for the study. In the first stage, one senatorial district was selected from the three senatorial districts in each of the State (Osun West in Osun State, and Ekiti Central in Ekiti State). At the second stage, the selected senatorial districts were divided into local government areas, and the local government area hosting a tertiary health institution was purposefully selected for the study. At the third stage, one primary, one secondary, and one tertiary health institution with a moderate to large antenatal attendee population was purposively selected in each of the local government area. A moderate population is defined as having a monthly antenatal attendance of approximately 30–50 patients, while a large population refers to institutions with antenatal attendance exceeding 50 patients. At the fourth stage, study participants were selected from each of the six health facilities. Participants for the study were purposively selected. The physician-in-charge and directors of nurses at each health facility were engaged to help identify potential respondents who met the eligibility criteria, for the interviews in their respective institutions. Subsequently, the research team, independent of these gatekeepers, contacted the individuals who met the inclusion criteria and invited them to participate in the study.

Data collection

The in-depth interviews were conducted in English and/or Yoruba based on participants’ preferences. The guides for the interview were developed in both languages using back-to-back translation of the interview guide, and approved by the ethics committee. However, probing of participants’ responses was permissible. Interviews were conducted in private venues during the participants’ lunch breaks to minimize disruption to healthcare activities. The date, time and space for the interview was determined by the participant.

Each interview, lasting between 35 and 60 min, and was audio-recorded. Participants were informed about the audio recording, and their permission to record was sought prior to the interviews. The semi-structured in-depth guide was developed following the review of the literature [22, 23], and tested for language appropriateness with a representative of each group of healthcare workers eligible for study participation. These individuals were not included in the main study. No significant methodological adjustments were required for the guide.

The four interviewers—two public health specialists, a nurse-midwife, and a social scientist—have at least 5 years of experience conducting qualitative research between them. They are also fluent in both English and Yoruba. All are actively involved in healthcare delivery research. The study’s Principal Investigator (LBA) trained them on the study protocol, data collection techniques, communication skills, ethical research practices, and interviewing methods to ensure consistency, methodological rigor, and effective participant engagement. The training was conducted for 1–2 h every day for three days.

Data analysis

The researcher utilized Braun and Clarke’s six-step thematic analysis approach to analyze the qualitative data [25]. These steps encompassed familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, developing themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report. This method facilitated the systematic identification, organization, and interpretation of themes within the data, ensuring a rigorous and comprehensive analysis process [25].

The recordings from the interviews were transcribed. The research team listened to the audio recordings repeatedly and re-read transcripts to ensure they accurately reflected the participants’ thoughts and opinions. Manual coding of the audio discussions’ transcriptions was performed, leading to the development of a codebook. Additionally, computer-assisted qualitative data analysis software (Atlas.ti version 8) was employed to extract key themes from the data. To mitigate potential bias, an independent coder was engaged, and thorough verification of all collected data was conducted through meticulous scrutiny, ensuring rigor in the analysis process.

Rigor

The study ensured credible data generation through continuous interaction with participants, fostering a comfortable environment for open expression. Peer debriefing further strengthened data credibility, with input from qualitative researchers familiar with the methodology. Member checking validated interview guides, ensuring accurate capture of participant experiences. Verbatim transcription, fieldnotes and meticulous coding ensured data reliability, facilitating identification of key themes. Data saturation enhanced transferability, ensuring comprehensive representation of the study population. Dependability was upheld by meticulous documentation of research procedures, ensuring transparency and accountability. A thorough review process of raw materials, including audio recordings and field notes, enhanced consistency in findings, enhancing dependability. Confirmability was ensured through a comprehensive review of all raw materials, aligning research findings with collected data.

Results

Respondents’ socio-demographics

During this study, 40 healthcare professionals were approached, comprising 22 midwives, 10 obstetricians, and eight dentists. Of these, six did not meet the eligibility criteria and 18 declined participation. The healthcare professionals declined to participate due to factors such as perceived lack of expertise in oral health (n = 6), and time constraints (n = 12). However, the 16 healthcare workers who participated provided valuable insights into their knowledge and perspectives on the issue.

Table 1 outlines the demographic and professional characteristics of the 16 participants who took part in the in-depth interviews. The mean age of respondents was 43.25 ± 2.6 years, with 12 (75%) being female. Among the participants, dentists comprised 18.75%, midwives 56.25%, and obstetricians and gynecologists 25%, working across primary healthcare centers, community health centers, and tertiary healthcare facilities, with an average work experience of 17.63 ± 1.99 years.

The three main themes identified from the interviews are linked to specific interview guide questions, providing a structured insight into the oral health care during antenatal visits. For Question 1: “What are the current antenatal care services in relation to oral health in ANC?“, the first theme focuses on current antenatal care practices related to oral health. For Question 2: “What are the views of healthcare workers on women’s access to preventive oral health during antenatal care?“, the second theme explores the uptake of preventive oral care by pregnant women. This includes subthemes on facilitators (such as fear and pain) and barriers (such as lack of awareness, cost, and accessibility) to access and utilization of oral health services by pregnant women. For Question 3: “What are the views of healthcare workers on integration of oral health into antenatal care?“, the third theme discusses the integration of oral healthcare into antenatal care.

Theme 1 current oral health related practices at antenatal care services

Healthcare providers indicated that formal oral health protocols did not guide antenatal care procedures in the facilities. Instead, the focus was primarily on registering clients and providing general education about healthcare during pregnancy. Two distinct subthemes emerged from their responses: the existing scope of antenatal care and the assessment and provision of oral health services.

Existing scope of antenatal care

Healthcare workers noted that the established procedures for antenatal care includes informative sessions on various health topics, consultations with medical professionals, and conduct of routine investigations.

“The established procedures for Antenatal Care typically encompass informative discussions on various health subjects… Beyond these educational sessions, the process involves scheduled consultations with medical professionals for thorough assessments. This includes conducting regular medical tests…” (Participant 7, Female, Midwife).

This includes conducting regular medical tests…” (Participant 7, Female, Midwife).

Oral health assessment and care

Non-oral healthcare workers identified they had limited knowledge and competency about oral health and do very little about oral health care within antenatal care units. However, when there are complaints, they rely on basic history taking, visual examinations and referral when they perceive they need to see a doctor. An excerpt says.

As a midwife, my knowledge about oral health issues is limited. Helping my client starts with the Health Education and assessment. If they need oral care at that moment, For instance, in cases of pain, I would consider referral, if they need to see the doctors or the specialists. (Participant 3, Female, Midwife)

Theme 2 uptake of preventive oral care by pregnant women

The uptake of preventive oral care by pregnant women is influenced by both facilitators and barriers, as highlighted by participants in the study. While facilitators include seeking dental care when experiencing pain or bleeding and referrals for addressing oral diseases, barriers include poor awareness of preventive care during pregnancy, misconceptions about dental visits, financial constraints, and reluctance to be referred.

Facilitators to seeking oral health care

The participants narrated that pregnant women often seek dental care only when experiencing pain or bleeding while brushing because of concerns that the oral problem may result from systematic diseases. In addition, uptake of oral healthcare is driven by referrals for oral health problems.

“If it is painful when they brush, or there is some bleeding, the concern that there may be other underlying ailments making them bleed may make them visit the dentist…” (Participant 12, Female, Dentist).“… and for some of them that eventually do come it’s based on referral. Eventually they may be referred and we will manage them.” (Participant 11, Male, Dentist).

Barriers to seeking oral health care

Participants identified that pregnant women do not seek oral health care mainly due to poor awareness of the need for preventive oral care during pregnancy. In addition, misconceptions like associating dental visits with pain and harm to the unborn child, deter some from seeking dental services. Other reasons for poor use of dental services during pregnancy include financial constraints especially if the National Health Insurance Scheme does not cover costs; and reluctance to be referred especially if the referral entail additional time and inconveniences.

“They don’t seek care because they are unaware… and some believe oral care and procedures could negatively affect their baby (Participant 11, Male, Dentist).“When they have any complaints relating to their mouth, they might lodge them with the doctor, who then refers them to the dentist. If you ask them to go there, despite being given a referral from here, they might not be able to go, maybe because of the time factor or they may be entertaining fear that maybe they are still going to spend [money] there apart from spending here if they are not an NHIS beneficiary.” (Participant 15, Male, O&G Doctor).

Theme 3 integration of oral healthcare into antenatal care

Healthcare workers advocated for integrating oral healthcare into antenatal care to enhance oral health awareness, preventive practices, and timely intervention, potentially reducing maternal discomfort and infection risks while positively impacting infant health.

Perceived benefits and potential measures

Healthcare workers had positive views about integrating oral healthcare. Reasons for their support for integrated oral care include increased awareness on the need to access oral health services, improved preventive practices, and early detection of oral diseases, and prompt referral for management. Non-dental health workers, however, opined that pregnant women with oral health problems be referred to specialists, due to limited oral care facilities in antenatal care. Participants also emphasized the need to ensure oral health care is accessible and affordable.

“…Integrating oral care into pregnancy policies enhances maternal well-being by reducing discomfort and infection risks. Favorable oral health can impact pregnancy outcomes and infant health positively.” (Participant 8, Female, Midwife).“It is advisable to refer pregnant women with dental caries to oral health care providers as there are no dentists available during antenatal care” (Participant 6, Female, Midwife).“…of course, the pregnant women will benefit. If the scheme or initiative (oral health integration) is something that is not too expensive, affordable, and accessible so it is something they will…They will like to do. But if it is something that is too expensive, I think they will shy away from it.” (Participant 4, Male, Midwife).

Discussion

The current study revealed that antenatal care services in Southwestern Nigeria lack provision for oral health care; pregnant women do not seek preventive oral health care services during pregnancy; and though oral health care needs maybe important for pregnant women, many antenatal care facilities do not provide oral health care services. Therefore, effective referral systems for pregnant women to access preventive oral health care services during antenatal care is needed.

The current study provides evidence on contributory factors to poor uptake of oral healthcare in primary healthcare facilities in Nigeria by pregnant women. Prior studies had highlighted the poor access to oral healthcare services in primary health care centers in Nigeria despite the Alma Ata declaration [26]. However, no study had highlighted the implication of this for maternal and child health. Despite this strength, the study has a few limitations. In addition, although interviews were conducted in both English and Yoruba, some participants may have felt more comfortable expressing themselves in one language over the other, potentially affecting the richness and depth of the data collected. Participants may have provided responses that they perceived as socially desirable or in line with expectations. These strengths and weaknesses should be considered when interpreting the study’s findings and their applicability to the broader Nigerian context. Despite these limitations, the study highlights a few significant findings.

First, oral healthcare is not integrated into antenatal care in Nigeria [13]. This poor integration may have stemmed from the absence of an oral, maternal, and childcare policy. This is a huge concern as the rate of preterm deliveries remains significantly high in Nigeria [27]. The country has a pre-term birth rate of 23.7% [28], and low birth weight affects about 5–6 million children every year [28] with the incidence ranging between 11.4% and 16.9% [29–31]. The contributory role of poor oral health to this figure is unknown, but the plausibility that poor oral health during pregnancy may contribute to these figures is high [10]. Evaluating the integration of oral healthcare into antenatal care in the absence of dedicated policies [32] leaves the process unstructured and vulnerable to inconsistencies. Accessibility issues, particularly in rural areas, further compound the problem, as transportation costs and distance often deter utilization of health services, inclusive of oral health services [33]. Perhaps most crucially, cost concerns pose a significant threat to the successes of an integration effort in Nigeria [18].

The integration of oral health care into primary healthcare services especially where antenatal care is provided, may improve access of pregnant women to oral health care due to the elimination of referral and cost related barriers. Integration of oral health education and screening sessions into the antenatal clinics for mothers, provided by healthcare workers conducting the antenatal clinics, is feasible and essential [34–36]. Continued capacity building for healthcare workers who are not dentists, on oral health education and screening, can be provided through supportive supervisory visits, and on-the-job trainings. Pregnant women who need oral health care can then access services within the health institutions rather than travel to another institution, thereby reducing the risk of the client falling out of the care continuum [11].

Integration, however, does not address all the factors mitigating against the uptake of oral health services by pregnant women. Poor awareness about the importance of uptake of preventive oral health care during pregnancy, and information dissemination on the advantage for the health and wellness of mother and child needs to be addressed [22]. Awareness programs can also address myths and misconceptions breeding fear of dental procedures [37]. Access to subsidized care programs through targeted financial assistance, and strategic partnerships with community organizations can also improve access to oral health care by pregnant women [38]. In addition, accessibility barriers, particularly in rural areas, can be tackled through mobile dental clinics, transportation support initiatives, and expanding clinic reach [39].

The study emphasizes key policy areas to integrate oral healthcare for pregnant women in Southwestern Nigeria. Financial sustainability through cost-sharing, public-private partnerships, and subsidies is essential. Tailored communication strategies are vital to dispel misconceptions and promote positive oral health behaviors. There should also be standardization and training within antenatal care frameworks are crucial for seamless integration and effective service delivery.

The 2024 revised National Oral Health Policy [40] presents a pivotal opportunity to engage stakeholders and initiate actions to address critical gaps in oral health care. Section 4.1.1 of the policy highlights the importance of integrating oral health care into maternal and preschool health services. Among the five indicators outlined, one aims to ensure that at least 50% of pregnant women receive routine oral care. Implementing this policy provision could significantly mitigate the concerns highlighted by current findings.

Achieving this goal, however, requires robust multisectoral collaboration and significant reforms in institutional systems and structures. Immediate action is needed to mobilize stakeholders, given the established links between oral health and pregnancy outcomes. The FDI factsheet for non-oral healthcare professionals offers a valuable resource that can be adapted for Nigeria, facilitating the screening of pregnant women and establishing effective referral pathways for those requiring oral health care [41].

Strategies need to include those that address perceived barriers and enhance perceived benefits essential for promoting the uptake of oral healthcare services during pregnancy. These strategies align with the health belief model constructs that aims to increase self-efficacy and cues to action to facilitate behavior change among pregnant women regarding oral health practices [10]. Conclusion

The present study has revealed the lack of established oral health care protocols within antenatal care services in Southwest Nigeria. This absence, alongside various obstacles, impedes pregnant women’s access to essential oral health care services, despite their significance in mitigating the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. It is therefore suggested that implementing robust policies to facilitate integration could enhance awareness, education, and counseling services. This, in turn, would help dispel misconceptions and address knowledge gaps, thus reducing barriers to access.
