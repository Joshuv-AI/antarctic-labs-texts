Epidemiological investigation of a cholera outbreak and its socioeconomic determinants in southwest state, Somalia, January − August, 2024
Authors: Abdullahi Ahmed Tahlil, Nur Moallim Adan, Hassan Mohamed Abdi, Saido Gedi, Marian Muse Osman, Suad Moallin Aden, Chukwuma David Umeokonkwo, Mohamed Abdelrahman Mohamed, Kasim Mahdi Sultan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCholera remains a significant public health challenge in Somalia, particularly in the Southwest State, where recurrent outbreaks have been reported in January 2024. We investigated the epidemiological characteristics of a recent cholera outbreak in the southwest of Somalia and explored the socioeconomic determinants contributing to its persistence.MethodologyA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in Southwest State, Somalia, from January to August 2024, to characterize the outbreak using time, place, and person analyses, and to evaluate the frequency and distribution of cholera cases. Data were collected through structured interviews, a review of medical records, and a line list of suspected cases and epidemiologically linked patients at cholera treatment centers. Data were analyzed using SPSS. Logistic regression was used to examine the factors associated with cholera. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.ResultsA total of 5,639 cholera cases were recorded during the study period, with 2891 (51.3%) female. The case fatality rate was 0.5%. Among the cases, 663 (11.8%) tested positive for Vibrio cholerae 01 Inaba at the National Public Health Reference Laboratory. The outbreak predominantly affected children under five, accounting for 3203 (56.8%) of cases with a male-to-female ratio of 1.1:1 and an attack rate of 11.3 per 1,000. Watery Diarrhoea 4716 (83.6%), vomiting 4004 (71.0%), fever 5208 (92.4%), and abdominal cramps 3108 (55.1%) were the most common symptoms observed in the patients. Only 451 (11.8%) of cases reported having received the oral cholera vaccine. There were significant associations between high cholera incidence and low vaccination rate (AOR: 4.6, 95% CI: 2.63–8.49, p < 0.001) and limited access to safe drinking water (AOR: 2.4, 95% CI: 2.05–2.89, p < 0.001). Conversely, the availability of latrines (AOR: 0.5, 95% CI: 0.36–0.69, p < 0.001) and handwashing facilities with soap (AOR: 0.6, 95% CI: 0.48–0.79, p < 0.001) showed protective effects against cholera incidence.ConclusionThe cholera outbreak in Southwest State significantly impacted children under five. Low vaccination rate and limited access to safe drinking water were strong risk factors, whereas the presence of latrines and availability of handwashing facilities with soap were protective against the outbreak. Urgent interventions are essential to enhance vaccination efforts, improve sanitation infrastructure by increasing the number and accessibility of latrines, providing adequately spaced handwashing stations with soap, water, and drying facilities in public places, and ensuring access to safe drinking water to prevent future outbreaks.Keywords: Cholera outbreak, Epidemiology, Public health, Socioeconomic determinants, Southwest state, Somalia

Introduction

Cholera is an acute Diarrhoeal disease caused by the ingestion of food or water contaminated with the pathogen Vibrio cholerae [1]. It is described as the rapid onset of profuse watery Diarrhoea that may cause severe dehydration, resulting in death unless appropriate interventions are taken at an early stage [2]. Transmission primarily occurs through the fecal-oral route via contaminated food or water sources, with symptoms typically manifesting within 12 h to 5 days [3]. Cholera is still one major public health challenge for the world, particularly in places with poor water, sanitation, and hygiene infrastructure [4]. Cholera ranks as the second leading cause of mortality among children under five years of age and serves as a significant contributor to morbidity, while also posing a major risk for severe dehydrating Diarrhoea in adults [5].

Cholera outbreaks frequently occur in settings characterized by population displacement, conflict, and poverty [6]. Twenty-four countries face endemicity, with an estimated 2.9 million cholera cases and 95,000 deaths annually in these regions [7, 8]. The largest burden of this disease occurs in low- and middle-income countries, especially sub-Saharan Africa, where cholera epidemics are recurrent and devastating [9, 10]. In Africa, cholera remains endemic, with over 86% of reported cases coming from the continent [11]. This year, a total of 118,148 cases have been reported in Africa, and 2012 people died, giving it a case fatality ratio of 1.7%. The Democratic Republic of Congo, Ethiopia, Zambia, Zimbabwe, and Somalia are the most affected countries, with children under five whom the most affected [12, 13].

The situation in Somalia is particularly dire. The country’s protracted conflict and poverty, combined with severe climatic challenges such as recurrent drought and flooding, have resulted in the displacement of the population, a weakened health system, limited public health infrastructure, and restricted access to safe water and proper sanitation, rendering communities highly vulnerable to cholera outbreaks [14]. Approximately 3 million people in Somalia remain at risk of cholera due to inadequate access to clean water, sanitation services, and healthcare facilities [14]. Effective management of cholera is essential for the attainment of the Sustainable Development Goals, which emphasize the need for good health and well-being for all individuals and the reduction of inequities [15].

Cholera is endemic in Somalia and has experienced recurrent outbreaks every year, often correlated with periods of heavy rainfall that contaminate water supplies and facilitate the spread of Vibrio cholerae [14]. In 2022, there were over 13,430 suspected cholera cases and 73 deaths reported, resulting in a case fatality rate of 0.6% [16]. In 2023, over 18,304 suspected cholera cases were reported, leading to 46 fatalities and a case fatality rate of 0.3%. Notably, more than 10,000 of the affected individuals were children under the age of five, accounting for 55% of the cases [17].

The Southwest State of Somalia has been significantly affected by recurrent cholera outbreaks, especially during the rainy season when flooding occurs. In the two years leading up to 2024, severe climatic events have intensified the challenges associated with managing water supply and sanitation. The region is particularly vulnerable due to a combination of socioeconomic factors, including limited access to clean drinking water, inadequate sanitation facilities, and insufficient health education, all of which heighten the risk of cholera transmission. Furthermore, ongoing conflicts and the repercussions of climate change have resulted in the displacement of populations, exacerbating the strain on already scarce health resources.

In January 2024, the Federal Ministry of Health officially confirmed an outbreak of cholera in the southwestern region of Somalia after isolating Vibrio cholerae, serotype O1 Inaba, from stool samples of suspected cases. The epidemic rapidly disseminated to numerous districts and regions within the Southwest State, reaching its peak incidence during March and April of 2024. This study aims to investigate the epidemiological aspects of the cholera outbreak in Southwest State, Somalia, from January to August 2024, while also exploring the socioeconomic determinants driving the outbreak. The findings of this study will provide insights that will inform public health policy and resource allocation to mitigate future outbreaks of cholera and enhance health outcomes for vulnerable populations.

Methods

Study design

This epidemiological investigation used a cross-sectional study design to evaluate the cholera outbreak in the Southwest State of Somalia. A standardized questionnaire was used to gather data on the clinical and demographic characteristics of the affected patients. Descriptive epidemiological analysis was conducted to characterize the outbreak using variables related to person, place, and time. Additionally, analytical methods, including odds ratios, were applied to assess associations between socioeconomic determinants and cholera cases. Laboratory investigations were also performed to confirm Vibrio cholerae infections.

Study setting

The investigation was conducted from January to August 2024. The study was conducted in the Southwest State of Somalia, a Federal Member State located in southwestern Somalia, bordered to the west by Jubbaland state, to the north by Ethiopia, to the east by Hirshabelle state, to the south by the Indian Ocean, and to the southeast by Banadir region. The state comprises three regions: Bay, Bakol, and Lower Shabelle, which consist of 19 districts: Baidoa, Berdale, Burhakaba, Dinsor, Qansahdhere, Elbarde, Hudur, Quracjome, Rabdhure, Tigeylow, Wajid, Afgoi, Awdhegle, Barawe, Kurtunwarey, Marka, Qoryoley, Sablale, and Wanlaweyn (Fig. 1). According to the 2022 Population Estimation Survey (PESS), the estimated population of the Southwest State is 3,012,907. The state hosts the second-largest proportion of internally displaced persons (IDPs) in the country, estimated at 649,465. Currently, the Southwest State has a total of 10 operational Cholera Treatment Centers (CTCs) to manage cholera cases and provide necessary treatment to affected individuals.

Case definition

The WHO standard case definition for cholera was used to classify the identified cases into the following categories:

Suspected cholera case

Any person residing in the southwest state who is aged 2 years or older and has acute watery Diarrhoea (three or more loose stools in 24 h) and severe dehydration, or dies from acute watery Diarrhoea, and has had contact with a confirmed case or in an area where there is a cholera epidemic.

Confirmed cholera case

Any person residing in the southwest state with severe dehydration or dehydrating Diarrhoea (three or more watery stools in 24 h) and with laboratory confirmation of Vibrio cholerae O1 or O139 isolated in a stool specimen [18].

Data collection

The data were gathered by reviewing existing medical records and creating a line list that identified the suspected and epidemiologically linked cases within cholera treatment centers as probable cases. A WHO-standardized data collection tool, designed to capture essential information for investigating and monitoring disease outbreaks, was utilized to gather data on.

The cases (https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/documents/emergencies/outbreak-toolkit/cifa-Diarrhoeal-syndrome.pdf? sfvrsn=119a5516_2) [19]. Face-to-face interviews were conducted with residents in the southwest state at their homes. Blood samples were aseptically collected from individuals who met the criteria for “suspected” cases. The collected samples were then transported to the national laboratory for confirmation.

Laboratory investigation

Stool samples collected from patients with acute Diarrhoea symptoms at a cholera treatment center, and then transported in Cary-Blair media for confirmatory testing at the National Public Health Reference Laboratory, employing standard bacteriological techniques for analysis. Concurrently, water samples were collected from two identified holy water sources, filtered through 0.22 μm membranes, and enriched in alkaline peptone water (pH 8.4) at 37 °C for 4–6 h prior to culturing on selective media to detect Vibrio cholerae. Anti-microbial susceptibility testing of isolated pathogens was conducted using the disc diffusion method to evaluate resistance profiles.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered into the Epi Info version 7.2.6 software. The data were then exported into Microsoft Excel 2016, and descriptive analyses by person, place, and time, as well as calculations of the attack rate by sex, age, and place, were performed. The data were then exported to IBM SPSS version 26.0 for statistical analysis. An epidemic curve was created to illustrate cholera cases by date of onset. Chi-squared tests and logistic regression analyses were utilized to examine the associations between cholera incidence and socioeconomic factors. A significance level of p < 0.05 was established for statistical significance.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of cholera cases

A total of 5,639 cholera cases were recorded during the study period, with 2,748 (48.7%) males and 2,891 (51.3%) females. The median age of the reported case was 4 years. The outbreak predominantly affected children under five, who accounted for 3,203 (56.8%) of the cases, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.1:1 and an attack rate of 11.3 per 1,000. The majority of the cases were from Afgoye with 1,955 (34.7%), followed by Marka with 1,224 (21.7%) and Burhakaba with 1,034 (18.3%). The overall attack rate was 187.2/100,000 people, and the case fatality rate was 28 (0.5%). (Table 1).

Socioeconomic determinants associated with cholera cases

The analysis revealed that individuals who had not received oral cholera vaccination were significantly more likely to be affected by cholera compared to those vaccinated (AOR = 4.6; 95% CI: 2.63–8.49; p < 0.001). Limited access to safe drinking water emerged as a significant risk factor for cholera, with individuals from households without safe water being more than twice as likely to develop the disease (AOR = 2.4; 95% CI: 2.05–2.89; p < 0.001). In contrast, the availability of sanitation infrastructure and hygiene facilities showed a protective effect. The availability of latrines in the household was associated with a 50% reduction in the odds of cholera cases (AOR: 0.5, 95% CI: 0.36–0.69, p < 0.001). Similarly, the availability of handwashing facilities with soap significantly reduced the likelihood of cholera infection (AOR: 0.6, 95% CI: 0.48–0.79, p < 0.001). (Table 3).

Discussion

The findings of this epidemiological investigation highlight the significant burden of cholera in Southwest State, Somalia, during the study period from January to August 2024. A total of 5,639 cholera cases were recorded, with a case fatality rate (CFR) of 0.5%, which is consistent with the global CFR for cholera outbreaks but remains concerning given the preventable nature of the disease [20, 21]. The outbreak predominantly affected children under five years of age, who accounted for 56.8% of the cases, with an attack rate of 11.3 per 1,000, underscoring the vulnerability of this demographic to cholera in low-resource settings [21]. This aligns with findings from similar outbreaks in sub-Saharan Africa, where children under five are often the most affected due to their weaker immune systems and higher exposure to contaminated environments [22]. The overall attack rate of 187.2 per 100,000 people and a case fatality rate (CFR) of 0.5% are comparable to cholera outbreaks reported in neighboring countries such as Kenya and Ethiopia, where similar socioeconomic and environmental conditions prevail [23, 24]. The CFR observed in this study is lower than that reported in other cholera outbreaks in sub-Saharan Africa, such as in Nigeria (CFR: 1.7%) and South Sudan (CFR: 2.3%), which may reflect differences in healthcare access, case management, or surveillance systems.

The high proportion of cases in Afgoi (34.7%), Marka (21.7%), and Burhakaba (18.3%) underscores the role of geographic and environmental factors in cholera transmission. These areas are characterized by poor sanitation infrastructure, limited access to clean water, and high population density, which are well-documented drivers of cholera outbreaks [25]. Similar patterns have been observed in cholera outbreaks in neighboring countries such as Kenya and Ethiopia, where urban and peri-urban areas with inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) facilities experienced higher cholera incidence [26, 27]. The predominance of Vibrio cholerae O1 Inaba serotype, identified in 11.8% of cases, aligns with findings from other cholera-endemic regions, including Yemen and Bangladesh, where this serotype has been associated with large-scale outbreaks [28, 29].

The low oral cholera vaccine (OCV) coverage, with only 11.8% of cases above 1 year reporting vaccination, was a critical factor in the outbreak’s spread. Statistical analysis revealed that low vaccination rates were significantly associated with a 4.6 times higher risk of cholera. This finding is consistent with studies from Bangladesh and Haiti, where increased OCV coverage was associated with reduced cholera transmission [29, 30]. The low vaccination coverage in the Southwest State may be attributed to logistical challenges, vaccine hesitancy or limited awareness of vaccination campaigns, and limited healthcare infrastructure, which are common barriers in conflict-affected settings [31–33].

The inverse association between latrine availability and cholera cases (AOR: 0.5, 95% CI: 0.36–0.69, p < 0.001) underscores the importance of improved sanitation in cholera prevention. Similar findings have been reported in India and Nigeria, where open defecation and inadequate sanitation facilities have been linked to higher cholera incidence [34, 35]. The availability of handwashing facilities with soap was also associated with a reduced risk of cholera, reinforcing the role of hygiene promotion in outbreak control, as demonstrated in studies from Zimbabwe and Tanzania [36]. These findings also align with evidence from Nigeria and Yemen, where poor sanitation and hygiene were key drivers of cholera outbreaks [27, 28].

Inadequate access to safe drinking water emerged as a significant risk factor for cholera, consistent with findings from cholera-endemic regions such as Yemen, South Sudan, and Uganda, where contaminated water sources were identified as major contributors to cholera transmission [37, 38]. The reliance on unsafe water sources, such as contaminated wells and surface water, exacerbates the risk of cholera transmission, particularly in resource-limited settings where water treatment infrastructure is lacking [39].

Limitations

The study uses a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to establish causality and temporal relationships between socioeconomic determinants and cholera incidence. Reliance on self-reported data during face-to-face interviews may lead to recall bias or inaccuracies in clinical information. The selection of study participants from a limited geographical area within the Southwest State of Somalia may not be representative of the broader population, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. The study’s focus on cholera treatment centers may also skew the population sampled, potentially excluding individuals who did not seek care or who were asymptomatic, thereby underestimating the true extent of the outbreak. Furthermore, the laboratory confirmation process for cholera may have been limited by the availability of resources and capacity, potentially impacting the accuracy of case classification. Finally, external factors such as environmental and infrastructural conditions, which may significantly influence cholera transmission dynamics, were not extensively analyzed, potentially limiting the comprehensive understanding of the outbreak’s determinants.

Conclusions

The cholera outbreak in Southwest State significantly impacted children under five, who had the highest attack rates. This study highlights significant associations between the incidence of cholera and various socioeconomic determinants. Low vaccination rate and limited access to safe drinking water were strong risk factors, whereas the presence of latrines and availability of handwashing facilities with soap were protective against the outbreak. The findings call for urgent investments in sanitation and hygiene infrastructure, particularly in high-risk areas such as Afgoi, Marka, and Burhakaba. Strengthening OCV campaigns and addressing barriers to vaccine uptake are essential to reducing cholera transmission. Additionally, community-based health education programs should be implemented to promote hygiene practices and raise awareness about cholera prevention. Collaborative efforts involving local governments, international organizations, and community leaders are crucial to addressing the socioeconomic determinants of cholera and mitigating future outbreaks in Southwest State, Somalia.
