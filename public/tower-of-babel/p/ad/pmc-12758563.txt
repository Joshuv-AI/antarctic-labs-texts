Chronic Kidney Disease in Patients With Heart Failure With a Preserved Ejection Fraction
Authors: Milton Packer, Jeffrey Testani, Javed Butler, Faiez Zannad, Carolyn SP Lam, Muthiah Vaduganathan, James C Fang, Barry A Borlaug
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt has been hypothesized that an impairment of kidney function may contribute to the development of heart failure with a preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). However, the conventional threshold for the identification of intrinsic renal disease in HFpEF (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) does not consider age-related physiological changes. When adjusted for age, the appropriate threshold is <45 mL/min/1.73 m2, leading to a prevalence of impaired glomerular function of ≈15% to 25% among patients with HFpEF. However, even this estimate of chronic kidney disease is inflated, since heart failure and its treatments lead to reversible changes in renal perfusion, renal venous pressures, and intrarenal hemodynamic changes, which do not reflect structural kidney disease. Accordingly, the risk of end-stage kidney disease in patients with HFpEF is exceptionally low, ie, <0.5% annually, far lower than the risk of death. It is generally assumed that hypertension and diabetes are the most important modifiable risk factors for the development of kidney disease in patients with HFpEF. However, in large-scale trials, pharmacological efforts to lower blood pressure or blood glucose have not had favorable effects on glomerular function or the risk of end-stage kidney disease. Instead, the principal driver of chronic kidney disease in patients with HFpEF appears to be obesity and visceral adiposity. Both obesity and visceral adiposity are accompanied by neurohormonal activation and imbalances in adipokine secretion that are established causal mechanisms—not only of HFpEF—but also of glomerular injury and tubulointerstitial fibrosis—actions that are potentially augmented by increases in the mass of adipose tissue surrounding the kidneys. Alleviation of obesity and visceral adiposity with the use of bariatric surgery and GLP-1 receptor agonists reduces the risk of both HFpEF and major adverse kidney outcomes. The sodium avidity and systemic inflammation seen in patients with HFpEF are not attributable to the presence of intrinsic renal disease, but are likely related to neurohormonal and adipokine-mediated pathways that are driven by secretions from an expanded fat mass. Taken together, these lines of evidence indicate that visceral adiposity is not only the prime driver of HFpEF, but it is also the principal determinant of its association with chronic kidney disease.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, obesity, visceral adiposity

Heart failure and a preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and chronic kidney disease can coexist,1 and chronic kidney disease has been proposed as a distinct pathway through which HFpEF may develop.2 However, the nature of and the potential mechanisms that may underlie a HFpEF–kidney relationship have not been critically evaluated.

In epidemiological studies, chronic kidney disease has been traditionally defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, and in people apparently free of cardiovascular disease, this finding is accompanied by an increased risk of subsequent heart failure,3 an association that is more closely linked with the development of HFpEF than with heart failure and a reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).4,5 Once HFpEF develops, the presence of chronic kidney disease is associated with greater degrees of left ventricular hypertrophy, worse left atrial and right ventricular strain, and higher levels of natriuretic peptides,6,7 as well as an increased risk of death or adverse heart failure outcomes.8,9 Yet, other reports have found that chronic kidney disease is more relevant to HFrEF than HFpEF and have suggested that renal function plays a bystander role in HFpEF.10 However, these epidemiological analyses do not account for a myriad of unmeasured confounding variables. Furthermore, observational associations cannot provide insights into potential mechanisms that may underlie and explain the interplay between the heart and the kidney in patients with HFpEF.

Do HFpEF and chronic kidney disease coexist because they are both prevalent in elderly people? Does HFpEF cause chronic kidney disease or does chronic kidney disease cause HFpEF? Do HFpEF and chronic kidney disease occur in the same patients because they are related to an independent shared mechanism (eg, obesity)? This paper presents a critical analysis of the experimental and clinical evidence that addresses the intriguing interplay of the heart and the kidney in HFpEF.

CHALLENGES IN IDENTIFYING INTRINSIC KIDNEY DISEASE IN PATIENTS WITH HFpEF

HFpEF is a disease of the elderly, with the mean age ranging from 70 to 75 years in clinical trials and from 75 to 80 years in the general community.10–12 This clinical characteristic is highly relevant, because the kidney experiences an age-related physiological decline in eGFR, which occurs in healthy people who do not have intrinsic renal pathology.13 This age-related decline in eGFR does not carry prognostic significance, either with respect to overall mortality or the risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease.14 The risk of end-stage renal disease in a 75-year-old person with an eGFR of 50 mL/min/1.73 m2 and no albuminuria is only 0.2% after 5 years, far lower than the risk of death. A patient with heart failure aged 75 years with no intrinsic kidney disease is expected to have an eGFR ≈55 mL/min/1.73 m2, and eGFR is no longer predictive of mortality when the decrease in eGFR is fully explained by aging.15

DO HFpEF AND REDUCED VALUES FOR GLOMERULAR FUNCTION COEXIST BECAUSE THEY ARE BOTH PREVALENT IN THE SAME PATIENTS?

Given the effects of physiological aging on glomerular function, Delanaye et al13,16 have persuasively argued that in the absence of meaningful albuminuria, the threshold for the diagnosis of intrinsic renal disease in people older than 70 years should be an eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2, not <60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Since meaningful albuminuria is uncommon in patients with HFpEF in the absence of diabetes,17 an eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 represents an appropriate age-adjusted definition of a meaningful decline in glomerular filtration in patients with HFpEF.

What impact does the use of an age-adjusted definition have on estimates of the prevalence of pathologically impaired glomerular function in patients with HFpEF? After accounting for aging, among large-scale trials of patients with HFpEF, the baseline prevalence of a nonaging-related impairment of glomerular filtration decreases markedly—from ≈45% to 55% (based on a threshold eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) to ≈15% to 25% (based a threshold eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2)18–20 (Figure 1). This diminished estimate of prevalence is consistent with observations that the progression to end-stage kidney disease is exceptionally uncommon during long-term follow-up of patients with HFpEF, typically occurring in <0.5% of patients per year.20–22

Prevalence of eGFR Rates in Patients With Heart Failure With a Preserved Ejection Fraction

Approximately 30% of patients who are currently designated as having chronic kidney disease because they have an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 have a diminished eGFR due to physiological aging.

OTHER CHALLENGES IN ASSESSING GLOMERULAR FUNCTION IN PATIENTS WITH HFpEF.

It should be noted that commonly used creatinine-based estimates of eGFR have never been validated in patients with obesity,23,24 which represents an extremely common comorbidity in HFpEF. In obesity, the weight component of body surface area used for indexing may not be driven by skeletal muscle mass, the primary determinant of serum creatinine.25 To complicate matters further, a reduced muscle mass in some elderly individuals may influence serum levels of creatinine.26 To address these potential complexities, some investigators have proposed that eGFR measurements in elderly patients should be based on cystatin C.27 However, the assessment of eGFR by cystatin C in patients with HFpEF is confounded by the concomitant expansion of adipose tissue mass. Adipocytes actively secrete cystatin C,28,29 and thus, the presence of excess adiposity leads to increases in circulating cystatin C, independent of glomerular function.23,30–32 In patients with HFpEF and obesity, eGFR assessments based on cystatin C are consistently lower than those based on serum creatinine. In the SUMMIT (A Study of Tirzepatide [LY3298176] in Participants With Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction [HFpEF] and Obesity) trial of patients with HFpEF and obesity,33 the prevalence of eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 increased from ≈45% (based on eGFR-creatinine) to >60% based on eGFR-cystatin C. The possibilities for confusion are compounded if patients with obesity are treated with drugs that cause weight loss. The shrinkage of adipose tissue lowers cystatin C production, leading to a perceived improvement in the measurement of eGFR by cystatin C in the absence of a true benefit on glomerular function.25,33,34 When obesity is considered as a confounding variable, sarcopenia is no longer a relevant variable in the assessment of eGFR.26

DOES HFpEF CAUSE CHRONIC KIDNEY DISEASE?

Patients with heart failure can exhibit decreases in renal blood flow that are disproportionately greater than the decrease in cardiac output.35 Furthermore, decreases in renal arterial perfusion pressure can be coupled with increases in right-sided filling pressures, and consequently, renal venous pressures, to drive decreases in glomerular filtration.35–42 Renal function is more likely to be impaired in patients with HFpEF who have pulmonary hypertension or tricuspid regurgitation.41,43,44 A marked elevation of renal venous pressures within a rigid renal capsule has been hypothesized to cause compressive dysfunction of the kidney (referred to as “renal tamponade”), which (experimentally) can be alleviated by surgical removal of the renal fascial perimeter.45

When the gradient between arterial and venous pressures across the kidney declines, glomerular filtration is preserved by activation of the renin-angiotensin system causing efferent arteriolar vasoconstriction,46 but angiotensin II can cause adverse structural and functional changes in the kidney.47 HFpEF is also accompanied by heightened activity of renal sympathetic nerves,48 promoting the release of neprilysin,49 enhancing renal tubular sodium reabsorption and leading to renal inflammation and fibrosis.50 Increased circulating levels of aldosterone in HFpEF (particularly in patients with obesity) can cause glomerular injury and promote the progression of intrinsic kidney disease,51 a state that is exacerbated if the action of natriuretic peptides to suppress renal inflammation and fibrosis is impaired.52

REVERSIBLE INTRARENAL HEMODYNAMIC CHANGES WITH HEART FAILURE AND ITS TREATMENT.

Activation of the intrarenal renin-angiotensin system maintains glomerular filtration when renal perfusion is diminished in heart failure, but this compensatory mechanism is lost when patients with HFpEF are treated with angiotensin receptor blockers or angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, used for the treatment of concomitant hypertension, type 2 diabetes, or HFpEF.46 In patients with impaired renal arterial perfusion or venous congestion, drugs that inhibit the renin-angiotensin system induce a hemodynamically driven reversible decline in eGFR that does not represent an intrinsic loss of kidney structural integrity.53–55 Even prolonged declines in eGFR that are induced by angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors do not lead to durable kidney injury (Figure 2).56

The effect of renin-angiotensin system inhibitors on eGFR is further magnified when diuretic agents are prescribed to treat the symptoms and fluid retention of heart failure.57,58 Diuretic agents are particularly likely to cause renal dysfunction in patients with HFpEF who have obesity.59 Moreover, treatment of patients with HFpEF with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors can also induce intrarenal changes that can cause persistent decreases in glomerular filtration.60,61 As in the case of inhibitors of the renin-angiotensin system, these drug-induced declines in eGFR do not represent worsening of intrinsic renal disease, and they are fully reversible when long-term treatment with these drugs is discontinued.62,63

In patients with heart failure and a reduced ejection fraction, left ventricular enlargement and distension leads to the synthesis and release of natriuretic peptides, which act to support glomerular filtration,64 a potential benefit if heart failure–related glomerular hypofiltration contributes to the progression of intrinsic kidney disease. However, in patients with HFpEF, the secretion of natriuretic peptides is muted,65 in part because of the lack of marked left ventricular dilation and in part because of the concomitant presence of adiposity,65,66 which acts to suppress circulating natriuretic peptides (potentially related to an action of hypertrophied adipocytes to secrete neprilysin).67 When neprilysin is inhibited (as with sacubitril/valsartan), the risk of major adverse renal outcomes in patients with HFpEF is reduced.68 This apparent renal benefit is particularly noteworthy since neprilysin inhibition promotes proteinuria,69 a change that is typically identified as a biomarker of worsening (rather than improved) kidney function.

Considered collectively, these observations highlight the difficulties in relying on surrogate metrics (eg, changes in eGFR or in proteinuria) in assessing the presence or progression intrinsic kidney disease in elderly patients with HFpEF. The effects of an expanded adipose tissue mass, glomerular hypofiltration and its intrarenal adaptations, together with the reversible effects of drugs for HFpEF on renal physiology make it difficult to assess the true prevalence of meaningful structural kidney disease in HFpEF, independent of the aging process.

HFpEF IS NOT LIKELY TO LEAD TO END-STAGE KIDNEY DISEASE.

As a result, it remains unclear if HFpEF alone can drive the progression of intrinsic kidney disease to end-stage kidney disease. The kidneys are extraordinarily able to adapt to reduce renal perfusion and heightened renal congestion that—although eGFR may be greatly impaired—renal structural integrity is generally preserved. The presence of heart failure has only a modest effect to accelerate age-related decline in glomerular function.70 Even with the onset of end-stage heart failure, any substantial impairment of glomerular function is largely reversible when renal blood flow and renal venous pressures are normalized following the implantation of a left ventricular assist device.71–73

Nevertheless, it is conceivable that the neurohormonal activation that accompanies HFpEF might promote on the progression of intrinsic kidney disease after long periods of follow-up; however, the required duration of such an effect may extend beyond the scope of clinical trials or the lifespan of patients with HFpEF. During clinically relevant time periods, even mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism exerts no benefit on renal outcomes, despite a favorable effect on the clinical course of HFpEF.68,74

WHAT CAUSES INTRINSIC KIDNEY DISEASE IN PATIENTS WITH HFpEF?

If HFpEF itself is a rare cause of end-stage kidney disease, then the evolution and progression of intrinsic kidney disease as a feature of patients with HFpEF must be related to the comorbidities that accompany HFpEF. The most common causes of chronic kidney disease in the general community are hypertension and diabetes, which are also prevalent comorbidities in patients with HFpEF.75 Among patients with HFpEF, both hypertension and diabetes have been noted to be more prevalent in patients with eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or with deteriorating renal function than in patients with better and maintained glomerular filtration rates.7,12,19,76–80

ROLE OF HYPERTENSION AND TYPE 2 DIABETES IN THE EVOLUTION OF KIDNEY DISEASE.

There exists an association between elevated systolic blood pressure and the incidence of end-stage kidney disease, but the mechanisms underlying this association have not been elucidated, especially concerning the effect of obesity to potentiate this risk.81,82 Nevertheless, it is widely believed the treatment of hypertension decreases the risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease. Many studies that have noted an association of blood pressure lowering and kidney disease have focused on by the proteinuria rather than changes in glomerular function.83–85 Some analyses suggested better kidney function in patients with a lower achieved blood pressure, but these observational analyses deviated from the randomized drug assignment.83

During the past 15 years, numerous trials have formally evaluated whether hypertension is a modifiable risk factor for chronic kidney disease by comparing the renal outcomes of patients who were treated more or less intensively to different blood pressure targets. Despite a 10 to 15 mm Hg difference in systolic blood pressure between the treatment arms, patients randomized to more intensive treatment (typically a goal systolic blood pressure of 120 to 130 mm Hg) did not experience a reduced risk of worsening glomerular function or end-stage kidney disease.84–93 Even when the analysis was restricted to patients with preexisting chronic kidney disease, intensive antihypertensive therapy did not reduce the incidence of progression of kidney disease, unless patients had substantial proteinuria,94 a finding with minimal relevance to most patients with HFpEF.17 Lowering of blood pressure to currently recommended targets was often accompanied by worsening of glomerular function.95 The reported benefit of angiotensin receptor blockers in diabetic nephropathy is not related to their blood pressure lowering effects.96 Furthermore, once patients develop HFpEF, treatment with drugs that lower systolic blood pressure in HFpEF (eg, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists) do not have consistent effects to improve kidney function. In the FINEARTS-HF trial (Finerenone Trial to Investigate Efficacy and Safety Superior to Placebo in Patients with Heart Failure) of patients with HFpEF (one-half of whom had hypertension at randomization), the use of finerenone lowered systolic blood pressure by 3 to 4 mm Hg, but this effect was accompanied by worse (not better) kidney outcomes.74,97

Similarly, there is a close association between type 1 diabetes and the incidence and progression of chronic kidney disease.98 However, it is not clear that glycemic control prevents renal injury in type 2 diabetes.99 Many large-scale placebo-controlled randomized trials of antihyperglycemic drugs have been carried out over the past 10 to 15 years focused on proteinuria rather than changes in glomerular filtration rate.100 In the randomized placebo-controlled ProACTIVE trial (PROspective pioglitAzone Clinical Trial In macroVascular Events), the use of pioglitazone in patients with type 2 diabetes and an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 led to a worsening of glomerular function, despite the presence of a meaningful antihyperglycemic effect and a lower risk of major cardiovascular events.101 In the DREAM (Diabetes Reduction Assessment With Ramipril and Rosiglitazone Medication) trial, treatment with rosiglitazone did not improve renal outcomes.102 Similarly, in the TECOS (Sitagliptin Cardiovascular Outcomes Study) trial, long-term treatment with the dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor sitagliptin led to worsening of glomerular function in patients with type 2 diabetes,103 and no benefit on renal function was seen with saxagliptin in the SAVOR-TIMI 53 trial (Saxagliptin Assessment of Vascular Outcomes Recorded in Patients with Diabetes Mellitus-Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction 53).104 The benefits of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors on the development of end-stage kidney disease in type 2 diabetes105,106 have not been attributed to their antihyperglycemic effects, since they are seen in patients without diabetes.107,108

Taken collectively, the findings of randomized controlled clinical trials raise questions about the degree to which hypertension and hyperglycemia represent modifiable risk factors in the development and progression of chronic kidney disease. Consequently, it is important to consider the possibility that the association of increases in systolic blood pressure or blood glucose to the development of chronic kidney disease may be related to their shared relationships to a common underlying mechanism—which (on the one hand) leads to both hypertension and diabetes, and (on the other hand) causes adverse structural changes in the kidney. Obesity and visceral adiposity have emerged as the principal candidates for a unifying driver of hypertension, diabetes and kidney disease.

ROLE OF VISCERAL ADIPOSITY IN THE DEVELOPMENT OF HYPERTENSION AND TYPE 2 DIABETES.

Obesity and visceral adiposity have powerful epidemiological links to the development of both hypertension and type 2 diabetes. Obesity is exceptionally common in patients with type 2 diabetes, and excess visceral adiposity precedes the development of type 2 diabetes and is present in >80% to 90% of patients with type 2 diabetes.109–113 An expansion of visceral fat mass drives changes in adipocyte biology that are the root cause of systemic insulin resistance and hyperglycemia.114–116

Simultaneously, obesity is the most important determinant of hypertension in the general community.117 When assessed by attributable risk, obesity is responsible for 65% to 80% of cases of hypertension.118 Excess adiposity causes enhanced central and renal sympathetic nerve activity, activation of the renin-angiotensin system and the augmented secretion of aldosterone, both from the adrenal gland and directly from adipocytes.119–122 Obesity-related upregulation of neprilysin secretion from adipocytes67 can suppress endogenous natriuretic peptide signaling, further promoting sodium avidity and vasoconstriction, and leading to hypertension.

Importantly, Mendelian randomization studies have demonstrated a causal link between visceral adiposity and the subsequent development of both type 2 diabetes and hypertension.123 Excess adiposity causes the expanded adipocyte mass to secrete an altered suite of adipokines (eg, augmented leptin and suppressed adiponectin)124,125 that not only promotes insulin resistance (leading to diabetes),124,125 but they also cause sodium retention, neurohormonal activation, and vascular injury (leading to hypertension).126–129 The interplay of these mechanisms explains why the treatment of obesity and visceral adiposity by bariatric surgery or with the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists acts to alleviate both hypertension and diabetes.130–133

If visceral adiposity can drive the development of both hypertension and type 2 diabetes, can it also lead to chronic kidney disease?

ROLE OF VISCERAL ADIPOSITY IN THE DEVELOPMENT OF INTRINSIC KIDNEY DISEASE.

Obesity is an established cause of chronic kidney disease.134 In the general population, the risk of end-stage kidney disease increases 3- to 6-fold in people with obesity, with a greater magnitude of enhanced risk as body mass index increases.135,136 Obesity accounts for chronic kidney disease in one-fourth of men and in one-third of women, even without considering the role for excess visceral adiposity or its interplay with diabetes in the pathogenesis of renal injury, particularly in the elderly.137 Obesity acting together with age are the 2 key covariates in analyses that link hypertension to the development of end-stage kidney disease,82 but obesity presages worsening kidney function even in the absence of other cardiovascular risk factors.138

Some have proposed that obesity induces a state of glomerular hyperfiltration that causes glomerulomegaly and renal tubular hypertrophy, typically associated with nephrotic-range proteinuria,139,140 but this disorder represents a tiny fraction of obesity-related kidney disease. More commonly, obesity-driven activation of the sympathetic nervous system, renin-angiotensin system, and aldosterone promotes renal injury,120,141 and adiposity-related proinflammatory adipokines cause renal fibrosis.142,143 Heightened circulating levels of leptin in the general community predicts the subsequent decline in glomerular function and an accelerated rate of worsening kidney function, independent of body mass index.144–146 Leptin promotes experimental glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis.147,148 The extent of structural injury to the kidney is exacerbated by the obesity-related loss of adiponectin signaling, thus exacerbating inflammation and fibrosis in the kidney.149–152 Importantly, upregulation of leptin signaling and down-regulation of adiponectin act coordinately to promote renal tubular sodium reabsorption, underlying the sodium retention that is seen in obesity, independent of eGFR.153,154 Surgical removal of fat alleviates experimental sodium retention.155 Other adipokines have been shown to contribute to renal disease and sodium retention in experimental models.142,143

It is therefore noteworthy that—independent of body mass index—an expanded visceral adipose tissue mass in the general population or in patients at increased cardiovascular risk is an important predictor of the development of chronic kidney disease, an accelerated decline in glomerular function, and end-stage kidney disease.156–162 Central obesity (assessed by the waist-to-height ratio) presages the development of and distinguishes patients with chronic kidney disease;137,163–167 it identifies patients with worsening glomerular function;168,169 and it is a key predictor of the risk of major adverse renal outcomes in the general community, and specifically, in patients HFpEF.169,170 Importantly, Mendelian randomization studies have demonstrated a causal link between the genetic determinants of visceral adiposity and the evolution of chronic kidney injury.171

The mass of visceral adipose tissue residing in close proximity to the kidney may be particularly important in the pathogenesis of renal impairment, since it allows for the secretion of deleterious adipokines from fat depots to exert direct paracrine effects on the neighboring renal parenchyma. The most relevant adipose depots include perirenal fat (residing between the renal capsule and the renal parenchyma) and the fat depot located within the renal sinus. The expansion of these 2 depots is associated with intrarenal hemodynamic derangements, a decline in glomerular filtration rate and an increased incidence of chronic kidney disease,172–176 and with worsening renal function in patients with decompensated heart failure177 and in those with HFpEF.178 Shrinkage of these pararenal fat depots may help to explain the improvement in glomerular function that has been observed following bariatric surgery and the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists in clinical trials.33,179–181 It has also been postulated that perirenal adipose tissue may contribute to the compression of renal structures, when renal venous pressures are increased.45

ROLE OF VISCERAL ADIPOSITY IN THE DEVELOPMENT OF HFpEF.

In addition to the role of visceral adiposity in the pathogenesis of hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease, the totality of evidence points to an expansion of visceral fat mass (in the presence or absence of overt obesity) as the most important pathophysiological feature of HFpEF.125 Obesity—defined by a body mass index ≥30 kg/m2—is present in two-thirds of patients with HFpEF.65 However, visceral adiposity precedes the development of HFpEF,182–184 and central obesity (ie, a waist-to-height ratio >0.5) and an expanded visceral fat mass is present in >85% to 90% of patients with HFpEF.185–187 The magnitude of visceral adiposity is closely associated with the severity of myocardial abnormalities, symptoms and exercise intolerance,188–192 and there exists a linear relationship between the severity of central obesity and the risk of cardiovascular death and worsening heart failure in patients with HFpEF.185,186,193 Furthermore, alleviation of visceral adiposity with bariatric surgery or GLP-1 agonists exerts favorable effects on the clinical status and progression of HFpEF.193,194

An expansion of visceral adipose tissue may influence HFpEF because it acts to enhance the secretion of proinflammatory adipokines while suppressing the secretion of cardioprotective adipokines.124,125 As in the case of perirenal fat and the kidney, the most important visceral fat depots are those that surrounds the heart. Both epicardial and pericardial adipose tissue mass is enhanced in patients with HFpEF,195–201 and their close proximity allows imbalances in adipokines to exert deleterious effects on the underlying myocardium, with the effects of epicardial adipose tissue representing a paracrine effect and the effects of pericardial fat being related to lymphatic drainage into the right atrium.202,203 The net result of the adiposity-induced imbalance in secreted adipokines is the induction of ventricular hypertrophy and a myocardial inflammatory state, involving cardiomyocytes and the coronary endothelium, leading to cardiac fibrosis and a decrease in ventricular distensibility, and which is also accompanied by renal sodium retention.124,125 Accordingly, the magnitude of epicardial adiposity is related to the hemodynamic and clinical severity of HFpEF.195–201

Hence, visceral adiposity leads to the evolution and progression of HFpEF, and it also promotes the development of its comorbidities—that is, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and chronic kidney disease (Figure 3). The occurrence of renal impairment in patients with HFpEF may be related to the fact that visceral adiposity is an underlying mechanism for both disorders. This hypothesis is supported by the finding that—in patients with HFpEF—central obesity is a primary determinant of worsening kidney function as well as the risk of major adverse renal outcomes,170,204 independent of body mass index. Alleviation of obesity with the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists reduces the risks of both HFpEF and of important kidney events.108,205

Visceral Adiposity Is the Central Mechanism That Underlies the Development of HFpEF, Type 2 Diabetes, Hypertension and Chronic Kidney Disease

Visceral adiposity is the root cause of heart failure with a preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) as well as its associated comorbidities.

CAN CHRONIC KIDNEY DISEASE CAUSE HFpEF?

Some have proposed that chronic kidney disease is the primary cause of the sodium retention that characterizes HFpEF, thus postulating that it is a prerequisite causal factor in the pathogenesis of HFpEF.206 The finding of an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 in epidemiological studies of people free of cardiovascular disease increases the risk of subsequent HFpEF,4,5,77,207–209 but these studies did not account for the potential mediating influence of visceral adiposity.

ROLE OF CHRONIC KIDNEY DISEASE AND VISCERAL ADIPOSITY IN VOLUME OVERLOAD IN HFpEF.

Although some investigators believe that the renal impairment typically seen in HFpEF can impair renal sodium homeostasis, it should be emphasized that even profound decreases in eGFR (eg, <10 mL/min/1.73 m2) do not impair the kidney’s ability to maintain sodium excretion and sodium balance during salt-loading conditions.210,211 As eGFR declines, the fractional excretion of sodium increases proportionately, thereby maintaining total body sodium balance. The sodium avidity in patients with HFpEF212 is related to the accompanying neurohormonal activation and adipokine imbalance seen in states of visceral adiposity and not to presence or absence of chronic kidney disease. Alleviation of adiposity by bariatric surgery and GLP-1 receptor agonists induces a natriuresis in obesity and improves volume overload in HFpEF.213–216

Nevertheless, it should be noted that—independently of diabetes and visceral adiposity—chronic kidney injury is capable of inducing HFpEF experimentally. Experimental and clinical kidney disease can activate the renin-angiotensin system and promote left ventricular hypertrophy,217,218 but the relevance of these observations to HFpEF are not clear. It has been estimated that ≈25% to 40% of patients on hemodialysis can be regarded as having HFpEF.219–222 However, the abnormalities of diastolic filling in patients on hemodialysis appear to be related to volume overload (related to the implantation of an arteriovenous fistula), since levels of NT-proBNP in these individuals are typically >6,000 pg/mL,220–222 >10-fold greater than those seen in patients with HFpEF in the community.66 Kidney transplantation may ameliorate left ventricular hypertrophy and reduce the risk of worsening heart failure events, an effect that is likely mediated by alleviation of fistula-related volume overload and the use of inhibitors of the renin-angiotensin system,223 but the magnitude of this benefit is attenuated by obesity.224

VISCERAL ADIPOSITY EXPLAINS THE PROPOSED MEDIATORS BY WHICH CHRONIC KIDNEY DISEASE CAN PROMOTE THE DEVELOPMENT OF HFpEF.

It has been hypothesized that chronic kidney disease can promote inflammation at distant sites, potentially involving the endothelium of the coronary arteries, leading to HFpEF.225 Experimental kidney injury has been reported to cause coronary artery endothelial inflammation,226,227 but the clinical relevance of these observations is unknown. The conventional metric for systemic inflammation—high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP)—is not related to abnormalities of diastolic dysfunction in patients with chronic kidney disease.228 Instead, it appears that visceral adiposity is the principal driver of systemic inflammation in HFpEF. Heightened levels of hsCRP in HFpEF are consistently related to body mass index,229–232 and pharmacological reduction of body weight reduces hsCRP in patients with HFpEF and obesity.130,229

Some have hypothesized that declines in renal function in HFpEF are caused by increased circulating levels of fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23),225 which can promote left ventricular hypertrophy experimentally in some models,233,234 but not in others.235 Circulating levels of FGF23 presage the subsequent development of heart failure, but not necessarily HFpEF.236–239 Increased FGF23 levels are associated with greater hemodynamic and clinical severity of HFpEF, but the prognostic importance of FGF23 is not related to kidney function.240–243 The associations of FGF23 with HFpEF are likely explained by the fact that FGF23 is an adipokine that is synthesized in adipose tissue in states of obesity.244 Heightened levels of FGF23 are related to the degree of visceral adiposity,245–247 and these levels decline following bariatric surgery.248 Experimental deletion of FGF23 specifically in adipose tissue rescues diet-induced obesity.244 Therefore, the association of FGF23 with mild degrees of renal impairment appears to be related to their shared link to visceral adiposity.

Similarly, lipocalin-2—also known as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin—is typically considered to represent a marker of distal renal tubular injury.249 However, it is also a proinflammatory adipokine, whose levels are increased in obesity and adiposity, and whose synthesis by adipose tissue plays a critical intermediary role in its profibrotic actions in the kidney and the heart.125,250 Selective deletion of lipocalin-2 in adipose tissue—but not in the kidney—provides protection from experimental aldosterone-induced renal injury,251 emphasizing the role of an expanded adipose tissue mass as a key intermediary in the development of chronic kidney disease.

In advanced kidney disease, it has been hypothesized that elevated levels of parathyroid hormone or uremic toxins might exert deleterious effects on vascular structure and function,225,252 but these observations are not likely to be relevant to the eGFR values typically seen in patients with HFpEF. Hyperparathyroidism has been linked to the development of vascular calcilfication,253 and calcification residing in the aorta may presage the development of HFpEF.254 However, changes in aortic calcification do not closely parallel the decline in kidney function,255 whereas visceral adiposity is closely linked to the calcifications in the aorta in epidemiological studies.256–258

It is noteworthy that kidney injury and chronic kidney disease can elicit adipose tissue expansion and inflammation,259–261 a factor that promote further renal dysfunction.141,261 Accordingly, human adipose stromal cell therapy reduces renal inflammation in experimentally-induced acute kidney injury.262

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

Although many patients with HFpEF have an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, the true prevalence of clinically meaningful intrinsic kidney disease in these patients is much lower. Based on an age-adjusted threshold for the identification of a meaningful loss of glomerular function, ≈15% to 25% of patients with HFpEF appear to have potentially important decreases in eGFR. However, even this lowered prevalence may be related to changes in renal perfusion, congestion or changes in intrarenal hemodynamics that accompanies HFpEF or its treatment. Fewer than 0.5% to 1.0% of patients per year with HFpEF show progression to end-stage kidney disease, as defined by an eGFR <10 to 15 mL/min/1.7 3m2 or the need for renal replacement therapy.

Visceral adiposity is a primary driver of HFpEF, and it is also the principal determinant of its comorbidities, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes—and importantly, chronic kidney disease. The adipokine imbalance that characterizes visceral adiposity has direct deleterious effects on the kidney to promote sodium retention and renal fibrosis; central obesity is a major factor that determines the occurrence of the progression of kidney disease in HFpEF; and the alleviation of visceral adiposity has favorable effects on glomerular function, volume overload, and the risk of end-stage kidney disease. Proposed mediators by which chronic kidney disease can lead to HFpEF—systemic inflammation and FGF23—are related to the altered profile of adipocyte secretion seen in patients with obesity. Recognition of the role played by an expanded visceral adipose tissue mass on the kidneys is likely to have important implications for the direction of future development of new treatments for HFpEF.
