Impact of Infratemporal Fossa Clearance on Survival in Advanced Buccal Mucosa Cancers
Authors: Sudhir Nair, Rathan Shetty, Amit Bhawalkar, Poonam Joshi, Arjun Singh, Hitesh Singhavi, Jitendra Kumar Sharma, Shwetabh Sinha, Anuj Kumar, Sarbani Ghosh Laskar, Amit Joshi, Pankaj Chaturvedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionBuccal mucosa cancers are the most prevalent oral cavity cancers in South Asia, often attributed to widespread tobacco use. The current NCCN guidelines generally advise against primary surgery for locally very advanced (cT4b) buccal cancers involving the masticatory space and infratemporal fossa (ITF). However, some surgeons have adopted an aggressive surgical approach for T4b oral squamous cell carcinoma, involving composite resections and ITF clearance.Materials and MethodsThis is a retrospective analytical survival study of 91 patients with very locally advanced buccal mucosa cancers with involvement of ITF (cT4b), who underwent radical surgery in the form of bi‐alveolar composite resection with ITF clearance and Modified Neck Dissection from 2017 to 2023.ResultsAt a median follow‐up of 27 months, 54.9% of patients were alive and disease‐free. The estimated mean 3‐year overall survival (OS) and disease‐free survival were 48% and 44%, respectively. In multivariate survival analysis, maxillary sinus involvement (p = 0.008), no response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (p = 0.004), and nodal involvement (p = 0.05) were independent predictors of poor OS. Patients with high ITF involvement (n = 23) had a higher rate of distant metastasis compared to those with low ITF involvement (n = 68; p < 0.015). Presence of extra‐nodal extension also significantly reduced survival across both groups (median OS: 10 months vs. 6.8 months; p = 0.037).ConclusionITF clearance is a safe and effective approach for managing locally very advanced (cT4b) buccal cancers.

Introduction

According to GLOBOCAN 2020, India accounts for the highest number of oral cavity cancers across the globe [1]. Among oral cavity cancers, buccal mucosa cancers are the most common in India [2]. This can be attributed to the rampant use of tobacco, especially smokeless tobacco, which is typically placed in the gingivobuccal sulcus. Furthermore, various studies have shown that squamous cell carcinoma of the buccal mucosa often presents at more advanced stages compared to tongue cancer [3, 4]. As per the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system, the T4b oral cavity cancer is defined as very advanced local disease, which invades the masticator space, pterygoid plates, or skull base and/or encases the internal carotid artery or infratemporal fossa [5]. Among the T4b stage diseases, those involving the skull base or encasing the internal carotid artery are deemed non‐resectable, while diseases involving the masticator space may be resectable [4, 5].

The masticator space includes the ramus and body of the mandible, the muscles of mastication, the internal maxillary artery and its branches, and the mandibular nerve and its branches. The infratemporal fossa (ITF) is bordered by a non‐fascial plane between the skull base, lateral pharyngeal wall, and the ramus of the mandible. Medially, it is bounded by the lateral pterygoid plate, tensor veli palatine, levator veli palatine, and superior constrictor muscle, and anteriorly by the posterior wall of the maxillary sinus. The ITF is further classified into a high and low ITF by the sigmoid notch [6].

Current guidelines, such as the NCCN guidelines, do not recommend primary surgery for locally very advanced stage (T4b) oral cancers [7] as these cancers often involve the infra‐temporal fossa (ITF) and the masticator space, which are anatomical regions with complex structures and difficult access [8]. However, some surgeons have adopted an aggressive surgical approach for T4b oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), involving composite resections and ITF clearance [8, 9, 10, 11]. The impact of this approach on the survival outcome of patients with T4b buccal mucosa cancers remains unclear and controversial. Previous studies have suggested that surgery can achieve similar outcomes as in T4a cases, if certain criteria are met [12]. However, the literature is inconsistent about the optimal criteria for selecting operable T4b buccal mucosa cancers. Some authors have proposed different dimensions of tumor extension, such as supero‐inferior or latero‐medial involvement, as indicators of resectability [10, 13]. Moreover, the role of adjuvant therapy after surgery for T4b OSCC with masticator space involvement has not been adequately evaluated. In this study, we aim to understand the benefit of complete surgical clearance of ITF in T4b buccal mucosa cancers.

Materials and Methods

This is a retrospective study of patients with very locally advanced buccal mucosa cancers with involvement of ITF (cT4b). These patients underwent radical surgery in the form of bi‐alveolar composite resection with infratemporal fossa clearance and modified neck dissection (MND) between 2017 and 2023 at our institute. Demographic data, clinico‐pathological features, treatment details, survival outcomes and follow up details were obtained from our institute's electronic medical record system. Before surgery, all patients underwent a comprehensive evaluation including a detailed history and clinical examination, routine blood investigations and a complete preoperative clinico‐radiological examination (contrast‐enhanced CT scan of face and neck for loco‐regional evaluation and a CT scan of thorax for excluding lung metastasis). Our study included only patients with clinico‐radiological involvement of the masticator space (cT4b) along with either low ITF (medial pterygoid and masseter) or high ITF (temporalis, lateral pterygoid, retroantral fat) who subsequently underwent surgery as their definitive management. Patients with pterygomaxillary fissure widening, carotid artery encasement > 270°, and prevertebral fascia involvement or major involvement of pterygoid plates were excluded.

The patients selected were either operated per primum or were given neo‐adjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) before surgery. The decision to administer NACT was based on specific criteria like facial skin edema reaching up to or beyond the zygoma, involvement of the high Infratemporal fossa (involving lateral pterygoid and temporalis muscles), and extensive skin infiltration impacting the achievement of negative margins and was decided in the multi‐disciplinary clinic, comprising of a surgical oncologist, a medical and a radiation oncologist. The response assessment was conducted as per the RECIST 1.1 guidelines. All patients eventually underwent radical surgery, comprising ipsilateral segmental mandibulectomy, upper alveolectomy or maxillectomy, infratemporal fossa clearance, and modified radical neck dissection. Reconstruction was performed in 79% of cases (n = 72), using a pectoralis major myocutaneous flap, while in 21% of cases (n = 20), free flap microvascular reconstruction with an anterolateral thigh flap was used. The average blood loss among all cases was 1472.5 mL.

Following radical surgery, all patients received adjuvant radiotherapy (RT) or concurrent chemotherapy (CTRT) as per the institutional protocol. Patients were followed up every 3 months for the first 2 years, followed by 6‐monthly follow‐ups for the subsequent 3 years. The study protocol was submitted to the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) TMC‐ ACTREC, and the research commenced only after receiving IEC approval.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive and inferential statistics were performed using SPSS 20 (IBM Corp., Chicago). Continuous variables were compared using the t tests, and the Chi‐square test was used to analyze categorical variables. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to calculate the overall survival (OS), defined as the period from the date of diagnosis until death from any cause. Disease‐free survival (DFS) was defined as the period from the initial date of diagnosis to the date of first recurrence. Cox regression was used for multivariate analyses. All statistical tests of comparison were based on a 5% level of significance.

Results

Demographic Details

We identified 91 eligible patients (cT4b) in our cohort based on the clinical and radiological evaluation and these patients subsequently underwent surgery (Table 1). Out of these 91 patients, 89% were males (n = 81) and 11% were females (n = 10). Of the patients, 94.5% (n = 86) regularly consumed tobacco either through chewing or smoking, whereas 5.5% (n = 5) did not have any such habits. We classified the infratemporal fossa (ITF) into high and low ITF involvement using the sigmoid notch as the landmark, clinico‐radiologically [13]. Table 2 provides the frequency of structures usually involved in case of ITF involvement. We noted low ITF involvement in 74.7% (n = 68) of the patients and high ITF involvement in 25.3% (n = 23). The pT staging presented in Table 1 is based on the final histopathology report. Some patients demonstrated partial or complete response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and may therefore have been down‐staged. Additionally, we considered cases as pT4b only when the pathologist explicitly confirmed involvement of any of the muscles of mastication. As this is a retrospective cohort, some tumors initially categorized as cT4b may have subsequently been staged as pT4a, resulting in a lower pT4b number (Table 1). About, 50.6% of patients were node positive (n = 46) and 49.4% of patients were node negative (n = 45). The pathologic nodal staging was as follows: 49.4% of the patients were p N0 (n = 45), 9.9% were p N1 (n = 9), 8.8% were pN2a (n = 8), 9.9% were pN2b (n = 9) and 22% were N3a (n = 20). None of the patients had distant metastasis at the time of surgery as we operated only those without evidence of distant metastasis at the time of presentation. At a median follow‐up of 27 months, distant metastasis developed in 5.9% (4/68) of patients with low ITF involvement and 26.1% (6/23) of those with high ITF involvement (Table 3).

Pattern of failure.

In our study, we defined an adequate margin as a tumor‐free margin of 5 mm or more, and based on this criterion, 94.5% of our patients (86 out of 91) had an adequate margin, while 5.5% (n = 5) did not. Most patients (n = 56; 61.5%) had moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, while 16 patients (17.6%) had poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, and 11 patients (12.1%) had well‐differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. Also, skin involvement was observed in 23 cases (25.3%) and bone involvement in 40 cases (44%). Perineural invasion (PNI) was observed in 28.6% of patients (n = 26), and lymphovascular invasion (LVI) in 2.2% of patients (n = 2) and are comparable to prior publications from our institute [14]. Maxillary sinus involvement was present in 8% of the patients (7 out of 91). The patients were followed for a median of 27 months (range, 1–86 months). At the last follow‐up, 54.9% (n = 50) of patients were alive and disease‐free, while 3.3% (n = 3) were alive with disease. 33% (n = 30) of patients died due to the disease. Additionally, 20% (n = 18) had locoregional recurrences, and 11% (n = 10) had distant metastasis.

Among the 35 patients who received NACT, a dual drug regimen (Taxanes + Cisplatin) was administered to 63% (n = 22). A three‐drug regimen (Taxanes + Cisplatin + 5‐Fluorouracil) was given to 28% (n = 10), and Oral Metronomic Chemotherapy (OMCT) was given to 9% (n = 3). In terms of response, 23% of patients had no response (n = 8), 57% had a partial response (n = 20), and 20% had a complete response (n = 7).

Discussion

Treating locally advanced OSCC remains a serious challenge to the surgeons, primarily due to the significant associated morbidity and mortality and the high cost of treatment [6]. Many patients present to our tertiary cancer care centre at an advanced stage, often requiring radical therapies like surgery, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT), or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CTRT). According to the eighth edition of the AJCC staging manual, involvement of the masticator space signifies locally advanced disease and is considered unresectable. Such cases typically go for palliative concurrent chemoradiotherapy or chemotherapy alone with questionable benefit [5]. Imaging plays a crucial role in determining the resectability of these advanced OSCC cases. Surgeons must thoroughly counsel patients before attempting extensive resections for tumor control, relying on the most accurate imaging techniques available [15].

Liao et al. divided these locally advanced cT4b tumors into supra‐notch and infra‐notch categories. They observed higher five‐year locoregional control and survival rates in patients with infra‐notch T4b disease [13]. However, their sample was small. There are limited studies available, many of which are small retrospective analyses, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions for these subsets of patients [9]. In this study, we have retrospectively collected and analyzed data of 91 eligible patients with locally advanced cT4b OSCC involving the masticatory space who underwent ITF clearance at our cancer center, making this one of the largest series worldwide.

The estimated mean 3‐year overall survival (OS) and disease‐free survival (DFS) in our study were 48% (30 months; 95% CI; 25–37) and 44% (25 months; 95% CI; 18–31), respectively. These figures are comparable to other studies in the literature [9, 13, 14]. In such locally advanced OSCC cases, there is a high probability of locoregional recurrence. We observed locoregional recurrence in 20% of our patients, which is significantly less than reported in other studies, emphasizing the importance of thorough and proper ITF clearance in these cases [9]. Furthermore, 94.5% of patients had adequate margin status, and 5.5% had close or positive margins. Patients with close or positive margins showed an increasing trend towards poorer OS compared to those with adequate margins. Also, while statistically not significant, we observed poor OS in patients with male gender, high ITF involvement, presence of LVI, PNI, PDSCC variant, skin involvement, and bone involvement.

At our center, we see many patients with buccal mucosa cancers due to the widespread habit of tobacco chewing in this part of the world. One of the common sites for gingivo‐buccal cancers is the retromolar trigone (RMT) [4]. People often keep chewable tobacco products either near the lower lip or in the posterior jaw near the RMT area. We observe that locally advanced RMT tumors usually spread in a retroantral fashion, involving the medial pterygoid, the masseter, the retroantral space, and subsequently the lateral pterygoids and the temporalis [16]. Maxillary sinus involvement is usually observed in predominantly upper alveolar tumors, which are less frequent in our centre. In the current series, we had seven cases with definitive maxillary sinus erosion having bony and mucosal involvement. We found a significant impact of the maxillary sinus involvement on overall survival (OS). Maxillary sinus involvement was present in 8% of the patients, and on multivariate analysis, those with maxillary sinus involvement had poor oncologic outcomes (p = 0.008, hazard ratio = 6.224). Although the numbers are too small to draw a definitive conclusion, this finding is interesting, as there are no studies in the literature highlighting the importance of maxillary sinus involvement as an independent prognostic factor in predicting oncological outcomes.

Lymph node metastasis is the single most important predictor of survival in OSCC. If nodal involvement is present, cumulative survival drops below 50% [17]. In our study, patients with positive nodal status in the final histopathological report had statistically significant poor OS as compared to those with no nodal positivity. Our cohort had 50.6% of patients with nodal positivity, and among these, 43% had pN3 disease. OS in N0 cases was 39.63 months, and those for N+ cases were 16.28 months.

Among patients with low vs. high ITF involvement, we found that node positivity, PNI, and LVE were comparable. Patients with high ITF involvement had a higher rate of distant recurrence when compared to those with low ITF involvement (p < 0.05), while the difference in locoregional recurrence rates was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). In the ENE negative group, there was no statistically significant survival difference between the two groups (p‐value: 0.214).

ITF clearance carries a higher risk of bleeding due to anatomical reasons. Typically, these patients also undergo microvascular reconstruction. The complete surgery often includes resection of the primary tumor, neck dissection, flap elevation, and flap in‐setting. In our series, the average blood loss among all cases was 1472.5 mL. However, with proper precautions and the use of electro‐surgical instruments such as LigaSure, we have observed a noticeable reduction in total blood loss in subsequent cases.

Various studies have reported improved outcomes when NACT is followed by surgery compared to surgery alone in borderline resectable locally advanced oral cancers [18, 19]. In our study, 35 patients received NACT. Of these, 23% had no response, 57% had a partial response, while 20% achieved a complete response. The OS for those responding to NACT was 29.8 months, which was significantly better than those with no response to NACT (OS: 9.6 months). We do not routinely administer NACT to all T4b cases. Only those patients who are considered borderline operable [18] are referred for NACT. As this is a retrospective study, we have included only those patients who were operable after neoadjuvant treatment. In a prior study from our institute [18], we observed that 44.7% of patients underwent surgery following NACT. Although the data in the area is limited, it strongly suggests that NACT non‐responders in such borderline locally advanced buccal cancers should be considered for palliative care upfront. Radical Surgery in these patients offers no significant benefit in terms of OS and would only add to their morbidity. Conversely, patients who show a response to NACT (either partial or complete) have benefited from surgery and should be given that opportunity. In this group, a thorough infratemporal fossa (ITF) clearance with adequate margins must always be attempted.

Conclusion

ITF clearance is a safe and effective approach for managing locally very advanced (cT4b) buccal cancers. Both maxillary sinus involvement and nodal positivity are associated with poorer oncological outcomes. Patients with high ITF involvement exhibited a higher rate of distant recurrence compared to those with low ITF involvement. However, in ENE negative patients, outcomes in the high ITF group were comparable to those in the low ITF group. Furthermore, patients who responded to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT; > 50% response rate) had significantly better outcomes compared to non‐responders.
