Shifting strains: Parenting daily burden in girls with eating disorders—Assessed via the Questionnaire‐Children with Difficulties
Authors: Masahide Usami, Yoshinori Sasaki, Mayuna Ichikawa, Miki Matsudo, Ayaka Hashimoto, Shun Kusanish, Nobutada Kato, Ryuwa Makiyama, Mutsumi Ohashi, Yui Higashino, Yusuke Kono, Haruna Matsudo, Minjae Ma, Yuuki Sako, Maiko Odaka, Kotoe Itagaki, Keita Yamamoto, Momoka Takahashi, Yuta Yoshimura, Saori Inoue, Masahiro Ishida, Masaya Ito, Katsunaka Mikami, Noa Tsujii, Hiroaki Kihara, Kumi Inazaki, Yuki Hakoshima, Yuki Mizumoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study evaluated daily functional difficulties among girls with EDs via the Questionnaire‐Children with Difficulties (QCD). We examined the associations between daily functioning and physical, psychosocial, and clinical factors. Furthermore, we explored how school attendance status affected functioning during school hours and evenings.MethodsWe used the QCD and compared time‐specific functioning between 179 girls with EDs (aged 6–15) and 640 age‐ and gender‐matched community controls. We conducted multiple regression analyses to examine associations between the QCD scores and clinical and psychosocial variables, which included percent median body mass index (%mBMI), depressive symptoms, inpatient history, sleep duration, and sibling presence.ResultsGirls with EDs had significantly lower QCD scores than controls across all time domains, with pronounced impairments during after‐school and evening periods. Multivariate analyses revealed that behavioral difficulties were associated with poorer functioning. However, sibling presence and weekday sleep duration had protective effects. No significant associations were observed with %mBMI, depressive symptoms, or separation anxiety.ConclusionDaily functioning among girls with EDs is influenced by behavioral and familial factors, rather than nutritional status alone, and varies by time of day. Time‐structured assessments, such as the QCD, may help clinicians tailor support strategies to vulnerable periods in their daily life.Keywords: adolescent, child, eating disorder, family burden

INTRODUCTION

Eating disorders (EDs) are serious mental illnesses that negatively affect an individual's physical health and cause impairments in their psychological well‐being and social functioning. 1 EDs are especially prevalent among adolescent girls and involve both physiological and complex psychosocial factors. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Although initial diagnoses often rely on physical indicators, such as height, weight, and body mass index (BMI), understanding patients' specific daily functional difficulties is essential. Such understanding can aid in symptom management, enhance quality of life (QOL), and support personalized treatment planning.

Mental disorders significantly impair daily functioning in children and are associated with lower self‐esteem and QOL. 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 These disorders disproportionately affect adolescent girls. The lifetime prevalence of all EDs was approximately 5.7% in women and 1.2% in men. 9 EDs negatively impact QOL, shaped by symptom severity, psychosocial factors, and recovery course. Individuals with EDs have consistently reported a significantly lower QOL than both the general population and those with other psychiatric disorders, including major depressive disorder. 4 , 10 , 11 This impairment often persists after symptom remission and has also been observed in subclinical cases. 4 Contributing factors include low self‐esteem, comorbid depression or anxiety, physical complications (e.g., osteoporosis, weight issues), and social isolation or lack of social support. 10 , 11 , 12 These findings highlight the requirement for multidimensional interventions that address both the symptoms and broader functioning.

Onset during adolescence is marked by severe physical consequences, such as growth retardation, muscle and bone loss, and malnutrition, as well as complex psychosocial influences, such as body image concerns, perfectionism, and emotional dysregulation, and sociocultural pressures, such as exposure to social media. 13 , 14 Similar to in other countries, adolescent hospitalizations for anorexia nervosa increased in Japan during the COVID‐19 pandemic, which suggested heightened functional impairments and parental burden under prolonged psychosocial stress. 15 , 16 , 17

The Questionnaire‐Children with Difficulties (QCD) is a parent‐reported instrument that evaluates children's daily functional difficulties across specific time periods: morning, school, after‐school, evening, night, and overall. 18 Higher QCD scores indicate better daily functioning and fewer difficulties. Its brevity and practical focus on basic tasks (e.g., face washing, brushing teeth, and changing clothes) make it especially useful in clinical settings for parent–provider communication. It is reliable and valid and has been used to assess functional impairments in children with depression, pervasive developmental disorders (PDDs), and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). These groups consistently scored lower in both the total and subscale QCD scores compared with healthy controls.

Previous studies that utilized the QCD reported gender, psychiatric symptoms, and time of day as factors that contributed to daily life difficulties among children with psychiatric conditions. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Children with depression and school nonattendance had significantly lower QCD scores in the mornings and evenings and at school compared with those with depression alone. Boys with PDD scored lower in certain time‐specific domains compared with girls, whereas girls with ADHD had more difficulty in the evening, which possibly reflected symptom intensity. 19

In addition to clinical characteristics, family context, which includes sibling composition, may influence daily functioning among children with EDs. 23 Siblings can serve as sources of emotional and practical support, particularly during high‐burden time periods, such as the evening.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate daily functional difficulties among girls with EDs via the QCD. We examined the associations between daily functioning and physical (e.g., BMI), psychosocial (e.g., school nonattendance and family structure), and clinical factors (e.g., psychiatric history). Furthermore, since school nonattendance was common among adolescents with EDs, we explored how school attendance status affected functioning during school hours and evenings.

Family dynamics, particularly the presence of siblings and parental involvement, may influence ED progression. Family‐based therapy (FBT), or the Maudsley model, positions the family as the central agent during treatment. 24 Therefore, siblings may serve as either sources of support or stress. Understanding how sibling configuration affects evening routines may reveal critical intervention points. Moreover, we examined whether prior psychiatric hospitalization or comorbid diagnoses corresponded to the functional impairments perceived by caregivers.

We proposed the following hypotheses: (1) Girls with EDs would have significantly lower QCD scores compared with community controls; (2) QCD scores would be correlated with BMI and behavioral, depressive, or anxiety symptoms; (3) family‐context variables (e.g., sibling presence, school attendance) would moderate specific time‐of‐day impairments; and (4) history of hospitalization and psychiatric comorbidity would be associated with more severe daily functional difficulties.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This retrospective case–control study evaluated daily functioning among children with EDs via the QCD. Participants were categorized into the clinical (girls with ED) and community‐based control groups, which comprised junior high school students from Ichikawa City, located in western Chiba Prefecture, Tokyo. Ichikawa is a residential and educational hub approximately 20 km from Tokyo, with a population of 501,209 as of April 2024, and ranks as the fourth largest city in the prefecture.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its future amendments. Informed consent procedures followed the Ethical Guidelines for Clinical Epidemiology Research established by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan. Direct consent was not always required for observational studies that used anonymized clinical records without human tissue samples; however, researchers must publicly disclose the study's purpose and implementation. This disclosure was posted in the outpatient clinic of the participating hospital, and all personal identifiers (e.g., names, dates of birth) were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethical Review Board of the National Center for Global Health and Medicine.

Recruitment and participants

Data were obtained from the clinical registry maintained by the Department of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry at the National Kohnodai Medical Center, Japan Institute for Health Security. This registry has been collecting information on first‐time outpatient visits since January 2016, which enabled comparisons with community samples.

We used registry data to examine the experiences of parents of children with EDs who visited the outpatient clinic. Inclusion criteria were girls in the ninth grade or younger, first‐time patients between January 2016 and March 2025, and had been diagnosed with EDs by a board‐certified child and adolescent psychiatrist via the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM‐5) criteria.

Of the 4368 registrants, 263 were diagnosed with EDs. After 20 boys and cases with incomplete clinical data were excluded, data from 179 girls were analyzed. Patients were diagnosed with anorexia nervosa, avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID), bulimia nervosa (BN), or other specified feeding or eating disorders (OSFEDs), and most cases were restrictive in nature. 25 Clinical data included responses from the QCD, Depression Self‐Rating Scale (DSRS), and Oppositional Defiant Behavior Inventory (ODBI) as well as medical record information on suicidal ideation and school nonattendance. Patients with incomplete questionnaires or clinical data were excluded.

Patients were categorized based on inpatient history: Those hospitalized at any time after their initial psychiatric evaluation were coded as “yes,” and those managed exclusively as outpatients were coded as “no.”

Community control group

Ichikawa City Education Committee approved the survey of public elementary and junior high school students. Teachers distributed consent and assent forms along with four questionnaires (which included the QCD) to the parents of 10,000 randomly selected children from September 20 and 30, 2008. We used the QCD scores of girls from this community sample, as reported in a prior study. 19

Measures

Questionnaire‐Children with Difficulties

The QCD comprised 20 parent‐rated items that assessed children's functioning at specific times of the day: early morning/before school (Items 1–4), during school (5–7), after‐school (8–10), evening (11–14), at night (15–18), and overall behavior (19–20). Each item was rated on a 4‐point Likert scale that ranged from 0 (completely disagree) to 3 (completely agree). Higher scores reflected better functioning and fewer daily difficulties. 18 The QCD included practical, easy‐to‐understand items, such as washing one's face, brushing teeth, and getting dressed. Previous studies have established its internal consistency and validity. We calculated both the total and subscale scores for each time domain.

Depression Self‐Rating Scale

This 18‐item scale measured depressive symptoms in children. It included items with either positive or negative associations with depression, each rated as “always” (2 points), “sometimes” (1 point), or “never” (0 points); half the items were reverse scored. The total scores ranged from 0 to 27 points, and higher scores indicated more severe depressive symptoms. A score of ≥16 points was used as the clinical cutoff in Japan. 26

Spence Children's Anxiety Scale

This 38‐item scale assessed anxiety symptoms across several domains, which included social phobia, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and panic disorder. 27 It was completed by either the child or their caregiver. Each item was rated from 0 to 3 points, and higher total scores indicated greater anxiety severity. Although no universally accepted cutoff exists, a score of 42 points has been suggested as the threshold for clinically significant anxiety.

Oppositional Defiant Behavior Inventory

The ODBI included 41 items that measured behaviors associated with ADHD, ODD, and conduct disorder. Responses were rated on a 4‐point Likert scale that ranged from 0 (not at all) to 3 (very much). Scores of >20 points indicated a high likelihood of ODD. The ODBI demonstrated good internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and concurrent and divergent validity. 28

All the psychological questionnaires (the DSRS, Spence Children's Anxiety Scale [SCAS], and ODBI) were completed by the primary caregiver during the child's first psychiatric consultation. Data were obtained during routine clinical assessments and used retrospectively for this analysis.

Sleep duration and history of inpatient treatment

A self‐administered questionnaire on sleep time on weekdays and holidays was administered during the child's first visit and accumulated in the medical record as clinical data. Data were stored as registry data and used in this study. Regarding the history of inpatient treatment, the history of hospitalization during treatment after the initial consultation was also accumulated and extracted.

Statistical analysis

Before parametric analyses, such as t‐tests and Pearson's correlations, were conducted, we assessed the normality of the continuous variables via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Most variables followed a normal distribution. For variables that deviated from normality, we performed nonparametric analyses via the Mann–Whitney U test and Spearman's rank correlation. These analyses yielded results comparable to those of parametric tests. Therefore, this manuscript only presents parametric test results.

All statistical analyses were conducted via PRISM for Mac. Independent samples t‐tests were used to compare between‐group QCD subscale scores. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d. Pearson's correlation coefficients were used to examine the associations between the QCD scores and clinical measures (BMI, the DSRS, SCAS, and ODBI). Statistical significance was set as p < 0.05 for all the tests, and corrections for multiple comparisons were applied via the Bonferroni method where appropriate.

Multivariate regression analysis

To examine the independent associations between clinical and psychosocial variables and daily functioning, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted with the QCD total and subscale scores as the dependent variables. Covariates included age, percent median BMI‐for‐age (%mBMI), weekday sleep duration, and history of inpatient treatment (coded as 1 = yes, 0 = no). In the regression analysis, the variable “anorexia nervosa (AN) diagnosis” was coded as a dummy variable (AN = 1; ARFID and others = 0). Similarly, the variable “Inpatient history” was coded as 1 and 0 for participants with and without a history of hospitalization, respectively.

These variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and previous literature. A separate regression model was performed for each QCD domain (morning, school, after‐school, evening, night, and overall behavior). Standardized beta coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, and p‐values were reported. All analyses were conducted via R software.

Morning

No variables were significantly associated with morning functioning. Age, behavioral problems (ODBI), anxiety symptoms, and %mBMI did not predict the QCD morning scores.

School

Although no psychological or clinical factors revealed statistically significant associations with school‐time functioning, the number of siblings was positively associated (β = 0.992, p < 0.001), which indicated that children with more siblings tended to have better school‐related functioning.

After‐school

Similar to the school domain, a greater number of siblings predicted better after‐school functioning (β = 0.464, p < 0.01), whereas other clinical or psychosocial factors, which included %mBMI, the ODBI, and anxiety symptoms, were not significantly associated.

Evening

Evening functioning was significantly associated with weekday sleep duration (β = 0.557, p < 0.05) and number of siblings (β = 1.242, p < 0.001), which suggested that both adequate sleep and sibling support contributed to improved evening routines. No significant effects were found for psychological scales or diagnostic subtypes.

Night

Although most variables were not significantly associated with nighttime functioning, weekday sleep duration had a modest positive association (β = 0.557, p < 0.05). Notably, the number of siblings was also positively associated with better nighttime functioning (β = 1.242, p < 0.001). However, separation anxiety symptoms, as measured by the SCAS subscale, were not significantly related to nighttime difficulties (β = 0.054, 95% CI [–0.12, 0.23], n.s.).

Overall behavior

Behavioral problems measured by the ODBI were significantly associated with lower overall functioning (β = 0.043, p < 0.01), albeit with a small effect size. Other variables, which included depressive symptoms (DSRS), %mBMI, and clinical history, were not significant predictors.

These findings underscore the role of family composition, particularly the presence of siblings, and weekday sleep duration in moderating functional impairments across specific time periods in girls with EDs. In contrast, clinical severity markers, such as %mBMI, psychiatric comorbidities, and school nonattendance, did not significantly affect daily functioning.

DISCUSSION

Characteristics of daily functional impairments among girls with EDs

This study revealed that girls with EDs experienced substantial impairments in physical health and daily functioning, as captured via the QCD. Compared with community controls, girls with EDs had significantly lower QCD scores across all time domains, especially during school, after‐school, and evening hours. These findings suggested that EDs impacted nutritional status, diminished self‐esteem, and impaired behavioral adjustment, most evident during socially and emotionally demanding times of the day.

Although previous studies reported decreased QCD scores among children with depression and ADHD, our study demonstrated a unique temporal profile among those with EDs. 19 , 20 , 21 This highlights the need for intervention strategies that expand beyond morning routines and include after‐school support, evening meal assistance, and family‐based approaches to promote rest and emotional regulation during later hours of the day.

Furthermore, the time‐specific impairments observed—particularly during school, after‐school, and evening periods—may be theoretically contextualized by factors commonly implicated in ED psychopathology. For instance, academic environments often evoke heightened stress, performance anxiety, and peer comparison, which may exacerbate emotional dysregulation and body image concerns in girls with EDs. The after‐school period may reflect accumulated fatigue and diminished executive control, while evenings often coincide with family interactions surrounding meals—situations that may provoke conflict, control dynamics, or avoidance behaviors.

When comparing our findings to those in other psychiatric populations, such as children with ADHD and depressive disorders, some similarities and distinctions emerge. In ADHD, school‐time challenges have been linked to executive dysfunction and attentional deficits, whereas in depressive disorders, mornings and evenings are typically problematic due to inertia and rumination. However, in EDs, evening impairments may reflect both behavioral resistance around eating and relational withdrawal during family routines. These patterns suggest partially overlapping but disorder‐specific profiles of functional impairment, thereby supporting the utility of time‐structured tools like the QCD in differentiating clinical presentations.

Regarding physical indicators, we found that lower BMI was significantly associated with psychiatric hospitalization; hence, BMI was a useful marker of clinical severity. However, BMI was not correlated with the QCD scores, which indicated a disconnect between physical severity and parents' perception of daily difficulties. This suggests that caregivers may experience greater stress from behavioral and emotional disruptions than from physical symptoms, such as low body weight.

Impact of physical and psychosocial factors on daily functioning

Psychological and behavioral symptoms also influenced daily functioning. Higher scores on the ODBI were moderately associated with lower overall QCD scores, which suggested that behavioral challenges contributed to caregivers' perceived burden. In addition, anxiety symptoms, which included separation anxiety, were not significantly associated with nighttime difficulties in the adjusted model, which suggested limited predictive value after accounting for covariates. Thus, multidimensional assessments that integrate both clinical and family‐reported functioning are essential for holistic treatment planning.

School attendance did not significantly affect the QCD scores, and only small, nonstatistically significant differences were observed for children who refused school. This suggests that home environments may compensate for school‐related impairments. Sibling presence was a potential protective factor, especially in the evening. Girls with siblings demonstrated better evening functioning than those without, possibly due to emotional and practical support during family routines. These findings support the inclusion of siblings in therapeutic settings, consistent with FBT models that emphasize whole‐family engagement.

Finally, neither psychiatric hospitalization history nor comorbid diagnoses were associated with significant differences in the QCD scores. Hence, a short‐term treatment history may not influence the chronic patterns of functional impairment perceived by caregivers. Overall, our findings underscore the value of time‐specific, behaviorally focused assessments in understanding and addressing the daily burden faced by families of girls with EDs.

Importantly, time‐specific impairments remained statistically significant, even after adjusted for confounding variables, such as age, %mBMI, comorbid symptoms (the SCAS, DSRS, and ODBI), and inpatient status. This suggests that the observed burden in school and evening periods is robust and not merely attributable to general disease severity or nutritional status.

Three of our four hypotheses were supported. Although behavioral and familial factors had significant associations with daily functioning, no significant associations were observed between QCD scores and %mBMI or depressive symptoms.

Limitations

All participants were recruited from a single clinical center, which may have limited the external validity of our findings across different geographic regions and healthcare settings. Furthermore, we excluded cases with incomplete clinical records or missing questionnaire data, which could have introduced a selection bias, as these patients may have systematically differed in illness severity or sociodemographic characteristics. Additionally, the retrospective nature of data collection prevented a standardized assessment of potential confounders, such as dietary habits, sleep patterns, and physical activity levels. Therefore, future studies should adopt prospective, multicenter designs with predefined inclusion criteria and rigorous data‐management protocols to minimize missing information.

In addition, although our findings suggest a protective effect of sibling presence, we were unable to assess more nuanced aspects of sibling dynamics, such as birth order or gender composition, due to inconsistent documentation in clinical records. These variables may significantly influence family interactions and functional outcomes. Future studies should collect detailed family demographic information to enable stratified analyses and tailor family‐based interventions accordingly.

Furthermore, studies should integrate direct observational methods or digital activity monitoring alongside parent‐reported QCD assessments to reduce reporting bias and provide a further comprehensive evaluation of daily functioning.

Although we did not assess directly QOL and self‐esteem, observed impairments in daily functioning may reflect underlying deficits in these domains. Future research should incorporate validated measures of QOL to further elucidate their contribution to caregiver burden in EDs.

Cases with incomplete clinical data or missing questionnaire responses were excluded to reduce potential bias. However, since this was a retrospective study, confounding factors, such as socioeconomic status, prior treatment history, and family psychiatric background, were not systematically controlled. Future studies should use multivariate regression analyses to adjust for confounding factors.

Finally, we used the term “school nonattendance” instead of “school refusal” to avoid the implication of voluntary rejection. Although this choice reflects recent clinical preferences, it may limit comparability with studies using traditional terminology.

These recommendations aim to enhance the practical applicability of care plans for youths with EDs.

CONCLUSION

The QCD effectively captured significant time‐of‐day fluctuations in parental stress related to their daughters' ED‐associated functional impairments. Girls with EDs demonstrated greater difficulties during school hours and after‐school periods, and elevated evening challenges influenced by family composition. Moreover, impulsivity, as reflected in behavioral problems, influenced functioning, whereas separation anxiety had no significant associations after adjusting for covariates.

Future longitudinal studies should refine these temporal profiles and evaluate tailored, time‐aligned treatment protocols to optimize daily functioning and overall QOL in this population.
