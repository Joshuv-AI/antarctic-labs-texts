Leveraging nationwide epidemiological big data for diabetes modeling and disease surveillance: an innovative approach for advancing public health policy in developing countries
Authors: Hadi Hayati, Razieh Askari Zahabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDiabetes is escalating into a global crisis, with low- and middle-income countries bearing the heaviest burden. Yet few nations openly harness nationwide health data to guide precise, evidence-based interventions. This study pioneers the large-scale integration of Iran’s decade-long electronic prescription records with advanced modelling tools to illuminate national and regional dynamics in antidiabetic drug use. By fusing big data analytics with policy-driven insight, our aim was not only to expose inequities and inefficiencies but to create a transferable blueprint for equitable, guideline-aligned diabetes care worldwide.ResultsAcross 84 million prescriptions (2013–2023), metformin dominated treatment, but novel agents such as DPP-4 and SGLT2 inhibitors surged after 2017, reshaping the therapeutic landscape. Stark contrasts emerged: first-line metformin initiation ranged from 58.3% in underserved Sistan & Baluchestan to 91.7% in resource-advantaged Tehran; inappropriate early adoption of new agents and premature insulinisation persisted in several regions. These patterns mirror global fault lines—where innovation coexists with inequity—and underscore the urgency for adaptive, data-led policy. Our proposed multi-tier model—combining real-time digital surveillance, prescriber upskilling, and patient empowerment—offers a novel, globally relevant pathway for transforming big data into equitable, future-ready diabetes strategies in resource-limited health systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07497-5.Keywords: Big data, Epidemiology, Antidiabetic agents, Open dataset, Disease modeling, Surveillance, Non-communicable diseases, Sustainable development goals, Public health policy, Iran

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is one of the most significant public health challenges of the 21 st century, with sharply increasing prevalence and associated healthcare burdens worldwide [1–3]. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) estimates that approximately 9.2 million Iranian adults are currently living with diabetes or impaired fasting glucose, reflecting rapid epidemiological transition within the region [4, 5]. In response, national policies have aimed to expand access to pharmacological therapies, particularly metformin and other oral antidiabetic drugs (OADs), and to rationalize insulin prescribing [6, 7]. However, despite formal guideline issuance and essential medicines’ affordability, substantial geographic and systemic disparities persist in Iran’s antidiabetic drug consumption patterns [8, 9].

During the past decade, Iran has witnessed a 235% increase in total antidiabetic drug utilization, with insulin usage rising disproportionately compared to oral agents [6, 10]. Similar patterns of suboptimal rational prescribing and premature insulinization have been reported in other middle-income countries [11–13]. National audits reveal that rational prescription—such as preferential initiation with metformin and cautious adoption of newer agents—remains below international benchmarks across several provinces [14, 15]. Policy analyses confirm that this variability is influenced by complex multilevel factors, including healthcare system design, clinician behavior, patient access, and regulatory environments [5, 6, 16].

To address these challenges, a comprehensive, data-driven approach is needed—one that maps epidemiological trends to actionable gaps in prescribing practice and resource allocation [17]. This study analyzes antidiabetic drug consumption in Iran from 2013 to 2023, identifies critical gaps in rational use, and integrates findings into a multi-level policy model aimed at optimizing pharmacological management for diabetic patients across the country.

Methodology

This observational analytical time-series study investigated national and provincial trends in antidiabetic drug prescriptions in Iran from 2013 to 2023. A mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative and qualitative analyses, was employed to provide robust, policy-oriented evidence.

Study design and setting

This study utilized an observational, analytical time-series design, analyzing prescription and aggregate sales data for antidiabetic medications across all 31 provinces of Iran, covering a 10-year period (2013–2023). The rationale for integrating both quantitative and qualitative methods was to thoroughly quantify prescribing patterns and explore contextual barriers and facilitators for rational antidiabetic drug use in real-world settings, ultimately informing practical policy action [18, 19]. Such a comprehensive approach allowed in-depth assessment of temporal trends, regional disparities, and systemic factors impacting the rational use of antidiabetic agents.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population included adults aged ≥ 18 years who received at least one antidiabetic prescription according to national pharmacy electronic registries and aggregated sales data.

Inclusion criteria: Prescriptions linked to adult patients (≥ 18 years), clearly documented diagnoses of diabetes (ICD-10 codes E10–E14), and complete demographic data.

Ethical considerations

This research exclusively utilized anonymized secondary data from routine health information systems. No direct patient contact or primary data collection occurred, and therefore, ethics approval or individual informed consent was not required under national and international regulations governing secondary analyses of de-identified data.

All data were de-identified prior to access and analysis. The protocol was reviewed by the institutional data protection board, ensuring compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and national bioethics standards.

Reporting standards

The study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE statement (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) and the ISPE Guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices, with detailed documentation of analytic processes, coding schemes, and data management procedures to ensure reproducibility.

Results

National trends in antidiabetic drug consumption (2013–2023)

Over the 10-year period under review, total consumption of antidiabetic medications in Iran increased steadily across all major drug classes. Figure 1 presents the yearly consumption (in DDD/1000 inhabitants/day) for the main drug types and illustrates these trends graphically.

Annual consumption of major antidiabetic drug classes, Iran (2013–2023)

This line graph demonstrates continuous growth in the use of metformin and novel agents (notably DPP-4 and SGLT2 inhibitors), a gradual decline in sulfonylurea use, and a moderate increase in insulin consumption over the past decade.

Notably, metformin remained the most widely used agent, while utilization of DPP-4 inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors exhibited the fastest growth, particularly since 2017. Sulfonylurea use declined both in absolute and relative terms.

Joinpoint regression analysis identified statistically significant changes in prescribing trends for several drug classes. The most pronounced shifts were observed in 2017 for DPP-4 inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors (APC: +18.4%, 95% CI 12.1–25.0, p < 0.01) and in 2019 for first-line metformin initiation rates (APC: +4.2%, 95% CI 1.6–6.9, p = 0.002). Discussion

This national analysis over a 10-year period highlights dynamic shifts in Iran’s antidiabetic prescribing patterns. While metformin and sulfonylureas remained the most prescribed first-line agents, notable growth in the use of DPP-4 inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors has occurred since 2017, coinciding with formulary changes and greater clinical awareness of cardiometabolic benefits. However, the extent and nature of these changes differed markedly between provinces. In 2023, first-line metformin initiation ranged from only 58% in Sistan & Baluchestan to more than 91% in Tehran, illustrating persistent geographic and socioeconomic disparities in evidence-based care.

In 2023, the proportion of first-line metformin initiation in certain provinces was up to 25% points lower than the WHO and ADA-recommended target of 75%.

These disparities echo global patterns in which innovation takes root unevenly, often in wealthier and urban settings first. Similar experiences have been reported in other middle-income countries, where newer agents are used in high-risk patients yet sometimes bypass recommended sequencing, or where premature insulin initiation remains common [8, 13, 19, 21, 25–27]. In our study, inappropriate early adoption of novel therapies and non-rational polypharmacy were concentrated in certain urban centers, while underuse of optimal first-line therapy was more common in underserved areas. Policy constraints, affordability, prescriber knowledge gaps, and inconsistent access contribute to these trends.

International lessons suggest that such variation can be reduced through integrated e-prescription systems, prescriber feedback loops, and continuous education, supported by purchaser-provider alignment in drug procurement [26, 27]. In Iran, where more than 84 million electronic prescriptions were analyzed, the opportunity exists to embed these mechanisms into routine practice. Our mixed-methods approach—combining large-scale quantitative analytics with qualitative insights from clinicians and policymakers—produced a multi-tier policy model that is directly responsive to the challenges observed.

Similar disparities in access to and rational use of antidiabetic medications have been reported in other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In Brazil, nationwide pharmacy claims data revealed marked regional differences in first-line metformin initiation and uneven adoption of SGLT2 inhibitors, paralleling the urban–rural divide we observed [28, 29]. Studies from India have documented both premature transitions to insulin and underuse of newer agents in resource-limited states, underscoring the influence of healthcare infrastructure and drug affordability [30]. In South Africa, despite recent formulary inclusions, uptake of cardioprotective agents remains concentrated in insured populations, with public-sector patients often relying on older therapies [31]. These cross-country patterns suggest that while the drivers of inequality vary—ranging from procurement policies to prescriber training—structural and economic determinants are a common thread, reinforcing the relevance and transferability of our policy model to other LMIC settings.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings:

Data Source Coverage—The dataset was restricted to prescriptions recorded in the national electronic prescribing registry (SEP System). As such, it may not capture patients who received care outside registered centers (e.g. private clinics without SEP integration, informal providers, or special military/charitable facilities). This limitation could result in conservative estimates of national antidiabetic drug use.

Conclusion

These findings show that advances in diabetes pharmacotherapy in Iran have been accompanied by widening equity gaps in access and adherence to guidelines. Addressing these requires sustained investment in three interconnected areas: (1) continuous national prescription surveillance to detect and act on deviations in real time, (2) structured professional development to improve safe and cost-effective use of both established and emerging agents, and (3) patient empowerment initiatives to strengthen understanding and adherence.

The proposed hybrid model—rooted in data integration, stakeholder collaboration, and context-sensitive adaptation—offers a transferable framework for developing countries seeking to balance innovation, affordability, and rational use. Its implementation will require joint stewardship from ministries of health, scientific societies, and the pharmaceutical sector, ensuring that every patient, regardless of geography or income, can benefit from optimal pharmacological management. By harnessing the power of big data with locally grounded strategies, countries can move closer to equitable, sustainable control of diabetes and other chronic diseases.
