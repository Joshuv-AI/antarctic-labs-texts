Neoadjuvant Danburstotug (IMC-001) therapy in gastric, esophageal, and hepatocellular carcinoma: the NeoChance phase II study
Authors: Yeong Hak Bang, Dawon Kang, Seungwon Lee, Seungsoo Kim, Ina Jeon, Eunjin Park, Jungmin Choi, Gi-Won Song, Moon-Won Yoo, Joon Seon Song, In Hye Song, Yong-Hee Kim, Hyeong Ryul Kim, Ho June Song, Do Hoon Kim, Kee Don Choi, Jeong Hoon Lee, Ji Yong Ahn, Ju Hyun Shim, Danbi Lee, Jonggi Choi, Ji Eun Kim, Sang-Yeob Kim, Dong-Jun Bae, Sook Ryun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeoadjuvant immunotherapy may improve long-term outcomes by activating antitumor immunity before surgery. We evaluated danburstotug (IMC-001), an anti–PD-L1 antibody, in resectable gastric cancer (GC), esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). In this phase II trial, patients received two cycles of danburstotug (20 mg/kg every 2 weeks) before surgery. The primary endpoint was major pathologic response (MPR, <10% viable tumor). Key secondary endpoints included safety, radiologic/metabolic response, survival outcomes, and translational immune profiling. Forty-eight patients were evaluable (16 per cohort). The study did not meet its pre-specified primary endpoint, with MPR achieved in 2/48 patients (4.2%)—one ESCC and one HCC (6.3% per cohort). However, pathologic tumor regression to ≤50% viable tumor was observed in 11/48 patients (22.9%; GC 25.0%, ESCC 31.3%, HCC 12.5%). Among evaluable patients, partial responses were observed radiographically in 17.6% and metabolically in 23.3%. Treatment was well tolerated, with grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events in 6.0% and no grade 4–5 events; two surgical delays were observed, of which one was treatment-related. All patients underwent surgery with R0 resection. Immune profiling revealed cancer type-specific immune-remodeling. Neoadjuvant danburstotug was safe, did not compromise surgical outcomes, and provided exploratory insights into treatment-induced immune microenvironment remodeling. Introduction

Over the past decade, immunotherapy—particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)—has revolutionized the treatment of advanced cancers, becoming the standard of care for a wide range of malignancies. Given its remarkable success in the palliative treatment of metastatic disease, there is growing interest in expanding its application to early-stage cancers. Neoadjuvant immunotherapy offers several biological advantages, including a less immunosuppressed tumor microenvironment, a lower disease burden, and the potential for antitumor immune priming in the presence of the primary tumor, which may enhance long-term outcomes after surgery1. Notably, the neoadjuvant use of an anti-programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) inhibitor in combination with chemotherapy has recently demonstrated clinical efficacy in certain cancers, such as non-small cell lung cancer and triple-negative breast cancer2,3.

Gastrointestinal cancers, including gastroesophageal cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), have shown survival benefits from ICIs in the palliative setting, either as monotherapy or in combination with cytotoxic chemotherapy or a molecularly targeted agent4–9. These findings provide a strong rationale for investigating ICIs in the neoadjuvant setting for these malignancies.

Danburstotug (IMC-001) is a fully human IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting PD-L1 that inhibits PD-1/PD-L1 interaction and restores T-cell–mediated antitumor immunity. As an IgG1 antibody, it may additionally mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, which could enhance antitumor activity10. Danburstotug is currently under clinical evaluation in early-phase studies11–13. In this phase II NeoChance study, we conducted a neoadjuvant trial with a window-of-opportunity design to assess the clinical efficacy and safety of danburstotug (IMC-001), an anti-programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibody, as well as its impact on the tumor immune microenvironment (TME) in patients with potentially resectable gastric cancer (GC), esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), and HCC.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between September 2019 and November 2022, 50 patients were enrolled. All received at least one dose of danburstotug and were included in the safety population. Of these, two patients in the HCC cohort were deemed inevaluable for efficacy: one was diagnosed with angiomyolipoma at resection, and the other, with confirmed HCC, underwent transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) after two cycles of danburstotug per the surgeon’s decision. The remaining 48 patients comprised the per-protocol population.

Baseline characteristics of the per-protocol population are summarized in Table 1. Sixteen patients each had GC, ESCC, and HCC. The median age was 62 years (range, 39‒77), and 81.3% were male. Clinical stage distribution was 56.3% stage I, 33.3% stage II, and 10.4% stage III. All tumors were microsatellite stable, and PD-L1 expression (combined positive score ≥1) was observed in 9 patients (18.8%).

Completion of Neoadjuvant treatment

Of the 48 patients, 45 (93.8%) completed the planned two cycles of danburstotug. Three patients discontinued treatment after the first cycle due to immune-related hepatitis, manifested as grade 3 elevations in aspartate transaminase (AST) and alanine transaminase (ALT).

Safety and feasibility

Of the 50 patients who received at least one dose of danburstotug, 30 (60.0%) experienced treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs). Most were grade 1 (n = 17, 34.0%) or grade 2 (n = 10, 20.0%) (Table 2). Grade 3 TRAEs occurred in three patients (6.0%), all presenting as immune-related AST/ALT elevations and were successfully managed with systemic corticosteroids. No grade 4 or 5 AEs were observed. The most common TRAEs of any grade were hyperthyroidism (n = 9, 18.0%), hypothyroidism (n = 7, 14.0%), fatigue (n = 7, 14.0%), pruritus (n = 6, 12.0%), rash (n = 4, 8.0%), and infusion-related reactions (n = 4, 8.0%). There were no serious AEs leading to hospitalization or death.

Feasibility was demonstrated by the fact that all patients proceeded to surgery after neoadjuvant therapy. Among GC patients, 43.8% underwent total gastrectomy, and among ESCC patients, 43.8% underwent a McKeown operation. Surgical delay occurred in only two patients (4.0%): one due to immune-related hepatitis, and the other due to the surgeon’s decision to perform TACE, which was unrelated to study treatment. The median interval from the first danburstotug dose to surgery was 5.0 weeks (range, 3.7‒10.9). Surgical complications are summarized in Table S3. No complications were attributed to neoadjuvant therapy, and no deaths occurred within 30 or 90 days postoperatively.

Radiographic and metabolic tumor response

No patients experienced disease progression during neoadjuvant therapy per RECIST v1.1. Among the 17 patients with measurable lesions, 10 (58.8%) exhibited tumor shrinkage (Fig. 1A). Three patients (17.6%)—one with GC and two with HCC— achieved a PR, while the remaining 14 (82.4%) had SD. The median change in tumor burden was –3.7% (range, –41.2% to +4.8%). Among the 30 patients evaluable for metabolic response by EORTC criteria, 7 (23.3%)—three with GC and four with ESCC—achieved a metabolic PR, 20 (66.7%) had metabolic SD, and 3 (10.0%) had metabolic progressive disease (PD). Notably, despite meeting metabolic PD criteria, two patients (one with GC and one with ESCC) demonstrated increased TILs and histologic regression at resection. The median change in FDG uptake was –12.5% (range, –76.2% to +58.8%) (Fig. 1B).

Pathologic tumor response

All patients underwent curative R0 resection. Final pathologic stage was I in 36 patients (75.0%), II in 6 (12.5%), and III in 6 (12.5%). Among the 48 patients in the per-protocol population, no pathologic CRs were observed. An MPR was achieved in one patient each with ESCC and HCC, corresponding to an MPR rate of 6.3% per cohort (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.2–30.2%) (Table 3). Overall, 11 patients (22.9%) demonstrated pathologic tumor regression with ≤50% residual viable tumor cells.

Survival and recurrence pattern

At the data cutoff (March 19, 2025), the median follow-up was 55.3 months (range, 10.1–65.0). Six patients (12.5%) experienced disease recurrence: three with ESCC (18.8%), two with HCC (12.5%), and one with GC (6.3%) (Supplementary Table S4).

Four patients (8.3%) died during the study period. Two deaths (one ESCC, one GC) were attributed to recurrent disease, while two additional ESCC patients died of pneumonia—one with recurrent disease and the other without evidence of recurrence at 8.7 months post-surgery.

The median PFS and OS were not reached in the overall population or within any individual cancer cohort. The 3-year PFS rates were 93.8% (95% CI, 83.5–100%) for GC, 80.0% (95% CI, 59.8–100.0%) for ESCC, and 86.5% (95% CI, 70.1–100.0%) for HCC. The 3-year OS rates were 93.8% (95% CI, 81.9–100.0%) for GC, 87.5% (95% CI, 71.2–100.0%) for ESCC, and 100% (95% CI, not estimable) for HCC (Fig. 1C, D).

Clinical biomarker correlates of pathologic response

Neither pretreatment PD-L1 expression nor tumor mutation burden (TMB) showed significant association with pathologic response across cutoffs (Supplementary Tables S5, S6). In contrast, radiologic PR correlated with higher MPR rates than SD or nonCR/nonPD (33.3% vs. 2.2%, P = 0.050; Supplementary Table S7).

Multiplex immunofluorescence (immune cell dynamics)

When comparing paired pre-treatment biopsy samples with post-treatment surgical specimens, immune remodeling in ESCC was broader and more pronounced than in GC (Fig. 2A–C, Supplementary Fig. S1). Helper T cells (103.6 → 410.0; P < 0.0001), cytotoxic T cells (66.3 → 232.3; P < 0.0001), CD4⁺CD8⁺ T cells (0.6 → 12.6; P = 0.0002), B cells (7.4 → 147.4; P = 0.0003), NK/NKT cells (1.8 → 5.9; P = 0.0189), and MHC class II+ NK/NKT cells (0.7 → 7.1; P = 0.0060) all expanded. Myeloid subsets also increased: M1 macrophages (57.5 → 263.7; P = 0.0002), MHC class II+ M1 macrophages (6.2 → 47.5; P = 0.0004), dendritic cells (2.2 → 32.1; P < 0.0001), and monocytes (8.3 → 103.5; P < 0.0001). In parallel, immunosuppressive populations rose, including regulatory T cells (Tregs) (84.6 → 214.6; P = 0.0002), M2 macrophages (1.2 → 30.9; P < 0.0001), and MHC class II+ M2 macrophages (0.1 → 4.7; P = 0.0009) In GC, treatment significantly increased helper T cells (median 99.2 → 173.4 cells/mm²; P = 0.0052), cytotoxic T cells (36.2 → 70.6; P = 0.0002), and CD4⁺CD8⁺ T cells (5.7 → 9.1; P = 0.0374), indicating enhanced T cell infiltration (Fig. 2B, C, Supplementary Fig. S1B). ESCC exhibited the lowest post-treatment cytotoxic T/Treg ratio (median, HCC 12.6 vs. GC 3.7 vs. ESCC 1.8; P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. S2A).

AI-based immune phenotype analysis (Lunit SCOPE IO)

Baseline immune phenotypes varied by cancer types (Table S8). GC tumors showed a heterogeneous distribution (18.8% inflamed, 43.8% immune-excluded, 37.5% immune-desert), whereas 93.8% of ESCC tumors were immune-desert (P = 0.004). After treatment, phenotype distributions shifted significantly (P = 0.015): GC became predominantly inflamed (56.3%), and ESCC frequently converted to inflamed (43.8%) or immune-excluded (50.0%). Among the 32 patients with paired samples (16 GC, 16 ESCC), 26 (81.3%) underwent phenotypic shifts, more commonly in ESCC (93.8% vs. 68.8%). Conversions from immune-desert to inflamed (37.5% vs. 6.3%) or to immune-excluded (50.0% vs. 31.3%) were significantly enriched in ESCC (P = 0.027) (Fig. 2D, E, Supplementary Table S8).

Although no statistically significant association between immune phenotype and pathologic response was found (Supplementary Table S9), importantly, all MPRs occurred in tumors that became inflamed post-treatment. Regarding PFS, trends diverged by cancer type: in GC, post-treatment inflamed tumors tended to show better outcomes than immune-excluded (P = 0.257) (Supplementary Fig. S3A); in HCC, inflamed or immune-excluded tumors tended to fare better than immune-desert (P = 0.195) (Supplementary Fig. S3C); whereas in ESCC, post-treatment inflamed tumors tended to show worse PFS than immune-excluded or immune-desert types (P = 0.132) (Supplementary Fig. S3E). To further explore this discrepancy, Multiplex IF corroborated these ESCC-specific findings: post-treatment inflamed tumors had higher cytotoxic T/Treg ratios than non-inflamed (median, 2.4 vs, 1.0; P = 0.0442), but significantly lower MHC class II+ M1/M2 macrophage ratio (median, 5.7 vs, 17.2; P = 0.0229), a pattern not seen in GC or HCC (Supplementary Fig. S2B, S2D, and S2F).

When comparing pre- and post-treatment samples, analysis of TILs, macrophages, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells in cancer areas showed a significant increase in TILs in both ESCC (P < 0.001) and GC (P < 0.001). Fibroblasts also showed a significant increase in GC (P = 0.002), whereas no significant changes were observed in the remaining cell populations (Fig. 2F).

Transcriptomic analyses of response and treatment-induced remodeling

To further explore the treatment-associated transcriptional changes, we performed bulk RNA sequencing on paired pre- and post-treatment tumor specimens from patients with ESCC and GC, and on post-treatment specimens from patients with HCC. For transcriptomic analyses, responders were defined as Becker grade 0–2 (<50% viable tumor cells) and non-responders as grade 3 (≥50%).

Pre-treatment transcriptomes (predictors of danburstotug response)

In ESCC, DEA identified two responder‑upregulated genes—NCAM2 (Padj = 9.86 × 10⁻³) and FOXL2NB (Padj = 1.94 × 10⁻²) (Fig. 3A). GSEA revealed enrichment exclusively in non-responders of inflammatory and alloimmune programs, including TNF-α signaling via NF-κB (Padj = 1.69 × 10⁻⁸), Inflammatory Response (Padj = 2.09 × 10⁻³), IL-6/JAK/STAT3 Signaling (Padj = 3.65 × 10⁻²), and Allograft Rejection (Padj = 5.96 × 10⁻³) (Fig. 3A). Transcription factor (TF) activity inference showed higher CEBPA activity in responders (Padj = 4.39 × 10⁻²), whereas REST, MYCN, TFDP1, FOXL2, and FOSL1 were higher in non-responders (all Padj < 0.05) (Fig. 3A)14–18. Although RELA activity did not reach significance (Padj = 8.97 × 10⁻²), RELA (NF-κB subunit) targets most strongly overlapped with GSEA leading-edge genes (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Immune‑cell deconvolution detected no significant differences in the proportions of 32 immune cell types between response groups (Fig. 3B).

Post-treatment analysis

In post-treatment HCC tumors, DEA identified 45 upregulated and 25 downregulated genes in responders. Upregulated genes included HTRA4 (Padj = 3.80 × 10⁻²), UNC5B (Padj = 7.79 × 10⁻3), TES (Padj = 1.76 × 10⁻²), and ZNF382 (Padj = 2.56 × 10⁻²); downregulated genes included CDKL5 (Padj = 3.37 × 10⁻4), ETFA (Padj = 4.26 × 10⁻3), and SUGCT (Padj = 4.92 × 10⁻²) (Fig. 3E)22–27. GSEA revealed enrichment in responders of immune/inflammatory programs (Allograft Rejection, Inflammatory Response) and cell-cycle programs (G2M Checkpoint, E2F Targets), whereas non-responders were enriched for metabolic pathways (Fatty Acid Metabolism, Oxidative Phosphorylation); all FDR-adjusted P-values were <0.05 (Fig. 3E). TF analysis indicated higher activity of SOX10 (Padj = 1.67 × 10⁻²) and MYCN (Padj = 3.33 × 10⁻²) in responders and higher CEBPD (Padj = 1.67 × 10⁻²) and FOXO4 (Padj = 3.33 × 10⁻²) activity in non‑responders; CEBPD and MYCN targets exhibited the largest overlap with GSEA leading‑edge genes (Supplementary Fig. S4C)28–30. Immune‑cell deconvolution did not reveal significant group differences, with nominal increases in adaptive lymphocyte signatures in responders (Fig. 3F).

Treatment-induced reprogramming

Paired pre- and post-treatment analyses revealed that danburstotug reprogrammed tumors toward immune activation. In ESCC, 847 genes were upregulated and 184 were downregulated after treatment (Fig. 4A). GSEA showed a shift from pre‑treatment enrichment of proliferation programs (E2F Targets, G2M Checkpoint; Padj < 0.05) to post‑treatment enrichment of microenvironment/immune programs (EMT, Allograft Rejection, Angiogenesis, Inflammatory Response; Padj < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). TF activity analysis identified 26 TFs with higher pre‑treatment activity (led by E2F4, Padj = 1.44 × 10⁻³) and 45 with higher post‑treatment activity (led by REL, Padj = 7.56 × 10⁻⁵) (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. S4D)31,32. Immune deconvolution showed post‑treatment increases in naive B cells (Padj = 3.46 × 10⁻⁴), and conventional dendritic cells (cDCs) (Padj = 4.14 × 10⁻³) (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

This multicohort phase II study evaluated the feasibility, safety, efficacy, and immunologic effects of neoadjuvant anti-PD-L1 monotherapy with danburstotug in resectable GC, ESCC, and HCC. Based on the pre-specified Simon’s single-stage design, the study did not meet its pre-specified primary endpoint in any cohort, with an MPR rate of 4.2%. Accordingly, this trial should be interpreted as negative with respect to its primary efficacy endpoint. In this context, the principal contribution of this study lie in demonstrating the safety and surgical feasibility of neoadjuvant danburstotug, as well as providing exploratory insights into treatment-induced immune microenvironment remodeling across gastrointestinal tumor types. All patients proceeded to surgery with R0 resection, without treatment-related perioperative morbidity or mortality, supporting the feasibility of this approach.

Clinically, tumor shrinkage and metabolic responses were observed in a subset of patients, with radiologic PR in 17.6% and metabolic PR in 23.3% of evaluable cases. The proportion of patients achieving MPR was low, and the overall efficacy signal remained limited based on the pre-specified primary endpoint. Broader measures of pathologic regression (≤50% residual viable tumor) were observed in 22.9% of patients (HCC 12.5%, GC 25.0%, ESCC 31.3%). These findings suggest biological activity but should be interpreted cautiously in the context of the negative primary outcome.

Neither pretreatment PD-L1 expression nor TMB showed a significant association with pathologic response in our cohort. This finding may be interpreted in two ways. First, these biomarkers may have limited predictive value for response to danburstotug in this setting. Alternatively, the lack of association may reflect the relative lack of biomarker heterogeneity in the study population, in which most patients were microsatellite stable and had low PD-L1 expression, thereby reducing the ability to detect meaningful correlations. In contrast, multiomics-based analyses revealed distinct tumor-type–specific remodeling of the TME. In GC, treatment was associated predominantly with enhanced T cell infiltration, with less pronounced changes in immunosuppressive populations. In contrast, ESCC exhibited a broader remodeling pattern, with concurrent expansion of immune-activating populations (cytotoxic T cells, B cells, M1 macrophages) alongside increase in immunosuppressive subsets such as Tregs and M2 macrophages. This dual remodeling resulted in the lowest post-treatment cytotoxic T/Treg ratio across cohorts, reflecting a suppressive immune balance that was more pronounced in ESCC. Similar paradoxical findings have been reported in other ICI studies of ESCC, where enhanced dendritic and T cell recruitment coexisted with enrichment of immunoregulatory networks, potentially limiting durable antitumor effects37,38. Our results extend these observations by demonstrating that such concurrent immune remodeling can emerge even after only two cycles of neoadjuvant therapy. This apparent paradox may reflect coordinated immune feedback mechanisms, in which activation of effector T cells can simultaneously drive expansion of immunosuppressive populations such as Tregs. Indeed, prior studies have shown that PD-1 blockade can promote Treg accumulation through CD8⁺ T cell–derived IL-2 signaling39, highlighting a biologically coupled process rather than mutually exclusive immune responses.

AI-based spatial profiling (Lunit SCOPE IO) further supported these distinctions. Post-treatment phenotype conversion was common, particularly in ESCC, where nearly all tumors transitioned from immune-desert to either inflamed or immune-excluded states. Importantly, all MPRs occurred in tumors with a post-treatment inflamed phenotype, highlighting the necessity of adaptive immune engagement for pathologic regression. However, inflamed phenotypes in ESCC paradoxically trended toward worse PFS, in contrast to GC and HCC. This observation should be interpreted with caution, as it is based on a very small number of patients and events. These findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating and do not support definitive conclusions. Multiplex IF provided mechanistic insight: although inflamed ESCC tumors displayed higher cytotoxic T/Treg ratios, they also harbored a significantly lower MHC class II⁺ M1/M2 macrophage ratio, suggesting dominance of immunosuppressive myeloid programs. This divergence between infiltration and functional balance highlights the complexity of the ESCC immune landscape, where immune activation alone may not translate into durable control.

Transcriptomic profiling corroborated these tumor-type–specific patterns. In ESCC, pre-treatment biomarkers for responders were characterized by upregulation of NCAM2 (a neural cell adhesion molecule primarily involved in cell–cell interactions and neural synapse formation, with potential implications for tumor cell interaction) and FOXL2NB (a regulatory locus near FOXL2 with potential transcriptional control functions). By contrast, non-responders were enriched for dysregulated inflammatory pathways—TNF-α signaling via NF-κB, IL-6/JAK/STAT3, Inflammatory Response, and Allograft Rejection—suggesting that pre-existing maladaptive inflammation may prime ESCC tumors for resistance. In GC responders exhibited higher expression of DKK4 (a Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibitor that may mitigate immune-exclusion)19 DNAH9 (a dynein heavy chain gene primarily involved in ciliary function and cytoskeletal organization, with potential implications in tumor biology), and ACY3 (an enzyme of amino acid metabolism potentially influencing TME composition). In contrast, non-responders expressed higher levels of DUSP9 (a dual-specificity phosphatase that negatively regulates MAPK signaling, implicated in controlling cell proliferation and stress responses, with potential effects on immune pathways) and MYO18B (a cytoskeletal tumor suppressor frequently inactivated in cancer). Notably, GC responders demonstrated enrichment of the same pro-inflammatory pathways that signified resistance in ESCC, emphasizing a striking biological divergence: where inflammation was maladaptive in ESCC, it was productive in GC, promoting effective immune priming and tumor regression.

In post-treatment HCC tumors, responders upregulated HTRA4 (a stress-response serine protease), UNC5B (a dependence receptor regulating apoptosis and angiogenesis), TES (a cytoskeletal tumor suppressor), and ZNF382 (a zinc-finger transcriptional repressor with tumor-suppressive properties). Non-responders, conversely, upregulated metabolic genes such as ETFA and SUGCT (key mitochondrial enzymes in fatty acid oxidation and the TCA cycle), suggesting that metabolic reprogramming may blunt immune cytotoxicity and represent a distinct resistance mechanism in HCC.

Paired pre- and post-treatment analyses in both GC and ESCC further revealed that danburstotug induced a global transcriptional shift from proliferative to immune-driven programs. This was evidenced by the suppression of pathways regulated by the E2F family of TFs (e.g., E2F Targets, G2M Checkpoint) and the concomitant upregulation of immune activation signatures such as Allograft Rejection, with IFN-γ enrichment observed particularly in GC. TF activity analysis consistent with this shift, showing that ESCC tumors transitioned from proliferation-linked factors such as E2F4 toward immune regulators like REL, while GC tumors shifted from FOXM1 toward RFX5 (a key regulator of MHC class II expression)40, STAT4 (critical for IL-12–driven Th1 differentiation and IFN-γ production)41, and IRF1 (a central mediator of interferon responses and antigen-presentation programs)42. These findings confirm that danburstotug can reprogram the TME toward immune activation, but the baseline transcriptional state dictates whether this programming results in productive or futile anti-tumor immunity.

Together, these results underscore that the efficacy of PD-L1 blockade is not dictated solely by tumor shrinkage or MPR, but also by the qualitative nature of immune remodeling within the TME. In GC and HCC, immune activation occurred in a manner consistent with effective T-cell priming, leading to meaningful regression in a subset of patients. In contrast, ESCC displayed paradoxical remodeling in which immune activation was counterbalanced by myeloid and regulatory suppression. This duality may explain the modest depth of pathologic response despite strong immune engagement, and highlights ESCC as a disease where rational combination strategies—such as pairing PD-L1 blockade with myeloid targeting or Treg-depleting agents—may be essential to realize the full therapeutic potential of checkpoint blockade.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size, particularly within individual cancer cohorts, limited statistical power for subgroup analyses. In addition, the small number of responders defined by the alternative threshold (<50% viable tumor) further constrained the robustness of the translational analyses and introduced a potential risk of overfitting in the gene expression signatures identified from bulk RNA sequencing. Second, the absence of paired pretreatment samples in the HCC cohort limited our ability to evaluate treatment-induced immune remodeling. This reflects clinical practice where baseline biopsy is not routinely performed and poses a challenge for biomarker development in neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Accordingly, comparisons of transcriptomic findings between HCC and the paired analyses in GC and ESCC should be interpreted with caution. Third, because genomic sequencing was performed in a clinical testing context, access to detailed mutation-level genomic data was limited, restricting additional exploratory genomic analyses integrating genomic alterations with immune phenotypes and transcriptional profiles. Fourth, the single-arm study design without a control group limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the clinical efficacy of danburstotug. In addition, the high proportion of early-stage disease and the limited number of events limit the interpretation of survival outcomes. Furthermore, the small number of events results in wide confidence intervals, with upper bounds approaching 100%, reflecting substantial statistical uncertainty. Future studies with larger cohorts and controlled designs will be needed to validate these findings.

In conclusion, although this study did not meet its pre-specified primary endpoint, neoadjuvant danburstotug was safe and surgically feasible, and provided detailed insights into immune microenvironment remodeling across resectable GC, ESCC, and HCC. These findings highlight the potential value of comprehensive biomarker profiling beyond PD-L1 or TMB and support further investigation of multidimensional approaches integrating spatial immune phenotypes, transcriptional programs, and effector-to-suppressor balance, as well as rational combination strategies in this setting.

Methods

Trial design and participants

The NeoChance study was a prospective, single-center, multi-cohort, open-label, phase II trial evaluating danburstotug as neoadjuvant therapy for resectable GC, ESCC, and HCC. Eligible patients were ≥19 years with histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma, ESCC, or HCC (for HCC, clinical diagnosis per the American Association for the Study of Liver Disease (AASLD) guidelines was acceptable)43. Patients were required to have potentially resectable localized disease, defined by the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer staging system as follows: ≥T2 or N+ for GC, ≥T1b or N+ for ESCC, and ≤3 liver lesions without invasion of the main portal trunk for HCC44. Additional eligibility criteria included measurable or evaluable lesions per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) v1.145, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1, and adequate organ function. Pretreatment biopsy and post-treatment surgical samples were mandated for biomarker studies, except that pretreatment biopsy was optional in HCC, as HCC is commonly diagnosed based on characteristic radiological findings without histologic confirmation. Key exclusion criteria were prior therapy (except HCC patients with prior local therapy ≥6 months earlier and subsequently experienced resectable disease), prior ICI therapy, active autoimmune disease or immunodeficiency, organ transplantation, uncontrolled infection, or other recent malignancies.

The study was approved by the institutional review board of Asan Medical Center (Seoul, South Korea) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04196465). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the trial was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Treatment and assessments

Patients received two cycles of danburstotug (20 mg/kg IV every two weeks) and underwent surgery 11–42 days after the final dose. Surgical procedures adhered to international standards, and postoperative management, including adjuvant therapy or surveillance, was at the investigators’ discretion per guidelines.

Baseline staging included thoraco-abdomino-pelvic computed tomography (CT) and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography (PET); upper endoscopy was performed in GC and ESCC, and endoscopic ultrasound additionally in ESCC. After completion of neoadjuvant therapy, radiographic responses were evaluated using CT according to RECIST v1.1, and metabolic response, when applicable, by the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC)-PET criteria46. Pathologic response was assessed using the Becker criteria: grade 1a (no viable tumor), 1b (<10%), 2 (10–50%), and 3 (>50%)47.

After resection, patients were followed with clinic visits and CT scans every three months for two years and every six months thereafter up to five years. Annual upper endoscopy was performed in GC and ESCC. Adverse events (AEs) were graded per the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 4.03.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was the major pathologic response (MPR) rate, defined as <10% residual viable tumor cells at surgery. Secondary endpoints included feasibility of neoadjuvant danburstotug, assessed by the proportion of patients who underwent surgery without delays (>8 weeks from treatment initiation), safety, R0 resection rate, objective response rate (complete response [CR] + partial response [PR]), disease control rate, (CR + PR + stable disease [SD]), progression-free survival (PFS), recurrence-free survival, overall survival (OS), patterns of failure, and exploratory biomarker analyses.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining and analysis

Multiplex immunofluorescence (IF) staining was performed on pretreatment biopsy and post-treatment resection samples to characterize the TME. Two antibody panels were designed: Panel 1 (CK, DAPI, CD4, CD8, FOXP3, CD20, PD-L1) and Panel 2 (CK, DAPI, CD56, CD68, CD206, CD11c, MHC class II) (Supplementary Table S1). Staining was conducted on 4-μm formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections using the Leica Bond Rx Automated Stainer (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany) following standard protocols (Supplementary Tables S1, 2).

Slides were scanned with the Vectra Polaris Automated Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA). Image processing and cell segmentation were performed using Phenochart and inForm Image Analysis software (Akoya Biosciences). Tumor areas were defined by CK expression, and single-cell segmentation was based on DAPI. Phenotypes were assigned according to marker expression and intensity. Regions of interest (ROIs) were analyzed in batch, and quantitative data were exported into R for downstream processing using the phenoptr and phenoptrReport packages (Akoya Biosciences).

AI-based immune phenotype analysis

Lunit SCOPE IO (Lunit Inc., Seoul, Korea) is an advanced artificial intelligence (AI)-powered platform that classifies tumor immune phenotypes (inflamed, immune-excluded, immune-desert) based on the spatial distribution of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in hematoxylin and eosin–stained whole-slide images (WSIs)48–52. The platform quantifies intratumoral and stromal TILs densities using validated cell detection and tissue segmentation models. Immune phenotypes are defined at the patch level (0.25 mm²): inflamed if intratumoral TIL density ≥130/mm²; immune-excluded if intratumoral TIL density <130/mm² with stromal TIL density ≥260/mm²; and immune-desert otherwise. At the WSI level, tumors are categorized as inflamed if ≥33.3% of patches are inflamed; immune-excluded if ≥33.3% of patches are immune-excluded and <33.3% are inflamed; and immune-desert in all other cases44–48. For more detailed characterization of the TME, we further applied an expanded version of the AI-powered TME analyzer, capable of segmenting and quantifying not only TILs but also macrophages, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells in cancer areas53.

Targeted next-generation sequencing

Targeted next-generation sequencing was performed on pretreatment biopsy or post-treatment surgical specimens using the MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was conducted as part of routine clinical test using the OncoPanel AMC version 4.0, an in-house panel covering 323 cancer-related genes and selected intronic regions recurrently rearranged in cancer, as previously described54,55.

RNA-sequencing and data processing

RNA-seq was performed on snap-frozen tumor tissues collected at baseline (except for HCC) and at surgery. RNA with Integrity Number >7 was extracted using QIAGEN kits, and libraries were prepared with the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina). Sequencing was conducted on the NovaSeq platform (Illumina, 2 × 100 bp paired-end reads).Reads were quality-trimmed and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1)56. Gene expression was quantified with StringTie (v2.1.4)57, and gene-level count matrices were generated for differential expression analysis (DEA). Batch effects were corrected using established R packages (RUVSeq, SVA, ComBat-Seq, and limma). For exploratory analyses, responders were defined as Becker grade 0–2 (<50% viable tumor cells) to enable biologically meaningful comparisons, given the limited number of patients meeting the strict MPR definition (<10%). Accordingly, all translational findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating in nature. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2 (v1.42.1)58, applying Benjamini-Hochberg correction (adjusted P < 0.05). Gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) were conducted with fgsea (v1.28.0) using MSigDB Hallmark59 and Gene Ontology Biological Process gene sets. Transcript- and isoform-level analyses used Salmon (v1.10.0)60 and IsoformSwitchAnalyzeR package (v2.2.0)61. Gene co-expression networks were constructed using the Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis package (v1.73)62 and visualized with the igraph (v2.1.1) to identify hub genes and module relationships.

Statistical analysis

The planned sample size was 48 patients (16 per cohort), based on Simon’s single-stage design with 80% power to detect an MPR rate of 20% (P1), versus a null rate of 5% (P0), allowing for a two-sided alpha error of 15.3% and a 10% dropout rate. The null hypothesis would be rejected if at least 2 of 14 evaluable patients in a cohort achieved an MPR.

Safety analyses included all patients who received at least one dose of danburstotug. Efficacy analyses were performed in the per-protocol population, defined as all eligible patients who received at least one dose of danburstotug and were evaluable for efficacy. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables using the Mann–Whitney U-test, Kruskal-Wallis test, or Wilcoxon signed-rank test, as appropriate. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses and the limited sample size, correction for multiple comparisons was not applied, and the results should be interpreted with caution given the potential for false positives. Time-to-event outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test.

For transcriptomic analyses, DEGs were identified using the Wald test, and P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (reported as adjusted P-values, Padj). GSEA was performed to assess pathway-level differences. Transcription factor activity was inferred using both t-tests and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, depending on data distribution. All P-values were two-sided, and values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
