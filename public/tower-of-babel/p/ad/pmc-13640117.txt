Association of immunohistochemistry-based molecular subtypes with adnexal involvement in endometrial cancer
Authors: Oğuz Kaan Köksal, Hande Nur Öncü, Firdevs Şahin Duran, Şahin Kaan Baydemir, Neslihan Öztürk, Gökçen Ege, Candost Hanedan, Vakkas Korkmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdnexal involvement in endometrial cancer is traditionally regarded as an anatomical spread pattern within FIGO stage IIIA; however, its biological heterogeneity remains incompletely understood. The 2023 FIGO staging system distinguishes between favorable “synchronous” ovarian involvement (stage IA3) and true metastatic spread to the ovary (stage IIIA1), highlighting the biological heterogeneity of adnexal involvement. This study aimed to investigate the association between immunohistochemistry (IHC)-based molecular subtypes and adnexal involvement, together with relevant clinicopathological factors.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center study included patients with surgically staged endometrial cancer. Tumors were classified into p53-abnormal, mismatch repair-deficient (MMRd) and non-specific molecular profile (NSMP) subtypes based on immunohistochemistry. Associations between adnexal involvement and molecular subtypes, preoperative serum CA-125 levels, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) and other clinicopathological variables were analyzed.ResultsAmong 661 patients, adnexal involvement was identified in 3.8% (n = 25) and varied significantly across molecular subtypes (p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, p53-abnormal tumors showed a strong independent association with adnexal involvement (aOR 5.15, 95% CI 1.87–14.19, p = 0.001), whereas NSMP tumors were associated with lower risk. Elevated preoperative CA-125 levels (aOR 1.01, p < 0.001) and the presence of LVSI (aOR 6.62, p < 0.001) were also independent predictors.ConclusionIHC-based molecular subtypes are significantly associated with adnexal involvement in endometrial cancer. Integrating molecular classification with clinicopathological factors may enhance more individualized surgical planning, particularly regarding ovarian preservation decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16510-4.Keywords: Adnexal involvement, Endometrial cancer, Immunohistochemistry, Molecular classification, Risk stratification

Introduction

Endometrial cancer is the most common gynecological malignancy in developed countries, with a large proportion of patients diagnosed at an early stage [1, 2]. Traditionally, the presence of adnexal involvement has been considered an indicator of advanced disease and poor prognosis in endometrial cancer [3]. Most synchronous endometrioid carcinomas of the endometrium and ovaries are clonally related and have traditionally been considered as disseminated disease with poor prognosis. However, low-grade endometrioid tumors without clinicopathological risk factors generally show indolent biological behavior and can be managed as independent rather than metastasized tumors [4, 5]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that adnexal involvement represents a biologically heterogeneous condition rather than a uniform marker of advanced disease [2, 3]. A better understanding of the biological heterogeneity of adnexal involvement has direct clinical implications for determining the scope of surgery, ovarian preservation decisions, and personalizing adjuvant treatment strategies [6, 7].

Previous studies and systematic reviews have identified deep myometrial invasion, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), non-endometrioid histology, cervical invasion, and lymph node metastasis as the main clinicopathological factors associated with adnexal involvement [2, 3, 8, 9]. However, significant variability has been observed in the presence of adnexal involvement even among patients with similar clinicopathological features, suggesting that these factors—which primarily reflect anatomical tumor spread—may be insufficient to fully explain the biological heterogeneity of adnexal involvement [3, 9].

Preoperative serum CA-125 levels have been extensively investigated as a biomarker associated with extrauterine disease, lymph node metastasis, and ovarian involvement in endometrial cancer [10–13]. Although high CA-125 levels have been consistently shown to be associated with advanced disease and aggressive clinicopathological features, CA-125 is generally considered an indirect marker reflecting tumor burden and disease extent rather than intrinsic tumor biology [10, 11, 13]. Accordingly, CA-125 may provide valuable complementary information but lacks sufficient specificity to reflect intrinsic tumor biology when used alone.

In recent years, the integration of TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas)-based molecular classification into the clinical management of endometrial cancer has significantly improved prognostic assessment [14]. The immunohistochemistry (IHC)-based ProMisE classification, a practical application of this approach, divides tumors into four molecular subtypes: POLE-mutant, mismatch repair-deficient (MMRd), non specific molecular profile (NSMP), and p53-abnormal [13, 15, 16]. Among these subtypes, p53-abnormal tumors in particular have been shown to be strongly associated with aggressive biological behavior and adverse clinical outcomes [9, 15, 16].

This biology-based approach, integrated into the FIGO 2023 staging system, emphasizes that prognostic assessment should not be limited to anatomical spread alone [6, 17]. Specifically, FIGO 2023 highlights the biological heterogeneity of adnexal involvement and recommends distinguishing low-risk synchronous ovarian involvement from true metastatic adnexal disease, underscoring the importance of interpreting adnexal spread within a molecular context [6, 17].

Current ESGO–ESTRO–ESP guidelines recommend integrating molecular classification into risk group identification and treatment decision-making processes; specifically noting that the p53-abnormal molecular subtype is strongly associated with aggressive biological behavior [7, 18]. The updated 2025 ESGO-ESTRO-ESP guidelines specifically recommend that ovarian preservation be considered only in premenopausal patients younger than 45 years with low recurrence risk based on both histopathological and molecular classification [7, 18]. However, despite these developments, studies systematically evaluating the association between molecular subtypes and adnexal involvement in large, well-characterized patient cohorts remain limited. Furthermore, holistic analyses that consider this relationship together with classical clinicopathological factors and biomarkers are insufficient in the literature.

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the association between molecular subtypes and adnexal involvement in patients with endometrial cancer. Secondary objectives were to assess the relationship between adnexal involvement, clinicopathological variables and preoperative CA-125 levels.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a single-center, retrospective observational study conducted at the Department of Gynecologic Oncology, Ankara Etlik City Hospital. Patients with a diagnosis of endometrial cancer who underwent primary surgical treatment between November 2022 and June 2025 were retrospectively evaluated. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (AEŞH-BADEK-2025-0267), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The study population consisted of patients aged 18 years or older with a histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of endometrial cancer who underwent total hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy as part of primary surgical treatment. Pelvic and/or paraaortic lymph node assessment was performed according to clinical indications. Patients were included if primary surgery was performed at our institution and complete clinical, pathological and immunohistochemical data required for molecular classification were available. Patients who received neoadjuvant therapy, had a history of synchronous or metachronous malignancy, had insufficient pathological material or incomplete immunohistochemical evaluation or had missing clinical, pathological or laboratory data were excluded. The patient selection process and formation of the study cohort are summarized in Fig. 1.

Patient selection flowchart. A total of 713 patients with endometrial cancer were initially evaluated. After exclusion of 52 patients (FIGO stage IV disease n = 21, missing molecular classification data n = 30, second primary adnexal tumor n = 1), 661 patients were included in the final analysis. Demographic and clinicopathological variables, including age, body mass index (BMI), preoperative serum CA-125 levels, tumor size, histological subtype, tumor grade, depth of myometrial invasion, cervical stromal invasion, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), lymph node status and tumor stage according to the FIGO 2023 staging system, were extracted from medical records and pathology reports.

All surgical procedures were performed at a tertiary gynecologic oncology center by experienced gynecologic oncologists, in accordance with standard oncological surgical principles. The surgical approach (minimally invasive or open surgery) and the extent of lymph node assessment were individualized based on patient-specific clinical characteristics and contemporary guideline recommendations [7, 18].

Adnexal involvement was defined as the presence of tumor on histopathological examination of the ovaries and/or fallopian tubes. All histopathological evaluations were performed by experienced gynecopathologists. Adnexal involvement was classified according to pathological findings in accordance with the FIGO 2023 staging system. Assessment of lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) was performed based on pathology reports, in accordance with World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [19]. The distinction between adnexal metastasis from endometrial carcinoma and a synchronous independent primary ovarian malignancy was made by experienced gynecopathologists based on established morphological criteria, including tumor histotype concordance with the endometrial primary, pattern of ovarian involvement (surface vs. parenchymal), and absence of an independent ovarian precursor lesion.

Tumors were classified into molecular subtypes using the immunohistochemistry-based ProMisE approach. Immunohistochemical evaluation included assessment of p53 expression status and mismatch repair (MMR) proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6). p53 abnormality was defined as diffuse strong nuclear overexpression (> 75% of tumor cells) or complete absence (null pattern) of p53 staining. MMR deficiency was defined as loss of nuclear expression of one or more MMR proteins. Tumors with intact MMR expression and wild-type p53 pattern were classified as non-specific molecular profile (NSMP). Immunohistochemical staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections using the following antibodies: anti-p53 (clone SP5, rabbit monoclonal, LabVision), anti-MLH1 (clone BS29, mouse monoclonal, LabVision), anti-MSH2 (clone FE11, mouse monoclonal, LabVision), anti-MSH6 (clone EP49, rabbit monoclonal, LabVision), and anti-PMS2 (clone EP51, rabbit monoclonal, LabVision). Cases demonstrating cytoplasmic-only p53 staining without nuclear positivity were not classified as p53-abnormal. Subclonal or heterogeneous staining patterns were evaluated according to the predominant staining pattern across the tumor, in accordance with current interpretation guidelines.

As POLE mutation analysis is not routinely performed at our institution, the POLE-mutant subtype was not included in the analysis. In cases with overlapping immunohistochemical abnormalities (e.g., concurrent p53 abnormality and MMR deficiency), molecular classification was assigned according to the hierarchical approach prioritizing MMR status over p53 status, as recommended in current classification algorithms [15, 16, 18]. No case classified as p53-abnormal in this cohort demonstrated concurrent MMR deficiency.

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi 2.6.45 (The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). Continuous variables were assessed for distributional characteristics and were presented as median (minimum–maximum) or mean ± standard deviation as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups with and without adnexal involvement were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables as appropriate. In analyses of molecular classification, each immunohistochemistry-based molecular subtype was evaluated as an independent binary variable, comparing the presence of the relevant subtype with the group comprising all other molecular subtypes. Accordingly, odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated separately for p53-abnormal, NSMP, and MMRd tumors with the comparison group consisting of all remaining cases. To identify factors independently associated with adnexal involvement, logistic regression analyses were performed. Variables demonstrating statistical significance (p < 0.10) in univariable analyses, along with clinically relevant variables, were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF < 5), and model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, a total of 713 patients diagnosed with endometrial cancer were retrospectively evaluated. Of these, 52 patients were excluded from the study due to not meeting the inclusion criteria, including FIGO stage IV disease (n = 21), missing immunohistochemical molecular classification data (n = 30), and the presence of a second primary adnexal tumor (n = 1). Consequently, 661 patients were included in the final analysis. Among these patients, 636 (96.2%) had no adnexal involvement, whereas 25 patients (3.8%) were identified with adnexal involvement. The patient selection process and study flow are illustrated in Fig. 1.

The median age was 60 years (range, 27–86) and the median body mass index (BMI) was 32 kg/m² (range, 19–64). The median parity was 3 (range, 0–11). The median tumor size was 35 mm (range, 2-300) and the median serum CA-125 level was 13.3 U/mL (range, 3-4290).

Adnexal involvement was detected in 25 patients (3.8%), while 636 patients (96.2%) had no evidence of adnexal involvement. Among the 25 patients with adnexal involvement, all had ovarian involvement (n = 25, 100%), while 11 patients (44.0%) had concurrent fallopian tube involvement; isolated fallopian tube involvement without ovarian involvement was not observed. The most common surgical approach was laparoscopy (49.8%), followed by laparotomy (28.1%) and robotic surgery (18.9%). According to the FIGO 2023 staging system, 414 patients (62.6%) were classified as stage I, 51 patients (7.7%) as stage II, and 196 patients (29.7%) as stage III. Endometrioid histology was the predominant subtype (82.5%). Basic demographic, clinical and pathological characteristics of study population are summarized in Table 1.

Patients with adnexal involvement had significantly larger tumors (median 55.0 mm vs. 35.0 mm, p = 0.012) and higher CA-125 levels (median 67.0 U/mL vs. 13.0 U/mL, p < 0.001) than those without adnexal involvement whereas no significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age or BMI (p > 0.05 for both).

Advanced disease was more frequent in patients with adnexal involvement; 88.0% were classified as FIGO stage III compared to 10.0% in the group without adnexal involvement (p < 0.001). Lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) was significantly more common in the adnexal involvement group (60.0% vs. 14.2%, p < 0.001). Deep myometrial invasion (≥ 50%) was observed in 72.0% of patients with adnexal involvement, compared to 32.2% in those without involvement (p < 0.001). Among patients who underwent lymph node evaluation (n = 526), lymph node metastasis was significantly more frequent in patients with adnexal involvement (47.8% vs. 9.7%, p < 0.001). Among the 25 patients with adnexal involvement, the FIGO 2023 stage distribution was as follows: 3 patients (12.0%) were classified as stage IA3, representing low-risk synchronous ovarian involvement, while 7 patients (28.0%) were classified as stage IIIA1, representing metastatic adnexal spread. The remaining 15 patients (60.0%) had more advanced disease (stages IIIA2 through IIIC2). Notably, among the 3 patients classified as stage IA3, molecular subtypes were NSMP (n = 2) and MMRd (n = 1); none were p53-abnormal. Menopausal status was available for all patients with adnexal involvement; 7 patients (28.0%) were premenopausal and 18 patients (72.0%) were postmenopausal. Among the 7 premenopausal patients with adnexal involvement, molecular subtypes were NSMP (n = 4, 57.1%), MMRd (n = 2, 28.6%), and p53-abnormal (n = 1, 14.3%).

Serous histology and higher tumor grade among endometrioid tumors were more common in patients with adnexal involvement (p < 0.001). Detailed comparisons of clinicopathological features according to adnexal involvement are presented in Table 2.

To further address potential information loss from combining heterogeneous subtypes, we additionally performed a multivariable analysis using NSMP as the reference molecular subtype. In this model, p53-abnormal status remained significantly associated with adnexal involvement (OR 4.63, 95% CI 1.54–13.94, p = 0.006), while MMRd status was not significantly different from NSMP (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.13–3.74, p = 0.674), consistent with the primary analysis. These results are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Given the well-established association between p53-abnormal status and serous histology, the relationship between these variables was further examined. Linear regression analysis demonstrated a strong association between p53-abnormal status and histological subtype (R²=0.519), indicating substantial overlap between these two variables. When histological subtype was incorporated into the multivariable model together with p53-abnormal status, p53-abnormal status lost statistical significance, while serous histology emerged as an independent predictor of adnexal involvement (OR 10.70, 95% CI 1.51–75.85, p = 0.017).

In a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients who underwent lymph node assessment (n = 526), p53-abnormal subtype (OR 4.03, 95% CI 1.44–11.24, p = 0.008), CA-125 (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.003–1.014, p = 0.004), and LVSI (OR 5.40, 95% CI 1.65–17.68, p = 0.005) remained independent predictors of adnexal involvement, consistent with the primary analysis. These results are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a significant association between adnexal involvement and immunohistochemistry-based molecular subtypes. Our findings suggest that adnexal involvement should not be regarded solely as a manifestation of anatomical tumor spread but rather as a biologically heterogeneous process shaped by underlying tumor biology. In particular, adnexal involvement was significantly more frequent in the p53-abnormal subtype and less common in the NSMP subtype, supporting distinct biological behaviors among molecular subgroups.

In our cohort, adnexal involvement was observed in 3.8% of cases (n = 25), a prevalence consistent with contemporary surgical series that employ comprehensive staging with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and exclude stage IV disease [2, 3, 9]. While this relatively low incidence reflects current surgical practice patterns, it also presents methodological considerations for statistical power, particularly in subgroup analyses. Notably, the distribution of adnexal involvement varied substantially across molecular subtypes: p53-abnormal tumors accounted for 60.0% of cases with adnexal involvement (n = 15) despite representing only 15.6% of the overall cohort, whereas MMRd tumors demonstrated unexpectedly low rates (n = 3, 12.0%). This disproportionate distribution underscores the biological heterogeneity of adnexal spread and suggests distinct metastatic mechanisms across molecular subgroups.

In the literature, predictors of adnexal involvement have predominantly been evaluated using classical clinicopathological factors. Deep myometrial invasion, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), high tumor grade, non-endometrioid histology and elevated preoperative CA-125 levels are among the factors most frequently associated with adnexal involvement [2, 3, 8, 20]. These findings suggest that adnexal involvement generally occurs in tumors with more aggressive biological behavior [3, 9]. However, in most previous studies, adnexal involvement has primarily been considered an anatomical pattern of spread within the framework of FIGO stage IIIA, whereas its relationship with molecular tumor biology has been explored only to a limited extent [6, 17, 20].

In the evaluation based on molecular subtypes, the high rates of adnexal involvement observed in p53-abnormal tumors are consistent with the well-established aggressive biological behavior and increased tendency for extrauterine spread of this subtype [14, 16, 21]. In contrast, adnexal involvement in mismatch repair-deficient (MMRd) tumors showed a more heterogeneous distribution, suggesting that adnexal involvement in the MMRd subtype may be influenced by accompanying clinicopathological factors rather than molecular features alone [14, 16]. The rarity of adnexal involvement in the NSMP subtype is consistent with the more indolent biological characteristics of this group [14, 15].

In our study, preoperative serum CA-125 levels and the presence of LVSI were strongly associated with adnexal involvement. Elevated CA-125 levels have been reported in the literature to be associated with extrauterine spread and ovarian metastasis [10, 12, 13]. Similarly, the presence of LVSI increases the risk of adnexal involvement as a marker of invasive tumor biology [8, 9]. These findings suggest that integrating molecular subtypes with classical clinicopathological factors such as CA-125 and LVSI may strengthen clinical risk stratification and prediction [13–16].

An important methodological consideration concerns the relationship between p53-abnormal status and histological subtype, given that serous carcinomas predominantly harbor p53 abnormalities. When histological subtype was incorporated into the multivariable model alongside p53-abnormal status, p53-abnormal status lost statistical significance, while serous histology emerged as the dominant predictor, reflecting a strong association between these two variables (linear regression R²=0.519), indicating substantial overlap in the biological information they capture. The loss of significance of p53-abnormal status after adjustment for histology suggests that the observed association may partially reflect the close biological relationship between p53 abnormality and serous carcinoma, rather than an entirely independent effect. This finding is consistent with well-established biology, as serous carcinomas are characterized by p53 mutations in the vast majority of cases. From a clinical standpoint, however, p53-abnormal status offers a practical advantage over histological classification: it can be reliably determined preoperatively on endometrial biopsy specimens via immunohistochemistry, whereas definitive histological subtyping—particularly distinguishing high-grade endometrioid from serous carcinoma—often requires final surgical pathology. Therefore, despite its biological overlap with histology, p53-abnormal status may serve as an earlier and more readily available preoperative biomarker for risk stratification.

The FIGO 2023 staging system provides a contemporary framework for endometrial cancer that incorporates not only anatomical extent but also biological behavior [6, 17]. Although adnexal involvement is categorized within FIGO stage IIIA1 (and, in select low-risk cases, stage IA3), this group is recognized as biologically heterogeneous [6, 17]. In our study, the observation that a small proportion of cases with ovarian involvement were classified as IA3 according to FIGO 2023 suggests that ovarian involvement does not invariably reflect advanced biological aggressiveness [6, 17, 22].

The strengths of this study include a relatively large patient series from a single center and the evaluation of all cases using immunohistochemistry-based molecular classification. Furthermore, the exclusion of FIGO stage IV cases allowed for the assessment of adnexal involvement within a more homogeneous patient population. The integration of molecular subtypes with classical clinicopathological factors enhances the clinical applicability of the findings.

Several methodological considerations merit discussion. First, while the observed rate of adnexal involvement (3.8%, n = 25) is consistent with contemporary surgical series [2, 3, 9, 20], the limited number of events reduced statistical power for subgroup analyses. This limitation is particularly notable for the MMRd subtype, where only three cases with adnexal involvement were identified, precluding definitive conclusions regarding this molecular subgroup. Second, the relatively wide confidence intervals observed in multivariable analysis, particularly for the p53-abnormal association (OR5.15, 95% CI 1.87–14.19), reflect sample size constraints and underscore the need for external validation in larger, multicenter cohorts. The limited number of outcome events relative to the number of covariates included in the multivariable model may also increase the risk of model overfitting. Third, although our single-center design ensured uniform pathological assessment and molecular classification, it may limit generalizability to centers with different patient populations or staging protocols. Fourth, although a detailed analysis stratified by menopausal status was not feasible due to limited subgroup size, among the 25 patients with adnexal involvement, 7 (28.0%) were premenopausal, and the majority of these cases (6/7, 85.7%) had NSMP or MMRd subtypes rather than p53-abnormal tumors, indicating that adnexal spread is not restricted to aggressive molecular subtypes and that molecular classification alone may not be sufficient to exclude the risk of adnexal involvement in premenopausal patients being considered for ovarian preservation. Despite these limitations, the strong and independent association observed between the p53-abnormal subtype and adnexal involvement (OR 5.15, p = 0.001) provides clinically meaningful insight that warrants further investigation.

As with all immunohistochemistry-based studies, inter-observer variability in IHC interpretation constitutes an inherent limitation. In particular, p53 staining patterns— especially the distinction between weak wild-type and null patterns, and between strong overexpressing wild-type and mutation-related overexpression—may occasionally be subject to interobserver differences in interpretation among pathologists. In the present study, all IHC evaluations were performed by experienced gynecopathologists at a single center, which minimized, but did not completely eliminate, this potential source of variability.

The inability to perform POLE mutation analysis constitutes another limitation of the study. Since POLE-mutant tumors are known to be associated with favorable prognosis despite occasionally exhibiting advanced pathological features, potential misclassification of these tumors as NSMP or MMRd may have affected the interpretation of these subgroups. Specifically, possible POLE-mutant cases misclassified as NSMP or MMRd may have contributed to the relatively low rates of adnexal involvement observed in these groups, and this possibility cannot be ruled out without universal POLE testing. However, considering that POLE testing has not yet been fully integrated into routine clinical practice in many centers [18, 19, 23, 24], our findings suggest that IHC-based molecular classification alone can provide clinicians with meaningful biological information in real-world conditions. Future studies applying full molecular profiling including all four classifiers will be critical for fully elucidating the association of each molecular subtype with adnexal involvement.

Regarding the preoperative detectability of adnexal involvement, current imaging modalities have limited sensitivity for microscopic or early adnexal spread, and definitive diagnosis is typically established only through final surgical pathology. In this context, preoperative biomarkers such as molecular subtype and CA-125, although unable to directly visualize adnexal disease, may help identify patients at higher risk who warrant more careful intraoperative evaluation. These findings have direct clinical significance, particularly for decisions regarding ovarian preservation in premenopausal women with early-stage endometrial cancer. While current guidelines acknowledge that ovarian preservation may be considered in selected low-risk patients, molecular subtype has not yet been systematically integrated into this decision-making process [7, 18]. Recent work by Manning-Geist et al. similarly demonstrated that molecular subtype, alongside pathological factors, can help identify endometrioid endometrial cancer patients at higher risk of concurrent or recurrent ovarian disease, supporting the relevance of molecular classification in ovarian preservation decisions [25]. Our findings demonstrate that p53-abnormal tumors are associated with significantly higher rates of adnexal involvement, supporting a more cautious approach to ovarian preservation in this subgroup. However, the observation that adnexal involvement also occurred in a subset of premenopausal patients with NSMP or MMRd tumors indicates that favorable molecular subtype alone does not eliminate this risk, underscoring the need for comprehensive preoperative risk assessment rather than reliance on molecular classification in isolation. Furthermore, evaluating molecular subtype together with preoperative CA-125 levels and LVSI status (both of which were identified as independent predictors of adnexal involvement in the present study) could strengthen preoperative risk stratification algorithms and contribute to more individualized surgical planning.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that adnexal involvement in endometrial cancer reflects not only anatomical spread but also underlying tumor biology, with molecular subtypes serving as independent biological determinants. While limited by sample size for certain molecular subgroups, the strong association between the p53-abnormal subtype and adnexal involvement (OR 5.15, p = 0.001) has direct clinical implications for preoperative risk stratification and surgical planning, particularly regarding ovarian preservation. These findings support incorporating molecular classification as one component of surgical decision-making and underscore the biological heterogeneity underlying adnexal spread. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these associations across all molecular subtypes and to further elucidate the mechanisms of adnexal involvement in different biological contexts.
