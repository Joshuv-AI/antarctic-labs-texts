Surveying the features of screening strategies for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease: A scoping review
Authors: Nazanin Mir, Hamid R Baradaran, Seidamir Pasha Tabaeian, Nasrin Abolhasanbeigi Gallehzan, Aziz Rezapour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most common chronic liver disease, affecting about one-quarter of the general population worldwide. Owing to the prevalence and high burden of NAFLD in special groups, this study aimed to examine the features of screening strategies for NAFLD in special populations. We carried out a scoping review using the main online databases (Medline (PubMed), Scopus, and Web of Science) with the following keywords: Screening, Nonalcoholic fatty liver, willingness to pay, economic evaluation, and preferences. We included publications from January 1, 2000, to August 27, 2024, which focused on NAFLD screening strategies in special populations. Quality assessment of the included studies was performed using the QADAS checklist. The AUC ranged from 0.5 to 93.30, the maximum value corresponding to the WC test in the MAFLD population. The highest sensitivity and specificity were related to the BMI test (96.89) and the BRI test in the T2DM MAFLD population. The cutoff value ranged from -1.44 to 876.17, the highest value about the TYG-WC test in obese individuals without T2DM. Additionally, the highest PLR and PPV were related to NAFLD-MS in the high-risk MetS population. The highest AUROC was observed for the SHSI test in the obese population. FLI, TYG-WC, TYG-BM, and BMI were among the best NAFLD screening tests in different populations. NAFLD-LFS, NHTR, and LAPI tests were found to be the best and most applicable tests in special populations.

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is an increasing epidemic and has become a leading cause of liver-related mortality and an indication for liver transplantation worldwide. Nearly 25% of the world’s population is estimated to have NAFLD.[1] Also, NAFLD is accountable for 4.36 million YLL and 4.42 million DALY, according to a Global Burden of Disease Study in 2019.[2] In 2017, there were 62.16 million DALYs related to NAFLD; these were mostly (96.8%) attributed to YLL (96.8%). This indicates that only 3.2% of the total NAFLD DALYs were attributable to YLD.[3]

An unhealthy lifestyle and increased prevalence of T2DM, overweight (BMI ≥30), and Mets (e.g., blood pressure, blood lipids, obesity, diabetes, etc.) contribute to increased NAFLD.[4]

More than 50% of the patients with T2DM and all overweight people (BMI ≥30) have NAFLD.[5]

NAFLD is significantly increasing in people with an unhealthy lifestyle, diabetes, MetS (diabetes, blood pressure, overweight, blood lipids, etc.), overweight, non-obese, lean individuals, and pregnant women. More than 50% of people with T2DM and all overweight individuals (BMI ≥30) have NAFLD. Approximately 90% of the patients with NAFLD have at least one MetS risk factor, and about 33% have all MetS risk factors.[6] A meta-analysis of 45 studies by Shi et al.[7] showed that the pooled NAFLD prevalence in lean and non-obese individuals was 10.2% and 15.7%, respectively.

In the Asian-Pacific region, the prevalence of NAFLD is estimated to increase by approximately 20% by 2030, and NAFLD-related mortality is projected to increase by more than 65%.[8] It can progress to cirrhosis and liver-related complications such as HCC and liver failure without appropriate management.[9]

In addition to liver-related diseases, this disease increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and chronic kidney disease. The most common cause of death in patients with NAFLD is related to cardiovascular complications.[10]

According to a study in 2020, NAFLD can increase the risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and atrial fibrillation by 1.20%, 1.35%, and 1.37%, respectively.[11] As the population increases, NAFLD imposes a significant economic burden on countries. A study estimated the annual economic burden of NAFLD within the USA to be $103 billion.[12] NAFLD alone accounted for nearly $87.0 billion of the total cost across all age groups. Considering the increasing incidence of NAFLD in different groups, limited health resources, and lack of appropriate treatment, it is important to consider screening programs for NAFLD.[13]

Owing to the high associated direct and indirect costs, the low predictive value of non-invasive tests, and the risks of liver biopsy for NAFLD, the need for NAFLD screening in the community has been questioned.[13] The guidelines do not recommend screening for NAFLD in the general population.[14] The EASD, EASO, and EASL guidelines recommend screening in individuals with obesity or MetS.[15]

According to a systematic review, in 2023, the global prevalence of NAFLD was estimated at 30%, which calls for comprehensive strategies to raise awareness and address all aspects of NAFLD.[16] Considering the increase in the prevalence and incidence of NAFLD in different population groups and the significant health risks associated with it, especially in patients with progressive disease, it is essential to consider screening programs for NAFLD. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the features of screening strategies for NAFLD in special populations.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This review follows the JBI guidelines for conducting a scoping review.[17] A scoping review is an appropriate method for determining the scope of a body of literature on a topic by mapping and summarizing available evidence. Results are reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidance on reporting literature reviews.[18]

PCC framework (Population, Concept, and Context) was formulated in our review: (1) Population: People with certain diseases (people with diabetes, MetS (e.g., diabetes, blood pressure, overweight, blood lipids, etc.), overweight, not-obese, lean individuals, and pregnant women); (2) Concept: Types of NAFLD screening tests; (3) Context: Health system and health care setting.

Literature search strategies

We searched the literature using Medline (PubMed), Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science from 1 January 2000 to 27 August 2025. In addition to electronic databases, Google Scholar, the reference lists of included studies, and grey literature were searched for additional relevant articles. The following keywords or MESH were used: Non-alcoholic fatty liver, screening, willingness to pay, Contingent valuation method, economic evaluation, stated preference, Aspartate Aminotransferase, Alanine Aminotransferase, Alkaline Phosphatase, Ultrasonography. The search was updated until August 2024.

In this study, indices such as AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, cut-off point, PLR, NLR, PPV, and NPV were used to investigate NAFLD screening strategies among specific populations over 18 years old.

Eligibility criteria

Studies published after 2000 were included. Studies were included if they examined features of screening strategies in groups without a previous diagnosis of acute or chronic liver disease and individuals in specific subgroups.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: letters to the editor, articles that could not be accessed as full texts, articles that entered the diagnosis phase or examined screening in hepatic steatosis and higher stages of fatty liver and did not get the required score in the qualitative assessment.

Data collection and analysis

After removing duplicates using the EndNote software, titles and abstracts were reviewed. Two researchers independently assessed the full-text versions of all articles. In case of disagreement, a third researcher (A.R) was consulted. Next, key characteristics of each article were collected, and the differences between the results were checked.

Heterogeneity across the included studies was assessed to explore the possibility of a meta-analysis.

Data extraction

Once the article was deemed to be eligible, data from eligible articles were extracted independently by two researchers (N.M and N.A) using a standardized data extraction format. We extracted data on the title, year of publication, location, populations studied (including sample size and age), subgroup, type of screening strategy, and reference standard.

Quality assessment

Quality assessment of the reporting of identified studies was performed according to the QADAS checklist. QUADAS is a tool to assess the quality of diagnostic accuracy studies included in systematic reviews. The QUADAS-2 tool analyzes four domains in terms of their risk of bias: (1) Patient Selection, (2) Index test, (3) Reference Standard, and 4) Flow and timing [Table 2].[19] Two researchers (N.M and N.A.) independently assessed the quality of identified studies using the QUADAS-2 checklist. The quality of each study was assessed as “yes,” “unclear,” or “no.”

Results

Screening results

As shown in Figure 1, the search identified 10247 records, of which 2944 were duplicates. After title/abstract screening, 529 studies remained for full-text assessment. Twenty-four additional relevant studies were identified by hand-searching. A total of 553 studies remained for full-text assessment. 513 studies were excluded due to their relation to advanced stages, diagnosis stage, and lack of regard for the target population subgroup. Finally, 40 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in our scoping review [Figure 1].

Flow diagram of the selection process

Quality appraisal results

The quality appraisal results focused on the key elements used in the analysis. The lowest total score was related to the study of Cai et al.[20] and Khang et al.[21] at 5 (of 14), and the highest was related to the study of Ajabnoor et al.[22] at 14 (of 14). Twenty-two studies had a good quality (score >10), fifteen studies had a medium quality (score between 8 and 10), and three studies had a poor quality (score < 8) [Table 1].

Characteristics of included studies

The included studies in this review were published between 2012 and 2024 in 16 different countries (the highest number in China) and covered time intervals of 1 month to 13 years. The sample size of the studies ranged from 63 in Thailand[42] in the PCOS population to 10,721 in Korea[40] in T2DM, obesity, and Mets patients.

In our review, 17 studies were cross-sectional,[20222731333637404245474951535455] seven were retrospective,[26283538414856] two were case-control.[2332] The remaining were cross-sectional cohort, cross-sectional clinic-base, retrospective observational study, cross-sectional -observational, pilot, clinical trials, longitudinal, prospective cohort, and retrospective case-control. One study used a National representative sample,[21] and one used a retrospective analysis of a prospectively collected cohort[35] [Table 2].

Target population

The age of participants in the included studies ranged from 18 to 100 years. Of the included studies for NAFLD diagnosis, 30 studies used Sonography, ultrasonography, and ultrasound as a reference standard, four studies used transient elastography, two used polysomnography, one used MRI, one study used elastography as a reference standard, and two studies did not report the reference standard [Table 2].

Most studies (n = 9)[222430373940414451] included people with T2DM, eight studies[2324252735384055] included obese individuals, seven studies[23455053555758] included non-obese, four studies[24454749] included patients with metabolic disorder, four studies[21293640] included MetS patients, three studies[202842] included polycystic ovary syndrome and three studies[264656] included patients with obstructive sleep apnea [Table 2].

Risk scores for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease prediction

In the 40 studies for NAFLD screening, 54 different risk scores were found [Table 3]. The most common were FLI (n = 15), BMI (n = 14), TYG-BMI (n = 10), HSI (n = 7), WC (n = 7), and LAP (n = 5), respectively.

Discussion

In this study, a comprehensive search strategy was conducted to investigate the characteristics of NAFLD screening strategies. In this review, 54 screening strategies in 12 different disease categories were investigated. FLI, BMI, TYG-BMI, HSI, WC, and LAP screening tests were the most common among the studies.

In people with T2DM, Angelidi et al.[30] showed that NAFLD-LFS, as a simple and affordable scoring method, with a sensitivity of 93% (the third overall sensitivity), and LAP index, with a sensitivity of 94% (the second overall sensitivity), were the best screening strategies in people with T2D. A study by Ajabnoor et al.[22] showed NAFLD-LFS was the best screening strategies in people with T2DM. A study by Liu et al.[24] demonstrated that the BRI test, with a sensitivity of 81% and a specificity of 100% (the highest overall specificity), and the BMI test, with a sensitivity of 96.89% and a specificity of 78.05% (the highest overall sensitivity), were the best tests for diagnosing NAFLD. Fangfei et al.[59] demonstrated BRI test with a sensitivity of %78 and a specificity of 80% were found to be the best predictors for NAFLD, which is accordance with our results. In Nong Li et al’s study,[39] TYG-BMI and HOMA-IR with an AUC of 0.72 and 0.65, respectively were the best screening tests for NAFLD in people with T2DM. This result was supported in Bockarie et al.’s study.[60] In Song et al. and Silaghi et al.’s studies,[4041] FLI test with an AUROC of 0.73 and an accuracy of 85.4 (model I) was a good predictor for NAFLD. Additionally, previous studies showed that, ALT and GGtT tests with high AUC and PPV were found to be the best tests screening strategies for NAFLD. It seems ALT and GGT tests useful and simple and non-invasive tools for predicting NAFLD.[3744]

In people with T1DM, the FLI with a sensitivity of 90 and an AUROC of 30.8 (second overall figure),[43] followed by the HSI with a sensitivity of 86%, were the best indexes for predicting NAFLD in people with T1DM. This result is in line with a previous study that showed the FLI test with a sensitivity of 79% was a better test than HIS.[61] Moreover, Mondal et al.[31] evaluated the role of NC and NHtR as predictors of liver stiffness measure (LSM) in people with prediabetes. They showed NHtR with an AUC and a sensitivity of 0.66% and 90% in men and 0.62% and 80% in women, respectively, and NC with an AUC of 0.66 was the best test for predicting NAFLD. Their results are in line with a previous study[62] in which the NC index was found to be a good index for predicting NAFLD. In pregnant women, Koralegedara et al.[52] developed six mathematical models for predicting NAFLD in pregnant women and showed FLI in all six models was a good screening tool for NAFLD. Their results were in line with previous studies.[6364]

In our review, screening strategies for NAFLD in the PCOS population were assessed in 3 studies. Tantanavipas et al.[42] showed that CAP with an AUC of 0.81 and a sensitivity of 84% was found to be a good index for predicting NAFLD. Their results are in keeping with a previous study[64] in which a CAP index with a sensitivity of 70% was a good index for predicting NAFLD. Two studies demonstrated that BMI, visceral fat, and CRP were good indicators for diagnosing NAFLD.[2033] BMI is a non-invasive index that effectively identifies patients suspected of NAFLD.[20]

In the OSA population, the BMI index (AUC: 75 (the third overall AUC), sensitivity: 78%, specificity: 69%), and CAP index (AUC: 0.54, sensitivity: 67%, specificity: 54% and cut-off: 243.5 (the fourth overall cut-off)) were good indicators to predict NAFLD.[26] Also, a study by Chen et al.[46] showed that FLI had better performance in diagnosing NAFLD than HSI.

In the lean population, a study by Hsu et al.[35] showed that the FLI index with a sensitivity of 79% and a specificity of 60% FLI was the best index to predict lean-NAFLD compared to the other 11 tests. Zeng et al.[48] also showed that WHR was a better index compared to FLI to predict NAFLD in lean adults, which is in line with previous studies.[6566]

In people with MetS, Hui et al.[36] showed that in both women and men, WHtR test was the best for predicting in adults. By contrast, in some studies, the WHtR test differed between women and men.[585960] This difference between studies can be attributed to sample size, type of population, and age groups. Song et al.[40] showed that FLI and TYG-WC with an AUROC of 0.83 had the highest predictive power among the MetS group. They also reported that FLI was a good index for predicting NAFLD, but its complicated formula hinders its widespread use. Saokaew et al.[29] showed that the FLI index (with a sensitivity of %88 and a specificity of %82 in the low-risk MetS group and with a sensitivity of 43% and a specificity of 85% in the MetS group with low-risk of T2DM) and LAP index (with a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 40% in the high-risk MetS, and with a sensitivity of 41% and a specificity of 89% in patients MetS with high-risk of T2DM) were the best screening indicators in this population group. Furthermore, Khang et al.[21] demonstrated that FLI with an AUC of 0.84 was a suitable screening tool for diagnosing NAFLD in patients with MetS.

In patients with NAFLD, Liu et al.[24] showed that LAP (AUC: 0.87, sensitivity of 87% and specificity: 72%) and FLI (AUC: 0.87, sensitivity of 88% and specificity: 72%) were the best screening indicators for predicting NAFLD. The results of a systematic review study showed that the LAP index (sensitivity: 94%, specificity: 85%) was a good index for NAFLD screening. This meta-analysis reported the LAP index was an available, affordable, and accurate tool for NAFLD screening.[67] Peng et al.[45] demonstrated that the WC test with AUC of 93.30 (the highest overall AUC) BMI with an AUC of 26 were the best indexes for NAFLD screening. They also showed WC was better and more effective compared to BMI for NAFLD. Xue et al.[49] showed that TYG-WC (AUC: 0.86, sensitivity: 72%, specificity: 87%) and TYG-BMI (AUC: 0.86, sensitivity: 72%, specificity: 88%) were the indexes for NAFLD screening. NAFLD. Moreover, in a study by Ze Yang et al.[47] TYG-BM (AUC: 0.88) was found to be a good index for predicting NAFLD.

In our review, screening strategies among the obese population were assessed in 8 studies. Simental et al.[27] showed that ALT/TGL (sensitivity: 84%, specificity: 91%) was the best index for NAFLD screening. Leivas et al.[38] demonstrated the ultrasound had a good sensitivity (88%) in diagnosing NAFLD among patients with II and III obesity. A study by Fu et al.[35] demonstrated that ZJU, HSI, LAP, and VAI with high sensitivity were good indices for predicting NAFLD. Their results were consistent with a previous study in which VAI (AUC: 0.81, sensitivity: 71%) was found to be a good index for predicting NAFLD in the general population. Liu et al.[24] reported that ZJU (AUC: 0.81, sensitivity: 71%) was a good index in obese individuals. Some studies[2324] have highlighted that ZJU (AUC: 0.81, sensitivity: 71%) and FLI were good indices for predicting NAFLD. Also, A study by Song et al.[40] showed that FLI with an AUROC of 0.72 and TYG-BMI and TYG-WC with an AUROC of 0.71 were suitable indices for predicting NAFLD. Jie-Chen et al.[25] demonstrated that SHSI (AUC: 0.87) was the best index for predicting NAFLD, among other indices. In a study by Zeng et al.[55] HOMA-IR and TYG-BMI were found to be good indices for predicting NAFLD within the obese populations. Khamseh et al.[54] demonstrated that TYG-WC (sensitivity: 81%, specificity: 51%, Cut-off value: 876.7 (highest overall cut-off)) and TYG-INDEX (sensitivity: 77%, specificity: 71%) were the best indices for predicting NAFLD in obese individuals without T2DM. These results were in line with a previous study in which TYG-INDEX (AUC: 0.77, sensitivity: 71%) was found to be a good index for predicting NAFLD in general population.[63]

In the non-obese population, Kiyak et al.[57] and Li et al.[45] showed that SAMI (sensitivity: 85%, specificity: 66%), and TYG-WC (AUC: 0.80) and TYG-BMI (AUC: 0.78) were suitable indexes for predicting NAFLD. Some studies have shown that TYG-BMI was the best index for predicting NAFLD in non-obese individuals.[535558] Also, ZJU (AUC: 82.29) and FLI (AUC: 0.75 and 0.86) were found to be good indices for NAFLD screening in both women and men.[2350] A study by Takahashi et al.[23] showed that the FLI (AUC: 0.82 and 0.91) was the index for NAFLD screening in non-obese/obese people. Cui et al.[28] showed that the UHR (AUC 0.69) was a good index to evaluate NAFLD among obese/non-obese people with T2DM.

Limitations and strengths

Our review has limitations that should be highlighted. Although a biopsy is the gold standard for identifying and classifying NAFLD, this method is used less due to poor acceptability, high cost, and invasiveness. In our review, the reference test in most of the studies was ultrasound, which leads to delays in diagnosis and increased costs due to the need for specialized personnel and being performed in specialized centers. Most importantly, operators tend to overestimate ultrasound findings in obese people, and this leads to an increase in the false positive rate. Second, this review was carried out among special populations, thus preventing the generalization of the results to the general population. Finally, the impact of important variables such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and unhealthy lifestyles was not taken into account on NAFLD. The strength of our review was the simultaneous evaluation and comparison of several valid non-invasive scores for the early identification of people with NAFLD in special populations.

Conclusion

The findings of this review showed that the FLI index in eight population groups (obesity (n = 2), T2DM (n = 2), MetS (n = 3), obese/non-obese, T1DM, lean individuals, and pregnant women) was the best index for NAFLD screening. Also, the TYG-WC index in four population groups (non-obese, MAFLD, MetS people, and obese people without T2DM), the TYG-BMI tests in three population groups (non-obese, metabolic disorder, and MetS), and the TYG-BMI index in non-obese individuals (n = 3) and those with T2DM were found to be the best indexes for NAFLD screening. Furthermore, the BMI index in the PCOS population, the ZJU index in the obese (n = 2) and non-obese population, the NAFLD-LFS index in the T2DM population (n = 2), the NHtR in people with prediabetes and MetS, and the LAP index in MetS people were found to be the best NAFLD screening tests. The findings of this study highlighted the importance of choosing the appropriate screening tests and screening indices for NAFLD according to the specific characteristics of the population.
