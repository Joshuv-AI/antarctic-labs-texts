Shedding light on bacterial fitness in a tug-of-war with liquid crystal emulsions
Authors: Hannah Feldstein, Harikrishnan Vijayamohanan, Jan F Totz, Alberto Concellón, Jie Li, Georgios Toupalas, Mathias Kolle, Timothy M Swager
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapid and reliable quantification of bacterial dynamics at the cellular level is critical for pathogen sensing, live-dead bacterial assays, and monitoring of bacteria fitness and viability. Here, we demonstrate bacterial fitness quantification by capturing individual cells on topological defects of micro-scale liquid crystal emulsion droplets. The emulsion droplets are composed of phase-separated nematic liquid crystal and fluorocarbon components and exhibit an asymmetric mass distribution. A topological singularity in the director field of the liquid crystal phase localizes tailormade surfactants that tether a single bacterium per droplet. Active motion of the bacterium induces a tilt and azimuthal rotation of the droplet trap, which is counteracted by gravity acting on the droplet center of mass. By comparing the observed dynamics of a tethered bacterium’s stochastic movement to a computational model of bacterial motion on spherical surfaces that is based on the classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, we quantify the fitness of bacteria subjected to starvation over several days. This pathogen fitness sensing concept, which relies on the scalable chemical design of single bacterial cell traps, a robust optical readout, and a theoretical understanding of bacterial dynamics on spherical surfaces, offers opportunities for rapid pathogen activity assessment, micro-biological sensing, and biologically powered micro-actuator systems.Subject terms: Applied physics, Liquid crystals, Mechanical engineering, Fluidics

Introduction

Rapid assessment of pathogen activity and metabolic state is a significant challenge in the early detection of food-borne pathogens, water quality assessment, and monitoring of antibiotic-resistance1–3. Conventional cell culture methods offer excellent accuracy, but require several days of amplification before a positive result can be observed4. State-of-the-art techniques like polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays have very high sensitivity and are capable of amplifying genetic fragments from a single cell within hours5, but are expensive and require specialized equipment and training6. Direct immunofluorescence detects bacteria faster than traditional methods can, but suffers from high cost, poor sensitivity, and potential interference from contaminants4,7. Additionally, PCR and immunofluorescence cannot assess the vital status, fitness, and metabolic activity of bacterial pathogens that are present in a sample or of other targeted bacterial population5,8.

Here, we demonstrate a concept for pathogen detection and bacterial fitness assessment with the potential to address the aforementioned issues in conventional approaches. The challenge with early detection and assessment of bacterial dynamics—akin to finding a needle in a haystack—is the recognition of rare and spatially localized chemical binding events and their transduction into a reliable and robust read-out. To tackle this challenge, we developed a strategy to entrap individual bacteria with tailored pathogen-binding surfactants that help to trap and localize bacterial cells at the topological defect of micro-scale nematic liquid crystal Janus emulsions droplets (Fig. 1a). These emulsions can be produced in bulk quantities, and provide a quantitative assessment of pathogen presence and activity within minutes. We assess the trapped cells’ stochastic run-and-tumble dynamics confined to the droplets’ micro-scale spherical surface via the droplets’ optical signature. The motion of a bacterium trapped at the apex of a Janus droplet induces a torque and a corresponding tilt of the droplet’s symmetry axis as well as an azimuthal rotation (Fig. 1b, c). The droplet’s tilt and rotational motion, powered by the swimming movement of the trapped bacterium, in turn modulates the intensity of polarized light transmitted through the droplet. This modulated optical signature can be analyzed with a crossed polarizer and a camera (Fig. 1d, e), which reveals information about the tug-of-war between the trapped bacterium and the droplet and enables quantification of bacterial fitness. This optical signal is robust against a range of incoming light intensities and imaging planes of the camera.

Emulsion droplet traps, the critical enabling element of the pathogen sensing approach presented here, are formed in a “one-pot”-process. 4-Cyano-4’-pentylbiphenyl (5CB), which exhibits a nematic liquid crystal (LC) phase, and ethyl nonafluorobutyl ether (HFE-7200) as the fluorocarbon (FC) phase are dissolved in dichloromethane (DCM), then uniformly emulsified in an aqueous medium via a microfluidic chip (Telos 2 Reagent Chip, Dolomite Microfluidics). Phase separation occurs with DCM evaporation to form monodisperse Janus droplets of around 7 μm diameter (Fig. 1a). Customized surfactants stabilize the droplet’s internal interface and the surfaces contacting the aqueous medium (see “Methods”)9,10. Chemical tailoring of the interfacial tensions and anchoring at the liquid crystal-water (LC-W) interface allows us to tune the droplet morphology and control the director field of the LC phase. By design, the LC director is aligned parallel to the LC-W interface and perpendicular to the liquid crystal-fluorocarbon (LC-FC) interface, generating a topological point defect at the droplet’s apex (Fig. 1a, bottom). The topological defect has a special role on the droplet surface: minority surfactants with pathogen binding groups that disrupt the dominant organization of the LC-W interface aggregate at this position to minimize elastic strain in the liquid crystal11,12. This allows us to localize a high concentration of tetradecyl (C-14)-functionalized mannose surfactants at the single topological defect13. Mannose is known to act as an antigen that binds with a very high affinity to the FimH lectin found on the attachment pili of Escherichia coli bacteria14–16. To observe bacteria attachment to the mannose-functionalized droplet, we imaged green fluorescent protein-tagged E. coli interacting with the droplet traps, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

We design the average emulsion droplet diameter to be larger than 5 μm and smaller than 10 μm (in the results reported here it is 7.4 ± 0.7 μm, see Supplementary Fig. 2). This maximizes the likelihood that only a single bacterium (diameter ≈ 1 μm) is anchored to the topological defect of an individual droplet and also ensures that the restoring force of the droplet tumbler is large enough to minimize the probability of artifacts in the measured intensity fluctuations related to bacteria being able to flip the droplets over.

Bacterial cells are captured on the droplet traps simply by mixing and swirling emulsion droplets and bacteria in a vial. Next, the droplets are placed in a glass sample holder, where they settle to the bottom owing to their average density being higher than the density of water. We then image light transmitted and focused by the droplets, which are positioned between two crossed polarizers (Fig. 1b). The density difference between the LC phase (ρ = 1.008 g/mL) and the FC phase (ρ = 1.428 g/mL) provides an orientation force that locates the topological defect of the liquid crystal at the droplet apex. A bacterial cell that is anchored by the mannose surfactant to the topological defect displays confined swimming motion at the spherical droplet-water interface. The bacterium’s motion imparts a torque on the droplet tumbler and results in a tilt away from the droplet’s equilibrium orientation (Fig. 1c). This elicits an opposing gravitational torque that balances the bacterium’s pull. E. coli’s natural run-and-tumble behavior involves stochastic changes in its swimming direction, which leads to fluctuations in droplet tilt and azimuthal rotation parameterized by the temporarily varying polar angle θ(t) and azimuthal angle ϕ(t), shown in Supplementary Video 117. This, in essence, is the dynamic tug-of-war observed between the tethered bacteria and the droplet traps. Polarized light transmitted and focused by an emulsion droplet and analyzed with a second crossed polarizer exhibits a typical Maltese cross intensity pattern due to the inherent birefringence of the LC phase (Fig. 1b, e). The intensity pattern is characteristic of the droplet tilt and azimuthal orientation and the stochastic fluctuations in its aggregate intensity due to the bacterium-droplet tug-of-war and can easily be quantified with a simple microscope and camera (Fig. 1d).

Results

Signature of bacteria on droplets

We observe a characteristic ‘blinking’ (shown in Supplementary Video 2) that develops within a few minutes upon mixing the droplets with a bacterial culture and generally persists for at least half an hour. The temporal variation of transmitted intensity patterns and the spatially integrated light intensity transmitted by the droplets (Fig. 2a) tracks the dynamic tug-of-war between a tethered bacterium and its droplet trap18. The power spectral densities associated with the observed temporal intensity variation allow us to distinguish between bacteria of high activity levels (Fig. 2b) and bacteria of low activity levels (Fig. 2c). Specifically, we extract the frequency at half maximum fHM of the power spectral densities, which serves as a metric to assess the activity levels of the trapped bacteria. To confirm that the observed intensity fluctuations and associated characteristics of their power spectral densities result from the activity of tethered bacteria, we prepared controls consisting of droplets without mannose surfactants that cannot capture bacterial cells. These inactivated droplet traps do not show the same extent of intensity fluctuations discussed above, independent of whether live bacteria are present in the aqueous medium or not (see Supplementary Video 3). Previous research has shown that gram-negative bacteria can adsorb to 5CB LC emulsions and alter the liquid crystal ordering from a bipolar pattern (parallel to the LC-W interface) to a radial pattern (perpendicular to the LC-W interface)19. We also observe this effect. After a prolonged period of agitation of a suspension of control droplets in bacterial cultures (≈ 30 min), the droplets begin to take on a radial geometry, and the adsorbed bacteria perturb the LC structure in the droplet. However, unlike the rapid response observed for active droplet traps with mannose functionalization at the topological defect, the fluctuations obtained by cell-wall-adsorbed bacteria are slow to develop and are not persistent as a result of the cytotoxicity of 5CB. This cytotoxicity plays out on timescales much larger than the time needed to realize the presented pathogen fitness assessment concept.

Empty droplet traps or traps that captured a dead bacterium are stationary and do not blink. We also observe that live blinking droplets cease to respond almost immediately after an antiseptic solution (70% ethanol) is added (See Supplementary Video 4 and the “Methods” section), implying that the tethered bacteria transitioned from an active state before exposure to the antiseptic to an incapacitated/dead state after exposure.

A metric for bacterial fitness

In addition to simply interpreting the presence or absence of ‘blinking’ as bacteria being alive or dead, we can harness our sensing approach to quantify the activity level of bacteria present in the sample. To do so, we assess the behavior of well-nourished relative to starved bacteria, with the assumption that starved bacteria display lower strength and thus fitness in a tug-of-war with their droplet traps. Droplets anchored to starved bacteria are shown in Supplementary Video 5. The power spectral densities of the intensity fluctuations of droplets tethered to a bacterial cell are shown for a culture with fresh medium (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Video 6) and a culture that was left in that same medium for six days (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Video 7, with nutrients progressively depleted. As the concentration of nutrients in the culture medium decreases, bacteria weaken. This is reflected in a decrease in the width at half maximum fHM of the power spectral densities. The frequency fHM can therefore be considered to be a metric of bacterial fitness, which can be related to the bacteria’s swimming velocity, run-and-tumble behavior, and the tilt angle θ of their droplet traps. The average droplet tilt angle θ in turn represents an intuitive indicator of bacterial fitness, since a stronger bacterium will tilt the droplet more before its torque is balanced by the droplet’s gravitational torque. Naturally, we observe that the average tilt angle of the droplet traps declines as bacterial starvation progresses.

Modeling the bacteria-trap dynamics

To validate our experimental approach of interpreting the width fHM of the power spectral density of the observed temporal intensity fluctuations as a measure of bacterial fitness, we established a theoretical model for the run-and-tumble motion of bacteria confined to the spherical droplet surface. This model accounts for the forces present in the dynamic tug-of-war between a bacterium and a droplet. Accounting for the self-propulsion force Fb of the bacterium and the gravitational restoring force Fg of the droplet, the tethered bacterium’s position on the droplet in the relevant spherical coordinates (θ, ϕ) and its orientation angle Ω can be represented by a set of overdamped stochastic differential equations of motion:

In this set of equations, μ is the drag coefficient governing the motion of the bacterium-droplet pair in the surrounding medium at low Reynolds number (approximately 2 × 10−4), where inertia is negligible. We approximate the bacterium-droplet drag coefficient, μ, as the sum of μd, the rotational Stokes drag coefficient of the droplet, given by μd = 8πηRd, and μb, the translational Stokes drag coefficient of the bacterium, given by μb = 6πηRb, where η is the dynamic viscosity of water. Visual evidence strongly suggests that droplets remain spherical during interaction with the tethered bacterium; we do not observe any deviation from the original shape of the droplets (see Supplementary Video 1). In addition, the Laplace pressure of a droplet greatly exceeds the maximum pressure that the bacterium can exert, as detailed in Supplementary Section 1. The cylindrical bacterium is approximated to be spherical. Drag on an ellipsoid does not differ significantly from drag on a sphere in the Stokes regime, and is small relative to μd20. The orientation angle Ω (shown in Fig. 3a) is measured from the direction tangential to the droplet in the plane defined by the z-axis and the bacteria’s position vector x. It describes the tangential orientation of the bacterium on the droplet, which is important to decompose the bacterium’s propulsion force F⃗b into the force component fθ=Fb⋅eθRd+Rb acting to cause a droplet tilt θ (see Fig. 3b), and the force component fφ=Fb⋅eφ(Rd+Rb)sinθ, causing azimuthal droplet rotation in the spherical coordinate φ. Here, eθ⃗ and eφ⃗ are the base vectors in the polar and azimuthal directions, respectively, and Rd and Rb are the radii of the droplet trap and bacterium, respectively.

Since the motion of the bacterium occurs in a fluid at low Reynolds numbers, we neglect inertia and express the bacterial propulsion force as Fb=μbu(s)⋅p^, where u(s) is the swimming speed of the bacterium, which is a function of the stochastic variable s explained further below, and the unit vector p^ indicates the bacterium’s swimming direction. The restoring gravitational force acting on the bacterium follows from a torque balance as: fg=mgrg(Rd+Rb)2sinθ depending on droplet mass m, gravitational constant g, and distance rg of the droplet center-of-mass to the droplet center of symmetry. In Eq. (3), which describes the dynamics of Ω21, the first term fΩ=−μbu(s)RdtanθsinΩ is due to the motion being on a non-planar manifold. The second term describes the rotational diffusive motion with a position-dependent noise term ξ(θ, φ). The rotational diffusion coefficient D(s) is a function of the discrete stochastic variable s, which sets the character of the bacterium’s stochastic run-and-tumble dynamics. The stochastic switching between run-and-tumble motion is modeled as a random telegraph process with average run times tr and tumble times tt22. During runs, the speed is u(s) = u and the rotational diffusion coefficient D(s) = 0, whereas during tumbles the opposite scenario is true: u(s) = 0 and D(s) = D. Mechanical theories are expanded upon in Supplementary Section 2. The values for the speed u and the rotational diffusion are estimated relying on available literature data, and observed run-and-tumble times of free swimming E. coli23,24. We compared the empirically observed rotational velocities in Supplementary Video 8 to theoretical velocities determined with Eq. (1) and determined the drag coefficient to be one order of magnitude smaller than the theoretical prediction of a solid sphere rotating in liquid (μtheory = 7.829 × 10−8 kg/s) 25. For details, see Supplementary Section 3, Supplementary Fig. 3, and Supplementary Video 9. We speculate that this friction reduction is caused by the lubricating effect of the surfactants used to form the droplet traps, and apply a 76% reduction to droplet friction (μ = 1.879 × 10−8 kg/s) for our model, which is in agreement with values from literature26–29. To account in our model for the reduced activity in starved bacteria as compared to cells in fresh culture medium, we reduce the swimming speed and tumbling time, informed by experiments on bacterial starvation, and find good agreement with our experimental observations30. Figure 3c, d illustrate example trajectories of fresh and starved bacteria over 20 s. By modeling the spatially varying phase delay that light incurs upon propagating through an emulsion droplet in the LC and the fluorocarbon phases as a function of droplet tilt and resulting LC orientation, we estimate the intensity fluctuations and their average power spectral densities from the theoretical bacterial trajectories (Fig. 3e–h). Supplementary Fig. 4 details the simulated intensity outputs using MIT Electromagnetic Equation Propagation (MEEP) finite-difference time-domain wave optics simulations31. See Supplementary Section 4 for detailed background on the optical modeling. The model assumes no significant distortion of the liquid crystal director field during the bacterium-droplet tug-of-war (a rationale is provided in Supplementary Section 1 based on a scaling argument suggesting that the liquid crystal director field is expected to remain largely unperturbed in the presence of an active trapped bacterium). The trajectories of bacteria in fresh medium exhibit larger tilt angles and show higher frequency components in the power spectral density as compared to the starved bacteria. In a starved bacterial culture, lower bacterial swimming speed and reduced tumbling times result in a power spectrum with a narrower spectral width. Simulation results for the fresh and starved bacteria media are shown in Supplementary Videos 10 and 11, respectively.

Discussion

Figures 4a, b display the power spectral densities of experimentally observed intensity fluctuations from bacterium-droplet tumbler pairs and the power spectral densities extracted from the model of bacteria-droplet dynamics. The experimental and modeling data show agreement, providing evidence that the model is suitable to describe the observed dynamics. The spectral widths fHM of the experimentally determined power spectral densities are matched by the spectral widths predicted by the model, which takes into account changes in swimming speed and tumbling time of the bacteria. Experimental and modeled power spectral densities follow an approximate Lorentzian distribution. This can be rationalized with the run-and-tumble motion being described for long times as a diffusive process32, such that for small tilt angles, the Eqs. (1–3) simplify into the classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process in the polar coordinate θ, whose power spectral density is given by a Lorentzian distribution33. As expected, with increasing starvation levels, the width of the power spectral density distribution of the observed intensity fluctuations narrows (Fig. 4b). This is equivalent to a decrease in the droplets’ mean tilt angles, which is expected due to the starvation-induced reduction in the bacteria’s swimming speeds. This can also be observed experimentally, as the starved bacteria exhibit Maltese cross patterns indicative of low tilt angles (see Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 4). A reduced speed results in a smaller forces applied by a tethered bacterium to a droplet, leading to smaller perturbations of the orientation of the droplet tumbler from its vertical equilibrium position (Fig. 4c). Supplementary Fig. 5 details the mapping of spectrum width to mean tilt angle (Fig. 4d), which was subsequently used to relate tilt angle to age, as shown in Fig. 4e.

We have demonstrated the design of emulsion-based droplet traps that can be used to quantify bacterial activity levels and fitness. The droplets consist of Janus emulsions that are formed from a liquid crystal and a fluorocarbon phase. A topological singularity in the liquid crystal director field located at the droplet apex harbors tailored surfactants that bind to bacterial pathogens and enable the entrapment of a single bacterium on each droplet. The bacteria tethered to the droplets’ topological defect display a run-and-tumble motion confined to a spherical manifold with a dynamic restoring force originating from gravity. The swimming motion of the tethered bacterium results in a ‘tug-of-war’ with the droplet, causing a tilt and azimuthal rotation, which is manifested in an easily monitored fluctuation of the intensity of light that propagates through the droplet. Our experimental characterization of this interplay between bacterial cell and emulsion droplet is complemented and confirmed by theoretical modeling, allowing us to explain the phenomenon underlying the observed intensity fluctuations and reduce this data to an intuitive observable of bacterial fitness, namely the average droplet tilt angle. In doing so, we have demonstrated emulsion-based droplet traps and sensors for bacterial pathogens that can distinguish between live and dead bacteria and also allow us to quantify bacterial activity levels. Our quantification of bacterial viability and fitness using tumbling droplet traps is inexpensive, rapid, provides fast responses, and requires only simple sensing equipment, including crossed polarizers, a lens, and a camera to record the output. This approach can likely be extended to other motile bacteria, wherein the maximum restoring force produced by the droplet can be tuned to match the active propulsion force generated by the respective bacteria. In this proof-of-concept sensing study, we modeled the difference in diffusion dynamics of bacteria in the run and tumble states by a simple binary change in the diffusion coefficient D, which is justified by the match between model and experimental validation. The model could be further refined by better knowledge of the continuous statistical distribution of the effective diffusion of a run-and-tumble motion, as discussed in the literature34. Tailored recognition and binding groups for other bacterial pathogens, including Listeria, Staphylococcus sp., and Salmonella, should allow for recognition and distinction of different pathogens in multiplexed sensing and activity quantification scenarios35,36. Combining the surface anchoring of bacteria along with coupling to the director field of liquid crystal droplets can also open new avenues for controlling the dynamics of individual bacteria-powered devices.

Methods

Fabrication of emulsion-based droplets

The emulsion-based droplets are formed from 4-Cyano-4’-pentylbiphenyl (5CB) as the nematic liquid crystal (LC) phase, and ethyl nonafluorobutyl ether (HFE-7200) as the fluorocarbon (FC) phase. A custom-synthesized surfactant, 4-Cyano-4’-(3-perfluoro-octyl-propyl)biphenyl (CB-RF), is used to stabilize the LC/FC interface, while the custom-designed bola-amphiphilic surfactant CB-diTEG stabilizes the LC/water interface10. A tailor-made tetradecyl (C-14)-functionalized mannose surfactant13 is employed as the molecular binding group to capture bacteria. The LC and FC phases, together with these three surfactants, are first dissolved in dichloromethane (DCM). The DCM solution is then dispersed in an aqueous phase containing sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) to create DCM-rich oil-in-water emulsions.

The emulsions are then allowed to sit until the DCM evaporates (12 h) to produce the desired Janus emulsions. The SDS solution is then exchanged with a custom-designed surfactant, CB-diSO3Na, that promotes the desired anchoring of the liquid crystal molecules parallel to the LC/water interface, creating a topological defect at the pole of the droplet. Monodisperse droplets are prepared by using a microfluidic chip to prepare DCM in water emulsions before solvent evaporation.

The details of the synthesis of the surfactant which anchors the LC chains parallel to the LC-W interface (CB-diSO3Na) to induce the formation of a topological defect at the droplet pole is provided in the Supplementary Fig. 6. The synthesis of surfactants which stabilize the interface between the LC and FC phase (4-Cyano-4’-(3-perfluoro-octyl-propyl)biphenyl, (CB-RF)), and which harbor mannose groups for attachment of bacteria (ManC14) and which are used for coupling magnetic nanoparticles (4-dodecoxy-benzaldehyde, C12-Ph-CHO) are described in previous publications10,13,37. For a typical formulation, 0.2 mL of 5CB (TCI America), 0.2 mL of HFE-7200 (TCI America), 10 mg of (CB-RF) and 10 mg of CB-diTEG are dissolved in 0.4 mL of dichloromethane. For anchoring of bacteria, 2 mg of ManC14 is added whereas for anchoring magnetic nanoparticles, 2 mg of C12-Ph-CHO is added. Monodisperse emulsions containing dichloromethane in water are prepared using a Dolomite microfluidic setup with a 30 μL channel size Telos 2 reagent encapsulation chip and 1 wt% solution of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, Sigma Aldrich) as surfactant. The diameter of the droplet is controlled by tuning the relative flow rates of the disperse and continuous phases. The emulsions are kept overnight for DCM to evaporate, following which they are redispersed in a 0.1 wt% CB-diSO3Na solution.

Magnetic nanoparticle proof of concept

To demonstrate a proof-of-concept of such a system, controlled mechanical stimulation of the droplet is established by binding superparamagnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles (10.6 ± 2.6 nm) with surface amines to the droplet’s topological defects via an aldehyde surfactant and applying an alternating current (AC) magnetic field38. The AC magnetic field creates oscillations in the intensity pattern that can be captured using video processing and precisely reveal the frequency of the stimulation (see Supplementary Fig. 7 for details regarding setup and plots).

Culturing E. coli bacteria

Escherichia coli (E. coli) strain 10536 was purchased from ATCC for pathogen-sensing experiments. E. coli strain 25922GFP was used for bacterium-droplet attachment visualization. Sterilized equipment was used to transfer 20 mL of nutrient broth (sterile from bioWORLD) into a capped, 50 mL sterile plastic culture tube. Next, a sterile cotton inoculating swab was used to remove an iced chunk of E. coli from the frozen stock of the bacteria and put it into the nutrient broth in a culture tube. This culture tube was placed in an incubator at 37 ∘C for one day for further growth. Subsequently, the solution from the culture tube was directly used for measurements. By colony counting method, the population of the bacterial culture was quantified to be 5 × 108 CFU (colony-forming units/mL).

Coupling E. coli to droplet traps

In a 2 mL vial, 10 μL of droplets are added to 0.5 mL of bacteria solution. After 90 s, the mixture is dispersed in a cell-chamber (Attofluor A7816, ThermoFisher) with a plasma-treated glass cover slip for imaging.

Fixing E. coli cells

E. coli culture solution was centrifuged at 3214 × g for 10 mins to compact E. coli into a pellet. The supernatant was removed, and then 10 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde was added to the E. coli culture. The culture was mixed gently and incubated at room temperature for 3 h to fix the cells. Then the cells were centrifuged at 3214 × g for 10 mins to remove the 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed cells were washed with PBS buffer three times. 10 wt% of 70% aqueous ethanol solution was then added to the culture tube.

Optical characterization of droplets

Side view and top view imaging were performed on an in-house custom microscope setup comprising a mercury light source (Olympus U-LH100HG), an air objective (Olympus Achromat Objective, Thorlabs RMS10X), a tube lens (Thorlabs TTL180A) and a CCD detector (DFM 37UX273-ML). Individual pixel values were measured at random frames in order to confirm that oversaturation was not an issue (see Supplementary Fig. 8). For top-view imaging, crossed polarizers (Thorlabs LPVISE100-A) were placed before and after the droplets in the optical beam path. To apply an oscillating magnetic field, a Helmholtz loop arrangement was generated using a waveform amplifier (Acel Instruments TS250) and a function generator (Agilent 33220 A).

Data analysis and modeling

ImageJ, Matlab and Mathematica were used for analyzing droplet videos and extracting the power spectral densities of the observed intensity fluctuations (shown in Supplementary Fig. 9). A custom C++ code was developed for modeling the constrained random walk of E. coli on the droplet surface.
