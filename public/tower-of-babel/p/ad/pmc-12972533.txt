Richter-like transformation of CLL/SLL after a temporary hold of ibrutinib: A case report
Authors: Tung-Lin Chiang, John Frater, Amanda Cashen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors are used to treat chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL), providing survival benefits over traditional chemoimmunotherapy. Richter transformation is the transformation of CLL/SLL into an aggressive lymphoma, typically necessitating an immediate start of treatment. This case report presents a CLL/SLL patient developing pathology-proven Richter transformation after a short hold of the BTK inhibitor, without clinical evidence of disease progression. This case also demonstrates that the pathology-only Richter transformation after a pause of the BTK inhibitor can respond to resumption of the inhibitor, without the addition of intensive chemotherapy.

Introduction

Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors are frequently used in both the frontline and relapsed treatment of chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL) [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Specifically, ibrutinib has been demonstrated to provide significant survival benefits over chemoimmunotherapy when used as the frontline therapy for CLL/SLL [2,5,6]. Hemorrhage was identified as a main side effect of ibrutinib during early clinical trials [7,8]. Thus, the package insert for ibrutinib advises withholding the medication for a period of three to seven days both before and after surgery, based on clinical judgment regarding the risk of bleeding, in order to minimize peri‑operative bleeding.

Richter transformation is the transformation of CLL/SLL into diffuse large B cell lymphoma, an aggressive hematological malignancy. The pathogenesis of Richter transformation is complex, involving inactivation of genes related to tumor suppression or cell cycle regulation [9,10]. The clinical course of Richter transformation is usually acute in nature, including a sudden worsening of lymphadenopathy, an increase in serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level, and constitutional symptoms [10,11]. Given the relatively rapid and aggressive clinical course of Richter transformation, clinicians typically start combination chemotherapy without delay.

This case report presents a patient who had pathology-proven Richter transformation after a short hold of ibrutinib. Since only a limited number of articles discuss Richter transformation after a temporary hold of BTK inhibitors, we also aim to provide a clinical perspective regarding this condition.

Case presentation

The patient is a 66-year-old female diagnosed with CLL/SLL at age 52. She initially presented with a mildly elevated white blood cell count (WBC) and diffuse lymphadenopathy, consistent with Rai stage I, Binet stage B. She was observed off therapy for 5 years, until her WBC reached 50 k/cumm, hemoglobin and platelet count dropped below the normal range, and the spleen was palpable below the costal margin. Computerized tomography (CT) scan showed splenomegaly and increased axillary (largest 2.4 × 1.5 cm), mediastinal (sub-centimeter), and abdominal (largest 2.5 × 2.1 cm) lymphadenopathy, and bone marrow showed greater than 90 % cellularity with 80 % involvement with CLL (Fig. 1). Fluorescence in-situ hybridization (FISH) analysis was positive for deletion 11q (20 %) and trisomy 12 (68 %) and was negative for deletion of 17p/TP53. At that time, she was initiated on a treatment regimen consisting of seven cycles of rituximab and ibrutinib, followed by daily maintenance of ibrutinib. She tolerated the treatment well without complications. After 6 months of treatment, her WBC was within normal limits, and CT scan showed resolved splenomegaly and markedly decreased lymphadenopathy, with all lymph nodes measuring less than one centimeter. The clinical findings were consistent with a complete remission [12]. Bone marrow analysis after one year of treatment showed no evidence of CLL and no clonal aberrations. She continued to take ibrutinib daily, with excellent adherence. She underwent a colonoscopy after 3 years on treatment, for which ibrutinib was held for two days before the procedure. After eight years of treatment with ibrutinib, she was diagnosed with stage IA (T1bN0M0) malignant melanoma on her right calf without evidence of regional, nodal, or distant metastasis She underwent a wide excision of the primary melanoma and sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) of two right inguinal region lymph nodes. Ibrutinib was held five days before and three days after the surgery. SLNB was negative for metastatic melanoma. However, there was an incidental finding of non-germinal center type diffuse large B-cell lymphoma in both lymph nodes (Fig. 2), with a predominance of CD20 positive large B-cells showing a Ki-67 proliferative rate greater than 90 %. The cells were positive for co-expression of PAX5, CD79, CD5, CD23 (partially), MUM1, and BCL2, but negative for cyclin D1, CD10, BCL6, CD21, CD30, and ISH-EBER. C-myc was expressed in approximately 30 % of the malignant cells. P53 showed a nuclear and cytoplasmic over-expression pattern in approximately 5 % of the cells. FISH was negative for translocations in BCL2, BCL6, and MYC. These findings were diagnostic of Richter transformation of CLL/SLL. She was otherwise asymptomatic, without fever, chills, or night sweats, and her follow-up blood counts and LDH were within normal limits 16 days after the brief hold. Positron emission tomography (PET)/CT scan did not identify any fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) avid lymphadenopathy. In the absence of clinical, laboratory, or radiologic evidence of aggressive lymphoma, ibrutinib was continued and no treatment of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma was administered. Discussion

Progression of CLL/SLL is well-described during temporary interruption of ibrutinib treatment, but Richter transformation after the hold of ibrutinib has only been reported in a few case reports [13]. Hampel et al. reported three cases of incidental pathological Richter transformation in CLL/SLL patients under a temporary pause of ibrutinib [14]. Two of the cases were asymptomatic, and one of the cases experienced low-grade fevers and malaise which resolved after restarting ibrutinib. In all three cases, lymph nodes were obtained during surgeries for other diseases (colon adenocarcinoma, rectal adenocarcinoma, and lipoma). For these cases, ibrutinib was restarted after the surgeries. Although CLL/SLL progression necessitating subsequent therapy occurred six months and nine months after the hold of ibrutinib in two of the patients, there was no observed aggressive course of Richter transformation in any of the three patients. Slonim et al. also reported five cases of biopsy-proven Richter transformation after a temporary hold of ibrutinib due to surgeries or infections [15]. These cases demonstrated signs of lymphoma progression after the temporary hold of ibrutinib, including lymphadenopathy, lymphocytosis, and elevated LDH levels. After the resumption of ibrutinib, lymphadenopathy largely improved, and the laboratory values normalized within several weeks. Three of the patients underwent a re-biopsy, which showed no evidence of Richter transformation or DLBCL.

Long-term follow-up of a recent clinical trial showed that for CLL/SLL patients who stopped receiving ibrutinib for reasons unrelated to disease progression, the medium progression-free survival was 25 months (defined as the duration between the discontinuation of ibrutinib to disease progression or death) [6]. This observation reinforces the presumption that a brief hold of ibrutinib rarely causes an immediate full-scale Richter transformation of CLL/SLL, although there is no tissue evidence to show whether Richter transformation existed in these cases.

The case we present here, alongside the above cases, represents a distinct group of pathology-only Richter transformation occurring after a brief pause in BTK inhibitor treatment. This phenomenon has been referred to in other reports as “Richter-like transformation,” or “pseudo-Richter transformation,” where the clinical manifestations are incompatible with the pathological findings. Given the limited number of reported cases, the clinical significance of this finding remains uncertain. It remains unclear whether the pathology-only Richter transformation could occur prior to the interruption of ibrutinib since routine biopsies are not warranted for tissue diagnosis in the absence of clinical evidence for Richter transformation. Because ibrutinib was promptly resumed in these cases, it is unknown whether Richter-like transformation would evolve into full-blown Richter transformation if the patients remained off BTK inhibitors for a longer duration. It is reassuring that in all the reported cases, patients have been followed for months or even years after resumption of ibrutinib without any symptoms or signs of aggressive lymphoma.

Although a patient presented by Hampel et al. and cases presented by Slonim et al. demonstrated constitutional symptoms or laboratory findings of worsening CLL/SLL, these occurrences may only reflect a transient progression or flare-up of the disease following the sudden discontinuation of ibrutinib. The relationship between these presentations and the patients' pathology-only Richter transformation remains uncertain. Furthermore, it is indeterminate whether patients with Richter-like transformation after the hold of BTK inhibitors are at higher risk of future CLL/SLL progression. The connection between this specific category of pathology-only Richter transformation and the likelihood of progression in the future is not well established. Patients within this group may require a different approach to treatment.

As the number of CLL/SLL patients receiving BTK inhibitor increases, there is a critical need to establish a clinical guideline for the evaluation and potential treatment of pathology-only Richter transformation. Based on our experience and the available published reports, we recommend resumption of the BTK inhibitor and close clinical follow-up when Richter’s transformation is identified incidentally. To our knowledge, there is only one pathology image publication of Richter-like transformation following a brief interruption of acalabrutinib [16]. There is no report regarding this phenomenon related to other BTK inhibitors, such as zanubrutinib and pirtobrutinib. Nonetheless, it is plausible that Richter-like transformation could occur with all drugs in the class, given their similar mechanism of action. We would recommend resumption of the BTK inhibitor in those cases as well.

Conclusion

Pathology-only Richter transformation can occur in patients with CLL/SLL following a brief hold of BTK inhibitor. The clinical significance of Richter-like transformation and its relationship to the progression of CLL/SLL remain unclear. In the current case and others reported to date, resuming treatment with a BTK inhibitor provided disease control without clinical evidence of progression to aggressive lymphoma.
