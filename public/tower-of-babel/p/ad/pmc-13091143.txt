Transforming oncology clinical trial matching through neuro-symbolic, multi-agent AI and an oncology-specific knowledge graph: a prospective evaluation in 3804 patients
Authors: A Loaiza-Bonilla, C Yost, S Kurnaz, E Tuysuz, NG Thaker, D Giritlioglu, JP Noel Meza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical trial enrollment in oncology remains critically low, with fewer than 5% of eligible adults participating, in large part due to the complexity and labor intensity of eligibility screening. We prospectively evaluated a neuro-symbolic, multi-agent artificial intelligence (AI) platform integrating domain-specific large language model (LLM) agents, an oncology-specific knowledge graph, a real-time recommendation engine, and human-in-the-loop review to determine whether automated extraction and reasoning can safely improve trial identification, efficiency, and equity at scale.MethodsConsecutive patients [N = 3804; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) 0-2] balanced for cancer type incidence with metastatic or progressive malignancies were screened across a 12-month period. A multi-agent architecture—OncoAgents (LLM-based extraction and reasoning agents), OncoGraph (oncology knowledge graph), OncoRecommend (prioritization engine), and OncoSet (expert-curated corpus)—carried out automated data extraction, harmonization, and trial matching over 157 367 clinical pages (∼86.5 M tokens). Dual oncologists produced a gold standard of trial eligibility labels (Cohen’s κ = 0.92). The primary unit of analysis was the patient–trial pair. Baselines included manual screening, GPT-4 zero-shot prompting, GPT-4 chain-of-thought, and frontier GPT-4o extraction/matching benchmarks. Outcomes included sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score, calibration of eligibility confidence scores, time-to-recommendation, fairness across demographic subgroups, and operational burden.ResultsThe multi-agent neuro-symbolic system achieved an F1 score of 0.82 (95% confidence interval 0.81-0.83). In comparison, the GPT-4 zero-shot baseline achieved an F1 of 0.47, and the GPT-4 chain-of-thought baseline achieved an F1 of 0.67. Per-patient screening time decreased from a median of 120 min (manual review) to ∼30 min total (15 min automated processing + 15 min clinical review). Across the cohort, the system processed 157 000 pages, screened 23 912 candidate patient–trial pairs, and produced 17 912 oncologist-confirmed matches, with median time-to-recommendation <7 days. No demographic subgroup exceeded a 10-percentage point F1 gap; the largest observed difference was ∼7 points between white and black/African American patients. Ablation experiments showed that both knowledge graph grounding and multi-agent decomposition contributed materially to performance and efficiency. Eligibility confidence scores exhibited reasonable calibration in the clinically relevant operating range.ConclusionsA neuro-symbolic, multi-agent architecture that couples LLM-based extraction with ontology-grounded, deterministic eligibility reasoning improved the accuracy, throughput, and timeliness of oncology clinical trial matching versus LLM-only baselines, while preserving clinician oversight and maintaining modest subgroup performance gaps. These results support scalable, equity-aware deployment of AI-assisted trial screening in routine oncology practice.Key words: neuro-symbolic AI, clinical trial matching, knowledge graph, multi-agent systems, large language models (LLMs), oncology decision support

Introduction

The persistent crisis of oncology trial enrollment

Clinical trials drive oncology progress, yet only 3%-5% of adult cancer patients enroll in research studies.1,2 Enrollment is limited less by trial availability than by the effort of mapping fragmented longitudinal records to complex, exception-rich eligibility criteria that are increasingly genomic, temporal, and organ-function specific.

Manual screening typically takes ∼120 min per patient and requires multi-year chart review across disparate systems; in high-volume (especially community) practice this is unsustainable and contributes to missed trial opportunities.

From deterministic rules to neuro-symbolic artificial intelligence

Trial-matching tools have progressed from rule/keyword systems to natural language processing (NLP), retrieval-augmented pipelines, and zero-shot large language models (LLMs),3,4 but the hardest part of eligibility screening is still not simply finding relevant text. It is translating free-text oncology criteria into executable, auditable logic that can be applied to fragmented longitudinal records containing negation, temporality, laboratory thresholds, and exceptions.

This is the specific gap addressed here. Prior artificial intelligence (AI)-based matching systems have shown promise for candidate retrieval or narrow-scope matching, but fewer studies have prospectively evaluated broad oncology workflows that combine multimodal electronic health record (EHR) extraction, ontology normalization, deterministic temporal reasoning, and clinician review within one framework. Even frontier LLMs (e.g. GPT-4o) remain probabilistic and can hallucinate criteria or mis-handle timing, making direct end-to-end deployment risky.

We therefore evaluated a neuro-symbolic architecture in which LLM agents carry out narrative extraction and normalization, while an oncology-specific knowledge graph executes explicit inclusion, exclusion, and exception logic. This differs from LLM-only matching systems because recommendations are not returned directly from free text; they must first be mapped to canonical concepts and pass graph-based checks for numeric, Boolean, and temporal consistency.5, 6, 7

Methods

Study design and cohort

From January to December 2024, we prospectively evaluated 3804 consecutive patients with metastatic or progressive solid tumors [Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) 0-2] across US academic–community sites. Screening eligibility was intentionally broad to mirror routine practice (active/progressing disease and candidates for additional therapy), minimizing selection bias; patients were screened at referral and re-screened as new imaging, pathology, or genomic results accrued (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100706).

OncoSet served only for development and few-shot prompting; the prospective cohort was fully held out from prompt engineering and calibration. The trial library included open interventional phase I-III trials recruiting locally, plus curated nationwide referral trials for rare molecular subtypes. Trial availability was updated regularly during the study period.

Gold standard annotation

Two board-certified medical oncologists independently reviewed each patient’s complete EHR (pathology, imaging, clinical notes, genomics, and treatment history) to label eligibility for relevant candidate trials (see Baselines and comparative models) using a predefined interpretation protocol.

κ Inter-rater reliability was high (Cohen’s κ = 0.92); discrepancies were resolved by consensus. This dual-oncologist dataset served as the gold standard for evaluation and as a calibration corpus for error analysis.

To prioritize safety, if a mandatory criterion (e.g. a required biomarker) was undocumented, patients were labeled Not Eligible for that constraint. Ambiguous temporal references without dates were flagged as Requires Clinician Judgment and excluded from the binary F1 calculation.

Knowledge graph curation and temporal logic

Trials from ClinicalTrials.gov are ingested monthly. Free-text criteria are parsed using pattern rules plus LLM-assisted extraction, then represented as atomic clauses that are mapped to ontology terms. Curators store each clause at three levels: the literal trial text, a normalized concept representation, and an executable graph rule. Temporal constraints are represented with event nodes (e.g. treatment start/end, progression, steroid exposure) and interval windows anchored to a screening date; negations and exceptions are encoded as explicit exclusion or override edges then mapped to ontology terms.

Laboratory thresholds are normalized to common units with assay notes when needed; ambiguous criteria are flagged for curator review. Protocol amendments are reconciled conservatively, and unresolved ambiguity is routed to Requires Clinician Judgment rather than permissive auto-matching. All OncoGraph updates are version-controlled to support drift analysis (Boxes 1 and 2).

The primary unit of analysis was the ‘patient–trial pair’. Candidate trials were pre-filtered using high-level attributes (site/histology, metastatic status, ECOG, key biomarker flags), then the gold standard labeled each patient–trial pair as:

•Eligible: all automatable criteria satisfied with no disqualifying criterion.

Eligible: all automatable criteria satisfied with no disqualifying criterion.

•Not eligible: at least one clearly disqualifying criterion present.

•Requires Clinician Judgment: eligibility depends on nuanced interpretation (e.g. borderline imaging or complex comorbidity trade-offs).

We computed sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 over pairs labeled eligible or not eligible. Pairs labeled Requires Clinician Judgment were described separately but excluded from the binary F1 calculation to mirror real-world clinician decision-making.

A pre-specified absolute F1 gap >10 points between any two groups triggered a fairness alert. Subgroup sizes and 95% confidence intervals (nonparametric bootstrap) are reported in Supplementary Table S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100706.

Deployment, data governance, and compute footprint

The system ran in the health networks’ secure environment under a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)-aligned business associate agreement and patient consent, processing protected health information on-premises or in a role-controlled virtual private cloud.

Intermediate agent messages and CoT explanations remained within this environment; clinician-facing rationales linked to EHR evidence rather than reproducing long text.

Median model-processed volume was ∼23 000 tokens per patient, corresponding to an estimated low single-digit dollar compute cost; costs vary by model choice and re-screening frequency.

No demographic subgroup exceeded the pre-specified 10-point F1 gap threshold. We treated subgroup gaps as equity signals, not only statistical artifacts. Because the system is conservative—flagging missing mandatory criteria as Requires Clinician Judgment or Ineligible rather than hallucinating—missing data reduced automated recall for some patients. Bootstrap comparisons suggested some gaps may be statistically significant, but we primarily interpreted magnitude and direction as equity risk. Black/African American patients had a 12% higher rate of missing structured staging data than white patients, which correlated with lower recall, indicating the disparity was driven mainly by upstream documentation completeness and interoperability rather than model reasoning bias. This links algorithmic fairness to data equity: improving performance for underrepresented groups requires upstream documentation and access interventions.

Ablation analysis

We conducted ablation experiments on the held-out test set (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100706) to estimate the contribution of key components.

Removing OncoGraph grounding (‘no-graph’ variant) reduced precision and F1 (0.8246→0.79) and produced more hallucinated matches that violated numeric or temporal criteria, highlighting the graph’s role in preventing logically inconsistent recommendations.

Collapsing extraction and matching into a single LLM call (‘single-agent’ variant) simplified the pipeline but lowered extraction fidelity and increased unresolved feature–criteria conflicts. F1 fell to 0.78 versus 0.8246 for the full multi-agent system, suggesting decomposition helps maintain consistency. The multi-agent decomposition appears to help the model maintain focus and consistency on extraction before reasoning.

Disabling uncertainty-based triage increased reviewer time by spreading attention across clear and ambiguous cases; focusing review on the highest-uncertainty ∼25% of pairs concentrated oversight where errors were most likely while preserving sensitivity.

Together, these ablations indicate that both graph grounding and agentic decomposition materially contribute to performance and usability.

Calibration and uncertainty triage

Eligibility confidence scores showed near-optimal calibration across typical screening thresholds (Supplementary Table S5; Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100706), with mild underconfidence at lower bins and modest overconfidence at extremes.

Varying the ‘high-uncertainty’ threshold traded reviewer workload against sensitivity (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100706). The selected threshold directed attention to ∼25% of pairs with the highest uncertainty, balancing safety, efficiency, and human-in-the-loop oversight.

Discussion

Why a neuro-symbolic, multi-agent architecture matters

Across tasks, the neuro-symbolic multi-agent architecture outperformed naive and CoT-prompted LLM baselines by grounding eligibility logic in OncoGraph. This is not a marginal gain: graph constraints enforce numeric and temporal rules, reduce hallucinated matches, and make reasoning auditable, while agent decomposition keeps extraction and matching focused and consistent. The pattern aligns with Moravec’s Paradox10: LLMs handle messy narrative understanding but are fragile on Boolean and temporal checks; separating semantic extraction (OncoAgents) from deterministic reasoning (OncoGraph) improved reliability. This division of labor supports clinician review with concise evidence-linked rationales rather than opaque end-to-end prompts.

The central message of Table 1 is therefore not simply that one model scored higher than another, but that performance improved in the same order as structural constraints were added. Zero-shot prompting performed worst because raw clinical notes and raw eligibility text had to be interpreted in a single unconstrained step. CoT prompting improved performance, consistent with the value of explicit intermediate reasoning, but it still relied on latent, non-executable representations of timing, negation, and eligibility exceptions. The full system’s further gain to an F1 of 0.82, together with the ablation drops observed when graph grounding or agentic decomposition were removed, suggests that the largest incremental benefit came from externalizing patient state, normalizing it to oncology concepts, and then executing eligibility rules deterministically.

In practical terms, OncoGraph appears to contribute most strongly to precision by blocking logically inconsistent matches, whereas decomposition into extraction, normalization, and matching agents improves recall and reviewer efficiency by reducing omitted clinical features and resolving conflicts across fragmented documentation.

Relation to prior work

Prior multi-agent, knowledge-grounded clinical systems provide important context.4,11 We extend this approach to broad tumor coverage and a large consecutive prospective cohort, showing that modular agents plus graph grounding can scale in routine practice (3804 patients; 23 912 patient-trial pairs). This broad coverage complements tightly scoped pre-screening programs and stresses robustness to real-world documentation heterogeneity.

These findings address a specific knowledge gap in the trial-matching literature. Prior systems have demonstrated that LLMs, retrieval, and ontology alignment can help identify candidate trials,3,4,11, 12, 13 but many published approaches remain retrospective, disease-limited, or optimized for text similarity and retrieval rather than auditable patient–trial execution over longitudinal records. Our framework differs in combining multimodal EHR extraction, ontology grounding, explicit temporal and exception handling, uncertainty triage, and prospective clinician adjudication within a broad oncology cohort. The contribution is therefore less a claim of universal superiority than a demonstration that neuro-symbolic orchestration can be deployed prospectively at scale with transparent logic and measurable operational benefit.

ClinicalAgent and related knowledge graph CoT/RAG systems7,12 advocate modular reasoning over structured knowledge, while frameworks like EHRMIND emphasize verifiable, calibrated EHR-grounded methods.5 Our study adds prospective, equity-aware real-world evidence, including calibration and uncertainty triage.

Direct head-to-head benchmarking against proprietary systems was not feasible, but our metrics align with or exceed published retrieval-augmented screening results in oncology, particularly in reducing hallucinated matches through knowledge graph grounding.

Pipelines such as TRIALSCOPE demonstrate oncology-scale text normalization and ontology alignment.13 Our work builds on these structuring foundations by closing the loop to patient-level trial recommendations.

Overall, the field is converging on LLMs embedded in structured, verifiable, equity-aware clinical systems—aligned with guidance such as TRIPOD-LLM.14

Equity as a first-class performance metric

The ∼7-point F1 deficit for black/African American patients, though below our alert threshold, is clinically important given longstanding underrepresentation in oncology trials. The equity results are encouraging but should not be overinterpreted. No subgroup crossed the pre-specified 10-point F1 alert threshold, yet the ∼7-point gap observed between white and black/African American patients remains clinically meaningful because disparities in trial access are already well documented. The observed error pattern suggests that this gap was driven primarily by upstream data completeness—particularly fragmented care histories, missing staging information, and incompletely documented comorbidities—rather than by qualitatively different reasoning behavior of the model itself.

This distinction is important because improving fairness in this context is as much a documentation and interoperability problem as a model optimization problem. Accordingly, mitigation requires both modeling work and upstream improvements in clinical documentation completeness.

Distribution shift and external validation

This evaluation occurred within a single health system with relatively consistent documentation norms. External validation across diverse EHR vendors, sites, and patient populations is essential before broad deployment to mitigate distribution shift. Our single-system prospective evaluation demonstrates that the architecture can function under real clinical documentation conditions, but distribution shift is likely to arise both from local documentation conventions and from evolving trial portfolios, biomarker requirements, and protocol language. For this reason, site-specific calibration, local ontology mapping, and routine drift monitoring should be considered ongoing operational requirements rather than one-time validation exercises.

Technical debt and knowledge graph maintenance

Maintaining OncoGraph is essential but nontrivial: ontologies evolve and trial portfolios change, requiring ongoing curation, conflict resolution, and monitoring.

We tracked maintenance effort to keep OncoGraph current: ∼20 h/month of expert curator time plus ∼4 h/month of senior oncologist oversight. This fixed cost functions as a ‘price of safety’—a deterministic logic layer that can be centrally audited and updated, unlike pure LLM systems that require continual re-prompting or retraining to correct hallucinations. Knowledge graph maintenance therefore represents a form of intentional technical debt. While ingestion and mapping are partly automated, ongoing human review remains necessary as ontologies, biomarkers, and trial portfolios evolve. Pure LLM pipelines may appear lighter weight but often relocate this burden into repeated prompt tuning, silent failure modes, or post hoc chart review.

Limitations and future directions

Our evaluation reports identification efficiency—time, accuracy, calibration, and fairness—but not downstream endpoints such as consent, enrollment, or trial completion. We distinguish between ‘identification efficiency’ and ‘enrollment success’: accurate matching is necessary but not sufficient because logistical, socioeconomic, and consent barriers persist. Reported gains primarily reduce the burden of identifying eligible patients; future work will measure conversion from AI-matched to consented across the full enrollment funnel.

Head-to-head comparison with every proprietary matching system was not feasible, and ablation analyses reflect our specific implementation. The recurring failure modes observed in the LLM-only baselines, however—hallucinated criteria, inconsistent temporal reasoning, and vulnerability to documentation gaps—suggest that the value of symbolic constraint is structural rather than model-specific.

Future work should therefore focus on multi-site validation, version-to-version drift reporting, and end-to-end measurement of how improved matching translates into actual trial enrollment.

Conclusions

In a prospective evaluation of 3804 consecutive patients, processing 157 367 clinical pages and >23 000 candidate patient–trial pairs, a neuro-symbolic multi-agent platform improved trial-matching accuracy, throughput, and timeliness while preserving clinician oversight and an equity-aware deployment framework.

By outperforming zero-shot, CoT, and frontier GPT-4o models and reducing screening time fourfold, the system offers a practical path for responsible AI integration into oncology research. The findings provide a transparent, calibrated, and equity-aware foundation for future collaborative validation studies and illustrate how agentic LLM components can be constrained by symbolic structure for safety-critical deployment.
