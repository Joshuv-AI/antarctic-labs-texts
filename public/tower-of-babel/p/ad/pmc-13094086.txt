Prediction of academic outcomes by serum cortisol, perceived academic stress, perceived memory performance, and physical activity among preclinical undergraduate medical students: a short-term observational study
Authors: Pathmavarthini Ganesh, Ughanthinii Kaliyappan, Shalini Sella Balakanapathi, Sylvia Rubini Santhanasamy, Trisha Parantaman, Devaashiny Swami Vivekananda, Nursiati Mohamad Taridi, Raja Zarith Fatiah Raja Malek Ridhuan, Rubythra Dewi Rajo, Khairul Anwar Zarkasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStress is common among medical students and adversely affects memory function and academic performance. Cortisol and physical activity (PA) are important physiological and lifestyle factors, yet evidence on their combined effects in military medical students is limited. This study examined whether serum cortisol, perceived stress, perceived memory performance, and PA predict academic outcomes among preclinical students in a military-based institution.MethodsA short-term prospective observational study was conducted among Years 1–2 preclinical medical students at Universiti Pertahanan Nasional Malaysia from January to September 2025, five weeks before the end-of-semester I examination (n = 106). Baseline assessments included perceived stress (Medical Student Stressor Questionnaire, MSSQ), perceived memory performance (Prospective-Retrospective Memory Questionnaire, PRMQ), and PA levels (Short-Form International Physical Activity Questionnaire, IPAQ-SF), and morning serum cortisol concentration. Participants were stratified into risk groups and sub-groups based on combinations of perceived stress, serum cortisol tertiles, and PA levels (expressed as metabolic equivalent of task, MET) and were followed across two semesters. Academic outcomes included grade point average (GPA) and the requirement for repeat semesters.ResultsThe participants’ median age was 20 years (interquartile range: 19.6–20.7) with approximately 1.5:1.0 female-to-male ratio. Perceived stress correlated positively with perceived memory problems (r = 0.445, p < 0.001), while serum cortisol showed no continuous correlation with the other study variables. On ordinal scales, the “high stress + high cortisol” was associated with poorer perceived memory performance (p = 0.034), whereas “low stress + high PA” was associated with better perceived memory performance (p = 0.027). High-risk students exhibited significantly increased risks of attaining lower GPA in semester I and semester II, with relative risks of 3.10 and 2.54, respectively (p < 0.01). Although PA moderated these associations, it did not eliminate the elevated academic risk. Compared with the “low risk + high MET” group, both “high risk + high MET” (RR = 3.33 and 3.87, respectively, p < 0.01) and “high risk + low MET” (RR = 3.24 and 3.91, respectively, p < 0.05) showed further increased risks of lower GPA in both semesters. Risk stratifications did not predict repeat semester requirements.ConclusionHigher perceived stress combined with higher cortisol was associated with poorer perceived memory and increased risk of lower academic performance across two semesters. These effects were partially mitigated by PA. The study findings support the implementation of integrated stress-management strategies and structured PA programmes to enhance cognitive function and academic performance among medical students in military institutions.Keywords: Serum cortisol, Perceived academic stress, Perceived memory performance, Physical activity, Medical students, Academic achievement

Introduction

Medical education is commonly associated with multiple stressors, including demanding schedules, high academic workloads, self-imposed performance targets, and substantial expectations from academic staff, family, and peers, in addition to high-stakes examinations [1]. In addition, medical students are frequently subjected to peer-related performance pressure, where academic achievement is often perceived competitively rather than as a reflection of individual learning capacity [1]. Such an environment may contribute to elevated stress levels that adversely affect cognitive performance and well-being. These stressors are further amplified among medical students in military-based institutions who are additionally exposed to regimented discipline, military responsibilities, and mandatory physical training requirements [2].

Physiologically, stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis to induce cortisol secretion, a glucocorticoid hormone that plays a critical role in energy metabolism and neurocognitive regulation [3]. Mineralocorticoid receptors (MR) have 6–10 times higher affinity towards glucocorticoids, particularly cortisol, than glucocorticoid receptors (GR) [4]. As cortisol level increases, MRs get saturated, which then leads to GRs activation [4]. Memory performance is favoured by MRs but unfavoured by GRs [4]. Subsequently, increased cortisol levels are linked to decreased attention span as well as impaired working and episodic memories through their effects on hippocampal and prefrontal circuits, brain regions that are critical for learning and performance during examinations [5, 6]. Based on this knowledge, serum cortisol serves as a reliable biomarker and an objective indicator of stress.

Perceived stress has been consistently associated with poorer memory performance, especially under conditions of high cognitive demand [7]. However, correlations between subjective stress and cortisol concentration are often weak or inconsistent, underscoring the importance of examining both psychological and physiological markers concurrently. Physical activity represents another key factor that can regulate the HPA-axis and mitigate stress. Regular physical activity improves the stress coping ability and stabilises cortisol levels through mechanisms such as endorphin release and autonomic regulation [8, 9]. Certainly, physical activity is an integral aspect of daily life for medical students in military-based institutions. Military medical students engage in physical activity not only as a compulsory military training, but also as a stress coping strategy [10]. However, the moderating role of physical activity on stress-related academic outcomes remains underexplored.

Despite these established theoretical links, previous studies have not comprehensively examined serum cortisol level, perceived academic stress, perceived memory performance, and physical activity in relation to academic outcomes among military medical students. Medical students in military-based institutions face a high rate of academic failures [11], with cognitive problems identified as one of the contributing factors [12]. However, current evidence has largely examined these variables in isolation and outside military context. At the same time, Malaysia continues to experience substantial shortages of medical graduates and house officers [13], highlighting the urgent need to better understand factors that influence academic success in medical training. Therefore, confirming how physiological stress markers, psychological stress perception, perceived memory performance, and physical activity interact to affect academic outcomes is essential for identifying students at risk and for informing targeted interventions aimed at improving academic performance and retention.

This short-term observational study conducted at Universiti Pertahanan Nasional Malaysia (UPNM) was designed to address this gap by evaluating these variables among Years 1 and 2 preclinical medical students, stratifying participants into risk groups and sub-groups, and prospectively following their grade point average (GPA) outcomes across two academic semesters. We hypothesised that the combination of high perceived stress and elevated cortisol represents a “high-risk” profile for poorer perceived memory performance that could affect academic outcomes, and that these adverse effects are moderated by physical activity.

Methods

Study design

A prospective cohort study was conducted from January to September 2025, following ethical clearance by the university’s research ethics committee. Participants were recruited during regular academic sessions approximately five weeks before the commencement of the end-of-semester I examination. Baseline data on participants’ demographic characteristics, serum cortisol levels, self-perceived stress levels, self-perceived memory performance, and PA levels were collected at baseline during recruitment. Participants were subsequently followed over the academic year, and their end-of-semesters I and II academic outcomes were obtained from the faculty’s academic office.

Study settings

The study was conducted at the Faculty of Medicine and Defence Health, Universiti Pertahanan Nasional Malaysia (UPNM), the only military-based university in Malaysia offering an undergraduate Doctor of Medicine (MD) programme. With this background, the medical students were uniquely comprised of cadets, including military and reserve army units, as well as non-cadets, with military-oriented activities extensively integrated into the core medical curriculum and university-mandated extracurricular programmes. The university’s environment provided a combination of high levels of physical activity from the military-based activities, along with elevated stress due to the inherent requirement of medical education, as well as a regimented disciplinary structure and strict lifestyle expectations.

Sample size calculation

Based on a mean difference of 3.1 between the case and control groups, a standard deviation (SD) of 5.2 within the cases, a between-group ratio of 1.02 [14], a 95% confidence interval, and at 80% statistical power, the minimum required sample size was 75. Allowing for an anticipated 10% attrition rate, the total target sample size was increased to 83. Universal sampling was applied, whereby all eligible Years 1 and 2 preclinical undergraduate medical students were invited to participate, which resulted in the final sample of 106.

Questionnaire tools

Participants were instructed to answer a self-administered questionnaire comprising sociodemographic, academic, and lifestyle characteristics, assessment of perceived stress, perceived memory performance, and physical activity levels. Perceived stress was measured using the validated Medical Student Stressor Questionnaire (MSSQ), which comprised 40 items divided into six stressor domains, comprising Academic Related Stressor, Intrapersonal and Interpersonal Related Stressor, Teaching and Learning Related Stressor, Social Related Stressor, Drive and Desire Related Stressor, and Group Activities Related Stressor. Each item was rated on a 6-point Likert scale from 0 (no stress) to 5 (severe stress). Domain scores were calculated as the mean of item scores within each domain, and the overall MSSQ score was derived as the average of the six domain scores. Overall stress levels were categorised as mild (0.00–1.00), moderate (1.01–2.00), high (2.01–3.00), or severe (3.01–4.00) [15].

Perceived memory performance was assessed using the validated Prospective-Retrospective Memory Questionnaire (PRMQ), which consisted of 16 items across prospective and retrospective memory domains. Participants rated the frequency of memory-related symptoms from 1 (never) to 5 (very often), with higher scores indicating greater self-perceived memory impairment. The overall PRMQ score was calculated by summing both retrospective and prospective scores [16]. Originally, PRMQ was developed to assess perceived memory performance among people with dementia [16], but subsequent studies have demonstrated its applicability across a wide age range, including young adults [17, 18]. These studies confirmed that the correlated prospective-retrospective memory factor model provides the best fit, with evidence of measurement invariance across adjacent age groups, supporting the scale’s structural validity in non‑clinical young populations [18]. Although no prior study has specifically examined PRMQ use among medical students, several investigations have administered the PRMQ to undergraduate university populations, further supporting its usability in assessing perceived memory performance in healthy young adults [19–21].

Physical activity (PA) levels were evaluated using the validated Short Form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF). Participants reported the frequency and duration of vigorous and moderate physical activities, walking, and the time they spent sitting for the past week. The metabolic equivalent of task (MET) was calculated using this information and further categorised into high, moderate, and low PA levels according to the standard IPAQ scoring protocols [22]. During further analysis, the PA levels were re-categorised into low and high according to the median total MET-minutes of the study population (cut-off value defined at 2,375 MET-minutes). A pilot study conducted among a small sample of medical students (n = 20) demonstrated high internal consistencies for the MSSQ and PRMQ instruments, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.938 and 0.874, respectively.

Blood sampling and serum cortisol measurement

Participants were instructed to avoid strenuous physical activity for 24 h, obtain 6–8 h of sleep, and refrain from consuming heavy meals prior to blood sampling. Compliance with pre‑sampling instructions was confirmed through brief verbal self‑report upon arrival at the study site. Those who reported non‑adherence were rescheduled to a later sampling session to minimise potential confounding effects on cortisol levels. Following completion of the questionnaire, 3 mL of whole blood was withdrawn from the antecubital vein between 08:00 and 10:00 h. Samples were placed in a plain tube and allowed to clot for 30 min at 4 °C. Once clotted, the serum was transferred into a fresh microcentrifuge tube and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Serum cortisol concentrations were quantified using the DetectX Cortisol Enzyme Immunoassay Kit (Arbor Assays, Michigan, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 50 µL of standards and serum samples were mixed with Dissociation Reagent in a 96-well plate, diluted at 1:100 using 1X Assay Buffer. Subsequently, 25 µL of DetectX Cortisol Conjugate and DetectX Cortisol Antibody were added to each well, followed by 1 h incubation at room temperature. Next, the wells were then aspirated, washed with 1X Wash Buffer, and tapped dry. Thereafter, 100 µL of TMB Substrate was added to each well and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of Stop Solution, and optical density was measured at 450 nm using SpectraMax M5 microplate reader (Molecular Device LLC., California, USA). Cortisol concentration was determined from a standard curve generated using the device’s built-in four-parameter logistic curve (4PLC) software.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS Statistics v26 software (IBM Corp., New York, USA) and R v4.3.1 (Bell Labs, New Jersey, USA) within the RStudio v2023.12.1 environment (Posit Software, Massachusetts, USA). Continuous variables demonstrated skewed distributions based on the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and were therefore summarised as median and interquartile ranges (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage, n (%).

The correlations among serum cortisol, overall MSSQ score, overall PRMQ score, and total MET were first analysed as continuous variables by Spearman’s ρ correlation test, followed by multiple linear regression for any significant correlations. Subsequently, they were converted to ordinal variables for further analysis. Since serum cortisol showed a skewed distribution in our dataset, categorising cortisol into tertiles avoided assumptions of normality, allowed exploration of potential threshold effects of cortisol, and aligned with prior studies where cortisol has been analysed using tertiles due to its variability [23]. As for perceived stress using MSSQ and PA levels using IPAQ-SF, the two tools have established cut-off ranges, which are parts of their validated scoring protocol [15, 22]. These categorisations assisted in stratifying the participants into risk groups and subgroups for longitudinal follow-up.

Differences in overall PRMQ scores among different participant groups defined by combinations of serum cortisol tertile and perceived stress levels, serum cortisol tertile and physical activity levels, as well as physical activity and perceived stress levels, were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Because several subgroup categories had small sample sizes, post‑hoc pairwise comparisons were not performed. Accordingly, only the omnibus Kruskal–Wallis p‑value was interpreted, and no multiple‑comparison correction was required. The distributions of participants’ characteristics according to the risk groups and sub-groups were assessed using Fisher’s exact test. The effect sizes for each statistical test were interpreted as follows: Kruskal Wallis H test (η2: small ~ 0.01, medium ~ 0.06, and large ~ 0.14), Fisher’s exact test (Cramer’s V: small ~ 0.10, medium 0.30, and large ~ 0.50), Mann-Whitney U test (r: small ~ 0.10, medium ~ 0.30, and large ~ 0.50) [24, 25].

Lastly, the predictive effects of risk groups (“high risk” vs. “low risk”) and risk sub-groups (“high risk + low MET”, “high risk + high MET”, and “low risk + low MET” vs. “low risk + high MET”) on academic outcomes at end-of-semester examinations were examined using multivariate ordinal regression models with adjustment for potential confounders (selected when p < 0.25 on association analysis) [26]. Odds ratios (OR) derived from the regression analyses were converted to estimated relative risks (RR) using the following equation: RR = OR ÷ [(1 – P0) + (P0 × OR)], where P0 represents the event outcome (GPA ≤ 3.00) within the unexposed group (assigned to the “low risk” and “low risk + high MET” groups) [27]. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value of < 0.05.

Results

With respect to sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics, participants had a median age of 20 years, were predominantly female (63.2%) and of Malay ethnicity (69.8%), with a median family income of RM6,500. Nearly equal proportions originated from the rural or semi-urban (47.2%) and urban areas (52.8%). Regarding the academic characteristics, most participants were non-cadets (89.6%), received study loans (51.9%), and were almost equally distributed between Year 1 (50.9%) and Year 2 (49.1%) of the medical programme. The median self-study duration was four hours per day, while the median sleep duration was five hours per night. In terms of personal characteristics, the majority of the participants had never smoked a cigarette or vaped (95.3%), reported moderate physical activity levels (51.9%), had no comorbidities (75.5%), and were not on long-term medications (90.6%). Compared with the low-risk group, high-risk participants exhibited a significantly higher median serum cortisol concentrations (148.6 vs. 68.6 ng/mL, p < 0.001, r = 0.72), nominally higher perceived stress levels as reflected by overall MSSQ score (2.0 vs. 1.8, p = 0.070, r = 0.18), nominally poorer perceived memory performance indicated by higher overall PRMQ score (38.0 vs. 35.0, p = 0.094, r = 0.16), and similar PA levels measured by total MET-minutes (2,493 vs. 2,175 MET-minutes, p = 0.327, r = 0.72). No statistically significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics according to this risk stratification. However, several variables demonstrated a nominal difference between the two risk groups at p < 0.25, including age group (p = 0.101, Cramer’s V = 0.17), ethnicity (p = 0.217, Cramer’s V = 0.17), and academic year (p = 0.101, Cramer’s V = 0.17), and were therefore considered potential confounders in subsequent analyses (Table 3).

Participants were further sub-stratified into low (< 2,373 MET-minutes) and high (≥ 2,373 MET-minutes) PA sub-groups within each risk group to examine the potential mediating role of physical activity on academic outcomes. A progressive and significant increase in serum cortisol concentrations was observed across sub-groups from “low risk + high MET” to “high risk + low MET” (83.8–160.2 ng/mL, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.52). A similar increasing trend was noted for perceived stress (overall MSSQ score: 1.7 to 2.1, p = 0.160, η2 = 0.02) and perceived memory performance (overall PRMQ score: 35.5 to 38.0, p = 0.269, η2 = 0.01), with nominal differences for MSSQ and non-significant differences for PRMQ. The total MET-minutes were significantly different among all risk subgroups, comprising the “high risk + low MET”, “high risk + high MET”, “low risk + low MET”, and “low risk + high MET” (1,215, 3,800, 1,314, and 3,333 MET-minutes, respectively, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.75) (Table 3).

There were significant differences in the distribution of age (p = 0.044, Cramer’s V = 0.28) and academic year (p = 0.026, Cramer’s V = 0.29) across the risk sub-groups. Additionally, nominal differences were observed for gender (p = 0.204, Cramer’s V = 0.21) and student type (p = 0.128, Cramer’s V = 0.23) (Table 3). Based on these findings, gender, ethnicity, student type, and academic year were identified as potential confounders and included in subsequent multivariate analyses. The age group was excluded due to its strong positive correlation with academic year (rs=0.869, p < 0.001).

Students were followed through the end of semesters I and II to determine whether the risk groups and sub-groups could predict examination outcomes, defined by grade point average (GPA) of ≤ 3.00, 3.01–3.50, and 3.51–4.00, as well as the requirement to repeat a particular semester. For semester I, students classified within the high-risk group demonstrated a significantly increased risk of attaining a lower GPA, with an adjusted relative risk (RR) of 3.10 (95% CI = 1.77–4.51, p < 0.001) after confounders adjustment (Table 4). Incorporation of physical activity levels slightly but significantly augmented these risks. Compared with the “low risk + high MET”, students in the “high risk + high MET” and “high risk + low MET” sub-groups exhibited RRs of 3.33 (95% CI = 1.42–5.72, p = 0.008) and 3.24 (95% CI = 1.31–5.72, p = 0.014), respectively, corresponding to an approximate 6% increase over the baseline risk.

For semester II, high-risk students had a 2.54-fold increased risk of attaining a lower GPA after adjustment for confounders. Relative to the “low risk + high MET”, the “high risk + high MET” and “high risk + low MET” sub-groups demonstrated greater risks with an RRs of 3.87 (95% CI = 1.52–7.06, p = 0.007) and 3.91 (95% CI = 1.49–7.21, p = 0.008), respectively, which represents approximately a 53% increase over the initial risks. Discussion

In this study, a significant and positive correlation was observed between overall MSSQ and overall PRMQ scores, indicating that a higher perceived stress level was associated with poorer perceived memory performance. This observation corroborated findings from previous research performed across diverse age groups, including young, middle-aged, and older adults [7, 28, 29]. Prior evidence has demonstrated that both perceived stress and experimentally induced acute stress are associated with impaired working memory performance, particularly under high cognitive load conditions, with more pronounced effects reported among males [7, 28, 29]. In contrast, this study did not identify any substantial correlation between serum cortisol levels and overall MSSQ scores, overall PRMQ scores, or total MET-minutes when analysed on a continuous scale.

However, when examined using ordinal categories, the combination of higher perceived stress with elevated cortisol tertile, as well as higher perceived stress with lower physical activity levels, was associated with poorer perceived memory performance, as reflected by higher overall PRMQ scores. These findings suggest that cortisol may exert measurable influence through threshold effects and that its impact could depend on synergistic interactions with other variables (i.e., perceived stress and PA levels).

Threshold‑like patterns in cortisol have been reported previously, with studies showing that individuals in the highest cortisol tertile exhibited reduced brain volume, impaired memory performance, impaired visual perception, and increased cardiovascular disease risk [23, 30], while those in the lowest tertile showed elevated metabolic syndrome risk [31]. Prior research has also demonstrated that cortisol interacts with other biological and behavioural factors to influence clinical outcomes, including interactions with serotonin transporter gene in negatively impacting memory [32], with amyloid-β in affecting global cognition and episodic memory [33], with smoking status in exacerbating psychological distress [34], and with psychiatric history in amplifying anxiety and depressive symptoms [34]. These findings collectively support the plausibility of both threshold effects and synergistic interactions in explaining the observed associations in the present study. Nevertheless, there was no prior research specifically reporting cortisol interaction with perceived memory and PA.

Cortisol is widely recognised as a principal stress hormone with diverse physiological actions. Stress activates the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, stimulating the production and secretion of cortisol from the zona fasciculata layer of the adrenal cortex [3]. In this study, serum cortisol was measured once between 08:00 to 10:00 h in the morning following standard pre‑sampling instructions to minimise diurnal and behavioural variability. Single morning serum cortisol measurements are commonly used in clinical settings to determine the baseline of basal HPA axis activity [35]. Although a single measurement may not fully capture long‑term or chronic stress patterns, adopting this methodology is also more feasible in our setting due to logistical constraints and to minimise the burden of repeated sampling among participants. It also allowed for the early categorisation of students into different physiological stress profiles, combining serum cortisol tertiles with levels of perceived stress and PA levels, at the start of the semester.

Increased cortisol levels have been consistently associated with adverse cognitive effects, including impaired memory retrieval [36], decreased global cognitive function [37], decreased short-term memory [38], and impaired memory consolidation [39]. Conversely, higher levels of physical activity are known to alleviate stress by modulating the HPA axis activity, resulting in lower cortisol levels and better stress coping [40–43]. Physical activity promotes endorphin release and autonomic regulation, which are the mechanisms that contribute to reduced cortisol levels [8, 9]. These physiological effects may explain the significantly poorer perceived memory performance (indicated by higher overall PRMQ scores) observed among participants who had a combination of higher perceived stress and lower physical activity in this study. A similar trend of poorer perceived memory performance was also observed among participants with higher cortisol tertile combined with lower physical activity levels, although this association did not reach statistical significance.

Participants were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on their serum cortisol tertile and perceived stress levels, with further sub-stratification according to their physical activity (MET) levels, which corresponded to differences in perceived memory performance among the participants. Baseline characteristics were comparable between the high- and low-risk groups. However, within the risk sub-groups, older participants (≥ 20 years) and Year 2 students were disproportionately represented in the “high risk + low MET” category as compared to younger participants (< 20 years) and those in Year 1.

The Faculty of Medicine and Defence Health at UPNM adopts a traditional medical curriculum. The curriculum structure comprised Anatomy, Physiology, and Biochemistry subjects in Year 1, whereas Pathology, Microbiology and Immunology, Pharmacology, Parasitology, and Community Medicine subjects in Year 2. Therefore, we hypothesised that Year 2 UPNM’s medical students, who are generally older, face heavier academic demands and tighter schedules, which may contribute to higher cortisol levels, with reduced physical activity due to limited discretionary time outside academic commitments.

The participants were predominantly non-cadets compared to cadets (89.6% vs. 10.4%), which may have introduced residual confounding despite adjustments made in the multivariate regression analyses. Although non-cadets are generally exposed to fewer military-related stressors, the university environment incorporates numerous military-based activities, such as morning parades before entering the academic hall, physical penalties for disciplinary violations, and military-style attendance reporting, that continue to impose a degree of military-associated demands on all students. Additionally, both cadets and non-cadets undertake the Military Medicine course, which includes structured military‑training components such as field exercises, drill sessions, and team‑based operational tasks related to medicine. In terms of PA levels, the difference between cadets and non-cadets was only nominal (3,093 vs. 2,226 MET-minutes per week, p = 0.068), suggesting that both groups experienced relatively similar physical demands.

Overall, participants exhibited moderate to high levels of physical activity, with only 10.4% reporting low levels of physical activity, reflected by a median of 2,373 total MET-minute per week. This was the basis of sub-stratifying the participants into high and low-MET sub-groups rather than using the standard IPAQ classifications. Physical activity constitutes a major stress-coping strategy among military medical students and plays a crucial role in maintaining holistic well-being [10]. After adjustment for confounders, participants in the high-risk group demonstrated significantly increased risks of attaining a lower GPA in both semesters I and II. Further stratification by MET levels revealed additional increases of approximately 6% and 50% during respective semesters, suggesting that physical activity moderated the adverse effects of elevated stress and cortisol levels on academic achievements.

As discussed earlier, regular physical activity contributes to overall stress reduction and stabilisation of cortisol secretion through modulation of the HPA axis [40–43], whereas heightened stress and cortisol levels negatively impact academic achievements [44]. By attenuating these negative physiological and psychological effects, physical activity may enhance academic achievements, corroborating the findings reported by Chung et al. (2018) [45].

A key strength of this study lies in its prospective design with participants’ follow-up across two semesters. It incorporated both physiological and psychological markers, including serum cortisol, perceived stress, and perceived memory performance. Besides, the inclusion of physical activity as a potential moderator reflects lifestyle factors commonly employed by medical students.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. This includes the short-term observation period and restriction of participants to preclinical cohorts, which was intended to homogenise the exposure to stressor types but may limit the generalisability of findings to clinical year students. In addition, this study was conducted within a single institution, in which UPNM is the only military-based higher education institution in the country offering an undergraduate medical programme, which further limits external generalisability. Additionally, perceived memory performance was assessed using a self-report instrument rather than objective neurocognitive testing, which may not fully capture actual cognitive function. Besides, reliance on self-reported instrument may also introduce recall bias; and a single serum cortisol measurement may not reflect chronic stress. Finally, cognitive performance and academic achievements were not assessed beyond the two semesters, precluding evaluation of long-term academic trajectories, particularly during the clinical year.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that higher perceived academic stress in combination with elevated serum cortisol tertile was associated with poorer self-perceived memory performance and a significantly increased risk of attaining lower GPAs across two consecutive semesters. Physical activity moderated these relationships, with high-risk students showing increased academic risk compared to low-risk students who maintained high levels of physical activity. These findings underscore the importance of implementing integrated stress management strategies and structured physical activity programmes to support cognitive function and academic performance among medical students in military-based institutions. Nonetheless, given that the study was conducted in a single military institution and the sample was predominantly composed of non‑cadet students, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Future research involving multi-institution cohorts and more diverse student profiles are required to verify the generalisability and robustness of these findings. Future studies measuring cortisol level across multiple timepoints is also recommended for comprehensive assessment of chronic stress.
