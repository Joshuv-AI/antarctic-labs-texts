Microvesicles secreted by CoCl2-primed mesenchymal stromal cells boost the functionality of hematopoietic stem cells cultured with them
Authors: Shalmali Pendse, Vaijayanti Kale, Anuradha Vaidya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapid hematopoietic regeneration post-hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is vital for prompt immune recovery, infection prevention, and averting complications such as anemia and thrombocytopenia. The hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) produce mature blood cells through a process known as hematopoiesis that is tightly regulated by the hypoxic bone marrow microenvironment. However, the limited number of HSCs present in the bone marrow is one of the major challenges for successful HSCT. Hence, developing novel ex vivo expansion strategies that address the clinical limitations of the current HSCT becomes imperative. Our prior work demonstrated that priming of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) with a hypoxia-mimetic compound, cobalt chloride (CoCl2; CoCl2-MSCs) promotes the expansion of functional HSCs co-cultured with them. In this study, we investigated the role of CoCl2-MSCs-derived microvesicles (Co-MVs) and exosomes (Co-Exo) in regulating hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs). MVs and exosomes from naïve and atmospheric hypoxia (1% O2)-primed MSCs were used as controls. We found that CoCl2 induces the expression of Jagged-1-specific mRNA in the MSCs, which gets selectively partitioned into the Co-MVs. These Co-MVs, in turn, upregulate the expression of Notch2 and Hes1 in the HSPCs cultured with them. Furthermore, Co-MVs induce the expression of quiescence and self-renewal genes in the HSPCs and boost their long-term engraftment ability. These beneficial effects of Co-MVs on the functionality of HSPCs hold significant clinical implications as cell-free and cost-effective therapeutics in improving the success rate of HSCT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06652-z.Keywords: Priming, Mesenchymal stromal cells, Hypoxia, Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, Extracellular vesicles, Notch signaling pathway

Introduction

Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) are multipotent stem cells with a crucial functional characteristic to facilitate the lifelong reconstitution of hematopoiesis [1]. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) involves the transplantation of HSCs, allogenic or autologous, into myeloablated patients with malignant and non-malignant hematological disorders. However, at times, an inadequate number of HSCs present in the graft poses a major obstacle to successful HSCT. Therefore, ex vivo expansion of functional HSCs becomes imperative [2].

Bone marrow-derived stromal cells, commonly referred to as mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), are among the essential constituents of the bone marrow microenvironment (BMM) and are known to regulate the vital processes of HSCs, such as proliferation, differentiation, self-renewal, quiescence, and apoptosis [3–6]. They provide the microenvironment that facilitates the reconstitution of hematopoiesis and reduces the severity of the graft versus host disease post-HSCT [7]. MSCs release various factors and signaling molecules, including cytokines and chemokines, that interact with the HSCs, promoting their self-renewal, differentiation, and maintenance [8]. Due to these unique attributes, traditionally, they have been used as a feeder layer for the ex vivo expansion of HSCs [9]. Independent cultures of HSCs with the cytokines have been shown to promote their differentiation, thereby exhausting the stem cell pool. Hence, the co-culturing of MSCs with HSCs, the addition of small molecules and biocompatible polymers, and 3D cultures using hydrogels are used in order to maintain the stemness of HSCs in vitro [10–12].

Bone marrow (BM) is a relatively hypoxic tissue with oxygen levels ranging from 1% to 6% [13]. Within the BM, the HSCs predominantly reside in close association with osteoblasts, occupying an osteoblastic niche present at the lowest end of the oxygen gradient [14]. In this specialized niche, the HSCs primarily exist in a quiescent state, displaying reduced metabolic activity and minimal cell division, contributing to their long-term self-renewal capacity and protection against spontaneous differentiation [15, 16]. A few studies have shown that the in vitro culturing of HSCs in hypoxic conditions induced quiescence in them and increased their transplantation efficacy in immunodeficient mice [17, 18].

Previously, we have shown that MSCs primed with CoCl2 expand functional HSCs co-cultured with them [19]. In this study, we examined the effect of microvesicles (MVs) and exosomes (Exo) isolated from MSCs primed with CoCl2 on the regulation of HSCs. MVs and Exo secreted by MSCs primed with atmospheric hypoxia (1% O2; hypoxic-MSCs) and naïve (untreated/control) MSCs were used as controls. We found that CoCl2 induced a significantly high expression of Jag1 in the MSCs, which got selectively partitioned into their MVs and subsequently delivered to the HSPCs cultured with them. Furthermore, the Co-MVs induced the expression of Hes1 and Notch2 in the HSPCs and increased their long-term engraftment ability in vivo, indicating the role of the Notch signaling in the HSC regulation.

Overall, this study highlights the differential role of MVs and Exo isolated from CoCl2-MSCs in the regulation of HSCs. Based on our data, we speculate that MVs isolated from MSCs primed with hypoxia-mimetic compounds like CoCl2 could be used as cell-free biologics for ex vivo expansion of functional HSCs. Being independent of atmospheric oxygen, this system would be both convenient and cost-effective in a clinical setting.

Methodology

Animals

6–8 weeks old C57BL/6 mice (CD45.2; RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664) were purchased from the Advanced Centre for Treatment, Research, and Education in Cancer (ACTREC, Mumbai, India) and Global Bioresearch Solutions Pvt. Ltd (Pune, India). B6.SJL-ptprc Pep3/Boy J (CD45.1; RRID: IMSR_JAX:002014) and B6 ACTb-EGFP (RRID: IMSR_JAX:003291) (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, USA) were inbred in the Experimental Animal Facility of Symbiosis School of Biological Sciences (SSBS).

Isolation of mouse bone marrow-derived MSCs

The marrow stromal cells or mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) were isolated from the femurs and tibiae of C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) and cultured in a 100 mm dish in IMDM supplemented with 10% FBS in 5% CO2 at 37 °C [20].

Isolation of lineage-negative cells

Lin− cells were isolated using paramagnetic beads as previously described [20]. Briefly, mononuclear cells were isolated from whole BM cells using density gradient centrifugation. The lineage-negative cells (Lin−) were separated from the lineage-positive cells using a Biotinylated lineage cocktail (BD Biosciences) per the manufacturer’s instructions. The fraction of Lin− cells isolated is henceforth termed HSPCs.

Cell culture treatments to induce hypoxic conditions in the MSCs

Hypoxic conditions in the MSCs were created as described before [19].

i)Induction of chemical hypoxia:MSCs were cultured in IMDM supplemented with 10% FBS and treated with 100µM CoCl2 for 48 h. These MSCs were termed CoCl2-MSCs.

Induction of chemical hypoxia:

MSCs were cultured in IMDM supplemented with 10% FBS and treated with 100µM CoCl2 for 48 h. These MSCs were termed CoCl2-MSCs.

ii)Induction of atmospheric hypoxia:The cultured MSCs were kept in a sealed, humidified hypoxia chamber (Stem Cell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) for 48 h, purged with a gas mixture of 1% O2 + 5% CO2 + 94% N2. These MSCs were termed hypoxic-MSCs.

Induction of atmospheric hypoxia:

The cultured MSCs were kept in a sealed, humidified hypoxia chamber (Stem Cell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) for 48 h, purged with a gas mixture of 1% O2 + 5% CO2 + 94% N2. These MSCs were termed hypoxic-MSCs.

Isolation of total EVs, MVs, and Exo from MSCs primed with chemincal or atmospheric hypoxia using differential ultracentrifugation

The MSCs were treated as described above. After 48 h, the treatment was removed, and the MSCs were cultured in IMDM supplemented with 0.5% EV-depleted FBS. Hypoxic-MSCs were incubated under hypoxia after feeding with 0.5% EV-depleted FBS. The supernatant collected after 48 h was termed conditioned medium (CM) and centrifuged at 2600 X g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris and apoptotic bodies. The supernatant was ultra-centrifuged at 100,000 X g to isolate total EVs. Conversely, the supernatant was sequentially centrifuged at 10,000 X g for 1 h at 4 °C and 100,000 X g for 1 h at 4 °C to obtain MVs and Exo, respectively [21].

Field emission scanning electron microscopy

The MVs and Exo isolated from control-MSCs, CoCl2-MSCs, and hypoxic-MSCs were characterized using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) as described before [22]. Briefly, MVs and Exo were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 30 min and loaded on the Silicon wafers (Global Nanotech). Samples were then dehydrated by washing them with increasing concentrations of Ethanol (Merck) – 0%, 10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%. The images were captured on the FE-SEM machine and analysed on XT microscope EDS: ESspirit 1.9 software at Central Instrumentation Facility, Savitribai Phule Pune University, Pune. The size of MVs and Exo was analysed using ImageJ software [21].

Culturing HSPCs with total EVs, MVs, and Exo isolated from MSCs primed with chemical or atmospheric hypoxia

2 × 105 HSPCs were cultured independently with 10 µg protein-equivalent of EVs, MVs, and Exo isolated from control-MSCs, CoCl2-MSCs, and hypoxic-MSCs for 5 days in the presence of IL-3 (10ng/ml), IL-6 (25ng/ml), and SCF (25ng/ml). On the 6th day, the cells were harvested, enumerated using the trypan blue dye exclusion method, and subjected to phenotypic analyses and various functional assays.

Colony forming unit assay

The harvested HSPCs were evaluated for their ability to form progenitor cells by colony-forming unit (CFU) assay as described before [20]. Briefly, the harvested cells were incubated in the semi-solid methylcellulose medium containing a cocktail of growth factors - IL-3, IL-6, SCF, Epo, and insulin. After 10–12 days, GM, GEMM, BFU-E, and CFU-E colonies were counted manually using a phase contrast microscope (Carl Zeiss Inverted Microscope Phase contrast Axio. Vert 1, Germany).

Phenotypic analyses of HSPCs by flow cytometry

As described earlier, harvested HSPCs were subjected to phenotypic analysis using fluorescence-tagged antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) to assess various hematopoietic subsets [23]. Acquiring was done on BD FACS Melody™ sorter. The data were analysed using FlowJo software (Version 10).

Chemotactic migration assay

The assay was conducted as previously described [20]. Briefly, 5 × 105 harvested cells were seeded into the inserts (Corning, New York, USA) that were placed over the wells of a 24-well plate containing IMDM with 50ng SDF-1α. After 5 hours, the cells that migrated towards SDF-1α (B) were counted using the trypan blue dye exclusion method. The cells that spontaneously migrated in the absence of SDF-1α (C) were subtracted from those that migrated towards SDF-1α.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA from control-MSCs, CoCl2-MSCs, and hypoxic-MSCs was isolated using Trizol as described earlier [24]. Results

MVs and Exo expand HSPCs in vitro

Total EVs, MVs and Exo were isolated using differential ultracentrifugation and were characterized based on their size using FE-SEM. MVs and Exo isolated from control-MSCs, CoCl2-MSCs, and hypoxic-MSCs exhibited typical spherical morphology (Fig. 1A and B) with an average diameter of ~ 117 nm and ~ 74 nm, respectively (Fig. 1C). The MVs from all three groups had comparable sizes. However, there was a significant, albeit marginal, difference in the size of the Exo isolated from hypoxic-MSCs compared to those isolated from control-MSCs. We have also previously shown that the MSC-derived EVs express the CD63 tetraspanin marker [22, 26].

Previously, we have demonstrated that the CM isolated from CoCl2-MSCs expands HSCs in vitro [19]. Hence, here, we sought to examine whether EVs secreted by the CoCl2-MSCs and hypoxic-MSCs could do the same. Since in our earlier studies we had observed a differential effect of MVs and Exo in the rejuvenation of aged HSCs [22], in this study we examined the effect of independently culturing them with total EVs, MVs, and Exo for 5 days. On the 6th day, we harvested the cells and enumerated them using the trypan blue dye exclusion method.

To our surprise, the output cell number was significantly low when the HSPCs were cultured with total EVs, compared to the MVs and Exo, suggesting that total EVs strongly inhibit the proliferation of hematopoietic cells in vitro (Fig. 2A 1 st set of bars & Supp. Figure 1A). Furthermore, both Co-Exo and Hy-Exo groups showed significantly higher proliferation of the output hematopoietic cells than Co-MVs and Hy-MVs (Fig. 2A 2nd & 3rd set of bars, Supp. Figure 1B & 1C). Interestingly, unlike the con-MVs and Hy-MVs groups, there was no significant difference in the number of output cells between the con-MVs and Co-MVs groups (Fig. 2A, 2nd set of bars, 1 st and 2nd bars). These data suggest that Exo promote the expansion of hematopoietic cells, whereas MVs limit their proliferation in vitro.

Since culturing HSPCs with total EVs strongly inhibited their proliferation in vitro, we performed further experiments with HSPCs independently cultured with MVs and Exo isolated from control-MSCs, CoCl2-MSCs, and hypoxic-MSCs.

Co-MVs and Co-Exo promote the expansion of LT-HSCs

In order to assess the presence of various hematopoietic subsets in the harvested population, we subjected the harvested cells to flow cytometry analysis. We observed that the percentage of Lin− cells was significantly higher when HSPCs were cultured with Co-MVs and Co-Exo as compared to their control and hypoxic counterparts (Fig. 2B & Supp. Figure 1F). The overall percentage of Lin− cells was significantly higher in the Exo groups as compared to the MVs (Fig. 2B). Moreover, the absolute number of Lin− cells was significantly higher in the Exo groups as compared to the MVs groups due to the higher number of total output cells in the Exo cultures (Supp. Figure 1D).

HSCs are characterized based on the absence of any lineage-specific marker (Lin−) and the presence of Sca-1 and c-kit markers (Lin− Sca-1+ and c-kit+; termed LSK HSCs). Hence, we analysed the percentage of LSK HSCs in the harvested population. We observed no significant difference in the percentage of LSK HSCs between Co-MVs and Co-Exo groups and their control counterparts. However, the percentage of LSK HSCs in the Hy-MVs and Hy-Exo groups was significantly lower than the MVs and Exo isolated from the control-MSCs and CoCl2-MSCs (Fig. 2C & Supp. Figure 1G). The absolute number of the LSK HSCs was higher in the Exo groups as compared to MVs (Supp. Figure 1E). Moreover, Co-Exo showed the highest absolute number of LSK HSCs compared to con-Exo and Hy-Exo (Supp. Figure 1E). These data show that, as compared to the Hy-MVs and Hy-Exo, Co-MVs and Co-Exo promote the expansion of LSK HSCs.

We further assessed the output cells for the expression of CD34, a lineage commitment marker [27]. We observed that the percentage and absolute numbers of LSK CD34− cells (the cells having long-term engraftment ability; LT-HSCs) were significantly higher in both Co-MVs and Co-Exo groups, compared to their control- and hypoxic-counterparts (Fig. 2D & Supp. Figure 2A & Supp. Figure 2C). Conversely, the percentage and the absolute numbers of LSK CD34+ cells (cells with short-term engraftment ability; ST-HSCs) were significantly lower in both Co-MVs and Co-Exo groups (Fig. 2E, Supp. Figure 2B & Supp. Figure 2C). The absolute numbers of both LT-HSCs and ST-HSCs were significantly higher in the overall Exo groups than the MVs groups due to the higher number of output cells (Supp. Figure 2A & Supp. Figure 2B).

Altogether, these data suggest that the MVs and Exo isolated from CoCl2-MSCs independently promote the expansion of LT-HSCs.

Co-MVs enhance the chemotaxis of HSPCs and compromise their colony formation in vitro

Next, we analysed the expression of C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), a marker responsible for the migration and homing of HSCs to the BM [28] in the output cells from all six groups. There was no significant difference in the percentage of LSK CXCR4+ cells between the MVs and Exo groups (Fig. 3A & Supp. Figure 3A). However, the absolute number of LSK CXCR4+ cells was significantly higher in the Co-Exo and Hy-Exo groups than their MVs counterparts (Supp. Figure 3B). Moreover, the HSPCs cultured with Hy-Exo contained the highest absolute number of LSK CXCR4+ cells than those cultured with con-Exo and Co-Exo (Supp. Figure 3B).

SDF-1α secreted by the stromal cells in the BM serves as the ligand for the CXCR4. This CXCR4/SDF-1α axis plays a crucial role in the migration and homing of the HSCs in the BM post-transplantation [28]. As there was no significant difference in the percentage of LSK CXCR4+ cells in the MVs and Exo cultures, we hypothesized that perhaps the harvested cells from all the groups would show a similarin vitro migration ability towards SDF-1α. Contrary to our expectations, the HSPCs cultured with MVs showed a significantly higher migration ability than those cultured with Exo (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, amongst the MV sets, HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs exhibited significantly higher migration ability towards SDF-1α than those cultured with con-MVs and Hy-MVs (Fig. 3B; left-hand set of bars).

Since the HSPCs cultured with Exo exhibited compromised functionality in terms of in vitro migration ability, we performed further experiments on HSPCs cultured with MVs isolated from control-MSCs, CoCl2-MSCs, and hypoxic-MSCs.

Next, we subjected the harvested HSPCs to phenotypic analyses to assess the presence of different types of progenitors such as common lymphoid progenitors (CLP; Lin− Sca-1lowc-kitlowIL7Rα+), common myeloid progenitors (CMP; Lin−Sca-1−c-kit+CD16/32−CD34+), granulocyte-macrophage progenitors (GMP; Lin−Sca-1−c-kit+CD16/32+CD34+), and megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (MEP; Lin−Sca-1−c-kit+CD16/32−CD34−) in the harvested population. We observed that the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs had an increased percentage of CMP and MEP populations (Supp. Figure 3C 2nd and 4th set of bars and Supp. Figure 3E). Furthermore, there was a significant increase in the absolute numbers of both CLP and CMP populations in the Co-MVs group (Supp. Figure 3D 1 st and 2nd set of bars) and significantly lower absolute numbers of GMP (Supp. Figure 3D 3rd set of bars).

These data show that the Co-MVs promote the expansion of LT-HSCs and support the differentiation of all the lineage cells without creating any lineage bias, thereby denoting the ability of Co-MVs to reconstitute the entire hematopoiesis.

We further analysed the differentiation capacity of the harvested cells by performing a CFU assay. When the output cells were clonally seeded and cultured in a semi-solid methylcellulose medium supplemented with growth factors for 14 days, we observed that the number of GEMM, BFU-E, and total colonies were significantly lower in the Co-MVs group as compared to con-MVs and Hy-MVs (Fig. 3C 2nd, 3rd, and last set of bars). This could be due to the fact that this group contained a lower percentage of ST-HSCs (Fig. 2D), which have the propensity to differentiate in a short span of time [29]. On the other hand, they contained a higher percentage of LT-HSCs that defer undergoing differentiation in a short span of time [29] contributing towards their long-term engraftment potential.

Priming MSCs with CoCl2 induces the expression of HSC-supportive genes in them

The significant difference between MVs and Exo isolated from CoCl2-MSCs and hypoxic-MSCs on HSCs cultured with them prompted us to screen CoCl2-MSCs and hypoxic-MSCs for the presence of HSC-supportive factors in them. We observed that vascular endothelial growth factor A (Vegfa), the most common downstream effector of HIF-1α [30], was significantly upregulated in both CoCl2-MSCs and hypoxic-MSCs with the highest expression in the CoCl2-MSCs (Fig. 4A 1 st set of bars). Cxcr4 is another downstream effector gene of HIF-1α [31], whose expression was significantly higher in both CoCl2-MSCs and hypoxic-MSCs than in control-MSCs (Fig. 4A 2nd set of bars). Interestingly, the two crucial HSC-supportive genes, Scf and Jag1, were significantly upregulated in the CoCl2-MSCs compared to control-MSCs and hypoxic-MSCs (Fig. 4A 3rd and 4th set of bars, respectively). This indicates that the CoCl2 treatment induces the expression of HSC-supportive factors in the MSCs.

Priming the MSCs with CoCl2 leads to selective partitioning of HSC-supportive genes in their MVs

We then examined whether the HSC-supportive genes are present in MVs and Exo secreted by the CoCl2-MSCs by performing qRT-PCR analyses of MVs and Exo isolated from control-MSCs, CoCl2-MSCs, and hypoxic-MSCs. We observed that the expression of Cxcr4 and Vegfa was significantly higher in the Co-MVs and Hy-MVs as compared to the Co-Exo and Hy-Exo, respectively (Fig. 4B and C). Interestingly, the expression of Cxcr4 and Vegfa was the highest in the Hy-MVs (Fig. 4B and C; Left-hand set of bars). Furthermore, the expression of Jag1 was strikingly high in the Co-MVs (Fig. 4D), suggesting that treatment of MSCs with CoCl2 leads to the selective partitioning of Jagged-1-specific mRNA into their MVs. Although the expression of Scf was high in CoCl2-MSCs, it could not be detected in their MVs, suggesting that perhaps this mRNA was not partitioned into the MVs in response to CoCl2.

These data suggest that priming of MSCs with CoCl2 or atmospheric hypoxia (1% O2) leads to a selective partitioning of hematopoiesis-supportive factors into their MVs.

To confirm whether Jagged-1 transferred to the HSPCs by the Co-MVs gets translated in them, we immuno-stained the HSPCs cultured with Con-MVs, Co-MVs, and Hy-MVs with an antibody against Jagged-1. We found that the expression of Jagged-1 was significantly higher in the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs compared to those cultured with con-MVs and Hy-MVs (Fig. 4E). This indicates that the Jagged-1 mRNA transferred to the HSPCs via Co-MVs gets effectively translated into protein.

Co-MVs induce the activation of quiescence- and self-renewal-related genes in the HSPCs

Next, we assessed the effect of Co-MVs and Hy-MVs on the gene expression profile of the HSPCs cultured with them. Upon subjecting the harvested cells to qRT-PCR analyses, we observed that the expression of Early growth response 1 (Egr1) [32], was upregulated in the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs, compared to those cultured with con-MVs and Hy-MVs (Fig. 5A, 1 st set of bars). Moreover, the expression of p21, a downstream effector gene of Egr1, was significantly higher in the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs and Hy-MVs than those with con-MVs. The expression of p21 was the highest in the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs (Fig. 5A, 2nd set of bars).

We also assessed the expression of some of the genes related to the self-renewal of HSCs (Fig. 5B). Both Runx1 and Foxo3 are known to play key roles in the HSC self-renewal. Runx1 is expressed in LT-HSCs in mouse embryos and adult HSCs [33]. Foxo3 is crucial for maintaining the HSC pool [34]. We observed that the expression of both Runx1 and Foxo3 was significantly higher in the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs (Fig. 5B, 1 st and 2nd set of bars).

Dnmt1 is crucially required for ensuring robust self-renewal and effective niche retention of HSCs, maintaining their ability to give rise to multilineage hematopoiesis [35]. We observed that there was also an upregulation in the expression of Dnmt1 in the HSPCs cultured with both Co-MVs and Hy-MVs (Fig. 5B, 3rd set of bars). Nfya is known to be responsible for the proliferation of HSCs [36], whose expression was upregulated in the HSPCs cultured with Hy-MVs (Fig. 4B, 4th set of bars).

Consistent with the upregulated expression of Jagged-1 in the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs, the expression of Notch-2, one of the receptors of the Notch signaling pathway (Fig. 5B, 2nd last set of bars) and Hes1, a down-stream target of the Notch signaling (Fig. 5B, last set of bars), was also significantly upregulated in them, as compared to those cultured with con-MVs and Hy-MVs.

Altogether, these data indicate that Co-MVs significantly upregulate the expression of genes related to self-renewal and quiescence in the HSPCs cultured with them by activating the Jagged-1-Notch axis in them.

Co-MVs promote the homing and long-term engraftment ability of HSPCs in vivo

We had observed that the Co-MVs promote the in vitro migration ability of HSPCs towards SDF-1α (Fig. 3B). Hence, we further assessed if the increased in vitro migration ability of HSPCs reflects their in vivo homing ability as well. We hypothesized that perhaps only the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs would exhibit efficient homing in the BM of the recipient mice. Contrary to our expectations, HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs and Hy-MVs showed significantly higher homing to the BM of the recipient mice as compared to the HSPCs cultured with con-MVs (Fig. 6A and B). The data showed that Co-MVs increase both in vitro migration ability as well as the in vivo homing ability of HSPCs. On the other hand, Hy-MVs do not increase the migration ability of HSPCs in vitro but promote their homing ability in vivo. These data indicate the role of some additional chemoattractant in the BM niche in addition to SDF-1α, such as SCF [37, 38], and adhesion molecules such as α6 [39] to which the HSPCs cultured with Hy-MVs responded.

Since Co-MVs supported the expansion of LT-HSCs in vitro and also improved the homing ability of HSPCs in vivo, it was imperative to examine whether the interaction of the HSPCs with Co-MVs promotes their long-term engraftment ability in vivo. To this end, we transplanted an equal number of GFP-HSPCs cultured with various types of MVs (1 × 106 cells/recipient mouse) into Busulfan-treated non-GFP C57BL/6 recipients and monitored the percent chimerism in the PB at 6 weeks and 16 weeks of transplantation. We also assessed the percent engraftment in the BM of the recipient mice at 16 weeks post-transplant.

We observed that at 6 weeks post-transplant, there was no significant difference in the percent chimerism in the PB of recipient mice receiving HSPCs cultured with either Co-MVs or Hy-MVs (Supp. Figure 4A). But at 16 weeks post-transplant, the HSPCs cultured with Hy-MVs showed a significantly higher percentage of donor cells in the recipients’ PB (Fig. 6C). This data is consistent with the presence of a higher percentage of ST-HSCs present in them, which give rise to short-term progenitors. Furthermore, the myeloid and lymphoid lineage cell analysis done on the donor cells present in the PB of recipient mice showed no lineage bias in any of the groups (Fig. 6D and Supp. Figure 4B). However, consistent with the higher number of LT-HSCs present in them, the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs showed significantly higher levels of engraftment in the recipients’ BM than the HSPCs cultured with con-MVs and Hy-MVs at 16 weeks of transplantation (Fig. 6E and Supp. Figure 4C). These data demonstrate that the Co-MVs promote the long-term engraftment ability of HSPCs without causing lineage-bias in them.

Discussion

Since BMM is a relatively hypoxic tissue with 1–6% of O2 levels [40, 41], researchers have been focussing on the role of MSCs cultured under hypoxic conditions in maintaining the HSCs in vitro [42]. Most studies use atmospheric hypoxia to create hypoxic conditions in vitro [43, 44]. However, in a clinical setup maintaining hypoxic conditions in the MSCs till their final infusion in the patients’ body makes the process very cumbersome. Any lapse in the protocol could lead to their exposure to normoxia, partially affecting their regenerative potential. To achieve a persistent, oxygen-independent activation of HIF-1α in the cells, hypoxia mimetic compounds such as CoCl2 are used. This approach is also cost-effective as it does not require high-end incubators or special processing units. Previously, we have reported the differential effect of MSCs cultured under hypoxic conditions induced by atmospheric hypoxia (hypoxic-MSCs) and chemical hypoxia (CoCl2-MSCs) on their HSC-supportive ability [19]. However, at that time, we did not investigate the paracrine mechanisms involved in the effect of these cells. Hence, in this research, we examined the differential effect of MVs and Exo isolated from CoCl2-MSCs and compared their effect with the MVs and Exo isolated from naïve and hypoxic-MSCs.

Interestingly, we found that the total EVs strongly inhibited the in vitro proliferation of HSPCs cultured with them; however, independent culturing of HSPCs with MVs and Exo promoted their expansion, underscoring the differential effect of MVs and Exo on the in vitro proliferation of HSPCs. This suggests that the cargo of MVs and Exo collectively imposes inhibitory effect on the proliferation of HSPCs, but when applied independently to the HSPCs they induce proliferative effects. A detailed study of the cargo of the EVs, MVs and Exo respectively would provide insights for this unique observation. They contain specific molecules, which, when applied independently, produce a salutary effect on the HSCs, but when applied together/simultaneously, they oppose each other’s action [22].

Chemotactic migration and homing of intravenously injected HSCs to the BM microenvironment is the first critical step in a successful HSCT. This process primarily involves the CXCR4/SDF-1α axis [45], though the contribution of other molecules and chemoattractants such as the DEK nuclear protein [46] and adhesion molecules are also being appreciated. Interestingly, we found that in spite of having a comparable percentage of LSK CXCR4+ cells, the HSPCs from MV groups migrated more efficiently towards SDF-1α as compared to their Exo counterparts. Even in the MV groups, the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs showed the highest in vitro migration ability towards SDF-1α. These data show that the mere expression of CXCR4 on the HSPCs does not always translate into their functionality. The data also indicate the presence of additional receptors or guidance molecules such as cadherins, integrins, Ca2+ receptor, and S1P receptor 1 [47, 48] on the HSPCs cultured with MVs. CXCR7 is another mediator of HSC migration through the SDF-1α/CXCR4 axis [28]. Moreover, Robo4 is a known guidance molecule on the HSCs that plays a crucial role in assisting the homing of HSCs in the BM [49]. The role of these molecules in the migration ability of HSPCs cultured with MVs vis-à-vis Exo remains to be elucidated.

When we analyzed the short-term in vivo homing of the cultured HSPCs, we observed that the HSPCs cultured with Hy-MVs exhibited even higher homing ability as compared to those cultured with Co-MVs, despite their lower in vitro migration towards SDF-1α. This result indicates the presence of additional cues in the BM microenvironment to which the HSPCs cultured with Hy-MVs responded to.

However, the HSPCs cultured with Hy-MVs promoted the expansion of ST-HSCs and showed efficient short-term homing highlighting their inability to not undergo efficient long-term engraftment in the BM. Furthermore, the higher percentage of HSPCs cultured with Hy-MVs in the peripheral blood suggests that the efficiently homed population perhaps comprised of ST-HSCs that quickly leave the marrow to give rise to blood cells. These data indicate that the ST-HSCs did not respond to SDF-1α and exhibited poor in vitro migration ability. However, since they showed efficient homing ability in vivo, they perhaps responded to other chemo-attractants such as SCF [37, 38], and adhesion molecules such as α6 [39]. On the other hand, Co-MVs promoted the proliferation of LT-HSCs and exhibited higher migration and long-term engraftment ability in comparison to Hy-MVs. We, therefore, speculate that pre-incubation of hematopoietic cells with Co-MVs may be a better strategy to ensure long-term hematopoiesis post-transplantation. Whether a hybrid graft prepared by mixing the HSPCs independently incubated with Co-MVs and Co-Exo i.e., the transplantation of an equal number of LT-HSCs and ST-HSCs together, would lead to a better outcome in transplantation needs to be examined.

MSC-derived MVs and Exo have been demonstrated to harbor a plethora of macromolecules such as mRNA, miRNA, and proteins [50]. Niazi et al. (2022) have reported enrichment of Jagged-1 in the exosomes derived from hypoxia-preconditioned MSCs, which resulted in the expansion of CD133+ human HSPCs [44]. In their study, they didn’t examine the levels of Jagged-1 in MVs. We found that the levels of Jagged-1 in the Co-MVs are very high as compared to those in the Hy-MVs, indicating that Co-MVs perhaps would be a better choice to expand the human HSPCs as well. Jalnapurkar et al. (2018) observed the selective partitioning of Jagged-1- and VEGF-A- specific mRNAs in the MSC-derived MVs after priming with nitric oxide (NO) donors [26]. A high degree of cross-talk exists between NO and HIF-1α signaling. NO can stabilize HIF1 by protecting it from degradation, and HIF-1α can increase NO production via multiple mechanisms [51]. Thus, it is possible that CoCl2-mediated partitioning of Jagged-1 into the MVs of MSCs also involves the activation of NO signaling. This needs to be formally examined.

Furthermore, we also observed the higher expression of Jagged-1 protein and the significant upregulation of Notch2 and Hes1 in the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs. These data suggest that MVs are the crucial mediators of intercellular communication between CoCl2-MSCs and HSPCs via activation of the Notch signaling pathway. Increased expression of Notch signaling components activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling in the MSCs. These Wnt/β-catenin signals in MSCs aid in enhancing the self-renewal of HSCs by inducing the cross-talk between Notch and Wnt signals in the HSC niche [52, 53]. We speculate that the CoCl2 treatment might also affect the Wnt signaling in MSCs, and the cross-talk between Wnt and Notch Signaling would help in the in vitro expansion and maintenance of HSCs. This aspect needs to be studied in depth at the molecular level.

Furthermore, we observed a significantly high expression of Egr1 and p21 mRNAs in the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs. HSCs exhibit high levels of Egr1, which diminish upon cell division [32], implying that Egr1 is typically expressed in the quiescent HSCs. Egr1 activates p21cip [54], one of the CIP/KIP members family, and it is usually expressed in the adult HSCs rather than their differentiated progenitors and fetal HSCs. Our data suggest that the Co-MVs promote the quiescence of HSCs by upregulating Egr1/p21 axis. This could also be the reason behind the formation of a lower number of colonies by the HSPCs cultured with Co-MVs.

Our preliminary experiments indicated that Exo derived from both CoCl2-MSCs and hypoxic-MSCs enhanced the proliferation of hematopoietic cells in vitro. This suggests that perhaps MVs are responsible for the quiescence of HSCs, whereas Exo stimulate their proliferation and differentiation. Detailed investigation of the cargo present in both MVs and Exo would provide valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms responsible for this differential effect of MVs and Exo in regulating HSCs. Understanding these differential regulatory effects of MVs and Exo would facilitate the ex vivo expansion of HSPCs for targeted therapies in several hematologic disorders as per the need for reconstitution of short-term and long-term hematopoiesis and various hematopoietic lineages.

EVs derived from hypoxic-MSCs have demonstrated efficacy in alleviating various tissue injuries and promoting the ex vivo expansion of HSCs [55, 56]. Our data reveal that the MVs secreted by CoCl2-primed MSCs possess better HSC-supportive ability than those secreted by the hypoxia-primed MSCs. Since using siRNA approach, we have previously established that the HSC-supportive nature of CoCl2-MSCs depends on HIF-1α-mediated signaling [19], we speculate that the observed difference could be attributed to the persistent activation of HIF-mediated signaling in the CoCl2-MSCs as opposed to the transient HIF activation in the hypoxic-MSCs [19]. It is possible that the cargo of Hy-MVs quickly reverts to its naïve state, and therefore, we contemplate that the MVs derived from CoCl2-MSCs could be used as cell-free and cost-effective biologics to expand and maintain the HSCs ex vivo, which, when transplanted in a clinical set-up, would rapidly reconstitute normal hematopoiesis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our data show that MVs isolated from CoCl2-primed MSCs form a cell-free cost-effective biologic for ex vivo expansion of functional HSCs for use in clinical transplantation.
