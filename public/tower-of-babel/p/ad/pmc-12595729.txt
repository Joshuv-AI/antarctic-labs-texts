Glycemic variability and postoperative mortality following cardiac surgery: evidence from a real-world ICU cohort
Authors: Fengwei Yao, Fan Yang, Xiaolan Chen, Lei Liu, Zhijun He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlycemic variability (GV) may capture peri-operative metabolic instability more sensitively than absolute glucose levels, but its prognostic value after major cardiac surgery remains uncertain.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 2,943 ICU patients from the MIMIC-IV database who underwent coronary artery bypass grafting or valve surgery. GV was defined as the coefficient of variation of glucose during ICU stay. Primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality, with 90- and 360-day mortality as secondary endpoints. Missing covariates (< 20%) were multiply imputed with CART. Multivariable Cox regression, restricted cubic splines, and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) were applied. Subgroup analyses with interaction testing were conducted. Discriminatory performance was assessed by ROC curves, and nine machine learning models were benchmarked with LASSO-based feature selection and cross-validation.ResultsHigher GV was consistently associated with elevated mortality risk. In fully adjusted analyses, patients in the highest vs. lowest quartile had hazard ratios of 6.72 (95%CI 1.89–23.93) for 30-day, 2.46 (1.31–4.59) for 90-day, and 2.19 (1.44–3.32) for 360-day mortality. Associations appeared linear, remained robust after IPTW, and were confirmed in sensitivity analyses. No significant interactions were detected across clinical subgroups. GV alone showed fair discrimination for 30-day mortality (AUC 0.686), whereas the XGBoost model incorporating perioperative variables achieved the highest discrimination (AUC 0.845). SHAP analysis indicated GV contributed incremental prognostic information but was not the leading predictor.ConclusionAmong ICU patients undergoing cardiac surgery, greater GV was independently associated with higher short- and long-term mortality. GV may serve as a complementary marker to established clinical predictors for postoperative risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05259-6.Keywords: Glycemic variability, Cardiac surgery, ICU, Mortality, MIMIC-IV

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of global disease burden, with marked regional variations in age-standardized mortality rates, ranging from 73.6 per 100,000 in the high-income Asia Pacific region to 432.3 per 100,000 in Eastern Europe [1, 2]. Among CVD subtypes, coronary atherosclerotic disease and valvular heart disease are the main contributors to mortality, substantially impairing both survival and quality of life. For patients with moderate to severe lesions, cardiac surgery—particularly coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and valve repair or replacement—remains one of the most effective therapeutic options [3, 4].

Despite advances in surgical techniques and improved survival, patients continue to face considerable postoperative risks, especially during the early recovery period. Complications such as acute kidney injury, arrhythmias, delirium, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) remain common and significantly contribute to mortality [5–8]. Metabolic stress and glycemic dysregulation are also prevalent during ICU stays after cardiac surgery, playing a critical role in adverse outcomes [9]. Traditionally, most studies have focused on the impact of stress hyperglycemia on postoperative prognosis [10–13].

Emerging evidence indicates that the extent of glycemic fluctuations may better capture metabolic instability and inflammatory burden than absolute glucose levels, providing greater sensitivity for predicting outcomes [14, 15]. Glycemic variability (GV)—quantified as the coefficient of variation (CV), derived from the ratio of standard deviation to mean glucose—has therefore gained increasing attention. GV is now widely studied in ICU monitoring, postoperative care, and diabetes management [16]. Growing evidence links GV to postoperative complications such as atrial fibrillation and acute kidney injury in cardiac surgery patients, and to prognosis in critically ill and heart failure populations [15, 17–20]. While postoperative GV has been associated with higher one-year mortality after transcatheter aortic valve replacement [14]; its broader prognostic role across major cardiac surgeries, including CABG and valve procedures, remains underexplored.

Given the profound metabolic perturbations induced by cardiopulmonary bypass, sedation, and mechanical ventilation, investigating GV in this population is of significant clinical relevance. This study aims to expand current knowledge on postoperative risk factors by evaluating GV as a prognostic marker across diverse cardiac surgical populations. The findings may support early risk stratification and inform strategies for ICU-based metabolic monitoring and long-term outcome prediction.

Methods

Study population

This study was based on the MIMIC-IV database and included postoperative cardiac surgery patients admitted to the ICU. It aimed to evaluate the association between glycemic variability (GV) and patient outcomes and to explore the potential clinical utility of GV for risk prediction and early intervention. The Laboratory for Computational Physiology at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology created and maintains MIMIC-IV, which includes comprehensive clinical data from intensive care unit admissions at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center [21]. All members of the research team completed the required training and certification for database use (Certification number: 65083321) and adhered to the data usage protocols for information extraction. Patients were selected based on ICU type and diagnosis codes to identify postoperative cardiac surgery cases. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged < 18 years at first admission or those who underwent cardiac surgery before hospital admission or after ICU entry; (2) patients with multiple ICU admissions, retaining only the first ICU stay; and (3) patients with fewer than three blood glucose measurements during ICU stay. A total of 2,943 eligible patients were included in the final analysis.

Glycemic variability was calculated for each patient and initially categorized into quartiles for descriptive and regression analyses. In addition, the optimal threshold of GV (20.4) was identified using X-tile software (version 3.6.1, Yale University) [22], and patients were dichotomized into high GV (≥ 20.4) and low GV (< 20.4) groups for propensity score–based analyses (Fig. 1). The outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality, outcomes included 90-day and 360-day all-cause mortality.

Data collection and processing

Navicat Premium (version 17) was used to retrieve clinical data using a structured query language. ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes were used to identify individuals undergoing cardiac surgery, and retrieval keywords such as “coronary artery bypass grafting,” “valve replacement,” and “valve repair” were used. Potential covariates were collected from the first recorded measurements on the first postoperative day in the ICU, encompassing demographics, vital signs, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, severity scores, and medication use. GV was calculated as: (standard deviation of blood glucose ÷ mean blood glucose) × 100% [23].

Variables with more than 20% missing values were excluded. For variables with less than 20% missing data, multiple imputation was performed using the Classification and Regression Trees (CART) algorithm via the “mice” package in R (version 4.4.1). Five imputed datasets (m = 5) were generated using a fixed random seed (seed = 123) under the assumption of missing at random (MAR). The CART method was selected for its capacity to handle both categorical and continuous variables, as well as non-linear associations, which are frequently encountered in ICU data.

Among the five completed datasets, one was randomly selected (dataset 2) and used for the primary analysis. Finally, each imputed dataset was analyzed separately using Cox proportional hazards regression, with glycemic variability (GV) included as both a continuous predictor and as quartiles. The models were fully adjusted for demographic characteristics, vital signs, laboratory parameters, comorbidities, and therapeutic interventions (Model 4). Estimates from the five datasets were then combined using Rubin’s rules, which appropriately account for both within-imputation and between-imputation variance, to yield pooled hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

To minimize the influence of extreme values on model training, Winsorization was applied to the collected mean and standard deviation of blood glucose using the “DescTools” package in R [24]. Values beyond the 1 st and 99th percentiles were replaced with their respective percentile values, thereby effectively constraining the range of outliers while preserving the overall data distribution.The distribution of raw and Winsorized data was visualized using boxplots, demonstrating that Winsorization effectively mitigated the impact of extreme outliers (see Supplementary Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on the results of normality testing, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test were used to compare non-normally distributed variables, whereas the t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare continuous variables with a normal distribution for between-group comparisons.

The association between glycemic variability (GV) and all-cause mortality was evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression. GV was modeled both as a continuous variable and as quartiles (Q1 as reference), with P-values for linear trend calculated. To reduce potential confounding, candidate covariates were first screened using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. In addition, clinical relevance and prior literature were considered when selecting final adjustment variables, consistent with established epidemiologic principles. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were used to assess multicollinearity, and variables with VIF > 10 were excluded. Four progressively adjusted Cox models were constructed to examine the independent association between GV and mortality outcomes: Model 1 (crude model): unadjusted, assessing the preliminary association between GV and mortality. Model 2: adjusted for demographic characteristics and basic vital signs, including sex, admission age, oxygen saturation (SpO₂), and respiratory rate. Model 3: further adjusted for laboratory parameters based on Model 2, including international normalized ratio (INR), chloride, anion gap, blood glucose, creatinine, and lactate. Model 4: additionally adjusted for comorbidities and intervention variables based on Model 3, including cerebrovascular disease, mild liver disease, use of antiplatelet agents, insulin use, β-blocker use, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and sepsis.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles was used to explore potential nonlinear relationships between GV and mortality. The significance of nonlinearity was tested using the Wald chi-square test. To assess GV’s discriminatory ability for 30-, 90-, and 360-day mortality, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted and area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated. X-tile software (Version 3.6.1, Yale University School of Medicine) was used to determine the optimal GV cutoff for predicting mortality, identified as 20.4 based on the maximum χ² principle derived from the log-rank test [22].

To address residual confounding, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied using propensity scores estimated via a gradient boosting machine (GBM) algorithm. Stabilized weights were computed, and covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMD < 0.1 considered acceptable. Cox regression was re-applied in the weighted sample to assess consistency of associations.

Pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed stratified by age, sex, acute heart failure, diabetes, hypertension, sepsis, and coronary artery disease. Interaction terms were tested using likelihood ratio tests. All analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.4.1).

We developed nine prediction models (logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting machine, XGBoost, LightGBM, AdaBoost, support vector machine, and neural network). To prevent overfitting, we applied LASSO-based feature selection, five-fold cross-validation, and automated hyperparameter tuning. The model with the highest test-set AUC (XGBoost) was retained for further analysis. Model discrimination was assessed using AUC, and SHAP values were calculated to quantify the relative contribution of each predictor, including GV.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 2,943 patients who were admitted to the intensive care unit following cardiac surgery. Patients were categorized into quartiles based on glycemic variability (GV), with comparable group sizes. Significant differences in clinical and laboratory characteristics were observed across the GV quartiles.

Higher GV quartiles were associated with increased heart rate and reduced diastolic blood pressure. Illness severity indicators, including the Charlson Comorbidity Index, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), showed stepwise increases across quartiles, suggesting a trend toward greater physiological derangement with higher GV.

Laboratory parameters also varied by GV level. Patients in the higher GV quartiles had elevated international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), glucose, anion gap, lactate, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine. In contrast, lower levels of hemoglobin and red blood cell count were observed, indicating more frequent anemia in these groups.

Comorbidities such as diabetes, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and sepsis were more common among patients in the upper GV quartiles. Additionally, higher rates of acute kidney injury (AKI) and use of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) were observed.

Regarding outcomes, 30-day, 90-day, and 360-day all-cause mortality rates progressively increased from Q1 to Q4. These trends suggest that patients with higher GV experienced more severe illness and worse outcomes. A detailed summary of baseline characteristics across GV quartiles is presented in Table 1.

Analysis of differences between quartile groups

LASSO regression and multicollinearity control

To assist in the selection of covariates for multivariable modeling and to reduce potential overfitting, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied to all candidate variables, combined with 10-fold cross-validation. LASSO applies L1 regularization, which shrinks regression coefficients toward zero, thereby enabling variable selection in high-dimensional data settings.

The regularization parameter (λ) was selected using the “one-standard-error” rule, which prioritizes a more parsimonious model within one standard error of the minimum cross-validation error to balance model complexity and stability. The optimal λ value was determined to be 0.00472, resulting in the retention of 17 variables for further modeling. The coefficient profiles and cross-validation error curve are presented in Fig. 2. A ranking of variable importance based on their non-zero LASSO coefficients is shown in Supplementary Figure S2. To ensure inclusion of key demographic factors, sex was forcibly retained in the model.To evaluate and reduce potential multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for all included variables. A detailed summary of the VIF results is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Association between GV and all-cause mortality

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted to examine cumulative in-hospital survival across glycemic variability (GV) quartiles (Fig. 3). The curves demonstrated a progressive decline in survival over time, with significant differences among the four groups based on the log-rank test (p < 0.0001). Testing of Schoenfeld residuals indicated no violation of the proportional hazards assumption for all Cox models (global test P > 0.05).

Kaplan-Meier

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the association between GV and all-cause mortality after cardiac surgery (Table 2). When modeled as a categorical variable, higher GV quartiles were associated with increased mortality risk in a stepwise manner. For 30-day mortality, the hazard ratio (HR) for Q4 compared with Q1 was 9.17 (95% CI: 2.78–30.24) in the unadjusted model and 6.72 (95% CI: 1.89–23.93) in the fully adjusted model. Similar associations were observed for longer-term outcomes: HRs for Q4 vs. Q1 were 2.46 (95% CI: 1.31–4.59) for 90-day mortality and 2.19 (95% CI: 1.44–3.32) for 360-day mortality. Trend tests confirmed a significant increasing gradient in risk across GV quartiles (p for trend < 0.001 across all models).

When modeled continuously, GV was positively associated with mortality, although quartile-based modeling yielded more stable estimates. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis indicated a linear relationship between GV and 30-day mortality, with no evidence of nonlinearity (p for nonlinearity = 0.4256), as shown in Fig. 4. Similar patterns were seen for 90- and 360-day mortality. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses showed that GV had fair discriminatory ability for 30-day mortality (AUC = 0.686), with lower AUCs for 90-day (0.627) and 360-day mortality (0.599). Compared with first-day SOFA and SAPS II scores, GV demonstrated comparable or slightly higher discrimination for short-term mortality, though its performance for long-term prediction was lower than that of SAPS II. These results suggest that GV may complement existing risk metrics for postoperative outcome assessment.

Risk stratification and propensity score weighting

X-tile software (version 3.6.1, Yale University) was used to identify a data-driven threshold of glycemic variability (GV) for stratified analysis. As shown in Supplementary Figure S3, panel A displays a χ² heatmap where color intensity represents the separation of survival curves across potential GV cutoffs. Panel B shows the distribution histogram of GV with the identified threshold, while panel C presents Kaplan–Meier survival curves for the dichotomized groups. The corresponding statistical table is provided in panel D. Based on the maximum log-rank χ² statistic, a GV value of 20.4 was selected to dichotomize patients into high (≥ 20.4) and low (< 20.4) GV groups for further analyses.

To further explore the relationship between GV and mortality, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was applied. Propensity scores were estimated using a Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM) algorithm to account for potential non-linear interactions among baseline variables. Stabilized weights were used to reduce variance and improve balance between groups. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMD < 0.1 indicating acceptable balance. As shown in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S3, all covariates except GV components and outcome variables met the balance criteria.

Standardized Mean Differences (SMD) After IPTW

For secondary outcomes, GV ≥ 20.4 was also associated with increased risks of 90-day (HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.00–2.43) and 360-day mortality (HR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.04–1.84), as summarized in Table 3.

Sensitivity analysis

After excluding patients with an ICU stay shorter than 24 h or with fewer than five glucose measurements, 1486 patients remained eligible for sensitivity analysis of 30-day mortality. The results were consistent with the main findings (Supplementary Table S4). Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), patients in Q4 had significantly higher 30-day mortality risk across fully adjusted models. In Model 4, the hazard ratio (HR) for Q4 was 4.30 (95% CI: 1.19–15.58, P = 0.026), while Q3 showed a borderline association (HR 3.52, 95% CI: 0.99–12.51, P = 0.052). When modeled continuously, GV remained significantly associated with 30-day mortality (HR 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02–1.07, P < 0.001), and the trend test confirmed a positive dose–response relationship (HR 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.09, P = 0.014).

Subgroup analyses and interaction effects

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of the association between glycemic variability (GV) and postoperative mortality across various clinical subpopulations. The 30-day mortality results are presented in Fig. 6. Higher GV was associated with increased 30-day mortality among male patients, those aged ≥ 65 years, and patients with heart failure, coronary artery disease (CAD), or sepsis. Similar associations were also observed in subgroups without cerebrovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, or hypertension. In contrast, no clear association was found among female patients or among those with diabetes, hypertension, or without sepsis.

30-day mortality subgroup analysis

For 90-day mortality (Supplementary Figure S4) and 360-day mortality (Supplementary Figure S5), similar trends were observed. Higher GV was associated with increased long-term mortality among. However, given the retrospective observational design, these findings are descriptive and should be interpreted with caution. Formal statistical tests of interaction were performed for all subgroups. None of the interaction terms reached statistical significance (all p for interaction > 0.05), indicating no robust evidence of effect modification. Therefore, although effect sizes varied numerically across strata, the overall effect estimate should be considered the most reliable summary of the relationship between GV and postoperative mortality.

Construction of machine learning prediction models

Using the variables retained by LASSO regression (admission age, respiratory rate, SpO₂, cerebrovascular disease, mild liver disease, antiplatelet use, insulin use, β-blocker use, CRRT, sepsis, INR, chloride, anion gap, glucose, creatinine, lactate, and GV), we developed nine machine learning models. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%). In the testing set, the discriminatory performance of the models was as follows (Fig. 7): XGBoost achieved the highest AUC of 0.845 (95% CI: 0.740–0.950), followed closely by logistic regression (AUC 0.841, 95% CI: 0.742–0.939), AdaBoost (AUC 0.825, 95% CI: 0.719–0.932), and neural network (AUC 0.819, 95% CI: 0.710–0.929). Moderate performance was observed for GBM (AUC 0.811, 95% CI: 0.667–0.955) and random forest (AUC 0.745, 95% CI: 0.596–0.894), while SVM (AUC 0.732, 95% CI: 0.581–0.883) and KNN (AUC 0.673, 95% CI: 0.556–0.791) demonstrated lower discrimination. LightGBM performed poorly (AUC 0.444, 95% CI: 0.269–0.618). Among these, XGBoost achieved the highest discrimination and was therefore selected as the optimal model for further interpretation. By contrast, GV alone showed only fair predictive ability (AUC = 0.686). For the XGBoost model, ROC curves, and decision curve analyses (DCA) confirmed good discrimination, and net clinical benefit in both training and testing sets (Fig. 7). SHAP analysis (Fig. 8) demonstrated that the most influential predictors were anion gap, glucose, INR, chloride, and lactate. GV ranked among the top predictors, though not the leading one, confirming that it provided incremental prognostic information when combined with other perioperative risk factors.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study of 2,943 ICU patients undergoing cardiac surgery found that higher glycemic variability (GV) was consistently associated with increased risks of 30-, 90-, and 360-day all-cause mortality. The association remained robust across Cox models, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses, and after adjustment using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Subgroup analyses further confirmed the consistency of this relationship across clinical strata. Although subgroup analyses suggested that the association between GV and mortality appeared stronger in certain populations (e.g., males, patients with hypertension, younger patients), formal tests of interaction were not statistically significant. This indicates that the apparent differences may reflect random variation rather than true effect modification.

GV reflects not only blood glucose levels but also metabolic stability under stress conditions [16]. Rapid glucose fluctuations can induce oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, activation of inflammatory pathways, and immune dysregulation, leading to impaired tissue perfusion and organ recovery, thereby increasing mortality risk [25, 26]. Additionally, large glucose swings can trigger the so-called “metabolic memory” phenomenon, where intracellular vascular stress mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) persists even after glucose normalization [27]. Unlike single mean glucose or HbA1c values, GV provides a more sensitive marker of perioperative metabolic instability, especially during the early postoperative period when inflammatory responses, catecholamine surges, and insulin resistance are common. Elevated GV may promote the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, damage vascular endothelium, impair immune responses, and increase the risk of infections and cardiovascular complications [28]. Moreover, high glycemic variability may predispose patients to hypoglycemic episodes, leading to cerebral ischemia, arrhythmias, and other severe complications. These mechanisms collectively explain the close association between GV and postoperative mortality.

Previous studies have validated GV as a prognostic marker in diabetes, sepsis, and critically ill populations [19, 20, 28, 29]. For instance, Qingyun Yu et al. found that GV was closely associated with both short- and long-term mortality in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement [14]. Similarly, Kathleen C. Clement reported that GV was related to major adverse events following coronary artery bypass grafting [30], and Siyu Kong demonstrated that GV could predict persistent acute kidney injury in non-cardiac surgical patients [31]. Given the complexity of surgical trauma, cardiopulmonary bypass, and metabolic disturbances after cardiac surgery, GV holds substantial clinical significance in this population.

In the clinical context, it is important to emphasize that GV should not be regarded as a direct therapeutic target in isolation, but rather as a supplementary tool for risk stratification. Critically ill patients often experience labile physiological changes due to vasoactive medication, respiratory compromise, pain, and stress responses, all of which may influence glycemic variability. Therefore, while GV captures an aspect of metabolic instability, its primary clinical utility lies in complementing existing prognostic indicators such as SOFA or SAPS II scores. Patients identified with higher GV could be prioritized for closer glucose monitoring, including the potential use of continuous glucose monitoring systems, to allow more timely detection of dysglycemia. Thus, the predictive value of GV may assist in identifying high-risk patients and guiding the intensity of surveillance, but it should be interpreted as an adjunctive marker rather than a standalone treatment target.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the retrospective and single-center nature of the study limits the generalizability of findings. The data were derived from a single academic center in the United States, which may not reflect clinical practices, patient characteristics, or ICU protocols in other healthcare settings or countries. Second, GV was calculated using glucose values recorded during ICU stay, which may be affected by variability in testing frequency, measurement methods, and clinician-driven decisions. Third, although LASSO feature selection, cross-validation, and hyperparameter tuning were applied to reduce overfitting, the possibility of residual overfitting cannot be excluded. Furthermore, the absence of external validation limits the generalizability of our findings. Finally, detailed perioperative variables—such as nutritional strategies, insulin administration protocols, and individualized glycemic targets—were not available, limiting adjustment for potential confounding factors.

Future studies should aim to validate these findings in diverse, multi-center cohorts to enhance generalizability across different healthcare systems. Prospective studies with standardized glucose monitoring protocols are needed to better characterize the temporal dynamics of glycemic variability and their causal role in postoperative outcomes. Incorporating detailed perioperative data—such as nutritional intake, insulin administration, and inflammatory markers—may help uncover mechanisms linking GV to mortality. In addition, this study focused on mortality as the primary endpoint, given its clinical importance and reliability in the database. Other postoperative complications, such as acute kidney injury, atrial fibrillation, and infections, could provide further mechanistic insights into how glycemic variability contributes to adverse outcomes. However, these endpoints were not analyzed here due to data heterogeneity and the scope of the present study. Future studies incorporating a broader spectrum of postoperative complications are warranted to clarify the pathophysiological pathways linking glycemic variability to outcomes in surgical ICU patients.

Conclusion

In this large retrospective cohort of ICU patients undergoing cardiac surgery, higher glycemic variability was significantly associated with increased short- and long-term mortality. These findings underscore the importance of metabolic stability in surgical recovery.
