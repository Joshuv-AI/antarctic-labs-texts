A phase I trial of combination CAR-NK92MI immunotherapy by dual targeting MUC-1 and PD-L1 for patients with relapsed or refractory solid tumors: focus on non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Jing Lin, Wenchao Xu, Leiming Xia, Congshu Zhang, Lei Ge, Junfeng Gao, Jian Bao, Yang Xu, Xiang Sun, Hongxia Li, Yi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNK92MI cells are well-defined natural killer (NK) cell lines for tumor immunotherapy. In this study, we investigated a combination therapy of MUC-1 and PD-L1 targeted chimeric antigen receptor NK92MI (CAR-NK92MI) cells for advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).Materials and methodsFrom 2016 to 2018, 7 patients with relapsed or refractory NSCLC were included in this clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT02839954.Registered in 21/07/2016.). 1:1 mixed MUC-1 and PD-L1 CAR-NK92MI cells were infused, and the dosage was set at 1 × 108 to 2 × 109 CAR-NK92MI cells once. The infusion times were ranged from 3 to 37, and the median time were 22. All patients completed CAR-NK92MI cells infusion within 1 year unless intolerable toxicities appeared or disease progression (PD) occurred.ResultsAll 7 patients were well-tolerated in the treatment. No cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and serious adverse events related to CAR-NK92MI treatment were observed. 3/7 patients achieved stable disease within more than 23 months. The median progression-free survival (PFS) was 12 months (2 to 60months) from the initial treatment, and the median overall survival (OS) was 19 months (3 to 60 months).ConclusionOur results demonstrated that the combined MUC-1 and PD-L1 targeted CAR-NK92MI cell therapy is safe and efficient approach for relapsed and refractory NSCLC patients with metastasis.Trial registrationOur study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (https://classic.clinicaltrials.gov/) (NCT02839954) in 21/07/2016.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03944-y.

Introduction

Tumors have become a serious problem affecting human health. Solid tumors are cancers that occur in solid organs or tissues, usually being invasive and metastatic, and are one of the main causes of cancer deaths [1]. According to the 2022 Global Cancer Statistics, lung cancer, female breast cancer and colorectal cancer are the three types of solid tumors with relatively high incidence rates, imposing a huge burden on the global public health system and medical resources. Current treatment options for solid tumors include surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, immunotherapy and targeted therapy [2, 3]. However, there are significant differences in treatment responses among different patients. Targeted therapy is not suitable for all patients with malignant tumors. The immunosuppressive agents targeting the programmed death-1 (PD-1) pathway, which have gained popularity in the past five years, have shown relatively significant anti-tumor effects in various solid tumors, especially in malignant melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, and non-small cell lung cancer. However, for recurrent and refractory solid tumors (ovarian cancer, pancreatic cancer, cholangiocarcinoma, metastatic peritoneal cancer, metastatic liver cancer, mesothelioma, soft tissue sarcoma), due to the heavy tumor burden in the body, the anti-tumor immune microenvironment has changed, and the body is unable to initiate an effective tumor immune response, thus being insensitive to single immunotherapy. Therefore, the treatment of recurrent and refractory solid tumors has always been a major challenge in the field of tumor treatment. At present, cell therapy has been regarded as a research focus in the field of anti-tumor treatment both domestically and internationally. On November 29, 2022, three series of reviews were published online in The Lancet Haematology, describing the development of cell therapy technology in China [4–6]. Hu et al. pointed out that in addition to targeting B-cell malignancies, China’s CAR-T cell therapy is also actively exploring the application of CAR-T cell clinical trials in the treatment of solid tumors such as liver cancer, pancreatic cancer and brain cancer [5]. Therefore, we explored the efficacy of CAR-NK92MI cell therapy for patients with recurrent or refractory solid tumors. However, considering factors such as patient compliance, follow-up time, and sample size, this preliminary analysis focused on NSCLC patients to ensure scientific rigor.

Lung cancer is the second most common type of cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, with an estimated 1.8 million deaths (18%)in 2020 [7]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 80–85% of all lung cancers [7, 8], with an estimated a 5-year survival rate of only 19% [9, 10]. Immunotherapy, alone or in combination with conventional treatments, can significantly improve outcomes of patients with lung cancer. Recently, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)/anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have been introduced as the first-line treatment for NSCLC patients harboring sensitizing activating mutations. Moreover, programmed cell death protein 1/programmed cell death Ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen-4 (CTLA-4) monoclonal antibodies have also been used in the treatment of NSCLC. Yet, recent clinical trials of immunological checkpoint blockages (ICBs) have found that partial patients derive clinical benefits from ICBs therapies [11, 12]. Furthermore, it has been observed that some patients relapse after a period of response [13].ICBs were limited to enhance the efficacy due to acquired resistance, low infiltrating of tumor tissue, unfavorable immunologic balance and failed to immunocytes expansion [14–17].Yet, these above limitations can be compensated by chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-modified immunotherapy [18, 19].

CAR-T cells targeting various tumor-related antigens (CD19, CD33, CD138, etc.) have shown good response efficiency and effectiveness in hematologic malignancies [20]. However, CAR-based immunotherapy does not seem to be sufficient for solid tumor therapies, considering the antigen loss variants, varying degrees of intratumoral heterogeneity, and inhibitory TME [21]. In CAR-T therapy, CAR-T manufacturing often fails due to functionally defective T cells derived from patient- or failure to effector T cells expansion [22]. In addition, severe adverse events related to CAR-T therapy, such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and tumor lysis syndrome (TLS), have been observed [23]. NK cells performed non-specific surveillance and killing effect which could be remolded to enhance the specific anti-tumor capability by antigen targeting CARs transfection [24].

MUCIN-1 (MUC-1) is a recently discovered tumor-associated antigen [25] that has a significant role in protecting epithelial cells, mediating signal transduction, and regulating immunity [26]. MUC-1 overexpressed in > 90% of NSCLC cases, characterized by tumor-specific structural alterations such as hypoglycosylation and loss of polarizable glycosylation sites [27, 28]. These modifications expose epitopes that enhance CAR-NK cell recognition while minimizing on-target/off-tumor toxicity. Mechanistically, MUC-1 promotes tumor progression by disrupting E-cadherin-mediated cell adhesion, activating β-catenin signaling, and suppressing antitumor immunity through Treg expansion and dendritic cell inhibition [27, 28]. Its dual role as both a driver of oncogenesis and an immunosuppressive mediator establishes MUC-1 as a therapeutically actionable target for NSCLC. Clinical studies further support its specificity, as structural changes in malignant cells distinguish tumor-associated MUC-1 from normal epithelial isoforms, providing a critical rationale for prioritizing MUC-1 over conventional targets like EGFR or HER2 in CAR-based immunotherapy. However, in addition to choosing a safer target, adoptive cell therapy often forms a TME that is unfavorable to immune cells due to the expression of PD-L1 [29]. The initial design principle of PD-1/PD-L1 pathway inhibitors is mainly to relieve the depletion of T lymphocytes by the TME and improve the anti-tumor effect of T cells. However, numerous clinical trials have shown that the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway is also involved in NK cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity [30, 31]. Therefore, it may be beneficial to improve the curative effect by targeting tumor-associated antigens while blocking PD-L1 to break the inhibitory immune microenvironment.

Here, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of MUC-1 and PD-L1 modified CAR-NK92MI cells for relapsed and refractory NSCLC patients, in order to explore the novel combination immunotherapy strategy.

Materials and methods

Study design

The trial was initially designed to evaluate the safety and feasibility of infusing CAR-NK92MI-cells in patients with relapsed or refractory solid tumor approved by The Third Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (Anhui, China). The ClinicalTrials.gov number is NCT 02839954. This preliminary analysis focuses on NSCLC patients to ensure scientific rigor. We define relapsed or refractory solid tumors as those that have undergone standard treatment based on guidelines and have failed in the second-line treatment as per the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN).

All participants were informed of the trial procedure, adverse effects, and possible risks of the therapy. They voluntarily provided written informed consent to participate in the study. Patients received multiple rounds of CAR-NK cell infusions. As this novel technology was recently developed in our department, an initial dose-escalation protocol was implemented for the first infusion cycle to enhance safety: 10% of the total cells were infused on Day 0, 30% on Day 2, and 60% on Day 4, constituting one complete cycle. For individual patients with suboptimal compliance, the cycle structure was adjusted from three infusions (Days 0/2/4) to two infusions (Days 0/2) per protocol amendment. Based on clinical experience, the initial infusion dose was modified for some patients, resulting in cell Quantities ranging from 1× 10⁸ to 2 × 10⁹ per infusion. All infusions were administered within a 1-year timeframe unless discontinued due to intolerable toxicities or disease progression (PD). The criteria for early termination are defined as radiological progression (PD) as per RECIST 1.1 or ≥ grade 3 intolerable toxicity as per CTCAE v4.0.

The primary endpoint was to determine the feasibility and safety of the cell treatments. The cells were transported to the ward in cryopreserved chambers, where they were cooled and returned to the patient. MUC-1/PD-L1 CAR-NK92MI cells were rapidly and intravenously injected at the rate of 10-20mL/min, which was completed in approximately 15 min. Before CAR-NK92MI infusion, patients were started on hydration with normal saline at 500mL for 2 h, and were pre-medicated with cimetidine 400 mg and one tablet of celecoxib in advance to prevent allergy and fever half an hour before infusion.

The infusion was monitored by an electrocardiogram (ECG) to detect vital signs. Changes in body temperature were detected during the infusion, and the body temperature was re-measured 1 h after the infusion. The corresponding test indicators (including blood routine, liver and kidney function, electrolyte, cytokine, etc.) were analyzed 2 h after the infusion. The second scheduled infusion of CAR-NK92MI cells was given at least 24 h later only if the first infusion was well tolerated and without adverse events grade > 2. The severity of adverse events was graded using the National Cancer Institute CTCAE v4.0. (NCI-CTCAE 4.0; https://dctd.cancer.gov/research/ctep-trials/for-sites/adverse-events/ctcae-v4.pdf).

Patient eligibility

As part of the informed consent, patients with solid tumor were asked for permission to test their tumor for MUC-1 and PD-L1 expression, which was one of the eligibility criteria. The surgically excised specimens and biopsy specimens of the primary lesion were pretreated, and eligible patients were diagnosed with PD-L1 and MUC-1 via pathological examinations. The eligible diseases include malignant glioma, colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, non-small cell lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, and triple-negative basal-like breast cancer. Inclusion criteria were: all patients with a positive expression rate of PD-L1 on IHC ≥ 1% (defined as a product of % tumor cells staining positive for PD-L1 on IHC), and positive expression rate of MUC-1 on IHC ≥ 10%; age between 18 and 75 years old, and must have a life expectancy > 12 weeks; Eastern cooperative oncology group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2 or karnofsky performance status (KPS) score is higher than 60;Presence of measurable disease by RECIST. adequate hematologic function (white blood count ≥ 2.5 × 109/L; platelet count ≥ 50 × 109/L; hemoglobin ≥ 90 g/L); an adequate renal function defined as serum creatinine < 2 times the upper limit of normal; an adequate hepatic function defined as AST and ALT ≤ 2.5 times the upper limit of normal (liver metastases ≤ 5 times upper limit of normal). Exclusion criteria were: acute or chronic infections, relevant concomitant hematological, cardiovascular, pulmonary, hepatic, renal, pancreatic, or endocrinal disease, with immune deficiency, autoimmune diseases, severe allergic disorders, or exposure to cell-based therapy in the preceding 3 months. Those receiving systemic steroid therapy or those who were pregnant or lactating were also excluded.

Cells and cell culture conditions

The NK-92MI cells used in this study were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA). NK-92MI cells were incubated in an alpha modification of Eagle’s minimum essential medium (MEM-α, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) containing 2mM l-glutamine and 1.5 g L-1 sodium bicarbonate. All cells were cultured in the presence of 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD) and 100U/mL penicillin (Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD) in a Humidified atmosphere containing 5%CO2/95% air at 37 °C. The inoculation density was 3–10 × 105/mL according to cell growth. Cell passage was performed when the cell density was > 1 × 106/mL. Three days before the end of cell preparation, 10 ml cells were sampled and submitted for Incoming Quality Control (QC) testing.

Construction and transfection of CAR-NK92MI

Signal peptide sequence, MUC-1 scFv sequence, human IgG4 Fc sequence, IgD extracellular region sequence, CD28 sequence, 41-BB sequence, and CD3zeta sequence were successively tandem detected and sent to the gene company (Suzhou Hongxun Biotechnologies CO., LTD, Suzhou, CHN) for gene synthesis. Then, they were subcloned into a pcDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-PURO lentiviral vector (Soochow University, Suzhou, CHN) using XbaI/NotI enzymes. The plasmid structure was MUC-1-CAR-pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro. Similarly, signal peptide sequence, PD-1 extracellular region sequence, human IgG4 Fc sequence, 41-BB sequence, and CD3zeta sequence were sequentially connected. The next steps were the same as the ones described above to obtain of PD-L1-CAR-pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puroplasmid structure. Then, 1 × 106 NK92MI cells were centrifuged to remove supernatant and mixed with concentrated MUC-1-CAR lentivirus (with MOI = 30.1) and cultured in Petri dishes at 37℃ for 4–6 hours. Flow cytometry was used to monitor the efficacy of transfection after culture for another 72 h. A stable MUC-1-CAR-NK92MI cell line was achieved by fluorescence-activated cell sorting with an APC-labeled anti-human Fc antibody (109-605-008, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, Inc, Pennsylvania, USA) followed by puromycin selection. PD-L1-CAR-NK92MI cells were produced in a similar way.

Amplification and phenotype identification of CAR-NK92MI

In order to detect the expression of MUC-1-CAR and PD-L1-CAR on the surface of NK-92MI cells, PD-L1/MUC-1 CAR-NK cells were stained with CD56-APC (341027, Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), CD3-FITC (ab34275, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and FC-PE (ab98596, Abcam), mixed with 7. ADD dye and analyzed by flow cytometry (ACEA Biosciences Inc., Hangzhou, CHN). 7-AAD-CD3−CD56+ cell ratio was using for CAR-NK purity, and 7-AAD-Fc+ cells was definded as CAR+ ratio. Flow cytometry was used to control the quality of cell products.

For cell products, CAR+ ratio, T cell proportion (CD3+), NK cell proportion (CD56+), NKT cell proportion (CD3+CD56+), and PD-1 and MUC-1 on the surface of NK cells were measured the day before CAR-NK infusion. To ensure transfection, we also applied CAR+% assay 3 days before the end of cell preparation. After the medium was removed by centrifugation at room temperature, the NK92 cells were collected and washed with reinfusion solution, resuspended, and transferred into the reinfusion bag. The 60 Co γ-ray generator (Soochow University, Suzhou, CHN) was irradiated at room temperature with a dose of 10 Gy and an irradiation time of 20 min. So far, the cells used for cell reinfusion have been preliminarily obtained.

Selective killing effect in vitro

A 7-AAD/CFSE Cell-Mediated Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (ABN1293, Abnova, CHN) was used to detect the cytotoxicity effect-target ratio of PD-L1-CAR-NK-92MI cells to PD-L1-293T cells: 1:1,5:1, and 7.5:1, respectively. 293 T cells were the target cells of the negative control. Briefly, 1 × 106/100µl PD-L1-293T cells were aliquoted into FACS tubes. Then, 10 µl fluorescein-based dye CFSE was added to each tube and placed in a 37℃ incubator (DK-8B, Shanghai Jing Hong Laboratory Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, CHN) for 10 min. The cells were washed twice and transferred to 24-well plates. After counting and suspension in 500 µl MEM-α medium, effector cells were added into 24-well plates containing PDL1-293T cells according to the effector target ratio. After incubation for 4 h, the cells were collected into EP tubes with 1 µl 7-AAD. The death rate of tumor cells (CFSE positive cell population) was measured by FACSCalibur HG flow cytometer (Becton, Dickinson, and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and analyzed using Cell-Quest software (BD Bioscience). The same method was used to detect the cytotoxicity of MUC-1-CAR-NK92MI cells to T47D cells. The effect-target ratios were 5:1, 15:1, and 293 T cells were the negative control target cells.FlowJo 7.6 software was used for flow cytometry analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The surgically excised specimens and biopsy specimens of the primary lesion were harvested, fixed, and embedded in paraffin. Section (3-mm thickness) were cut from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor blocks and routinely deparaffinized and rehydrated, after which an antigen was retrieved. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies (anti-PD-L1 and anti-MUC-1) at 4℃overnight and then with secondary antibodies to IgG-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) for 2 h at room temperature. Next, diaminobenzidine (DAB) plus counterstaining with hematoxylin was performed.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

In brief, 5 ml of patients’ venous blood was collected, centrifuged, and stored in a refrigerator at −80℃for determination. The levels of cytokines (IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, IFN-r, TNF-α, and granzyme-B) were determined using specific ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentrations of cytokines were calculated based on the standard curve. ELISA kits included IL-2 (ab100566, Abcam UK), IL-6 (ab178013, Abcam UK), IL-10 (ab185986, Abcam UK), IFN-r (ab236895, Abcam UK), TNF-α (ab181421, Abcam UK), and granzyme-B (ab461142, Abcam UK).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 6 software was applied to process the data, and the results were expressed as mean plus standard deviation.T-test was used for inter-group comparison, and univariate or multivariate analysis of variance was used for multi-group comparison. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of CAR-NK92MI cells

The fluorescent-activated cell sorting (FACs) results showed that NK92MI cells accounted for more than 99% of all cells. CAR-NK92MI cells successfully transfected with PD-L1-CAR and MUC-1-CAR accounted for approximately 50.68 ± 2.91% and 20.35 ± 5.44% of the total cell number, respectively(Fig. 2C). The results suggested that PD-L1-CAR and MUC-1-CAR can be successfully transfected into NK92MI cells obtaining stable expression. Also, transfected cells could also be stably expanded under certain culture conditions.

In order to further explore the cytotoxicity of two different CAR-NK92MI cells, we conducted an in vitro cytotoxic assay. Firstly, MUC-1-CAR-NK cells were cultured against T47D cell lines (MUC-I-positive tumor cell lines) at different target ratios (15:1 and 5:1), while MUC-I-negative 293 T cells were used as the negative control group. The killing efficiency of 293 T cell lines and T47D cell Lines were 16.58 ± 3.93% and 39.76 ± 1.71% at 15:1 E/T ratio, 11.46 ± 1.33 and 26.87 ± 1.64 at 5:1 E/T ratio, respectively. The cell lytic effect of MUC-1-CAR-NK cells against T47D was significantly higher than that of 293 T cells (P < 0.05), suggesting that MUC-1-CAR-NK had selective killing effects on MUC-1 positive cells.

Meanwhile, the killing results in vitro of PD-L1-CAR-NK cells against PD-L1 positive 293 T at different effective target ratios also suggested that PD-L1-CAR-NK can selectively target PD-L1 positive cells. The killing efficiency of 293 T cell lines and PD-L1-293T cell Lines were 16.26 ± 1.28% and 33.48 ± 2.54% at 7.5:1 E/T ratio, 13.88 ± 0.88 and 24.04 ± 1.62 at 5:1 E/T ratio, 1.36 ± 0.69 and 7.37 ± 0.31 at 1:1 E/T ratio, respectively. The results showed that the killing capacity of NK92 cells expressing PD-L1-CAR or MUC-1-CAR was significantly enhanced compared with unmodified NK92 cells (P < 0.05), with target-specific cytotoxicity. The specific results are shown in Fig. 2D.

CAR-NK92MI cell administration

A total of 55 cycles (128 infusions) of a mixed CAR-NK92MI cells (CAR-NK92MI) composed of 50% of MUC-1 CAR-NK92MI cells and 50% of PD-L1 CAR-NK92MI cells were given within 1 year; 2 patients received 13 cycles, 1 patient received 11 cycles, and 4 patients were administered with no more than 10 cycles. Cells in the final products were above 90% viable before infusion.

Patient characteristics

Seven relapsed and refractory NSCLC patients were enrolled for clinical trials of CAR-NK92MI therapy. All patients had measurable lesions and previously failed the second-line treatment following the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. Patient clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 2. The median age was 60 years (range, 39 to 65); 4 patients were male and 3 were female. The median time from infusion to data cutoff was 9 months (range, 0.2 to 16). No patient had a Karnofsky score (KPS) < 70, and all patients were diagnosed with adenocarcinoma according to the pathology results. All patients had stage IV disease, and the expected survival was > 3 months. All patients completed their anti-tumor therapies (e.g., chemotherapy) more than 3 weeks before enrollment. Immunohistochemical detection of the expression of MUC-1 and PD-L1 in tumor cells of all patients was consistent with the inclusion criteria. Among 7 NSCLC patients, 6 were EGFR-positive, and the genetic results of 1 patient were unclear. The specific treatment plan of the patients before enrollment is shown in Table 2.

Adverse events experienced from CAR-NK92MI cell therapy

We evaluated adverse events in all patients according to the National Cancer Institution Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v4.0. No serious adverse event (grade 3 or higher) was observed(Table 3). Fever and Shivering were the most common events after cell infusion; 7 patients developed fever and 5 had Shivering. The incidence rates were 5.47% and 3.91%, respectively. Patient 6 had a fever after 12th, 17th, 18th, 22nd, and 26th cell infusions that peaked at 39℃. The body temperature Quickly returned to normal after celecoxib treatment. Other patients tolerated the treatment well; yet, patient 1 and patient 4 developed fever of 37.5℃ and 37.8℃ within an hour after 3rd infusion. The fever only lasted for a few hours after physical cooling. More than half of these febrile patients had varying degrees of Shivers. Higher fever and Shivering incidence rates were associated with a temporary higher peak level of serum C-reactive protein. There was no difference in leucocyte neutrophil and hemoglobin levels, and platelet amount, before and after infusion. Only patient 1 experienced transient leukopenia after the 5th infusion. Observation and follow-up were given because leukocyte reduction did not meet the criteria of drug therapy. The blood routine returned to normal three days later.

Cytokines levels

Cytokines were detected by ELISA before and 2 h after each cell infusion. During the reinfusion period, the up-regulation of granzyme B was significant (P < 0.05), while the levels of tumor necrosis factor and interferon did not significantly change (P > 0.05). The inflammatory cytokine IL-6 was significantly upregulated during transfusion, accompanied by the upregulation of IL-10, which may be related to the immune homeostasis of the body. No significant changes were observed in IL-2 (P > 0.05). The specific cytokine profile of each patient is shown in Figure S2.

Discussion

We investigated the safety and efficacy of dual-target CAR-NK92MI cells co-expressing MUC-1 and PD-L1 chimeric antigen receptors in seven patients with relapsed/refractory NSCLC. Three patients (42.9%) maintained stable disease for over 23 months, with two demonstrating exceptional overall survival durations of 53 and 60 months at September 2021 follow-up. Treatment-related adverse events were Limited to grade 1 fever and chills, with no instances of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) or graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) observed. Our Phase I trial (NCT02839954) initially enrolled patients with various solid tumors, including breast cancer (n = 2), gastric cancer (n = 1), and colorectal cancer (n = 2). However, the NSCLC cohort demonstrated superior clinical value, attributable to its largest patient proportion, significantly higher treatment compliance, and longer-term post-treatment follow-up.Therefore, this preliminary analysis focuses on NSCLC patients to ensure scientific rigor. We plan to report outcomes from other tumor types in subsequent publications as data mature.

Unlike CAR-T limited by PD-1/PD-L1 interactions, NK-92MI lacks inhibitory receptors to overcome immunosuppression in solid tumors, enabling scalable “off-the-shelf” allogeneic production without GvHD [32]. However, NK-92MI inability to mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) due to the absence of CD16 expression [33]. CAR-NK92MI mediates concurrent tumor recognition through engineered CAR constructs and non-specific killing via natural cytotoxicity receptors [34], thereby reducing risks of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and long-term toxicity compared to CAR-T therapies [35–37]. Key challenges in lung cancer CAR therapy involve identifying tumor-specific antigens (e.g.,EGFR, MSLN, MUC1, PSCA, CEA, PD-L1, CD80/CD86, ROR1, and HER2) [38, 39]. Our analysis of clinical lung cancer specimens revealed universal MUC1 expression (IHC positivity ≥ 10%) versus negligible expression in normal tissues [40, 41], aligning with ongoing trials targeting MUC1 in advanced NSCLC (NCT03501056). Unlike ADCs limited by off-target toxicity and linker instability, CAR therapies enable precise antigen engagement. To counteract PD-L1-mediated immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment, we developed bispecific MUC1/PD-L1-targeted CAR-NK92MI for relapsed/refractory NSCLC.

No severe treatment-related adverse events (e.g., CRS, neurotoxicity, or hematologic toxicity) were observed. This safety profile is considered to be related to NK cells’ distinct cytokine profile, shorter lifespan, and lower expansion rate—properties that collectively mitigate long-term toxicity risks [35–37].Transient grade 1 fever/chills and self-limiting IL-6/IL-10 elevation occurred post-infusion, consistent with immune activation rather than CRS-associated IL-6 hyperinflammation [42–44]. Elevated basal levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ) were detected, which were considered to be related to the chronic systemic inflammation and persistent immune activation of the tumor.Inflammatory cytokines remained at baseline levels, aligning with prior CAR-T safety reports [44]. This may be related to the short survival time of NK92MI cells, the baseline plateau period, and the tumor microenvironment, although previous reports have indicated that NK-92 cells can trigger the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (including IFN-γand GM-CSF) [35]. Robust granzyme B secretion correlated with tumor cell elimination, confirming CAR-NK92MI’s direct cytotoxic mechanism. Notably, CAR-T-associated neurotoxicity (potentially IL-1-dependent [45, 46]) was absent, though IL-1 levels were not quantified. In fact, IL- is mainly secreted by monocytes/macrophages, while NK cells rarely produce it [47].

Dose optimization remains challenging given technical variability in CAR design, cell activation protocols, and interpatient heterogeneity (age, disease stage, immune status). While higher CAR-T doses correlate with improved objective response rates (ORR) and PFS through prolonged cellular persistence [48, 49], CAR-NK regimens require personalized adaptation balancing tumor kinetics and treatment frequency. CAR-NK cell dosing strategies differ fundamentally from CAR-T approaches, where infusion ranges typically span 106–109 cells/kg [48, 50–52]. In this trial, CAR-NK92MI doses (1 × 108–2 × 109 cells) employed a dose-escalation protocol guided by preclinical data and individual tumor burden.Three patients (P-5, P-6, P-7) receiving 9–13 CAR-NK92MI infusions achieved progression-free survival (PFS) durations of 12, 23, and 25 months, respectively. An additional patient (P-2) maintained stable disease (SD) after 13 infusions as of December 2021 follow-up. This contrasts with prior CAR-NK studies limited to short-term or localized administration [53]. Early studies demonstrated that the cytotoxicity of the NK-92 cell Line declines after 24h and undergoes cell death within 72 h under conditions of IL-2 deprivation [54]. Furthermore, the mandatory irradiation of NK-92 cells prior to clinical infusion compromises their in vivo persistence and overall therapeutic efficacy [55]. These reflect the need for repeated infusions due to the short duration of NK cells’ presence in the body.A key limitation is the absence of direct pharmacokinetic measurements for CAR-NK cell persistence, including vector copy number analysis. This constraint arises from the biological reality of adoptively transferred primary NK cells: their transient engraftment and minimal in vivo expansion preclude reliable VCN detection via standard qPCR. We therefore assessed persistence indirectly through (1) sustained elevation of serum effector cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ), and (2) clinical response durability. Future trials should implement emerging single-cell multimodal technologies (e.g., scRNA-seq with CAR construct detection) to resolve this limitation while maintaining clinical correlation with observed responses.

First-generation EGFR-TKIs (gefitinib/erlotinib) significantly improved progression-free survival (PFS) in NSCLC patients with 19Del/21L858R mutations. In this study, 6/7 patients harbored EGFR-TKI-sensitive mutations (genetically confirmed), progressing after first-line TKI therapy (median PFS: 6.14 months; range 3–15 months) with radiographic progression (PD) at enrollment [56–58]. While EGFR-TKIs achieve 60–70% objective response rates, acquired resistance typically occurs within 9–15 months, predominantly via T790M mutations (50–60% of cases) [59–61]. Although osimertinib demonstrates 10.1-month PFS and 71% ORR in T790M + patients [62], its unavailability in China during trial initiation limited therapeutic options. Subsequent immune checkpoint inhibitors showed limited efficacy in EGFR-mutant NSCLC (nivolumab mPFS: 1.3 vs. 2.1 months for T790M + vs. T790M− [63–65]). Two T790M + patients (P-3, P-4) progressed post-osimertinib but achieved 2- and 4-month PFS with CAR-NK92MI therapy. Among enrolled patients, 1/7 developed PD after 1 treatment cycle, 2/7 after 2–4 cycles, while 3 EGFR-mutant patients and 1 EGFR-unknown patient maintained stable disease (SD) > 12 months.

Brain metastases (BMS) occur in 10% of newly diagnosed NSCLC patients [66], with 40–55% developing BMS during disease progression [67, 68]. EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma exhibits particularly high BMS susceptibility [69]. Despite macromolecular therapies’ limited blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration often excluding BMS patients from trials [70], we included a symptomatic EGFR-mutant BMS patient (dizziness/blurred vision at diagnosis, multiple intracranial metastases on MRI) who declined radiotherapy and progressed through chemotherapy/icotinib. EGFR-TKIs demonstrate partial BBB permeability [71], while activated T cells may traverse the BBB [72, 73]. This patient received 11 CAR-NK92MI infusions (26 total doses), achieving 25-month progression-free survival (PFS) and 30-month overall survival (OS)—while historical mOS for immunotherapy-treated NSCLC BMS was 5.09–8.6 months [74–76]. This may be related to CAR-NK92MI’s enhanced immune recognition and activation of inflammatory cells in the central nervous system (CNS) [77].Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was precluded by patient compliance. There are few reports about CAR-NK cell treatment of NSCLC brain metastasis, and related clinical trials are underway. Since there may be a chance in only one patient, it is unclear whether the expected therapeutic effect can be achieved on the whole, and large sample studies are needed to confirm the effectiveness.

This study observed inherent variability in initial total dose and treatment cycles across the cohort, stemming from three primary sources: (1) protocol flexibility permitting safety-adaptive dose adjustments and two-dose-per-cycle options for suboptimal compliance; (2) patient-related factors including disease progression (PD)-driven early withdrawal (reducing total cycles) and variable compliance (impacting cycle completion); and (3) the dose-finding elements inherent to this first-in-human exploratory trial of novel CAR-NK therapy. Consequently, these treatment parameter differences reflect our safety-adaptive design and heterogeneous patient characteristics (e.g., compliance variability, differential PD rates). We acknowledge this heterogeneity may introduce potential bias in efficacy/safety interpretation. Future phase II trials should validate findings under more standardized treatment frameworks with expanded dose/schedule assessments.

In summary, preliminary findings indicate that CAR-NK92MI cell therapy represents a safe and potentially effective approach for relapsed and refractory NSCLC. The MUC-1 and PD-L1 targeted CAR-NK92MI cells provide a new option for improving the outcomes of cancer immunotherapy, especially in developing safe “off-the-shelf” CAR therapy for allogeneic cell therapy.
