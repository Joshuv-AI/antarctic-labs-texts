Transforming health systems in Tanzania towards universal health coverage: a scoping review of policy evolution, 1961–2025
Authors: Michael Kiremeji, Stephen M Kibusi, Eliudi Eliakimu, Stella G Mpagama, Mkama Julius, Ruth Ngowi, Armour Armour, Janeth Masuma, Faraja Msemwa, Eric Nzeyimana, Humphry Medarakani, Said Kilindimo, Leonard Katalambula, Hendry Sawe, Grace Magembe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTanzania carries a dual burden of communicable and non-communicable diseases while remaining vulnerable to emerging pandemics of public health concern. Since its independence in 1961, Tanzania has implemented successive health reforms aimed at expanding access and moving towards universal health coverage (UHC). Despite notable progress, inequities in access, quality and financial protection persist. This review examined how policy evolution (1961–2025) addressed or reinforced inequities and the lessons for building resilience and equity in UHC.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review of national health policies, strategies, legislation and the related literature (1961–2025), following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. Eligible sources included government reports, strategic plans, evaluation reports and peer-reviewed or grey literature. Data were analysed using the Walt and Gilson Policy Triangle and mapped against the WHO Health Systems Building Blocks.ResultsOut of 10 435 records identified, 60 documents met the inclusion criteria. Policy evolution reflected five broad reform episodes, ranging from postindependence centralisation to primary healthcare, structural adjustment and cost-sharing, sector-wide reforms and recent UHC-focused financing strategies. Reforms shifted from politically driven, top-down policies to participatory and evidence-informed approaches. Mapping showed progressive but uneven gains across service delivery, workforce, financing, governance, medicines and information systems. Six thematic shifts towards UHC were identified: fragmented to pooled financing, routine delivery to resilient systems, paper to digital systems, workforce numbers to distribution and skills-mix, state-only to mixed providers and expansion to effective coverage.ConclusionTanzania’s reform trajectory illustrates adaptive progress, but persistent inequities in financing, workforce distribution and service access remain. Achieving equitable and resilient UHC will require stronger domestic financing, governance and primary care, with transferable lessons for other low- and middle-income countries.

Introduction

Tanzania, a lower-middle-income country (LMIC) in East Africa, has experienced substantial demographic and epidemiological transitions since independence in 1961. The population has grown rapidly from approximately 10 million in 1961 to over 65 million in 2025, accompanied by increasing urbanisation and a youthful age structure. Life expectancy has improved markedly, rising from around 37 years in the early post-independence period to over 66 years in recent estimates, reflecting gains in public health interventions, immunisation coverage and access to essential health services.

Despite these improvements, Tanzania continues to face a dual burden of disease. Communicable diseases remain a major public health challenge: malaria accounts for a significant proportion of outpatient visits and hospital admissions, tuberculosis incidence remains among the highest globally, and an estimated 1.6 million people are living with HIV, with adult prevalence of approximately 4.5%. At the same time, non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are increasing rapidly, contributing to an estimated 33–40% of all deaths. Hypertension prevalence among adults exceeds 25%, while diabetes and cancers are rising due to demographic ageing, urbanisation and lifestyle changes. These conditions contribute substantially to premature mortality and long-term morbidity, placing additional strain on an already resource-constrained health system.13 In addition, the country remains vulnerable to recurrent and emerging pandemics of biosecurity concern.46 Health policies play a central role in shaping health systems by defining priorities, allocating resources and establishing mechanisms of accountability.7 In LMICs, policy trajectories are frequently influenced by resource constraints, donor priorities and shifting political agendas.8 9 Historical legacies, including colonial governance, structural adjustment programmes (SAPs) and global health initiatives, have left enduring imprints on health sector structures, governance arrangements and models of service delivery.10 11 Understanding these dynamics is critical for developing policies that are contextually relevant and resilient to emerging health threats.

Tanzania provides a compelling case study of health policy evolution since independence in 1961. The country’s policy landscape has undergone successive transitions: from postcolonial expansion of health services and the Alma-Ata-inspired primary healthcare () era, through structural adjustment reforms and sector-wide approaches, to contemporary strategies aimed at achieving universal health coverage (UHC). These shifts reflect broader trends across LMICs, while also bearing the imprint of Tanzania’s unique political leadership, economic reforms, donor engagement and evolving public health priorities.1215

Several studies have examined individual reforms or programmes; there has been limited systematic mapping of the breadth and trajectory of national health policies over time.1618 Such an approach is essential to assess progress towards UHC and to distill lessons relevant to other LMICs navigating similar challenges.19

A scoping review methodology is well-suited to this task, as it allows for a comprehensive mapping of diverse policy documents, strategic plans and legislative reforms without restricting the analysis to narrowly defined outcomes.20 21 This approach enables identification of policy trends, key actors, policy instruments and evidence gaps across more than six decades of health sector development.

The objectives of this review were to: (1) identify and categorise national health policy documents in Tanzania from 1961 to 2025; (2) describe major shifts in policy priorities in relation to UHC; (3) examine actors and policy instruments shaping reforms and (4) highlight evidence gaps and opportunities for future policy development.

Conceptual framework

This study adopts a conceptual framework to guide the systematic mapping of Tanzania’s health policy evolution from 1961 to 2025. The purpose of the framework was to provide an integrated analytical structure capable of linking political context, policy processes, system functions and long-term reform outcomes within a single explanatory model.

The framework was developed through the integration of two established health policy models: the Walt and Gilson Policy Triangle and the WHO Health System Building Blocks. The Policy Triangle (context, content, process and actors) was used to analyse the political economy and governance dimensions shaping reform trajectories. The WHO Building Blocks (service delivery, health workforce, information systems, medicines and technologies, financing and leadership/governance) were applied to categorise system-level changes across reform periods. UHC and health system resilience dimensions were incorporated as evaluative lenses to assess progress towards equity, financial protection, service coverage and adaptive capacity.

The framework informed the study design by structuring document selection and periodisation into reform episodes. It guided data extraction through predefined analytical domains aligned with the Policy Triangle and Building Blocks. During analysis, the framework enabled thematic synthesis across time, facilitating identification of reform phases and cross-cutting shifts, including transitions in financing arrangements, primary care orientation, digitalisation and resilience strengthening.

By explicitly combining policy process analysis with system performance domains, the conceptual framework provides the analytical logic underpinning the review and ensures coherence between historical description and evaluative interpretation.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a scoping review guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology (2020) and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist. A scoping review was chosen to comprehensively map the breadth of health system policies, strategies, legislation and reforms in Tanzania (1961–2025), and to identify gaps relevant to progress towards UHC.

Protocol and registration

No formal protocol for this scoping review was registered. The review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology (2020) and reported following the PRISMA-ScR checklist.

Setting

The review focused on Tanzania, an LMIC in East Africa, where health services are delivered through a mixed public-private system. Tanzania was selected given its long history of health sector reforms and explicit national commitment to UHC.

Eligibility criteria

We included English-language documents published between 1961 and 2025 that addressed national health system policies, strategies, reforms or legislation, focused on healthcare availability, delivery or access and encompassed peer-reviewed articles, government reports, strategic plans, evaluation reports or grey literature. Only studies published in English were included due to feasibility and resource constraints. We excluded clinical guidelines without explicit policy implications and non-health sector documents unless directly impacting healthcare availability.

Information sources

We conducted a comprehensive search of both peer-reviewed and grey literature to capture the full scope of health policy development in Tanzania. Electronic database searches were performed in PubMed, which was selected due to its strong coverage of biomedical and health systems literature, making it particularly suitable for identifying relevant studies in this field.

The grey literature was systematically identified through targeted searches of institutional and governmental repositories. These included the Ministry of Health Tanzania official website and document archives, the WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (IRIS), the World Bank Open Knowledge Repository and additional repositories such as UNICEF, United Nations Development Programme and relevant academic institutional repositories hosting theses and policy reports.

Searches within these repositories were conducted using combinations of keywords and adapted search strings, including: “Tanzania,” “health policy,” “health sector reform,” “primary health care,” “universal health coverage,” “health financing” and “health system.” Where repository-specific search functionalities were limited, manual browsing of publication archives and policy document sections was performed.

In addition, reference lists of all included documents were screened to identify further relevant sources. Searches were restricted to materials published between 1961 and 2025 to capture the full trajectory of health policy evolution since independence.

Search strategy

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (eg, Medical Subject Headings-terms) and free-text keywords related to Tanzania, health systems, health policy and UHC. Boolean operators were applied to refine the search, and strategies were adapted for each database. Searches were limited to publications from 1961 to 2025. Grey literature searches were conducted to identify relevant policy documents and reports from national, regional and international sources, including the Ministry of Health Tanzania, the WHO IRIS, the World Bank Open Knowledge Repository and regional platforms such as the African Union. High-sensitivity policy-related terms (eg, “policy”, “strategy”, “framework”, “roadmap”) were combined with health system and UHC keywords. Detailed PubMed and grey literature search strategies are provided in the supplementary material (online supplemental table 1a or table S1b).

Selection of sources

All retrieved records were imported into Zotero, which was used to identify and merge duplicate entries. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts against eligibility criteria, followed by full-text screening. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus, with arbitration by a third reviewer when necessary.

Data extraction

We developed a standardised extraction form to capture key information, including document type and year of publication, policy focus and objectives, actors involved in formulation and implementation, policy instruments and strategies and reported outcomes or impacts on healthcare availability. Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers, with cross-checking to ensure accuracy. The data extraction form was piloted on a sample of five documents and refined iteratively to ensure consistent interpretation of data fields. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion between the two reviewers.

Data analysis

Data were analysed thematically using two complementary frameworks. The Walt and Gilson Policy Triangle was applied to examine context, content, actors and processes. The WHO Health System Building Blocks framework was used to map implications for service delivery, workforce, financing, information systems, access to essential medicines and technologies and leadership/governance. Findings were synthesised narratively and visually to highlight trends, gaps and shifts in health system priorities over time. Extracted data were synthesised in three steps: (1) chronological grouping of documents into reform episodes; (2) framework-based mapping using the Walt & Gilson Policy Triangle and WHO building blocks and (3) thematic synthesis to identify cross-cutting shifts aligned with UHC dimensions.

Critical appraisal of individual sources

In line with JBI guidance for scoping reviews, we did not conduct a formal critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence, as the aim was to map the breadth and nature of policy documents rather than to assess their methodological quality (figure 1).

Results

A total of 10 435 records were identified through database searching and grey literature sources. After removal of 229 duplicates, 10 206 records remained. Following title and abstract screening, 4491 were excluded. The full texts of 5715 documents were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 5655 were excluded, primarily because they focused on subnational or programme-specific issues rather than national-level health system reforms. No documents were excluded for being outside the study period or for irrelevance to health system reforms. A total of 60 papers were included in the final review, including government policy documents, strategic plans, laws and peer-reviewed articles spanning 1961–2025. Historical evolution of healthcare availability in Tanzania (1961–2025)

Postindependence era (1961–1967)

At independence, Tanzania’s health system exhibited marked urban-rural disparities, limited infrastructure and weak coverage of essential services. Life expectancy was under 40 years, maternal mortality exceeded 700 per 100 000 live births, and child mortality was high due to poor immunisation and limited skilled birth attendance.152224 Health was declared a fundamental right under the leadership of President Julius Kambarage Nyerere, with commitments to free universal care. These early policy shifts modestly expanded access and improved immunisation coverage; however, progress was constrained by limited resources and a weak system capacity.25

Socialist health planning and PHC expansion (1968–1980)

The Arusha Declaration (1968) institutionalised socialism as a national policy framework, positioning free healthcare as a constitutional right.26 PHC was prioritised, supported by the rollout of village health posts, community health workers (CHWs) and mobile outreach services.27 By the late 1970s, life expectancy had increased to approximately 50 years, and infant mortality declined, although maternal mortality remained high. Despite improved geographic access, service quality was limited by shortages of essential medicines, inadequate referral systems and insufficient numbers of trained health professionals. Importantly, this period marked strong community engagement, with participatory governance structures playing a central role in local health planning and service delivery.28

Economic crisis, structural adjustment and reform preparations (1981–1994)

The fiscal crisis of the 1980s reduced government health expenditure, resulting in service deterioration.29 SAPs introduced user fees and cost-sharing, shifting financing structures and increasing donor influence, shaping priorities and introducing vertical programmes targeting diseases such as HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis and malaria.30 31 Policy focus moved towards efficiency, governance and decentralisation in preparation for broader HSRs. Health indicators stagnated or worsened during this period. Immunisation coverage declined in the mid-1980s before recovering later, and both maternal and child mortality remained high. Urban-rural disparities widened, reflecting uneven distribution of resources and services.

Decentralisation and the essential health package (1995–2009)

The HSRs introduced in the mid-1990s marked a shift towards decentralised governance, with decision-making authority transferred to district-level authorities. Basket funding mechanisms improved coordination among development partners and enhanced resource pooling.24 32 The essential health package (EHP) prioritised cost-effective services, high-impact interventions, particularly in maternal, newborn and child health, as well as infectious disease control.33 34 During this period, Tanzania achieved notable improvements in key health indicators: under-five mortality declined significantly (from over 150 per 1000 in the early 1990s to below 100 by the late 2000s), and life expectancy began to recover. The scale-up of HIV/AIDS programmes, including antiretroviral therapy, alongside malaria control interventions (eg, insecticide-treated nets), contributed to reductions in disease burden. The health system became increasingly pluralistic, with non-governmental organisations, faith-based organisations (FBOs) and private providers playing a larger role in service delivery.

However, disparities persisted between urban and rural areas, particularly in access to skilled birth attendance and emergency obstetric care. Community participation remained a policy priority, but implementation varied across districts.

Health systems strengthening, innovation and UHC drive (2010–2025)

From 2010 onward, reforms focused on health systems strengthening and progress towards UHC. Successive Health Sector Strategic Plans (III–V) emphasised governance, health financing, service quality and equity.35 36

Life expectancy increased further to over 65 years, while under-five mortality declined to below 50 per 1000 live births. Maternal mortality also decreased but remained high relative to global targets (approximately 238 per 100 000 live births in recent estimates). The burden of disease shifted, with NCDs accounting for an increasing share of morbidity and mortality, alongside persistent infectious diseases.

Health insurance coverage expanded through the National Health Insurance Fund (NHIF) and Community Health Fund (CHF), although overall population coverage remained limited (below 20% by 2020),37 highlighting gaps in financial protection.

Innovations such as performance-based financing (PBF), digital health information systems and supply chain reforms improved efficiency and accountability.38 Community engagement strategies were revitalised through CHW programmes and participatory planning approaches, though sustainability challenges remained.

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed systemic vulnerabilities but also accelerated investments in surveillance, laboratory capacity and emergency preparedness. Subsequent responses to outbreaks, including Marburg Virus Disease, demonstrated improved national capacity for epidemic management.39 40

The UHC Roadmap (2023–2025) reaffirmed commitments to equity, financial risk protection and quality of care, aligning national priorities with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG target 3.8.41

Health system capacity (as of 2024)

By 2024, Tanzania’s health system comprised 12 846 facilities: 462 hospitals, 1254 health centres, 8328 dispensaries and 1013 clinics.41 Government-owned facilities (7545) remained the majority, supplemented by FBOs and the private sector. Bed capacity stood at 169 662, of which 153 683 were functional, leaving a deficit of nearly 16 000 beds. Most inpatient capacity was concentrated in general and maternity wards, with dispensaries also contributing to inpatient care.40

Human resources for health remained below WHO thresholds, with shortages particularly acute in rural areas.42 CHWs continued to play a critical role in extending services to underserved populations.

Diagnostic capacity improved with the installation of 399 digital X-ray units, 973 ultrasound machines, 42 CT scanners and 12 MRI machines.43 However, access remained limited at the primary care level, where only 69 dispensaries had basic ultrasound. Advanced technologies (Positron Emission Tomography-CT, catheterisation labs, C-arm systems) were concentrated in national and referral hospitals.

Emergency and critical care capacity expanded significantly. The number of emergency medicine departments increased to 133, following the construction of 116 new units after 2022. Intensive care units grew from eight units with 183 beds pre-2022 to 44 units with 988 beds by 2024.44

Pre-hospital services were strengthened by procuring 1199 ambulances between 2023 and 2024, achieving an estimated 88% functional coverage, including both Basic Life Support and Advanced Life Support.

Epidemic preparedness and response capacity improved through infrastructure investment, workforce training, preventive service delivery and strengthened surveillance systems. These measures enhanced Tanzania’s ability to manage COVID-19, Marburg Virus Disease and other outbreaks, reducing transmission and improving clinical outcomes.45

Correspondingly, policy content evolved from the expansion of free public health services and preventive care to community-based PHC followed by cost-sharing and decentralisation and later the introduction of EHPs, pooled financing and UHC-oriented reforms including insurance expansion and quality improvement initiatives.

The composition of policy actors broadened substantially over time. Early reforms were led primarily by government institutions, while subsequent periods saw increasing involvement of international and non-state actors, including the WHO, UNICEF, World Bank, International Monetary Fund, civil society organisations and the private sector.

In parallel, policy processes transitioned from centralised, state-led decision-making to more participatory and coordinated approaches. This included donor-influenced reforms during the structural adjustment period, followed by more structured collaboration under sector-wide approaches and, more recently, multi-stakeholder consultations with strengthened monitoring and accountability mechanisms.

The Alma-Ata era expanded rural outreach and introduced CHWs, modestly improving workforce and service coverage.49 50 Structural adjustment policies led to reduced service access, workforce attrition and financing instability, highlighting vulnerabilities in the health system under external economic pressures.

Implications

Addressing these gaps requires integrated financing reforms, stronger workforce strategies, equity-focused delivery models and a deliberate investment in policy evaluation and implementation research. The next phase of Tanzania’s health sector strategy should explicitly incorporate these areas to accelerate progress towards UHC and health system resilience.36 51 52

Discussion

This scoping review provides a comprehensive analysis of Tanzania’s health policy evolution over more than six decades, highlighting both the political-institutional processes that shaped reforms and the structural transformations across the health system. The findings demonstrate that while successive reforms expanded access to services and reflected a strong political commitment to UHC, persistent challenges in financing, workforce retention and equitable service delivery remain.33 36 51 53

Our analysis aligns with Walt and Gilson’s policy triangle framework, which emphasises the interplay of context, content, actors and processes in shaping reform trajectories.46 Tanzania’s reforms have consistently been shaped by macro-political and economic contexts: from socialist-driven centralisation in the 1960s–70s, to structural adjustment in the 1980s, to SWAp and decentralisation from the late 1990s onward.50 54 55 These shifts mirror trends across many LMICs, where external donor priorities and fiscal constraints significantly influenced reform direction.47 56 57

Compared with regional peers, Tanzania has made notable gains in policy development but lags in some implementation outcomes. Rwanda is often cited as a model of rapid progress towards UHC, with >90% health insurance coverage achieved through community-based schemes and strong PBF.58 Ethiopia has advanced PHC through the Health Extension Program, significantly increasing service utilisation and maternal-child health outcomes.59 60 Ghana’s National Health Insurance Scheme has expanded financial protection, though sustainability challenges persist.61 62 Tanzania’s progress—particularly in digital health, mixed financing models and decentralisation—is commendable, but gaps remain in health workforce retention, informal sector insurance coverage and rural-urban service equity.3138 6365

From a systems perspective, reforms in Tanzania have largely addressed the WHO’s six building blocks of health systems.66 Progress is evident in governance and service delivery through decentralisation and SWAp, in medicines through the establishment of the Medical Stores Department, and in information systems through digital innovations. However, financing remains fragmented, with multiple insurance schemes covering less than 20% of the population, and out-of-pocket spending remains high at around 33% of total health expenditure67 68. Human resources remain critically constrained, with only 0.6 doctors per 10 000 population compared with the WHO threshold of 2.369.

These findings have global relevance. Tanzania’s trajectory underscores the importance of embedding reforms in country-specific contexts, while also aligning with global UHC agendas (SDGs target 3.8) and lessons from comparable LMICs. It also highlights the risks of reform cycles that prioritise design over implementation, and the need for sustained financing, political will and adaptive governance.

Conclusion

The evolution of Tanzania’s health policy reflects a steady movement from centrally controlled, politically driven reforms towards more participatory, evidence-based and UHC-focused strategies. While challenges remain, particularly in financing, workforce distribution and equity, the integration of resilience and quality dimensions positions Tanzania on a promising trajectory. Continued commitment to domestic financing, robust governance and innovation in service delivery will be critical to sustaining gains and achieving UHC by 2030 and beyond. Tanzania’s pathway illustrates both opportunities and pitfalls of health reform in LMICs; achieving UHC requires embedding equity, resilience and sustainable domestic financing at the heart of policy reform.

Study limitations

This analysis relied on secondary data from national policy documents, strategic plans, reports and the published literature, which may reflect reporting biases or selective emphasis aligned with political priorities. In addition, the review relied primarily on a single bibliographic database (PubMed), which may have limited the retrieval of relevant studies indexed in other databases, thereby potentially affecting the comprehensiveness of the evidence base.

The exclusion of non-English sources, including Swahili-language documents, may have resulted in the omission of relevant local evidence and policy discussions, potentially limiting the contextual depth and representativeness of the findings.
