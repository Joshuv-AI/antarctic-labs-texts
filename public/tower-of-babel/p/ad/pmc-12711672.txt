Glucocorticoid-induced changes of the gut microbiota and metabolic markers in healthy young men: Outcome of a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Liwei Lyu, Yong Fan, Thomas Bryrup, Marc Clos-Garcia, Susanne Brix, Markus Eiken, Evelina Stankevic, Asger Bach Lund, Filip Krag Knop, Niklas Rye Jørgensen, Henrik Vestergaard, Torben Hansen, Tue Hansen, Trine Nielsen, Oluf Pedersen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryGlucocorticoids induce insulin resistance and suppress immunity, but their impact on gut microbiota, which may modulate metabolism and immunity remains under explored. In this 7-day trial, we assess glucocorticoid-induced changes in gut microbiota and metabolic markers in 56 healthy men, randomly assigned to three interventions: oral prednisolone (PO group), intramuscular methylprednisolone acetate (IM group), or saline (CTL group). Shotgun metagenomics reveal that PO glucocorticoid causes shifts in bacterial abundance, increasing Blautia and Collinsella, while decreasing Dysosmobacter welbionis and Anaerotignum faecicola, linked with insulin resistance and immunosuppression markers. Additionally, PO treatment alters microbial pathways and enzymes related to glycolysis and lipid metabolism, with changes in predicted metabolites such as hypoxanthine and phenylacetate. IM treatment results in minimal microbiota changes. These findings underscore the route-dependent effects of glucocorticoids on gut microbiota and their potential impact on host metabolism and immunity. The trial was approved by the Danish Medicine Agency (EudraCT protocol number: 2016-001850-16).Keywords: glucocorticoids, gut microbiota, intervention trial, metabolic markers, per oral, intramuscular injection, microbial metabolites, randomized controlled trial

Introduction

Glucocorticoid hormones play a crucial role in regulating metabolism, immune function, fluid balance, stress response, and nervous system function.1 Cushing’s syndrome, a hyper-glucocorticoid condition resulting from either endogenous overproduction or prolonged exogenous glucocorticoid use, is often associated with insulin resistance, immunosuppression, and significant gut dysbiosis.2,3,4 Beyond their endogenous role, glucocorticoids are widely used in clinical practice as first-line immunosuppressive agents due to their potent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. However, despite their therapeutic benefits, glucocorticoid therapy has several adverse effects, including an increased risk of infections, osteoporosis, metabolic disruptions, as well as gastrointestinal complications.5 Given the well-established role of the gut microbiota in regulating host metabolism and immunity,6,7 these observations may suggest a potential link between glucocorticoid-induced changes in the host and shifts in gut microbiota composition and function, highlighting the need for further investigation into the complex interplay.

A few human and animal studies have been conducted showing parallel albeit variegated changes in host biology and gut microbiota following glucocorticoid.8,9 As an example, a case-control study of 28 individuals with acute transverse myelitis (ATM) and glucocorticoid-induced obesity (per oral administration), along with 27 age- and gender-matched healthy controls, reported that based on 16S rRNA sequencing data, glucocorticoid treatment reduced gut microbial diversity and increased abundance of Firmicutes while decreasing Bacteroidetes.8 However, this study has limitations. The observed gut microbiota differences may not solely be due to glucocorticoids, as the treatment group were sedentary, overweight ATM patients, while the controls were healthy, introducing potential confounding factors. Additionally, the cross-sectional design without repeated sampling fails to capture natural gut microbiota fluctuations.10

Investigating the effects of an oral drug on the gut microbiota presents a challenge, as it remains unclear whether observed changes result from direct interactions within the gut and its microbial ecosystem or from systemic effects within the host. Here, we designed a randomized clinically controlled 7-day intervention trial encompassing 56 healthy men who were allocated three groups: oral prednisolone (PO group), an initial single intramuscular injection of methylprednisolone acetate (Depo-Medrol) (IM group), and a saline intramuscular injected placebo control group (CTL group). By comparing the effects of oral and intramuscular administration, we aimed to decipher whether putative gut microbiota changes result from direct exposure to glucocorticoids in the gastrointestinal tract or from systemic host-mediated effects. The placebo group was included as a control to account for natural fluctuations in gut microbiota composition and in markers of host biology (Figure 1A).

Results

Pre-intervention demographics and clinical profiles

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of study participants are detailed in Table S1.

Before enrollment, participants maintained a healthy body mass index (BMI) ranging from 20.1 to 26.9 kg/m2, optimal glucose metabolism with HbA1c levels from 25 to 38 mmol/mol, and fasting plasma glucose levels from 3.4 to 6.1 mmol/L. Biochemical measures of liver and kidney function were also within normal ranges, with plasma alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels from 12 to 53 U/L and plasma creatinine levels from 61 to 107 μmol/L, respectively. One-way ANOVA results showed no significant differences in these biomarkers among the three trial groups, affirming appropriate randomization.

Host phenotypic changes after glucocorticoid intervention

According to self-report and tablet counts of prepacked prednisolone, medication adherence in the PO group was complete. Both glucocorticoid interventions were well tolerated. Compared with individuals in the control group, glucocorticoid treated study participants reported in their visual analog scale (VAS) scores no more gastrointestinal or drug-related adverse effects (data not shown).

Markers of glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and immune regulation on day 1 and day 8 are provided in Figure 2 and Table S2. Regarding glucose metabolism, neither fasting plasma glucose levels nor plasma glucose responses during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) showed significant changes from study baseline to study endline in either intervention group (Figures 2A, 2C, and 2I). However, plasma insulin levels at 0, 30, and 120 min during the OGTT significantly increased at the study endline in the PO group (Figures 2B and 2J, adjusted p = 0.01, 0.02, and 0.02), with a corresponding rise in area under the curve (AUC) of plasma insulin during the OGTT (Figure 2D, adjusted p = 0.01). Fasting plasma insulin levels were also increased in the IM group (Figure 2B, adjusted p = 0.08). Moreover, insulin resistance markers in the PO group were significantly changed, with Matsuda insulin sensitivity index decreasing from 5.9 to 4.1 (Figure 2E, adjusted p = 0.01) and homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) index levels rising from 1.8 to 2.6 (Figure 2F, adjusted p = 0.02), confirming glucocorticoid-induced impairment of whole-body insulin sensitivity. A modest decrease in the Matsuda insulin sensitivity index (Figure 2E, adjusted p = 0.13) was observed in the IM group, suggesting a less pronounced glucocorticoid-induced effect on measures of insulin sensitivity.

Plasma C-reactive protein (CRP) levels decreased in the PO group, indicating the immunosuppressive effects of glucocorticoids, with levels declining from 342 to 214 ng/mL (Figure 2G, adjusted p = 0.03). Additionally, in both intervention groups, blood total leukocyte counts increased significantly (Figure 2H), with values rising from 5.3 to 8.5 ×109/L (adjusted p = 0.003) in the PO group and from 5.4 to 6.3 ×109/L (adjusted p = 0.05) in the IM group. No significant biomarker changes were observed in the control group. Finally, PO or IM administration of glucocorticoids also induced modest changes in other circulating cytokines or blood leukocytes as shown in Tables S3 and S5.

Impact of glucocorticoid intervention on gut microbiota richness, community structure, and prevalence of specific bacterial species

To investigate the putative impact of glucocorticoid intervention on gut microbial communities, we examined gut microbiota alpha diversity, intra-individual Bray-Curtis distances, and the prevalence of specific microbial taxa across time points and intervention groups. The data are shown in Figure S2.

Our analysis revealed no significant changes in the alpha diversity of gene families, bacterial species, viral species, or fungal species (Figures S2A–S2G) from day 1 and day 8 of intervention. These findings suggest that short-term glucocorticoid intervention did not markedly alter overall microbial diversity. Intra-individual Bray-Curtis distances were calculated from the relative abundance of gut bacterial or viral microbiota to assess compositional dissimilarity within each participant across the two time points. As shown in Figure S2H, in PO group, the intraindividual distance between day 1 and day 8 was slightly higher in the PO group than that in the control group (p = 0.04, adjusted p = 0.12). In the control group (Figure S2H), the initial slight and insignificant increase (adjusted p = 0.18) in Bray-Curtis distance from day 1 to days 2, 4, and 6 but a decrease at day 8 likely reflects natural short-term variability in gut bacterial microbiota composition, which can occur due to minor, transient fluctuations in diet, lifestyle, or due to technical variability. Otherwise, the analysis revealed no significant differences in bacterial or viral species composition between the pre-intervention and any time points after intervention within PO or IM groups (Figures S2H and S2I). Our findings indicate that glucocorticoid intervention did not lead to substantial shifts in the overall microbial community structure at the broad compositional level.

However, changes were observed in the prevalence of specific gut bacterial species following glucocorticoid administration as shown in Figure S3. In the PO group, we found an increased prevalence of Turicibacter bilis (day 1 vs. day 6 and day 8: both effect size = 0.4, adjusted p = 0.01, Figures S3A and S3C). Similarly, in the IM group, glucocorticoid intervention was associated with an increased prevalence of several species (Figure S3B), including Veillonella parvula (day 1 vs. day 2 and day 4: effect size = 0.3 to 0.4, adjusted p = 0.05), Streptococcus thermophilus (day 1 vs. day 2: effect size = 0.5, adjusted p = 0.02), and Clostridium sp. Marseille P3244 (day 1 vs. day 2 and day 8: effect size = 0.30 to 0.35, adjusted p = 0.08). In contrast, the prevalence of Bittarella massiliensis declined following IM intervention (day 1 vs. day 6 and day 8: effect size = −0.3, adjusted p = 0.08). In the control group, no significant temporal changes were observed in the prevalence of gut microbial taxa (data not shown), further highlighting the specific effects of glucocorticoid interventions on bacterial species prevalence.

Alterations in abundance of gut bacterial species induced by glucocorticoid intervention

To assess the impact of glucocorticoid intervention on the relative abundance of gut bacterial species, we conducted a linear mixed-effects analysis of the bacterial microbiota following glucocorticoid intervention. As shown in Figure 3A, significant changes in bacterial species abundance were observed in the PO glucocorticoid intervention group, particularly at the study endline (day 8) compared to study baseline (day 1).

In the PO intervention group, notable enrichment was observed in the Firmicutes phylum, especially among two species within the Blautia genus, indicating a consistent response to oral glucocorticoid intervention. Furthermore, an increase in the abundance of species within the Collinsella genus was evident, with Collinsella_SGB14861 showing the highest effect size (effect size = 0.37, adjusted p = 0.007). Conversely, several species exhibited significant reductions in abundance following oral glucocorticoid intervention. Sutterella wadsworthensis showed the largest decrease (effect size = −1.21, adjusted p = 5.6 e−05), followed by Dysosmobacter welbionis (effect size = −0.78, adjusted p = 6.9e−04) and Anaerotignum faecicola (effect size = −0.72, adjusted p = 6.9e−04). Additional significant declines were observed in Clostridium sp. AM33-3, Clostridium sp. AM22-11AC (effect size = −0.60 and −0.69, adjusted p = 5.8 e−03 to 3.2 e−03), Lachnospira pectinoschiza and Lachnospira eligens (effect size = −0.63 and −0.52, adjusted p = 4.9 e−03 and 0.03). These findings underscore the substantial shifts in bacterial abundance following oral glucocorticoid administration (Figure 3A).

In the IM group, fewer abundance changes of differential bacterial species were observed compared to the PO group. Notably, five species exhibited significant reductions in the relative abundance following the glucocorticoid intervention (Figure 3B). Interestingly, three of these species overlapped between the PO and IM groups, including two Lachnospira species (L. eligens and L. pectinoschiza) and Sutterella wadsworthensis (Figure 3C). This overlap may suggest a consistent and relatively marked effect of glucocorticoid intervention on specific gut bacteria, regardless of whether the intervention was administered orally or parenterally.

Alterations in community-level microbial functional pathways and enzymes induced by glucocorticoid intervention

To better understand the physiological implications of changes in gut microbiota composition, we investigated the predicted community-level microbial functional pathways and enzyme activities. This analysis included 207 MetaCyc pathways and 767 enzymes that were present in at least 20% of the samples and had a minimum rarefied read count of 50.

Pathways related to glucose metabolism were upregulated in the PO group, including glycolysis I, tricarboxylic acid cycle and lactose and galactose degradation pathway (effect size = 0.08 to 0.24, adjusted p = 0.08) (Figure 4A). Additionally, predicted enzymes involved in glucose and lipid metabolism were upregulated in the PO group, including triose-phosphate isomerase (EC 6.3.2.17, effect size = 0.04, adjusted p = 0.08), participating in both glycolysis and gluconeogenesis11 (Figure 5A). The triacylglycerol lipase related to lipid catabolism was similarly elevated (EC 3.1.1.3, effect size = 0.14, adjusted p = 0.08) (Figure 5A). These changes indicate a dysregulation of both glucose and lipid microbial metabolic pathways in response to glucocorticoid intervention.

The IM group (Figures 4B and 5B) exhibited fewer and less pronounced temporal abundance changes in metabolic pathways and enzymes compared to the PO group. However, shifts were still observed in the bacterial glycolytic pathways, particularly in glycolysis I and glycolysis II (both effect size = 0.14, adjusted p = 0.002). Moreover, a pathway involved in vitamin B1 synthesis,12 PWY-6892 (thiazole component of thiamine diphosphate biosynthesis I, effect size = 0.09, adjusted p = 0.04), was altered specifically in the IM group.

A comparison of the PO and IM groups revealed overlapping enrichment in the glycolysis I pathway (Figure 4C) and triacylglycerol lipase (Figure 5C). These findings suggest that glucocorticoid intervention may generally modulate the microbial utilization of glucose or lipids, potentially influencing broader metabolic functions in the human host.

Collectively, the results indicate that glucocorticoid intervention induces specific changes in microbial functional pathways, particularly in glucose and lipid metabolism, and amino acid synthesis, suggesting multiple metabolic alterations that may occur in both the gut microbiota and potentially in the host.

Predicted alterations of microbial metabolites specifically associated with oral glucocorticoid intervention

To further explore the changes in microbial functions observed following glucocorticoid intervention and to gain deeper insights into the potential bioactive microbial metabolites that may influence host metabolism, we employed a computational approach, MelonnPan13 to predict metabolites from metagenomic data.

Briefly, we first trained a cross-validated elastic-net regularized regression model on a dataset comprising paired metagenome and metabolite abundances from an independent cohort.14 This model was then applied to the individual metagenomes of the present trial to infer the predictive metabolome and identify putative metabolites associated with glucocorticoid intervention. In these analyses, we used the same linear regression approach employed for metagenomics, with adjustments made for bacterial cell load.

As shown in Figure 6 and Table S4, our analysis revealed that the PO group was predicted to have increased levels of hypoxanthine, a metabolite linked to inhibited inflammatory responses.15 Moreover, one gut bacterial metabolite, phenylacetate, was also predicted to be elevated following oral glucocorticoid intervention.

Bacterial species associated with human host phenotypic responses to glucocorticoid intervention

To explore the relationship between gut microbiota changes and host phenotypic responses following glucocorticoid intervention, we analyzed the associations between the relative abundance of gut bacterial species and human host phenotypes. This analysis is based on linear mixed effect modeling and visualized in Figure 7. The association analysis revealed several relationships between the relative abundance of specific bacterial species and host phenotypic markers.

Thus, the concentration of total blood leukocytes was positively associated with the relative abundance of Collinsella SGB14861, Dorea longicatena, and Blautia SGB4815 (effect sizes ranging from 0.17 to 0.20, adjusted p = 0.06). Conversely, inverse associations were observed with abundance of Clostridium sp. AM33-3, Lachnospira pectinoschiza, and GGB9699 SGB15216 (effect sizes ranging from −0.32 to −0.18, adjusted p = 0.06), further demonstrating the influence of the gut microbiota in shaping immune responses. Fasting plasma insulin was inversely associated with relative abundance of Clostridium sp. AM33-3 and Lachnospira pectinoschiza (effect sizes = −0.38 and −0.43, adjusted p = 0.06). AUC plasma insulin under the OGTT was inversely associated with the abundance of Clostridium sp. AM33-3 (effect size = –0.45, adjusted p = 0.09). These findings may suggest that the mentioned bacterial species may contribute to an altered insulin release under the condition of oral glucorticoid intervention. Moreover, Matsuda insulin sensitivity index levels showed positive associations with the relative abundance of Staphylococcus SGB6340, Lachnospira pectinoschiza, and Clostridium sp. AM33-3 (effect size = 0.42 to 0.56 adjusted p = 0.06) indicating a microbial influence on regulation of insulin sensitivity.

Overall, the findings underscore distinct associations in the PO group between the gut bacterial microbiota and markers of host metabolism and immunity. No such associations were found in the IM or control groups (data not shown), highlighting the specific impact of oral glucocorticoid intervention on gut microbiota-host interactions.

Discussion

In the present 7-day randomized controlled trial, we investigated alterations of the gut microbiota in healthy young men who developed glucocorticoid-induced impairment of markers of insulin sensitivity and immune function (Figure 1). By comparing PO and IM glucocorticoid interventions to a control group accounting for natural fluctuations, we identified both distinct and shared gut microbial responses to the two glucocorticoid administrations.

The trial outcome shows that short-term glucocorticoid administration did not induce substantial shifts in overall community structure of gut bacterial, viral, or fungal microbiota, suggesting that glucocorticoids have minimal impact on the broad diversity of gut microbial communities. However, more targeted effects were observed in the prevalence of Turicibacter bilis, which has been linked to increased serum levels of acyl carnitines,16 a marker of diabetic cardiomyopathy.17 These findings suggest that glucocorticoids may exert selective pressure on specific gut bacterial taxa, potentially influencing adaptive metabolic and immune pathways, despite their limited impact on overall microbial diversity.

In terms of bacterial abundance, the PO group exhibited several shifts, including an increase in the relative abundance of Blautia and Collinsella species, and decreases in the pro-inflammatory Sutterella wadsworthensis18 and the potential anti-obesity-related Dysosmobacter welbionis.19 These abundance changes were found in the PO group, where glucocorticoid induced impairment was more pronounced, as evidenced by both a decrease in the Matsuda insulin sensitivity index and an increase in HOMA-IR values. Along with the metabolic changes, the PO group also showed a reduction in plasma CRP levels, supporting the broader systemic impact of glucocorticoids on both metabolism and immune function. While the IM group showed less pronounced bacterial shifts compared to the PO group, it still shared shifts in the abundance of several bacterial species with the PO group. Therefore, these shared differential gut bacteria may be broadly associated with glucocorticoid intervention, likely driven by systemic effects on immune regulation and metabolism, regardless of the administration route.

The functional impact of glucocorticoid intervention on microbial metabolism was evident in our analysis of both microbial pathways and enzymes. In the PO group, we observed an over-representation of the abundance of microbial pathways involved in glycolysis, including those associated with glucose-6-phosphate and fructose-6-phosphate. At the enzyme level, we identified an increase in the abundance of gluconeogenesis enzymes, such as triose-phosphate isomerase, findings that align with the glucocorticoid-induced glucose dysregulation in this group. Compared with functional pathways, microbial enzymes showed weaker significance probably and partly due to their sparser annotation and lower prevalence compared to aggregated pathway features, which altogether may reduce statistical power.

Revealing putative associations between specific gut bacterial strains and host phenotypic changes was a key objective of the present trial. In the PO group, the relative abundance of Lachnospira pectinoschiza was inversely associated with fasting plasma insulin and total blood leukocytes while positively associated with insulin sensitivity. Interestingly, this gut bacterium has been reported to be inversely associated with ischemic heart disease and a metabolically healthy gut microbiota.20 Similarly, the relative abundance of Clostridium sp. AM33-3 has been shown to be inversely linked to plasma insulin and blood total leukocytes but positively linked to Matsuda insulin sensitivity index. While some bacterial species such as Lachnospira eligens, Anaerotignurn faecicola, Dysosmobacter welbionis, and Sutterella wadsworthensis, which showed significant changes in relative abundance along with glucocorticoid intervention while not associated with the observed host phenotypic changes in the PO group, may suggest direct effects elicited by glucocorticoid on the gut microbiota independent from the effects on the host biological markers. This distinction is critical for understanding long-term treatment consequences, as direct microbe-targeting effects could contribute to therapy complications independently of host pathways.

Additionally, when examining predicted microbial metabolites using a computational approach, we found significant alterations in the PO group, including increases in predicted abundance of phenylacetate and hypoxanthine. Phenylacetate,21,22,23,24,25 a microbial byproduct of bacterial phenylalanine metabolism exhibited increased predicted relative abundance following glucocorticoid intervention. This metabolite has been reported to link with type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, steatohepatitis and endothelial cell senescence through enhanced lipid accumulation or mitochondrial H2O2 production. Hypoxanthine, another metabolite of interest, recently was reported to inhibit inflammatory responses by regulating the MAPK and NLRP3-related pathways.15 The observed increase in predicted relative abundance of phenylacetate and hypoxanthine may suggest the two metabolites possibly serving as contributing factors or downstream effects of glucocorticoid-induced host insulin resistance or immunosuppression. The IM group showed no significant changes in predicted metabolite abundance, implying route-dependent effects on microbial metabolism.

Limitations of the study

Even though our clinical trial provides valuable insights into effects of glucocorticoids on the gut microbiota, the study comes with several limitations. The primary endpoint of our explorative trial assessing “structural change of gut microbiota” was intentionally designed and regulatorily approved, aiming to capture potentially correlative glucocorticoid effects on the gut microbiome and host biology. While this approach does not permit a conventional sample size calculation based on a single effect estimate, it allowed for an unbiased discovery-driven assessment of host and microbial responses. As discussed in STAR Methods, it was not possible to estimate a dosing of the two glucocorticoid compounds that could be predicted to trigger similar in vivo effects. Different pharmacokinetics behavior between administration routes of glucocorticoids were not assessed through serum or fecal drug level monitoring, precluding quantification or comparison of direct microbial-drug interactions. Thus, the observed differences in host phenotypes across the two groups may reflect combined effects of pharmacokinetics (peak concentration or duration) and route-specific tissue exposure.

In addition, our study did not control for formulation excipients. Their trace amounts are pharmacologically inert, but we cannot exclude an effect of trace amounts of starch and lactose on the gut microbiome readouts. Moreover, while weight-independent drug dosing may introduce BMI-dependent exposure variability or potentially modulating effect sizes, such dosing regimens reflect real-world clinical practice and strengthen the translational relevance of our study design.

Lack of a pre-intervention run-in period may induce baseline microbiota differences though randomization mitigates group-level biases. Similarly, while study participants were instructed to maintain their habitual diet and physical activity, the absence of formal monitoring of dietary variations and exercise habits prevented verification of adherence or stratification by compliance status. Our study included young healthy white men only and therefore our results do not generalize to other populations.

DNA extraction and sequencing workflow used in this study were optimized for gut bacteria, limiting gut viral or fungal DNA recovery. Thus, results may underrepresent true viral or fungal diversity. Finally, while computational predictions identified potential metabolite changes, the absence of stool metabolomics data precludes validation of these microbiota-derived metabolic shifts. Moreover, this MelonnPan’s IBD-inclusive training set enables broad metabolite prediction, its application to healthy cohorts may introduce bias, warranting caution in interpreting results. Future works are needed to integrate multi-omics validation, particularly targeted metabolomics, to quantitatively support microbiota-metabolite-host interactions suggested by our predictive analyses.

Overall, the outcome of the present clinically controlled trial highlights the nuanced relationship between glucocorticoid therapy, gut microbiota, and host metabolic and systemic immune responses. While oral glucocorticoid intervention induced more pronounced gut microbiota shifts and metabolic changes than intramuscular administration, both routes of intervention led to significant alterations in specific bacterial species and functional pathways/enzymes that were linked to changes in markers of host metabolism and immunity.

Experimental model and study participant details

The study was designed as an open, randomized, three-armed, single center study. At the pre-trial eligibility assessment visit to the clinical unit within three months before trial start, 61 men underwent screening. Study candidates were evaluated based on predetermined inclusion criteria: Danish white men living in the capital region of Denmark, aged 18–35 years with a body mass index (BMI) of 18.5–27.0 kg/m2, stable weight within the past two months, normal glucose metabolism (HbA1c < 39 mmol/mol), and standard kidney and liver function as indicated by plasma creatinine and ALT levels, respectively. Normal blood pressure was also required (systolic < 140 mmHg, diastolic < 90 mmHg). Exclusion criteria included known allergies to glucocorticoids, any illness, intake of medication, probiotics, antibiotics, or significant diet changes within four months prior to the trial. Additionally, smokers were excluded.

All participants included in this study are males, aged between 18 and 33 years. Before the trial started, one individual was excluded for not meeting the HbA1c criteria and four individuals were excluded for not meeting the BMI criteria. A total of 56 men met the outlined trial criteria, and all of these completed the trial (Figure S1). The final number of participants (n = 56) therefore deviates from the registered trial protocol, which anticipated a total of 60 participants. The 56 study participants were randomized by study ID using sample() function in R (version 4.3.2), ensuring equal probability of assignment to each study arm.

Method details

Intervention

The trial was conducted according to the International Conference on Harmonization’s Good Clinical Practice guidelines including the Declaration of Helsinki II. The trial protocol was approved by the Ethical Committees of the Capital Region of Denmark (H-16021787), the Danish Medicine Agency (EudraCT protocol number: 2016-001850-16), and the Data protection Agency (SUND-2016-42). The Good Clinical Practice (GCP)-unit of Bispebjerg- Frederiksberg Hospital monitored the trial. All participants gave written informed consent prior to their participation in the study. No changes to methods were made after trial commencement.

At first trial visit, the participants were asked to refrain from changing their habitual diet and physical activity during the interventional period. At both study visits, the study participants were asked to report gastrointestinal symptoms in a VAS-score. In addition, at end of trial, they were asked to inform the clinical investigators about any adverse events or adverse reactions experienced during the intervention. The trial was conducted at the clinical research unit of Novo Nordisk Foundation Center for Basic Metabolic Research located at Bispebjerg and Frederiksberg Hospital, Copenhagen.

Based on published data on glucocorticoid-induced insulin resistance, 30 mg prednisolone as per oral dose daily for seven days was estimated to be sufficient to induce a transient and reversible insulin resistance.30,31,32,33,34,35,36

Very few studies have explored the effects on insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance following IM administration of methylprednisolone acetate. However, effects on suppression of endogenous cortisol release for at least 14 days, with a maximum suppression after three days following IM injection of 80 mg of methylprednisolone acetate have been reported.37,38

Based on the literature findings, the Danish Agency of Medicines recommended and endorsed for the two different glucocorticoid interventions to administer 1) orally 30 mg of prednisolone once daily for seven days or 2) intramuscularly 80 mg of methylprednisolone acetate on first day of intervention only, knowingly that the pharmacological effects of the two different glucocorticoid compounds on insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism may not be directly congruent.

During the intervention trial period (April 2017 to March 2018), participants were examined at two visits with seven days apart (Figure 1B). Participants in the oral group (PO group) took 30 mg of prednisolone (5 mg + 25 mg tablets, Prednisolone “DAK”, Orifarm Healthcare) orally every morning with breakfast from day 1 to day 7. The prednisolone tablets used in our study contain pharmacologically inert excipients, potato starch, gelatin, lactose, magnesium stearate and talcum ensuring tablet integrity and dissolution. The intramuscular injection group (IM group) received a single 2 mL intramuscular injection of 40 mg/mL methylprednisolone acetate (Depo-Medrol, Pfizer) into the gluteus maximus, while the control group (CTL group) received a 2 mL intramuscular injection of isotonic saline water into the gluteus maximus as a placebo on the first day of the trial. Depo-Medrol’s pharmacologically inactive ingredients include trace amount of polyethylene glycol, polysorbate, phosphate buffer, benzyl alcohol, sodium chloride, and sodium hydroxide. In this clinical trial, data collection and analyses were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments, but the two groups (IM and CTL group) that were intramuscular injected were blinded to the participants. On the first day, blood and fecal sampling, along with host phenotyping, were conducted before glucocorticoid administration as baseline measurements for pre-intervention assessment.

To ensure compliance to oral prednisolone intake in the PO group, participants were instructed to download and use the smartphone-app: “drug-reminder”. In addition, daily text messages were sent to all participants to remind them about the upcoming fecal sample collection.

Bioclinical assessments

At the pre-trial eligibility assessment screening and on day 1 and day 8 of the intervention trial, study participants met for phenotyping in the clinical unit in the morning after a 10-h overnight fast. Details of the bioclinical assessments are previously reported.39 Anthropometrics included body weight and height with body mass index (BMI), being calculated by dividing weight (kg) by height squared (m2). Systolic and diastolic blood pressure was measured with an automated sphygmomanometer after participants had rested for 15 min in a lying position. Measurements were repeated three times, and the values were averaged.

Biochemical assessments were performed at Glostrup University Hospital and included ALT analyzed using an enzymatic slide test, and HbA1c measured with liquid chromatography. Plasma creatinine and glucose levels were determined using a colorimetric slide test, while insulin was quantified with immunoassay. Blood leukocyte populations counts were performed using flow cytometry, while ultrasensitive CRP levels were measured with an Image Automatic Immunoassay System (Beckman Coulter). The HOMA-IR index was calculated from fasting plasma glucose and insulin concentrations according to Matthews et al.40 The Matsuda insulin sensitivity index41 was calculated based on plasma concentrations of glucose and insulin obtained during an OGTT. The OGTT was performed in the fasting state on day 1 and day 8 of the trial. Participants consumed a 75 g glucose solution in 250 mL of cold water over five minutes. Venous blood samples were drawn at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min during the OGTT, and the blood was analyzed for concentrations of plasma glucose and plasma insulin. The levels of various cytokines in plasma were measured at The Technical University of Denmark using methods previously reported.42

Stool sample collection, gut bacterial cell counting, and fecal DNA extraction

Stool samples were collected on days 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 of intervention. The sampling was done in accordance with the International Human Microbiome Standards guidelines (SOP 03 V1). Feces were collected at home in provided kits and immediately frozen and stored at −20°C. Within 4–24 h, the samples were transported on dry ice to be stored at −80°C in a central biobank at University of Copenhagen.

Total bacterial cell counting in the fecal sample was performed using staining and flow cytometry as described.43 To ensure accurate assessment of bacterial species abundance, data on bacterial cell counts was used for quantitative microbial profiling as previously reported.43 DNA extraction from aliquots of fecal samples was performed by using the NucleoSpin Soil DNA extraction kit (Machery-Nagel) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Metagenomic sequencing

The DNA concentration was measured with a fluorometer (Qubit Fluorometer, Invitrogen). Sample integrity and purity were checked by Agarose Gel Electrophoresis (concentration of agarose gel: 1%; Voltage: 150 V, Electrophoresis time: 40 min). Genomic DNA (1 μg) was randomly fragmented by an ME220 Focused-ultrasonicator (Covaris). The genomic DNA fragments were selected with magnetic beads to an average size of 200–400 base pairs (bp). Fragments were end-repaired and then 3′ adenylated. Adaptors were ligated to the ends of these 3′ adenylated fragments. This process was undertaken to amplify fragments with adapters from previous steps. PCR products were purified with magnetic beads. The double stranded PCR products were heat-denatured and circularized by the splint oligo sequence. The single strand circle DNAs were formatted as the final library. The library was amplified with phi29 DNA polymerase (New England BioLabs) to make DNA nanoball that has more than 300 copies of one molecular. The DNA nanoballs were loaded into the patterned nanoarray and paired-end 100/150 bases reads were generated in the way of combinatorial Probe-Anchor Synthesis. The DNA was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform delivering 150bp paired-end sequence reads.

Metagenomic data processing

Metagenome sequencing data preprocessing was performed as previously described.44 Quality control of raw FASTQ files was performed using KneadData (version 0.6.1) to remove low-quality bases and host-derived reads. Trimmomatic45 (version 0.36) was used for quality trimming, which involved the removal of Nextera adapters, as well as leading and trailing bases with a Phred score below 20. Reads shorter than 100 bases after trimming were discarded.

Metagenomic sequencing data from our study were processed using the biobakery 3 pipeline,29 which integrates a comprehensive collection of 99,200 high-quality, fully annotated microbial genomes representing 16,800 species, alongside 87.3 million UniRef9046 functional annotations from UniProt (as of January 2019). Bacterial microbiota composition of all metagenomic samples was analyzed using MetaPhlAn v4.0.6, which employs a library of clade-specific markers to generate a pan-microbial profile. The viral and fungal gut microbiota was profiled by Phanta28 (version 1.1.0) and MetaPhlAn v4.0.6, respectively using default settings.

For analysis of microbiome functional potentials, we employed HUMAnN v3.8, following its default settings. HUMAnN3 works by mapping metagenomic reads to the pan genomes47 of species identified in the taxonomic profiling step. These pangenomes are pre-annotated with UniRef90 families providing a comprehensive, non-redundant protein sequence database. Reads that fail to align with a known pangenome were mapped separately to the entire UniRef90 database through translated search using DIAMOND.48 The quality of each alignment and the length of the sequences were factored into the scoring, and hits were categorized by species as well as unclassified hits to generate total counts for each protein family, measured in reads per kilobase units. These counts were then normalized to counts per million (cpm) units to correct for variations in sequencing depth across samples. The output consists of abundance profiles for gene families (UniRef90s) with stratification by species, which were further aggregated into higher-level gene categories, such as MetaCyc pathways or Enzyme Commission numbers (ECs).

Prediction of microbial metabolites from gut metagenomes

We predicted the microbial metabolite composition from metagenomic sequencing data using MelonnPan13 v0.99. This process began with the reannotation of publicly available PRISM metagenomic data,14 utilizing the updated HUMAnN3 database. Following this, we retrained the MelonnPan elastic net regularization model using 10-fold cross-validation. This model was then applied to identify which UniRef90 features from HUMAnN3 were predictive of the metabolite composition. The identified predictive features were then used to estimate the composite metabolome for the present study, based on over 8,000 metabolite features previously characterized in the PRISM dataset. As a result, the updated prediction model enabled the identification of 70 microbial metabolites in this study.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). p value was calculated by two-tailed Student’s t tests or Wilcoxon tests according to the data types. Statistical methods were depicted in figure legends. All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R (version 4.3.2). Given limited statistical power in our cohort arms (n = 18–19 per arm), we applied an FDR-adjusted threshold of adjusted p-value < 0.1 for exploratory analyses, prioritizing sensitivity.

Analysis of differences in host phenotypic markers and overall gut microbial richness

Baseline biomarker values among the three study groups (PO, IM, and CTL) were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess homogeneity across the groups prior to intervention, with a significance level set at 0.1.

To evaluate changes in host phenotypic markers and overall microbial richness between day 1 and days 2, 4, 6, and 8, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method, with a significance threshold of 0.1.

For intra-individual distance analysis, Bray-Curtis distance was calculated based on the relative abundance of bacterial species between day 1 and days 2, 4, 6, and 8. To assess if glucocorticoid exposure led to greater microbiota shifts relative to natural fluctuations observed in the control group, intra-individual distances at the same time points were compared between glucocorticoid intervention groups (PO and IM groups) and CTL group.

Analyses of temporal changes in prevalence of gut bacterial species

Differential analysis of the prevalence of gut bacterial species was performed using the mcnemar’s test function in R to evaluate changes in bacterial species prevalence across the intervention time points (day 2, day 4, day 6, and day 8) relative to day 1 (start of intervention). Each bacterial feature was analyzed separately within the PO group, IM group and CTL group. McNemar’s test was applied when discordant pairs were observed between the pre-intervention and intervention timepoints. Effect sizes were calculated as the net difference between transitions from absence to presence and vice versa, normalized by the total number of participants in each group. p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method, with a significance threshold of 0.1. To identify bacterial species with specific prevalence changes within the PO and IM groups, features detected in the CTL group were excluded to account for natural prevalence variations.

Analyses of temporal changes in relative abundance of gut bacterial microbiota features

Relative abundance of gut microbiota features, including bacterial species, MetaCyc pathways, EC features, and predicted microbial metabolites, significantly associated with the PO and IM glucocorticoid interventions were identified through a multi-step analysis approach. Features were considered significant if they met the following criteria: (1) they were identified as significant in the initial linear mixed-effects model analysis; (2) remained significant after adjusting for natural fluctuations observed in the control group; (3) continued to show significant differences at least at one intervention time point compared to the study baseline (day 1). This systematic approach ensured that only robust, intervention-specific microbial changes were identified.

Firstly, separate linear mixed models implemented in the MaAsLin2 package were constructed for the PO and IM and CTL groups. Subject IDs were treated as random effects, while timepoint and total bacterial cell load were included as fixed effects. The relative abundance of microbiota features was treated as the dependent variable in the models to identify significant associations between abundance and timepoint. The input data were log-transformed to stabilize variance and to normalize the dataset. To focus the analysis on common, relatively high-abundance features, selection criteria were applied with minimum prevalence thresholds of 0.2 and minimum abundance levels of 0.1% for bacterial species, 50 counts for MetaCyc pathways and EC features, and 0 for predicted microbial metabolites. Random gut bacterial microbiota fluctuations observed in the control group were subtracted to isolate features specifically associated with glucocorticoid interventions.

To further analyze intervention effects, paired t-tests were conducted to compare the relative abundance of microbiota features identified as significant in the linear mixed-effects models, with an adjusted p-value of less than 0.1. Comparisons were made between day 1 and multiple intervention timepoints (days 2, 4, 6, and 8).

Association analyses of host phenotypic markers and relative abundance of gut bacterial species

Association analysis was conducted to identify relationships between host phenotypic markers and bacterial species that over the trial period exhibited significant changes in relative abundance. The analysis was performed separately for the PO and IM groups using the lmer function from the lme4 package in R. The analysis incorporated repeated measurements from day 1 and day 8, with subject ID included as a random effect to account for correlation between samples from the same subjects. Bacterial cell load was added as a covariate to adjust for differences in gut bacterial density. Prior to analysis, all data were standardized using the scale function in R to mitigate the influence of differing measurement scales. Each host phenotypic marker was analyzed individually against the relative abundance of the specific bacterial species to identify significant associations.
