Delayed presentation of blunt scrotal trauma, an avoidable cause of testicular necrosis in an adolescent: a case report
Authors: Florent Tshibwid A Zeng, Vincent de Paul Kaoma Cabala, Georgia Mwange Bibi Feza, Pitchou Mbey Mukaz, Sébastien Mbuyi-Musanzayi, Catherine Saleh Ugumba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and clinical importance:Testicular trauma is a rare cause of acute scrotum, mainly affecting adolescents. It frequently occurs during sporting activities, and ultrasound enables the diagnosis of testicular lesions and the selection of appropriate treatment.Case presentation:We report the case of a 15-year-old admitted 2 weeks after a left blunt scrotal trauma. Preoperative scrotal ultrasound diagnosed a hematocele with blood flow interruption. The surgical exploration found an intratesticular hematoma with necrosis of the entire left testis. Prophylactic right testicular orchidopexy was performed, along with high-ligation left orchidectomy. The postoperative outcome was unremarkable 4 months after surgery.Clinical discussion:When testicular trauma occurs, diagnosis should be made in the early stages, as surgical management before 72 hours has a lower risk for orchidectomy. But hidden trauma and financial constraints may result in delayed diagnosis with subsequent darkening functional prognosis.Conclusion:Despite a theoretically good prognosis of intratesticular hematoma, delayed diagnosis due to socioeconomic factors may lead to testicular necrosis and subsequent avoidable orchidectomy.

Introduction

Testicular trauma due to blunt scrotal trauma is a rare cause of acute scrotum, accounting for less than 2% of pediatric cases[1]. It typically occurs in adolescents with sports, falls from heights, and motor vehicle accidents being the leading causes[2,3]. Various testicular injuries derive from blunt scrotal trauma, including testicular fracture, intratesticular hematoma, testicular contusion, testicular torsion, and testicular dislocation[2].

Diagnosis of testicular trauma is usually straightforward with a typical history and findings of physical examination. However, in 10% of cases, the trauma is undisclosed to parents, leading to delayed diagnosis and management, and endangering the functional prognosis[2]. Scrotal ultrasound makes it possible to diagnose the precise testicular injury and indicate the kind of management, which can be conservative or surgical, in emergency or delayed[3]. Ultrasound and surgical exploration allow to classify lesions into five grades, according to the American Association of Surgery for Trauma (AAST)[4]. In some injuries like intratesticular hematoma and testicular contusion, delayed management is fatal as increased intrascrotal pressure may result in ischemia and testicular necrosis due to the same mechanism as in compartment syndrome[2]. Authors reported a substantial reduction of orchidectomy with surgical exploration within the first 72 hours[2]. Case presentation

A 15-year-old adolescent was brought to our department for painful scrotal swelling. Symptoms started 2 weeks ago, when playing football, a direct trauma occurred when the ball hit his scrotum. Over hours, painful scrotal swelling developed, for which the patient took oral paracetamol. Since the patient was forbidden to play football by his parents, he concealed the accident from them. Over 4 days, pain persisted, but the swelling, which had progressively increased since the trauma, stabilized in size, and then he divulged the accident to his parents. Due to financial constraints, the patient did not present to any health facility. Then, oral paracetamol was continued, with slight pain regression but swelling persisted, which made the parents finally bring the patient to our department 2 weeks after trauma. He had no significant medical history.

On physical examination, his vitals were within normal range, without fever. Examination of the external genitalia revealed left-sided painful scrotal swelling, with augmentation of the volume of the intrascrotal left testis, which was painful, noted at five out of ten by the patient (Fig. 2). Cremasteric reflex was present, Angell sign was absent, governor sign was absent. Hypotheses were: post-traumatic neglected testicular (a) contusion, (b) hematocele, (c) torsion, or (d) resolved dislocation. Figure 2.Clinical aspect. Note the left scrotal swelling. Due to delayed presentation, no bruising or other cutaneous evidence of trauma could be noticed.

Emergency scrotal ultrasound was ordered to assess testicular blood flow and to assess testicular injury. It found a normal right scrotum, but interrupted blood flow to the left testis, with hypoechoic testicular parenchyma and subsequent hematocele. The preoperative diagnosis was left testicular contusion complicated with blood flow interruption and hematocele. A semi-elective scrotal exploration + right prophylactic orchidopexy ± left orchiectomy was planned in 24 hours. The patient and his parents previously had a meeting with the clinical psychologist to prepare them for the potential orchidectomy, to which they consented preoperatively.

Under spinal anesthesia, the testes were approached through the median raphe. Exploration revealed a normal right testis, with a hydatid of Morgagni, which was resected. Orchidopexy was performed by triangulation, using polyglactin 4/0, followed by closure in layers. Exploration of the left side revealed a swollen testis (6 ×4 cm), with a bruising area on the lower pole of the tunica vaginalis. No hematocele was intraoperatively identified. Incision of the albuginea showed a hematoma on the lower pole of the testis, with infarcted testicular parenchyma. No evidence of testicular torsion was noted. We classified the injury as AAST grade I (contusion/hematoma). Left orchidectomy with high ligation was performed (Fig. 3), and the wound was closed in layers. Figure 3.Intraoperative findings. In (A), a bruising on the tunica vaginalis was noticed, and no bleeding on incision of the albuginea (in B). A hematoma was identified at the lower pole of the testis with devitalization of the testicular parenchyma (C). High-ligation orchidectomy was performed (D).

Postoperatively, the patient received intravenous paracetamol for 5 days and was discharged on postoperative day 5, without any complications. Microscopical examination of the ablated left testis showed features of coagulative necrosis and infarction extended to the whole testis, with loss of the normal architecture of the seminiferous tubules and Leydig cells. There were areas of extravasation of erythrocytes and polynuclear leukocytes infiltration, and hemosiderin deposits. Taking into account intraoperative findings and pathology results, we retained post-traumatic testicular contusion complicated with necrosis. Four months postoperatively, the patient is free of any scrotal symptoms. Testicular prosthesis was denied by the parents due to cultural reasons.

Discussion

We reported a case of an adolescent with blunt scrotal trauma resulting in intratesticular trauma, an injury with a theoretically good prognosis. However, delayed presentation resulted in testicular necrosis and subsequent orchidectomy.

Blunt testicular trauma is an exceptional cause of acute scrotum, most often found in adolescents[2], as in our patient. Several circumstances of occurrence were reported, with sports being the most predominant in different series[3]. Among incriminated sports, football is the most frequent[2], as found in our patient. This may be due to the fact that it is the most popular sport in majority of countries and by the fact that while playing football, legs are usually wide-opened, which exposes the genitalia to a blow by the ball. Our patient reported this mechanism.

Due to pain and swelling that alter normal walking after blunt scrotal trauma, delayed diagnosis due to hidden scrotal trauma is rarely reported, accounting for less than 10% of cases, and when delayed diagnosis occurs, all patients reach the health facility within 72 hours[2,6]. In our case, the 2-week delay is due to a double factor: undisclosed trauma and financial constraints. The latter confirms that access to surgical care when needed is still challenging in low-income countries such as the DRC[7]. Due to social facts (injury occurring due to a forbidden activity or a taboo to talk about genitalia), adolescents may hide trauma from parents, leading to delayed diagnosis, which can be of disastrous consequences, as in our patient.

In case of scrotal trauma, differentials include: scrotal hydrocele and hematocele, testicular hematoma, testicular fracture, testicular rupture, testicular torsion, and testicular dislocation[3]. When some of these can clinically be suspected, others can only be accurately identified by scrotal ultrasound, as pain and scrotal swelling are not always proportionate to testicular injury[8]. In fact, patients with scrotal hematocele or hydrocele will present with a renitent scrotal swelling, with positive transillumination[2]. In case of post-traumatic testicular torsion, classical findings of physical examination are: scrotal swelling, positive Governor’s sign, and absent cremasteric reflex[2]. When the scrotum is empty without a history of undescended testis, palpation of the homolateral inguinal region may reveal the gonad. Such a finding is typical of testicular dislocation[2]. Despite history and physical examination, scrotal ultrasound evaluation of scrotal content, testicular integrity, and blood flow is crucial for an accurate diagnosis in a patient with blunt scrotal trauma[8,9].

Despite that, scrotal US with color Doppler can be misleading, as in our patient, in experienced hands, it is the investigation of choice[9]. By providing an accurate diagnosis, it guides the patient’s management towards conservative or surgical management (emergency or delayed)[9]. In our patient, an intratesticular hematoma was mistaken for a hematocele. We believe this can be due to the lack of experience of the ultrasound operator, as an acute hematocele presents as an echoic, while a chronic hematocele presents as a hypoechoic collection between the parietal and visceral layers of the tunica vaginalis[9]. Intratesticular hematoma presents as a hyperechoic focal lesion in the testicular parenchyma that becomes hypoechoic over time[9].

Current guidelines recommend conservative management of hematocele. However, large ones (three times larger than the contralateral testis) must be surgically managed within 3 days due to increased risk of necrosis and subsequent orchidectomy[8,10–12]. In our patient, we indicated surgical exploration due to persistent pain and interrupted blood flow, probably due to compressive hematocele. But intraoperatively, an intratesticular hematoma was found. When an intratesticular hematoma is diagnosed, it is important to determine whether it is associated with testicular ischemia. In an isolated intratesticular hematoma, close follow-up with Doppler US must be done until complete resolution. Whenever an intratesticular hematoma is compressive, with testicular vascular changes, emergency surgical exploration is recommended[8,11,12]. When left untreated, compressive intratesticular hematoma increases pressure within the albuginea, resulting in low testicular perfusion, such as in classical compartment syndrome in limbs[2]. The final consequence is testicular necrosis as seen in our patient, with subsequent avoidable orchidectomy. The latter is performed in nearly 5% of testicular trauma in grade AAST IV and V injuries[9].

Strengths and limitations

We reported a rare case of testicular necrosis due to intratesticular hematoma, a lesion theoretically with good potential for testicular salvage. In this report, we highlighted that delayed presentation may worsen the prognosis. The main limitation is the lack of microscopical images, which would enrich the iconography of the present work.

Patient perspective

The patient was satisfied that the surgical intervention relieved his pain and that the other testis was safe.

Conclusion

Blunt scrotal trauma in an adolescent can result in testicular trauma. Due to socioeconomic determinants (hidden trauma and financial constraints), delayed diagnosis can result in poor prognosis even for injuries with a theoretically good prognosis. This case of intratesticular hematoma leading to orchidectomy is an example. Patient education and improvement of pediatric surgery access in low-income countries would help avoid such situations.
