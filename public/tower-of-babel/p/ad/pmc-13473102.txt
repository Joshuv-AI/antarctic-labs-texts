AI-guided CRISPR screening reveals therapeutic targets in psoriasis
Authors: Chenlin Zhao, Mushaine Shih, Sharif Ahmed, Steven Song, Amber Lennon, Julia M Mayes, Mengqi Jonathan Fan, Pei-Ying Lo, Jason Perera, Alanur Tutar, Andrew Kang, Elizabeth Warren, Ryan McClure, Aly A Khan, Shana O Kelley, Abdalla M Abdrabou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsoriasis affects over 125 million people globally. Biologics targeting the IL-17/IL-17RA axis are effective but require systemic administration, are costly, and are unsuitable for some patients. Developing topical small-molecule alternatives requires a better understanding of how IL-17 receptor A (IL17RA) is regulated in keratinocytes, the principal effector cells of psoriatic lesions. Here, we report a genome-wide CRISPR knockout screen for regulators of surface IL17RA in primary human epidermal keratinocytes. We prioritize hits using experimental enrichment together with VirtualCRISPR, a language-model framework trained on functional-genomics data, and validate two regulators with minimal prior connection to IL17RA: 5-lipoxygenase (ALOX5) and the oxytocin receptor (OXTR), which act through distinct cell-intrinsic mechanisms. Topical zileuton, an ALOX5 inhibitor, and cligosiban, an OXTR antagonist, suppress imiquimod-induced psoriasiform dermatitis in mice, mirroring systemic anti-IL17RA antibody efficacy. By linking AI- guided selection to genetic perturbation screening, this study provides an efficient route from candidate gene nomination to biological validation and therapeutic discovery.Subject terms: Mutagenesis, Target identification, Target validation, CRISPR-Cas systems, Inflammation

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory skin disorder affecting 2–3% of the global population, characterized by keratinocyte hyperproliferation and immune dysregulation1,2. The IL-17/IL17RA axis is a critical driver of psoriasis pathogenesis, and biologics targeting this axis, such as secukinumab, ixekizumab, and brodalumab, demonstrate remarkable clinical efficacy3,4. However, the cellular mechanisms that govern IL-17 signaling in keratinocytes, the principal effector cells in psoriatic lesions, remain incompletely understood.

While biologics have revolutionized psoriasis treatment, their requirement for systemic administration, high cost, and potential immunogenicity limit long-term use5. Topical corticosteroids, the mainstay of localized therapy, cause tachyphylaxis and skin atrophy with chronic use6. These constraints underscore an urgent need for novel small-molecule therapeutics amenable to topical delivery.

Functional genomics, particularly CRISPR-Cas9 screening, has transformed our ability to systematically dissect disease mechanisms and identify therapeutics7,8. However, despite the central role of keratinocytes in psoriasis, genome-wide CRISPR screens in primary human keratinocytes remain technically challenging due to limited proliferative capacity and low transduction efficiency9. While recent studies have begun to employ primary human keratinocytes in genetic screens, a comprehensive genome-wide CRISPR knockout screen in primary human adult keratinocytes has not previously been reported, potentially obscuring keratinocyte-specific regulatory mechanisms10,11.

Here, we perform a genome-wide CRISPR knockout screen in primary human adult epidermal keratinocytes (HEKa cells), identifying regulators of IL17RA surface expression (Fig. 1a). To navigate the complex landscape of screening hits and extract novel hits that may reveal completely new targeting approaches, we employ a large language model-based framework to annotate candidates by their divergence from established literature (Fig. 1b, c)12. We then identify clinically validated drugs for repurposing against the highest-novelty hits and validate them using in vitro multi-omic profiling and in vivo murine models (Fig. 1d). Topical delivery of cligosiban, an OXTR antagonist, and zileuton, an ALOX5 inhibitor, achieves efficacy comparable to systemic anti-IL17RA antibody in the imiquimod-induced psoriasis model, linking neurohormonal and lipid signaling to IL-17-driven inflammation. This integrated platform, combining primary-cell functional genomics, large language model-based hit prioritization, and multi-omics validation, provides a generalizable blueprint for rational drug discovery in complex inflammatory diseases.

Results

Genome-wide CRISPR KO screen reveals regulators of IL17RA

To systematically identify regulators of surface IL17RA expression in HEKa, we developed a FACS-based CRISPR screening approach enabling isolation of two distinct cellular populations representing the top and bottom ~5% of surface IL17RA-expressing cells (Fig. 1a). Preliminary validation using wild-type (WT) and IL17RA knockout cells confirmed the ability of the sorting to reproducibly separate WT and low surface IL17RA-expressing populations (Supplementary Fig. 1).

HEKa cells are often refractory to genetic manipulation, posing substantial challenges for large-scale perturbation screens13. To address this, we conducted preliminary experiments optimizing transduction efficiency while maintaining cell viability and library representation. Transduction enhancers, such as polybrene, markedly inhibited HEKa proliferation even at low concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c), thereby excluding them from all transduction protocols. Although some chemical modulators, such as ROCK1 inhibitors, can overcome the growth-shunting effect, they may introduce pathway-specific biases.

We transduced HEKa cells from two healthy human donors with the human Brunello genome-wide CRISPR knockout library at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of ~0.35, ensuring single-guide representation per cell. Following puromycin selection, the transduced pool was expanded for five population doublings to allow for effective gene editing and depletion of essential genes. We then FACS-sorted the library-transduced population to isolate the top and bottom 5% of IL17RA surface-expressing cells (Fig. 1a). Sorting produced a clear shift in IL17RA expression, confirming robust phenotypic separation (Supplementary Fig. 3). To evaluate library representation, ~25 million cells were cryopreserved immediately after puromycin selection (T₀).

We applied the MAGeCK analysis pipeline to compute false discovery rate (FDR) and log₂ fold-change values, and to identify genes whose knockouts altered IL17RA expression. We retained more than 98% of the sgRNAs at the T0 population, indicating a high library coverage after transduction (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Moreover, consistent gene- and sgRNA-level correlations were observed across donors in the screen (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Using an FDR threshold of 0.1 and requiring at least two sgRNAs per gene, we identified significantly enriched genes in IL17RAlow populations (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Using these criteria, we identified 472 hits in the low-expression bin and 724 hits in the high-expression bin.

As expected, IL17RA sgRNAs were among the top hits enriched in the IL17RAlow population, validating the screen’s performance (Fig. 2a). The screen identified a broad network of genes with established roles in skin inflammation and immune regulation (Supplementary Data 1). Moreover, the identification of chromatin regulators such as KAT5 and ASH2L suggests that IL17RA expression may be governed by epigenetic mechanisms in keratinocytes14,15. We also identified LCE3B, a well-established psoriasis susceptibility gene, as a hit16.

Large language model-based annotation prioritizes non-canonical regulators

While these hits confirm the biological fidelity of the screen, existing psoriasis therapeutics already target well-characterized nodes within the IL-17 axis. To systematically prioritize candidates with high translational value but limited prior association with IL17RA regulation, we applied VirtualCRISPR12, a large language model framework trained on the BioGRID-ORCS database of functional genomic screens, which models the question “Does {perturbation} of {gene} in {cell type} result in {phenotype}?”. VirtualCRISPR independently predicts the probability that perturbation of a given gene in a given cell type will produce a defined phenotype (Fig. 1b).

By mapping experimental enrichment against VirtualCRISPR-predicted probability, we defined two distinct quadrants: known regulators (high experimental enrichment, high AI probability) and high-novelty candidates (high experimental enrichment, low AI probability) (Fig. 1c). This framework functions as an orthogonal layer of prioritization, annotating which experimental hits represent uncharacterized biology without filtering the unbiased primary screen data (Supplementary Data 1). In other words, a high predicted probability indicates that analogous gene-phenotype relationships are well represented in prior literature, whereas low-probability predictions highlight hits that are not readily explained by the model’s learned features, suggesting that they may represent biology extending beyond current functional genomics knowledge.

We selected candidates by combining (i) screen significance (FDR < 0.1, ≥2 sgRNAs), (ii) biological plausibility within the IL17RA inflammatory context, and (iii) pharmacological tractability. The oxytocin receptor (OXTR)17 and arachidonate 5-lipoxygenase (ALOX5)18 met all three criteria, and each received a VirtualCRISPR probability lower than IL17RA itself (Fig. 2b), indicating lower prior support in functional genomic screening literature. ALOX5 falls within the high-novelty, low-prior region, with mechanistic plausibility from its role in arachidonic acid metabolism, and is targeted by the FDA-approved inhibitor zileuton. OXTR occupied an intermediate region of the prior-knowledge distribution but uniquely combined experimental support with direct pharmacological tractability through the selective antagonist cligosiban. CYP3A5, linked to xenobiotic metabolism19, was retained as a lower-priority hit. Together, these candidates implicate neuroendocrine and lipid-metabolic pathways as upstream regulators of IL17RA in keratinocytes.

Pathway enrichment analysis identified the NF-κB signaling pathway, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and Notch signaling as enriched pathways (Fig. 2c). Notably, MAPK and Calcium signaling pathways were also enriched. In addition, the screen identified innate immune pattern recognition as a critical node controlling IL17RA expression in keratinocytes. TLR6, a cell-surface Toll-like receptor that detects microbial components, and SARM1, a TIR-domain-containing adapter that modulates Toll-like receptor signaling, emerged as regulators of IL17RA levels20,21. Analysis of public transcriptomic datasets revealed a consistent positive correlation between ALOX5 and IL17RA expression (r = 0.89) across skin and spleen tissues, highlighting a robust transcriptional association between these genes (Supplementary Fig. 5a). OXTR showed a moderate positive correlation, whereas CYP3A5 showed a moderate negative correlation (r = 0.41 and r = −0.42, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c).

ALOX5 and OXTR are cell-intrinsic regulators of IL17RA in keratinocytes

To validate the screen and assess the functional relevance of identified regulators, we performed targeted perturbation of OXTR, ALOX5, and CYP3A5. Using CRISPR-Cas9 knockout with two sgRNAs per gene, we perturbed each locus in primary human adult epidermal keratinocytes derived from two independent donors to control for interindividual variability. Across all replicates and sgRNA pairs, genetic ablation of these genes significantly reduced IL17RA surface abundance, as quantified by flow cytometry (Fig. 2d), validating these genes as bona fide regulators of IL17RA expression in primary human adult keratinocytes.

To determine whether reduced IL17RA expression translated into functional dampening of downstream inflammatory signaling, we next measured cytokine secretion by ELISA. Knockout of ALOX5 and OXTR in HEKa reduced secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including CXCL1, IL-6, and IL-1β (Fig. 2e), providing functional evidence of attenuated inflammatory output.

In line with these secretion data, we asked whether these perturbations act through coordinated changes in keratinocyte biology or require immune cell intermediates. To address this, we analyzed proteomic data from primary human keratinocytes to isolate epithelial-intrinsic responses.

Proteomic profiling following ALOX5 knockout revealed coordinated changes in inflammatory and epithelial structural programs relevant to psoriatic disease biology (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Data 2). Increased abundance of IVL and Periplakin indicated a shift toward a more differentiated keratinocyte state associated with barrier function. In parallel, decreased abundance of LPCAT2 and CYP2S1 was consistent with reduced inflammatory lipid metabolism and attenuation of pathways linked to keratinocyte activation and hyperproliferation.

Likewise, proteomic profiling of OXTR knockout revealed substantial changes in psoriasis-associated inflammatory pathways (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Data 3). Notably, S100A8 and S100A9, well-characterized alarmins in psoriatic skin, were among the most reduced proteins, along with S100A4. These changes are consistent with decreased activation of keratinocyte-derived inflammatory programs linked to neutrophil recruitment and amplification of skin inflammation. Reduced abundance of ISG15, MX1, and IFIT3 further indicated attenuation of a type I interferon-responsive program, a feature repeatedly implicated in psoriatic keratinocyte pathobiology.

Finally, we characterized the functional consequences of ALOX5 and OXTR on cell proliferation. We performed proliferation assays examining the effects of both genetic ablation and pharmacological inhibition on keratinocyte and immune cell growth kinetics. Neither candidate gene knockout nor treatment with their respective small-molecule inhibitors significantly altered the proliferative capacity of either cell type (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating that the observed phenotypic effects are unlikely to be attributable to changes in cell viability or growth. Consistent with expectations, pharmacological treatment resulted in a significant reduction in IL17RA protein levels, confirming effective target engagement (Supplementary Figs. 7a and 8a).

Collectively, these findings support two key conclusions. First, ALOX5 and OXTR exert keratinocyte-intrinsic effects on keratinocytes. Second, these perturbations induce coordinated proteomic remodeling characterized by the suppression of inflammatory pathways and cytokine production alongside induction of differentiation-associated programs, consistent with a shift toward a more homeostatic epithelial state. Taken together, these data support the conclusion that perturbation of ALOX5 and OXTR reprograms keratinocytes toward a less inflammatory, more differentiated state through epithelial-intrinsic mechanisms. Having established that OXTR and ALOX5 regulate IL17RA expression and keratinocyte inflammatory output through cell-intrinsic mechanisms, we next asked whether pharmacological inhibition of these targets could suppress psoriatic inflammation in vivo.

Inhibition of OXTR and ALOX5 in the IMQ-induced psoriasiform model

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of OXTR and ALOX5 inhibition in vivo, we formulated zileuton and cligosiban as topical gels using Carbomer 940 as the delivery matrix22. Carbomer 940 was selected for its rapid absorption, ease of application, and lack of inherent anti-inflammatory activity (Supplementary Fig. 9a–d). We employed the imiquimod (IMQ)-induced psoriasis model, a well-validated preclinical murine model for studying IL-17-driven skin inflammation23. Mice received zileuton gel, cligosiban gel, anti-IL17RA antibody, vehicle gel, or IMQ only. The topical IMQ only (5% Aldara cream) control group was not treated with a therapeutic intervention and only received vehicle gel (Fig. 3a). We monitored disease severity daily using Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) scoring and harvested skin on day 7 for flow cytometric immunophenotyping and proteomic analysis.

Quantitative assessment using the PASI showed that topical OXTR or ALOX5 inhibition substantially reduced IMQ-induced inflammation (Fig. 3b). Cligosiban and zileuton significantly reduced cumulative PASI scores relative to IMQ-only controls, with clinical improvement evident by Day 5 and sustained to almost full resolution by Day 7 (Fig. 3c). Both compounds achieved efficacy comparable to systemic anti-IL17RA antibody in this model, indicating that topical targeting can recapitulate key features of systemic blockade.

Quantification of epidermal thickness revealed significant normalization of acanthosis in both topical treatment groups (Fig. 3d). The magnitude of epidermal resolution was comparable to that observed with antibody treatment, reflecting effective suppression of keratinocyte IL-17-driven pathology.

To investigate the immunological basis of disease improvement, we quantified immune cell infiltration in the skin by flow cytometry. Both topical inhibitors significantly reduced Th17 and Tc17 cell populations (Fig. 3e, f). Notably, cligosiban was the only treatment that significantly increased the frequency of CD4⁺ regulatory T cells (Tregs). Zileuton and anti-IL17RA antibody treatment were associated with modest, non-significant increases in Treg frequency (Fig. 3g).

Analysis of the myeloid compartment revealed a pronounced shift toward an anti-inflammatory, M2-like phenotype. Notably, CD206⁺ and Arg1⁺ M2 macrophages were significantly enriched following topical treatment with cligosiban or zileuton, whereas systemic IL17RA antibody administration selectively increased Arg1⁺ M2 macrophages (Fig. 3h, i). Consistent with in vitro observations, IL17RA⁺ epidermal cells were markedly reduced in mice receiving either topical treatment compared to IMQ-only controls, confirming that both compounds downregulate receptor expression in vivo (Fig. 3j). Evaluation of systemic inflammatory burden through spleen weight measurements showed that IMQ induced splenomegaly across all treatment groups relative to healthy controls, indicating sustained systemic immune activation (Fig. 3k)24. Finally, Ki67 staining demonstrated that all treatments effectively reduced epidermal proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 9e), collectively supporting restoration of tissue homeostasis.

To evaluate whether combined targeting of OXTR and ALOX5 could further enhance therapeutic efficacy, we performed a combinatorial treatment experiment using both cligosiban and zileuton gels (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Dual inhibition produced a degree of disease amelioration similar to that observed with individual treatments, with no apparent loss of efficacy. Quantification of epidermal thickness further demonstrated significant normalization of acanthosis, with the magnitude of epidermal resolution comparable to that observed with antibody treatment reflecting effective suppression of keratinocyte hyperproliferation (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b), and IL-17-driven pathology (Supplementary Fig. 10c). Evaluation of systemic inflammatory burden through spleen weight measurements revealed persistent IMQ-induced splenomegaly across all treatment groups compared to healthy controls, indicating sustained systemic immune activation (Supplementary Fig. 10d). Immunophenotypic analysis revealed a consistent immune remodeling profile, characterized by significant reductions in both Th17 and Tc17 cell infiltration, along with a shift in the myeloid compartment toward an anti-inflammatory, M2-like macrophage phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 10e–h). These findings indicate that simultaneous inhibition of OXTR and ALOX5 recapitulates the anti-inflammatory effects observed with single-agent treatment without diminishing therapeutic benefit.

Together, these results show that topical administration of cligosiban and zileuton effectively suppresses psoriatic inflammation through multiple mechanisms: reducing IL17RA expression, suppressing pathogenic Th17/Tc17 responses, and polarizing macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype. The multi-pronged immunomodulation observed establishes proof-of-concept for topical small-molecule therapy targeting keratinocyte-specific pathways in psoriasis.

Proteomic characterization of responses to IL17RA, ALOX5, and OXTR targeting

To understand global proteomic changes induced by each treatment, we analyzed dorsal skin tissue. Examining the proteins with significant differential abundance across treatments provides mechanistic granularity to the global proteomic trends. Anti-IL17RA antibody treatment in mice suppressed a multi-layered effector program encompassing neutrophil-associated antimicrobial proteins (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Data 4) (ELANE, CTSG, PRTN3) and alarmins (S100A8, S100A9) and the neutrophil-derived iron-sequestration protein LCN2, that collectively drive psoriasiform inflammation25,26. The coordinate downregulation of keratinocyte-derived antimicrobial peptides (CAMP, DEFB3) and neutrophil-associated granule constituents (LTF, PGLYRP1) supports attenuation of the IL-17-driven keratinocyte-neutrophil inflammatory loop at both epithelial and myeloid levels, consistent with effective on-target pathway suppression.

Matrix metalloproteinases (MMP8, MMP9) and neutrophil-specific markers (NGP, CD177) were similarly suppressed, indicating reduced tissue remodeling and neutrophil infiltration27. Furthermore, the upregulation of late differentiation markers (IVL, SBSN, SCEL), together with desmosomal adhesion proteins (DSG1A) and extracellular matrix components (FIBULIN-1, FMOD, SERPINF1), indicates improved intercellular cohesion and barrier restoration.

Zileuton treatment recapitulated many of the anti-inflammatory effects observed with IL17RA blockade (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Data 5), including suppression of neutrophil effectors (ELANE, CTSG, PRTN3, CD177), antimicrobial peptides and alarmins (CAMP, DEFB3, S100A8, S100A9), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP8, MMP9). Consistent with effective ALOX5 pathway inhibition, zileuton treatment reduced the abundance of most components of the leukotriene biosynthetic pathway in psoriatic skin, including ALOX5, ALOX5AP, PLA2G4B, PLA2G4E, and LTA4H. In line with reduced oxidative effector capacity, zileuton decreased components of the NADPH-oxidase complex (NCF1, NCF2, NCF4).

To validate this circuit mechanistically, we interrogated the relationship between ALOX5 and IL17RA in HEKa. ALOX5 knockout resulted in IL17RA downregulation compared to non-targeting control (NTC) cells (Supplementary Fig. 7b), while exogenous Leukotriene B4 (LTB4), a downstream leukotriene mediator of ALOX5 activity, dose-dependently increased IL17RA surface expression (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Surface flow cytometry confirmed that zileuton treatment significantly reduced IL17RA surface levels (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Notably, this reduction was fully rescued by dynasore, a dynamin inhibitor that impairs dynamin-dependent endocytic trafficking, indicating that dynamin-dependent internalization contributes to the regulation of IL17RA surface expression (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Together, these findings support a model in which leukotriene activity helps maintain IL17RA surface availability, at least in part by limiting receptor internalization, although contributions from altered recycling or biosynthesis cannot be excluded.

Concurrent suppression of vesicular trafficking regulators, including RAB32, a GTPase involved in lysosome-related organelle biogenesis28, and the TRAPP complex subunit TRAPPC3, which participates in ER-to-Golgi vesicle trafficking29, suggests broad alterations in membrane trafficking pathways that could influence receptor surface replenishment. Consistent with dynamin-dependent endocytic trafficking, this signature suggests altered membrane dynamics that may reduce IL17RA surface availability under zileuton treatment. Defining the relative contributions of receptor recycling and degradation will refine this mechanistic model.

Cligosiban treatment elicited a proteomic response that phenocopied IL17RA blockade on core psoriasiform effectors (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Data 6), including neutrophil proteases (ELANE, CTSG, PRTN3), antimicrobial peptides and alarmins (CAMP, DEFB3, S100A8, S100A9), yet established a distinctive anti-inflammatory axis. Like zileuton and IL17RA blockade, cligosiban suppressed multiple core components of the NADPH oxidase complex (NCF1, NCF2, NCF4, CYBA, CYBB) and leukotriene biosynthetic enzymes (ALOX5, ALOX5AP, PLA2G4B, PLA2G4D, PLA2G4E), demonstrating shared control over oxidative burst and eicosanoid metabolism.

However, cligosiban induced a far more comprehensive metabolic reprogramming signature than ALOX5 pathway inhibition. Cligosiban uniquely reduced the abundance of multiple enzymes across the mevalonate–cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, including the rate-limiting enzyme HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR) alongside downstream sterol synthesis machinery (HMGCS1, MVD, FDPS, FDFT1, SQLE, LSS, CYP51A1, SC5D, HSD17B7, MSMO1, PMVK). Cligosiban also suppressed sphingolipid biosynthesis enzymes (SPTLC1, SPTLC2, DEGS1, CERS3) and fatty acid elongase (ELOVL1). This coordinated regulation suggests that OXTR signaling modulates membrane lipid biosynthetic programs that sustain inflammatory keratinocyte hyperproliferation and barrier dysfunction30,31.

Indeed, OXTR knockout in HEKa cells resulted in IL17RA downregulation alongside reduced calmodulin levels (Supplementary Fig. 8b). In addition, cligosiban treatment decreased IL17RA level in both a dose- and time-dependent manner while oxytocin treatment increased IL17RA level (Supplementary Fig. 8a, c). However, the effects were abolished by calcium chelation (BAPTA-AM) or calcineurin inhibition (tacrolimus) (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Consistently, pharmacological activation of calcium signaling using ionomycin reversed cligosiban-mediated inhibition of IL17RA expression (Supplementary Fig. 8d), whereas inhibition of NFAT signaling abrogated oxytocin-induced IL17RA upregulation (Supplementary Fig. 8e). These findings suggest that OXTR-mediated calcium mobilization regulates IL17RA expression in a calcineurin-dependent manner, consistent with involvement of the calmodulin–calcineurin–NFAT signaling axis32.

Supporting this mechanism, the cligosiban in vivo proteomic signature revealed coordinated suppression of calcium-activated enzymes (PLA2G4B, PLA2G4D, PLA2G4E, PADI4), and AP-1 components (FOSL2, JUNB), suggesting attenuation of calcium-dependent inflammatory signaling pathways (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Data 6). MARCKS, a calmodulin substrate, was suppressed alongside the calcium-binding protein EFHD2, consistent with attenuation of OXTR-dependent calcium signaling following receptor antagonism.

Cligosiban also reduced multiple inflammatory mediators and components of the AP-1 transcription factor (FOSL2, JUNB), which are known to contribute to inflammatory gene expression programs. Reduced abundance of IL1A, PTGES, CD44, and OSMR further indicates attenuation of pathways involved in inflammatory amplification and cytokine signaling33.

Beyond metabolic and transcriptional regulation, cligosiban was associated with reduced abundance of innate immune and danger-sensing pathway components. The coordinate downregulation of STING1 (cGAS-STING pathway)34, complement anaphylatoxin receptor C5AR1, formyl peptide receptor FPR235, and the alarmin IL-36γ (IL36G) suggests that OXTR antagonism dampens danger signal sensing and inflammatory amplification loops.

The convergent suppression of neutrophil effectors, antimicrobial peptides, and inflammatory mediators across mechanistically distinct interventions reinforces IL17RA as a central signaling node orchestrating psoriasiform inflammation. Moreover, it supports OXTR and ALOX5 as pharmacologically tractable upstream regulators of IL17RA-driven pathology. However, proteomic profiles diverged beyond the shared IL17RA-dependent inflammatory axis, revealing treatment-specific metabolic and transcriptional reprogramming signatures that account for their respective therapeutic efficacy.

Pathway enrichment analysis showed that all three treatments suppressed core inflammatory pathways: IL-17, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, cytokine-cytokine receptor, and chemokine signaling (Fig. 4d, e)36,37. The consistent downregulation of these modules across all treatments validates that each therapeutic approach effectively disrupts the keratinocyte-immune cell inflammatory amplification cascade.

Despite convergence on common inflammatory pathways, each experimental condition maintained unique mechanistic signatures (Fig. 4f, g). Zileuton showed the most pronounced suppression of arachidonic acid metabolism, consistent with ALOX5 inhibition. In contrast, cligosiban selectively downregulated steroid and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis pathways, including key rate-limiting enzymes in cholesterol synthesis, highlighting OXTR-dependent lipid metabolic reprogramming distinct from leukotriene-driven inflammation.

Systemic inflammatory markers contextualize the keratinocyte-identified regulatory axis

Psoriatic inflammation is driven by reciprocal signaling between the epithelial and systemic immune compartments. Having established that ALOX5 and OXTR govern IL17RA expression intrinsically within keratinocytes, we next investigated whether these regulatory nodes participate in the broader systemic inflammatory milieu. We performed exploratory single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with psoriasis and healthy controls (Fig. 5a), generating 40,169 high-quality transcriptomes that resolved into 22 distinct immune cell lineages (Fig. 5b–e). Consistent with established systemic psoriatic signatures, blood from psoriasis patients exhibited expanded populations of monocytes, dendritic cells, and CD8⁺ T cells, alongside a relative reduction in natural killer (NK) cells38 (Fig. 5f).

We next examined the expression profiles of CRISPR screen-validated targets across this systemic immune compartment. ALOX5 expression was upregulated in patients with psoriasis relative to healthy controls, mirroring the global elevation of systemic alarmins (S100A8, S100A9, S100A12)39 (Fig. 5g). By contrast, OXTR expression showed no significant enrichment in patient immune cells, confirming its role as a predominantly epithelial-restricted regulatory mechanism. Notably, CYP3A5 expression is also not significantly enriched in patients’ immune cells.

Because ALOX5 catalyzes the biosynthesis of inflammatory leukotrienes, we next asked whether systemic ALOX5-pathway activity might interface with keratinocyte IL17RA regulation. Supporting this possibility, patient samples exhibited significantly elevated concentrations of leukotriene B4 (LTB4), a direct downstream product of ALOX5 activity (Supplementary Fig. 7e). Together with our in vitro finding that exogenous LTB4 dose-dependently increases keratinocyte IL17RA surface expression (Supplementary Fig. 7c), these data suggest a potential paracrine link between systemic ALOX5-pathway activity and epithelial IL17RA regulation. Thus, the PBMC analysis provides disease context support for the keratinocyte screen without implying identical mechanisms across the immune and epithelial compartments.

Discussion

We report a genome-wide CRISPR screen performed in primary human adult keratinocytes to identify regulators of IL17RA expression. Integrating unbiased functional genomics with AI-driven target prioritization, we identified ALOX5 and OXTR as therapeutically tractable modulators of IL17RA. Topical delivery of their respective inhibitors, zileuton and cligosiban, achieved therapeutic effects comparable to systemic IL17RA blockade in preclinical models. This provides a proof-of-concept for keratinocyte-directed small-molecule strategies targeting upstream regulators of IL-17 signaling. While VirtualCRISPR successfully prioritized targets based on existing functional genomics literature, such AI-driven predictions remain inherently dependent on the scope of their training data and require rigorous downstream biological validation.

ALOX5 emerged as a key regulator of IL17RA surface expression, with the data supporting a contribution from limiting receptor internalization. Pharmacologic inhibition with zileuton promoted IL17RA internalization in a dynamin-dependent manner while reducing neutrophil effectors, NADPH oxidase components, and markers associated with neutrophil extracellular trap formation in vivo. These coordinated effects recapitulated core inflammatory features of systemic IL17RA blockade by inhibiting a single enzymatic pathway. In line with its established role as an ALOX5 inhibitor and its FDA-approved safety profile in asthma, zileuton may offer a feasible path toward topical clinical translation, particularly for localized disease or for patients who demonstrate inadequate responses to biologic therapy, although this requires prospective clinical evaluation40.

OXTR represents a less conventional discovery. As a Gq-coupled GPCR, OXTR regulates IL17RA expression through a calcineurin-dependent mechanism consistent with involvement of calcium–calcineurin–NFAT signaling. Beyond receptor regulation, the breadth of its proteomic footprint, encompassing mevalonate–cholesterol biosynthesis, sphingolipid metabolism, and innate immune sensing, suggests that OXTR occupies a central regulatory position in keratinocyte inflammatory pathways. FDA-approved OXTR antagonists may offer repurposing opportunities given their established human safety profiles, although dermatological formulation, dosing, and efficacy will require dedicated investigation41.

Our study establishes an integrated discovery framework that combines primary-cell CRISPR screening, AI-guided prioritization, and multi-omics validation to identify therapeutic targets in complex inflammatory disease. Within this framework, we uncover convergent regulation of IL17RA by ALOX5 and OXTR signaling, revealing previously underappreciated control nodes in keratinocyte inflammation. By showing that modulation of keratinocyte-intrinsic pathways can phenocopy systemic biologic therapy in a preclinical model, this work highlights a path toward accessible, locally delivered treatments for psoriasis and potentially other IL-17-driven disorders.

Methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Northwestern University (protocol IS00021267). All procedures complied with institutional and federal guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Publicly available datasets were analyzed in accordance with their respective data usage policies.

Primary human keratinocytes cell culture

Primary human adult epidermal keratinocytes (HEKa; PromoCell, cat. no. C-12003) were cultured for CRISPR screening in EpiLife™ Medium (Gibco) supplemented with 60 µM calcium, Human Keratinocyte Growth Supplement (Gibco), and Gentamicin-Amphotericin B (Lonza). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Cells were dissociated using Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) and neutralized with Trypsin Neutralization Solution (Gibco). Cells were passaged every 2–3 days upon reaching 70–80% confluency. All procedures followed the manufacturer’s recommended protocols to ensure reproducibility and compliance with standard cell culture practices.

VirtualCRISPR-based prediction of IL17RA regulators

To conduct our AI screening approach, we utilize the unsummarized embedding-based version of VirtualCRISPR12 trained on BioGRID-ORCS v1.1.16. Briefly, we conduct the virtual screen with the following terms: perturbation = “knockout,” cell = “primary adult keratinocytes,” phenotype = “IL17RA expression.” Following VirtualCRISPR, we align screen results to the GRCh38 human reference genome, reuse precomputed embeddings for perturbation method, cell type, and gene identifiers, and embed the phenotype description using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model. To identify novel hits for positive regulators of IL17RA expression, we predict the VirtualCRISPR score for the genes with FDR < 0.1. We plot the predicted probability against the predicted rank and annotate the known controls and the hypothesized novel hits.

CRISPR sgRNA lentivirus production

The Brunello sgRNA plasmid library lentivirus was produced following the previously published protocol42. Briefly, Lenti-X HEK293T cells (Takara Bio, cat. no. 632180) were seeded at a density of 3.6 × 10⁷ cells per T225 flask in 45 mL of Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Medium (Gibco) supplemented with GlutaMAX (Invitrogen), 5% FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 1× MEM nonessential amino acids (Fisher Scientific). Cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

The next day, HEK293T cells were transfected with 42 µg pLentiCRISPRv2-Brunello, 30 µg psPAX2 (Addgene), and 13 µg pMD2.G (Addgene) using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, 165 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 reagent was diluted in 5 mL of Opti-MEM without supplements. Separately, the plasmid mixture was prepared in 5 mL of Opti-MEM without supplements with 145 µL of p3000 reagent. The diluted Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent and plasmid mixture were combined by gentle inversion and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 20 mL of medium was removed from each T225 flask, and the 10 mL transfection mixture was carefully added without detaching the cells.

Six hours post-transfection, the medium was replaced with 45 mL of fresh complete Opti-MEM supplemented with 1× ViralBoost (Alstem Bio). Virus-containing supernatant was collected at 24 and 48 h after transfection, centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and filtered using 0.45 µm PVDF filters (Millipore Sigma) to remove cell debris. After that, one volume of Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara Bio) was added to three volumes of viral supernatant, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C. Lentiviral particles were pelleted by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 45 min at 4 °C and resuspended in Opti-MEM to 1% of the original culture volume. Viral aliquots were stored at −80 °C until use.

Functional titers were determined by infecting HEKa cells. The MOI was calculated 48 h post-infection by comparing cell survival between infected and uninfected controls following 24 h of selection with 3 µg/mL puromycin (Gibco).

Pooled genome-wide CRISPR screens in keratinocytes

We adopted a spinoculation-based transduction method in the absence of polybrene, followed by a 24-h viral incubation, achieving optimal library delivery at a low MOI (~0.25–0.35). 250 million HEKa cells were transduced with the Brunello lentiviral sgRNA library at an MOI of ~0.35, ensuring >500-fold coverage of the library after puromycin selection. HEKa cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 0.5 × 10⁶ cells per well and infected with high-titer virus (>2 × 109 TU/ml) by spinfection (900 × g, 1.5 h, 35 °C). The medium was replaced 24 h post-infection with a fresh growth medium containing 3 µg/mL puromycin. After 24 h, cells were transferred to T225 flasks at a seeding density of 2 × 10⁶ cells per flask, and maintained at >500-fold coverage throughout the screen. For sorting, cells were stained with APC anti-IL17RA (BioLegend) after collection. The top ~5% (high) and bottom ~5% (low) IL17RA-expressing populations were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). The unsorted T0 population was retained as a reference control. Genomic DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were performed as described below.

CRISPR screen analysis

We employed MAGeCK to quantify sgRNA enrichment and identify genes that regulate IL17RA expression43. This computational framework integrates normalized read counts with variance modeling to statistically rank both individual guides and their cognate genes.

Raw sequencing data were mapped to the Brunello library, and sgRNA counts across all samples were tabulated using MAGeCK’s “count” module. To determine which gene perturbations influenced IL17RA surface levels, we compared guide abundance between sorted IL17RAlow and IL17RAhigh paired populations. For each gene, the algorithm calculates a robust rank aggregation score by integrating the performance of all targeting guides, with significance determined through permutation-based testing. We applied Benjamini–Hochberg correction to adjust for multiple hypothesis testing, yielding FDR estimates. Genes meeting thresholds of FDR < 0.1 with concordant enrichment across at least two sgRNAs were designated as high-confidence regulators.

We evaluated screen quality through sgRNA retention rate (fraction of library guides detected at T₀) and the ability to detect enrichment of positive control sgRNAs (IL17RA) in low population.

Generation of OXTR, ALOX5, CYP3A5, IL17RA KO cells

Lentivirus for knockout experiments was generated following the procedure as described above. For transduction, vectors containing sgRNA sequences targeting OXTR, ALOX5, CYP3A5, and IL17RA were obtained from GenScript. The sgRNA sequences for each gene are listed in Supplementary Data 7. HEKa cells were transduced with target or control sgRNAs (MOI = 1) by spinfection (900 × g, 1.5 h, 35 °C) and incubated overnight. The following day, the medium was replaced with a fresh growth medium containing puromycin (3 µg/mL) for selection. After 24 h, surviving cells were expanded in a selection-free medium and maintained according to experimental requirements, with passaging every 2–4 days. An sgRNA targeting the AAVS1 locus was used as a negative control unless otherwise stated.

Genomic DNA extraction and library preparation

Genomic DNA was extracted from screen cell pellets using the QIAamp DNA blood mini kit (Qiagen) and unsorted cell pellets using the QIAamp DNA blood maxi kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing libraries were prepared by amplifying sgRNA sequences through a two-step PCR reaction using primers containing Illumina TruSeq adapters with i5 and i7 indices. The primer sequences used for sgRNA region amplification and barcoding are listed in Supplementary Data 8. All primers were synthesized as custom oligonucleotides by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). The reagents and workflow for the two-step PCR followed the Addgene protocol developed by Moffat’s laboratory for the TKOv3 library44. The 200-bp PCR 2 product was purified by gel extraction using the QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen), and the resulting libraries were sequenced by Novogene using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus sequencing platform.

Pharmacological inhibition validation

HEKa cells were cultured and plated at a density of 1.2 × 105 cells per well in 24-well plates. After overnight incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the indicated compounds under various treatment conditions and durations. Following treatment, supernatants were collected, and cells were detached using trypsin and neutralized with Trypsin Neutralization Solution (Gibco). IL17RA expression levels were assessed by flow cytometry. Cells were stained in FACS buffer (Invitrogen) with APC-conjugated anti-IL17RA antibody (BioLegend). Data were analyzed using FlowJo and GraphPad Prism software.

For OXTR mechanism-of-action (MOA) experiments, HEKa cells were pretreated with 10 nM tacrolimus (MCE) or 1 µM BAPTA-AM for 45 min, or 10 µM NFAT-Inhibitor-1 (MCE) for 24 h, followed by stimulation with 20 nM oxytocin (MCE) for 6 h. In separate conditions, HEKa cells were treated with 50 nM cligosiban for 3 days, followed by stimulation with 2 µM Ionomycin for 1 h.

For ALOX5 MOA studies, HEKa cells were pretreated with 15 µM dynasore (MCE) for 24 h, followed by treatment with 5 µM zileuton (MCE) for 1 h.

ELISA assays

For cytokine secretion analysis, HEKa knockout (KO) and NTC cells were seeded at 3.0 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates (2 mL per well) and cultured for 3 days. Supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 10 min prior to analysis. Concentrations of IL-6, IL-1β, and CXCL1 were measured using sandwich ELISA kits (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and concentrations were calculated from standard curves.

For LTB4 measurement, PBMCs from psoriasis patients (STEMCELL Technologies, cat. no. 70056) and healthy (STEMCELL Technologies, cat. no. 70025) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), seeded at 1 × 106 cells/mL in 12-well plates (Corning), and cultured for 24 h. Supernatants were collected, centrifuged, and subjected to solid-phase extraction and concentration according to the manufacturer’s protocol prior to analysis. LTB4 levels were measured using a competitive ELISA kit (Invitrogen), and concentrations were calculated using a four-parameter logistic (4-PL) standard curve.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, and significance was determined using ordinary one-way ANOVA or unpaired T test as described in the figure legends.

Cell proliferation assay

HEKa knockout (KO) and NTC cells were seeded at 5000 cells per well in tissue culture-treated white clear-bottom 96-well plates (Corning) in 100 μL of culture medium per well. Cells were cultured under standard conditions, and the medium was replaced every 2 days. Where indicated, cells were treated with zileuton or cligosiban at the specified concentrations.

Cell proliferation was assessed at the indicated time points using the CellTiter-Glo 2.0 Cell Viability Assay (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, an equal volume of CellTiter-Glo 2.0 reagent (100 μL) was added to each well, and plates were shaken for 2 min to induce cell lysis, followed by incubation at room temperature for 10 min to stabilize the luminescent signal. Luminescence was measured using the Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For T cell proliferation assays, human primary CD8+ T cells isolated from PBMCs (STEMCELL Technologies, cat. no. 70500.1) were cultured in T cell expansion medium (STEMCELL Technologies) supplemented with recombinant human IL-2 (STEMCELL Technologies) and seeded at 1 × 106 cells/mL in 96-well plates (200 μL per well). Cells were treated with zileuton or cligosiban as indicated.

Human CD4+ T cells were isolated from PBMCs (STEMCELL Technologies, cat. no. 70500.1) and cultured in T cell expansion medium (STEMCELL Technologies) supplemented with IL-2 (STEMCELL Technologies) and activated using CD3/CD28/CD2 stimulation (STEMCELL Technologies). CD4+ T cells were seeded at 0.5 × 106 cells/mL in 96-well plates and treated as indicated.

Vehicle control wells received DMSO (0.1% DMSO). Cell proliferation and viability were assessed at 24 and 48 h post-treatment using the Nexcelom Cellometer K2 Image Cytometer and ViaStain AOPI Staining Solution (Revvity). Samples were gently resuspended before counting to ensure an accurate representation of the population. Cell counts were averaged across replicates and plotted over time.

Relative proliferation was calculated by normalizing luminescence or cell number values to the initial time point (day 1 for keratinocytes or 24 h for T cells). Statistical significance was determined using two-way ANOVA with comparisons made between each treatment condition and the vehicle control using GraphPad Prism software.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 1× protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated on ice for 20 min. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were resolved on 4–15% precast SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in 1× TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with the following primary antibodies: anti-OXTR (Proteintech, 1:500), anti-ALOX5 (Proteintech, 1:1000), anti-IL17RA (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), anti-calmodulin (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (Invitrogen, 1:1000). After washing, membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) secondary antibody (Invitrogen, 1:5000) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) secondary antibody (Invitrogen, 1:10,000). Signal detection was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and blots were visualized using the Azure 300 imaging system.

Animal handling and treatment

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the IACUC of Northwestern University (protocol approval number: IS00021267). Female BALB/c mice (The Jackson Laboratory, Strain #000651, RRID: IMSR_JAX:000651) aged 6–8 weeks were used in this study. Animals were housed in groups of 3–5 per cage under a 12-h light/dark cycle, with humidity maintained between 30 and 70% and temperature controlled between 18 and 26 °C. Animals had free access to food and water throughout the experiment. Anesthesia was induced with 2.5% isoflurane and maintained with 1% isoflurane during all procedures. Prior to treatment, mice were anesthetized, and the dorsal skin was shaved.

Mice were randomly assigned into five groups: (1) vehicle control (base gel only), (2) IMQ + Vehicle control, (3) IMQ + Cligosiban (topical), (4) IMQ + Zileuton (topical), and (5) IMQ + anti-IL17RA antibody (positive control). Psoriasis-like inflammation was induced by daily topical application of 62.5 mg of 5% imiquimod cream to the shaved dorsal skin and ears from day 0 to day 6 for 7 consecutive days, and mice were analyzed on day 7.

Topical treatments with zileuton, or cligosiban, or vehicle gel were initiated on Day 3 and applied once daily on Days 3, 4, 5, and 6 as a thin layer over the affected skin, allowing 10 min for drying. Intraperitoneal (IP) injections were administered once daily on Days 3 and 5. Doses were as follows: Topical: zileuton (5 mg/mL; 300 µg/mouse), cligosiban (200 µg/mL; 12 µg/mouse), and IP: anti-IL17RA antibody (500 µg per mouse in 100 µl sterile saline).

Monitoring and data collection

Psoriasis severity was evaluated daily using the PASI, assessing erythema, scaling, and thickness (each scored 0–4, total score 0–12)45. Ear and skin thickness were measured with a digital caliper. Body weight and general condition were monitored throughout the study to assess systemic toxicity. At the endpoint (day 7 post-treatment), skin samples were collected for immunophenotyping and immunohistochemistry (IHC), and spleen weight was recorded.

Topical gel composition

Drug-containing gels were formulated using the following components: inactive ingredients, carbomer 940 (gelling agent), DMSO (co-solvent), purified water (vehicle), and triethanolamine (TEA) for pH adjustment. To prepare the gel, 1 g of carbomer 940 was dispersed in 50 mL of purified water and allowed to hydrate to form a uniform gel matrix. The active compound (Zileuton or Cligosiban) was dissolved in a minimal volume of DMSO, which served as a co-solvent to facilitate solubilization. The drug solution was then added dropwise to 5 mL of the hydrated gel while mixing to obtain final concentrations of zileuton (5 mg/mL) or cligosiban (200 µg/mL). The formulation pH was gradually adjusted to neutrality (pH 7) using triethanolamine (TEA).

Primary cell gene editing using electroporation

CRISPR-Cas9 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes were delivered into cells using the P3 Primary Cell 4D-Nucleofector X Kit (Lonza) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For assembly of RNPs, gRNA and Cas9 were added at a molar ratio of 2:1 (gRNA to Cas9) and incubated at room temperature for 20 min to form the RNP complex prior to electroporation. Cells were harvested, washed, and resuspended in P3 Nucleofector Solution, then combined with the RNP mixture. The cell–RNP suspension was transferred to Nucleocuvette strips and was electroporated using program DS138. Immediately after nucleofection, cells were incubated at room temperature for 10 min and then recovered in a pre-warmed culture medium. Cells were recovered for at least 2 days before downstream assays.

Skin sample processing and immunophenotyping

Mouse skin samples were dissociated using the tissue dissociation kit (Miltenyi) on the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator and filtered through a 70-µm filter to create single-cell suspensions. Samples were first stained with a live/dead stain for 30 min in PBS. For surface staining, cells were stained in FACS buffer (Invitrogen) with an appropriate amount of cell surface antibodies. For intracellular staining of FoxP3, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Invitrogen). For intracellular staining of IL17A and RORγ, cells were fixed and permeabilized using the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm™ Plus Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit with the BD GolgiPlug kit. For Th17 and Tc17 panels, cells were stimulated with eBioscience Cell Stimulation Cocktail (Invitrogen) at 37 °C for 1 h, and Brefeldin A was added for 4 h in RPMI medium before extracellular and intracellular staining described above. Samples were acquired using BD LSRFortessa or BD Melody cytometer, and the data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

PBMCs were isolated from two psoriasis patients (STEMCELL Technologies, cat. no. 70056) and two healthy controls (STEMCELL Technologies, cat. no. 70025) using density gradient centrifugation. Single-cell suspensions were processed using the 10x Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3′ Gene Expression v3.1 platform, and libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 to a depth of approximately 30,000 reads per cell. Raw sequencing data were processed with Cell Ranger (v7.0.0) using the GRCh38 reference genome to generate gene–barcode expression matrices.

Downstream analyses were performed in Seurat (v4.3.0, R v4.2.0). Standard QC metrics were applied. Briefly, cells with fewer than 200 detected genes or fewer than 500 UMI counts were excluded from downstream analysis. After filtering, 40,169 cells were retained for downstream analysis (mean ~10,042 cells per sample), followed by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for visualization.

A total of eight transcriptionally distinct clusters were annotated based on canonical immune cell markers, including CD14⁺ classical monocytes, Dendritic cells, Pan T cells, CD4⁺ T helper cells, CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, NK cells, and B cells. Differential gene expression between conditions was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with an adjusted p value threshold of 0.05.

Proteomics

Skin Tissue Prep

Six hundred microliters of 8 M urea, 5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and 50 mM tetraethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) were added to the skin tissue samples. Samples were homogenized using the Bead Mill 24 Homogenizer (Fisherbrand) in 2 mL tubes pre-filled with 2.8 mm ceramic beads (15-340-154, Fisherbrand). Homogenizer was set to run at 6 m/s for 1 cycle with 45 s of active time and 10 s of dwell time. Homogenate was transferred to 1.5 mL Protein LoBind tubes, and homogenization tubes were washed twice with 200 μL of lysis buffer. Skin samples were subjected to probe ultrasonication with a 3 mm microtip at 50% amplitude in pulsed mode (10 s on/10 s off) for a total on-time of 60 s. Skin lysates were centrifuged for 15 min at 13,000 × g. Supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher). All skin samples were processed using the S-trap microspin column (Protifi) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For each sample, 10 µg of protein lysate was reduced with 1 μL of 120 mM Tris(2-Carboxyethyl) Phosphine Hydrochloride (TCEP) at 55 °C for 15 min with shaking at 900 rpm, alkylated with 1 μL of 500 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) at room temperature for 10 min, and then acidified with 2.5 μL of 27.5% phosphoric acid (H3PO4). One hundred sixty-five microliters of 100 mM TEAB in 90% MeOH (Binding/Wash Buffer) was added to each sample. Samples were transferred to S-trap columns and spun at 2000 × g for 1 min in a Protein LoBind 96 Deepwell plate (951032921, Eppendorf). Each trap column was washed three times with 150 μL of Wash Buffer and spun at 2000 × g for 1 min. Trap columns were transferred to a new 96 Deepwell plate, and 20 μL of 0.05 μg/μL Trypsin/Lys-C Digestion Mix (V507A, Promega) (Digestion Buffer) in 50 mM TEAB was added to the columns. Digestion buffer was spun through the columns and then transferred back to the top of the columns. Samples were digested at 37 °C for 2 h without shaking. Digested peptides were sequentially eluted by spinning at 2000 × g for 1 using 40 μL of 50 mM TEAB, 40 μL of 0.2% formic acid (FA), and finally 40 μL of 50% acetonitrile (ACN). Eluted peptides were dried using a CentriVap concentrator (LabConco) and resuspended in 500 μL of 0.1% FA and 0.015% n-dodecyl-ß-D-maltoside (DDM).

Cell Pellet Prep

One hundred fifty microliters of 5% SDS and 50 mM TEAB was added to the cell pellet samples. Samples were subjected to probe ultrasonication with a 3 mm microtip at 30% amplitude in pulsed mode (5 s on/10 s off) for a total on-time of 20 s. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher). All cell pellet samples were processed using a 50:50 blend of Sera-Mag SpeedBead carboxylate-modified [E7] magnetic particles (45152105050250, Cytiva) and [E3] (65152105050250, Cytiva). For each sample, 10 µg of protein lysate was reduced with 1 μL of 120 mM TCEP at 55 °C for 15 min with shaking at 900 rpm, alkylated with 1 μL of 500 mM IAA at room temperature for 10 min. Reaction was quenched with 5 μL of 120 mM TCEP. Ten microliters of 10 μg/μL Sera-Mag Speedbead blend was added to protein lysates. Ethanol (EtOH) was added to samples so the final EtOH concentration was 50% of the sample by volume. Samples were shaken at 1000 rpm for 10 min. Samples were rinsed three times with 80% EtOH and left to air dry for 30 s. Beads were reconstituted in 40 μL of 50 mM TEAB and 1.25 ng/μL Trypsin/Lys-C and shaken at 1000 rpm for 2 h at 47 °C. Supernatant was removed, and beads were washed two times with 40 μL of 50 mM TEAB. The pooled peptides were dried down in a Centrivap and resuspended in 100 μL of 0.1% FA and 0.015% DDM.

LC-MS

Twenty-five nanograms of each peptide sample were loaded onto disposable Evotip C18 trap columns (Evotip, Evosep Biosystems) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, Evotips were conditioned with 20 µL of 0.1% FA in acetonitrile (Solvent B), centrifuged at 800 × g for 1 min, soaked in 1-propanol for 1–2 min, and equilibrated with 20 µL of 0.1% FA in water (Solvent A). Samples (20 µL) were then loaded onto each Evotip, centrifuged at 800 × g for 1 min, and washed with an additional 20 µL of Solvent A. Finally, 150 µL of Solvent A was added to each Evotip and centrifuged for 10 s at 800 × g to prevent drying of the C18 material. Mass spectrometry was performed using a timsTOF Ultra2 (Bruker Daltonics) coupled to an Evosep One (Evosep Biosystems). Peptides were separated with the WhisperZoom 40 SPD method using an Aurora Elite 15 × 75 µm, 1.7 µm column (IonOpticks) with an integrated emitter. The timsTOF Ultra2 was operated in dia-PASEF mode. Full MS scans were acquired from m/z 100–1700 and ion mobility range 1.45–0.64 1/K0. The dia-PASEF acquisition scheme spanned m/z 400–1000 and 0.64–1.45 1/K0 with 100 ms accumulation and ramp times. Collision energy was linearly ramped from 20 eV at 0.6 1/K0 to 59 eV at 1.6 1/K0. High-sensitivity detection for low sample amounts was enabled, with dia-PASEF denoising disabled. Data analysis was performed using DIA-NN v1.8.1 via a library-free search against a SwissProt-reviewed Mus musculus FASTA file for skin samples and a Homo sapiens FASTA file for cell pellet samples. Search parameters included protease specificity to Trypsin/P with one missed cleavage allowed, variable oxidation of methionine, and fixed carbamidomethylation of cysteine. Peptide length was restricted to 7–30 amino acids, precursor charge states to 2–4, precursor m/z to 300–1800, and fragment ion m/z to 200–1800. Protein groups and precursors were filtered at 1% FDR. Post-processing was conducted in R. Protein groups with >50% missing values within a given cell line were excluded. The remaining missing values were imputed using a k-nearest neighbors algorithm. Protein quantitation was normalized using variance stabilizing normalization. Differential expression analysis was performed with the limma package to determine protein fold changes and p values.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.0.3) and R (v4.3.1). Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m. unless otherwise indicated. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. Mice were randomized, and investigators were blinded to allocation during in vivo experiments and outcome assessment.

All experiments were independently repeated at least two times with similar results unless otherwise indicated.

A complete list of all reagents and materials used in this study is provided in Supplementary Data 9.
