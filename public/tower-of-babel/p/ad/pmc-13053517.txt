Long-term outcomes of peroral endoscopic myotomy for achalasia: a systematic review and meta-analysis with median follow-up ≥ 5 years
Authors: Yuxin Chen, Yifei Chen, Bin Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeroral Endoscopic Myotomy (POEM) has become the first-line minimally invasive treatment for achalasia. Its short-term and mid-term (1–5 years) efficacy has been consistently demonstrated, but long-term follow-up data (≥ 5 years) remain limited. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to comprehensively evaluate the long-term (median follow-up ≥ 5 years) clinical efficacy and safety of POEM.MethodsUsing medical literature databases, we retrieved randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-randomized comparative studies from the inception of the databases through September 2025. Data were extracted using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale to assess risk of bias for RCTs and cohort studies, respectively. Meta-analyses were conducted using either fixed-effect or random-effects models.ResultsA total of 16 studies involving 2421 patients (50.4% male) were included, with a median follow-up duration of 72 months (range, 60–144 months). The pooled clinical success rate was 87.1% (95% CI 82.4–91.8%; I2 = 84.2%). The pooled incidence of endoscopically detectable reflux esophagitis was 24.2% (95% CI 11.6–36.8%; I2 = 85.4%), whereas symptomatic reflux was inconsistently reported, with a pooled incidence of 27.2% (95% CI 19.2–35.3%; I2 = 83.6%). Long-term follow-up reported 2 cases of Barrett's esophagus and isolated cases of esophageal cancer.ConclusionPOEM has demonstrated consistent long-term efficacy and safety. Postoperative symptomatic reflux is the most common long-term complication, while the risks of Barrett's esophagus and peptic stricture remain low. POEM represents a reliable first-line treatment option; however, further multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate its long-term efficacy and safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00464-026-12618-7.

Achalasia (AC) is a rare idiopathic esophageal motility disorder characterized by inadequate relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) and loss of esophageal peristalsis. It commonly leads to dysphagia, reflux, chest pain, and weight loss, significantly impairing patients' quality of life [1]. The etiology and pathogenesis remain incompletely understood; however, histological analysis [2,3] suggests possible involvement of destruction of the myenteric plexus and LES smooth muscle ganglion cells, along with the loss of inhibitory neurons. The global annual incidence and prevalence rates range from 0.16–2.2 per 100,000 and 8.15–13.48 per 100,000, respectively [4–7], with a peak onset age between 30 and 60 years [8].

Peroral Endoscopic Myotomy (POEM) was first introduced by Inoue et al. in 2010 as a minimally invasive treatment for achalasia [9]. Multiple high-quality studies [10–12] have confirmed the short-term and mid-term (1–5 years) efficacy and safety of POEM: clinical success rates typically remain around 88–91%, with overall complications being manageable. However, existing clinical studies primarily focus on short-term or mixed follow-up data, lacking systematic evaluation of long-term efficacy (≥ 5 years). The sustained long-term efficacy of POEM and the risks of severe postoperative complications such as gastroesophageal reflux, Barrett's esophagus, and peptic strictures remain unclear, limiting our comprehensive understanding of POEM's long-term outcomes.

Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to integrate existing ≥ 5-year follow-up data specifically evaluating the long-term efficacy and safety of POEM. This study aims to refine the existing evidence base and providing scientific support for optimizing clinical treatment protocols and standardizing its application.

Methods

This study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (registration number CRD420251007156) and updated in October 2025. This systematic review and meta-analysis adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Interventions (PRISMA) statement and relevant guidelines [13,14] from the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews. Two researchers (Y.X.C and Y.F.C) independently searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Scopus, from their inception to October 2025, with no language restrictions. Search terms included “achalasia,” “esophageal achalasia,” “esophageal motility disorder,” “myotomy,” “peroral endoscopic myotomy,” “outcome,” and “efficacy.” Detailed search strategies are provided in Supplementary Digital Content 1 (Table S1).

Data extraction

Two researchers independently screened titles and abstracts for preliminary selection and extracted data from studies meeting inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third researcher making the final decision when necessary. Study quality assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool [15] is used for randomized controlled trials, while the modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [16] is used for non-randomized studies. The quality of included observational studies is assessed. Disagreements during scoring were resolved through discussion; when necessary, a third researcher made the final decision.

Data analysis

When ≥ 10 studies were included, statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.3.3, meta and metafor packages). Heterogeneity will be assessed using Cochran's Q test and the I2 statistic. To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, we performed sensitivity analyses (excluding studies one by one to evaluate robustness), subgroup analyses (by study design, disease duration, and achalasia classification), and meta-regression analysis.

Publication bias analysis

Publication bias was preliminarily assessed using funnel plots, with quantitative analysis conducted via Egger regression and Begg correlation tests. Where significant bias was evident, the trim-and-fill method was applied to correct pooled effect sizes.

Results

Study selection

Through a systematic literature review, we identified 5251 studies. After removing duplicates, we assessed a total of 2896 records based on their titles and abstracts. Subsequently, the search results identified 40 articles that potentially met all inclusion criteria for full-text review. Fifteen studies utilized the clinical success rate during long-term follow-up after POEM as the primary outcome measure, while one study used the time to reintervention as the primary outcome measure. Additionally, secondary endpoints of most studies included postoperative symptomatic reflux incidence, objective reflux (primarily endoscopically assessed, with limited use of 24 h pH monitoring), long-term PPI use, and recurrence rate.

Quality assessment indicated an overall moderate methodological quality across the included studies. The two RCTs demonstrated low-to-moderate risk of bias according to the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool, primarily attributable to limitations in allocation concealment and lack of blinding. Non-randomized studies, assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, were generally of moderate quality, showing adequate performance in the selection and outcome domains, though comparability and follow-up completeness varied across studies. Detailed results of the quality assessment are summarized in Table S2.

Main outcomes

Clinical success rate after long-term follow-up of POEM

A total of 16 studies were included, with a median follow-up duration of 60–120 months and a maximum follow-up of 144 months. All included studies reported postoperative Eckardt scores at baseline and at the longest available follow-up. The mean baseline Eckardt score was 6.65 ± 1.92, which decreased to 1.82 ± 1.26 during follow-up, corresponding to a mean reduction of 4.83 points compared with preoperative levels (p < 0.001).

The pooled analysis showed an overall clinical success rate of 87.1% (95% CI: 82.4–91.8%; I2 = 84.2%, p < 0.0001) after POEM (Fig. 2). Temporal trends in efficacy

Based on follow-up data from the included studies (60–144 months, Table 1), clinical success demonstrated a clear time-dependent pattern (Fig. 7). In the short-term (6 months to 1 year), 94–100% of patients experienced symptom relief. Mid-term follow-up (2–5 years) continued to show high success rates of approximately 90%, although a gradual decline was noted in some studies (e.g., Kuipers et al. [21] reported a reduction from 92% at 2 years to 81% at 5 years; Geeratragool et al. reported a decline from 100% at 2 years to 95.3% at 5 years). In contrast, McKay et al. observed a stable 5-year success rate of 91.9%, suggesting that the procedure provides durable mid-term efficacy.Fig. 7Temporal trend of clinical success rate over follow-up time

Postoperative gastroesophageal reflux

This meta-analysis included 15 studies comprising 583 patients, summarizing the incidence of symptomatic reflux at the longest available follow-up after POEM (Fig. 8). Only one study applied a DeMeester score ≥ 3 as the diagnostic criterion, whereas all others used the standardized GERD questionnaire (Gerd-Q; a score > 8 indicating GERD). The pooled incidence of symptomatic reflux was 27.2% (95% CI:19.2–35.3%; I2 = 83.6%). Only three studies [20,23,29] reported 24 h pH monitoring results, with positivity rates of 9/21 (42.9%), 3/7 (43%), and 26/63 (41%), respectively. Given the limited sample sizes, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to potential bias.Fig. 8Forest plot of pooled incidence of symptomatic reflux

Forest plot of pooled incidence of symptomatic reflux

Eight studies reported the incidence of endoscopic reflux esophagitis during follow-up (Fig. 9), with a pooled incidence of 24.2% (95% CI:11.6–36.8%). Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 85.4%), with reported rates ranging from 5.8 to 57.1%. Among studies reporting Los Angeles classification, most cases of reflux esophagitis were mild (LA grade A or B). Long-term proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use ranged from 25 to 75%, and the majority of patients achieved symptomatic or endoscopic improvement with medical therapy. Hugova et al. [20] reported a temporal pattern of gastroesophageal reflux after POEM, with favorable short-term outcomes but significantly worse long-term reflux severity (≥ 5 years) compared with the LHM group (p < 0.05). With respect to long-term serious adverse events, such as Barrett’s esophagus and esophageal cancer, only two studies to date have reported cases of Barrett’s esophagus. These limited data support the need for standardized long-term follow-up, with particular attention to reflux surveillance and management beyond 5 years postoperatively.Fig. 9Forest plot of pooled incidence of endoscopic reflux esophagitis

Assessment of publication bias

To assess publication bias, a funnel plot (Fig. 10) was constructed and statistical analysis was performed using Egger and Begg tests. The funnel plot revealed a degree of asymmetry, with studies of smaller sample sizes more likely to report higher or lower event rates. The Egger test revealed significant asymmetry (z = 4.88, p < 0.0001), suggesting potential publication bias or small-sample effects, while the Begg test was non-significant (p > 0.05). After adjustment using the trim-and-fill method, no missing studies requiring supplementation were identified. The pooled clinical success rate remained 87.1% (95% CI: 82.4–91.8%), indicating limited impact of publication bias on the overall effect. However, due to the limited number of included studies (n = 16) and high heterogeneity (I2 = 84.2%), the robustness of these results warrants cautious interpretation.Fig. 10Funnel plot for assessment of publication bias

Funnel plot for assessment of publication bias

The discrepancy between Egger's test and Begg's test results may stem from their differing statistical properties. Egger's test exhibits higher sensitivity to small-sample effects and is particularly suitable for studies with limited sample size, whereas the Begg test exhibits reduced statistical power with limited sample sizes (only 16 studies in this research), potentially failing to detect bias. Consequently, the significance of the Egger test more likely reflects the potential overestimation of success rates due to small-sample effects rather than purely publication bias.

However, gender and Chicago classification of the esophagus had no significant effect on success rates. This review retains limitations: most included studies were retrospective and single-center, with subjective quality assessments. Follow-up rates ranged from 70.8 to 92.5%, and substantial variation in follow-up duration may compromise comparability across studies. In addition, long-term data on Barrett’s esophagus, stricture formation, or malignant transformation after POEM remain limited. Future studies would benefit from multicenter, prospective designs with ≥ 10 years of follow-up, with particular attention to postoperative reflux management and malignant transformation risk. Furthermore, long-term outcomes could not be correlated with more granular esophageal physiological parameters, as high-resolution manometry and functional luminal impedance planimetry (FLIP) data were inconsistently reported or unavailable across the included studies, limiting mechanistic interpretation of symptom recurrence and reflux development.

Discussion

This systematic review included studies with a follow-up period of ≥ 5 years. A total of 16 studies met the inclusion criteria, thereby enhancing the robustness of the existing evidence. The pooled analysis revealed a long-term clinical success rate of 87.1% (95% CI: 82.4–91.8%). However, significant heterogeneity existed among studies regarding long-term efficacy (≥ 5 years). AbiMansour et al. [17] reported a 6-year success rate of 89%; Geeratragool et al. [19] observed a 90.6% success rate at 7 year follow-up, which declined to 71.4% at 10 years; McKay et al. [22] reported a 91.2% sustained remission rate at 6–7 years. These findings suggest that some patients may experience symptom recurrence or diminished efficacy during long-term follow-up. We also observed that early treatment response appears closely correlated with long-term efficacy. AbiMansour et al. [17] found that patients achieving remission within 6 months postoperatively demonstrated a 6-year success rate as high as 95.5%. Similarly, Pedersen et al. [27] indicated that treatment failure primarily occurred within the first 3 months postoperatively, with no new failures observed during 5-year follow-up. Collectively, these studies provide substantial evidence for long-term prognosis assessment. The mean Eckardt score decreased significantly from 6.65 at baseline to 1.82 after POEM (p < 0.001), further validating the long-term efficacy and reliability of POEM as a treatment modality.

Compared with other traditional treatments, the long-term efficacy of POEM is comparable to that of Heller myotomy (HM). Multiple previous long-term studies on HM [33–39] reported 5-year clinical success rates ranging from 49.8 to 91.8%, with a pooled analysis indicating approximately 82.6%, which is close to the efficacy of POEM in this study. In contrast, the long-term efficacy of balloon dilation (PD) is markedly inferior to both POEM and HM. Abid et al. [17] reported a 5 year cumulative remission rate of approximately 87%, but efficacy declined with increasing dilation frequency. Javed et al. [40] reported a 5-year success rate of only about 53%, with a year-by-year decrease, suggesting limited capacity of PD to maintain long-term efficacy.

Postoperative reflux is the most common adverse event associated with peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM). This meta-analysis indicates that approximately 27.2% (95% CI: 19.2–35.3%) experienced reflux symptoms, and 24.2% (95% CI: 11.6–36.8%) were endoscopically diagnosed with reflux esophagitis, consistent with previous reports of 19–21% [41–43]. Symptomatic reflux was typically assessed using the GERD-Q score (GERD defined as > 8 points), while endoscopic reflux esophagitis grading followed the Los Angeles classification. However, the included studies exhibited methodological limitations in clinical assessment: only two studies (AbiMansour 2021, Onimaru 2021) employed 24 h pH monitoring as the diagnostic gold standard. In the existing long-term literature, objective reflux assessment therefore relied predominantly on endoscopic findings, whereas only a small number of studies reported 24 h pH monitoring data, which were insufficient for pooled quantitative analysis. The remaining 14 studies primarily relied on subjective symptom questionnaires (e.g., GERD-Q) or endoscopic findings (e.g., LA grading) for assessment, which may limit the reliability of conclusions regarding objective reflux incidence. Subjective assessments are susceptible to patient perception bias, potentially leading to over- or underestimation of reflux incidence. While endoscopy can identify erosive esophagitis (e.g., the 8.5% endoscopic positivity rate reported by Modayil 2021), it cannot detect non-erosive reflux disease, making it difficult to comprehensively reflect the true disease burden of gastroesophageal reflux disease.

Compared to HM, POEM showed a slightly higher risk of reflux, though some differences were not statistically significant. A meta-analysis [44] showed no statistical difference between the two techniques in symptomatic reflux (RR = 1.03; 95% CI 0.61–1.73) or pathologic reflux confirmed by pH monitoring (RR = 1.22; 95% CI 0.67–2.25). Luigi et al. [45] reported significantly lower symptomatic reflux after HM compared to POEM (OR = 1.81; 95% CI 1.11–2.95; p < 0.05). Overall, although reflux incidence remains relatively high after POEM, most cases can be controlled with medications such as PPIs, underscoring the importance of long-term follow-up and individualized management.

Beyond reflux, long-term follow-up data on complications such as strictures, Barrett's esophagus, and esophageal cancer following peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) remain scarce. Notably, patients with achalasia constitute a high-risk cohort for esophageal cancer, with an incidence rate of approximately 3%—significantly higher than the general population (roughly 50 times greater) [46]. A recent large-scale retrospective study from Japan [47] further analyzed the esophageal cancer risk among patients with different subtypes of achalasia: the standardized incidence rates for St, S1, and S2 types were 1, 2.5, and 10%, respectively, with statistically significant differences between subgroups (p < 0.05). This indicates a marked tendency toward long-term malignant transformation in patients with specific clinical subtypes, providing crucial evidence for risk stratification and the development of individualized follow-up strategies.

Random-effects meta-regression analysis confirmed that disease duration (β = − 0.02; OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99; p < 0.001) and age (β = − 0.04; OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93–0.99; p = 0.004) were independent predictors of clinical success. This finding was highly consistent with subgroup analysis results: adolescent patients (≤ 18 years) demonstrated significantly higher success rates than adult patients (96.0% vs 85.0%, p < 0.05), potentially related to adolescents' stronger tissue regenerative capacity and neural plasticity [48]; simultaneously, patients with disease duration ≥ 10 years exhibited significantly lower success rates than those with shorter durations (50.0% vs 71.0%, p < 0.05), suggesting that prolonged disease course may lead to irreversible neuronal loss and fibrotic changes 49, underscoring the importance of early clinical intervention.

Although subgroup analysis showed that patients with Type III had numerically higher success rates than other subtypes (90.0% vs. 82.0% for Type I and 81.0% for Type II), consistent with the trend reported by Zhang et al. [50], meta-regression analysis did not reveal a significant effect of achalasia subtype on success rate (p > 0.05). Gender also showed no significant effect (p = 0.414). After further incorporating prior treatment history and myotomy length as covariates, analysis revealed a marginally negative trend for prior treatment history (β = − 0.03; OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.94–1.00; p = 0.08), potentially reflecting more complex pathological backgrounds in patients with prior treatment failure, while myotomy length showed a positive association (β = 0.01; OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.99–1.03; p = 0.12). Particularly in Type III patients, longer incisions (e.g., up to 10 cm as reported by Xiang et al.) may confer clinical benefits. However, as some original studies did not fully report these variables, current interpretations require caution. Future efforts should promote standardized data reporting to enhance the reliability of conclusions.

According to the GRADE evidence assessment, the certainty of evidence for primary outcomes in this systematic review with ≥ 5-year follow-up for POEM is as follows: Long-term clinical success rate is moderate (primarily observational studies with heterogeneity but consistent results); Postoperative reflux incidence is low (largely dependent on subjective scoring or endoscopy, lacking 24 h pH monitoring, and exhibiting high heterogeneity); Eckardt score improvement is moderate (significant improvement in scores, large sample size, but all studies were non-randomized); Subgroup analyses (by age, disease duration, and Type III) are low (limited sample size, observational data, risk of bias).

In summary, this study supports POEM as a sustainable and effective treatment modality, emphasizing the importance of postoperative reflux monitoring and robust follow-up mechanisms. Based on ≥ 5-year follow-up data, patient groups demonstrating the most significant benefits include younger patients, those with shorter disease duration, and Type III patients. Clinical follow-up should prioritize patients with prolonged disease duration, prior treatment failure, and high reflux risk. These findings align with the SAGES 2021 and 2024 guidelines [51,52], further establishing POEM's feasibility in Type I/II patients and demonstrating relative advantages for Type III patients, while showing superior efficacy compared to balloon dilation.
