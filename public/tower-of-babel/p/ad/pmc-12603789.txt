Circadian rhythms and the light‐dark cycle interact to regulate amyloid‐beta plaque accumulation and tau phosphorylation in 5xFAD mice
Authors: Melvin W King, Sophie M Jacob, Ashish Sharma, Jennifer H Lawrence, Erik S Musiek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONCircadian rhythm disruption is an early symptom of Alzheimer's disease (AD), though it remains unclear how the light‐dark (LD) cycle and the central circadian clock in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) influence AD pathobiology.METHODSWe disrupted the SCN clock via deletion of Bmal1 in GABAergic neurons (VGAT‐iCre; Bmal1 KO), crossed to the 5xFAD amyloid‐beta (Aβ) model, and raised mice under LD or constant dark (DD) conditions. BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive, debilitating neurological disorder that affects nearly 6.9 million people aged 65 or over and is the leading cause of dementia in the United States. 1 Disruption of endogenous circadian and sleep rhythms has long been appreciated as a downstream consequence of AD. 2 , 3 However, an upstream role for circadian disruption in AD progression – and in particular the aggregation of amyloid‐beta (Aβ) plaques – has yet to be defined. 4 Evidence consistent with a causal role for circadian disruption comes from numerous human studies demonstrating transcriptional and behavioral circadian abnormalities as a function of age, the primary risk factor for developing AD. 5 , 6 The presence of preclinical Aβ or tau pathology in cognitively normal individuals has been associated with increased circadian fragmentation, suggesting that circadian dysfunction occurs very early in the disease course. 7 Given the pervasiveness of the circadian system at various levels of biology, how these disruptions contribute to the initiation and progression of AD pathology is a significant unknown.

A few previous studies examined the impact of circadian disruption on AD pathology in mouse models using genetic ablation of the transcription factor Bmal1 (aka Arntl), as BMAL1 protein dimerizes with CLOCK or NPAS2 to drive cellular rhythms through the core circadian transcriptional–translational feedback loop (TTFL). 8 , 9 Our group previously found that post‐natal whole‐body knockout (KO) of Bmal1 causes behavioral arrhythmicity in mice and exacerbates hippocampal plaque pathology in the APP/PS1‐21 model of AD. 10 , 11 However, a suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN)‐sparing Bmal1 deletion failed to have the same impact on plaque deposition. 12 This suggests that disrupting Bmal1 across cell types in the brain and periphery may exert complicated circadian and non‐circadian effects that impact amyloid dynamics. It remains unknown whether disrupting Bmal1 expression specifically in the SCN, which coordinates brain‐ and body‐wide rhythms by a combination of synaptic and hormonal cues and whose rhythmic output degrades with age, influences Aβ plaque pathology. 13 , 14

One classic strategy for disrupting SCN function is through electrolytic lesioning. 15 However, the SCN is small and surrounded by dozens of other critical hypothalamic nuclei, which may be variably damaged in this process, exerting unknown effects. 16 Thus, a genetic approach is preferable. Since >95% of SCN neurons are GABAergic, targeted deletion of Bmal1 in GABAergic neurons using VGAT‐ires‐Cre produces mice that are behaviorally arrhythmic in constant darkness (DD). 17 , 18 It is unknown how this restricted Bmal1 deletion impacts amyloid pathology in mice. Additionally, the role of light in the interaction between Bmal1‐mediated rhythms and amyloid pathology is underexplored. Light is the principal entrainment signal or zeitgeber for the mammalian circadian clock and in a healthy animal entrains SCN Bmal1 and downstream physiology via the retino‐hypothalamic tract. 19 However, light exposure also suppresses locomotor activity, even in the absence of SCN Bmal1. As a result, an animal with a genetically ablated clock can remain behaviorally rhythmic in a regular light‐dark (LD) cycle, a phenomenon called masking. 20 , 21 The effect of masking on Aβ pathology is also unknown.

Here, we examined the effect of GABAergic Bmal1 deletion on circadian function and Aβ plaque‐related pathology in the 5xFAD mouse model of amyloid pathology. 22 We performed experiments with VGAT‐iCre;Bmal1 fl/fl;5xFAD+/− mice (referred to as VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD or 5x;Cre+) and Cre−;Bmal1 fl/fl;5xFAD+/− (5x;Cre−) controls under both standard 12h:12 h LD (in which Cre+ mice have some rhythmic behavior due to zeitgeber cues) and DD conditions (under which Cre+ mice are fully arrhythmic) to help isolate the potential effects of the LD cycle and circadian rhythmicity from non‐circadian effects of Bmal1 deletion. To our surprise, we found that disruption of behavioral and transcriptional rhythms via GABAergic Bmal1 deletion caused a decrease in Aβ plaque accumulation and plaque‐related phospho‐tau (p‐tau), but only in mice kept under LD conditions. Moreover, we found that 5xFAD mice raised in LD conditions accumulated more plaque pathology than those raised in DD conditions. These studies suggest that specific interactions between the LD cycle and the molecular clock may drive changes in Aβ aggregation, perhaps by optimizing neuronal activity and Aβ production, and provoke a rethinking of the proposed linear relationship between behavioral arrhythmicity and AD pathology.

METHODS

Mice

All animal procedures were approved by the Washington University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and were conducted in agreement with Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC) guidelines and under supervision of the Washington University Department of Comparative Medicine. 5xFAD mice were generated from mice originally purchased through the Jackson Laboratory (Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Centers; stock no. 34848). VGAT‐ires‐iCre C57Bl6j (Jackson Laboratory 028862) were generated and supplied by Dr. David Weaver (University of Massachusetts Chan School of Medicine, Worcester, MA, USA). VGAT‐ires‐iCre mice were bred at least three generations to generate appropriate Cre and floxed alleles, and Cre+ and Cre− littermates were compared to one another. Both male and female mice were used.

For aging 5xFAD mice in DD and LD conditions, mice were transferred to custom‐built tents with ventilation and light manipulation. Mice were group housed, and food and water were provided ad libitum. We exposed the mice to either a 12 h:12 h LD regime, where lights on is defined as zeitgeber time ZT0) or 12 h:12 h DD conditions with temperature ranges of 20°C to 26°C and 50% to 60% humidity. A visual inspection was performed once a week during cage changes under red light. Light intensity ranged from 200 to 300 lux during lights on, <1 lux during lights off, and 1 to 10 lux under red light. Unless otherwise specified, all mice were perfused and tissues harvested at 5 months of age. Both male and female mice were used.

Circadian activity measurement

Mice were transferred to single cages in custom‐built tents for no more than 4 weeks during recordings, and food and water were provided ad libitum. Mice were acclimated for 1 week in LD conditions before data acquisition. Activity was recorded via motion‐tracking infrared wireless nodes (Actimetrics), one per cage. Circadian statistical measures were plotted and analyzed via Clocklab 6.1.02 and Graphpad Prism 10.4.1. Free‐running period was calculated by χ2 periodogram using 8 days before the transition from LD to DD, and 8 days starting 2 days after the LD to DD transition. Fast Fourier transform (FFT) was used to calculate the relative amplitude of the circadian component from 18 to 30 h. Animals with a loss of circadian rhythms (NR) were excluded from period calculations.

Sleep measurement

Sleep measurements were performed using the PiezoSleep mouse behavioral tracking system (Signal Solutions, LLC, Lexington, KY, USA). 23 This method utilized thin dielectric piezo sensors that generate a signal in response to pressure changes on its surface. Mice were individually housed in home cages with the piezo pad underneath and were acclimated to housing conditions for 1 week before the start of data collection. They were given fresh bedding as well as food and water ad libitum. Cre− and Cre+ littermates, either 5xFAD− or 5xFAD+ were continuously monitored over the course of 2 weeks (7× LD, 7× DD). Data were acquired and analyzed using SleepStats software (Signal Solutions, LLC, Lexington, KY, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were anesthetized with 150 mg/kg i.p. pentobarbital and perfused for 3 min with cold Dulbecco's Modified PBS with 3% heparin. One hemisphere was dissected into cortical and hippocampal regions, flash frozen, and stored at –80°C for RNA and biochemical analysis. The other was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4°C. Brains were sectioned on a sliding microtome in 40‐µm sections and stored in cryoprotectant (30% ethylene glycol, 15% sucrose, and 15% phosphate buffer in ddH2O). For fibrillar plaque staining, sections were washed in tris‐buffered saline (TBS), incubated in TBS + 0.25% Triton X‐100 (TBSX) for 30 min, then incubated in X34 staining buffer (10 µM X34 and 20 mM NaOH in 60% PBS, 40% ethanol) for 20 min. Sections were then blocked in 3% donkey serum for 1 h, then incubated in TBSX containing 1% donkey serum and primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit Anti‐Bmal1 (Novus, NB100‐2288), rat anti‐Lamp1 (DSHB, 1D4B), biotinylated AT8 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MN1020B), goat anti‐Iba1 (Abcam, ab5076), rat anti‐CD68 (Bio‐Rad, MCA1957). Biotinylated HJ3.4 was generously provided by David M. Holtzman. On day 2, sections were washed and incubated in 1:1000 donkey fluorescent secondary antibodies before being mounted on slides using Prolong Gold.

Image acquisition and analysis

Images were obtained from two sections per mouse, 200 µm apart. Slides were scanned on a W1 CSU SoRa Spinning Disk Confocal (Nikon) and processed using ImageJ version 1.54 (National Institutes of Health). For whole‐region analysis, a stitched image was generated from a 3 × 3 tile scan of 9× z‐stacks (40 µm) of each section using the 10× objective generating a final image at 6682 × 6682 pixel resolution, then processed via a maximum intensity projection. Laser and detector settings were set for the acquisition of each image consistently. Quantification was performed in FIJI/ImageJ on manually drawn or immunostained regions of interest after thresholding using the Analyze Particles function. Parameters including area fraction (%Area), count, and size were imported into Excel and GraphPad Prism for statistical analysis. Peri‐plaque and parvalbumin/BMAL1 images were acquired with a 20× objective, 2× zoom at 1024 × 1024 resolution. Peri‐plaque images were analyzed on a semi‐automated platform using Imaris 9.5 (Bitplane) and MATLAB. Surfaces were created using X34, dilated by 15 µm, and colocalized with various immunostained surfaces. For volume ratio calculations, the combined volume of each surface in an image was divided by the combined X34. Parvalbumin/BMAL1 image analysis was carried out in FIJI/ImageJ using Analyze Particles to first generate regions of interest around parvalbumin+ soma, and apply these ROIs to the BMAL1 channel. All staining experiments were imaged and quantified by a blinded investigator. Mice were designated with serial numbers on the day of sexing and genotyping (P8) and referenced back to those numbers after the analysis.

RNA quantification and analysis

Tissue was homogenized in 500 uL of Trizol with RNase‐free beads in a bullet blender for 4 min. Trizol samples were then extracted with 1:6 chloroform and centrifugation at 12,500 × g for 15 min. RNA was extracted from the aqueous layer using PureLink RNA Mini Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. Nanodrop spectrophotometry was used to determine the RNA concentration, after which the samples were converted into cDNA using a high‐capacity RNA‐cDNA reverse transcriptase kit (Applied Biosystems) with 400 to 800 ng RNA per 20 uL reaction. We performed quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) using ABI Taqman Primers and ABI PCR Master Mix Buffer on ABI StepOnePLus or QuantStudio 12,000 thermocyclers. Taqman primers (Invitrogen) were used, and mRNA values were normalized to β‐Actin (Actb) or Gapdh. For microfluidic experiments, qPCR array measurements were performed by the Washington University Genome Technology Access Center (GTAC) using a Fluidigm Biomark HD system, again using TaqMan primers and normalized to GAPDH.

RNA‐seq analysis

RNA samples were harvested from VGAT‐iCre;Bmal1 fl/fl;5xFAD+/− as previously described. ZT of isolated samples are described in the Supplementary Table. RNA‐seq analysis was performed by the GTAC at Washington University, as previously described. 24 Sample RNA integrity was determined using a Tapestation and library preparation was performed with 10 ng of total RNA for samples with a Bioanalyzer RIN score > 8.0. ds‐cDNA was prepared using the SMARTer Ultra Low RNA kit for Illumina Sequencing (Takara‐Clontech) per the manufacturer's protocol. The cDNA was then fragmented using a Covaris E220 sonicator using peak incident power 18, duty factor 20%, cycles per burst 50 for 120 s. The cDNA was blunt ended, had an A base added to the 3′ ends, and then had Illumina sequencing adapters ligated to the ends. Ligated fragments were then amplified for 12 to 15 cycles using primers incorporating unique dual index tags. The fragments for each sample were then pooled in an equimolar ratio and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 using 150 base pair paired end reads. Basecalls and demultiplexing were performed with Illumina's RTA 1.9 software, and the reads were aligned to the Mus musculus Ensembl (release 76 GRCm38 primary assembly) using STAR version 2.5.1a. Gene counts were quantitated with Subread:featureCount version 1.4.6‐p5. All gene counts were imported into the R/Bioconductor package EdgeR and TMM normalization size factors were calculated to adjust the samples for differences in library size. Genes with an average expression of more than two counts per million across all samples were considered as expressed. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed through clusterProfiler. 25 ChIP‐X enrichment analysis (ChEA3) was performed using the online web tool.

Protein extraction

Flash‐frozen hippocampal brain tissue was immersed in RIPA buffer (pH 8.0): 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris, 0.5% deoxycholic acid, 1% Triton X‐100, 0.1% SDS, 5 mM EDTA, 20 mM NaF, and 1 mM sodium orthovanadate supplemented with 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor. Tissue was ground on ice for 15 s using a tissue homogenizer and centrifuged at 4°C at 10,000 × g for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was taken as the soluble fraction. The pellet was incubated in 1:6 5 M guanidine hydrochloride, subjected to ultrasonication for 30 s, and incubated at room temperature for 2 h with shaking. The resulting homogenate was then centrifuged for at 4°C at 20,000 × g for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected as the insoluble fraction for long‐term storage at –80°C. Protein concentration was analyzed via Pierce BCA Protein Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Western blot

Hippocampal and cortical samples were weighed and lysed according to the protein extraction protocol specified earlier. Equal amounts of protein samples (10 to 20 µg) were dissolved in sample buffer, heat treated at 95°C for 5 min, and treated to sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis before electrophoretic transfer to immunoblotting 0.2 µm PVDF membranes. The membranes were then pretreated with a blocking solution (8% dry skim milk in TBS, 0.1% Tween‐20) for 1 h at room temperature, then treated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti‐β amyloid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalogue No.: 51‐2700, 1:1000), rabbit anti‐PSEN1 (CST, Catalogue No.: 5643, 1:1000), rabbit anti‐BACE1 (Abcam, EPR3956, 1:1000), rabbit anti‐β actin (CST Catalogue No.: 4970), and mouse anti‐AEBP/ACLP (SC Biotechnology, sc‐271374). The next day, after three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated in horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies against rabbit IgG in 1% BSA for 1 h at room temperature. Proteins were detected by chemiluminescence using Super Signal Wes Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalogue No.: 34577). Western blots were visualized using a ChemiDoc Imaging System (BioRad) and analyzed with ImageJ software. Unless otherwise specified, blots were normalized to actin.

Statistics

Graphs display the mean ± SEM, and n indicates the number of animals, unless otherwise indicated in the figure legend. To examine whether variances were different, an F‐test was first performed for datasets with a single independent variable and two groups. If not, a two‐tailed unpaired t test was performed. If variances were significantly different, a non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U test was performed. For datasets with two independent variables, two‐way ANOVA was performed. Outliers were identified using ROUT (Q = 1%) and excluded. Statistical tests were performed with GraphPad Prism software version 10.4.1. P values greater than 0.05 were considered not significant (ns). P < 0.05 was considered significant and notes with asterisks indicating the p value: *p < 0.05, ** < 0.005, *** < 0.0005.

RESULTS

GABAergic Bmal1 deletion disrupts circadian behavior in LD and DD conditions

We sought to use the VGAT‐ires‐Cre line to target clock gene expression in the SCN, as previously described, so as to disrupt SCN circadian function without widespread deletion of clock genes in other cell types and regions of the brain. 17 Inhibitory GABAergic synaptic transmission is ubiquitous throughout the brain, so we first aimed to characterize the expression of VGAT‐ires‐Cre in various brain regions. We crossed VGAT‐iCre mice with the Ai9 mouse line, which expresses tdTomato upstream of a loxP‐flanked STOP cassette, enabling permanent labeling of VGAT‐expressing cells. 26 We observed strong tdTomato labeling within the SCN (Figures 1A, S1A), in agreement with previous reports. 27 Intense tdTomato signal was noted in the striatum and basal ganglia (Figure S1A), as most of these neurons are GABAergic. However, in the medial cortex and hippocampus, regions typically examined for plaque accumulation in AD models, only sparse labeling was seen with roughly 20% of NeuN+ expressing tdTomato across both regions (Figure S1B).

Given the role of the SCN and BMAL1 in maintaining organismal and cellular circadian rhythms, we then assessed the role of GABAergic Bmal1 in regulating circadian behavior under LD and DD conditions. We crossed VGAT‐ires‐Cre and Bmal1 fl/fl mice to generate Cre−; Bmal1 fl/fl controls and Cre+ VGAT‐BMAL1 KO mice and aged them to 2 months. Circadian activity was assessed with infrared actigraphy monitoring under LD (1 week) and DD (2 weeks) conditions. Under LD conditions, Cre+ mice showed some diurnal rhythms in activity but had noticeably dampened amplitude (Figure 1D,E), and had onset of their active phase nearly 4 h earlier than Cre− mice, which normally become active at lights off (ZT12, Figure 1G). Upon release into constant darkness, Cre− mice maintained circadian activity rhythms with an average period of 23.6 h, while Cre+ mice immediately lost coherent rhythmic activity by periodogram analysis, with no detectable circadian period (Figures 1F, S1D). BMAL1 immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis confirmed the near total loss of BMAL1 protein within the SCN (Figure 1B,C). Interestingly, despite intense tdTomato expression within the striatum, VGAT‐iCre‐mediated excision only reduced BMAL1 immunopositivity within this region by roughly half. Similarly, BMAL1 immunopositivity specifically within Parvalbumin‐positive interneurons revealed a loss of BMAL1 staining of 40% in the cortex and 50% in the hippocampus (Figure S1C,D), confirming that GABAergic Bmal1 deletion targets extra‐SCN inhibitory neuron populations. However, total BMAL1 immunofluorescence within the cortex and hippocampus was unaffected (Figure S1E), suggesting that most BMAL1 expression is within excitatory neurons. Collectively, these results confirm that GABAergic Bmal1 deletion disrupts SCN circadian function with minimal impact on total BMAL1 expression in the cortex and hippocampus.

GABAergic Bmal1 deletion disrupts sleep and transcriptional rhythms in 5xFAD mice

Our next goal was to explore how GABAergic BMAL1 influences rhythmic physiology in a mouse model of AD. We crossed 5xFAD mice, which express human amyloid precursor protein (APP) with the Swedish (KM670/671NL), Florida (I716V), and London (V717I) mutations and human presenilin 1 (PSEN1) with the M146L and L286V mutations, with the VGAT‐ires‐Cre;Bmal1 fl/fl line to generate VGAT‐iCre;Bmal1 fl/fl;5xFAD+/− mice (referred to as VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD or 5x;Cre+) and Cre−;Bmal1 fl/fl;5xFAD± controls (5x;Cre−) (Figure 2A). 22 Mice were aged in LD until 5 months, when substantial Aβ plaque development has taken place within the cortex and hippocampal regions. 28 As before, while 5x;Cre+ mice were able to entrain to a regular LD cycle, their circadian amplitude was negatively impacted compared to 5x;Cre− (Figures 2B,C, S2A). Constant darkness abrogated rhythms in 4/4 Cre+ mice by χ2 periodogram analysis, demonstrating the efficacy of this paradigm in ablating rhythms in 5xFAD mice in constant darkness (Figure S2B).

We simultaneously measured sleep – a circadian controlled process whose disruption is associated with risk of dementia in humans – using piezoelectric monitoring. 29 , 30 Total sleep percentage over a 24‐h period was unchanged between 5x;Cre+ and 5x;Cre− control mice. However, 5x;Cre+ mice spent less time sleeping during the inactive or light phase (ZT0 to ZT12) and more time sleeping during the dark phase (ZT12 to ZT24) than their Cre− littermates (Figure 2E,F). Free‐running behavior (5x;Cre−) and circadian fragmentation (5x;Cre+) prevented division of sleep behavior in DD into active and inactive epochs, but overall sleep amount remained the same between Cre− and Cre+ mice, similar to LD (Figure S2C). Despite significant circadian disruption between 5x;Cre− and 5x;Cre+ mice, the total amount of sleep, to which plaque deposition in mouse models is strongly sensitive, remained unchanged. 31 , 32 , 33

Finally, we examined the role of GABAergic BMAL1 in driving hippocampal diurnal transcriptional rhythms under LD conditions. Bulk hippocampal tissue was harvested from 5x;Cre+ mice to 5x;Cre− controls across six times of day under LD and analyzed for a panel of transcripts associated with circadian and clock function (Figure 2D). Because 5x;Cre+ mice are arrhythmic in DD, we did not perform gene expression under these conditions, as the phase dispersion of the Cre+ mice at each timepoint would make the data uninterpretable. We examined eight known circadian transcripts by qPCR, and all were identified as circadian via JTK_CYCLE analysis in 5x;Cre− control mice under LD. 34 However, all showed blunted amplitude in 5x;Cre+ mice under LD, with only Nr1d1 (encoding Rev‐Erbα) remaining rhythmic, although phase advanced by 2 h (Figures 2G, S2C). Thus, despite having partially intact behavioral rhythms under LD conditions, circadian gene expression is disrupted in the hippocampus of 5x;Cre+ mice. Together, our data demonstrate that GABAergic Bmal1 KO in 5xFAD mice disrupts both LD and DD circadian behavior, disrupts sleep timing and dampens oscillatory hippocampal clock gene expression.

GABAergic Bmal1 KO suppresses Aβ plaque pathology in LD conditions

As circadian disruption is a hallmark of preclinical AD, we next tested the hypothesis that excision of Bmal1 in GABAergic neurons might influence Aβ aggregation in 5xFAD mice. 7 We used exclusively female VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD (5x; Cre+) mice and 5x;Cre− littermates, as females have more aggressive Aβ plaque pathology. We kept mice under standard LD conditions until 5 months of age, when plaque accumulation is moderate. 28 At 5 months, staining with anti‐Aβ antibody HJ3.4, which labels total Aβ plaque (diffuse and fibrillar), revealed a surprising reduction in plaque density in 5x;Cre+ mice within the cortex of roughly 40%, with smaller reductions of 25% in the hippocampal and thalamic regions (Figure 3A,B). This was seen specifically in female mice, as male mice had less pathology and showed no differences in plaques between Cre− and Cre+ (Figure S3). Thus, we restricted our further analyses to female mice. As a partial explanation for these changes, we found a mild (∼10%) but statistically significant reduction in plaque size within the cortex and hippocampus, along with a trending but non‐significant reduction in plaque numbers in these regions (Figure 3D,E). Looking specifically at plaque numbers, histogram analysis revealed a significant reduction in the number of smaller plaques, between 30 and 250 µm2, in both the cortex and hippocampus (Figure 3F).

We next examined the accumulation of dense core fibrillar Aβ plaques using the congophilic stain X34. Unlike HJ3.4+ plaques, we saw no change in total X34+ plaque accumulation in VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice across regions. However, confocal imaging revealed changes in fibrillar plaque morphology, as X34+ plaques in Cre+ mice were more compact, with a smaller size and higher circularity index, which may suggest less toxicity (Figure S4). 35 We co‐stained sections with X34 and HJ3.4b and found volumetric reductions in HJ3.4+ “halo” of diffuse amyloid around the volume of fibrillar X34+ plaques, as well as a reduction in the amount of diffuse, non‐X34‐associated HJ3.4, particularly within the hippocampus (Figure 3B,C). These data suggest that GABAergic Bmal1 KO alters the morphology of fibrillar Aβ plaques and mitigates the accumulation of non‐fibrillar amyloid.

GABAergic Bmal1 KO mitigates neuropil p‐tau accumulation in light‐dark

We next examined peri‐plaque pathological changes in VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice and Cre−;5xFAD control mice. The growth of amyloid plaques induces a combination of maladaptive responses in nearby neurites including axonal swelling, overexpression of Aβ cleaving and lysosomal enzymes, and promotion of tau phosphorylation. 36 , 37 , 38 We stained for dystrophic neurites using an antibody against the lysosomal enzyme LAMP1 and colocalized them around X34+ Aβ plaques (Figure 4A). We found no major change in the total volume of neuritic dystrophy or in the amount of dystrophy normalized per X34 plaque (Figure 4A,B) between genotypes in any of the regions imaged. However, when we stained for peri‐plaque p‐tau (phosphorylated at S202 and T205) using the antibody AT8, we found a dramatic reduction in VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice compared to 5x;Cre− controls, with >75% of AT8 in the vicinity of imaged X34 plaques reduced per unit of X34 volume in both the hippocampus and thalamus (Figure 4C,D).

We next examined microglia, brain parenchymal immune cells capable of both protective and detrimental functions in the presence of Aβ pathology, to determine if they might be implicated in the amyloid and tau changes seen in VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice. 39 , 40 We used confocal imaging to characterize Iba1/CD68 double‐positive microglia around X34+ plaques, which indicate phagocytic activated microglia. Though a trending decrease in Iba1+ volume around plaques in the cortex was noted, we saw no other major changes in any of our microglia markers between VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice and 5x;Cre− mice (Figure S5). We also performed transcript analysis for various homeostatic and disease‐associated microglia (DAM) markers in bulk hippocampal tissue and saw no changes (Figure S5C). 41 These data suggest that GABAergic Bmal1 KO in the setting of LD reduces peri‐plaque p‐tau aggregation, without changing neuritic dystrophy or Aβ‐induced microgliosis.

GABAergic Bmal1 KO has minimal effect on Aβ plaque pathology in constant darkness

The effect of GABAergic Bmal1 KO on circadian behavior can be partially masked by the LD cycle, so we next asked if allowing Cre+ mice to be fully arrhythmic might modulate the effect on amyloid pathology. Starting at 6 weeks of age, we placed VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD (and 5x;Cre− littermates, all female) in DD until age 5 months, when they were harvested and analyzed immunohistochemically for Aβ aggregation and related pathology, as described earlier. Surprisingly, 5‐month‐old VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice aged in DD displayed no differences in total or fibrillar plaque density in any of the analyzed regions, as compared to 5x;Cre− littermate controls (Figure 5A–B). No effect was seen on peri‐plaque NP‐tau aggregation using AT8. However, VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice did show a consistent trend toward reductions in LAMP1+ neuritic dystrophy in all three regions, though none reached individual statistical significance (Figure 5D).

Since VGAT‐BMAL1 KO only impacted Aβ plaque accumulation under LD conditions, we next questioned whether the light cycle (LD vs DD) itself might impact plaque accumulation. We re‐examined our X34+ plaque burden data, comparing between LD‐ and DD‐exposed mice (regardless of Cre genotype, as X34 plaques are not impacted by VGAT‐BMAL1 KO), and observed that 5xFAD mice kept in LD in our paradigm developed 70% more fibrillar plaques in the cortex than those kept in DD (Figure 5E,F). This occurred in both Cre− and Cre+ mice, suggesting a Cre‐independent effect of light, and similar non‐significant trends were seen in the hippocampus and thalamic regions, particularly in Cre− mice. This suggests that LD may promote plaque formation and that the inhibitory effect of VGAT‐BMAL1 KO on plaque formation in 5xFAD mice may only be evident in the more plaque‐conducive environment provided by LD conditions.

GABAergic Bmal1 KO alters APP processing in LD conditions

We next sought to examine possible mechanisms mediating the effect of GABAergic Bmal1 KO on plaque burden, focusing specifically on LD conditions. As we did not see an effect on microglial markers, we examined APP processing, as APP undergoes a series of proteolytic cleavages to be processed into Aβ. APP is first cleaved by beta secretase/BACE1 to form soluble APPβ (sAPPβ) and a β‐carboxy terminal fragment (β‐CTF). sAPPβ is then further cleaved by the gamma secretase complex, which includes familial AD risk gene products PSEN1 and PSEN2, to produce Aβ. 42 , 43 Alternatively, APP can be processed first by α secretases to produce non‐amyloidogenic products, including α‐carboxy terminal fragment (α‐CTF). As a proxy for APP processing, we first measured the levels of mouse Bace1 and Psen1 transcripts in bulk hippocampus from VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice to 5x;Cre− controls at six time‐of‐day time points. We found a statistically significant rhythm in mPsen1 transcript in Cre− mice that was blunted in 5x;Cre+ mice. The acrophase or peak of Psen1 rhythm was at ZT20‐24/0, just before the transition from the active to inactive period (Figure S6A). The overall expression of Psen1 was also suppressed by 20% (Figure 6A). Similar examination of Bace1 transcript revealed no rhythmic activity (Figure S6B). Overall levels of mBace1 were suppressed roughly 20%, but this difference did not meet statistical significance (p = 0.055). Expression of both transcripts in DD mice was unchanged in Cre+ mice relative to Cre−, suggesting that suppression of mouse Psen1 by Bmal1 KO may be light‐dependent (Figure 6A).

We next analyzed the levels of APP processing proteins and products via immunoblotting in cortical lysates from 5x;Cre+ mice to 5x;Cre− controls in LD. Total CTF amount (α‐CTF + β‐CTF) relative to full‐length (FL) APP was decreased in 5x;Cre+ mice relative to Cre−;5xFAD littermates, suggesting that total proteolytic conversion of APP via amyloidogenic and non‐amyloidogenic pathways may be suppressed in VGAT‐BMAL1 KO mice (Figure 6B,C). However, protein levels of BACE1 and PSEN1 protein remained similar in 5x;Cre+ mice and Cre−;5xFAD controls (Figure 6B,C). With regard to PSEN1, this may be partially explained by the PSEN1 antibody measuring both endogenous murine and transgenic human PSEN1 from the 5xFAD transgene, the latter of which may be unaffected in GABAergic Bmal1 KO. These data suggest that GABAergic Bmal1 KO in LD suppresses APP proteolytic processing, possibly by inhibiting expression of murine PSEN1 transcript.

The LD cycle and GABAergic Bmal1 deletion interact to regulate ECM transcriptome

Finally, we explored transcriptomic changes occurring in VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice under both LD and DD conditions. We performed bulk RNA‐seq on hippocampal tissue from LD‐ and DD‐exposed 5x;Cre+ mice to 5x;Cre− controls. An initial analysis revealed 391 transcripts that were differentially regulated in Cre+ mice relative to Cre− (p < 0.005) in LD conditions (Figure 7A). Of these, 108 were categorized as statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.1). By contrast, under DD conditions, only 27 transcripts showed nominal differential expression between 5x;Cre− and 5x;Cre+ mice, none of which remained significant after FDR correction, underscoring the role of the LD cycle in gating the transcriptional response to GABAergic Bmal1 KO (Figure 7B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis of the DEG list from Cre− to Cre+;5xFAD mice in LD conditions revealed enrichment of transcripts related to extracellular matrix (ECM)–receptor interaction (including Col1a2, Lama5, and Lamc3) as well as lysosomal function (including Naga, Fuca1, and Glb1) (Figure 7C). A majority (94 out of 108) of these changes were the result of gene upregulation (Figure S7A). To identify putative upstream transcription factors that could regulate these transcripts, we performed in silico analysis using ChEA3, which uses a collection of libraries including ENCODE and ARCHS4 to identify transcription factor–target associations. 44 The top 10 upstream regulators by integrated mean rank were PRRX2, FOXC2, SOX18, GLIS2, FOXS1, AEBP1, ATOH8, HIC1, OSR1, and PRRX1 (Figure 7D). We then examined the rhythmicity of these factors, aside from FOXC2 and FOXS1, which were not detected, at the translatome level using our previously published mouse brain circadian gene expression atlas. 45 Of these eight factors, Aebp1 showed rhythmicity in the largest number of conditions, including in bulk cerebral cortex tissue from both WT and APP/PS1 mice, suggesting either direct or indirect circadian regulation (Figure S7B,C). AEBP1, a transcription factor involved in ECM formation, was previously shown to be upregulated in the human AD hippocampus and associated with amyloid plaque and pre‐tangle tau pathology. 46 , 47 We found that AEBP1 protein levels were increased by roughly 25% by Western blot in the cortex from VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice (Figure 7E). Moreover, we observed that 25 known AEBP1 target genes (identified by ChEA3) were upregulated in the cortex of VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice (compared to 5x;Cre− controls) under LD conditions, but not under DD conditions (Figure 7F). In general, AEBP1 target gene expression was increased 40% (Figures 7F, S7D). Together, these results suggest that GABAergic Bmal1 KO induces ECM and lysosomal gene expression in a light‐dependent manner and implicate AEBP1 – an upstream regulator of ECM and fibrotic pathways – as a partial mediator of these transcriptomic changes. 48 , 49

DISCUSSION

Disruption of circadian rhythms is highly prevalent in neurological disorders such as AD. 50 , 51 Age, the primary risk factor for developing AD, may contribute to disease progression partly through age‐related circadian decline. 52 Aβ pathology starts to develop roughly 20 years before the development of cognitive symptoms. In most AD patients, this constitutes middle age, when transcriptional and hormonal circadian rhythms begin to decline. 53 , 54 , 55 Thus, we sought to determine whether circadian fragmentation could influence Aβ‐related pathology, including neuritic dystrophy and NP tau aggregation. Given the SCN's principal role in regulating circadian rhythms, we employed a genetic model of SCN disruption using VGAT‐iCre to drive Bmal1 deletion within the SCN and other GABAergic neurons, 17 which caused arrhythmicity in DD and desynchronized rhythms, deconsolidated sleep, and blunted clock gene rhythms in LD conditions. When crossed with 5xFAD mice, VGAT‐BMAL1 KO unexpectedly mitigated Aβ plaque accumulation and plaque‐associated tau phosphorylation, reduced mPSEN1 expression and amyloidogenic APP cleavage, and induced AEBP1 and ECM gene expression. However, all of these effects only occurred under standard LD conditions and not in DD conditions. Moreover, 5xFAD mice had a lesser plaque burden in general when raised in DD rather than typical LD conditions. Our data suggest that the central circadian clock interacts with the LD cycle to regulate amyloid plaque accumulation and tau phosphorylation in a complex manner that has not been previously appreciated.

Our experiments suggest that GABAergic Bmal1 KO could mitigate Aβ plaque formation in LD by reducing Aβ production. Previous studies showed that interstitial fluid Aβ has a strong diurnal rhythm in mice, which is directly related to rhythms in neuronal activity. 12 , 56 , 57 However, the role of the circadian clock in regulating APP processing directly is unknown. We show that GABAergic Bmal1 KO suppresses amyloidogenic APP cleavage to produce CTFs, suggesting reduced Aβ production. This finding is associated with a blunting of the normal diurnal expression rhythm of murine PSEN1 under LD, culminating in overall decreased mPSEN1 expression. While these data provide a partial explanation of reduced plaque load in Bmal1 KO mice, further studies will be necessary to examine how both light exposure and the circadian clock regulate APP metabolism.

Second, our experiments imply a previously unexplored interaction between the GABAergic BMAL1 KO and the ECM. Structural proteins of the ECM – including collagens and laminins – are among the major non‐Aβ components of amyloid plaques from human patients. 58 , 59 , 60 Biochemical experiments demonstrate that Aβ–collagen and Aβ–laminin interactions can inhibit and, in some cases, reverse Aβ fibril formation. 61 , 62 Collagen I and VI treatment can protect cultured neurons from Aβ‐mediated cell death. 63 In vivo overexpression of collagen XXV in the J20 AD mouse model reduced plaque burden and caused smaller, more circular plaques, similar to what we observed in Bmal1 KO. 64 While we did not manipulate the expression of these proteins directly, our results are consistent with reports that demonstrate less Aβ accumulation in vitro and in vivo after ectopic expression of ECM proteins. It is notable that one of our predicted upstream factors, AEBP1, has been associated with both AD pathology and connective tissue deficits in humans. 46 , 47 , 49 AEBP1 regulation of the ECM is multifaceted, as the Aebp1 gene encodes both a transcription factor that regulates ECM gene expression and a secreted factor that activates profibrotic gene expression via the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway. 48 , 65 , 66 Further studies are needed to assess the role of AEBP1 specifically, and the ECM more generally, in the regulation of Aβ plaque pathology.

Another finding from our study is the reduction in peri‐plaque p‐tau in VGAT‐BMAL1 KO;5xFAD mice in LD conditions. This was independent of the effect on overall plaque burden, as it persisted on a per‐plaque basis. This result may reflect a decrease in the toxicity of plaques in Bmal1 KO mice in LD and could be associated with our described changes in Bmal1 KO plaque morphology and/or composition. Our group previously showed that global or astrocyte‐specific Bmal1 KO could mitigate tau pathology in the P301S PS19 tauopathy model. 67 In that work, the anti‐tau effect was attributed to changes in astrocyte activation and proteostatic function, while astrocytic Bmal1 in this model is intact due to the absence of astrocytic VGAT‐iCre expression. Future studies could use plaque‐associated tau seeding models to examine the impact of circadian disruption on amyloid‐tau interactions.

Our finding that GABAergic Bmal1 KO mice accumulate less Aβ and p‐tau pathology under LD conditions is perhaps unexpected, based on previous findings from our lab, as well as the general concept that circadian disruption should be detrimental in AD. 4 We previously showed that post‐natal whole‐body Bmal1 deletion accelerated plaque accumulation in APP/PS1‐21 mice, which harbor different APP and PS1 mutations than 5xFAD and tend to accumulate mostly fibrillar plaques. 68 Thus, that study differs from this one both in terms of the amyloid model as well as the type of Bmal1 deletion (global post‐natal deletion vs germline deletion in GABAergic neurons). BMAL1 exerts many effects on non‐neuronal cell types in the brain and periphery that could impact plaque formation, while VGAT‐mediated deletion avoids these effects. 69 Future studies examining VGAT‐BMAL1 KO on the APP/PS1‐21 background could help determine if these discrepancies in phenotype are due to differences between the APP/PS1‐21 and 5xFAD models, which might indicate that the circadian system influences aAβ deposition differently based on the specific attributes of the model (such as the Aβ40/Aβ42 ratios, morphology and distribution of plaques, and rate/age of accumulation). It also remains possible that VGAT‐BMAL1 KO alters overall neuronal inhibitory tone in the brain, leading to changes in activity‐related Aβ production and aggregation (independent of effects on circadian rhythms). 56 , 70 , 71 However, if this were true, we would expect to see similar effects of VGAT‐BMAL1 KO in both LD and DD conditions, which was not the case. Replicating our findings using a more SCN‐specific Cre, or another neuron‐specific Cre to delete Bmal1 (in non‐GABAergic neurons), could be an important next step.

The analysis of plaque development in both LD and DD conditions allowed us to evaluate the effects of GABAergic Bmal1 KO on Aβ pathology in both masking (LD) and non‐masking (DD) environments. 72 Bmal1 KO mice exhibited more severe circadian disruption in DD than LD conditions due to masking. We expected that masking might mitigate plaque accumulation, compared to full arrhythmicity in DD, though we observed the opposite. We entertain two possible, non‐exclusive explanations for this observation. The first is that Bmal1 KO in LD conditions shifts circadian behavior with respect to the light cycle, causing desynchrony between the activity rhythm and the light cycle, as evidenced by an earlier activity onset time and Nr1d1 gene expression (Figures 1 and 2). This desynchrony, which is absent in DD conditions, might cause cellular stress, which spurs gene expression, such as upregulation of AEBP1, which is not recapitulated by arrythmicity. 73 The second possible explanation is that the LD cycle enhances diurnal rhythms in neuronal activity and Aβ production in mice with an intact SCN, leading to increased peaks in Aβ levels in the brain, promoting amyloid deposition in Cre−;5xFAD mice. Disrupting this light cycle‐clock rhythmic synergy either by placing mice in DD conditions or deleting Bmal1 in the SCN (as in VGAT‐Bmal1 KO mice) would then mitigate peaks in neuronal activity, reducing Aβ production and plaque accumulation. Future studies will be needed to address the impact of the LD cycle and the circadian clock on rhythms in brain metabolism and activity in order to fully understand this phenomenon.

4.1 Limitations

In addition to depleting Bmal1 within the SCN, VGAT‐driven Cre also targets extra‐SCN interneurons in the hippocampus and cortex, which may influence Aβ pathology in these regions. While Aβ release and aggregation are highly correlated with excitatory synaptic activity, inhibitory interneurons do express APP and APP‐metabolizing enzymes and contribute to overall plaque load. 56 , 74 However, we would expect these local effects of inhibitory Bmal1 KO to persist under both LD and DD conditions, which was not the case. Additionally, it is notable that suppression of plaque aggregation by Bmal1 KO was restricted to female mice aged in LD conditions. In either of the two groups with less Aβ pathology overall (males and/or DD‐aged mice), Bmal1 KO had no effect (Figures 5, S5). This could indicate that the impact of Bmal1 depletion on Aβ pathology has measurable effects only on plaque formation when overall Aβ pathology is high. It may also reflect complex interactions between circadian‐regulated pathways that modulate Aβ aggregation, the LD cycle, and biological sex, which is an area for future study. 75

5 CONCLUSION

In summary, we found that deletion of Bmal1 specifically in VGAT‐expressing GABAergic neurons, which fully disrupts central circadian clock function, reduced Aβ plaque burden and p‐tau in female 5xFAD mice and that this effect is eliminated when mice are maintained in constant darkness. Our findings suggest a complex interaction between circadian rhythms and the LD cycle in regulating amyloid pathology and provide new insights into how circadian function might be optimized or manipulated to prevent AD.
