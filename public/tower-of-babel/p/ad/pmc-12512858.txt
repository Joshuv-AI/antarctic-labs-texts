Are future physicians ready to be global citizens?: social responsibility tendencies of medical students
Authors: Şengül Erden, İpek Gönüllü, Ayla Keçeci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe purpose of this study is to determine the social responsibility levels of medical students and analyze global social responsibility levels based on different variables.MethodsThe sample of the study consisted of 1203 medical students enrolled in a Faculty of Medicine in the 2023–2024 academic year. A sociodemographic information form and the Global Social Responsibility Scale were used to collect data. The collected data were analyzed using descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, and mean) and the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests.ResultsThe participants had the highest mean score in the ecological responsibility dimension and the lowest mean score in the national responsibility dimension. The global social responsibility levels of the participants were significantly related to their gender, class year, economic status, maternal education levels, membership in student societies and non-governmental organizations, history of taking part in social responsibility projects, status of having taken the social services course, and status of following developments happening in the world.ConclusionIn the resolution of global issues, the existence of responsible individuals is important. Offering incentives to students who take part in social responsibility projects, ensuring that they participate in national and international social responsibility projects, and encouraging them to become members of non-governmental organizations will have a key role in their acquisition of relevant skills.

Introduction

Social responsibility is a concept that involves defending the needs of others and focusing on social issues that affect modern global societies and communities [1]. While it used to be considered from a national point of view, social responsibility has started to be envisioned in the global sense as the influence of globalization has risen [2]. In the context of global social responsibility, individuals have responsibilities toward all living beings, nature, and the world [3]. In particular, physicians, who put the person at the center and need to work for the interests of people, are expected to fulfill their responsibilities in this sense. Roemer stated that in line with the ethical codes of social responsibility in medicine, “physicians have responsibilities in working to help prevent diseases and protect the health of individuals and society, providing services appropriate for the health-related needs of individuals without discrimination, and operating in collaboration with other members of the healthcare team and public authorities” [4]. In other words, the concept of social responsibility encompasses human rights, equality, social justice, and freedom of expression [5].

In 1995, the World Health Organization (WHO) defined the social responsibilities of medical schools as the responsibility to shape education, research, and service activities in a way that addresses the main health issues of the community, region, and/or nation that they are obliged to serve and associated the concept of “social responsibility” with the concept of “social accountability”, underlining that medical schools should be accountable to their communities [6]. In this context, medical schools, which have important duties in the provision of healthcare services, are expected to develop social responsibility skills in their students to meet the health-related needs of society and present evidence that these skills are taught to their students to the society in question [7]. Moreover, today, diseases are considered not only biomedical/pathophysiological but also behavioral, social, cultural, and ecological processes, and a qualified physician is expected to adopt a holistic approach to their patients [8].

A physician should not only resolve diseases but also become an agent of change in the process of monitoring regional and global events that lead to these diseases and making the necessary arrangements. It is known that today, there is an increasing number of factors that lead to the creation/exacerbation of disease vectors such as the pollution of the natural environment, global warming, depletion of the ozone layer, hazards posed by electromagnetic fields, armed conflicts, and ill-advised social habits. Therefore, there is a clear need for individuals who have made global social responsibility a part of their personality to resolve problems that threaten human life and the planet and can put this mindset into action effectively [3]. In particular, the developments experienced in the digital world lead students to encounter information about global problems more frequently. For this reason, while shaping curricula, arrangements that will develop a sense of global social responsibility should be made, global issues should be included in the curricula, and the relevant instruction activities should be structured in compliance with the content [9]. Considering the increasing mobility of students and personnel in the field of healthcare as a consequence of the disease outbreaks, wars, and economic problems experienced in the world in recent years, developing global social responsibility in curricula is a sensitive topic that needs to be addressed, especially in the context of healthcare professionals [3].

However, in the literature, it is seen that the number of studies measuring the social responsibility/global social responsibility levels of medical students is limited. Available studies have shown that medical students have positive attitudes toward social and familial responsibilities [10], male students have a more limited position in terms of issues such as practices carried out to achieve environmental sustainability and the use of public transportation [11], the social responsibility motivations of students are raised by factors such as selecting one’s department of study in an informed manner, personality, moral commitment, and the availability of a community-based program at the university [12], practices/projects/courses on social responsibility education increase the publicity and accountability of medical schools in society, and these programs contribute significantly to the capacities of students regarding social responsibility [11, 13]– [14].

In the National Core Curriculum for training in medicine before graduation in Turkey, in the context of global social responsibility, it is stated that topics such as the climate crisis, migration, discrimination, poverty, and inequality in healthcare should be integrated into curricula [15]. However, uncertainties and differences in the extent to which these issues are discussed in curricula and how they are discussed are a matter of curiosity. Likewise, the “2021 National Standards for Pre-Graduation Medical Training” created by the National Accreditation Council for Medical Education stated that “educational institutions are expected to have planned the projects or educational activities in which their students can take social responsibilities” [16]. Therefore, to achieve the requirements of this standard, it is first needed to structure curricula in an effective manner and know the preparedness levels of students for global social responsibility.

In light of this information, this study aimed to determine the global social responsibility levels of medical students and analyze their global responsibility levels in terms of different variables. For this purpose, answers to the following research questions were sought in the study:

What are the global social responsibility levels of medical students?Are the sociodemographic characteristics (gender, class year, economic status, maternal and paternal education levels, membership of student societies, status of taking the social services course, membership of non-governmental organizations (NGOs), participation in social projects, status of having someone with membership of NGOs in family/among friends, and status of following developments in Turkey and the rest of the world) of medical students significantly related to their social responsibility levels.

Methods

This descriptive and cross-sectional study was carried out with a quantitative design.

Participants

The sample of the study consisted of 1203 medical students enrolled in a Faculty of Medicine in the 2023–2024 academic year. Because it was aimed to reach the entire population (Year 1: 453, Year 2: 472, Year 3: 437, Year 4: 435, Year 5: 546, and Year 6: 383), students who agreed to participate in the study and filled out the data collection forms were included. Consequently, the data collection forms were filled out by 1267 of 2726 students. Data were collected from students who were available at the school/clinic during the data collection period. After the exclusion of those who did not fill out the forms completely and correctly, 1203 students (Year 1: 317, Year 2: 331, Year 3: 119, Year 4: 115, Year 5: 215, and Year 6: 106) constituted the sample.

Data collection instruments

A sociodemographic information form and the Global Social Responsibility Scale were used to collect data.

Sociodemographic information form

The form was created by the researchers. It included questions about the characteristics of the participants such as age, gender, class year, perceived economic status, paternal and maternal education levels, and NGO membership status. Two faculty members, one expert in medical education and health education and one expert in medical education, were consulted to determine whether the questions on the form were suitable for the study, and the form was finalized based on the recommendations of the experts.

Global social responsibility scale

The scale was developed by Başer and Kılınç [3]. It is a 5-point Likert-type scale (1: Absolutely Disagree, 2: Disagree, 3: Undecided, 4: Agree, and 5: Absolutely Agree) consisting of 30 items. Items 3, 7, 11, 17, 18, 20, 21, 26, and 27 consist of negative statements, while the rest of the items consist of positive statements. The scale has four dimensions. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was calculated as 0.89. Scale was obtained via e-mail from the authors who developed the scale. The dimensions of the scale are explained below.

Action-oriented responsibility

This dimension refers to individuals being aware of the responsibilities they are expected to fulfill and acting upon these responsibilities of their own volition and with willingness.

Ecological responsibility

This dimension encompasses components such as protecting the environment, gaining awareness about environmental threats and solutions, using resources efficiently, generating new resources in addition to existing ones, and feeling responsible, both economically and socially, for any kind of change and consequence that may arise from the resources they produce.

Altruistic responsibility

Since actions taken in the context of social responsibility affect not only the individual but also other people and the natural environment, this dimension involves the individual acting with sensitivity in their choices and actions without prioritizing their own interests, with the awareness that they may affect their family, friends, community, society, and the entire world.

National responsibility

This dimension is defined as social responsibility in the form of fulfilling duties undertaken through socialization, respecting the requirements of cohabitation and shared social environments, feeling responsible for the portion of voluntary services directed toward society and the public that falls to oneself, and playing an active role in public services [17]– [18].

Data collection

Data were collected in the Spring semester of 2023–2024. WhatsApp was used to reach students and give them information about the study. Preclinical students (Year 1–3) who agreed to participate in the study filled out the forms that they obtained from the Student Affairs Office when they were attending the occupational skills course. To include clinical students, assistance was received from the employees of the Student Affairs Office at their sites of compulsory clinical internship. These employees were provided with information about the study by the researcher and given the data collection forms. Clinical students (Year 4–6) who agreed to participate in the study filled out the forms and delivered the completed form to the employees.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed in the SPSS (25.0) program. Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, and mean values) were calculated for some demographic characteristics and social responsibility levels of the participants. The non-parametric Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to determine the relationships between the sociodemographic characteristics and social responsibility levels of the participants. The level of statistical significance was taken as 0.05.

Results

It was determined that 57% (n: 686) of the participants were women, 27.5% (n: 331) were 1st-year students, 53% (n: 638) were living at home, 52.9% (n: 636) had medium economic status, the mothers of 51.8% (n: 623) were university graduates, the fathers of 59.5% (n: 716) were university graduates, 58.2% (n: 700) were not members of any student society, 75.1% (n: 904) had not taken the Social Services course, 92.9% (n: 1117) were not members of any NGO, 87% (n: 1047) did not have any family member who was a member of an NGO, 61.9% (n: 745) did not have any friend who was a member of an NGO, 75.2% (n: 905) did not participate in social projects, 91.6% (n: 1102) followed developments in Turkey and/or the rest of the world, and the mean age of the participants was 20.51 ± 1.95.

Discussion

The medical students who participated in this study had the highest scores in the ecological responsibility dimension and the lowest scores in the national responsibility dimension of global social responsibility. Some other studies on the global social responsibility tendencies of university students have similarly revealed the highest scores in the ecological responsibility dimension and the lowest scores in the national responsibility dimension [18–20]. In another study conducted with university students, the students were found to have the highest scores in the altruistic responsibility dimension and the lowest scores in the national responsibility dimension [21]. It is seen that university students have scored high in the ecological responsibility dimension of global social responsibility in most studies. The similar result in this study may be associated with the provision of education about the protection of the environment at all levels of education. Additionally, the fact that global warming, the climate crisis, and environmental pollution impact the entire world may have played an important role in higher levels of ecological responsibility among university students. In addition to this, the increasing number of demonstrations and protests regarding environmental issues and the widespread expression of environmental issues on social media could have been related to favorable outcomes of studies concerning ecological responsibility [22–24]. The lower scores of the participants in the national responsibility dimension in comparison to their scores in other dimensions of global social responsibility may be explained by their increasing concerns about issues around the world rather than only those in their own country as a result of globalization.

In all dimensions of the Global Social Responsibility Scale, the female participants of this study had significantly higher scores than the male participants. The highest scores were obtained in the altruistic responsibility dimension by the female participants and the national responsibility dimension by the male participants. Other studies in the literature have also shown higher levels of responsibility among female participants in comparison to male participants [19, 25–31]. On the other hand, some studies have not shown a significant relationship between gender and social responsibility tendencies [17, 20, 32]. However, in studies focusing on gender and social responsibility, it has mostly been determined that women exhibit more altruistic, empathetic, and prosocial behaviors than men [33–35]. Although these findings vary between different cultures, women generally tend to care for children, healthy/sick family members, the elderly, and individuals in need within society. Therefore, it is thought that gender roles emphasizing caregiving may promote empathetic values and beliefs and help women develop altruistic attitudes [33]. In light of gender roles and empathetic tendencies, women donate more than men do [33, 36]– [37], show a stronger inclination to help their families and the world [33], and engage in altruistic behaviors that involve greater risk [34]. On the other hand, the existence of studies that do not find differences between genders [38, 39] suggests that there may be other dynamics at play in altruism.

In this study, the participants who were 2nd-year students had significantly higher action-oriented responsibility and national responsibility scores than those in other class years. Faiz and Karasu Avcı [26] found significantly different responsibility levels among university students in favor of those at lower class levels. As the class years of students increased, their responsibility levels decreased. On the other hand, in the study conducted with undergraduate and graduate nursing students by Işık and Keçeci [19], there were significant differences in all dimensions of global social responsibility in favor of the graduate students. Likewise, Konakay et al. [40] emphasized that participants with graduate education had higher levels of global social responsibility in comparison to those with pre-undergraduate education. Başeğmez [41] reported that the global social responsibility tendencies of high school students were directly proportional to their grade levels, and their global social responsibility tendencies increased as their grade levels increased. There are also other studies in the literature showing no significant change in the responsibility levels of students based on their class years or grade levels [32, 42–44]. Different relationships have been found between class levels and global responsibility tendencies in different studies. Conducting more studies to uncover the reason for these differences and employing qualitative methods in these studies will contribute to the understanding of factors other than class levels that could influence global social responsibility tendencies. Additionally, investigating whether the higher action-oriented responsibility and national responsibility levels of year 2 students are associated with the curriculum of the relevant faculty and making the necessary arrangements if they are associated may be beneficial to improve the global social responsibility levels of medical students.

The altruistic responsibility levels of the participants with high economic status were found to be higher than those of others in this study. Başeğmez [41] reported that the economic status of the family was a determinant in the global social responsibility tendencies of students, and students from high-income families had higher levels of global social responsibility. Asar [45] found higher responsibility levels in university students with higher levels of income in their families. Taylı [46] identified higher responsibility scores in students with middle-income families compared to those with low-income or high-income families, although the difference was not statistically significant. In contrast, other studies showed no significant relationship between family economic status and global social responsibility in students [18, 43]. Furthermore, Özkan et al. [47] reported lower levels of social responsibility in participants with higher levels of family income. It is seen that the perceived economic statuses of participants have affected their social responsibility levels in different ways in different studies. While it is thought that the higher altruistic responsibility levels of the participants of this study who had better perceived economic status were associated with their ability to financially help those in need and participate in philanthropic activities, more studies should be carried out on this issue.

In this study, while the education levels of the mothers of the participants were significantly associated with their global social responsibility levels, the education levels of their fathers were not a significant factor. The national social responsibility scores of the participants whose mothers had middle school degrees were higher than the scores of those whose mothers had primary school, high school, undergraduate, or graduate degrees. Faiz and Karasu Avcı [26] stated that maternal education level led to a significant difference in the action-oriented responsibility dimension of global social responsibility. Güdürü [43] reported that students with mothers who had primary school or high school degrees had higher responsibility levels than those whose mothers were illiterate, and as the education levels of their mothers increased, the responsibility levels of students also increased. On the other hand, Başeğmez [41] did not observe a significant difference in the global social responsibility levels of students linked to the education levels of their mothers or fathers. Asar [45] also found no significant difference in the responsibility tendencies of students based on the education levels of their fathers. It is seen that most previous studies have shown maternal education levels to be more influential on the responsibility levels of students than paternal education levels.

The action-oriented responsibility, ecological responsibility, and altruistic responsibility levels of the participants of this study were significantly associated with whether they were members of student societies and whether they had anyone among their friends who had membership in NGOs. The presence of family members with NGO memberships was also significantly associated with the scores of the participants in all dimensions of global social responsibility. Besides, the action-oriented responsibility scores of the participants varied significantly depending on whether they were members of NGOs. Previous studies have demonstrated higher levels of responsibility among students with NGO memberships [19, 21, 30, 47]. However, Kırılmaz et al. [44] reported that NGO membership did not create a significant difference in the global social responsibility tendencies of participants. Most studies on responsibility have indicated higher responsibility levels in participants with NGO memberships. The global social responsibility scores of the participants who were NGO members were also higher in this study. In addition to this, the global social responsibility scores of the participants who had individuals with NGO membership in their families or friendship circles were also higher. NGO membership is usually a voluntary behavior. Individuals who are members of NGOs act in compliance with the objectives of their organization to serve society. Thus, individuals who are members of NGOs are expected to have higher levels of global social responsibility. A similar situation is seen with respect to student societies. In student societies, individuals perform the tasks that are expected of them by their society. This contributes to the development of their sense of responsibility. The presence of NGO members in a person’s family or among their friends may also be effective in making them more sensitive to global issues by increasing their awareness regarding these issues.

In this study, the participants who had taken the social services course had higher scores in the action-oriented responsibility and ecological responsibility dimensions. Participation in social responsibility projects also led to a significant difference in the action-oriented responsibility, ecological responsibility, and altruistic responsibility scores of the participants. Similarly, in the literature, higher levels of responsibility have been seen in students who take part in social responsibility projects [17, 41]. Özkan et al. [47] identified a significant relationship between the social responsibility scores of their participants and whether the participants had participated in a group of volunteers in the last year. Some studies in the literature did not show a significant difference in global social responsibility levels based on whether their participants had taken social services courses [32, 48]. It is seen that different results have been reported in the literature on the relationship between responsibility levels and the statuses of individuals to take social services courses or participate in social responsibility projects. The higher global social responsibility scores of the participants of this study who had participated in social responsibility projects were associated with their more developed feelings of responsibility. Individuals usually participate in social responsibility projects voluntarily, and with the help of these projects, they help other people and living beings. It is believed that the voluntary participation of individuals in these projects is influenced by their feelings of responsibility toward other people and living beings.

The scores of the participants of this study in all dimensions of global social responsibility except for the national responsibility dimension were associated with their statuses of following developments in social issues. Gürbüz and Aydın [18] also reported that students who were interested in global issues had significantly higher social responsibility levels than those who got interested in these issues occasionally.

Conclusion

Today, several global issues such as war, migration, climate change, environmental pollution, and epidemic diseases threaten human life. In the resolution of global issues, it is important to have individuals with responsibility, and to train such individuals, it is needed to include practices that will improve the social responsibility levels of students more in university curricula. In this context, offering incentives to students who take part in social responsibility projects, ensuring that they participate in national and international social responsibility projects, and encouraging them to become members of NGOs will have a key role.

While data were collected from 1203 participants in this study, these individuals were students of the medical faculty of one public university. To present the general situation better, it is important to perform multicenter studies by collecting data from multiple universities. Collecting data not only from pre-graduation students but also from individuals actively working in health-related jobs such as physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and dietitians in these studies will inform educational institutions and policymakers regarding how the global social responsibility levels of individuals working in the field of health could be enhanced. Further, to better demonstrate the variables that can lead to significant differences in global social responsibility tendencies, it is recommended that quantitative and qualitative research methods be used together in future studies.
