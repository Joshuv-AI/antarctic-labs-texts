Pfannenstiel versus periumbilical specimen extraction in laparoscopic low anterior resection: a comparative analysis of incisional hernia risk, postoperative recovery, and oncologic outcomes
Authors: Seung Yeon Kang, Dong Hyun Shin, Hyeon Woo Bae, Yoon Dae Han, Min Soo Cho, Hyuk Hur, Byung Soh Min, Kang Young Lee, Seung Yoon Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIncisional hernia is a common long-term complication following colorectal surgery. In laparoscopic low anterior resection (LAR) for rectal cancer, specimen extraction is most commonly performed via a periumbilical midline incision, although the Pfannenstiel incision has emerged as an alternative approach with the potential to reduce hernia rates. However, its adoption has been limited by technical demands, and comparative evidence in a homogeneous rectal cancer cohort remains scarce. This study compared incisional hernia risk, postoperative recovery, and oncologic outcomes according to extraction site in laparoscopic LAR.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients who underwent laparoscopic LAR for rectal cancer between 2014 and 2021. Specimen extraction was performed via either a Pfannenstiel incision or a periumbilical midline incision. The primary outcome was incisional hernia at the extraction site, assessed by computed tomography. Secondary outcomes included postoperative recovery, perioperative complications, and long-term oncologic outcomes. Risk factors for incisional hernia were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression.ResultsAmong 242 patients, 115 underwent Pfannenstiel extraction and 127 periumbilical extraction. Incisional hernia occurred in one patient (0.9%) in the Pfannenstiel group and 12 patients (9.4%) in the periumbilical group (p = 0.003). Pfannenstiel extraction was associated with shorter hospital stay, earlier return of bowel function, and lower analgesic requirements. On multivariable analysis, periumbilical extraction was an independent risk factor for incisional hernia. Disease-free and overall survival were comparable between groups.ConclusionsPfannenstiel specimen extraction was associated with a lower risk of incisional hernia and improved postoperative recovery after laparoscopic LAR without compromising oncologic outcomes, supporting its use as an effective specimen retrieval option in minimally invasive rectal cancer surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10151-026-03393-7.

Introduction

Laparoscopic low anterior resection (LAR) has become the standard surgical approach for mid and low rectal cancers, offering reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, and improved cosmetic outcomes compared with open surgery [1, 2]. However, specimen extraction through a mini-laparotomy incision remains a potential source of complications, particularly incisional hernia (IH), which affects 10–20% of patients following abdominal surgery and leads to chronic pain, functional impairment, and substantial healthcare costs [3, 4].

Traditionally, an extended periumbilical midline incision at the umbilical port site has been widely used for specimen extraction in laparoscopic colorectal resections [5]. The Pfannenstiel incision, a low transverse suprapubic approach with muscle-splitting characteristics, has also been used as an alternative extraction site and has been associated with lower incisional hernia rates and favorable postoperative recovery in colorectal surgery [4, 6–15]. Midline incisions transect the linea alba and have been associated with impaired fascial healing, potentially predisposing patients to incisional hernia formation [3, 4].

Despite these advantages, the routine adoption of Pfannenstiel extraction in laparoscopic LAR for rectal cancer may be influenced by surgeon preference and technical considerations related to specimen exteriorization, proximal transection, and anvil placement through a more caudal extraction site.

Although prior studies have evaluated extraction site-related outcomes in heterogeneous colorectal populations, data focusing exclusively on patients undergoing laparoscopic LAR for rectal cancer remain limited. In particular, comparative evidence addressing incisional hernia risk, postoperative recovery, and oncologic outcomes according to extraction site within a homogeneous rectal cancer cohort is scarce.

Therefore, this study compared the incidence of IH between periumbilical and Pfannenstiel extraction sites in patients undergoing laparoscopic LAR for rectal cancer. In addition, postoperative recovery parameters and long-term oncologic outcomes were analyzed to provide technique-oriented, real-world evidence to inform practical decision-making regarding specimen extraction strategies in minimally invasive rectal cancer surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Severance Hospital, Yonsei University College of Medicine (IRB no. 4–2025-0595), and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the analysis.

The medical records of patients with rectal cancer at our institution between January 2014 and December 2021 were retrieved from a prospectively maintained database. We excluded patients with synchronous colon cancer, emergency surgery, stoma formation, conversion to open surgery, abdominoperineal resection, or surgery with a palliative aim. We also excluded patients with incomplete data, undocumented extraction site, non-standard extraction sites, and insufficient follow-up or imaging for IH evaluation.

Based on the specimen extraction site, the patients were categorized into the periumbilical (n = 127) and Pfannenstiel (n = 115) groups (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Patient selection flowchart. Treatment

Preoperative staging investigations included colonoscopy with biopsy; preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) testing; computed tomography (CT) chest, abdomen, and pelvis; and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) pelvis and rectum. Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) was used for patients with mrT3/4 middle or lower rectal cancer or positive lymph nodes. The treatment details were as described previously [16, 17]. Total mesorectal excision (TME) plus lymph node (LN) dissection was performed in all patients, with the extent of resection determined by the status of the primary tumor (as previously reported) [18, 19]. Adjuvant chemotherapy was based on the surgical pathology results and followed National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines [20].

The present study evaluated two specimen extraction incisions: periumbilical midline and Pfannenstiel. All procedures were performed by five board-certified colorectal surgeons at a high-volume tertiary referral center. All surgeons had substantial experience in laparoscopic colorectal and rectal cancer surgery and had performed standardized laparoscopic low anterior resection within a uniform perioperative care pathway. The extraction site was selected according to the operating surgeon's preference and intraoperative judgment.

In the periumbilical group, the specimen was extracted through a vertical midline incision around or just below the umbilicus (approximately 5–6 cm in length), created by extending the umbilical port site superiorly, inferiorly, or directly through the umbilicus. All fascial layers were closed using a continuous mass closure technique with a slowly absorbable suture.

In the Pfannenstiel group, specimen extraction was performed through a low transverse suprapubic incision (5–6 cm) made just above the pubic symphysis. The anterior rectus sheath was opened transversely, and the rectus muscles were separated along the midline without transection. The posterior sheath was then incised vertically to enter the peritoneal cavity. After specimen extraction, the posterior sheath was routinely closed vertically with a continuous closure technique, and the anterior sheath was closed in a transverse direction using a continuous mass closure technique with a slowly absorbable suture. A wound protector was routinely used during specimen extraction in both groups.

Follow-up evaluation

Patients were followed every 3 months for the first 3 years, every 6 months for the next 2 years, and then once annually. The follow-up evaluations consisted of physical examination, serum CEA testing, abdominopelvic CT, chest CT, endoscopy, and toxicity evaluations. Biopsy, MRI, and/or fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) were performed if recurrence was suspected. Disease recurrence was diagnosed using histology (when available) or radiologic imaging. Local recurrence was defined as histologic or clinical evidence of tumor re-growth near the primary tumor site after resection and systemic recurrence as the presence of distant metastasis confirmed by histology or imaging. Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time to first tumor recurrence (local or distant) or death, while overall survival (OS) was defined as the time to death from any cause.

Outcome measurements

The primary endpoint was the incidence of IH at the specimen extraction site. IH was defined as a postoperative fascial defect at a previous surgical incision site (excluding trocar sites) with protrusion of intra-abdominal contents, confirmed by CT imaging and typically assessed ≥ 12 months after surgery [21]. Postoperative CT scans were performed as part of a standardized oncologic surveillance protocol, independent of extraction site, and were reviewed by one study author; equivocal cases were adjudicated by consensus. Patients without follow-up CT imaging were excluded. Time to IH was defined as the interval between surgery and the CT scan that first confirmed the hernia, regardless of symptoms. Tumor location was determined by the distance from the anal verge on preoperative pelvic MRI.

The secondary outcomes included perioperative outcomes and postoperative complications. Perioperative outcomes included postoperative hospital stay, time to first flatus, time to resumed soft diet, postoperative resting visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores on postoperative days 1 to 4, and rate of intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (IV PCA) refill. Postoperative complications were classified according to the Clavien-Dindo system. Anastomotic leakage was graded using the International Study Group of Rectal Cancer (ISREC) classification [22].

Additional analyses evaluated potential risk factors for IH, including age, sex, body mass index, extraction site, prior abdominal surgery, diabetes mellitus, surgical site infection, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, alcohol consumption, and smoking status. Oncologic outcomes included 5-year DFS, OS, number of harvested lymph nodes, and proximal and distal resection margin lengths.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges or means with standard deviations, as appropriate, and were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Risk factors for incisional hernia were evaluated using univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models for time to hernia occurrence. Variables with clinical relevance and those showing significance in univariate analysis were included in the multivariable Cox model. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. DFS and OS were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test.

This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines, and no artificial intelligence tools were used in the conduct or reporting of this study.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

Among 1875 patients with rectal cancer treated at our institution during the study period, we included those with pathologically confirmed rectal adenocarcinoma who underwent laparoscopic LAR. Of these, we excluded 1322 patients because of synchronous colon cancer, emergency surgery, stoma formation, conversion to open surgery, or abdominoperineal resection. Among the remaining 553 patients, 311 were excluded for incomplete data, undocumented extraction site, non-standard extraction sites, follow-up duration of < 12 months, or absence of CT imaging for IH evaluation. Finally, the analysis included 242 patients.

Based on the specimen extraction site, the patients were categorized into the periumbilical (n = 127, 52.5%) and Pfannenstiel (n = 115, 47.5%) groups (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, prior abdominal surgery, alcohol intake, smoking history, ASA classification, and perioperative treatment, were comparable between groups (Table 1). Among patients with prior abdominal surgery, previous midline laparotomy was identified in 11 patients in the periumbilical group and 8 patients in the Pfannenstiel group, while prior low transverse/Pfannenstiel incisions, including cesarean section, were identified in three and two patients, respectively. Tumor location differed significantly between groups, with the Pfannenstiel group having a higher proportion of mid and lower rectal tumors (p < 0.001). Operative and pathologic findings

The operative and pathologic outcomes are summarized in Table 2. Operation time and intraoperative blood loss showed no significant differences between the groups. The rate of low ligation of the inferior mesenteric artery was significantly higher in the Pfannenstiel group (93.0% vs 70.9%, p < 0.001). The length of mini-laparotomy was comparable. Pathologic outcomes, including TNM stage, LN harvest, tumor size, and lymphovascular invasion, were similar between the two groups. However, the proximal margin length was significantly longer in the Pfannenstiel group (median 14.0 cm vs. 9.0 cm, p < 0.001), while the distal margin length was significantly longer in the periumbilical group (median 3.0 cm vs. 1.5 cm, p < 0.001). Incidence of IH and IH-free survival

The results of the primary outcome analysis demonstrated a significant difference in incisional hernia rates between extraction sites (Fig. 2). IH developed in 12 of 127 patients (9.4%) in the periumbilical group compared with 1 of 115 patients (0.9%) in the Pfannenstiel group (p = 0.003). All symptomatic hernias occurred in the periumbilical group (3 cases), whereas no symptomatic IH was observed after Pfannenstiel extraction. In the periumbilical group, IH events included three symptomatic and nine asymptomatic radiologically detected hernias, while the single IH in the Pfannenstiel group was asymptomatic. Most IH events occurred within the first 24 months after surgery (Supplementary Table 1).Fig. 2Incisional hernia occurrence rates according to specimen extraction site. Incisional hernia occurred in 9.4% of patients after periumbilical extraction and in 0.9% of patients after Pfannenstiel extraction

Incisional hernia occurrence rates according to specimen extraction site. Incisional hernia occurred in 9.4% of patients after periumbilical extraction and in 0.9% of patients after Pfannenstiel extraction

Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a significantly higher IH-free survival rate in the Pfannenstiel group (log-rank p = 0.003; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Perioperative outcomes

Perioperative recovery parameters favored the Pfannenstiel group (Table 3). Patients with Pfannenstiel incisions had shorter postoperative hospital stays (p < 0.001), earlier return of bowel function, reflected by shorter time to first flatus (p < 0.001) and resumed soft diet (p < 0.001), and lower analgesic requirements, with fewer IV PCA refills (1.7% vs 7.9%, p = 0.037). Postoperative resting VAS pain scores were low in both groups and did not differ significantly between the periumbilical and Pfannenstiel groups on POD 1, 2, 3, or 4. The mean VAS score over POD 1–4 was also comparable between groups (median 2.5 [IQR, 2.0–2.8] vs 2.5 [IQR, 2.0–2.8], p = 0.683). Postoperative complication rates were comparable between groups. There were no significant differences in the incidence of SSI, anastomotic leakage, postoperative bleeding, ileus, or chyle leakage. Risk factors for IH

Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors for incisional hernia. Candidate variables, including extraction site, age, sex, body mass index, diabetes mellitus, surgical site infection, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, smoking, alcohol history, prior abdominal operation, ASA classification, and adjuvant chemotherapy, were assessed by univariate analysis. In multivariable analysis, periumbilical extraction and increasing age were independently associated with incisional hernia occurrence. Detailed regression results are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Oncologic outcomes

The median follow-up was 56 months. The 5-year DFS rates were 87.7% (95% CI 81.4–94.5) in the periumbilical group and 86.8% (95% CI 80.5–93.6) in the Pfannenstiel group (log-rank p = 0.763; Fig. 3A). The 5-year OS rates were 97.7% (95% CI 94.5–100) and 96.6% (95% CI 92.8–100), respectively (log-rank p = 0.640; Fig. 3). Discussion

IH remains a clinically significant long-term complication following colorectal surgery, impairing patient quality of life and often requiring additional surgical intervention. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated lower IH rates with Pfannenstiel incisions, establishing this approach as a well-recognized alternative to midline incisions rather than a novel concept in colorectal surgery [23, 24]. In laparoscopic LAR, Pfannenstiel extraction requires attention to adequate colonic mobility and reach during specimen exteriorization, proximal transection, and anvil placement, given the more caudal location of the Pfannenstiel incision compared with a periumbilical midline incision. However, splenic flexure mobilization and the vascular ligation strategy are primarily guided by the need for a tension-free anastomosis, bowel perfusion, oncologic principles, vascular anatomy, and surgeon judgment rather than by the extraction site itself.

In this comparative study, specimen extraction through a Pfannenstiel transverse suprapubic incision was associated with a lower incidence of IH compared with a conventional periumbilical midline incision (0.9% vs 9.4%). This difference is consistent with prior evidence demonstrating lower IH rates with Pfannenstiel compared with midline incisions [24, 25]. The lower hernia risk with the Pfannenstiel incision is mainly attributed to its off-midline orientation, which preserves abdominal wall integrity compared with midline incisions [6].

Rather than introducing a new surgical concept, the present study provides confirmatory evidence in a specific, technically demanding clinical setting—laparoscopic low anterior resection for rectal cancer. Unlike many prior analyses involving heterogeneous colorectal procedures, this study focused on a homogeneous cohort of rectal cancer patients undergoing laparoscopic LAR, enabling a more direct assessment of extraction-site effects within a uniform surgical context.

Beyond hernia prevention, Pfannenstiel extraction was also associated with favorable functional recovery parameters. Although resting VAS pain scores were comparable between groups, IV PCA refill was less frequent in the Pfannenstiel group. This finding suggests that Pfannenstiel extraction may be associated with lower additional analgesic requirement but not necessarily lower resting pain intensity. Patients in the Pfannenstiel group also had faster return of bowel function and shorter hospital stays.

Since fentanyl-based IV PCA can delay bowel motility [26], the lower PCA refill rate in the Pfannenstiel group may support the observed improvement in postoperative recovery. However, because anastomotic leakage was numerically more frequent in the periumbilical group, it may have contributed, at least in part, to delayed bowel recovery, a later resumption of oral intake, and a longer hospital stay; therefore, these recovery outcomes should be interpreted with caution. By simultaneously evaluating postoperative recovery, incisional hernia, and oncologic outcomes within a rectal cancer-only cohort, this study extends existing literature by validating the clinical applicability of Pfannenstiel extraction in laparoscopic LAR.

Despite differences in postoperative recovery, there were no significant differences in overall postoperative complications or severe complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III) between the two extraction approaches, supporting the procedural safety of Pfannenstiel extraction.

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis identified periumbilical extraction and older age as independent risk factors for IH. These findings are consistent with previous reports identifying extraction site and age as key contributors to incisional hernia formation [27–29].

Although alcohol history showed a significant association with IH occurrence in univariate analysis, this association did not persist after multivariable adjustment and is therefore likely attributable to confounding rather than a true protective effect. Although the number of IH events was limited and confidence intervals were therefore wide, the direction and consistency of the associations support the robustness of the observed risk pattern and suggest that the protective effect of Pfannenstiel extraction may be particularly relevant in higher-risk patients.

Notably, the Pfannenstiel incisions in this study were associated with longer proximal resection margins despite less frequent high IMA ligation, suggesting that meticulous mobilization and mesocolon dissection can compensate for lower ligation levels. Operation time and blood loss were comparable between groups, indicating that this approach does not impose meaningful technical disadvantages. The shorter distal margin observed in the Pfannenstiel group likely reflects a higher proportion of mid- to lower-rectal tumors in this cohort and is unlikely to be attributable to the extraction site itself, as both groups achieved margins consistent with oncologic adequacy. Furthermore, LN retrieval, long-term oncologic outcomes including 5-year DFS and OS, and recurrence patterns were comparable between groups, supporting the oncologic safety of Pfannenstiel extraction.

Importantly, this study was conducted in an Asian population with a relatively low body mass index, a cohort that has been underrepresented in previous extraction-site studies, which have predominantly been derived from Western populations. The observed reduction in incisional hernia despite a generally low-risk baseline profile underscores the reproducibility of the protective effect of Pfannenstiel extraction across different patient populations.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and non-randomized selection of specimen extraction sites introduce potential for selection bias and surgeon-related confounding, which represent a major limitation of this study. Because the extraction site was determined by surgeon preference and intraoperative judgment, surgeon-specific technical preferences may have influenced other operative decisions, including the level of IMA ligation. Therefore, the independent effects of extraction site and operative strategy cannot be fully separated, and differences in IMA ligation strategy and postoperative recovery outcomes should be interpreted with caution.

Second, although most baseline characteristics were comparable, tumor location differed between groups, which may reflect differences in case mix. Third, a substantial proportion of patients were excluded because of incomplete data and/or lack of CT follow-up, which may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias.

Although radiographically detected incisional hernias may not always translate into clinically symptomatic disease, CT-based assessment was chosen to provide an objective and standardized evaluation of extraction site fascial integrity.

In addition, postoperative CT images were primarily reviewed by a single study author, and although equivocal cases were adjudicated by consensus, interpretation bias cannot be completely excluded. Several established risk factors for incisional hernia could not be incorporated into the regression model. Peripheral vascular disease and preoperative hypoalbuminemia were infrequent and balanced between groups and could not be stably included given the limited number of events. Frailty was not assessed using a validated index. These factors may therefore have contributed to residual confounding. Abdominal wall thickness and subcutaneous fat thickness on preoperative CT were not evaluated because CT protocols and measurement planes were not standardized for this purpose, and these anatomical factors may also have contributed. Finally, the small number of IH events resulted in wide confidence intervals and imprecise estimates. Prospective randomized studies are warranted to validate these findings.

A key strength of this study is its focus on a homogeneous cohort of patients with rectal cancer, allowing us to minimize heterogeneity related to disease site by focusing exclusively on rectal cancer. In addition, the use of CT imaging to detect IH ensured the capture of both symptomatic and asymptomatic cases, whereas previous studies relying mainly on physical examination may have underestimated the true incidence of hernia. Taken together, our findings do not propose a new surgical concept but rather provide clinical validation of Pfannenstiel specimen extraction in a specific, technically relevant setting: minimally invasive rectal cancer surgery. As single-port and robotic platforms become more widely adopted, our results provide timely guidance for optimizing extraction site selection, with implications for both functional and cosmetic outcomes.

Conclusion

Pfannenstiel specimen extraction was associated with a significantly lower risk of incisional hernia after laparoscopic low anterior resection and was associated with favorable postoperative recovery, without compromising oncologic outcomes. These findings support Pfannenstiel extraction as a feasible and effective specimen retrieval option in minimally invasive rectal cancer surgery.
