Experimental investigation into friction reduction induced by normal vibration from frequency dependence to unified vibration velocity amplitude
Authors: Jiandong Lu, Zhen Zhao, Shuaibin Zhao, Caishan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVibration is recognized as an effective method for reducing friction. However, the underlying mechanisms governing friction reduction at different frequencies remains to be investigated. This study presents an experimental investigation into the frequency-dependent characteristics of friction reduction induced by normal vibration, covering a broad frequency range. At low frequencies, friction is significantly reduced only when macroscopic stick-slip and friction reversal occur, otherwise, the reduction in friction is limited. At high frequencies, vibration can reduce friction significantly, even in the absence of macroscopic stick-slip and friction reversal. Despite these distinct observations, the mechanism of friction reduction across the broad frequency range can be unified by the normal vibration velocity amplitude, which serves as a universal criterion for scaling friction reduction across frequencies. A further comparison with prior microscopic results reveals similar phenomena at both macro-scale and micro-scale.Keywords: Vibration, Friction reduction, Frequency dependence, Normal vibration velocity amplitudeSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Vibration and friction are widely present in multi-body systems and have also been applied in robotics, an emerging research frontier1–3. Research on how vibration affects friction, as a fundamental study, is crucial for practical applications in vibration-friction interactions. Vibration is recognized as an effective method of reducing friction4–7.

At the macro-scale, Storck and Littermann et al. experimentally show that vibration reduces friction when vibration alters the direction of friction force between two objects in relative motion, which is known as friction reversal8,9. Gutowski et al. show that when vibration causes the tangential relative velocity at the interface to attain a zero value, i.e. a momentary macroscopic stick, the instantaneous friction force decreases gradually without reversing its direction10. This behavior arises from the cyclic elastic deformation within the contact. The moment when the relative velocity reaches zero corresponds to the turning point of this elastic deformation cycle. It is this cyclic deformation that reduces the average friction, even though no macroscopic friction reversal occurs10–12. The above experimental phenomena can be well explained based on Coulomb and Dahl models8–12. The previous research findings mentioned above indicate that friction reduction is associated with macroscopic friction reversal and stick-slip caused by vibration. However, when the objects on the vibrating interface maintain continuous sliding all the time, it remains to be investigated whether vibration can effectively reduce friction.

Introducing normal vibration to the interface is one of the methods that can be attempted to achieve continuous sliding on a vibrating interface. But it is noted that the vibrator will generate a small-amplitude tangential vibration, even though only normal vibration excitation is input to the vibrator. This phenomenon arises from the structures of the vibrators. There must be a small gap between the vibration rod and the surrounding components of the rod. Otherwise, the rod will be stuck and the vibrator can not vibrate. Since the gap must exist, this phenomenon is almost inevitable. The above fact indicates that normal vibration and tangential vibration often occur simultaneously in vibration experiments. Notably, the prior macroscopic research on friction reduction resulting from normal vibration has not considered the tangential vibration existing simultaneously13,14.

With the advent of AFM (atomic force microscope) or FFM (friction force microscope), experimental studies have been conducted to examine the effect of vibration on friction at the micro-scale. Riedo and Jeon find that vibration can effectively reduce friction at the micro-scale15,16. Socoliuc finds that friction gradually decreases as the normal amplitude increases. This decrease become more significant near the normal resonance frequency. Moreover, when the normal amplitude exceeds a certain threshold, friction almost vanishes17. Shi and Luo also find that friction can be reduced to a very small value and can even reach superlubricity with the increase of amplitude18. The phenomenon that vibration leads to friction reduction at the micro-scale can be explained by contact potential energy17,19 or Prandtl-Tomlinson model18. Although Filippov argues that macroscopic friction is related to the kinetic behavior of microscopic contact pairs at non-vibrating interfaces20, there is a lack of experimental results that can prove the existence of a connection between friction reduction observed at the macro-scale and that at the micro-scale on the vibration interface.

Significantly, most existing studies explore the mechanisms underlying friction reduction primarily from the perspective of amplitude. Regarding vibration frequency, current research has only focused on its influence on the extent of friction reduction. Few studies have investigated whether the mechanism of friction reduction is related to frequency variations.

In this study, our experimental study was conducted by utilizing an independently designed experimental setup. During the experiment, only normal vibration excitation was sent to the vibration plate. The tangential vibration of the vibrating plate that occurred simultaneously was also monitored. The friction force was measured. The mechanism of friction reduction was analyzed based on the friction forces measured at different vibration frequencies.

Experimental setup and methods

The schematic diagram and the actual picture of the experimental setup are shown in Fig. 1. The vibration plate can provide sinusoidal vibration. A cylindrical slider is pulled by a metal sheet. The other end of the metal sheet is connected to a force sensor fixed with the tensile machine. The tensile machine pulls the slider horizontally at the constant pulling velocity on the vibrating plate. The friction force between the slider and the vibrating plate is measured by recording the pulling force with the force sensor. Under the stipulation that the pulling velocity is invariant, the average sliding velocity of the slider in the sliding direction remains unchanged. The measured pulling force represents the average friction force. It should be noted that a thin metal sheet is used to connect the slider to the force sensor in order to prevent the tangential connection between them from affecting the normal movement of the slider. This metal sheet is so thin that its normal rigidity can be considered negligible and it can not disturb the normal vibration of the slider. The metal sheet can also ensure effective tangential connection rigidity and elasticity in the sliding direction.

Three laser vibrometers are employed to measure the velocities of the slider and the vibration plate. Laser vibrometer 1 records the normal vibration velocity of the vibrating plate. Experimental materials

The cylindrical slider was made of steel and consisted of nine coaxially stacked cylindrical discs. The bottom disc had a smooth lower surface that contacted the vibrating plate. A threaded rod with a diameter of 5 mm was mounted perpendicularly on the top surface of the bottom disc and passed through the aligned 5-mm central holes of the remaining eight discs. The entire assembly was secured using a fastening nut. Each disc had an outer diameter of 30 mm and a height of 4.7 mm, giving the slider a total mass of approximately 0.23 kg. The average roughness of the bottom surface contacting the vibration plate was 0.4 m. The thin metal sheet linking the force sensor to the slider was made of steel, with a length of 35mm, a width of 35mm and a thickness of 25.1 m. The measured tangential elastic stiffness of the metal sheet was 3671 N/m.

Two types of vibration plates were manufactured, one made of steel and the other aluminium. Both plates had dimensions of 55mm in length and width, with a thickness of 3mm. The average surface roughness of the steel vibration plate was 0.4 m, and the average surface roughness of the aluminium vibration plate was 0.68 m. The experiments were completed separately on two vibration plates.

Firstly, a spirit level was utilized to adjust the vibration plate to a good level state, set the normal amplitude voltage and frequency using a vibration signal generator, and input the sinusoidal normal vibration excitation signals into the vibrator. Once the vibrator received the signals, it activated the vibration plate to vibrate. The vibration amplitude of the vibration plate was proportional to the input voltage value of the signal. Higher input voltage resulted in larger vibration amplitude.

Secondly, the pulling velocity was adjusted and the experimental duration was set through the tensile machine. The tensile machine then pulled the slider horizontally from a stationary position to a sliding state. In this progress, the force sensor measured the pulling force, corresponding to the friction force between the slider and the vibration plate. Three laser vibrometers recorded the normal and tangential vibration velocities of the vibrating plate as well as the sliding velocity of the slider in the direction of movement. The measured velocities were collected in the signal acquisition card. Specifically, the sampling frequency for the real-time friction force measurement was 1 kHz, while the sampling frequencies for the normal and tangential vibration velocities of the vibrating plate and the tangential sliding velocity of the slider were all 15 kHz.

Thirdly, the vibration plate ceased vibrating after the set duration, and the slider stopped sliding and returned to the initial position. To ensure accuracy, we repeated the friction force measurements at least three times for each set of vibration parameters. The experiment was completed when the friction forces in three consecutive measurements showed consistency.

Finally, the amplitude or frequency was altered, and the measurement process was repeated. Throughout all the experiments, the experimental temperature was maintained approximately 26C and the relative humidity was around 35%.

The vibration velocities measured by laser vibrometers 2 and 3 were plotted in one graph to determine whether the two velocity curves intersected and overlapped. The intersection indicated the change in the direction of the friction force at the macro-scale, meaning friction reversal happened, and overlap signified that the relative velocity was zero at the macro-scale, indicating stick occurred.

Given the constant pulling velocity of the tensile machine, the average sliding velocity of the slider remained constant, and the measured pulling force equated to the friction force. The measured pulling force was used to determine the sliding friction force, because this experiment concentrated on sliding friction.

Discussion

The experimental results presented in this work demonstrate that normal vibration can reduce macroscopic friction across a broad frequency range, from several tens of hertz to 10–12 kHz. The degree of friction reduction exhibits the frequency dependence.

The observed trends in the experiment are consistent with the contact-mechanical framework established by Popov and co-workers21–23. Popov’s theory indicates that friction reduction under normal vibration is governed by two physical parameters: the instantaneous indentation depth and the normal vibration velocity amplitude . The frequency dependence observed in our experiments reflects the importance of these two variables.

In the low-frequency regime, the system behavior aligns with the displacement-controlled quasi-static model21. Friction reduction originates primarily from the periodic unloading of the normal force, which interrupts the accumulation of tangential force. It is experimentally observed that the measured friction decreases with increasing vibration amplitude. Moreover, the addition of tangential vibration can serve as an effective strategy for achieving greater friction reduction at low frequencies, under the experimental conditions of this work.

In the high-frequency regime, the system transitions to inertia-dominated dynamics22. The experiments reveal that even extremely small displacement amplitudes can induce a pronounced reduction in friction. This is consistent with theoretical predictions: the velocity amplitude becomes the dominant parameter. The high frequency enables a minute displacement to generate a significant , thereby destabilizing the stick phase through dynamic mechanisms such as rapid indentation-induced transient

Although the manifestation of friction reduction differs between low and high frequencies, the underlying physical mechanism can be interpreted within a unified mechanical framework. By converting the displacement amplitudes of Figs. 2, 4b, 5b, 8b and 9b at different frequencies into the corresponding velocity amplitudes , the originally scattered data spanning several orders of magnitude exhibit a trend of convergence, as shown in Fig. 11. For the low-frequency experiments, only the range of small normal vibration amplitudes, where no macroscopic friction reversal or stick occurs, is selected for analysis. This selection aims to avoid interference from tangential vibration effects.

The results show that friction decreases with increasing . This supports the conclusion that the essential condition for governing friction reduction is , not the displacement amplitude 23. The fundamental mechanism by which normal vibration reduces friction lies in its ability to disrupt tangential sticking, and this capability is governed by the normal vibration velocity amplitude . A larger leads to a more rapid decrease in normal load, making tangential sticking easier to break, thereby resulting in lower friction force.

Furthermore, the friction reduction curves at different frequencies do not perfectly overlap in Fig. 11. The reason is that, in addition to governing friction reduction, the magnitude of reduction and the detailed dynamic behavior also depend on the system configuration21–23. The convergence of reveals the universal condition of friction reduction, whereas the remaining differences in the curves reflect the individual dynamic characteristics of the system.

Comparing the above macroscopic results presented in Figs. 8 and 9 with Socoliuc and Luo’s microscopic experimental findings, the macroscopic friction reduction with increasing normal amplitude resemble the microscopic observations17,18. Friction decreases as the normal amplitude increases, and when the amplitude increases beyond a certain threshold, friction decreases to a very low level or even reaches superlubricity. The above comparisons clearly demonstrate that there is the similarity between macroscopic and microscopic friction phenomena at the vibrating interfaces.

Conclusions

This work experimentally demonstrates the transition from quasi-static displacement control to inertia-dominated dynamics across a broad frequency range, providing a relatively comprehensive experimental perspective for understanding the mechanisms of friction reduction induced by normal vibration.

The study elucidates the evolution of friction reduction induced by normal vibration at different frequencies. The experimental data align with Popov’s theoretical framework: the low-frequency regime adheres to the quasi-static displacement-controlled model, while the high-frequency regime is governed by the inertia-driven dynamics model. Both regimes are unified under the criterion of the normal vibration velocity amplitude . Furthermore, cross-frequency normalization confirms the completeness and universality of this theoretical framework.

At the high-frequency vibrating interface, friction force decreases gradually with increasing normal amplitude. Once normal vibration amplitude exceeds a certain threshold, the friction force value tends towards very small or zero. At both macro-scale and micro-scale, the trend of friction force varying with the normal amplitude is similar. The similarity suggests that there might be some kind of correlation between the reduction of macroscopic friction and the variation of microscopic friction at the vibrating interface.
