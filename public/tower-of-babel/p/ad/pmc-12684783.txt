Foster Care Status as an Understudied Social Driver of Health Outcomes for Children Needing Cardiac Surgery
Authors: Anna Martens, Kimberlee Gauvreau, Taylor Smith, Michael Laccavole, Jennifer M Perez, Joanne Cox, Richard Antonelli, Snehal N Shah, Jane W Newburger, Katie Moynihan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren and youth in foster care (CYFC) are known to have greater health care needs than children not in foster care; however, little is known about the outcomes among those with heart disease.MethodsThis single‐center, retrospective study included patients (<18 years) who underwent cardiac surgery between 2017 and 2022. CYFC were identified using institutional medical records. Demographics, clinical characteristics, and outcomes were compared with non‐CYFC. Generalized linear models assessed associations with long‐term mortality, readmissions, and cumulative hospital days, adjusting for clinical factors and follow‐up duration. Child Opportunity Index, payer, and race were separately added to models. This cohort was also identified in the Pediatric Health Information System to calculate International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) code sensitivity and specificity.ResultsOf 4431 patients post‐cardiac surgery, 67 (1.5%) were CYFC. Compared with non‐CYFC, CYFC had lower Child Opportunity Index scores (median 61 versus 72, P=0.016) and were more likely to be Black and have Medicaid coverage. When adjusting for clinical variables, CYFC had higher mortality (adjusted odds ratio, 2.7 [95% CI, 1.0–6.9]), readmissions (adjusted incidence rate ratio, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.04–1.48]), and total hospital days (adjusted exponentiated coefficient, 1.54 [95% CI, 1.13–2.15]). Including Child Opportunity Index scores, payer, and race variably affected associations with long‐term outcomes. Only 16/67 (24%) CYFC were correctly coded in the Pediatric Health Information System, yielding sensitivity of 24% and specificity of 100%.ConclusionsCYFC with heart disease have adverse long‐term outcomes and greater hospital resource use. Improved capture of foster care status in administrative data is needed to guide future studies and reduce disparities.Keywords: congenital heart disease, foster care, outcomes, social driver, surgerySubject Categories: Disparities, Mortality/Survival, Social Determinants of Health, Pediatrics

Social drivers of health are factors that contribute to adverse outcomes for children with heart disease. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Identification of social drivers and understanding their influence on health outcomes guides efforts to addressing inequities. Social drivers are commonly perceived to include socioeconomic and insurance status; however, placement in foster care is an important, yet understudied social driver with impacts on economic stability, neighborhood environment, and social context. 9

Children and youth in foster care (CYFC) have disproportionately higher rates of medical and behavioral needs, including developmental delay, mental illness, and chronic health conditions, than those who are not in foster care (non‐CYFC). 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Among the nearly 400 000 children in foster care each year, 10% are considered medically complex and they are twice as likely to use medical equipment or require specialist services. 14 , 15 , 16 CYFC with medical complexity have 4 times longer lengths of stay than those in matched controls and significantly higher medical charges. 17 Their adverse health outcomes extend beyond their time in foster care, with an increased prevalence of chronic health conditions in adults previously placed in foster care. 18 , 19 Medical providers caring for hospitalized CYFC face unique challenges due to the additional medical, social, and legal complexities. 20 , 21 Foster caregivers who newly assume care of a child at hospital discharge are twice as likely to misunderstand discharge instructions than parents of children not in foster care and often leave feeling unprepared to provide effective posthospitalization care. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25

There is a paucity of research exploring the relationship between foster care and outcomes in children with heart disease. Pediatric heart disease serves as both a model for other complex chronic conditions and a condition with unique factors that may heighten impacts of being in foster care, underscoring the importance of studying this population. Children with heart disease are already at higher risk for additional health challenges, such as neurodevelopmental delays, and these risks may be further exacerbated by being in foster care. To address this gap, we characterize the population of CYFC undergoing cardiac surgery, assess impacts of being in foster care on outcomes and hospital resource use, and evaluate validity of identification of this variable in administrative data sets.

METHODS

We conducted a retrospective single‐institution cohort study in a quaternary pediatric hospital. All patients under the age of 18 years who underwent cardiac surgery at Boston Children’s Hospital between January 1, 2017 and December 31, 2022 were included. The patient’s first surgical admission during the study period was used as the index admission for surgical and acute outcome data, and last admission was used for follow‐up duration. The institutional review board determined that the protocol qualifies as exempt (IRB‐P00047053). The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article and its Data S1.

Multiple modalities were used to capture CYFC (Figure 1). The first method used International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes (Z‐code categories Z55–Z65), focusing on codes that specifically indicate a high‐risk social situation for the child, such as ICD‐10 code Z62.21 “child in welfare custody” (Appendix S1). 26 , 27 The second modality used natural language processing to identify key words in the medical record, such as the “Department of Children and Families” (Appendix S2). The final modality used the institutional Fyler code 8810 “not raised by parents.” All charts pulled from these modalities were then manually reviewed to confirm the child was in foster care at the time of hospital admission. We considered children in foster care to encompass care provided by both relatives and nonrelatives who were in formal custody of the child welfare system. 28 Patients in foster care for only part of the study period were counted as CYFC, and their index admission was defined as the first admission for which they were in welfare custody.

Demographics and clinical variables were obtained from institutional electronic medical records, as well as the Pediatric Health Information System (PHIS), which is an administrative database capturing clinical and resource use data across encounters at contributing freestanding children’s hospitals. In order to risk stratify the patients, we used scores from Risk Stratification for Congenital Heart Surgery‐2, a validated tool derived to assess the complexity of pediatric cardiac surgical procedures using ICD‐10 codes based on operative and in‐hospital mortality. 29 Medical complexity was determined by number of complex chronic conditions, which were also defined by ICD‐10 codes. 30 Follow‐up duration was calculated from date of first surgery to last admission or date of death. Other social drivers included race, payer (private, public [Medicaid/Medicare], self with other), zip code derived Child Opportunity Index (COI), 31 distance from hospital (miles), as well as need for interpreter. Race and ethnicity, defined by self‐report, was extracted from the electronic medical records in the following categories: White non‐Hispanic, Black non‐Hispanic, Hispanic, or Other (including unavailable, Asian non‐Hispanic, multiracial non‐Hispanic, or another race non‐Hispanic). COI 3.0 is a zip‐code derived measure that characterizes the quality of neighborhood environments to promote healthy child development on a scale of 1 to 100 (lower indicates less resources and opportunity). 32

Based on prior literature suggesting that longer‐term resource use and outcomes are more heavily influenced in this population than acute outcomes, primary outcome measures were (1) mortality (death at current follow‐up, binary), (2) cumulative days admitted to the hospital over the study period (continuous), and (3) number of total readmissions (counts), both planned and unplanned. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 33

Statistical Analysis

Variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages (categorical), or medians and interquartile ranges [IQR] (continuous). Comparisons of baseline characteristics and short‐term outcomes between CYFC and non‐CYFC were performed using Fisher’s exact or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests as appropriate. We assessed associations between CYFC status and the 3 primary long‐term outcomes using logistic regression for mortality at last follow‐up; log‐linear gamma regression for total hospital days; and Poisson regression for readmission counts. Length of follow‐up was included as an adjustment variable in the logistic and gamma models, and as an offset term in the Poisson regression model.

Relationships between CYFC status and outcomes are quantified using odds ratios (OR) for mortality, exponentiated regression coefficients for total hospital days, and incidence rate ratios for readmission. After initially adjusting for length of follow‐up, we included a fixed set of a priori defined clinical variables as illness severity proxies during index admission: Risk Stratification for Congenital Heart Surgery‐2 scores, number of complex chronic conditions, age at surgery, and surgical reoperation. For long‐term mortality we also included 30‐day readmission. 34 Finally, COI, payer, and race were each added separately to the models containing clinical variables. 2 , 35 , 36

Models were compared using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for mortality, coefficient of determination (R 2) for total length of stay, and McFadden Pseudo R 2 for readmissions. Analyses were conducted using complete cases; observations with missing data for any variables of interest were excluded from the corresponding multivariable regression (missingness: <1% for mortality/length of stay, <2.5% for readmission models). Denominators are reported. Analyses were conducted in R Studio version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Validity of ICD‐10 Code Z62.21

The institutional cohort identified from electronic medical record methodology (assumed to be the true rate of CYFC) was then compared with the same population in the PHIS to calculate the sensitivity and specificity of the ICD code Z62.21 for accurately identifying CYFC. These values were calculated based on counts of true positive, true negative, false positive, and false negative findings (sensitivity=100×true positive/(true positive+false negative) and specificity=100×TN/(false positive+true negative)). 37

RESULTS

Children and Youth in Foster Care Undergoing Cardiac Surgery

Of 4431 patients undergoing cardiac surgery, 67 (1.5%) were identified to be in foster care (Figure 1). We compared clinical and sociodemographic characteristics of CYFC with those of non‐CYFC (Table 1), and both acute and long‐term outcomes are presented (Table 2). Age, number of complex chronic conditions, and distribution of Risk Stratification for Congenital Heart Surgery‐2 scores did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (Figures S1). Racial and ethnic distribution differed between the 2 groups (P=0.024) with a higher proportion of CYFC, having a non‐Hispanic Black background (12.0% versus 4.6%, respectively, Figure 2A). CYFC had higher rates of Medicaid primary coverage (86.6% versus 39.9%, P<0.001; Figure 2B), lower COI scores (median COI score 61 versus 72, P=0.016; Figure 2C) and resided closer to the hospital compared with non‐CYFC (Figure 2D). Median follow‐up duration for the entire study population was 561 days [IQR, 376–1349], and was longer for CYFC than for non‐CYFC (889 days [IQR, 393–1643] versus 564 days [IQR, 375–1349], P=0.015). The a priori defined clinical variables used for risk adjustment were each associated with all 3 primary outcomes (P<0.001 for all risk adjustment covariates with each outcome).

Primary Outcomes in CYFC

Mortality

CYFC, compared with non‐CYFC, had similar rates of survival to discharge post surgery (98.5% versus 96.9%, P=0.72) and similar rates of cardiac reoperation during the surgical readmission (9.0% versus 6.4%, P=0.45). Mortality at current follow‐up was 9.0% in CYFC (versus 5.9% in non‐CYFC, P=0.072 accounting for follow‐up duration, Figure 3 and Table 2). Including the fixed set of clinical variables, CYFC had higher adjusted odds of overall mortality (adjusted OR, 2.66 [95% CI, 1.02–6.92], P=0.046, AUC, 0.887). When COI or payer were separately added to the model adjusting for clinical variables, being in foster care was no longer significantly associated with mortality, with small decreases in the adjusted OR for CYFC and minimal change to the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (Figure 3, Table S1). When race was included in the model adjusting for clinical variables, being in foster care remained independently associated with mortality (adjusted OR, 2.83 [95% CI, 1.10–7.28], P=0.032, area under the curve: 0.892) (Table S1).

Duration of Hospitalization

Hospital length of stay during the index admission was similar in CYFC and non‐CYFC (median 8 days [IQR, 5–22] versus 7 [IQR 5–16], P=0.35); however, cumulative hospital days over the study period were significantly higher for CYFC (median 22 days [IQR, 10–45] versus 12 days [IQR, 6–27], P=0.003 after adjusting for length of follow‐up) Figure 4). Adjusting for the fixed set of clinical variables, CYFC have 1.54 times more cumulative hospital days than non‐CYFC (95% CI, 1.13–2.15; P=0.008). When COI, payer, or race were included in the model, foster care remained independently associated with more hospital days (P=0.011, P=0.030, P=0.016, respectively) with minimal change to adjusted coefficients (Table S1).

Readmissions

CYFC had a 13.4% rate of 30‐day readmissions (versus 9.4% for non‐CYFC, P=0.29). The total number of readmissions throughout the study period was higher in CYFC (median 2 [IQR, 1–3]) versus non‐CYFC (median 1 [IQR, 1–2]), adjusting for follow‐up duration (incidence rate ratio, 1.44 [95% CI, 1.21–1.72]; P<0.001). Adjusting for clinical variables, foster care placement was associated with 1.24 times higher rate of readmission (95% CI, 1.04–1.48; P=0.018). Readmissions remained significantly higher in CYFC when COI or race were included in the model (incidence rate ratio, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.02–1.45] and 1.21 [95% CI, 1.01–1.44], respectively) but not with payer (incidence rate ratio, 1.16 [95% CI, 0.97–1.39]) (Table S1).

Validity of ICD ‐10 Code Z62.21

Only 16 of 67 (24%) documented CYFC, and none without, were identified with the ICD‐10 code Z62.21 in the PHIS data set, yielding a sensitivity of 24% (95% CI, 13–33), and a specificity of 100% (95% CI, 99.9–100) for use of the ICD‐10 code. Manual chart review failed to identify substantial differences between identified versus nonidentified CYFC in the administrative data set by reason for placement into foster care (for example physical abuse versus neglect) or placement timing (occurring during the hospitalization compared with patients already in foster care at time of admission). Documentation was, however, insufficient to further characterize CYFC status in many instances.

DISCUSSION

In this single‐center analysis of children following cardiac surgery, CYFC differed significantly from non‐CYFC in demographic characteristics, use of hospital resources, and long‐term outcomes. The rate of CYFC identified in our study via record review (1.5%) aligns with national estimates (1%). 14 Although foster care was not significantly associated with short‐term outcomes such as reoperation, mortality, length of stay, or 30‐day readmission following surgery, CYFC had significantly more cumulative days hospitalized, a greater number of readmissions throughout the study period and higher overall mortality at follow‐up when adjusting for clinical variables. These children had similar metrics for illness severity and complexity, but lower neighborhood‐level resources as defined by COI, and higher rates of public health insurance coverage. These social drivers variably affected associations with outcomes for this population, suggesting that for children with heart disease, being in foster care is an independent risk factor for increased resource use and overall mortality, in agreement with data from other populations. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 16 , 17 , 33 Importantly, we showed low sensitivity in identifying CYFC in the PHIS administrative data set. These findings highlight the need to explore the key drivers of the adverse long‐term outcomes observed in CYFC and derive targeted interventions to address modifiable risk factors and improve outcomes.

Risk Factors for Adverse Outcomes

Increased exposure of CYFC to other social drivers is a key factor, though not the sole cause of these adverse outcomes. 2 , 3 , 4 In our cohort, admitted CYFC lived closer to the hospital, raising concerns that other children elsewhere may not have equitable access to care if they are far from high‐quality centers. 4 There are also higher rates of CYFC with Black race in our study, consistent with prior literature, adding additional barriers related to structural racism. 38 , 39 Moreover, CYFC have higher rates of public payer insurance, likely reflecting state policies that enable CYFC to be eligible for Medicaid. 40 Although having insurance is essential for these children, patients with Medicaid often are more reliant on nonprofit hospitals and emergency departments, given the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act, and therefore may be subject to more emergency department visits. 41 , 42 , 43 Our data reflect inpatient admissions, so overall hospital resource use may be underestimated for this population.

The intersectionality of traditional social determinants and the impact of foster care on hospital use and outcomes is evident in the variable results from models adjusting for race, COI, and payer. Such sequential modeling approaches help explore interrelationships between social determinants. 44 Our results suggest that although COI, payer, and race are known risk factors for adverse health outcomes in children with heart disease, being in foster care amplifies long‐term risks. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Although many of these factors are nonmodifiable, they highlight the importance of addressing structural barriers and mitigating their impact through supportive interventions wherever possible and focusingon modifiable aspects of care to improve outcomes.

Targets to Improve Outcomes

Caregiver Preparedness and Support

When newly assuming care of a child, foster caregivers are tasked with quickly learning how to accommodate and care for a child’s physical and emotional needs, often with limited preparation. 23 , 24 , 25 This is especially challenging when caring for medically complex children. Inpatient providers are in a unique position to educate and prepare foster caregivers before discharge, though this opportunity is often missed. 23 At our institution, we use a “ticket to home” checklist for every child after cardiac surgery, which parents complete before discharge. The checklist covers items like cardiopulmonary resuscitation training, medication administration, and follow‐up plans. Tailoring this checklist for foster parents and implementing a more widespread “discharge bundle” across all units for children entering foster care would ensure that inpatient teams are using this opportunity to prepare foster parents for safely taking a child home.

CYFC with complex medical conditions have also been shown to experience inconsistent placement stability, further contributing to the lack of caregiver continuity. 45 , 46 Initiatives to promote placement stability, such as standardized training for foster parents on caring for children with complex medical conditions, are essential for improved recruitment and retention. For instance, the Keeping Foster and Kin Parents Supported and Trained intervention focuses on behavior management techniques to address the needs of children with various challenges, including medical complexities. The study found that program participation increased the likelihood of positive placement outcomes, such as reunification with biological families, and mitigated the risk of placement disruptions associated with children’s prior placement histories. 47 , 48 Alternate strategies direct resources toward primary prevention, with enhanced services for biological parents to prevent foster care placement and support reunification. 49 , 50

Enhanced Outpatient Support

Frequent placement changes disrupt care continuity and can lead to gaps in follow‐up with medical providers, limiting the ability to advocate for a child’s needs and assist caregivers in navigating the complex health care system. 20 Subspecialists, often the only consistent providers due to regionalized care, should be trained to recognize that CYFC are at higher risk for adverse outcomes. 51 , 52 Tailored interventions, such as providing developmentally appropriate education about their condition from an early age, may empower CYFC to gain greater autonomy and self‐sufficiency. Additional strategies may include automatic referrals to neurodevelopmental follow‐up programs for for all CYFC with congenital heart disease. This is crucial, given that CYFC often experience educational interruptions due to placement changes, are more likely to be in special education, and face higher rates of grade retention. 10 , 53 When combined with the increased risk of neurodevelopmental delays in children congenital heart disease face, CYFC with heart disease should be considered a high‐risk group in need of additional developmental resources and support. 54 Finally, assigning a special needs medical coordinator for CYFC with complex medical needs can enhance care, particularly during transitions. 55 A randomized control trial of children with special health care needs found that those assigned a care coordinator had fewer unmet needs, required less information, and reported greater satisfaction with mental health and specialty care coordination. 56 Although similar studies on children in foster care are lacking, these services are likely even more critical for this vulnerable population.

Improved Collaboration Among Medical Providers and Child Welfare Agencies

Improved care coordination and collaboration between child welfare agencies and medical teams are crucial for meeting the unique needs of CYFC. Medical systems need to have the ability to track foster care placements and changes to inform providers of a child’s specific care needs and prevent loss to follow‐up. For example, if a foster child under a subspecialist’s care is relocated, automated notifications to their medical team about the placement change and new contact information would help ensure follow‐up visits are communicated to the new foster family and prompt the provider to offer necessary education and support to prevent care gaps. Child welfare agencies would also benefit from streamlined access to medical records to ensure that CYFC with complex medical conditions receive the care they need. 57 Although addressing this issue is challenging, proposed solutions include improving foster care status documentation in medical records and integrating welfare databases with health care systems.

Documenting social drivers of health in electronic medical records using ICD‐10 codes helps identify populations and flag charts for targeted interventions. Although ICD‐10 codes are imperfect, they are commonly used to analyze populations and support evidence‐based quality improvement efforts, although other data sets (eg, Medicaid) may use different markers (eg, foster care eligibility code). 30 , 58 Our comparison of the number of patients identified as being in foster care by ICD‐10 codes and electronic medical record reviews revealed that ICD‐10 codes grossly underrepresented CYFC, with a sensitivity of only 24%. This information highlights that analyses using administrative databases such as PHIS will fail to identify the majority of CYFC, substantially limiting their utility in studying outcomes in this population. Importantly, the high specificity indicates that when foster care is coded, it is likely a true positive.

To address this gap in documentation, a primary care quality improvement initiative at Johns Hopkins University used multidisciplinary education for providers, collaboration with child welfare agencies to obtain lists of CYFC in surrounding counties, standardization of documentation, and designation of clinic champions to improve foster care status documentation from 20% to 60% over 12 months. 59 This relatively low‐cost, high‐impact intervention improved provider awareness of foster care status and allowed them to tailor care to the special health care needs of CYFC. Integration of electronic medical records with the child welfare database is another solution. Enhanced data sharing fosters collaborative services and “wrap‐around” care, while also enabling quality improvement initiatives and research to optimize health outcomes for CYFC. 60 Cincinnati Children’s Hospital also demonstrated successful implementation of a data‐sharing system (Integrated Data Environment to Enhance Outcomes in Custody Youth) that links child welfare and health care systems, merging and displaying information from both sets of records. 61 Successful initiatives rely on strong collaboration between child welfare agencies and health care providers. Further research is needed to support policies that prioritize these interventions for widespread implementation.

LIMITATIONS

This study was performed in a single center allowing for the collection of more granular patient factors and accurate cohort identification; however, this inherently limits sample size and generalizability. As a single‐center study conducted at a quaternary center serving as a referral center for complex cases, the institution may attract patients who have more resources, knowledge, or support systems necessary to access specialized care. This may be indicated by the overall high COI observed. As a result, the patient population may not be representative of the broader community, potentially limiting generalizability. Zip codes were used to derive linear distance traveled and COI. Although COI is validated at the zip‐code level and data support that these community‐level metrics strongly correlate with self‐reported constituent data, 62 it remains an aggregate measure that reflects broader neighborhood characteristics rather than individual circumstances. Recorded zip codes were obtained from the medical record and reflect the patient’s residence at the time of admission. Therefore, for patients in foster care at admission, their zip code reflects their foster care address, whereas those placed in foster care during the admission will likely have a zip code reflecting their prior residence. The number of foster care placements could not be determined due to limitations in tracking this information in the electronic medical record, highlighting the need for improved documentation and communication between the child welfare database and medical record system. Despite using broad keywords and ICD‐10 codes, some children in foster care may not have been identified through these methods. This would bias our results toward the null, likely underestimating the observed differences. Adverse long‐term outcomes may be underestimated due to survivor bias, although the follow‐up duration was longer in the CYFC population. Race is a social construct and was included as a variable in this study with the understanding that any observed associations with race are interpreted as reflecting effects of structural racism and systemic discrimination rather than inherent biological or genetic differences. Because reoperation and readmission occur after the index surgical admission and are strongly associated with mortality, regression models accounting for length of follow‐up were used for this outcome, although reason for early readmission was not able to be obtained. Despite these limitations, we believe this information is valuable for understanding the impact that being in foster care has on outcomes.

Foster care plays a vital role in safeguarding vulnerable children. Although our study could not determine the specific factors leading to foster care placement due to electronic medical record limitations, or whether placement delays contributed to longer hospitalization, it is likely that preplacement risks impacted outcomes. For children whose parents are unable to provide proper care, timely identification and placement in foster care often result in better outcomes compared with those who remain in unsafe conditions. As foster care is crucial for protecting children, it is imperative to continuously improve the system and the care provided to enhance their overall well‐being.

CONCLUSIONS

CYFC with cardiac conditions are a population with greater hospital resource use, adverse long‐term outcomes, and relative social disadvantage despite having similar illness severity to children not in foster care. Findings have significant implications for clinical care and health equity, providing opportunities for targeted interventions at the individual, institutional, and policy levels to improve outcomes for patients with this relatively understudied social driver of health.
