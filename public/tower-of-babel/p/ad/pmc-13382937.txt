Adult-onset moyamoya disease presenting with recurrent ischemic stroke: The role of CT Perfusion and digital subtraction angiography in bypass planning
Authors: Mohammed Alkhanafsa, Jamil Wafi, Mohammed Aloqaily, Ra’ed Ababneh, Abdallah Almuhtasib, Ibrahim Alfarrajin, Faris W Naffa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMoyamoya disease is a rare progressive cerebrovascular disorder characterized by stenosis of the terminal internal carotid arteries and compensatory development of fragile basal collateral vessels. We report a 32-year-old Filipino woman with poorly controlled hypertension who presented with abrupt right facial and upper limb weakness followed by recurrent left hemispheric ischemic events despite antiplatelet therapy, statin treatment, and blood pressure optimization. Magnetic resonance imaging showed recurrent left-sided watershed and cortical infarcts, while magnetic resonance angiography suggested bilateral terminal internal carotid artery stenosis with collateralization. Digital subtraction angiography confirmed bilateral distal internal carotid artery tapering, bilateral M1 involvement, and prominent collateral revascularization, consistent with moyamoya disease. CT perfusion demonstrated left hemispheric hemodynamic compromise with delayed perfusion and relatively preserved cerebral blood volume, supporting prioritization of left-sided superficial temporal artery-to-middle cerebral artery bypass, with staged contralateral reassessment planned after postoperative recovery. This case emphasizes that recurrent ischemic events in young adults should prompt evaluation for moyamoya disease and illustrates the complementary role of digital subtraction angiography and CT perfusion in diagnosis, mechanism clarification, and individualized bypass planning.Keywords: Moyamoya disease, Adult-onset ischemic stroke, Recurrent stroke, CT perfusion, Digital subtraction angiography, STA-MCA bypass

Introduction

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a chronic progressive cerebrovascular disorder characterized by idiopathic stenosis or occlusion of the terminal internal carotid arteries and their proximal branches, accompanied by compensatory collateral vessels at the base of the brain [1,2]. These collateral networks produce the characteristic angiographic “puff-of-smoke” appearance originally described by Suzuki and Takaku [3]. Although MMD was first described in Japan and remains most prevalent in East Asian populations, it is increasingly recognized worldwide, including among adults from non-East Asian backgrounds [1,4].

Adult-onset MMD may present with ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attacks, intracranial hemorrhage, cognitive symptoms, or headache [4]. In ischemic presentations, recurrent events often reflect impaired cerebrovascular reserve and hemodynamic failure, although artery-to-artery embolism from stenotic vessels or collateral networks may also contribute [4,5]. Because conventional vascular risk factors such as hypertension can coexist with MMD, young adults with recurrent or atypically distributed infarcts require careful vascular and perfusion assessment rather than attribution to hypertension alone.

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) remains the reference standard for confirming the diagnosis, defining collateral pathways, and planning revascularization [2,4]. CT perfusion (CTP) and other perfusion techniques add functional information by identifying reduced cerebral blood flow, delayed transit, preserved or exhausted cerebral blood volume, and tissue at risk [5,6]. These data are particularly valuable in bilateral disease, where the symptomatic or hemodynamically threatened hemisphere may be prioritized for direct or combined revascularization [7,8]. This report focuses on the role of DSA and CTP in clarifying the mechanism of recurrent ischemia and guiding surgical planning in adult-onset MMD.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old Filipino woman with poorly controlled hypertension, prescribed perindopril 5 mg daily but with poor adherence, presented with a 36-hour history of abrupt right upper limb numbness and weakness, right facial weakness, and a sensation of tongue heaviness. The deficits had remained stable since onset. She denied visual symptoms, gait disturbance, altered consciousness, trauma, emotional stress, or prior similar episodes. There was no history of head or neck irradiation, and no family history of moyamoya disease, young-onset stroke, or other cerebrovascular disorders. She was a nonsmoker and was not taking regular medications other than perindopril.

On presentation, she was severely hypertensive with a blood pressure of 181/118 mmHg. Temperature was 36.7°C, respiratory rate was 18 breaths/min, and pulse was 96 beats/min. Neurological examination showed a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15, mild difficulty speaking, right upper motor neuron-type facial weakness, and right upper limb weakness with Medical Research Council power 2/5. Sensory examination, cerebellar testing, cranial nerve reflexes, and cognition were otherwise preserved.

Laboratory investigations were largely unremarkable. WBC was mildly elevated at 13.4 × 103/µL, LDL was 2.9 mmol/L, and HbA1c was 6.1%. Hb, platelet count, coagulation profile, renal and liver function tests, electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, β-hCG, and TSH were within normal limits. Further evaluation for causes of stroke in a young adult was unrevealing, with normal ESR and CRP, negative ANA, ENA, ANCA, and antiphospholipid antibodies, normal hemoglobin electrophoresis, and unremarkable thrombophilia screening. ECG showed sinus rhythm, and transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal left ventricular function without a cardiac source of embolism.

Noncontrast CT of the head showed no acute intracranial hemorrhage. Initial brain MRI demonstrated left hemispheric cortical and watershed signal abnormalities (Fig. 1), while MR angiography revealed tight stenosis of the terminal internal carotid arteries involving the origins of the anterior and middle cerebral arteries. Severe stenosis of the right middle cerebral artery was also noted, with multiple tiny collateral vessels along the M1 segment producing the characteristic moyamoya-type “puff-of-smoke” appearance (Fig. 2). No aneurysm or arteriovenous malformation was identified.

MR angiography showing tight stenosis of the terminal internal carotid arteries at the origins of the anterior and middle cerebral arteries, severe right middle cerebral artery stenosis, and multiple collateral vessels producing the characteristic moyamoya-type “puff-of-smoke” appearance.

The patient was initially managed as an ischemic stroke in the setting of suspected moyamoya disease. Aspirin 100 mg daily and atorvastatin 40 mg daily were started. Blood pressure treatment was optimized with amlodipine 10 mg daily and an increased perindopril dose of 10 mg daily. Her neurological deficits resolved completely during the admission, and she was discharged with normal motor function and outpatient follow-up with neurology and neurosurgery.

Three weeks later, she re-presented with recurrent right-sided neurological symptoms. Diffusion-weighted MRI revealed multiple foci of restricted diffusion in the left frontoparietal cortical and subcortical regions, consistent with acute ischemic infarcts in watershed areas (Fig. 3). She was observed for 2 days and continued medical therapy, followed by neurology and neurosurgery review.

Diffusion-weighted MRI during the first recurrence showing multiple foci of restricted diffusion in the left frontoparietal cortical and subcortical regions, consistent with acute ischemic infarcts in watershed areas.

One month later, she returned with another recurrence of right facial and upper limb weakness associated with difficulty speaking. Repeat diffusion-weighted MRI demonstrated several small focal areas of restricted diffusion involving the left insular cortex and the left frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes, in the left middle cerebral artery distribution (Fig. 4). Although the cortical distribution could suggest an embolic pattern, the overall clinical-radiological picture favored recurrent hemodynamic ischemia in the setting of severe moyamoya vasculopathy, with artery-to-artery embolism considered a possible additional mechanism. At discharge, she had persistent expressive dysphasia and right facial and upper limb weakness, with right upper limb power improved to 3/5.

Diffusion-weighted MRI during the second recurrence showing multifocal acute ischemic lesions in the left insular, frontal, parietal, and temporal regions within the left middle cerebral artery territory.

Further evaluation with DSA demonstrated bilateral tapering and stenosis of the terminal internal carotid arteries with associated involvement of the proximal M1 segments. Extensive basal collateral vessel formation and retrograde revascularization of the distal middle cerebral artery territories through anterior and posterior cerebral artery collaterals were identified, producing the characteristic “puff-of-smoke” appearance of moyamoya disease (Fig. 5). These angiographic findings confirmed moyamoya vasculopathy and supported the diagnosis of adult-onset moyamoya disease in the appropriate clinical context.

Digital subtraction angiography. (A) Frontal projection demonstrating terminal internal carotid artery/proximal middle cerebral artery narrowing with extensive basal collateral vessel formation. (B) Lateral projection showing prominent moyamoya collateralization (“puff-of-smoke” appearance) with distal reconstitution of the cerebral circulation. Findings are consistent with moyamoya disease.

CT perfusion imaging was subsequently performed to assess cerebral hemodynamics and guide surgical planning. Perfusion analysis demonstrated reduced cerebral blood flow and prolonged transit-time parameters involving the left frontoparietotemporal cortical and subcortical regions, with relatively preserved cerebral blood volume, consistent with hemodynamic compromise and hypoperfused tissue at risk (Fig. 6). Automated processing used a Tmax threshold >6 seconds to define hypoperfused tissue and a relative cerebral blood flow threshold <20% to define nonviable infarct core. Additional areas of delayed perfusion were identified in the right hemisphere without corresponding established infarction, suggesting chronic contralateral hemodynamic compromise. These findings supported prioritization of left-sided superficial temporal artery-to-middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) bypass, with planned staged contralateral reassessment after postoperative recovery.

CT perfusion imaging demonstrating left hemispheric hemodynamic compromise. (A) Cerebral blood flow (CBF) map showing reduced perfusion. (B) Cerebral blood volume (CBV) map demonstrating relative preservation of blood volume. (C) Tmax map showing delayed perfusion within the left cerebral hemisphere, consistent with impaired cerebrovascular reserve.

Based on the recurrent ischemic events, DSA-confirmed moyamoya vasculopathy, and CT perfusion evidence of symptomatic left hemispheric hemodynamic compromise with surrounding hypoperfused tissue at risk, the multidisciplinary team recommended left-sided STA-MCA bypass. While awaiting surgical revascularization, the patient was managed medically with antiplatelet therapy, statin treatment, optimization of blood pressure control, avoidance of hypotension and dehydration, and close neurological follow-up. Given the bilateral nature of the disease and evidence of contralateral perfusion impairment, staged right-sided revascularization was planned to be considered following postoperative clinical and perfusion reassessment.

Discussion

Diagnostic focus: Moyamoya disease vs moyamoya syndrome

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenge of recurrent ischemic stroke in a young adult with poorly controlled hypertension. Hypertension is an important modifiable vascular risk factor and may worsen perfusion vulnerability in patients with impaired cerebrovascular reserve. However, the bilateral distal internal carotid artery involvement, proximal middle cerebral artery stenosis, and prominent collateralization on MRA and DSA were more characteristic of moyamoya vasculopathy than isolated hypertensive small-vessel disease or conventional intracranial atherosclerosis.

The distinction between MMD and moyamoya syndrome is clinically relevant. MMD refers to idiopathic moyamoya vasculopathy, whereas moyamoya syndrome describes similar angiographic changes occurring in association with secondary conditions such as vasculitis, autoimmune disease, sickle cell disease, thyroid disease, hypercoagulable states, cranial irradiation, or atherosclerosis [2,4]. In this patient, thyroid function was normal, inflammatory and autoimmune markers were negative, hemoglobin electrophoresis and thrombophilia screening were unremarkable, and there was no history of cranial irradiation or family history of young-onset cerebrovascular disease. These findings, together with the angiographic pattern, supported primary adult-onset MMD in the available clinical context.

Mechanism of recurrent ischemia

The recurrent infarct mechanism should be interpreted in light of both infarct topography and perfusion status. The initial and first recurrent events involved left hemispheric watershed regions, strongly supporting hemodynamic failure due to impaired cerebrovascular reserve. The later multifocal cortical infarcts in the left MCA territory could appear embolic in distribution; however, the coexistence of severe proximal stenosis, collateral-dependent flow, and CT perfusion delay favored a predominantly hemodynamic mechanism, with artery-to-artery embolism considered a possible secondary contributor. This hemodynamic-predominant interpretation aligns the MRI findings with DSA and CTP and explains why revascularization, rather than escalation of antithrombotic therapy alone, was prioritized.

Role of DSA and CT perfusion in surgical planning

DSA was essential for confirming the diagnosis, defining bilateral distal internal carotid and proximal M1 involvement, and demonstrating collateral routes supplying distal MCA territories. In adult MMD, DSA remains critical when noninvasive angiography suggests moyamoya vasculopathy, because treatment decisions depend not only on the presence of stenosis but also on collateral anatomy, donor and recipient vessel suitability, and disease laterality [2,4].

CTP complemented DSA by showing the functional consequences of the angiographic disease. The left hemisphere demonstrated reduced cerebral blood flow, delayed Tmax, and relative cerebral blood volume preservation, indicating hypoperfused tissue at risk and supporting prioritization of left-sided revascularization [6,5]. The right hemisphere showed delayed perfusion without established infarction, supporting staged reassessment rather than simultaneous bilateral intervention. In this context, perfusion imaging helped determine the operative side and provided a baseline for future postoperative comparison.

Medical and surgical management considerations

Medical therapy in MMD is supportive and directed at secondary stroke prevention before revascularization and during follow-up. Antiplatelet therapy is commonly used in ischemic presentations, although evidence for preventing recurrence is limited and largely observational [4,8]. Control of vascular risk factors remains important, but overly aggressive blood pressure reduction should be avoided in patients with impaired cerebrovascular reserve. Dehydration, hypotension, and perioperative fluctuations in perfusion pressure can precipitate ischemia and should be actively prevented [4,8].

For symptomatic adults with recurrent ischemic events and hemodynamic compromise, surgical revascularization is the cornerstone of management. Direct STA-MCA bypass provides immediate augmentation of cortical blood flow and is commonly favored in adults when suitable donor and recipient vessels are present [7,8]. In this patient, left-sided bypass was prioritized because symptoms and perfusion compromise were dominant in the left hemisphere, while staged right-sided bypass remained under consideration after interval reassessment. Perioperative blood pressure management is essential to reduce both ischemic risk and cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome after direct bypass [9].

Conclusion

Adult-onset moyamoya disease should be considered in young adults with recurrent ischemic strokes, particularly when infarcts recur despite medical therapy and vascular imaging shows terminal internal carotid or proximal middle cerebral artery stenosis with collateralization. In this case, DSA confirmed moyamoya vasculopathy and CT perfusion demonstrated symptomatic left hemispheric hemodynamic compromise with hypoperfused tissue at risk, guiding prioritization of left-sided STA-MCA bypass. The case highlights the importance of distinguishing moyamoya disease from moyamoya syndrome, reconciling infarct mechanism with perfusion data, and using multimodal imaging to individualize surgical timing and laterality.

Key learning points

Moyamoya disease should be considered in young adults with recurrent ischemic strokes, even when conventional vascular risk factors such as hypertension are present. Excluding secondary causes is essential to distinguish moyamoya disease from moyamoya syndrome. Digital subtraction angiography remains the reference standard for diagnosis, demonstrating characteristic arterial stenosis and collateral vessel formation. CT perfusion provides valuable hemodynamic information by identifying hypoperfused tissue and impaired cerebrovascular reserve, helping guide surgical planning. In symptomatic adult patients with recurrent ischemic events and perfusion compromise, direct STA-MCA bypass is an effective treatment strategy. Postoperative clinical and imaging follow-up is important to assess revascularization success and guide further management.
