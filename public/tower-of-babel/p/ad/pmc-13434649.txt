The benefit of advanced mapping techniques to address the spatial distribution of soil sulfur—total, gypsum-based, and total-reduced—in the dried Sawa Lakebed
Authors: Mohammed Rashid Majeed, Farzin Shahbazi, Shahin Oustan, Ahmed Hashem Al-Sulttani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study employs digital soil mapping (DSM) and Sentinel-2A satellite imagery to investigate the impact of Sawa Lake’s desiccation in Iraq on the spatial variability of soil sulfur. The findings assist in risk assessments. For this, two distinct strata were identified: St1(never inundated) and St2 (which dried between 2016 and 2023). Ninety-nine surface soil samples (0–20 cm) were taken in July 2022. Total sulfur (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{tot}$$\end{document}), was directly measured using an X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer. The gypsum-based sulfur (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{gyp}$$\end{document}) was also directly determined by measuring sulfate in a dilute soil extract following dissolution. The total-reduced sulfur (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{red}^{r}$$\end{document}) was then roughly estimated by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{red}^{r}=\left({S}_{tot}-{S}_{gyp}\right)-\varepsilon $$\end{document}. Hence, further evaluation is needed to understand how sulfur accounts for the ɛ (non-gypsum sulfate). A total of 17 predictors were calculated from the spectral bands data. Given the small size and homogeneous topography of the study area, climatic and terrain-related attributes were not included as predictors. Regarding the concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) and RMSE values in the calibration-fitted model, the Cubist outperformed the Random Forest. Accordingly, the highest performance was for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{gyp}$$\end{document} (CCC = 0.79; RMSE = 0.70), followed by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{red}^{r}$$\end{document} (CCC = 0.63; RMSE = 0.56), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{tot}$$\end{document} (CCC = 0.54; RMSE = 0.47). Multicollinearity analysis and variance inflation factor for feature selection did not improve the model accuracy. Overall, the digital maps showed that the studied sulfur-bearing soil materials were directly related to the distance from the shoreline, demonstrating the dynamic behavior of the study area. Between the two strata, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{red}^{r}$$\end{document} increased by 4.07% (w/w) in contrast, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{gyp}$$\end{document} decreased by 1.07% (w/w), illustrating that the sulfur speciation and chemistry analysis following the lake desiccation would be of interest. Ultimately, this research demonstrates that DSM can address the knowledge gap regarding the spatial distribution of hard-to-measure properties (e.g., sulfur-bearing soil materials) using remote sensing data alone, even in dynamic environments such as the dried Sawa Lakebed.

Introduction

Sawa Lake is in southern Iraq, near Al-Samawah City in Al-Muthanna Province. It was fed only by the Euphrates River through aquifers beneath it. The lake is bounded by gypsum barriers, which are the primary cause of its high salinity due to groundwater capillaries1. Recently (in early 2014), the Sawa Lake water level started declining, losing five km2 of its area extent. Therefore, the change in lake size is indicative of lake desiccation2. One possible reason for this event is the overuse of water for agricultural practices, indicating the impact of anthropogenic activities on lake desiccation3. Climate change also accelerated this phenomenon4, providing a rare opportunity to unlock the spatially distributed sulfur-bearing soil materials due to water level decline.

Sulfur-bearing soil materials are the most hazardous constituent, posing a significant environmental risk in the degraded lake area5. Since a dry lakebed, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions, is a major source of wind-blown dust and particulate matter, i.e., the dried Jazmurian lake in Iran6, uncovering the spatial variability of sulfur-bearing soil materials, especially elemental sulfur, is indispensable. The presence of elemental sulfur is responsible for sediment toxicity7. In the soils surrounding the dried Sawa lakebed, the sulfur cycle—under conditions of water recession—is driven by consecutive processes of sulfate reduction and sulfide reoxidation. Factors controlling the sulfate reduction rate include oxygen and sulfate concentrations, soil organic matter, and temperature. Sulfate reduction is less significant in areas with limited organic matter supply, such as our case study area. In such conditions, sulfate availability may control the reduction rate8. On the other hand, sulfides produced during sulfate reduction may undergo reoxidation by several processes, including chemical oxidation with oxygen, bacterial oxidation under oxidized conditions, phototrophic oxidation, anoxic chemical oxidation, and bacterial oxidation under anoxic conditions9. While this research did not address sulfur chemistry, it introduces important new avenues for future work. These include using digital soil mapping (DSM) to assess the impact of Sawa Lake desiccation on sulfur-bearing soil materials.

In areas with no variation in topography and no digital elevation data in the base digital elevation model (DEM), such as lakebeds, solely remotely sensed multispectral data can be successfully employed as major predictors10. For instance, in the case of Urmia Lake (Iran), the spatial distribution of potentially toxic elements11, soil salt composition12, and iron forms and features13 were digitally mapped using solely RS data. Similar research performed in Türkiye also showed the importance of RS in the rapid assessment of elemental concentrations on the dried lakebed of Burdur Lake14. The application of remote sensing (RS) for lake monitoring and research has been extensively reported in previous literature15. Among available RS data sources (e.g., Landsat, MODIS, SPOT, Sentinel), the free and open data from Landsat and Sentinel have been widely utilized, though their prediction accuracies vary16. McBratney et al.17 noted the crucial role of such RS data for DSM, as it provides readily available data for deriving key environmental covariates.

Advanced digital mapping techniques now use diverse prediction methods to model the spatiotemporal variability of hard-to-measure soil properties from environmental covariates18. For instance, the Random Forest (RF) algorithm has demonstrated high performance in predicting the spatial distribution of soil organic carbon (SOC) across Africa19. Similarly, non-linear models such as Cubist20 and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)21 have proven to be parsimonious and effective for mapping key soil properties. Briefly, the assumptions of DSM application in this study were that: i) in areas with no or slight topographical variation, such as the desiccated Sawa Lake in Iraq, the environmental covariates derived from Sentinel-2A MSI imagery play a significant role in predictive mappings22; ii) choosing a proper machine learning (ML) algorithm for DSM dramatically improves the prediction accuracy23; and iii) spatial mapping of sulfur-bearing soil materials using RS data alone, following lake desiccation, increases our understanding of environmental concerns. It is worth noting that the soils in our study area represents an early stage of soil formation (incipient pedogenesis), hence, we called them soil not sediment.

Specifically, this study provides the first retrospective monitoring of sulfur-bearing soil materials in Iraq's Sawa Lake, establishing a novel spatial distribution analysis for the region. To investigate this, total sulfur (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{tot}$$\end{document}), gypsum-based sulfur (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{gyp}$$\end{document}), and total-reduced sulfur (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{red}^{r}$$\end{document}) were studied. It is assumed that the environmental covariates used in this study, with an appropriate ML algorithm, can profitably address the spatial distribution of the studied sulfur-bearing soil materials across the study area.

Materials and methods

Site description and soil sampling

This study was performed in the northern part of Sawa Lake, Iraq. It is located between east longitudes 44º59′34″–45°00′35″ and north latitudes 31º18′30″–31°19′50″, 23 km to the west of Al-Samawah city, with an area extent of 561.62 ha. The unique characteristic of the study area is a gypsum barrier that surrounds the lake with a total path of 12.5 km 1. It is the primary source of gypsum-based sulfur (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{gyp}$$\end{document}) discharge to this lake through the Euphrates River. This River provides the water that enables dissolution of gypsum from the aquifer, creating a plume of sulfate-rich groundwater that feeds the lake. The geological map of the study area24 shows that floodplain sediments are the dominant geological unit. The data also indicate a faster drying rate in 2022. The reason for selecting Sawa Lake as a case study is our expectation of finding differences in sulfur-bearing soil materials due to water level decline. Two distinct strata were identified as: i) never inundated (St1 = 299.25 ha), and ii) dried over six years from 2016 to 2023 (St2 = 262.37 ha). Sampling design also improves DSM results25. According to Wang et al.26, who noted that stratified random sampling is a well-established method in DSM projects, we collected 99 surface soil samples (0–20 cm) from the northern shore of Sawa Lake, using a sampling grid of 6.25 ha for modeling. Although sulfur chemistry is acknowledged to be depth-dependent, the shallow nature of our study area—where sediments are actively transitioning into soils—led us to focus on surface sampling.

Soil analysis

As indicated in (Fig. 1), 44 and 55 soil samples were taken from St1 and St2, respectively. The samples were transported to the University of Kufa, Iraq, and analyzed using an XRF spectrometer (EDX3600B, Skyray Instrument). Those were also air-dried at room temperature and then sieved through a 2 mm sieve prepared for further analyses. It should be noted that only pH and EC27 might be helpful in discussions and for further interpretation. Gypsum was determined directly in all samples by measuring sulfate in a dilute soil extract following dissolution28. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{tot}$$\end{document} was directly measured using XRF analysis. Before becoming deployable, the accuracy of the implemented instrument should be quantified29. A GSS20 reference sample (soil, IGGE, China) was used to calibrate the analytical method30. The relative error of the reference sample was 8.8%. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate using XRF, and the reported values represent the mean of these three measurements.

The chemistry of sulfur influences the processes involved in all compartments of the Earth's environment31. The first direction in the accumulation of sulfur in lake-bodies and beds is the rock minerals weathering in the basin and then charging into the lakebed associated with water32. The second source would be atmospheric precipitation of sulfur dioxide carried into the lake by the surface water system. The first source is very likely because there is no inlet or outlet in Sawa Lake, and the water flows from the surrounding Euphrates and valleys, transmitted through gypsum barriers. Sentinel-2A MSI imagery acquisition

To prepare the environmental covariates to meet the SCORPAN formula17, in areas with no topographic variation and even with no digital elevation data, the best choice is to implement RS imageries, such as Sentinel-2A MSI. The main reason is that it is not only freely available and facilitates the calculation of environmental covariates, but also shows some advantages. For modeling, a cloud-free Sentinel-2A MSI imagery for July 2022 (Product ID: S2A_MSIL1C_20220702T073631_N0400_R092_T38RMV_20220702T082856.SAFE) was acquired via the following website: https://browser.dataspace.copernicus.eu/. For retrospective monitoring and stratum delineation, a Sentinel-2A scene from July 23, 2016 (Product ID: S2A_OPER_MSI_L1C_TL_SGS__20160723T075733_A005666_T38RMV) was also acquired. A total of five individual bands (Blue, Green, Red, and Near-infrared (NIR: 0.78–0.90 µm) with a grid resolution of 10 m, and Short-wave infrared: bands 11 (SWIR1: 1.57–1.65 µm) and 12 (SWIR2: 2.10–2.28 µm) with a grid resolution of 20 m) were used to calculate of spectral indices related to 2023 to match with sampling date.

The predictors

The acquired Sentinel-2A MSI image, which covered the entire study area, was used to generate 17 environmental predictors. This was based on the hypothesis that the spatial distribution of sulfur-bearing soil materials is controlled by soil-forming factors, which are captured by these covariates. Feature selection strategy

Before modeling, we assessed the effect of multicollinearity, as it can negatively impact the quality of regression models53. Several approaches exist to address this. One method involves modifying the correlation matrix by generating a new matrix with a specified structure54. Combining variables into a single index could be another procedure55. The next approach is variable filtering, which removes highly correlated variables to reduce multicollinearity. In this study, we analyzed multicollinearity in R using the “caret” package56, omitting variables with a pairwise correlation coefficient greater than 0.8.

Continuous modeling

First, soil data from all 99 field samples were imported into the R software. The required R packages were also loaded in the environment in advance. Our dataset uses a projected coordinate system (WGS 1984 UTM Zone 38N) for all spatial coordinates. Subsequently, the provided environmental covariates with and without multicollinearity testing results were added to the same extent and resolution. Two models, Cubist and RF, were then fitted to the available dataset.

Cubist model

The first candidate model to predict sulfur-bearing soil materials is Cubist, a prediction-oriented regression model. The model construction produced a tree structure, where each distinct path was converted into a rule. A regression model is then fit for each rule based on the data subset defined by the provided rules. It first segregates the data into subsets according to the similarity of examined soil properties and available environmental covariates. Each rule takes the form of a sequence of “if, then, else”. The effectiveness of this model was justified for DSM57. Contrary to other tree-based models, such as RF, the outcomes are rules accompanied by sets of multivariate models. One hypothesis for testing the Cubist model in our research relates to its interpretability compared to RF23. In this study, the number of committees (indicating the number of boosting iterations to create rulesets in the outputs) and control (including the extrapolation parameter) were set at 5 and 10%, respectively. Average error (standard calculation of MSE), relative error (ratio of the average error magnitude to the error magnitude), and correlation coefficient (R-squared measurement) were also retrieved.

Random forest

The second candidate model is RF, which was nominated as a suitable model among the ML algorithms in many DSM projects58–60. To find an accurate result, it is possible to adjust three parameters, i.e., “ntree” (number of trees), “mtry” (number of covariates) for each split, and the minimum number of samples that a node must contain. Although the “ntree” value is set to 500 by default, the optimal value of 1000 was found in this research. The “mtry” parameter was set to 17, corresponding to the total number of covariates used in this study. The final model was then built using this selected set of covariates, which were chosen based on the multicollinearity analysis. Suppose RF was selected as the parsimonious model in predictions; in that case, the importance of the covariates used in modeling can be ranked using the “varImpPlot” function within R based on two calculated criteria, i.e., %IncMSE (Mean Squared Error) and IncNodePurity18.

Model validation and spatial mapping

Among the two candidate models, one was applied for spatial mapping. We calculated the Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for both the calibration and validation datasets. The model performance was assessed using a bootstrap averaging method61, where each iteration randomly assigned 70% of the data to the calibration set and 30% to the validation set. Subsequently, the modeling results were represented as digital maps. Quantification of prediction uncertainties

Our study area is likely a dynamic system of soils, and Fröhlich et al.62 announced that estimating properties of a dynamical system is uncertain. Hence, the uncertainty of each map was quantified, but little attention has been paid to this analysis63. Additionally, uncertainties in model predictions may be caused by the combination of uncertainties in model inputs and the uncertainties from the implemented ML algorithm. Consequently, evaluating the uncertainty of prepared maps for the study area is essential. Among the various approaches fully described by Malone et al.18, a bootstrapping method was applied to quantify the uncertainty of the predictions. It involves repeated random sampling with the replacement of the observations before generating a digital soil map. It was successfully implemented in different sub-disciplines beyond soil science64. The upper and lower limits of the 95% prediction interval (PI) range were mapped to visualize the uncertainty of the predictions. This range reports the range of values within which the true value is expected to occur 95 times out of 100. The CV of the prepared sulfur-bearing soil materials map per stratum is the subsequent procedure to assess the map’s accuracy from the soil surveying aspect.

Multicollinearity analysis

Figure 2 shows the multicollinearity analysis heatmap for the predictors used in this study.Fig. 2The heatmap of predictor variable correlations used in this study.

The heatmap of predictor variable correlations used in this study.

Specifically, the correlation analysis revealed that among the 17 predictors, only six features met the independence criterion, including NDVI, VARI, NDWI, MNDWI1, FII2, and SI1. A number of 11 features with a high correlation (≥ 0.8) were removed to avoid the multicollinearity issue. This indicates extreme redundancy in our original predictors. Subsequently, based on the calculated Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for the six predictors, previously selected in accordance with correlation analysis, MNDWI1 (VIF = 2.52), FII2 (VIF = 4.95), and SI1 (VIF = 3.36) were selected for modeling once again. However, even after retaining only three predictors with low VIF scores, the model performance remained unsatisfactory. This finding can be explained by the fact that although the selected features might eliminate redundancy, they could also introduce a new type of bias. For instance, removing some important predictors related to sulfur (such as CMR) is not rational from a geochemical perspective, as their theoretical relationship with sulfur-bearing soil materials is well-established. Therefore, to rigorously evaluate the trade-off between statistical purity and theoretical completeness, the modeling processes were conducted twice. First, with the full set of 17 predictors to capture all potentially relevant information, and again with the six uncorrelated predictors to ensure a statistically robust model. This approach allows us to evaluate the efficacy of multicollinearity analysis for the RF and Cubist models among the ML algorithms.

Across all sulfur-bearing soil materials, the primary (most important) rule set exhibited the smallest standard error (SE): 0.51 for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{gyp}$$\end{document}, 1.15 for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{red}^{r}$$\end{document}, and 2.63 for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{tot}$$\end{document}. Limitations and future works

While this research has several potential limitations, it also opens numerous avenues for future investigation. This is a key advantage of conducting research in a lakebed environment. There are at least four potential limitations concerning the procedure and the findings.

The first limitation concerns the nature of the study area. The DEM contained no digital elevation data for the St2 lakebed due to its recent inundation. Consequently, it was not feasible to include the DEM as a key environmental covariate in the analysis. High-resolution SRTM DEMs enable the estimation of lake level variations in newly inundated areas through quasi-global investigations84.

The second limitation is the existence of collinearity between predictors used in this study. Such limited number of RS-derived covariates based on the calculated VIF caused a challenge for enhancing the model performance, however, no improvement was observed.

The third potential limitation is the absence of concurrent water analysis from Sawa Lake, which would have aided in data interpretation. While the present study cannot eliminate this uncertainty, identifying it highlights critical considerations for future research on Sawa Lake85.

The fourth limitation is the absence of detailed sulfur chemistry analysis—including direct sulfur speciation, redox indicators, and porewater chemistry. Investigating these parameters is important, as the concentrations of certain trace elements in pore waters (e.g., copper and iron) are known to be controlled by sulfide formation86.

In addition to those already mentioned, we suggest at least three important avenues that may be valuable as follows: I)This study can be extended by investigating soil chronosequence and biosequence in the shrinking Sawa Lake, as demonstrated by Gozukara et al.87, and by assessing the degree of soil evolution22. The hypothesis that more ripened soils exist in St1, based on analogous findings from Urmia Lake13, can be tested by comparing iron forms and features between the two strata.II)Future work should improve these findings using other RS data coupled with XGBoost tree model. For instance, Landsat-9 OLI or hyperspectral imagery—combined with techniques like the optimized denoising method introduced by Meng et al.88—could enhance the predictions. RS imagery from late 2025 confirms that Sawa Lake is now completely dry. Consequently, the spatial distribution of heavy metals and associated risk in the lakebed's soils should be assessed using predictive mapping techniques.III)Given the novelty of this research in the Iraqi context, particularly concerning Sawa Lake, it holds substantial theoretical and practical89,90. The efficacy of other ML algorithms should be assessed to map key salinity-related properties (e.g., Ca, CaCO₃, CaSO₄, SO₄) across the study area91.

Conclusions

While DSM has progressed globally since 2003, its application in Iraqi soils remains nascent. This study demonstrates its potential by using DSM and RS to generate a quantitative, map-based hypothesis for the spatial distribution of sulfur-bearing soil materials (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{tot}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{gyp}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{red}^{r}$$\end{document}) in the desiccated Sawa Lakebed. We compared two strata: a never-inundated area (St1) and an area that dried between 2016 and 2023 (St2). Due to the minor difference (~ 10%) in their areal extent, the data were interpreted without normalization by area (per unit area).

Comparative results demonstrated that the Cubist model outperformed the RF model in predicting sulfur-bearing soil materials. The highest predictive performance was achieved for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{gyp}$$\end{document}, followed by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{red}^{r}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{tot}$$\end{document}. Collectively, these findings indicate that the Cubist model not only delivered satisfactory results but also provided an effective balance between interpretability and predictive power. The model identified the CMR as a critical driver for both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{tot}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{gyp}$$\end{document}, suggesting that specific clay and moisture conditions define sulfur accumulation hotspots. Subsequently, NBR2 was the most contributed covariate among the 17 predictors, underscoring the importance of landscape and geological attributes in the digital mapping of sulfur-bearing soil materials.

Our findings reveal a clear geochemical partitioning between the two strata. Concentrations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{tot}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{red}^{r}$$\end{document} were significantly higher in the recently desiccated area (St2), measuring 1.19-fold and 1.36-fold greater than in the never-inundated area (St1), respectively. In contrast, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${S}_{gyp}$$\end{document} demonstrated an inverse trend, with its concentration in St2 decreasing by 40% compared to St1. This distribution pattern is likely driven by a combination of factors, including the activity of sulfate-reducing bacteria, the solubility and redistribution of gypsum, and the effects of historical water overuse in the region.

Methodologically, this study demonstrates the strong performance of nel-2A MSI data for DSM in this environment. The quantified uncertainty maps further confirmed the higher reliability of the predictions for St2, as indicated by a lower mean prediction interval range value for all examined sulfur-bearing soil materials compared to St1. This, coupled with the more dynamic and pronounced geochemical signals in St2, leads us to conclude that the St1 area represents a more stable, less dynamic geochemical system.
