City-scale high-resolution traffic datasets with refined networks for hierarchical traffic control
Authors: Qinzhou Ma, Xinling Guo, Weifan Zhong, Zhaocheng He, Zicheng Su, Wenfei Ma, Renxin Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban-scale traffic control faces significant challenges due to the limited availability of large-scale and high-resolution datasets to support traffic control strategies across various network topologies and scales. To address this limitation, we present comprehensive traffic datasets for benchmarking traffic control strategies, possibly with hierarchical architectures. The datasets include five cities of varying scales with diverse intersection layouts and network topologies. We highlight the Xuancheng dataset, which adopts a refined network representation with hundreds of intersections, and achieves high spatiotemporal resolution by recording complete vehicle paths across the network. The dataset incorporates vehicle type information and provides historical trip data over long-term periods, with demand patterns exhibiting both periodic fluctuations and holiday-induced variations. Together with the refined road network and the high-resolution trip data, the dataset supports the construction of realistic traffic simulation environments. Furthermore, we derive multi-level traffic state data from the processed datasets, which facilitate the traffic control strategies spanning vehicular, lane-intersection, and network levels.

Background & Summary

Traffic congestion remains a persistent challenge in metropolises worldwide, leading to economic inefficiencies and environmental burdens1. Addressing this challenge requires control strategies that coordinate decisions across multiple spatial and temporal scales, as both microscopic vehicle interactions and macroscopic network-level flow evolution shape traffic dynamics. In real-world traffic control practice, hierarchical traffic control architectures of different scales are often implemented as a prominent framework to alleviate traffic congestion2. It integrates control strategies across multiple levels, including microscopic vehicular-level control, mesoscopic lane-intersection-level signal control, and macroscopic network-level perimeter control. Such architectures typically rely on heterogeneous traffic state data spanning multiple spatial scales3,4. For example, Su et al.2 integrated perimeter control based on network-level traffic states with local signal control driven by lane pressure5 to improve network throughput. Guo et al.3 leveraged vehicular-level state information (e.g., speed and acceleration) together with lane-level density to coordinate connected autonomous vehicles and signal control, resulting in reduced travel time and fuel consumption. However, high-quality traffic data capturing large-scale urban mobility, link-intersection traffic dynamics, and individual route-choice behaviors are still missing to empower hierarchical urban traffic control6,7.

Existing open-source datasets for traffic control have several limitations. Firstly, most available datasets only cover networks with dozens of intersections in partial city areas, typically featuring simplified network topologies with predominantly four-approach intersections in regular grid structures8,9. These datasets cannot capture the structural variety in real urban traffic networks, such as diverse intersection types, varying road segment lengths, and flexible junction topologies. Therefore, their capability to evaluate model generalizability across various urban network configurations is limited10,11. Secondly, while some datasets encompass large networks with thousands of intersections, they often suffer from limited data collection periods, e.g., one hour3,12,13. This limited temporal coverage restricts the diversity of traffic scenarios captured, particularly variations across weekdays and weekends, as well as peak and off-peak hours. As a result, control strategies developed on these temporally limited datasets may struggle to generalize to previously unseen traffic scenarios, leading to suboptimal or unreliable performance when deployed in real-world systems with significant temporal variations. Thirdly, the sparse sampling rate of heterogeneous sensors restricts the spatio-temporal data resolution, thus confining the granularity of traffic flow analysis12,14. Although some methods attempt to synthesize traffic trip data to cover the entire road network over extended periods13, the resulting route assignments fail to reflect the actual travel behavior and the travel preferences of drivers15,16. Nevertheless, existing datasets exhibit limited representation of traffic heterogeneity by enforcing the homogeneous traffic assumption, e.g., uniform vehicle type, homogeneous traffic network configuration, and evenly distributed travel demand. These simplifications result in a notable mismatch between simulated environments and actual traffic conditions, eventually compromising the deployment of traffic control schemes from theory to practice. Given the limitations in network scale and topology, temporal coverage, spatio-temporal resolution, and traffic heterogeneity, existing datasets pose obstacles to developing robust and generalizable traffic control strategies for large-scale urban transportation systems.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we present city-scale high-resolution data to support hierarchical traffic control. The datasets are compiled using a data processing workflow that includes network reconstruction, signal standardization, and flow construction, transforming heterogeneous traffic records into a unified format. Using this workflow, we construct comprehensive datasets that span five cities of varying scales: Jinan, Hangzhou, Manhattan, Nanchang, and Xuancheng. Among them, the Xuancheng dataset is particularly notable for its refinement and resolution, encompassing hundreds of fine-grained intersections and providing highly sampled traffic trip data. The trip data is collected by automatic vehicle identification (AVI) over an extended period, where the AVI system classifies vehicles into different types. Building on this, we derive traffic state data from the datasets to support coordinated control actions across vehicular, lane-intersection, and network levels. Validation results demonstrate the effectiveness of the processing workflow in reconstructing network and trip data, and confirm that these city-scale high-resolution datasets enable the development and evaluation of hierarchical traffic control schemes.

Data acquisition

The five datasets are collected through two distinct acquisition approaches. The Jinan, Hangzhou, Manhattan, and Nanchang datasets are obtained from publicly available sources8,13. These datasets are primarily derived from the observations of traffic cameras, with both road network representations and trip-level traffic demand released in the CityFlow12 and CBData13 formats.

The Xuancheng dataset is constructed from a city-scale AVI system, with AVI sensors deployed at key intersections to capture vehicle passages across the network. As illustrated in Fig. 2, AVI observations cover most main roads in the study area. Among the 396 road segments without AVI observations, 394 correspond to one-lane branch roads with low traffic demand, while only two are main roads. This AVI system offers several advantages over camera-based observations. First, it records the entry and exit of nearly every vehicle within each road segment and matches vehicle identifiers across the network. Specifically, the entry table Tentry contains the fields (vehicle ID, vehicle type, road ID, entry time), while the exit table Texit contains the fields (vehicle ID, vehicle type, road ID, exit time). By associating records from these two tables using anonymized vehicle identifiers, complete vehicle trips across multiple road segments can be reconstructed. Furthermore, the dataset enables concise classification of vehicles, providing explicit information on heterogeneous vehicle types. In addition to trip-level records, the road network of the Xuancheng dataset is derived from OpenStreetMap and pre-processed using official SUMO17 tools into the SUMO format.Fig. 2Observation coverage of the city-scale AVI system in the Xuancheng dataset.

Data characterization

As illustrated in Table 1, the datasets span multiple network scales, with the Xuancheng dataset providing a fine-grained city-scale representation. At the local scale, the Jinan dataset with 12 intersections and the Hangzhou dataset with 16 intersections represent typical arterial corridors. Due to their limited scales, these networks are widely utilized as benchmarks for vehicular-level control and signal control. At a larger spatial scale, the Manhattan dataset contains 48 intersections and exemplifies a grid-based urban network. To further examine scalability under increased network complexity, the Nanchang dataset expands the network size to 2,048 intersections, representing a city-scale scenario constructed using a coarse network representation. This dataset is suitable for examining the effectiveness of perimeter control in mitigating network-wide congestion. Distinct from these coarse datasets, the Xuancheng dataset comprises 791 intersections and adopts a refined network representation that captures detailed lane configurations, channelization, and complex intersection layouts. Beyond structural diversity, the datasets vary significantly in demand patterns and data sources. This variety enables analysis of both peak-hour dynamics and sustained demand fluctuations. Specifically, the Jinan, Hangzhou, Manhattan, and Nanchang datasets provide one-hour empirical intervals from traffic cameras, facilitating short-term analysis. Spanning one month with approximately 350,000 daily trips, it characterizes temporal variability across daily and weekly cycles, facilitating the development of hierarchical control under evolving conditions.

Unified workflow for city-scale data processing

The datasets are designed to support the simulation of hierarchical traffic control in large-scale urban networks, allowing the exploration of control strategies under diverse scenarios and reducing potential safety risks prior to real-world deployment. However, the incompatibilities among the diverse formats limit the direct applicability of the datasets in Table 1 and other similar datasets. To support reproducible and efficient traffic simulations, we adopt the open-source CityFlow simulator12 as the core simulation environment and convert the source data into the CityFlow format. Among various well-established transportation simulation engines, such as Vissim18 and SUMO17, CityFlow is particularly well-suited for large-scale traffic simulation due to the following advantages: Scalability - It can reliably handle extensive urban networks with thousands of intersections while supporting high-volume traffic flows;High performance - It provides an interactive simulation environment with high computational efficiency;Generalizability - It includes user-friendly built-in functions along with generalizability for customized implementations. This is important for researchers to easily build system components for implementing hierarchical traffic control strategies.

To standardize heterogeneous traffic datasets into the CityFlow format, this paper proposes a unified workflow consisting of three core stages: road network reconstruction, traffic signal standardization, and flow construction.

Network reconstruction

Converting traffic network data into CityFlow format presents two major challenges: (1) the diversity of source formats and (2) accurate reconstruction of refined road networks. Firstly, to accommodate diverse input formats, including SUMO, CBData, and Geographic Information Systems (GIS), we develop a data parsing component capable of extracting structural elements and inferring topological relationships across formats. This component identifies intersections (nodes) and roads (edges), and accurately maps their connectivity, thereby establishing a consistent foundation for subsequent network reconstruction. Secondly, to reconstruct refined road networks that contain detailed lane configurations, channelization, and diverse intersection layouts, we incorporate parameter estimation and direction assignment to model complex intersections. These components handle multi-approach intersections with diverse geometric layouts, as well as bidirectional intersections with varying lane numbers and segment channelization.

The first component of the reconstruction workflow is data parsing, which aims to extract the core structural elements of road networks, specifically intersections (nodes) and roads (edges), from heterogeneous source formats. The elements available in each format are summarized in Table 2. Among these formats, GIS data is primarily used as a base map, providing spatial and semantic information. However, it lacks explicit representations of dynamic traffic elements such as movements and signal phase logic. In contrast, CBData and SUMO are designed for traffic simulation and encode additional elements such as movements and signal configurations. However, CBData does not provide geometric representations of intersections, and its signal control is limited to directional permissions without defining turning movements. SUMO offers a more detailed and modular representation of intersections, roads, movements, and traffic signals, yet still requires inferring topological relationships to construct a coherent network graph. The core task in this component is to construct the network’s topological structure. The traffic network is represented as a directed graph G = (V, E), where V denotes the set of intersections (nodes), and E denotes the set of directed roads (edges) that capture connectivity among the intersections. The second component of the reconstruction workflow is parameter estimation, which focuses on reconstructing intersection geometries and estimating their associated parameters. Two limitations hinder the direct use of topology parameters derived from raw geometric boundaries. First, traffic simulators employ different intersection modeling approaches, resulting in inconsistent definitions of geometric attributes. For example, CityFlow characterizes an intersection using a center point and a width, while SUMO represents an intersection using boundary points extracted from stop-line geometries. Second, existing conversion tools often rely on simplified assumptions that fail to capture the variability and geometric complexity of real-world intersections. For instance, the official SUMO-to-CityFlow converter assigns a uniform width of 15 meters to all intersections. To address these issues, we first apply convex hulls to normalize heterogeneous intersection geometries, converting irregular boundaries into closed polygons. Then, minimum bounding rectangles are derived from the hulls to estimate the parameters. The third component of the reconstruction workflow is direction assignment, which is essential for movement classification and consistent phase design. Existing methods typically assign directions based on interval, angle ordering, and angle difference19. However, these rule-based methods often perform poorly when applied to real intersections and fail to handle cases with three or five approaches. We formulate direction assignment as a global optimization problem, which minimizes the total absolute angular deviation between actual approaches and their assigned cardinal directions. A standard intersection is characterized by four approaches aligned with the cardinal directions, as illustrated in Fig. 4a. For a real-world four-approach intersection (e.g., Fig. 3b), we compute the absolute angular deviation between each approach and each cardinal direction (see Fig. 4b), and determine the assignment that minimizes the total deviation. This formulation can be easily extended to intersections with fewer approaches, since cardinal directions could remain unassigned without altering the optimization structure. For five-approach intersections (e.g., Fig. 3c), we select the four approaches that yield the minimum total angular deviation (as illustrated by the red approaches in Fig. 4c). Signal standardization

Signal phase data serve as the basis for both intersection coordination and higher-level strategies, such as perimeter control21. However, designing phases for hundreds of intersections in a city-scale network requires significant effort. We propose a standardized phase design that unifies movement classification and automates phase matching. It can significantly reduce manual configuration across diverse intersection layouts. The design consists of two steps: (1) retrieving the sequence index of available movements and (2) selecting feasible signal phases based on the layout of each intersection.

To retrieve the sequence index of available movements, we apply movement classification at each intersection to derive all permissible movements based on the direction assignment described above. Flow construction

Existing public traffic datasets often suffer from limited temporal duration, which restrains both the analysis of urban traffic patterns and the training of data-driven algorithms. Datasets such as Jinan12, Hangzhou22, and Manhattan12 cover only partial metropolitan networks and span just one hour of observations derived from camera data. With such short durations, traffic phenomena such as congestion propagation and spillover effects cannot be adequately captured. Therefore, city-scale datasets with sufficient observation periods are essential to capture the complex dynamics of urban traffic systems23. Although the Nanchang dataset13 contains a road network with more than 2,000 intersections, the sampled traffic volume within one hour includes only 9,786 vehicles, which may be insufficient to reflect the spatial and temporal variability of traffic demand across a large network.

To remedy this, the Xuancheng dataset was collected over one month, enabling the analysis of day-to-day and weekly traffic patterns. By equipping AVI sensors at the entrances and exits of nearly all road segments in Xuancheng24,25, the city generates high-resolution traffic data amounting to approximately 4,000,000 records per day. In contrast to camera-based approaches that rely on predefined turning ratios to estimate complete trip paths22, the AVI-based approach directly records the movements of almost every vehicle within each road segment26. Therefore, by tracking each vehicle across multiple road segments, as illustrated in Fig. 6, we aggregate millions of flow records to derive around 350,000 trip data points per day. To ensure the reliability of constructed trips, a data cleaning procedure is typically applied before finalizing the trip dataset27. First, trips involving maneuvers inconsistent with the road network, such as turning movements prohibited at specific intersections, are identified as invalid and excluded. Second, trips with missing or incomplete AVI passage records are repaired via route inference. This process leverages CityFlow’s Dijkstra-based routing module to compute the shortest feasible path consistent with the predefined road topology.

Utility of multi-scale traffic data

The reconstructed datasets provide the key information needed for hierarchical traffic control, including network topology and trip data. The collection of datasets differs in their network and demand characteristics, offering complementary strengths in spatial resolution, temporal coverage, and network scale. For example, the Jinan and Hangzhou datasets focus on small-scale arterial corridors, while the Manhattan dataset represents a medium-scale grid network, and the Nanchang dataset captures city-scale traffic dynamics using a coarse network representation. In contrast, the Xuancheng dataset provides fine-grained network topology and long-term reconstructed vehicle trips derived from AVI records. Collectively, they span a wide range of urban traffic conditions, from small grid networks to city-scale networks, and from short-term peak observations to long-term demand dynamics. This diversity enables the construction of realistic simulation environments for the development and validation of advanced traffic control strategies. We replay the trip data in a traffic simulation environment to derive traffic state data that characterizes traffic dynamics, serving as the basis for design and evaluation of hierarchical control21.

Technical Validation

This study provides city-scale, high-resolution data that support hierarchical traffic control strategies. To ensure consistency between the converted data and real-world traffic conditions, we validate the outputs from two perspectives: network data and trip data. Furthermore, we conduct a series of experiments to demonstrate the feasibility of using traffic state data. All experiments are carried out using the Xuancheng dataset, which provides a realistic city-scale environment for validation. The Xuancheng network comprises hundreds of signalized intersections and thousands of road segments, encompassing a diverse range of road types and intersection layouts. This dataset also provides city-scale traffic demand data, which allows the simulation to reproduce realistic traffic volumes and urban traffic dynamics.

The computational setup consists of a workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen 7 5800X CPU and 64 GB of RAM. Simulating one hour of traffic signal control in the Jinan network at a one-second resolution requires roughly half a minute of computation. When scaling to larger scenarios with hierarchical traffic control, such as simulating signal control and perimeter control for one hour across the entire Xuancheng network at a one-second resolution, the computation requires approximately five minutes. Additionally, simulations involving multiple cities can be executed in parallel to distribute the computational workload.

Trip data validation

The converted trip data are validated by comparing simulated travel times with their real-world counterparts. To mitigate the impact of external variability, a road segment of approximately 560 meters is selected, with traffic data recorded before 6:00 AM. The selected segment is a single-lane road that widens to two lanes only at the downstream intersection approach. The upstream and downstream intersections comprise three and four approaches, respectively, and both operate under a Webster-based signal control scheme. The single-lane configuration eliminates the influence of lane-changing maneuvers, thereby enabling a more accurate assessment of car-following behavior and travel time consistency between the simulation and observations.

We reconstruct the real-world traffic environment by replicating empirical signal control protocols and assigning trip paths consistent with observed real-world movements. As shown in Fig. 11, the simulated travel times closely match the real-world observations. Specifically, the average simulated travel time is 78.5 seconds, while the observed average travel time is 76.6 seconds. The discrepancy can be attributed to two primary factors. First, in real-world conditions, drivers may accelerate near the end of the green phase, a behavior not represented in the simulation. Second, the simulation enforces a strict speed limit constraint, which ensures that simulated minimum travel times reflect ideal free-flow conditions. In contrast, real-world drivers may exceed posted speed limits, producing shorter travel times than the simulated minimum.Fig. 11Travel time from simulation and observation on the selected road segment.

Usage Notes

Each city dataset consists of three main files: a configuration file, a road network file, and a traffic trip file. The configuration file allows researchers to customize key simulation parameters, including the simulation time step, phase activation status, and specify the road network and trip files to be loaded. Details of the road network and traffic trip files are provided in the Data Records section. The datasets are organized into two main directories: cfg and data. The cfg directory contains multiple subfolders, each corresponding to a specific dataset and storing its associated configuration files for that dataset. The data directory contains subfolders for each dataset, in which the associated road network file and traffic trip file are stored together to define the static network topology and traffic demand.

To facilitate seamless adoption and reproducibility, we provide detailed online usage documentation in Figshare41. The documentation introduces a hierarchical traffic control architecture spanning vehicular, lane-intersection, and network levels, and describes the operational logic of each control level. It further presents the formulation and computation procedures of the proposed multidimensional performance metrics across different levels. In addition, representative case studies based on the Xuancheng dataset are provided to demonstrate the practical use of multidimensional evaluation in hierarchical traffic control. Specifically, vehicular-level control is evaluated using vehicle trajectories and travel time, lane-intersection-level control is assessed using density, outflow, and delay, and network-level control is evaluated through MFD-based metrics.
