Emergence of NOX1/DUOX NADPH oxidases is a key feature of functional neutrophil reprogramming at the gut and lung barrier
Authors: Ashish K Singh, Maurice O'Mara, Julie Drieu La Rochelle, Noemie Therry, Aurora D'Alessio, John Baugh, Ramya S Barre, Misaki Matsumoto, Aitor Nogales, Luis Martínez-Sobrido, Ulla G Knaus
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctional neutrophil diversity is recognized as a driver of development, progression and resolution of disease. Recruited neutrophils are imprinted by biochemical, biophysical and mechanical stimuli of the encountered microenvironment, altering their genetic and phenotypic program. The functional implications of this reprogramming are of critical importance for devising strategies to modify neutrophil behavior. Oxidant production affects neutrophil responses, shapes the microenvironment and often determines disease outcome in inflammation, infection and cancer. Here we report neutrophil diversification at mucosal barriers in inflammatory and infectious disease, culminating in tissue and stimulus-dependent de novo expression of the NADPH oxidases NOX1, DUOX2, and DUOX1 in recruited neutrophils. In contrast to proinflammatory DUOX2, myeloid NOX1 ameliorated colonic inflammation, yet epithelial NOX1 increased neutrophil recruitment from the onset, with a similar response observed in pulmonary S. aureus infection. In contrast, neutrophil DUOX expression did not alter S. aureus disease progression but extended host survival in influenza A virus infection. Thus, at gut and lung barriers an expansion of neutrophil oxidases occurs that highlights proinflammatory and antimicrobial DUOX2 activity, while NOX1 function seems intricate with multiple inputs. Evaluation of these de novo expressed oxidases in other neutrophil-driven diseases will further uncover their contribution to host protection and pathogenesis.Keywords: Neutrophils, NADPH oxidase, Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), Pulmonary disease, Staphylococcus aureus, Influenza A virus

Introduction

Neutrophils are the most abundant immune cells in the body, comprising ∼50–70 % of the circulating white blood cells in humans and ∼10–25 % in mice. Continuously produced and matured in the bone marrow, they enter the bloodstream, migrate through tissues as part of immune surveillance, and ultimately undergo clearance [[1], [2], [3]]. During infection, neutrophils are among the first immune cells recruited in response to microbial invasion. Following their migration into affected tissues, they deploy a range of antimicrobial mechanisms, including phagocytosis, the release of antimicrobial peptides and proteolytic enzymes, generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [[4], [5], [6]]. These actions are essential for limiting pathogen dissemination and initiating early immune responses. Beyond their protective role, neutrophils contribute to the pathogenesis of various inflammatory disorders such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [[7], [8], [9]]. Persistent or excessive neutrophil activation is linked to tissue injury and chronic inflammation, largely due to the uncontrolled release of cytotoxic granule contents, oxidants and pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Historically considered key effector cells of immune defense and inflammation, neutrophils are now recognized as important regulators of tissue homeostasis, contributing to B cell maturation and antibody production, hematopoiesis, granulopoiesis, the maintenance of lung and gut physiology, and the regulation of core metabolic processes [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. Neutrophils exhibit substantial heterogeneity, remarkable phenotypic plasticity, and functional reprogramming within tissue environments, all traits that are increasingly being recognized in both physiological and pathological contexts [9,[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. The superoxide production by the phagocyte NADPH oxidase (NOX2) is essential for microbial killing, and its deficiency causes chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) and CGD-associated inflammatory bowel disease (CGD-IBD) [5,20]. Recently, we demonstrated that neutrophils infiltrating gastrointestinal tissue in IBD, CGD-IBD, and infection undergo reprogramming to express DUOX2 that plays a dichotomous role, exacerbating intestinal inflammation while supporting host defense against the food-borne pathogen Salmonella [19]. Here, we report further expansion of the NADPH oxidase family in neutrophils infiltrating mucosal barrier tissues, leading to widespread NOX1 and stimulus-dependent DUOX1/2 expression, and explore the functional significance of these enzymes in shaping intestinal and lung disease.

Materials and methods

Human samples

Pediatric IBD biopsy tissues were collected under the DOCHAS study, approved by the CHI Research Ethics Committee (GEN/193/11). CGD-IBD biopsy samples were obtained from patients enrolled in the NIH IRB-approved protocol 93-I-0119 (NCT00001355) at the National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA. The tissue samples used in this study have been described previously [19].

Mice and in vivo experimentation

C57BL/6J (JAX000664), Duoxa1a2fl/fl [21], B6.129X1-Nox1tm1Kkr/J (Nox1-/y) (JAX018787), B6.129S-Cybbtm1Din/J (Cybb-/y) (JAX002365) and B6. Cg-Tg (S100A8-cre,-EGFP)1Ilw/J (Mrp8cre+, JAX021614) strains were used. Animals were bred and maintained at the UCD Biomedical facility in individually ventilated cages containing enrichment in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12h light/dark cycle, unlimited food (Teklad 2018, Envigo), and filtered sterile water. HEPA-filtered microisolator cages were used to house germ-free C57BL/6NTac mice during experiments. Animal experiments using male and female mice were initiated during the light cycle and followed the ARRIVE guidelines. The study was approved by the UCD Animal Research Ethics Committee and the Health Products Regulatory Authority of Ireland following the EU Directive 2010/63/EU.

Colitis TNBS colitis was induced as described [19]. Briefly, 8-12-week-old male and female mice received 100 μl of 2.5 % TNBS (in 50 % ethanol) or vehicle (50 % ethanol/water) intrarectally under anesthesia (100 mg/kg BW ketamine and 12.8 mg/kg BW xylazine i. p.). DSS colitis was induced following a 10-day conditioning period. Male mice (8–10 weeks old) were given 3 % DSS in their drinking water for 5 days, followed by regular water only. Daily scoring was performed as per approved protocols.

Salmonella enterica intestinal infection 8-10-week-old mice received a single oral gavage of streptomycin (25 mg/mouse), followed 24 h later by oral administration of 108 CFU of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (GFP Cm+) suspended in PBS. Salmonella was grown in Luria-Bertani broth supplemented with 25 μg/ml chloramphenicol, harvested at the exponential phase, and resuspended in PBS for treatment.

Bleomycin treatment 10-12-week-old C57BL/6J wildtype mice were instilled with 3U/kg BW of bleomycin intratracheally. Animals were monitored for 4 days, followed by euthanasia using pentobarbital (200 mg/kg BW) and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) collection.

Intranasal LPS instillation 10-12-week-old C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized with ketamine (75 mg/kg BW s. c.) and medetomidine (1 mg/kg BW s. c.), followed by intranasal instillation of LPS (3 mg/kg BW). Anesthesia was reversed 10 min later using atipamezole (1 mg/kg BW s. c.). 2 days post-instillation bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid was collected after euthanasia with pentobarbital (200 mg/kg BW i. p.).

Pulmonary infection (S. aureus, Influenza A virus) Mice were infected with 5 x 108 or 5 x 109 CFU Staphylococcus aureus SH1000-GFP (SA) or with 104 PFU Influenza A virus (pH1N1-Nluc; IAV) intranasally under anesthesia (75 mg/kg BW ketamine and 1 mg/kg BW medetomidine i. p.). 1 mg/kg BW atipamezole (i.p.) was used for anesthesia reversal 10 min after pathogen instillation. SA was grown on tryptic soy broth/agar media supplemented with 25 μg/ml chloramphenicol. SA was freshly grown in tryptic soy broth, harvested from the exponential phase and resuspended in PBS for infection. IAV was diluted in PBS from titrated frozen aliquots. Daily weight and welfare scoring, and disease parameters were recorded. Influenza A virus (pH1N1-Nluc): We used reverse genetics approaches to generate the recombinant pandemic Influenza A/California/04/09 H1N1 (pH1N1) expressing nanoluciferase (pH1N1-Nluc) [22]. Briefly, co-cultures of 293T and MDCK cells (1:1) (6-well plate format, 106 cells/well, triplicates) were co-transfected in suspension using lipofectamine 2000, with pH1N1 ambisense pDZ-PB2,-PB1,-PA, -HA, -NP, -NA, and -M plasmids together with a pDZ-NS plasmid encoding Nluc fused to the viral NS1 protein as described [22,23]. 48 h after transfection cell culture supernatants were collected and used to infect fresh MDCK cell monolayers in 6-well plate format, 106 cells/well, in triplicate. At 48 h post-infection, cell culture supernatants were collected and the presence of pH1N1-Nluc was verified by HA assay. A clonal pH1N1-Nluc virus was selected by plaque assay and used to generate a viral stock in MDCK cells [22,23].

In vivo imaging For analysis of pulmonary IAV infection by IVIS imaging, thoracic hair was removed using a hair clipper and hair removal cream. Afterwards, mice were anesthetized with 5 % isoflurane and injected with 100 μl of Nano-Glo® luciferase assay substrate (Promega; N1110) retro-orbitally. Whole body imaging was performed with 1 min exposure using the IVIS® Spectrum CT (PerkinElmer). For intestinal ROS measurements, hair clippers were first used to remove dorsal side hair over the peritoneum. Mice were anesthetized with 5 % isoflurane and injected with L-012 (20 mg/kg BW i. p.). Mice were euthanized after 2 min, and the intestines were immediately removed and imaged with a 5 s exposure. Total flux was calculated as photons/sec. Image acquisition and analysis were performed using Living Image® v.4.7.3 software (Revvity).

Isolation of colonic cells

Colonic lamina propria cells were collected as described [19,24]. Briefly, mouse colonic tissues were enzymatically digested after the epithelial layer was removed. The resulting cell suspension was resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10 mM HEPES and 10 % fetal bovine serum. These cells were then used for flow cytometry analysis or further enriched for neutrophils using the EasySep™ Mouse Neutrophil Enrichment Kit (Stemcell; 19762) following the provided protocol. Neutrophils were subsequently isolated via positive selection using anti-Ly6G microbeads (Miltenyi; 130-120-337).

Isolation of bone marrow cells

Bone marrow cells were isolated by harvesting the mouse femur and tibia bones without the use of ethanol [19]. The bones were flushed with calcium- and magnesium-free HBSS using a 26-gauge needle. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a sterile 70-μm mesh and subjected to Percoll density gradient centrifugation. Cells were layered over a four-layer Percoll gradient (50 %, 55 %, 62 %, 81 %) in 45 % Percoll, followed by RBC lysis. For sorting, the entire bone marrow suspension, without neutrophil enrichment, underwent RBC lysis, staining, and FACS sorting.

BAL collection and analysis

Mice were euthanized by i. p. injection of 200 mg/kg BW pentobarbital followed by bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) collection. 1 ml calcium- and magnesium-free HBSS supplemented with 10 mM HEPES buffer and 1 mM EDTA was pushed through a small slit in the trachea into the lungs and then withdrawn [25]. This process was repeated ten times for optimal recovery of BAL cells. EDTA was washed out, and the combined cell pellets were used for analysis. BAL neutrophils were sorted via positive selection using anti-Ly6G microbeads as described earlier. For analysis of BAL cell counts and cytokine/chemokine profiling, the first 1 ml of BAL fluid collected was used. The Proteome Profiler Mouse Cytokine Array Panel A kit (40 cytokines/chemokines/growth factors on dot blot, Bio-Techne; ARY006) was used to analyze BAL cytokines. Following centrifugation, BAL fluid was filtered through a 0.2 μm filter. Samples from four animals were pooled per group, and 800 μl of pooled BAL fluid was used for each blot (n = 4 mice/blot/group). Signal detection was performed using the SuperSignal™ West Dura Extended Duration Substrate and imaged with a Vilber FUSION FX7 Western blot imager (technical duplicates).

Mass spectrometry

A previously published colonic (TNBS day2) and peritoneal (CXCL1/2, 6h) neutrophil dataset [19], accessible via the ProteomeXchange Consortium through PRIDE (PXD061238), was analyzed using Perseus software. As described therein, a volcano plot (FDR = 0.05; S0 = 0.1) was generated with missing values imputed as “0”.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Human and mouse tissues were fixed with 4 % formaldehyde solution and embedded in paraffin blocks. 5 μm-thick sections were used for staining and analysis as described [19]. For light microscopy, tissues were stained with Mayer's hematoxylin and Eosin Y. Images were acquired using the Aperio AT2 DX system (Leica Biosystems) and analyzed using Aperio ImageScope software (Leica Biosystems).

For fluorescence microscopy, tissues were rehydrated and underwent antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6, 0.05 % Tween 20) at 95 °C for 18min. Blocking was performed with TBS containing 5 % goat or donkey serum, 1 % BSA, and 0.05 % Tween 20 at room temperature for 1 h. An additional 1 h block with Mouse-on-Mouse IgG Blocking Solution (Thermo Fisher; R37621) was included for mouse primary antibodies.

Tissues were incubated with anti-mouse NOX1 [26], anti-mouse/human NOS2 (sc7271, Santa Cruz), anti-human NOX1 [27], anti-mouse/human MPO (AF3137, Bio-Techne), and anti-mouse F4/80 (123101, Biolegend) overnight at 4 °C, washed five times with TBS containing 0.025 % Triton X-100, then incubated with secondary antibodies for 1h at room temperature, followed by washes. Then, tissues were stained with DAPI and mounted with Prolong™ glass antifade mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were acquired using an LSM800 Airyscan (Zeiss) and analyzed using ICY (Institut Pasteur) software.

Flow cytometry

Staining for flow cytometry analysis and cell sorting was performed as described [19]. Briefly, 1x106 cells were stained with viability dye for 15 min, followed by Fc blocking (anti-mouse CD16/32; 1:100; 10 min RT) and conjugated antibodies (1:100; 20 min; 40C). Cells were fixed with eBioscience™ IC Fixation Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed. Cells were fixed and permeabilized for intracellular staining using eBioscience™ Fixation/Permeabilization buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1X eBioscience™ Permeabilization buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 5 % donkey serum was used for blocking (10 min RT), followed by staining with primary anti-DUOX (UK) antibody 30 min at 4 °C [19,28,29] and conjugated donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody (30 min at 4 °C). Cells were resuspended in PBS after washing, followed by analysis.

Sorting mouse bone marrow neutrophil lineage populations was performed as described [19]. Samples from two mice were pooled together for sorting to achieve optimal cell numbers for gene expression analysis. All antibodies were titrated to ensure optimal resolution of cell populations. Compensation was carried out using UltraComp eBeads Plus (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and gating strategies were established based on the corresponding fluorescence minus one (FMO) control. Flow cytometry antibodies are listed in Table S1.

Flow cytometry was performed on a CytoFLEX-LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). FACS Aria IIIu cell sorter (BD Biosciences) or CytoFLEX-SRT (Beckman Coulter) was used for sorting cells. Data was analyzed using CytExpert v.2.6 (Beckman Coulter) and FlowJo™ v.10 (Becton Dickinson).

Quantitative real-time PCR

RNA from cells was extracted using the TRIzol™ method (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) was used to extract RNA from tissues. RNA was quantified by NanoDrop® spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and cDNA synthesis was carried out using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene expression analysis was performed with TaqMan Real-time PCR assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or SYBR chemistry using 50 ng cDNA per reaction. TaqMan probes and SYBR green primers are listed in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Unpaired two-tailed student's t-test was used for two independent groups. Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test was used for survival analysis. One-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison test was used to determine the statistical difference between the three independent groups. P values < 0.05 were defined as significant. Asterisks indicate statistical significance (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ns nonsignificant). The specific n values, representing the total number of animals or human samples per group, are provided in the corresponding figure legends, along with additional methodological details. Statistical analysis was carried out using Prism v.8.0, and final figures were prepared using Adobe Photoshop V.2024. Biorender was used to prepare the graphical abstract.

Results

NOX1 and NOS2 expression in intestinal neutrophils

We reported that neutrophils undergo extensive functional reprogramming within the inflamed or infected intestinal environment [19]. A key feature of this reprogramming was the expansion of the oxidative repertoire, marked by expression of DUOX2 in IBD patients and mice that exacerbated intestinal inflammation and tissue injury. Re-analysis of mass spectrometry data obtained from colonic neutrophils isolated at the disease peak of TNBS colitis revealed additionally the expression of NOX1 (Fig. 1A). Until now, intestinal NOX1 expression was thought to be restricted to the barrier epithelium where it has been linked to stem cell maintenance, Toll receptor and TNFα signaling, inflammation and host defense [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. The unexpected expression of NOX1 in highly activated intestinal neutrophils suggests a functional role in modulating immune responses. The presence of inducible nitric oxide synthetase (NOS2) in these neutrophils will facilitate peroxynitrite generation by combined NOX1/NOS2 activity as reported in the ileal epithelium [34,35]. Confocal microscopy of colonic tissue collected from wildtype mice on day 2 of TNBS colitis confirmed the presence of NOX1 and NOS2 in recruited neutrophils (Fig. 1B, Fig. S1A). The antibody detecting murine NOX1 was revalidated by immunofluorescence of colonic tissues derived from wildtype and Nox1-/y mice in homeostasis and at the peak of TNBS colitis (Fig. S1B and C). NOX1 expression was also detected in colonic neutrophils following Salmonella enterica intestinal infection (Fig. S1D). NOX1 expression in intestinal neutrophils during colitis and infection mirrors our earlier findings with DUOX2 [19], highlighting a broader dimension of ROS-centered neutrophil reprogramming within the intestinal environment.

To further establish NOX1 and NOS2 expression as a particular feature of recruited, proinflammatory neutrophils, gene expression profiles of neutrophil lineage populations from naïve bone marrow to mature neutrophils were compared with purified colonic neutrophils (Fig. 1C and D). Nox1, its organizer Noxo1, and Nos2 mRNA were detected in granulocyte-monocyte progenitor (GMP) populations during the early stages of neutrophil development (mean Ct: Nox1∼29.7, Noxo1∼28.8, Nos2∼32), but their expression was not detectable during later developmental stages and in mature neutrophils. However, in colitis the expression of these genes is induced in colonic neutrophils with high upregulation in SPF mice (microbiome present) versus very moderate expression in germ-free mice (mean Ct: SPF Nox1∼22.6, Noxo1∼22.1, Nos2∼19.5; GF Nox1∼35, Noxo1∼33.8, Nos2∼29.4), illustrating the profound effect of the microbiota on NOX1 and NOS2 expression in infiltrating neutrophils.

ROS generation by activated neutrophils has been associated with the NOX2 phagocyte oxidase and recently with DUOX2 in the inflamed intestine of IBD and CGD-IBD patients [19,36]. Immunofluorescence analysis of biopsies derived from both IBD and CGD-IBD patient populations revealed additionally the presence of NOX1 and NOS2 in recruited intestinal neutrophils (Fig. 1E–H, Fig. S1E), underscoring the clinical relevance of neutrophil reprogramming in intestinal inflammation. As the NOX2 complex is dysfunctional in CGD-IBD, NOX1 may generate superoxide and/or form peroxynitrite for limited, compensatory host defense in these patients or augment inflammation by oxidative modification of signaling pathways in neutrophils or adjacent epithelial cells.

Myeloid NOX1 protects against intestinal inflammation in colitis

Although NOX1 deficiency is considered a risk factor for developing very early onset IBD [37,38], NOX1-deficient mice do not spontaneously develop colitis. Even when colitis is induced, Nox1-/y mice exhibited either attenuated disease (TNBS) [31,39] or showed no substantial difference to wildtype mice (DSS) [[40], [41], [42], [43]]. As NOX1 was detected in neutrophils recruited by TNBS, we confirmed and extended these colitis studies, showing accelerated recovery 24 h after disease induction, markedly decreased inflammation, and downregulation of chemokines and cytokines including Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Il1b, Il6, Lcn2, while Il17a and Ifng expression was upregulated, and neither Nos2, Duox2 or Duoxa2 expression were affected (Fig. 2A–F). Downregulation of immune regulators may indicate interference with neutrophil recruitment to the chemically injured intestine. Indeed, neutrophil infiltration was markedly decreased 24 h and 48 h after colitis induction in Nox1-/y mice, which was also reflected in the diminished L-012 signal obtained in the colon of TNBS-treated NOX1-deficient mice (Fig. 2G and H). In living mice, the L-012 signal reflects mainly peroxynitrite formed by superoxide (NOX1) and nitric oxide (NOS2), or neutrophil-generated hypochlorous acid [41,44,45]. In Nox1-/y mice decreased colonic oxidant generation in TNBS colitis is likely due to a combined reduction of epithelial peroxynitrite generation and the paucity of activated neutrophils generating peroxynitrite and hypochlorous acid.

Assessing the role of neutrophil NOX1 in intestinal inflammation was not feasible as floxed Nox1 mice could not be sourced. Generating bone marrow chimeric mice with NOX1 deficiency in the myeloid compartment will alter several innate immune cell populations. Especially macrophages play an important role in intestinal inflammation by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines, presenting antigens, and clearing pathogens and dead cells [46], and their dysregulated activation can drive chronic inflammation, tissue damage, and impaired mucosal healing. Activated M1-type macrophages are well known to express NOX1 and NOS2 [39,44,47]. We examined the expression of these proteins in intestinal macrophages and neutrophils at the peak of neutrophil infiltration during intestinal inflammation. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that both NOX1 and NOS2 are expressed in F4/80+ macrophages and MPO+ neutrophils (Fig. 2I). BM chimeric mice reflecting NOX1 deficiency in neutrophils and macrophages will permit analysis of the innate immune compartment during the early stages of colitis (Fig. 3A). Chimeric myeloid NOX1-deficient mice developed more severe colitis with decreased body weight, higher disease activity, and a significantly inflamed colon as apparent by the decreased colon length (Fig. 3B–D). At the peak of colitis, the expression of Duox2 and Duoxa2 was not altered, while Cybb (NOX2), Mpo, Nos2, Lcn2, and the chemokines Cxcl1 and Cxcl2 were upregulated, indicating increased infiltration of activated neutrophils (Fig. 3E). The amplified expression of proinflammatory cytokines (Il1b, Il6) likely stems from deleting NOX1 in both neutrophils and macrophages. In conclusion, the contribution of NOX1 to neutrophil-driven acute colitis is complex, with epithelial NOX1 promoting intestinal inflammation by enhancing immune cell recruitment, whereas inflammation-induced expression of NOX1 in myeloid cells is protective by limiting the severity of colitis.

Expansion of the neutrophil NADPH oxidase repertoire in pulmonary disease

Intestine and lung have structural and functional similarities, both featuring epithelial barriers, protective mucus layers, local immune networks, and interaction with resident microbiota. As acute pulmonary disease is typically accompanied by neutrophil infiltration, we explored whether neutrophil subsets infiltrating the lung during various pulmonary diseases will induce de novo expression of NADPH oxidases distinct from NOX2. Initially, LPS instillation was used as it stimulates rapid Nox1 expression in macrophages [44,48]. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) Ly6G+CD11b+ neutrophils induced Nox1 mRNA (mean Ct∼29.3) but not Duox2 mRNA (mean Ct∼38.9) nor Duox1 (not detected) 48 h after LPS treatment (Fig. 4A, Fig. S2A). Nox4 expression in infiltrating neutrophils following LPS inhalation has been debated [49]. We did not detect Nox4 expression in BAL neutrophils 48 h post-LPS treatment, nor in bone marrow neutrophils or earlier neutrophil lineage populations, nor in colonic neutrophils 48 h post-TNBS treatment (Fig. S2B and C). Validated antibodies for the detection of murine NOX4 are not available. As lung macrophages and lung fibroblasts induced DUOX1 expression when challenged by other stimuli [[50], [51], [52], [53]], we examined if DUOX expression could be induced in neutrophils recruited by bleomycin, an agent causing lung injury and pulmonary fibrosis. Flow cytometry indicated that BAL Ly6G+CD11b+ neutrophils did not express DUOX (antibody detects murine DUOX1 and DUOX2) (Fig. 4B), but a fraction of not further identified Ly6G−CD11b− cells in BAL were DUOX positive (13 %, Fig. S3A). Neutrophils recruited to BAL after bleomycin treatment were sparse, thus only protein expression was analyzed. Additionally, due to poor performance of the anti-NOX1 antibody in flow cytometry, NOX1 expression in different cell populations was not analyzed.

De novo expression of DUOX2 in colonic neutrophils was dependent on the microbiome or introduction of a single pathogenic bacterium in germ-free mice [19], and thus we reasoned that introduction of a pathogen might be required for DUOX expression in neutrophils recruited to the lung. Intranasal instillation of Staphylococcus aureus (SA) was performed in wildtype mice, and 48 h later over 80 % of BAL Ly6G+CD11b+ neutrophils were DUOX positive (Fig. 4C) as were over 50 % of BAL Siglec-F+CD11b−CD11c+ alveolar macrophages (Fig. S3B). Quantitative PCR analysis of Ly6G+CD11b+ neutrophils recovered from BAL of SA-treated mice confirmed expression of Duox1 (mean Ct∼32.7), Duox2 (mean Ct∼33.8), Nox1 (mean Ct∼28.4), Noxo1 (mean Ct∼28.7), and Nos2 (mean Ct∼25.3) (Fig. 4A, Fig. S2A). Thus, neutrophils recruited to the lung by inflammatory or infectious agents express not only the phagocyte oxidase NOX2 but also, depending on the stimulus, the oxidases NOX1, DUOX1, and DUOX2.

NOX1 promotes Staphylococcus aureus lung infection

The role of NOX1 in bacterial lung infections remains poorly defined. As Nox1, Noxo1, and Nos2 were all highly upregulated in neutrophils in the early stage of SA infection, we compared disease severity of infected Nox1-/y mice with wildtype mice. Analogous to TNBS colitis, NOX1-deficient mice showed rapid recovery from SA infection as evident by increased body weight and reduced disease severity 24 h after intranasal treatment (Fig. 4D and E). CD45+CD11b+Ly6G+ neutrophil counts in BAL collected from infected Nox1-/y mice were significantly lower than in infected wildtype mice, yet alveolar macrophages (CD45+SiglecF+CD11c+CD11b−F4/80+) in BAL increased post-infection in Nox1-/y mice (Fig. 4F, G, Fig. S3C). At the same time decreased levels of TNF-α, CXCL9, CXCL10, and M-CSF were detected in BAL fluid of SA infected Nox1-/y mice while nitric oxide levels were not altered (Fig. 4H, Fig. S3D, and not shown). As lung epithelial DUOX has been linked to antimicrobial defense [54], and expression of neutrophil DUOX was evident during S. aureus infection, mice with neutrophil-specific inactivation of DUOX1/2 (Duoxa1a2fl/flMrp8cre+; D12-IA) and control mice (Duoxa1a2fl/flMrp8cre-; D12-C) [19] were analyzed (Fig. 4I and J). We detected no difference in disease outcome and survival, even when a higher SA dose was administered.

Neutrophil DUOX is protective in influenza A virus infection

Prior studies in Nox1-/y mice subjected to influenza A virus (IAV) infection, either by using H3N2 or PR8 virus, suggested a complex role for NOX1, being protective in the early stage, but showing increased morbidity later on [55,56]. Thus, we focused on DUOX1/2 as these enzymes are known to provide antiviral host defense, and our created mouse strains will permit to interrogate neutrophil-specific changes in disease outcome. DUOX-mediated protection in IAV infections was previously linked to global DUOX isoform downregulation or inactivation, and to Duox2 deletion in type 2 alveolar epithelial cells [[57], [58], [59], [60]]. To explore whether neutrophils induce DUOX in response to viral infection, we investigated DUOX expression in BAL neutrophils following intranasal IAV infection of wildtype mice. We used a newly created bioluminescent influenza A pH1N1-Nluc virus that permits simultaneous analysis of disease progression and viral replication [61]. After 48 h of IAV infection, DUOX expression was detected in over 60 % of Ly6G+CD11b+ neutrophils and in 87 % of Ly6G−CD11b+ cells in BAL (Fig. 5A and B). Neutrophil-specific DUOX1/2 inactivated mice (Duoxa1a2fl/flMrp8cre+, D12-IA) and control mice (Duoxa1a2fl/flMrp8cre-, D12-C) were treated with bioluminescent IAV (104 PFU/mouse) and followed for up to nine days. Neutrophil-specific inactivation of DUOX did not notably impact viral load or dissemination (Fig. 5C and D). However, significant host protection and extended survival were observed in control mice expressing active DUOX in neutrophils (Fig. 5E and F). This protection was accompanied by lower chemokine levels (e.g., CXCL9, CXCL10) and elevated TNF-α levels in BAL (Fig. 5G) that have been linked to attenuated lung immunopathology [62,63]. Thus, de novo DUOX expression in recruited neutrophils expands the established contribution of DUOX-mediated antiviral immunity in the lung beyond epithelial cells and DUOX+ alveolar macrophages.

Discussion

The heterogeneity of neutrophils arising from microenvironmental signals is reminiscent of the phenotypic and functional changes in tissue macrophages. Yet understanding how these changes alter neutrophil functions is still in its infancy as isolation, purification and analysis of highly activated neutrophil populations from tissues and organs is challenging, especially as neutrophils cannot be cultured, and thus functional studies are usually performed in freshly isolated human blood neutrophils and mouse bone marrow neutrophils. Even though blood neutrophils can be modified by disease-induced mediators, the task of migrating into tissues and being exposed to a myriad of biochemical and biophysical changes while mounting their own responses sets tissue infiltrating neutrophils apart, illustrating again similarities to macrophages and underscoring the difficulties of understanding functional reprogramming in detail. However, the presence of additional NADPH oxidase isoforms will undoubtedly alter redox signaling, not only in these neutrophils but also in adjacent cells or microorganisms, an important feature for modifying gut and lung barrier epithelia in disease. The induced NOX/DUOX repertoire was slightly different in the tissue environments we created, with neutrophil DUOX2 and NOX1 being highly expressed in the chemically inflamed (TNBS, DSS) and infected intestine, while the agents chosen to provoke lung inflammation only induced NOX1 expression in BAL neutrophils. Thus, NOX1 induction in recruited neutrophils seems to be less complex, which might be associated with its distinct expression in granulocyte-monocyte progenitors (GMPs) and/or a ‘priming’ effect on particular neutrophil chromatin regions [15]. In bacterial lung infection, NOX1, DUOX2, and even DUOX1 were newly expressed in BAL neutrophils. Understanding the contribution of NOX1 and DUOX to neutrophil function is challenging in the presence of NOX2 with its massive oxidative burst, but the creation of several mouse strains enabled insights into neutrophil DUOX2-mediated H2O2 generation and associated disease outcomes in the gut [19] and here in IAV infection. DUOX2 expression in neutrophils might also affect other viral lung diseases as both DUOX2 and DUOXA2 were detected in COVID-19 patient neutrophils [64]. While the observed pathophysiology and cytokine expression in inflammation or infection models will reflect the combined engagement of all NADPH oxidases and other ROS sources in certain cell types, specialized roles in redox signaling and promoting functional outputs are, in our opinion, likely.

This neutrophil-centered approach could not be taken for NOX1 as the generation of neutrophil NOX1-deleted mice was not feasible. Nevertheless, subjecting mice with global or myeloid lineage deleted NOX1 to chemical colitis in the same experimental conditions indicated a complex role for this oxidase, either proinflammatory by inducing neutrophil chemotaxis, reflecting constitutively expressed epithelial NOX1, or limiting inflammation as neutrophil/macrophage-induced NOX1. A proinflammatory role for NOX1 was also observed in pulmonary S. aureus infection, reflecting constitutive NOX1 expression in airway and alveolar epithelial cells [65] and its upregulation in innate immune cells including neutrophils, as shown here [66,67]. Recruited intestinal neutrophils, BAL neutrophils and activated M1-type macrophages in the vicinity additionally expressed NOS2, providing a microenvironment rich in diffusible nitric oxide. At least in the intestine, NOX1 is the major superoxide source for peroxynitrite formation in homeostasis [34,35,41,44], in DSS colitis [40], and in TNBS colitis (Fig. 2H). Understanding whether peroxynitrite generated by the epithelium or immune cells is pro- or anti-inflammatory is hindered by the controversies and opposing results in the nitric oxide field [68]. In general, the NOS2-specific inhibitor 1400W reduced neutrophil infiltration and ameliorated murine colitis, in line with observations in Nox1-/y mice, while global Nos2−/− deficiency reduced or increased colitis susceptibility. Murine colitis severity is strongly influenced by the composition of the microbiota in different animal facilities, which may explain these divergent results.

In conclusion, neutrophils now join other cell types known to express multiple NADPH oxidase isoforms, either constitutively or stimulus induced. Even in epithelial cells or fibroblasts, the specialization of a particular NOX/DUOX enzyme in signal transmission and functional outcome is often unresolved, primarily due to missing information on oxidase isoform localization in space and time, compensatory mechanisms, and lack of oxidase isoform-specific inhibitors. In the intestine, de novo expression of NOX1/DUOX2 in neutrophils was consistent across human and murine tissues, suggesting human disease relevance and a microbiota-associated expression pattern. However, our observations in pulmonary inflammation and infection point to the appearance of distinct neutrophil subsets that rely on the disease context and are likely also relevant to neutrophil-driven inflammation in non-barrier tissues.
