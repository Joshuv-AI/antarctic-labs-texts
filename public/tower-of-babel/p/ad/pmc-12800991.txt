Glenoid Exposure for Anatomic Shoulder Arthroplasty in the Setting of Previous Latarjet Procedure
Authors: Jawaher Alkhateeb, Charlotte Langley, Andrew Trenholm, Ivan Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Performing arthroplasty on shoulders that have previously undergone a Latarjet procedure is challenging. The alterations in surgical landmarks, anatomic planes, and extensive scarring complicate the surgical dissection and obscure glenoid exposure. This article describes a stepwise approach to glenoid exposure and preparation for anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty in the context of a prior Latarjet procedure. We provide a visual demonstration of the altered tissue planes, as well as the close proximity of neurovascular structures relative to the transferred bone block, and further discuss the technical difficulties in removing the Latarjet screws.

The Latarjet procedure is a widely used surgical intervention for managing recurrent anterior shoulder instability.1,2 The procedure involves transferring a portion of the coracoid process, along with its conjoined tendon attachment, to the anteroinferior glenoid through a split in the subscapularis tendon. Since its introduction,3 the Latarjet procedure has demonstrated excellent outcomes in preventing recurrent dislocations.4,5 However, the procedure is associated with a relatively high complication rate, ranging from 15% to 30%.6,7

Patients with a history of recurrent shoulder dislocations have a significantly higher risk of developing glenohumeral arthritis, often requiring shoulder arthroplasty, compared to those without instability.8, 9, 10 Performing arthroplasty on shoulders that have previously undergone the Latarjet procedure presents several technical challenges, including loss of the coracoid and conjoined tendon as surgical landmarks, altered tissue planes, distorted neurovascular anatomy, existing hardware, potential bone loss, and difficulties with soft tissue balancing. These anatomic alterations complicate the interval dissection, obscure glenoid exposure and preparation, and potentially increase the risk of damaging nearby neurovascular structures.11, 12, 13

In this Technical Note, we describe a stepwise approach for glenoid exposure for anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty in the context of a prior Latarjet procedure (Video 1). Intraoperative Challenges and Pearls

Surgical Technique

Positioning and Landmarks

Under general anesthesia, the patient is placed in a beach-chair position. Examination is performed to assess for any residual instability. Skin landmarks are identified, including the clavicle, the deltoid insertion, and, in thinner patients, the base of the coracoid stump. The skin incision can also be located relative to the deltopectoral groove and previous surgical scars (Fig 1).Fig 1Skin incision. (C, clavicle; CB, coracoid base; D, deltoid insertion.)

Deltopectoral Approach

The deltopectoral interval is developed by identifying the cephalic vein. If the cephalic vein is absent following a previous open Latarjet procedure, the interval is identified proximally by the change of orientation between the deltoid and pectoralis major fibers (Fig 2). The subdeltoid space is then released from adhesions between the deltoid and lateral humerus. Two deltoid retractors can be placed: one at the level of the subdeltoid space and the other just proximal to the deltoid insertion.Fig 2Deep interval. View of a left shoulder after developing the deltopectoral interval, showing the underlying deep structures that are scarred and difficult to identify. (Con, conjoined tendon; Delt, deltoid; Pec, pectoralis major tendon; Ssc, subscapularis.)

Deep Dissection

At this level, attention is focused on identifying the change in surgical planes. The subscapularis tendon is extensively scarred. The conjoined tendon is adherent to the medial portion of the subscapularis tendon, at its middle or inferior third, depending on where the tendon split was performed. A distinct change in fiber orientation can be observed between the horizontal fibers of the subscapularis tendon and the vertical fibers of the conjoined tendon. If scarring obscures the plane, carefully resect clavipectoral fascia adhesions (Fig 3). Identify the plane without developing it, and stay lateral to it during the subscapularis tenotomy to avoid inadvertently cutting into the conjoined tendon and potentially injuring neurovascular structures. Next, the upper edge of the pectoralis major tendon is identified, and a few millimeters of its insertion are released to expose the biceps tendon. The biceps tendon is then tenodesed to the upper edge of the pectoralis major and followed proximally to release the rotator interval.Fig 3Altered anatomy after Latarjet. Subscapularis Takedown

The rotator interval marks the superior border of the subscapularis, while the anterior circumflex humeral vessels define the inferior border. A tenotomy incision is placed at 2 cm medial to the bicipital groove. During the tenotomy, 2 nonabsorbable sutures are placed at the superolateral corner, lateral midportion, and inferior portion of the subscapularis. These sutures are used for reflecting the subscapularis to protect neurovascular structures and for later repair (Fig 4). Next, a circumferential humeral-sided capsular release is performed while gradually taking the arm into external rotation until it is dislocated. The humerus is then cut and prepared following the implant design steps.Fig 4Subscapularis takedown. View of a left shoulder showing the subscapularis (Ssc) tenotomy. The conjoined tendon (star) must be identified and avoided when elevating the lateral edge of the subscapularis. Tag sutures are placed for tendon reflection, mobilization, and later repair. (Delt, deltoid.)

Glenoid Exposure

Exposure of the glenoid is facilitated by placing a posteroinferior retractor to displace the humerus and a posterosuperior retractor to retract the deltoid, and anteriorly, a glenoid neck retractor is placed after the subscapularis is mobilized and reflected medially. Mayo scissors are used to release the superior glenohumeral and coracohumeral ligaments. Inferiorly, careful blunt dissection is carried out. We recommend a subperiosteal elevation of the soft tissue attached to the transferred coracoid. In some cases, partial release of the conjoined tendon may be necessary to further facilitate glenoid exposure (Fig 5).Fig 5Glenoid exposure. Latarjet Screws

The graft is evaluated for union, and if unstable, we recommend removing the screws to prevent potential infection from the hardware. The decision to release part of the conjoined tendon from the transferred bone block depends on the extent of exposure needed for hardware removal. We recommend releasing the conjoined tendon from within the joint while maintaining a subperiosteal dissection using electrocautery. The key is to avoid dissection anterior to the transferred conjoined tendon, as this area poses a risk to neurovascular structures (Fig 6). At this level, the axillary artery may become visible (Fig 7). Meticulous dissection medial to the glenoid is critical, particularly from the 3-o’clock to 6-o’clock position. Protect the artery while removing each screw. The anterior rim bone stock should be assessed to ensure stable placement of the glenoid component. Closure and Rehabilitation

The subscapularis is repaired using the previously placed sutures, securing the sutures into the intact lateral humeral insertion footprint. The wound is closed in the standard fashion. The postoperative rehabilitation protocol for anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty is then followed.14

Discussion

Previous studies on reverse shoulder arthroplasty following instability procedures have shown higher complication and revision rates compared to arthroplasty for primary arthritis.11, 12, 13 Specifically, in cases involving postcoracoid transfer procedures, such as the Latarjet or Bristow procedures, the available evidence is scarce, and the reported outcomes are variable. Willemot et al.13 reported a 33% instability rate requiring revision, while Bender et al.12 found no significant difference in revision rates compared to a primary cohort. Despite the limited and variable evidence, there is a consensus that performing arthroplasty on shoulders that have undergone a coracoid transfer procedure is technically demanding.11, 12, 13

The complexity of revising shoulders with a prior coracoid transfer is primarily due to distorted tissue planes, altered neurovascular anatomy, and soft tissue balancing. In a cadaveric study, LaPrade et al.15 defined the minimum distance of each neurovascular structure in relation to the transferred coracoid. The axillary nerve was the closest to the 3-o’clock and 6-o’clock positions on the glenoid, with a minimum distance of 19.8 mm. The musculocutaneous nerve was within 20 mm, and the axillary artery was located within 31 mm.15 However, since these measurements were obtained from a cadaver, where physiological tension and tissue scarring are absent, the actual distances in a living patient may be underestimated. We found the axillary artery to be directly adjacent to the transferred coracoid. Two factors may account for this close proximity. First, the formation of extensive osteophytes beyond the measured bone block may decrease the distance between the artery and the coracoid. Second, the inferior portion of the subscapularis tendon, which typically provides protection to the artery when reflected, may have been retracted or compromised due to the prior subscapularis split. In managing the conjoined tendon, Altintas et al.16 described a complete tenotomy to improve glenoid exposure. However, due to the potential concern of elbow flexion and supination weakness, we recommend limiting the release of the tendon to partial and only when absolutely necessary.

The decision to remove or retain the Latarjet screws depends on specific factors. If nonunion or signs of loosening are present, screws removal is recommended.12,17 This removal requires extensive dissection to expose the screw heads medially, which involves meticulous work closer to the danger zone.15 If there are no signs of screws loosening and the graft is stable, the hardware can be potentially retained. However, a challenge may arise when using a pegged glenoid, as the inferior Latarjet screw could obstruct the drilling of the anteroinferior peg.12 In this case, modifying the pegged glenoid by trimming 2 to 3 mm off the peripheral peg may be considered, while care must be taken to avoid component rocking.18
