The association between anthropometric failure and zinc deficiency among preschool-age children in rural Sidama region, Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Assefa Philipos Kare, Yoseph Demelash Alemu, Beruk Berhanu Desalegn, Ayalew Astatkie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition remains a significant global public health challenge, particularly affecting preschool-age children in low- and middle-income countries. Zinc, an essential micronutrient for growth and immunity, is often deficient in such settings, exacerbating undernutrition risks. Despite evidence linking zinc deficiency to poor anthropometric outcomes, findings remain inconsistent. Limited research exists on its association with combined growth failures, assessed via the Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure (CIAF). In Ethiopia, where undernutrition rates are high, localized data—especially from regions like Sidama—are scarce. This study examines the prevalence of anthropometric failures and their association with zinc deficiency among rural preschool-age children (24–59 months) in Sidama, Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from September 17 to October 3, 2024 with 364 children selected via multistage sampling. Data were collected electronically (KoboCollect), and anthropometric indices (stunting, wasting, underweight) were analyzed using WHO Anthro 2007. Serum zinc levels were measured at the Ethiopian Public Health Institute’s Nutrition Laboratory. Zinc deficiency was defined as < 65 µg/dL in morning non-fasting samples and < 57 µg/dL in afternoon non-fasting samples. Modified Poisson Regression assessed associations and determinants in Stata 17.ResultThe prevalence of CIAF (Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure) was 29.67% (95% CI: 24.96–34.39), with 20.05% (n = 73), 7.97% (n = 29), and 1.65% (n = 6) failing one, two, or all three indices, respectively. Stunting (23.08%; 95% CI: 18.73–27.43), underweight (10.16%; 95% CI: 7.05–13.28), and wasting (7.69%; 95% CI: 4.94–10.44) were observed. Zinc deficiency showed no significant association with CIAF (APR(adjusted prevalence ratios) = 0.89; 95% CI: 0.65–1.23). However, compared to children aged 24–35 months, older children had markedly lower CIAF prevalence: those aged 36–47 months had 35% lower prevalence (APR = 0.65; 95% CI: 0.44–0.96), and those aged 48–59 months had 52% lower prevalence (APR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.34–0.69).Children from households using unimproved water sources had 48% higher CIAF prevalence (APR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.03–2.07). Unexpectedly, household wealth, dietary diversity, maternal education, and food security showed no significant associations.ConclusionThe findings indicate a high prevalence of composite anthropometric failure among preschool-aged children in rural Sidama Region. Zinc deficiency was not a significant predictor. Background

Malnutrition remains a significant global public health challenge, particularly affecting preschool-age (24 to 59 months old) children in low- and middle-income countries. Anthropometric indices such as stunting (low height-for-age), wasting (low weight-for-height), and underweight (low weight-for-age) are critical indicators of undernutrition, reflecting chronic and acute deficiencies in essential nutrients. According to the World Health Organization, 2022 [1], globally in 2022, 149 million children under 5 were estimated to be stunted (too short for age), 45 million were estimated to be wasted (too thin for height). These conditions are associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and long-term developmental consequences, including poor cognitive function, physical impairments, and reduced economic productivity [2]. Undernutrition has a devastating impact, contributing to nearly 45% of all child deaths worldwide [3].

Zinc is an essential micronutrient crucial for growth hormone function, protein synthesis, immune response, and cellular development [4]. It supports normal growth, strengthens immunity, and enhances cognitive function, particularly in children under five. Zinc deficiency can lead to growth retardation, impaired weight gain, and an increased risk of stunting and wasting [5, 6]. Zinc deficiency, common in developing countries due to limited access to zinc-rich foods, is associated with impaired growth, increased infections, and delayed cognitive development [7]. The World Health Organization estimates that 17.3% of the global population is at risk of inadequate zinc intake, with young children most vulnerable [8]. In Ethiopia, zinc deficiency significantly contributes to stunting, child morbidity, and mortality due to poor dietary intake, low bioavailability from plant-based diets, and infections [9]. This issue is worsened by food insecurity and lack of dietary diversity, impacting linear growth and body weight [10].

Emerging evidence suggests a strong association between zinc deficiency and adverse anthropometric outcomes. A study conducted in Japan revealed that serum zinc levels were linked to the linear growth of under-five children [11]. However, research from East Gojjam, Amhara Region of Ethiopia [12] and Edo State, Nigeria [13] found no significant correlation between zinc deficiency and linear growth. Several studies have demonstrated that zinc supplementation improves linear growth and reduces stunting prevalence [14–16]. The inconsistency in these findings may be explained by several factors. First, the role of infections is critical; repeated or chronic infections trigger inflammatory responses that lower serum zinc concentrations through cytokine-mediated redistribution, independent of true zinc status, potentially masking associations [17]. Second, dietary zinc bioavailability varies substantially across populations; plant-based diets dominant in low-income settings contain high levels of phytates, which bind zinc and inhibit its intestinal absorption, reducing the amount of zinc available for physiological functions including growth [18]. Third, variations in study design, sample size, and adjustment for confounding factors such as socioeconomic status and dietary diversity may contribute to divergent results.

Furthermore, the interpretation of serum zinc as a biomarker is complicated by homeostatic regulation and the acute-phase response, as inflammation lowers serum zinc concentrations independently of true nutritional status, potentially biasing associations toward the null [19–21].

Ethiopia faces one of the highest rates of childhood undernutrition globally. According to the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey 2024-25, 40% of children under five are stunted, 5% wasted, and 18% underweight [22]. These conditions frequently overlap, yet most research examines them separately. The country’s nutritional challenges are compounded by food insecurity, poor dietary diversity, and micronutrient deficiencies [15]. Zinc deficiency remains prevalent due to diets dominated by plant-based foods with low bioavailability [23].

In Southern Ethiopia’s Sidama region, where localized studies have documented food insecurity and limited dietary diversity among young children, undernutrition remains a concern [24, 25]. Despite this, research on zinc deficiency’s association with combined growth failures is limited [26, 27]. Most studies focus on single indicators or other micronutrients [28, 29], leaving a critical gap in understanding how zinc deficiency relates to the overlapping burden of malnutrition. This gap is evident in preschool-age children and regions like Sidama, where micronutrient-specific research is scarce [30]. Dietary practices, food security disparities, and localized food availability further highlight the need for targeted research [31]. Although biologically plausible, the link between zinc deficiency and growth remains inconsistent, requiring further investigation [16].

While existing studies provide important insights into zinc’s role in stunting, the relationship between zinc deficiency and combined anthropometric failures remains understudied, particularly in Ethiopia, where malnutrition manifests as complex, overlapping conditions [32]. The Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure offers a comprehensive framework to assess this relationship by capturing the co-occurrence of stunting, wasting, and underweight. Unlike single anthropometric indicators that examine stunting, wasting, or underweight in isolation, CIAF identifies children with multiple concurrent forms of anthropometric failure and provides a more complete picture of the overall malnutrition burden in a population [33, 34]. This is particularly important in settings like rural Sidama, where children often experience more than one growth deficit simultaneously, and using single indicators alone may underestimate the true prevalence of undernutrition [35, 36]. Therefore, this study aims to examine the association between zinc deficiency and anthropometric failure among preschool-age children in the rural Sidama region, Ethiopia. We hypothesized that zinc deficiency is positively associated with anthropometric failure (as measured by CIAF) among preschool-age children. This study provides a more holistic assessment of how zinc deficiency relates to complex patterns of child growth failure in this high-burden setting.

Methods

Study design, & setting

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among preschool-age children in the rural districts of Sidama region, Ethiopia from September 17 to October 3, 2024. Sidama Region, located 273 km south of Addis Ababa, is one of Ethiopia’s twelve regional states, with its capital in Hawassa City. The region is divided into four zones and one city administration, encompassing thirty rural districts and six town administrations. It features diverse agro-ecological conditions, including highland, midland, and lowland climates. Over 85% of the population relies on mixed crop-livestock farming [37], producing livestock products and a variety of crops such as enset, barley, maize, wheat, teff, root crops, and vegetables [38].

Population

According to the 2007 population projection report from the Central Statistical Agency of Ethiopia, the total population of the Sidama Region was estimated to reach 5,301,868 by the year 2024, comprising 2,953,043 males (49.57%) and 2,636,559 females [39]. The estimated number of preschool-aged children in the region is 553,038, with 274,141 males and 278,897 females [39].

The present study targeted preschool-aged children residing in rural areas of the Sidama Region, with the aim of assessing anthropometric indices indicative of nutritional status. Inclusion criteria required that children had lived in the region for at least six months to ensure exposure to the local dietary, environmental, and socioeconomic conditions. Children exhibiting signs of acute illness or congenital skeletal deformities were excluded, as these conditions could transiently affect growth parameters and distort the assessment of chronic nutritional status.

Sample size determination

The sample size for evaluating the association between zinc status and anthropometric indices among preschool children in rural Sidama, Ethiopia, was calculated using a 95% confidence level, 80% power, and an assumed stunting prevalence of 45% [40] among children age 24–35 months. With an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 2.5, a design effect of 1.5, and a 90% response rate adjustment, the required sample size was 290.

Because this research involved two additional aims, the study adopted a sample size of 383 individuals. This number had initially been determined to estimate stunting prevalence in the same population—one of the parallel objectives. The chosen sample proved sufficient to meet the minimum statistical needs for all research goals, while remaining practically achievable given the available resources and budget.

Participant selection

The study employed a multistage sampling technique to select the study participants. First, two zones (Central Sidama and Southern Sidama) were randomly selected from the four zonal administrations using simple random sampling. Then, four rural districts were chosen randomly from fourteen districts using the same method. From the chosen districts, two districts were from highland areas (Hula, Teticha), one from the midland (Dale), and one from the lowland (Aleta Chuko).

Kebeles were selected through simple random sampling (lottery method) across three agro-ecological zones. From each zone, four kebeles were chosen: Loya, Gidicho, Dagala Ganjure, and Godayo Guno (highlands); Wicho, Magara, Soyama, and Semen Masinkala (midlands); and Gambela, Gundo, Dibicha, and Rufo Chancho (lowlands), totaling twelve kebeles. Notably, only Aleta Chuko district contained lowland kebeles (n = 8), as other districts lacked this zone.

With the assistance of voluntary community health workers who helped identify eligible households, preschool-aged children were identified through systematic sampling of family records across the selected kebeles (n = 383). Initial selection employed a lottery method, with one child randomly chosen per household.

Study variables

The dependent variable for this study was the Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure (CIAF), a measure that consolidates three conventional anthropometric indicators—wasting, stunting, and underweight—into a single metric to classify children into mutually exclusive groups based on their growth failure status [33]. As shown in Table 1, the CIAF framework categorizes children into seven groups (A–G), where Group A represents children with no anthropometric failure (i.e., normal growth in height, weight, and weight-for-height). Groups B through G represent the six distinct forms of anthropometric failure, encompassing all possible combinations of stunting, wasting, and/or underweight. Thus, any child classified in Groups B–G is considered to have at least one form of anthropometric failure.

Anthropometric indices were evaluated using WHO Anthro 2007 Software. Wasting was defined as a weight-for-height Z-score (WHZ) below − 2, underweight as a weight-for-age Z-score (WAZ) below − 2, and stunting as a height-for-age Z-score (HAZ) below − 2 [41, 42].

Zinc deficiency, the main independent variable, was assessed by comparing the serum zinc levels against specific thresholds. For morning non-fasting samples, a threshold of below 65 µg/dL was used, while for afternoon non-fasting samples, a threshold of below 57 µg/dL was applied [43]. Individuals with serum zinc levels below this threshold was assigned a value of ‘1’ to indicate deficiency, while those with levels equal to or above the threshold was assigned a value of ‘0’ to indicate normal serum zinc level.

Other independent variables for this study were socio-demographic and economic factors, dietary intake and diversity, and household food security. Anemia status of preschool-age children was assessed and classified as anemic for hemoglobin level of less than < 11.0 g/dl.

Data collection tools and procedure

Interviews

A pretested, interviewer-administered questionnaire was used to collect data, based on a comprehensive review of relevant literature [44–47]. It covered socio-demographic and economic factors, dietary intake and diversity [44, 45], household food security [47], morbidity-related factors, anthropometric measurements [48], and biological sample collection [49]. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and translated into Sidaamu Afoo by a native speaker with a Master’s in language studies. Back translation was done by a different language expert who is proficient in both languages to verify that the original meaning was preserved and that the tool was reliable for data collection. Survey data were collected using the KoboCollect.

Data collectors and supervisors were recruited through an open application process based on their prior experience, language skills, and familiarity with the local culture. The data collection team included four nutritionists with bachelor’s degrees and four senior laboratory technologists, overseen by two Public Health Nutrition experts.

The collection process involved gathering socio-demographic information, economic indicators, and diet-related information, anthropometric measurements, and biological samples.

Dietary diversity was assessed using a 24-hour recall method based on the Food and Agriculture Organization’s (FAO) recommended sixteen food groups, excluding items used solely as condiments [50]. Mothers/caregivers of preschool-age children were interviewed regarding their children’s food and beverage intake from the previous day [44, 45]. Since food consumption during special events often differs from daily habits, interviews were conducted only on regular days, avoiding holidays and fasting periods.

Knowledge of balanced diets—with emphasis on zinc nutrition—was measured via a nine-item questionnaire spanning four key dimensions: (i) general understanding of balanced diets (2 items), (ii) nutrition’s role in health (3 items), (iii) nutritional recommendations for children (2 items), and (iv) healthy eating practices (2 items). Differential response options were employed to holistically assess awareness and comprehension.

The Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) was used to evaluate household food insecurity by examining nine food insecurity-related events in the past 30 days [51, 52]. Following standard HFIAS scoring protocols, households were classified dichotomously: those reporting no affirmative responses across all nine items were categorized as food secure, whereas households endorsing one or more items were classified as food insecure, reflecting compromised access to food at the household level [53].

Body weight of preschool-aged children was measured using a calibrated SECA digital scale (precision: 100 gram) on a flat surface, with participants wearing light clothing and no footwear. For each subject, two measurements were obtained, and the mean value was calculated and recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg in standardized data collection forms [54–56].

Standing height was measured (in meters) to the nearest 0.1 centimeter using a vertical board with movable headboard. Participants stood barefoot with buttocks, shoulders, and occiput contacting the vertical surface. The headboard was gently lowered to compress hair and contact the vertex. Two measurements were taken, with the mean height recorded for analysis [54–56].

Biological sample

Blood sample collection and laboratory analysis followed established protocols [43, 57, 58]. Using standard phlebotomy techniques, a senior laboratory technologist collected 5 milliliter of venous blood from each preschool-aged child under aseptic conditions. The samples were taken from the median cubital vein in the left antecubital fossa. Venous blood samples were collected for serum zinc analysis and immediate hemoglobin concentration measurement. Following aseptic phlebotomy, samples were allowed to clot for 30 min at room temperature before centrifugation at 3000 revolutions per minute.

for 10 min in balanced tubes. Serum was carefully aspirated using sterile pipettes, transferred to labeled containers, sealed,, and temporarily stored at 2–8 °C at the nearest health facilities. To ensure sample integrity, serum separation was completed within 30 min post-centrifugation to minimize hemolysis.

All samples were transported in cold boxes to Yirgalem Hospital Medical College, where they were stored at -36 °C for a maximum of two weeks. For long-term preservation, serum samples were shipped on dry ice to the Ethiopia Public Health Institute’s nutrition laboratory and stored at -80 °C until analysis. Throughout processing and transport, whole blood and serum samples were protected from light and high temperatures to ensure accurate zinc measurements. Laboratory analyses were conducted by trained personnel using standardized protocols. Principal investigators supervised all procedures to ensure adherence to quality control measures. We acknowledge that standard yellow-top serum separator tubes (with clot activator and gel) were used instead of trace-element–free collection materials, potentially introducing exogenous zinc contamination via leaching from the tube, gel, or stopper.

Hemoglobin levels were measured using the HemoCue 301 system, with blood collected in micro cuvettes and values adjusted for altitude according to WHO guidelines [59]. Preschool-age children were classified as anemic if their hemoglobin levels were below 11.0 g/dL, in line with WHO criteria for anemia in this age group [59].

Data quality assurance

To ensure data quality, several measures were implemented, including designing a clear and simple data collection tool that underwent pretesting and customization. Data were gathered by well-trained collectors after three days of intensive training, under strict supervision. Supervisors and the principal investigator reviewed completed questionnaires daily and held regular evening feedback sessions. Children’s anthropometric measurements were taken with calibrated scales. Data was collected electronically using KoboCollect to improve data integrity. The tool enabled real-time validation through range checks and skip logic, reducing data entry errors. It automatically flagged missing or outlier responses for immediate correction and supported standardized exports (CSV/Excel) to streamline consistency checks and statistical validation.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected using KoboCollect, and subsequently exported and analyzed using STATA version 17. The data were summarized using frequency distributions, along with measures of central tendency and dispersion.

Principal Component Analysis was used to construct the household wealth index based on five asset domains: (a) durable household assets, (b) housing characteristics, (c) sanitation and water access, (d) agricultural and productive assets, and (e) additional wealth indicators (e.g., bank account ownership or membership in microfinance/savings groups, cooking fuel type, and kitchen facilities). All variables were dichotomized (1 = ownership; 0 = non-ownership) prior to analysis. Variables demonstrating substantial commonalities (> 0.5) were included in the final model [60–64]. Wealth tertiles (low, middle, high) were generated using the first principal component which accounting for 20.55% of the total variance.

To assess dietary diversity, the standard 16 food groups were aggregated into seven nutritionally cohesive categories based on FAO guidelines [65]. The Dietary Diversity Score, calculated as the count of unique food groups reported in a 24-hour period (frequency-independent), was dichotomized using WHO thresholds [45], distinguishing children who met (≥ 4 groups) or failed to meet (< 4 groups) minimum dietary diversity requirements.

Nutrition knowledge assessment responses, obtained from mothers of preschool-age children, were dichotomized for analysis (correct = 1; incorrect/missing = 0). A composite knowledge score (range: 0–9) was derived by summing correct responses, with higher scores reflecting greater knowledge. Participants were classified into two groups: ‘good knowledge’ (score ≥ 7, equivalent to ≥ 75% correct) or ‘poor knowledge’ (score < 7). The distribution of respondents across these categories was analyzed to evaluate population-level understanding of balanced nutrition.

To assess potential clustering effects, we computed the intracluster correlation coefficient for the primary outcome (CIAF) across sampling units. The observed intracluster correlation coefficient was < 0.01, indicating negligible clustering influence [66, 67]. Consequently, multilevel modeling was unnecessary. Given this finding, we utilized modified Poisson regression with robust error variance to examine associations between socioeconomic, demographic, and dietary predictors (independent variables) and the composite index of anthropometric failure (CIAF; 1 = failure, 0 = no failure), with cluster-robust standard errors as a conservative measure to account for any residual design effects. This approach was selected over logistic regression for three methodological advantages: [1] direct estimation of prevalence ratios (PRs) rather than odds ratios, preventing effect size overestimation when outcome prevalence exceeds 10% [68, 69] - particularly pertinent given our observed CIAF prevalence of 29.7%; [2] statistically efficient, unbiased PR estimation without the convergence limitations of log-binomial regression [70]; and [3] generation of epidemiologically meaningful population-attributable risk measures for public health application [71]. Cluster-robust standard errors accounted for sampling design effects.

The analysis followed a staged approach to confounding control and model optimization. Initial bivariable screening (p < 0.25 threshold) identified candidate variables for multivariable modeling [72], a conservative strategy to minimize Type II error in confounder selection [73]. Prior to multivariable modeling, we conducted rigorous multicollinearity assessment using variance inflation factors (VIF), employing a stringent exclusion criterion (VIF > 2.5) [74, 75] that was substantially more conservative than conventional thresholds [76] to ensure predictor independence.

Final model specification employed backward elimination (retention threshold p < 0.05, two-tailed) with AIC-based comparison of non-nested models to optimize fit [77]. Results are presented as adjusted prevalence ratios (APRs) with 95% confidence intervals to facilitate accurate interpretation [68], with comprehensive tabular and graphical displays of effect estimates and model diagnostics.

Anthropometric indices and zinc deficiency

The relationship between z-score values and serum zinc levels was evaluated using correlation analysis. The results indicated a weak positive correlation between WHZ and serum zinc levels (r (Pearson correlation coefficient) = 0.011, p = 0.833), suggesting no statistically significant association. Similarly, a weak negative correlation was observed between HAZ and serum zinc levels (r = -0.065, p = 0.216), as well as between WAZ and serum zinc levels (r = -0.048, p = 0.358).

Comparative analysis of anthropometric failure between zinc-deficient and non-deficient preschool-age children revealed no statistically significant differences. Figure 1 presents the prevalence of anthropometric indices by zinc deficiency status among preschool-age children. Wasting was more prevalent in zinc-deficient children (64.29%, n = 18) than in non-deficient children, though this difference was not statistically significant (χ² test, p = 0.237). Similarly, stunting was observed in 47.62% (n = 40) of zinc-deficient children (χ² test, p = 0.212), while the prevalence of underweight was 45.95% (n = 17) in the zinc-deficient group (χ² test, p = 0.326). The Composite Index of Anthropometric Failure (CIAF) revealed a near-equal prevalence in both groups (49.07%, n = 53 in zinc-deficient children; χ² test, p = 0.264).

Prevalence of anthropometric indices among preschool-age children

The overall prevalence of CIAF among preschool-aged children exhibiting any of the six forms of anthropometric failure was 29.67% (95% CI: 24.96, 34.39). Of these, 20.05% (n = 73) had failure in one anthropometric index, 7.97% (n = 29) in two indices, and 1.65% (n = 6) in all three indices. The prevalence of CIAF was slightly higher among non–zinc-deficient preschool-age children (32.54%; n = 55) compared to zinc-deficient children (27.18%; n = 53); however, this difference was not statistically significant (χ² test, p = 0.264). The study found that the prevalence of wasting, stunting, and underweight was 7.69% (95% CI: 4.94, 10.44), 23.08% (95% CI: 18.73, 27.43), and 10.16% (95% CI: 7.05, 13.28), respectively.

Determinants of CIAF

Bivariable analysis showed no significant association between zinc deficiency and CIAF (Crude PR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.61, 1.15). However, dietary diversity, child age, family size, water source, wealth index, and maternal education become candidate variables for multivariable analysis.

As presented in Table 4, the multivariable modified Poisson regression analysis revealed that zinc deficiency was not significantly associated with CIAF in the adjusted model (APR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.65–1.23). However, increasing child age was significantly associated with lower risk of CIAF, stunting, and underweight. For CIAF, compared to children aged 24–35 months, those aged 36–47 months had a 35% lower risk (APR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.44–0.96), and those aged 48–59 months had a 52% lower risk (APR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.34–0.70). Similar protective associations were observed for stunting and underweight.

Discussion

This study reveals a substantial burden of anthropometric failure among preschool-aged children in rural Sidama, Ethiopia, with a composite index of anthropometric failure (CIAF) prevalence of 29.67%. Wasting affected 7.69%, indicating moderate acute malnutrition, while stunting was more prevalent at 23.08%, reflecting chronic deficits, and underweight reached 10.16%. Multivariable analysis identified younger child age and unimproved household water sources as key risk factors for CIAF, whereas zinc deficiency showed no significant association.

Stunting prevalence substantially exceeded that of wasting and underweight, mirroring global and regional patterns where chronic malnutrition predominates among preschool children [3]. This persistent public health challenge aligns with recent Ethiopian and sub-Saharan African evidence [78, 79]. The distribution of deficits—20.1% across one index, 8% across two, and 1.6% across all three—highlights malnutrition’s multifaceted nature, consistent with sub-Saharan findings linking multiple failures to heightened risks of adverse health and developmental outcomes [33].

Our stunting rate (23.08%) was lower than reports from southwest Ethiopia [80] and the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey [81]. These disparities likely arise from three factors: temporal gains through national nutrition strategies driving stunting declines [82, 83]; geographic differences, including Sidama’s maize-based diets and seasonal food insecurity [31, 84–86]; and methodological influences like survey timing during lean seasons, which may inflate estimates [87–90].

Zinc deficiency impairs linear growth by reducing IGF-1 synthesis and disrupting growth hormone signaling [91, 92], while also compromising innate and adaptive immunity—particularly T-cell and neutrophil function—thereby increasing susceptibility to recurrent infections such as diarrhea and pneumonia, which in turn exacerbate undernutrition [93, 94]. Despite this established biological role, zinc deficiency showed no significant association with CIAF in this study, suggesting it is not a primary driver of growth impairment in this specific population and setting.

Zinc deficiency impairs linear growth by reducing IGF-1 synthesis and disrupting growth hormone signaling [32, 91, 92], while also compromising innate and adaptive immunity—particularly T-cell and neutrophil function—thereby increasing susceptibility to recurrent infections such as diarrhea and pneumonia, which in turn exacerbate undernutrition [7, 10, 93–95]. Despite this established biological role, zinc deficiency showed no significant association with CIAF in this study, suggesting it is not a primary driver of growth impairment in this specific population and setting. Biologically, this null finding may stem from three factors: first, inflammation from common infections lowers serum zinc independently of true deficiency, and we lacked inflammatory markers (CRP/AGP), potentially biasing estimates toward the null; second, dietary phytates—abundant in the region’s maize-based diets—bind zinc into insoluble complexes, limiting its bioavailability [15, 29]; and third, unmeasured deficiencies in iron, vitamin A, folate, vitamin B12 or copper may have masked or superseded zinc’s effect on growth [7, 16]. Contextually, severe chronic protein-energy malnutrition, recurrent infections, poor sanitation, and limited healthcare access likely overwhelm any independent effect of a single micronutrient. The body prioritizes immune defense over growth, while low dietary diversity—with scarce animal-source foods—means multiple nutritional deficits co-exist, making it difficult to isolate zinc’s specific role. We acknowledge potential residual confounding from unmeasured variables (inflammation, parasitic infections, other micronutrient deficiencies). Overall, protein-energy deficits, infections, socioeconomic barriers, and food insecurity [79, 96], appear to dominate, where dietary diversity alone is inadequate [79].

Younger children (24–35 months reference) faced elevated CIAF risk compared to those aged 36–47 months (APR = 0.65) or 48–59 months (APR = 0.48). This pattern matches Ethiopian and low-income settings, where early vulnerability arises from high growth demands, infection susceptibility, and suboptimal feeding [97, 98].

Children from households using unimproved water sources had 48% higher CIAF prevalence (APR = 1.46) than those with improved sources. Contaminated unimproved sources (e.g., unprotected wells) foster diarrhea via fecal pathogens, impairing absorption, appetite, and energy balance [99, 100] —major malnutrition drivers in Ethiopia [33, 101, 102]. This supports evidence from water, sanitation, and hygiene interventions reducing stunting and underweight [80, 101, 102], including systematic reviews linking improved water to lower risks in low-income countries [103].

Family size, dietary diversity score, household wealth, and maternal education lacked significant associations with CIAF, despite prior links elsewhere [7, 15, 80]. Such null results may reflect context-specific dynamics overriding general patterns [79].

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish temporal or causal relationships between the identified predictors and nutritional outcomes. Second, data collection occurred during a single season, which may have influenced the observed prevalence of malnutrition due to potential seasonal fluctuations in food availability, disease burden, and environmental conditions. Third, the interpretation of serum zinc as a biomarker is complicated by homeostatic regulation and the acute-phase response. We did not measure inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) or alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (AGP), which may have led to misclassification of zinc deficiency status. Inflammation lowers serum zinc concentrations independently of true nutritional status, potentially biasing our estimates toward the null. This could partly explain the lack of a significant association between zinc deficiency and anthropometric failure in this study. Fourth, other micronutrient deficiencies (e.g., iron, vitamin A) were not measured and could be residual confounders in the association between zinc deficiency and anthropometric failure. Fifth, dietary intake data were collected using a single 24-hour dietary recall, which may not accurately reflect usual dietary patterns and is subject to recall bias. This non-differential misclassification would likely bias associations toward the null, potentially underestimating true relationships between dietary factors and anthropometric failure. Finally, as the study was conducted exclusively in a rural setting within the Sidama Region of Ethiopia, the findings may not be generalizable to urban populations or to other regions of Ethiopia with different socioeconomic, dietary, and cultural characteristics.

Conclusion

The study findings indicate a high burden of composite anthropometric failure—encompassing stunting, wasting, and underweight—among preschool-aged children in rural Sidama Region, highlighting a critical public health concern. Although zinc deficiency was not significantly associated with anthropometric failure in this context, multivariate analysis identified child age and the use of unimproved water sources as significant independent correlates. These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of childhood undernutrition, which is influenced by a complex interplay of demographic, environmental, and dietary factors rather than any single nutrient deficiency. The results suggest the need for targeted, multi-sectoral interventions, including age-specific nutritional support and investments in safe water infrastructure, to effectively combat childhood undernutrition in the region. Future longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal relationships and should incorporate measurement of inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP, AGP), assessment of other micronutrient deficiencies (e.g., iron, vitamin A), and evaluation of dietary zinc bioavailability to further elucidate the role of zinc in child growth.
