Assessment of the Remineralizing Efficacy of Grape Seed Extract vs Sodium Fluoride on Surface and Subsurface Enamel Lesions: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Deepak Sharma, Yogesh Sahu, Reema Srichand, Swapnali Mhatre, Uttam Shetty, Nikhil A. Karhale, Sonu Acharya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Dental caries is a prevalent oral health issue, primarily resulting from demineralization of the enamel. Remineralizing agents such as sodium fluoride (NaF) have been widely used to counteract this process. Recent studies suggest that natural extracts, such as grape seed extract (GSE), may also have significant remineralizing properties. This study aims to compare the efficacy of GSE and NaF on surface and subsurface enamel lesions in an in vitro setting.Materials and Methods:In this in vitro study, 60 extracted human premolars were randomly divided into three groups of 20 each: control (no treatment), NaF treatment, and GSE treatment. Artificial enamel lesions were created on the buccal surfaces of all samples. The NaF group was treated with a 5% sodium fluoride solution, while the GSE group received a 6.5% grape seed extract solution. Treatments were applied daily for 10 days. Surface microhardness (SMH) and cross-sectional microhardness (CSMH) were measured before and after treatment to assess remineralization.Results:The NaF group showed a significant increase in SMH (45%) and CSMH (35%) compared to the control group. The GSE group demonstrated a similar trend with a 40% increase in SMH and a 30% increase in CSMH. Both treatments were significantly more effective than the control (P < 0.05), with no significant difference between the NaF and GSE groups (P > 0.05).Conclusion:Grape seed extract exhibits comparable remineralizing efficacy to sodium fluoride on both surface and subsurface enamel lesions in vitro. These findings suggest that GSE could be a viable natural alternative to fluoride for the prevention and treatment of dental caries.

INTRODUCTION

Dental caries is one of the most common chronic diseases affecting individuals worldwide, characterized by the demineralization of the dental enamel due to acid production by bacterial fermentation of dietary carbohydrates. This demineralization can lead to the formation of surface and subsurface lesions if not properly managed.[1] Traditional preventive measures include the use of fluoride, which has been well-documented for its efficacy in enhancing enamel remineralization and inhibiting caries progression.[2] Sodium fluoride (NaF) is the most commonly used fluoride compound in both clinical and home-care settings due to its proven effectiveness and ease of application.[3]

However, there is growing interest in alternative remineralizing agents, particularly those derived from natural sources. Among these, grape seed extract (GSE) has gained attention due to its high content of proanthocyanidins, which are known for their antioxidant and potential remineralizing properties.[4] Previous studies have demonstrated that proanthocyanidins can enhance the formation of hydroxyapatite, the mineral form of calcium apatite present in enamel, thereby suggesting a possible role in enamel repair.[5]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

Sixty extracted human premolars, free from caries, cracks, and any other defects, were selected for this study. The teeth were thoroughly cleaned using an ultrasonic scaler and stored in a 0.1% thymol solution until use. Each tooth was then sectioned to obtain a flat buccal surface, which was polished with 600-grit silicon carbide paper to create a standardized surface for testing.

Group allocation

The 60 teeth were randomly divided into three groups of 20 each:

Control Group: No treatment applied.NaF Group: Treated with a 5% sodium fluoride solution.GSE Group: Treated with a 6.5% grape seed extract solution.

Creation of artificial enamel lesions

Artificial enamel lesions were induced on the buccal surfaces of all samples by immersing them in a demineralizing solution (50 mM acetic acid, 2.2 mM calcium chloride, 2.2 mM sodium phosphate, pH 4.5) for 48 hours at 37°C. This procedure was standardized to ensure uniform lesion depth and severity across all samples.

Microhardness measurements

The remineralization efficacy of each treatment was assessed using surface microhardness (SMH) and cross-sectional microhardness (CSMH) measurements:

Surface Microhardness (SMH): SMH was measured using a Vickers microhardness tester with a 50 g load applied for 10 seconds. Indentations were made at three different points on each sample, and the average value was calculated.Cross-sectional Microhardness (CSMH): After the final treatment, each sample was sectioned longitudinally through the center of the lesion. CSMH was measured at various depths (10, 20, 30, 50, 70, and 100 μm) from the outer enamel surface using the same Vickers microhardness tester.

Cross-sectional Microhardness (CSMH): After the final treatment, each sample was sectioned longitudinally through the center of the lesion. CSMH was measured at various depths (10, 20, 30, 50, 70, and 100 μm) from the outer enamel surface using the same Vickers microhardness tester.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from SMH and CSMH measurements were analyzed using statistical software. One-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test were used to determine the significance of differences between the groups. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

The increase in SMH observed in the NaF group aligns with existing literature that highlights the potent remineralizing properties of fluoride.[1] Fluoride enhances enamel remineralization by promoting the formation of fluorapatite, a more acid-resistant mineral phase, thereby increasing enamel hardness.[2] Our findings showed a 45% increase in SMH, consistent with previous studies demonstrating significant enamel hardening following fluoride treatment.[3]

Similarly, the GSE group exhibited a substantial increase in SMH (40%), suggesting that GSE is an effective remineralizing agent. This result supports earlier research indicating that proanthocyanidins in GSE can enhance hydroxyapatite formation and promote enamel remineralization.[4] The ability of GSE to improve enamel hardness may be attributed to its high antioxidant content, which could neutralize free radicals and inhibit demineralization processes.[5]

Both NaF and GSE treatments significantly increased CSMH at various depths, indicating effective remineralization of subsurface lesions. Fluoride’s ability to penetrate deeper into enamel and promote remineralization at subsurface levels is well-documented. The observed improvements in CSMH in the NaF group corroborate these findings, demonstrating enhanced mineral deposition within the enamel matrix.

GSE’s comparable performance in increasing CSMH suggests that it also possesses the capacity to remineralize subsurface lesions effectively. Proanthocyanidins in GSE may facilitate remineralization by binding to enamel proteins and promoting mineral deposition within the subsurface layers. This property makes GSE a promising natural alternative to fluoride-based treatments.

The comparable efficacy of GSE and NaF in this study suggests that GSE could be a viable natural alternative for caries prevention and treatment. Given the growing concerns over the potential side effects of fluoride, such as dental fluorosis and toxicity at high doses, GSE presents a safer option with additional health benefits due to its antioxidant properties. Moreover, the use of natural extracts like GSE aligns with the increasing demand for natural and holistic approaches in dental care.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrated that grape seed extract exhibits comparable remineralizing efficacy to sodium fluoride on surface and subsurface enamel lesions in vitro. These findings suggest that GSE could serve as a natural alternative to fluoride for the prevention and treatment of dental caries. Further research is warranted to explore the clinical applications and long-term benefits of GSE in dental care.
