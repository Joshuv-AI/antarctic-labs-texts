Quantifying diagnostic signal decay in dementia: a national study of Medicare hospitalization data
Authors: Federica Spoto, Jiazi Tian, Jonas Hügel, Daniel T. Ortega, Christine S. Ritchie, Deborah Blacker, Francesca Dominici, Chirag J. Patel, Daniel Mork, Hossein Estiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONArtificial intelligence (AI) models in healthcare require accurate diagnostic data. In dementia, diagnostic ambiguity and inconsistent coding may distort data quality.METHODSThis cohort study analyzed 2016 to 2018 Medicare Part A hospitalization claims across 3000+ U.S. counties. Seventeen International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision dementia codes were grouped into five categories. Temporal patterns were modeled using the transitive sequential pattern mining (tSPM+) algorithm; matrix similarity and multivariable regression assessed geographic and demographic variation.RESULTSNon‐specific codes were most common. Alzheimer's and vascular dementia codes showed high regional variability. Frequent transitions from specific to non‐specific codes indicated diagnostic signal decay. Counties with more rural, Medicaid‐eligible, and Black or Hispanic patients had lower alignment with national patterns.DISCUSSIONDementia documentation varies widely and systematically across the United States. Much of this reflects inconsistent diagnostic practices, not true disease differences. Signal decay introduces bias into claims‐based research and AI. Linking claims to validated cohorts may improve data quality and model fairness.HIGHLIGHTS Non‐specific dementia codes dominate Medicare hospitalization data.Temporal analysis shows diagnostic signal decay over time.Geographic variation is linked to rurality and racial demographics.Signal decay poses bias risks for AI and claims‐based research.Model explains 38% of variation in diagnostic pattern similarity.

BACKGROUND

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning models are rapidly reshaping how clinicians identify, monitor, and manage neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 AI models use administrative and electronic health record (EHR) data to define phenotypes, identify at‐risk groups, and predict outcomes. However, the reliability of clinical signals in this data is under‐examined, especially in dementia, where diagnoses are often missing, delayed, ambiguous, or inconsistently recorded. 6 , 7 , 8 We hypothesize that these inconsistencies are manifestations of a broader phenomenon we term diagnostic signal decay.

Unlike biomarker assays or structured imaging reports, diagnosis codes are not direct reflections of pathophysiology. They are artifacts of a sociotechnical process shaped by institutional policies, provider incentives, EHR interfaces, documentation culture, healthcare systems dynamics, 9 access to care, and social context. 7 , 10 , 11 The complex and fragmented coding process leads to semantic drift, where International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes vary in meaning by context. This causes “signal decay,” degrading the accuracy of coded clinical data over time and populations.

Dementia is often underrecognized due to its insidious onset. The syndrome's diverse presentations features and diagnostic uncertainty, especially in later stages. The frequent use of “unspecified dementia” codes highlights both disease complexity and documentation challenges, particularly in resource‐limited settings. 6 , 7 , 12 These practices hide clinical subtypes, delay interventions, and distort surveillance and AI training. This risks compromising data quality and amplifies health disparities through models that fail to generalize across marginalized populations and lack diagnostic specificity. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

Although validated phenotyping algorithms have been developed to improve the consistency and validity of ADRD identification in administrative data, 20 , 21 these tools remain susceptible to upstream variability in diagnostic code usage. Previous work identified racial and geographic disparities in dementia incidence, outcomes, and access to care, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 but few studies have evaluated how regional and population‐level variation in diagnostic coding practices may systematically alter the informational content of datasets used to train predictive models.

In this study, we analyze Medicare inpatient claims from 2016 to 2018 to assess the magnitude and drivers of diagnostic signal decay in dementia‐related ICD‐10 code utilization. We apply a clinically informed classification of 17 ICD‐10 codes, grouped into five diagnostic categories, and use transitive sequential pattern mining (tSPM+) 28 , 29 , 30 to uncover the temporal structure of code usage across over 3000 U.S. counties.

We analyzed geographic and racial differences in dementia diagnoses to correct inconsistencies in real‐world data, ensuring valid health service research and preventing AI models from perpetuating inequities. This is vital for reliable data‐driven dementia care tools. Dementia disproportionately affects marginalized U.S. populations, particularly Black and Hispanic individuals, due to unequal disease burden, limited outpatient care, and social/environmental factors. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 31 Yet superimposed on these inequities is a less visible but equally critical issue: variability in how dementia is diagnosed and recorded in clinical data.

Across U.S. regions, diagnostic coding practices differ markedly, influenced by institutional incentives, provider norms, and healthcare fragmentation. 10 , 32 , 33 , 34 The adoption of ICD‐10 in 2015 enabled greater specificity but introduced discontinuities that hinder comparison across time and space. 12 Practices such as upcoding, diagnostic ambiguity, and regional “DRG creep” further erode the interpretability of claims data. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 Dementia diagnosis is complicated by “diagnostic signal decay” – a degradation of data fidelity from inconsistent translation of clinical reality into administrative codes. Non‐specific codes (e.g., F03) are frequent, particularly in inpatient settings and when specialist evaluations are limited. This occurs in outpatient care, too, as primary care physicians (PCPs) use terms like “memory loss” due to time, diagnostic uncertainty, or stigma. Understanding drivers of diagnostic signal decay in dementia coding can illuminate how inequities in care are encoded into data and how they might be corrected in the age of computational medicine.

METHODS

Study framework and hypotheses

This study was designed to empirically assess “diagnostic signal decay,” defined here as the degradation of informational fidelity in administrative claims due to institutional, demographic, and regional variability in coding practices. We conceptualize this decay as a structured process influenced by health system fragmentation, provider behavior, and differential access to diagnostic precision.

We tested two primary hypotheses: H1 (signal variability hypothesis): Dementia‐related ICD‐10 code utilization exhibits significant variation across racial, ethnic, and geographic strata, reflecting differential signal fidelity in how dementia is recorded.H2 (structural signal decay hypothesis): Geographic deviations from nationally observed diagnostic patterns are systematically associated with county‐level sociodemographic and structural characteristics, indicating that signal decay is not random but shaped by structural factors such as poverty, healthcare access, and racial composition.

Data

We obtained Medicare Part A MedPAR data files from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS), encompassing all inpatient hospitalization claims for Medicare fee‐for‐service (FFS) beneficiaries aged 65 years and older between January 1, 2016 and December 31, 2018. Each record contained demographic information, including age, sex, race, ethnicity, Medicaid dual‐eligibility status, and residential zip code. Additionally, the dataset provided information regarding the original reason for Medicare eligibility – whether due to age, disability insurance benefits, or end‐stage renal disease. Each beneficiary was assigned a unique identifier to facilitate the longitudinal tracking of hospitalization claims across the study period.

Claims data reflect documented diagnoses at the hospital level and may incorporate a patient's prior recorded medical history within that hospital system. However, the data do not provide information on the diagnostic source, such as whether the diagnosis was made by a PCP, hospitalist, or specialist (e.g., neurologist or psychiatrist). Thus, we were unable to determine which clinician issued the dementia diagnosis in each case. Each hospitalization claim included up to 25 billing diagnoses based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10). We identified a set of 17 ICD‐10 codes indicative of dementia, designated as dementia‐qualifying codes. The selected codes were grouped into five diagnostic categories: vascular dementia, non‐specific dementia, Alzheimer's disease, Pick's disease (now more commonly called behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia [bvFTD]), and other neurocognitive disorders (Table S1).

We included in our analysis all beneficiaries who experienced at least one hospitalization with a dementia‐qualifying ICD‐10 code during the study period and who resided in the same zip code continuously throughout. This criterion was applied to ensure that measures reflecting the Healthcare Utilization Patterns (HUP) of a given geographic area accurately represented the population residing there. We restricted our analytic sample to hospitalizations where the source of admission was not a nursing facility and where at least one dementia‐qualifying code was present in the billing documentation.

Additionally, we considered county‐level data on educational attainment, unemployment rates, and rural population proportions, obtained from the IPUMS National Historical Geographic Information System (NHGIS). 40 Specifically, the percentage of individuals with less than a high school education and the unemployment rate were derived from summary tables based on the 2016–2020 American Community Survey (ACS) 5‐Year Estimates. The proportion of rural residents was based on data from the 2020 Decennial Census, using the U.S. Census Bureau's 2020 definition of rural, which classifies all populations located outside of urban areas (defined as areas with at least 5000 residents or 2000 housing units) as rural.

RESULTS

Our final analytic sample included 1,827,468 Medicare FFS beneficiaries aged ≥65 years with at least one hospitalization for which a dementia‐related ICD‐10 diagnosis was coded between 2016 and 2018, totaling 3,045,819 hospitalizations. The mean age at first qualifying hospitalization was 84 years (SD = 7.8), and 60.4% of patients were female. Most individuals were non‐Hispanic White (79.8%), with 32.7% eligible for Medicaid at the time of hospitalization (Table 1).

Prevalence of diagnostic categories: frequency as signal density

Non‐specific dementia codes were the most frequently used, with ICD‐10 code F03.90 (“Unspecified dementia and unspecified severity”) representing the single most common diagnosis nationally (Figure 1). Alzheimer's disease codes (e.g., G30.9) and vascular dementia codes (e.g., F01.50) exhibited more limited but variable usage. The predominance of non‐specific codes reflects reduced diagnostic resolution, an early indicator of signal density loss in administrative data. This pattern persisted across all US states, with non‐specific codes appearing in over 75% of dementia hospitalizations nationally (Figure 2). As described in the methods section, to minimize capturing dismissible variability in ICD‐10 codes, we grouped the 17 codes into five categories (Table S1). Analyzing variations in the usage of diagnosis codes (Figure 2) showed a consistently high utilization of the non‐specific dementia group of dementia ICD‐10 codes (including, F02.80, F02.81, F03.90, F03.91, G31.09, G31.1, G31.89, G31.9). The highest variable utilization was in the Alzheimer's disease group (including, G30.0, G30.1, G30.8, G30.9) ranging between 42.1% and 59.3%, with the national average being 51.9%, followed by vascular dementia codes (including, F01.50, F01.51) ranging between 6.41% and 15.8%, with the national average 10.8%.

Temporal signal architecture: code transition patterns

Application of the tSPM+ algorithm revealed that temporal correlations were strongest within diagnostic categories, particularly for non‐specific dementia (Figure 3). These self‐associations suggest persistent documentation of vague diagnoses over time – for example, patients initially coded with non‐specific dementia were likely to receive similar codes in subsequent hospitalizations. This pattern may, in part, reflect the design of EHR systems such as Epic, which are configured to facilitate the carry‐forward of prior codes, making it the path of least resistance for both clinicians and medical coders.

Transitions from specific to non‐specific codes, especially from Alzheimer's disease to non‐specific dementia, were consistently observed, further supporting the concept of temporal signal decay, where the granularity of diagnosis erodes longitudinally. Stratified analyses by race/ethnicity revealed minimal deviation in overall temporal structure, with RS similarity values of 0.98 to 0.99 across groups, indicating high alignment with national‐level signal architecture.

Geographic signal divergence

Despite overall consistency in racial subgroup patterns, we observed substantial geographic variation in diagnostic code utilization structures. Using the RS method, we computed matrix similarity scores between each state's or county's temporal correlation structure and the national pattern (Figure S2). The highest alignment was found in Florida (RS = 1.00), Alabama (RS = 0.99), and Mississippi (RS = 0.99). The greatest divergence occurred in Montana (RS = 0.77), Utah (RS = 0.84), and South Dakota (RS = 0.86). County‐level analysis revealed even broader variation, particularly in rural and northwestern regions, suggesting localized documentation practices contribute to geospatial signal decay.

Structural drivers of signal decay

To quantify how structural and sociodemographic factors influence diagnostic signal divergence, we regressed county‐level RS scores on local characteristics (Table 2). We found that lower similarity to national coding patterns was associated with (a) higher rural population share (β = −0.19, p < 0.05), (b) greater Medicaid eligibility among dementia patients (β = −0.07), and (c) higher representation of Hispanic (β = −0.16) and Black patients (β = −0.13). In contrast, counties with higher proportions of dementia patients and those with more patients living with disabilities showed higher fidelity to national diagnostic patterns (β = 1.27 and β = 0.32, respectively). The model explained 38% of the variance in diagnostic signal similarity (R2 = 0.38), indicating that structural signal decay is partially (but not fully) explained by observable socioeconomic context.

Sensitivity analyses

Our results remained robust under alternative similarity metrics (mean absolute difference) and when repeating analyses using individual ICD‐10 codes rather than grouped categories (Table S2). These findings confirm that the observed signal decay patterns are not artifacts of coding aggregation or measurement method. Moreover, we repeated the regression analysis, including the county‐level rate of Medicare beneficiaries enrolled in Medicare Advantage (MA) (Table S3). The inclusion of MA penetrance did not materially change the identified associations, likely because related contextual factors – such as unemployment rates, Medicaid eligibility, and racial/ethnic composition – already capture overlapping dimensions of healthcare access and socioeconomic context.

DISCUSSION

The burden of dementia in the United States is deeply divergent, stratified by race, income, geography, and access to care. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 31 , 43 , 44 , 45 Yet beyond disparities in incidence and outcomes, this study reveals a more insidious layer of inequity: variability in how dementia is recorded in clinical data. We demonstrate that the informational quality of diagnosis codes – what we call diagnostic signal fidelity – is systematically shaped by structural and institutional factors. In regions with fewer resources, more racially minoritized populations, or limited access to specialty care, the fidelity of dementia documentation degrades. This decay is not random; it follows lines of structural disadvantage.

Our analysis of over 3000 counties, using tSPM+ and cross‐matrix similarity, found “diagnostic signal decay” in rural and underresourced areas, indicating local factors influence dementia documentation. Counties with more Black/Hispanic patients, higher Medicaid eligibility, or greater rurality showed significantly lower alignment with national diagnostic patterns, even after covariate adjustment.

Urban counties mirrored national dementia coding, possibly due to integrated care. Rural areas, lacking local services, may show fragmented documentation as residents seek care elsewhere. Urban areas, despite more providers, benefit from stronger data sharing. Future research with outpatient data and care‐seeking patterns could clarify how healthcare infrastructure impacts diagnostic coding.

We observed a trend where specific diagnostic codes, like Alzheimer's, transitioned to less specific ones, such as unspecified dementia, over time. This “temporal signal decay” is contrary to the expected refinement of general diagnoses into specific subtypes. Dementia is often diagnosed reactively during acute hospitalizations, rather than proactively. Factors like documentation fatigue or EHR workflows may contribute to this shift, leading to reduced diagnostic clarity in long‐term data, impacting clinical management and administrative data use.

These findings carry significant implications for health data science. In predictive modeling, diagnosis codes are often treated as static, reliable targets. But our results demonstrate that these codes may mean different things in different contexts; an Alzheimer's diagnosis in Florida may reflect a well‐resourced, specialist‐evaluated case, while the same code in rural Montana may reflect diagnostic uncertainty or reimbursement‐driven documentation. Such semantic instability can introduce a foundational vulnerability into AI models or any computational algorithm trained on claims or EHR data.

This decay is structured, not stochastic. It aligns with systemic inequities in healthcare access and infrastructure. Signal decay can limit the generalizability of AI predictive models, particularly in conditions like ADRD where early diagnosis is crucial for effective treatment and support. AI tools developed in high‐resource settings may underperform in diverse populations and regions. By quantifying diagnostic signal fidelity, we move toward more context‐aware and bias‐sensitive modeling frameworks.

Our study also offers a methodological contribution: a replicable pipeline for assessing diagnostic signal fidelity in administrative data. The integration of tSPM+ with matrix similarity scoring enables the measurement of not only what codes are used, but also how they are sequenced and co‐occur over time. This structural view of diagnosis coding may be particularly valuable in other complex conditions, such as long COVID, psychiatric disorders, and rheumatologic disorders, where semantic ambiguity is common and documentation can vary across settings and subpopulations.

The differences in utilization patterns that we observe may in part be influenced by structural factors such as the number of physicians in the area, hospital type, availability of neurologists, and size of the healthcare facility, especially when analyzing variation across rural versus non‐rural areas or between White and non‐White populations. Future studies that have access to these covariates could further investigate the extent to which such structural differences drive the observed coding patterns. When examining the five macro‐categories of ADRD diagnoses, we found that the distribution of code position within the billing record was very similar across categories (Figure S3). This suggests that the ordering of ICD‐10 codes is not associated with whether a dementia diagnosis falls into a particular category.

Note, moreover, that, although claims‐based algorithms, such as those developed by CMS and implemented in the Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse (CCW), improve identification of dementia and ADRD cases by leveraging information across multiple claims, they remain limited in capturing the temporal dynamics of disease progression. Static diagnosis codes do not necessarily indicate when dementia is first recognized, how symptoms evolve, or when disease milestones are reached. Recent efforts by CMS to revisit CCW algorithms by considering more comprehensive measures that could approximate staging highlight a critical pathway forward. Further refinement of these algorithms, with greater attention to disease progression and temporal context, would enhance the quality of administrative data for research and improve our ability to study longitudinal patterns of dementia care.

We suggest that future work could explore the development of a Signal Fidelity Index (SFI), a composite measure that captures the clarity, consistency, and specificity of diagnostic code usage across healthcare settings. Such an index could provide a systematic way to assess the informational quality of clinical data and its susceptibility to signal decay. A SFI could serve as a contextual modifier in predictive modeling, a calibration tool for cross‐site algorithm deployment, or a metric for auditing fairness across populations. Just as imaging studies report signal‐to‐noise ratios to quantify interpretive reliability, clinical AI may benefit from parallel measures that reflect the fidelity of its input data. While we do not propose a definitive formulation here, the concept offers a promising direction for quantifying and mitigating structural artifacts in real‐world health data.

Several limitations should be noted. First and foremost, our analysis cannot definitively separate diagnostic divergence from true epidemiological heterogeneity. The observed variation may partly reflect genuine differences in dementia subtype prevalence, differential disease staging at hospitalization, or population‐level differences in care‐seeking behavior. While our temporal analysis and structural correlates suggest substantial documentation artifacts, we cannot quantify the precise contribution of each factor using claims data alone. Second, our analysis is based on Medicare Part A claims, capturing inpatient hospitalizations but excluding outpatient, long‐term care, and community‐based services settings where dementia is often diagnosed or managed, which constrains the generalizability of our findings to the broader dementia population. Hospitalized patients differ systematically from those seen in outpatient settings: They are typically older, have more comorbid conditions, and are often at later stages of dementia when behavioral symptoms become more pronounced. Hospitalization itself may unmask previously unrecognized cognitive impairment due to the stress of acute illness or increased clinical attention, or it may precipitate stepwise decline or delirium – conditions that can be misclassified as dementia. Moreover, diagnostic codes in hospital settings are primarily assigned by professional coders rather than treating providers, which may further influence coding patterns and specificity. These factors should be considered when interpreting our findings and their relevance to outpatient or community‐based populations.

Third, the use of county‐level covariates introduces the possibility of ecological fallacy. While area‐level indicators are essential for identifying contextual effects, they may obscure patient‐level nuances. Fourth, while tSPM+ offers a robust tool for quantifying temporal code structure, it cannot distinguish between clinically warranted and administratively motivated transitions. Thus, some observed decay may reflect real clinical uncertainty rather than documentation failure. An additional limitation of this study is that claims data do not contain the granularity necessary to identify which clinician made the dementia diagnosis. The diagnostic source (e.g., PCP vs specialist) may vary across settings, and in structures with limited physician availability or lack of a dementia specialist, diagnostic and coding patterns may differ. This limitation should be considered when interpreting variation in dementia classification.

Finally, although our model accounted for 38% of the variance in local‐to‐national diagnostic pattern similarity, a substantial proportion remains unexplained. This residual variability may reflect unmeasured institutional influences, including EHR system platforms and their specific configurations (such as problem‐list carry‐forward features and coding interface design), local billing conventions, coder training protocols, or inherent limitations of the modeling framework. Without accounting for signal decay, computational models risk amplifying coding artifacts as if they were true clinical signals. Future work should incorporate health system‐level metadata (including EHR vendor and version information) and chart‐reviewed clinical validation to untangle heterogeneity in coding practices from real differences in disease prevalence and care‐seeking behaviors, such as delayed presentation arising from cultural, structural, or access‐related factors.

Diagnostic codes are more than labels; they are the linguistic infrastructure of data‐driven medicine. When that infrastructure degrades, through omission, vagueness, or fragmentation, it alters what we can know, predict, and act upon. This study shows that in the case of dementia, degradation in diagnostic coding is neither uniform nor inconsequential, though it often reflects documentation priorities tailored to patient care and billing rather than the nuanced needs of secondary data use. In both inpatient and outpatient settings, essential clinical details may reside in narrative notes rather than structured codes, underscoring the limits of relying solely on administrative data for population‐level inferences. It is structurally patterned and consequential. To ensure that AI in healthcare is not only accurate but equitable, we must build tools that see not just the data, but also the decay.
