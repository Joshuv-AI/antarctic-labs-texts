Why take the eye? A systematic review of corneal retrieval methods and donor consent: implications for the UK
Authors: Anya Edwards, Jack Ao, John D Bridson, Stephen B Kaye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo evaluate the effect of the corneal retrieval method on three outcome domains: donor consent rate, tissue quality and microbial contamination.MethodsA systematic literature review was conducted using the Scopus, PubMed and Web of Science databases employing predefined search terms pertinent to corneal retrieval methods, consent rates, tissue quality and microbial contamination. Studies relevant to corneal donation and consent rates as well as reports comparing in situ corneal excision with whole-globe enucleation that evaluated at least one of the three primary outcome domains were included. This study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.Results12 out of 91 studies met the inclusion criteria. In situ corneal excision is associated with higher rates of consent from the family members of the deceased. Corneas harvested via in situ excision were of comparable tissue quality to those obtained from enucleation. Additionally, contamination rates were similar.ConclusionsIn situ excision of corneal tissue may provide a potential advantage in donor acceptance, while offering comparable tissue quality and microbial safety.Keywords: Cornea, Eye (Globe), Eye (Tissue) Banking

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

The UK faces an annual deficit of approximately 1500 donor corneas due to low consent rates. While enucleation remains standard in the UK, in situ corneal excision is an alternative method with the potential to improve consent rates, but good quality evidence comparing the impact of these methods on consent rates and tissue quality is lacking.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This systematic review shows that in situ excision may increase consent rates by addressing concerns about disfigurement and beliefs held by some people about the psychological or emotional significance of the eye, while yielding corneas with comparable quality and safety to enucleation. It identifies a gap in the UK-specific data on how attitudes towards corneal donation are influenced by the method of retrieval.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings support the consideration of in situ excision as a safe and, to some potential donors, more acceptable alternative to enucleation in UK practice. For policy in the UK National Health Service (NHS), these findings highlight the need to clarify references to retrieval procedures in the NHS Blood and Transplant (NHSBT) service’s public information. For many, that information may be the determining factor in decisions that people make about tissue donation, which they are encouraged to share with family and friends and to record on the NHS Organ Donor Register. Future research should prioritise UK studies on how retrieval method affects consent rates and, in turn, may address tissue shortages.

Introduction

Corneal transplantation is a sight-restoring surgical procedure in which a damaged or diseased cornea is replaced with a donor cornea. In the UK, the National Health Service Blood and Transplant (NHSBT) has the primary responsibility for supporting corneal donation for transplantation.1 Compared with the USA and European countries, there has been a persistent shortage of corneas for transplantation in the UK. The primary reason for this deficit is the very low consent rates for ‘eye’ donation in the UK.1 2 For example, the number of corneas retrieved and transplanted per population in the UK is far fewer than in the USA and in other European countries, such as Germany and Italy, and as a consequence, corneal tissue needs to be imported into the UK to try and compensate for this deficit.3 Notably, the UK model contrasts with the USA and many European countries, where only the cornea is removed from the decedent, leaving the rest of the eye intact.4

The Organ Donation (Deemed Consent) Act 2019 introduced an opt-out system, whereby unless there was evidence that the deceased had chosen not to donate, their consent for organ and tissue donation (including corneas) is presumed. This legislative change was implemented to enhance donor organ availability. Despite this Act, however, a significant annual deficit of more than 1500 donor corneas per year remains.1

Among the range of transplantable tissues, the Organ Donor Register (ODR) form offers the public the option to donate the ‘cornea’. Neither the ODR form,5 nor the NHSBT public information website6 clarifies that corneal donation in the UK involves retrieving the whole eye and not just the corneal tissue from the decedent.

This raises ethical questions. For example, the ODR form and the NHSBT website may be the only information on which some people base their decision to give consent to donate corneal tissue on the ODR. The prior consent of a decedent in the UK to donate based on a belief that only corneal tissue is excised, without knowing that it involves enucleation, would not be adequately informed.

Excision of only the cornea rather than the ‘whole eye' was first described in 1975, with the hope that it would make donations more psychologically acceptable, thereby improving consent rates.7 Over time, the in situ method has been refined and adapted and has been shown to be a viable alternative to ‘whole eye’ removal. For example, in the USA, more than 95% of corneal donations are cornea-only removal.8 Despite the apparent success of cornea-only retrieval, and its widespread adoption in many countries, it has not been adopted in the UK.

This systematic review aims to evaluate the evidence on donation rates, tissue quality and contamination rates between in situ donor cornea excision and whole eye removal, and to assess whether transitioning from ‘whole eye’ enucleation to in situ corneal excision might enhance donation rates in the UK and help to address the national corneal tissue shortage.

Methods

Two search strategies were applied to identify relevant studies; first relating to corneal donation and consent rates, and then second, relating to corneal retrieval methods. Searches were conducted using the following electronic databases: Scopus, PubMed and Web of Science. The first search was completed using the following terms “Cornea donation”, “Cornea transplant”, “Keratoplasty”, “Eye donation”, “Corneal transplantation”, “Cornea harvesting”, “Cornea retrieval”, “Eye tissue donation” and “Consent rate”, “Acceptance rate”, “Approval rate”, “Refusal”, “Denial”, “Consenting”. A second search was conducted including the following terms: “Whole eye”, “Whole globe”, “Eye enucleation” and “In situ cornea excision”, “Cornea only”, “Corneoscleral disc excision”.

There were no inclusion or exclusion criteria based on study date or design. Abstracts were subsequently screened by a single reviewer (AE) for their relevance and included if they examined the impact of retrieval methods on consent rates, reported reasons for non-consent, attitudes towards corneal donation or provided a comparison between in situ excision and whole-globe enucleation. Abstracts were excluded if they focused on the method of obtaining consent (eg, communication method or professional requesting consent), the influence of demographics on consent, or did not offer a direct comparison between the methodologies.

On identifying pertinent studies, the full-text articles were reviewed by two reviewers (AE and JA) and data were extracted and analysed. The studies were categorised into two areas: consent rates and attitudes towards donation, and retrieval method comparisons. This grouping enabled a comparison of findings and themes. Given the heterogeneous nature of the studies, which included variations in study design, technician skill and measurement time points, a narrative synthesis approach was employed to describe the findings.

Results

After conducting the database search, 97 abstracts were identified (figure 1). After screening the abstracts and reviewing the studies for eligibility, 12 studies were deemed suitable and were included in this review. Five of these studies focused on evaluating the impact of corneal retrieval methods on donation consent rates or examined participants' attitudes towards corneal donation, including the reasons for non-consent or family refusal. The remaining seven studies provided comparative data on tissue quality or contamination rates.

Consent rates

Two studies reported an increase in the consent rate associated with in situ corneal excision. A further three explored participants’ attitudes towards corneal donation and reasons for non-consent or family refusal.

Hudde et al9 conducted a retrospective observational study at the Lions Eye Bank of Düsseldorf during the transition from whole-eye enucleation to corneoscleral excision. The study reported an increase in the consent rate to 34%, with an overall collection rate of 25% over 2 years following the transition to corneal scleral excision. They report the reasons for this high consent rate as a switch in retrieval method, making donation more psychologically acceptable, as well as their education work within the hospital.

In a similar approach, Filev et al10 carried out a retrospective analysis at the Hamburg Eye bank from 2003 to 2013, as it changed from whole-eye removal to in-situ excision in 2008 (with the application of The German Tissue Act). Consent rates were measured before and after this change, as well as measures of tissue quality (eg,corneal endothelial cell counts), clinical outcomes (eg, visual acuity) and contamination rates. Corneal tissue was obtained from donor bodies at The University Hospital Hamburg, and consent for corneal transplantation via phone calls with the relatives of the deceased. From 2003 to 2007, consent rates for donation decreased consistently from 16.5% to 5.3%, providing 437 and 180 eyes in each year respectively. Following the introduction of corneoscleral disc excision, donor numbers increased with a peak acceptance rate of 8% in 2010, providing 302 corneas.10

Several studies have assessed participants’ attitudes and concerns regarding eye and corneal donation. Lawlor et al11 conducted a cross-sectional study in 2008 and evaluated attitudes towards corneal donation in 371 Australian adults. This was achieved through a survey of 18 questions related to willingness to donate, knowledge of the procedure involved and sociodemographics. 41% of the participants expressed unwillingness to donate, with 18% attributing their reluctance to fear mutilation or disfigurement.

Subsequently, Lawlor and Kerridge12 used a qualitative approach to gain a deeper understanding of the perspective of family members of the deceased who made donor decisions through interviews with individuals’ willingness to donate and conducted interviews with 21 participants who had made a decision for a deceased relative. Grounded theory was used to analyse participants’ responses and identify key themes and factors influencing their consent decisions. Analysis revealed three main areas of concern relating to ‘disfigurement, identity and beauty’.12 Some participants expressed discomfort with an empty space being left behind following whole-eye removal. Others described the emotional connection to the eyes, due to their role in communication and attraction. The unique and personal nature of eyes was also mentioned, and their association with the identity of the individual who possesses them. These concerns appear to be associated with the eyes, in their whole form, rather than the cornea. Bracher et al conducted similar research in the UK, performing a secondary analysis of national data from the NHS Blood and Transplant Services (NHSBT) from 2014 to 2017. Qualitative data were also collected, with specialist nurses completing a free-text box after discussions with non-donating families, to provide more insight into their reasoning. Responses were organised into disfigurement concerns and discomfort reactions, examples including refusing as ‘(the family) could not bear the thought of (the deceased) without them’. 44% of families expressed concerns about disfigurement as their reason for declining eye donation.2

Tissue quality

Four studies found no difference in endothelial cell count between in situ corneal excision and enucleation.13,16 Despite this, Lane et al13 reported that endothelial striae were significantly more prevalent in the in situ excision group than in the enucleation group. Conversely, Rootman et al14 noted higher epithelial haze and Descemet folds; however, the endothelial cell count remained similar. Filev et al10 initially reported a significantly lower endothelial cell count with in situ excision; however, this normalised over time as tissue retrieval proficiency improved. Kim et al17 observed a slightly higher endothelial count in the enucleation group (2758±41) than in the in situ excision group (2674±388), which was statistically significant. However, there was no difference in primary graft failure or the number of corneas with fewer than 2000 cells/mm2. Jhanji et al16 in a comparative prospective study, also reported no difference in postoperative endothelial count, clarity or visual function at 3 months (table 1).

Microbial contamination risk

Three studies reported similar contamination rates between the two methods.15 16 18 Lane et al13 found that initial cultures postexcision, which tested positive for contamination, were more frequent in the in situ excision group (10/24) than in the enucleated group (4/24). Nonetheless, the number of subsequent repeat cultures remained unchanged after being placed in the 7 day culture medium. Filev et al10 observed an initially higher contamination rate associated with in situ cornea excision; however, this difference decreased over time and became insignificant.

Garweg et al assessed the biological quality and contamination rate of 23 pairs of corneas, half of which were excised in situ and half from an enucleated eye. When assessing contamination, there was no significant difference between the two methods with microbial growth found in the conjunctival swab of 12/23 of the enucleated group, and 14/23 of the in situ cornea group.15 Contamination rates were investigated by Schroeter et al in a prospective 9-year study, from January 2001 to the end of 2009. The organ cultures of 2805 corneas were microbiologically tested, 975 retrieved via enucleation and 1830 excised in situ. No significant difference was found between each group, with a contamination rate of 1.5% in the enucleation group and 2.5% of the in-situ group.18 These findings suggest contamination of the corneas was not dependent on retrieval method (table 2).

Discussion

Our review included only published studies; hence, a possible limitation may be that some relevant information from ‘grey literature’ may have been missed. Although the evidence is somewhat limited, it is suggested that the adoption of in-situ cornea excision, as opposed to whole-globe enucleation, may result in higher consent rates from donors and their families. Retrospective analyses by Hudde et al9 and Filev et al10 suggested an increase in family member consent following the implementation of the in situ technique. Initially, Filev et al observed a decline in donation rates at the Hamburg Eye Bank, which was reversed by the introduction of in situ excision, suggesting that this methodological change played a role in enhancing donation rates.10 Although there was a sharp increase in donor consent rate following the transition, the consent rate decreased several years later, which the authors attributed to the German Organ Donation Scandal in 2012. Hudde et al9 did not report baseline donation rates under enucleation, complicating the assessment of the methodological impact on donation rates. However, their observed consent rate of 34% under cornea-only excision compared favourably with similar studies employing the whole-eye method, as Filev et al reported a consent rate of 16% under enucleation.10 These studies directly evaluated how retrieval methods affect consent rates and have broader implications for corneal donation. They suggested that adopting the in situ method could help address the significant shortage of available tissue. Furthermore, the retrospective nature of these studies introduces potential confounding factors, such as whether the observed changes in consent are due to methodology or educational efforts.

Notably, the NHS ODR does not clearly communicate the details of the procedure and merely offers the option to donate the cornea without explaining the need for ‘whole eye’ removal. In the ‘Helping you to decide’ section of its public information website, NHSBT states that, “You may hear people talk about ‘eye donation’, but eyes are never transplanted whole. Eye donation involves donating your corneas, not your iris or the whole eye.”6 This emphasis on a distinction between donating the cornea and donating the iris or whole eye, and reticence about enucleation, risks giving the lay visitor to the website the impression that only the corneal tissue is excised from the decedent. This raises ethical concerns regarding the validity of consent. Individuals who would not consent to enucleation may record their consent to donate their corneas on the ODR, mistakenly believing that this would involve excision of only the cornea. This recorded consent then becomes an important component of the conversation with the family of the decedent who will be asked within hours of the person’s death to fulfil their wishes about corneal donation as recorded by them on the ODR. The misinformed prior consent of the decedent might then influence the family to consent to enucleation to which the decedent would not have consented. Aside from issues of the validity of consent, NHSBT’s public information about corneal donation is not sufficiently candid. Much of NHSBT’s campaigning focuses on encouraging the public to talk about organ and tissue donation. Steps 1 and 5 of their recommended six steps to starting a conversation about donation are: to ‘get the facts’ from their website19 and to ‘talk about your beliefs’. This implies accuracy and candour on the website, ensuring that consent is not based on a mistaken belief that corneal donation in the UK is performed by excising only the corneal tissue. This is especially important if there are known to be beliefs among the public about the psychological or emotional significance of the eye.

In examining attitudes towards corneal donation and obstacles to consent, distinct themes emerged across the studies. Lawlor and Kerridge12 and Bracher et al2 identified concerns about disfigurement and mutilation as primary reasons for non-consent. Participants from both Australia and the UK expressed discomfort with the removal of their eyes and the idea that their loved ones were without them. This raises the question of whether a less invasive procedure involving only the removal of corneal tissue rather than the entire orbit might be more acceptable. Such an approach would be consistent with a widely accepted principle in medical practice that non-invasive or less invasive interventions should be attempted before more invasive interventions, in the absence of a clinical reason to prioritise a more invasive approach.20 Additionally, the qualitative approach employed by Lawlor and Kerridge and Bracher et al provides insight into the social and cultural significance attached to the eyes, as well as the complex and emotional responses elicited by eye donation.2 12 Participants in both studies viewed eyes as ‘windows to the soul’ and an essential part of their loved one’s identity. It remains uncertain whether the cornea, being only one translucent and relatively impersonal part of the eye, holds the same significance. These findings underscore the potential role of in-situ excision in alleviating the psychological distress and fear of mutilation associated with corneal removal. There is at least a prima facie case for offering a choice to potential donors and their families between excision of only the cornea and enucleation.

Although the quality of the studies varied, Garweg et al15 conducted a more robust study, benefiting from a paired design that limited variability owing to donor differences and enhanced the reliability of the comparison. Filev et al10 initially reported a lower endothelial count and higher contamination rates with the in situ corneal excision method, but these findings were confounded by the technician’s learning curve, as evidenced by diminishing differences over time.

Lane et al13 observed a higher incidence of endothelial striae in the in situ corneal excision group, indicating traumatic stress, despite no observed difference in endothelial count. The authors proposed that because the endothelial count was measured immediately after excision, no loss of endothelial cells had occurred yet. Conducting a repeat endothelial cell count at a later time and using a larger sample size would be beneficial for drawing any definitive conclusions.

The initially higher contamination rate reported by Lane et al13 for the in situ corneal excision group may be the result of different disinfection protocols between the two methods. Rootman et al14 observed similar results between both methods, measuring corneal tissue quality across various parameters (endothelial cell density, endothelial striae, Descemet folds) and finding in-situ corneas to be of comparable if not superior quality. Schroeter et al conducted a 9-year study of contamination rates in 2805 corneas and found no significant difference.18 These findings indicate that there is no higher risk of contamination associated with either method.

The limitations of this review relate to the number and quality of the studies that were included. Also missing is a cost analysis. Despite these limitations, the overall evidence suggests that in-situ corneal retrieval can produce tissue of similar quality to ‘whole eye’ enucleation, and with a similar risk of microbial contamination. In addition, in-situ cornea retrieval leads to higher consent rates from donors and families. This raises the question as to why in the UK, ‘whole eye’ removal continues when harvesting corneas improves consent rates and runs counter to the information provided on the ODR.
