Thoracoscopic resection of the second rib via a periareolar approach: a case report
Authors: Jingyu Zhang, Xi Zhao, Jiantao Zhang, Zhaolei You, Chenxiao Yang, Xiaofeng Yu, Jian Fang
Published: 2026

Background

Resection of high-positioned ribs (first and second ribs) adjacent to the thoracic inlet is technically challenging. Traditional open surgery requires extensive dissection of the scapula, pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, and other tissues to adequately expose the target rib. Thoracoscopic surgery, as an alternative approach, can effectively reduce intraoperative trauma and postoperative complications; however, it still leaves a permanent surgical scar approximately 5 cm in length on the lateral chest wall. In recent years, the periareolar approach has been applied in radical lung cancer resection. This incision is more minimally invasive and concealed compared with conventional incisions, and patients report high satisfaction with the cosmetic outcomes [1–4]. To date, rib tumor resection via the periareolar approach has not been reported. Herein, we describe a case of thoracoscopic resection of a second rib tumor using a Gigli saw through the periareolar approach.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old male patient was admitted with a mass in the right second rib detected during a routine physical examination. The patient reported no obvious discomfort and had no significant past medical history. Physical examination on admission revealed no positive findings. Chest computed tomography (CT) showed osteolysis of the right second rib with surrounding low-density changes. Positron emission tomography (PET)-CT (Fig. 1) revealed osteolysis of the right second rib with expansile changes, thickening of the surrounding soft tissue, and increased fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of approximately 11.7, suggesting a primary bone lesion as the most likely diagnosis; plasmacytoma was considered as a possibility. Preoperative relevant auxiliary examinations showed no abnormalities.

PET-CT images showing osteolysis of the right second rib with expansile changes, thickening of the surrounding soft tissue, and increased FDG uptake

Given the patient’s absence of clinical symptoms and the imaging features suggestive of a primary bone tumor (expansile osteolysis with intense FDG uptake, SUVmax 11.7), a multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting was convened, including thoracic surgeons, radiologists, and oncologists. After thorough discussion, the MDT reached a consensus that proceeding directly to surgical resection without preoperative biopsy was justified for the following reasons: percutaneous needle biopsy of the second rib carries a non-negligible risk of pneumothorax, bleeding, and possible tumor seeding, while potentially yielding insufficient tissue due to the sclerotic and osteolytic nature of the lesion; the tumor was localized and amenable to complete resection with negative margins regardless of histology; and complete surgical excision would serve as both a diagnostic and therapeutic procedure, particularly if the lesion proved benign or low-grade malignant. The patient was fully informed of the risks and benefits of this approach, including the possibility of benign pathology, and provided written informed consent for the planned surgery. The surgery was performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation.

Given the diagnostic uncertainty in the absence of preoperative pathology, the oncologic strategy was based on the imaging features highly suggestive of a primary bone tumor (expansile osteolysis with SUVmax 11.7). The goal was to achieve an R0 resection, defined as complete removal of the tumor with negative margins. The transection points were planned at approximately 5 cm from each end of the tumor, which is consistent with standard oncologic margins for low-grade malignant or locally aggressive benign bone tumors. Intraoperatively, after incising the parietal pleura, the tumor was visually confirmed to be confined to the rib with no gross extraosseous extension. The Gigli saw was used to transect the posterior aspect of the rib at the predetermined points, and the anterior cartilaginous portion was divided with electrocautery. The surgical specimen was sent en bloc for pathologic examination, and intraoperative frozen section was not performed because the planned resection would not be altered by a benign diagnosis and the risks of delayed fixation and potential sampling error were deemed unnecessary. The final pathology confirmed Langerhans cell histiocytosis with negative bilateral margins, validating the oncologic soundness of the planned resection margins.

The surgery was performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. The patient was placed in the left lateral decubitus position. A periareolar incision was used as the main operating port: a skin incision approximately 2 cm in length was made at the lateral margin of the areola–skin junction in the outer lower quadrant of the right areola, occupying approximately one-quarter of the areolar circumference (Fig. 2A). After reaching the subcutaneous fat layer, a fine dissection was performed using electrocautery along the fatty layer between the lateral border of the mammary gland and the subcutaneous tissue to create a subcutaneous tunnel leading to the lateral chest wall. The thoracic cavity was entered via the fourth intercostal space, and a 25-mm wound protector was placed. A 1-cm incision was made at the sixth intercostal space in the midaxillary line as a thoracoscopic port. The surgeon and assistant both stood on the left side of the patient. A 10-mm 30° thoracoscope was placed through the sixth intercostal space observation port, and other instruments were introduced through the periareolar incision. Intraoperative exploration revealed pleural elevation beneath the second rib (Fig. 2B), which was confirmed as the tumor location. The parietal pleura above and below the second rib was incised using an electrocoagulation hook. Transection points were identified approximately 5 cm from each end of the tumor, and the soft tissue was completely dissected from the rib at these points. Given the proximity of the second rib to the thoracic inlet, particular attention was paid to protecting the adjacent neurovascular structures. The subclavian vessels and the brachial plexus course posterolaterally relative to the first rib; however, their relationship to the second rib is variable and depends on patient anatomy. To avoid iatrogenic injury, we first meticulously dissected the parietal pleura and endothoracic fascia off the posterior aspect of the second rib using a combination of blunt dissection and electrocautery under direct thoracoscopic visualization. The Gigli saw was passed posterior to the rib using two needle holders, ensuring that the saw remained in direct contact with the bone and did not entrap any soft tissue. The sawing motion was performed in a controlled, low-amplitude manner directed away from the thoracic inlet structures. No intraoperative complications such as vascular injury or brachial plexus traction occurred. Two needle holders were used to grasp both ends of a Gigli saw, which was passed posterior to the second rib to perform circumferential cutting at the transection points (Fig. 2C), achieving transection of the posterior aspect of the second rib. The anterior aspect of the second rib at the transection site consisted of cartilage and was divided using the electrocoagulation hook. The second rib was completely separated from the chest wall and removed (Fig. 2D). After complete resection of the second rib, a chest wall defect measuring approximately 5 cm in length was created. Because only a single rib was resected and the surrounding intercostal muscles remained intact, no paradoxical chest wall motion was observed intraoperatively. Given the absence of a full-thickness chest wall defect (the parietal pleura was preserved except along the rib bed) and the patient’s young age with good pulmonary reserve, we elected not to use a synthetic mesh or prosthetic material for reconstruction. Instead, the defect was managed by approximating the adjacent intercostal muscles to the periosteal remnants using interrupted 2 − 0 silk sutures, which provided adequate stabilization of the chest wall. The overlying soft tissue and subcutaneous layer were closed in layers. After adequate hemostasis, a chest tube was placed in the sixth intercostal space. The periareolar incision was closed using intradermal cosmetic suturing. The operative time was 110 min, with an estimated intraoperative blood loss of approximately 30 mL. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered postoperatively. The chest tube was removed on postoperative day 3, and the patient was discharged uneventfully.

Based on morphology and immunohistochemistry, the findings were consistent with Langerhans cell histiocytosis, with no involvement of the bilateral resection margins of the rib. Immunohistochemistry revealed the following results in tumor cells: CD1a(+), Langerin(+), cyclinD1(+), CD68(-), CD163(-), CK(-), S100(+), Vim (partial+), and Ki67 (approximately 30%). (Fig. 3)

Discussion

The periareolar incision offers significant cosmetic advantages over conventional thoracoscopic incisions. The areolar margin represents a naturally low-tension area, and the curved incision aligns with the direction of skin tension lines, conforming to Langer’s lines of the skin. Consequently, the periareolar incision results in superior scar outcomes in terms of width, thickness, and pliability compared with conventional incisions, while also allowing the scar to be concealed by areolar pigmentation and skin folds [5]. Studies have also demonstrated that postoperative pain and numbness are significantly reduced with the periareolar approach compared with conventional incisions, and no significant sensory abnormalities occur in the operative area, contributing to high patient satisfaction [4]. Furthermore, a critical step in the periareolar approach is the creation of a subcutaneous tunnel, which is developed within the fatty layer between the lateral border of the mammary gland and the subcutaneous tissue. This technique avoids injury to the glandular breast tissue, although this concern is typically minimal in male patients.

Thoracoscopic rib resection requires the use of smaller surgical instruments, as conventional rib shears and rongeurs are not suitable for this approach. Previous reports have described VATS combined with a high-speed pneumatic drill for rib resection; however, this technique still necessitates a larger incision [6]. The Gigli saw is widely used in orthopedic surgery and has also been reported in a few cases of rib resection [7, 8]. The present case also raises an important issue regarding the necessity of preoperative tissue diagnosis for suspected primary rib tumors. While image‑guided core needle biopsy is generally recommended before initiating oncological treatment, its routine application for small, resectable rib lesions remains debated. In this case, the multidisciplinary team decided to proceed directly to surgical resection without preoperative biopsy based on several considerations: the high procedural risks associated with biopsy of the second rib, which is adjacent to the subclavian vessels, brachial plexus, and pleural cavity; the strong imaging suspicion of a primary bone tumor rather than a metastasis or lymphoma, where complete resection would be the standard treatment; and the patient’s young age and desire to avoid a potentially non‑diagnostic or complicated biopsy procedure. Notably, the final pathology confirmed Langerhans cell histiocytosis, a benign but locally aggressive lesion that was completely cured by R0 resection. Therefore, in selected cases where imaging is highly characteristic and surgical resection is planned as the primary treatment, a direct surgical approach under MDT guidance may be an acceptable alternative to routine preoperative biopsy. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this strategy should be applied cautiously and only after rigorous MDT discussion and informed consent. Its unique flexible nature allows it to be maneuvered through a small incision. In this case, we applied the Gigli saw during surgery via a miniaturized periareolar incision and achieved satisfactory resection outcomes.

For patients, the surgical incision represents more than just a scar—it carries a dual impact of physical and psychological trauma. This is especially true for young patients, who are more concerned about scar appearance, and significant postoperative scarring can severely affect their mental health [9, 10]. In this case, by combining the periareolar approach with thoracoscopic surgery and the Gigli saw, we successfully performed rib tumor resection through a cosmetic incision, effectively improving scar aesthetics, reducing surgical trauma, and accelerating postoperative recovery. As only a single rib was resected, no mesh was required for chest wall reconstruction. The defect site exhibited mild inward retraction of the chest wall fat, which was simply repaired and secured with silk sutures. The patient experienced no postoperative complications such as lung herniation, and follow-up at 4 weeks and 6 months showed satisfactory outcomes (Fig. 4).

Conclusion

Our experience suggests that thoracoscopic rib resection via the periareolar approach is safe and feasible, representing a new option for rib resection.
