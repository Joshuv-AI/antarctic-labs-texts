Dose- and time-dependent relative biological effect of proton in different intracerebral cells
Authors: Xiaowen Wang, Yujiao Guo, Jianguang Zhang, Miaoqing Zhao, Yuanyuan Liu, Xiao Liu, Xingchen Ding, Shengying Zhou, Xiaoyan Yin, Jian Zhu, Yiyuan Zhang, Guanzhong Gong, Lizhen Wang, Ya Su, Tao Sun, Nancy Y Lee, Jinming Yu, Man Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo further explore relative biological effectiveness (RBE) variability, the RBE of different intracerebral cells at various irradiation (IR) dosages and time were determined in this study. A total of 120 rabbits were randomly divided into proton groups (0, 10, 20, 30, 40 Gy, RBE) (n = 3) and photon groups (0, 10, 20, 30, 40 Gy) (n = 3). The rabbits were sacrificed at 2, 4, 6, 8 weeks after brain IR. Neuronal survival, identified via Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, and immunohistochemical detection of neurofilament (NF), Olig2, and CD68 in the hippocampus and thalamus, were analyzed. Dose- and time-dependent RBE curves were fitted using the LQ model. Proton IR showed higher neuronal survival at 4-, 6-, and 8-weeks post 10 Gy, 20 Gy, 30 Gy IR (p < 0.05) compared to photon IR. Oligodendrocyte populations in photon group at 4-, 6-, and 8-weeks post 10 Gy IR and 6-, 8-weeks post 20 Gy were consistently higher than proton subgroups (p < 0.05). While proton IR showed higher microglial activation which was observed only at 4-weeks post 30y IR. Proton RBE for neurons and oligodendrocytes remained below 1.1 but exceeded 1.1 for microglial activation. These findings demonstrate the dose- and time- dependent nature of proton RBE and suggest brain tissue tolerates higher proton IR doses compared to photon IR, which fully confirmed the biological advantages of proton IR. Introduction

Proton therapy is distinguished by its unique dose distribution characteristics, notably the Bragg peak effect, positioning it at the forefront of radiation oncology. This spatial precision allows for high doses to be delivered to the tumor while minimizing exposure to surrounding normal tissues, thereby enhancing the therapeutic ratio in clinical applications1,2. RBE quantifies the efficacy of proton radiation in causing biological effect compared to conventional photon radiation, making it a significant area of research. Clinically, the RBE value of 1.1 is typically employed to guide proton therapy planning. However, numerous studies indicated that the RBE of protons is not constant, especially on normal tissue damage, whose possible misestimation of the RBE of proton should be observed. The employment of preclinical models has documented specific adverse effects associated with proton therapy. In clinical observation, Zhang et al.3reported that the rate of proton induced temporal lobe damage was higher than IMRT (the dose tolerance at D1% : 58.56 Gy vs. 69.07 Gy) in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and suggested that the RBE was 1.18 at D1%, even the end-of-range RBE was likely to be higher. The variety RBE may lead to underestimating or overestimating frequency and severity of toxicity4,5. Therefore, it plays a great role to clarify the real RBE of proton for organs at risk (OAR) and the probability of normal tissue complications (NTCP) in clinical application for accurately determining the dose limitation of OAR and the radiation dose of tumors to reduce radiation damage and improve tumor control.

RBE is related to a variety of factors, such as cell type, fractional dose, LET, etc6–8. , and at the same time, normal cells and tumor cells are also different. In the case of glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs) derived from seven different glioblastoma patients, proton RBE values range from 0.7 to 1.29.For head and neck tumors, the proton RBE values of head and neck tumors have been reported to around 1.110. Clonogenic assay showed that the RBE values of skin fibroblasts between 1.4 and 2.211,12. These findings indicate that RBE varies not only among different tumor cell types but also among different cell lines of the same tumor type. Although the results suggest that RBE manifests at specific sites within the therapeutic region, they fall short of fully representing the scenarios within tissues and the overall tissue response13,14. Beyond in vitro data, the variability of RBE has also been demonstrated in normal tissues and organs. For instance, Tracy et al.5 found that the hypothesis that changes in lung density result in a proton RBE exceeding 1.1. The brain, a complex structure composed of various cell types and tissues, presents limited data regarding the RBE across its different cellular and tissue components. Our previous research indicates that, 24 h post-irradiation, the RBE values for neurons, oligodendrocytes, and microglia within the brain all exceed 1.115.

However, the long-term response of hippocampus, thalamus and other regions after proton or photon irradiation deserves more attention. Lawrence et al. concluded that the five-year risk of radiation-induced brain injury (RIBI) with normal graded RT was ~ 5% at a total dose of 72 Gy16. Among these, delayed brain injury has a more significant impact on patients’ quality of life compared to acute brain injury, which is characterized by hippocamps-related learning and cognitive impairment and brain necrosis, and even eventually leads to uncontrolled brain herniation and death17. Therefore, it is the long-term RBE that truly necessitates thorough investigation in the context of radiation-induced brain injury.

In light of these challenges, the aim of the study was to explore the variable RBE in different brain cell types and the trends of RBE variation with dose and over time, which contribute valuable insights into the intricate interplay between radiation dose, temporal factors, and cell-type-specific responses, thereby enhancing the accuracy and efficacy of proton therapy for brain tumors and other central nervous system conditions.

Materials and methods

Animal models

15 weeks-old male New Zealand White rabbit, weighing 3.5–4 kg were purchased and housed at the Shandong Jinan Jinfeng Experimental Animal Co. Ltd, maintained in a 12 h/12 h light-dark cycle at 22 °C with free access to food and water. The control group (Ctrl) was not exposed to radiation and was sacrificed at 2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks. The proton groups were exposed to proton with doses of 10 Gy (RBE), 20 Gy (RBE), 30 Gy (RBE), and 40 Gy (RBE), respectively, and were sacrificed at the same time points with Ctrl. All experimental animals were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg body weight) at the end of the experiment. Correspondingly, the photon groups were exposed to proton with doses of 10 Gy, 20 Gy, 30 Gy, and 40 Gy, respectively, and were sacrificed at the same time points with Ctrl. Specifically, rabbits in the proton and photon group were labeled as Protondose, time, Photondose, time (e.g., Proton10,2, Photon10,2 mean the animal model were received proton and photon radiation with 10 Gy and were sacrificed post radiation 2 week). Each subgroup consisted of 3 rabbits. A total of 108 rabbits were included in the study. Detailed grouping was presented in.

All experiments related to animals were strictly performed in accordance with guidelines and approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Cancer Hospital (ID: 201911022). The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

CT simulation and irradiation protocol

Each rabbit underwent large aperture Computed Tomography simulation positioning machine (Philips Brilliance CT BigBore) before radiation. Contrast-free scanning was performed using a 64-slice CT scanner with a thickness of 0.625 mm. The rabbits were placed in a prone position on a positioning plate bed, and were irradiated simulated CT was performed in a transposable frame. A CT simulated treatment bed was placed in a rack and the reference isocenter was determined using a CT laser and make a cross mark at the intersection using a black marker pen. Paste opaque spectral marks on laser intersections. CT images were imported using Varian Eclipse treatment planning System (Varian Eclipse v.11.0, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA) and used to contour the target of whole brain for irradiation planning.

Radiotherapy was administered after evaluation and optimization of the plan. When the match is at least 95%, the plan is considered to be able to receive treatment. The evaluation of program quality included dose volume histograms (DVHs) for PTV (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). Each rabbit was positioned using the same CT analog positioning equipment, and the plan was developed and evaluated by two physics lab researchers. Rabbits were irradiated using indoor laser markers aligned with the reference isocentric markers in the radiotherapy library.

Proton and photon IR

The proton group of rabbits were placed on the treatment platform of proton therapy system (PROTEUS235 proton therapy system of IBA Belgium) after marking the median line of cranial parietal bone with a beam. Exposure of the whole‑brain irradiation area, with the median line of cranial parietal bone and the intersection of the line behind both ears as the irradiation location center. The initial beam energy induced by proton accelerator was 230 MeV, and proton beams with energy range of 70–230 MeV were obtained by energy degenerator at a dose rate of 20GE/min. The rabbits in 4 subgroups were subjected to a single whole-brain irradiation of 10 Gy (RBE), 20 Gy (RBE), 30 Gy (RBE), and 40 Gy (RBE), respectively. The proton irradiation dose was calculated based on the commonly used proton RBE (1.1). For example, the actual proton dose corresponding to a proton dose of 10 Gy (RBE) is 9.09 Gy (physical). The depth and width of spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP) were measured by a three-dimensional tank before irradiation, and the SOBP of 3 cm was obtained by a range modulator, so as to ensure uniform irradiation of the whole brain of rabbits.

In the photon group, the whole brain was irradiated with the median line of cranial parietal bone and the intersection of the line behind both ears as the irradiation positioning center, the source skin distance was adjusted to 100 cm, and the head was covered with 1 cm tissue equivalent membrane. A single whole-brain irradiation of 10 Gy, 20 Gy, 30 Gy, and 40 Gy, respectively, was performed on 4 subgroups of rats using a linear accelerator with 6MV photon energy (American Varian Vital Beam) at a dose rate of 1400MU/min.

HE and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

The rabbits were anesthetized and euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of 100 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium. Rabbit brains were harvested and was excised and immersed in 10% formalin for 24 h. Pathological tissue blocks were prepared using the paraffin embedding technique. Coronal serial sections were then produced, with a thickness of 3 μm each. After this, Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) and Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining were performed.

Neuronal survival in brain was identified using HE staining. IHC was used to stain Neurofilament (anti- Neurofilament antibody, ORI gene, dilution: 1:1), Olig2 (anti-Olig2 antibody, Venus gene, dilution: 1:1) and CD68 (anti-CD68 antibody, Venus gene, dilution: 1:1) to detect the remnants of neurofilament, oligodendrocytes and activation of microphages.

Slide scanning and analysis

HE and IHC slides were scanned using Zeiss digital slice scanner and observed by ZEN (BLUE) 2012 software. We individually analyzed the various regions of the hippocampus (CA1, CA2, CA3, CA4, and DG). Two pathologists were asked to (double-blind) analyze and count the neuronal damage in each region of the hippocampus in the same coronal plane of pathology sections in each subdivided area, with six random fields of view selected for each section, then the ratio of abnormal neurons to all the neurons under the field of view was calculated. Average optical density (AOD) was carried out to evaluate the density of neurofilament, oligodendrocytes, activated microglia cells and macrophages in hippocampus and thalamus, using ImageJ software version 1.54 (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

RBE was defined as the ratio of the photon and proton dose when these two kinds of IR produce same biology output. Consider that different cells in the brain have different effects after IR, we determined different biology output of them. Subsequently, the dose-dependent RBE curves of neurons, neurofilament, oligodendrocytes and macrophages were fitted by LQ model (E = αd + βd2, E: biological effect; d: dose)18. The RBE calculations were performed using physical doses, and the detailed calculation process for proton RBE is presented in the Supplementary Material. Specifically, RBE of different subgroups were labeled as RBEdose, time (e.g., RBE10,2, represents the RBE at two weeks after 10 Gy IR).

Statistics

The statistical analyses were performed using a two-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), designed to explore the effects of two independent variables—radiation type (control vs. proton vs. photon) and time post-irradiation (2, 4, 6, and 8 weeks). In instances where significant main effects or interactions were detected, post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test. This method allowed for the assessment of not only the main effects of each independent variable but also their interaction effect on the dependent variables. Results were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05. Data are presented as means ± SEM. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Chicago, Illinois) or GraphPad Prism Software (GraphPad, San Diego, California, USA).

Results

Proton IR damage of neuron in the hippocampus

Compared to the control group, both the proton and photon groups exhibited irregularly arranged hippocampal neurons, loose cytoplasm, cytoplasmic atrophy and deformation, hyperchromatic nuclei, nuclear division, nuclear fragmentation, and nuclear dissolution in HE-stained sections (Fig. 1). The rates of neuronal degeneration in the proton and photon groups rises with increase dose (Fig. 1). At 4, 6 and 8 weeks post-IR (IR4w, IR6w, IR8w), the rates of neuronal degeneration in the proton group were significantly higher than those in the photon group at all doses (proton10-4 vs. photon10-4, proton20-4 vs. photon20-4, proton30-4 vs. photon30-4, proton40-4 vs. photon40-4, proton10-6 vs. photon10-6, proton20-6 vs. photon20-6, proton30-6 vs. photon30-6, proton40-6 vs. photon40-6, proton10-8 vs. photon10-8, proton20-8 vs. photon20-8, proton30-8 vs. photon30-8, proton40-8 vs. photon40-8, all p < 0.05). However, in 2 weeks post-IR (IR2w), there was no significant differences were observed between the proton and photon groups across all doses (proton10-2 vs. photon10-2, proton20-2 vs. photon20-2, proton30-2 vs. photon30-2, proton40-2 vs. photon40-2, all p > 0.05) (Table 1). The neuronal degeneration rates in both the proton and photon groups were also time-dependent, with degeneration rates increasing over time at all doses (Fig. 1). Neurofilament (NF) proteins, one of the primary structural components of neuronal axons, were crucial for the maintenance of axonal structure and function, thus serving as a reliable indicator of neuronal survival (Fig. 2). Through the analysis of the AOD following NF staining, our findings were largely congruent with those obtained via HE staining. Notably, at IR4W, IR6W, and IR8W of 10 Gy, 20 Gy, and 30 Gy irradiation, the proton group exhibited a higher number of surviving neurons and fibers compared to the photon group, with these differences reaching statistical significance (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2).

Oligodendrocyte changes over time Post-Irradiation in hippocampus and thalamus

The quantification of Olig2 in the hippocampus and thalamus, indicative of oligodendrocyte populations, was conducted at various intervals following exposure to both photon and proton irradiation across doses of 10 Gy, 20 Gy, 30 Gy, and 40 Gy, in comparison to control groups (Fig. 3). These differences reached statistical significance in the cases of 10 Gy-IR4w (p < 0.01), 10 Gy-IR6w (p < 0.01), 10 Gy-IR8w (p < 0.05), 20 Gy-IR6w (p < 0.05), 20 Gy-IR8w (p < 0.05) (Table 2; Fig. 3). In analyzing the temporal dynamics of Olig2 expression post-irradiation, it was noted that at a dose of 10 Gy, the number of oligodendrocytes in the proton group gradually increased over time, while at higher doses, a progressive decline in oligodendrocyte counts was observed in both proton and photon groups irrespective of the dose administered (Fig. 3).

Microglial activation following irradiation in hippocampus and thalamus

The activation of CD68 increased with increasing dose. Significant increases in CD68 expression were an exception and were only observed in the proton therapy group at 30 Gy after 4 weeks (proton30-4 vs. photon30-4(61.55%±1.51% vs. 51.46%±0.98%), p < 0.01) when compared to photon therapy ((Fig. 4; Table 2). Regarding the trend over time, at any dose (10 Gy, 20 Gy, 30 Gy, and 40 Gy), both photon and proton therapies showed a trend of decreasing CD68 expression over time (Fig. 4).

Proton RBE changes: evaluation based on neuronal, oligodendrocyte, and microglial responses

Through immunohistochemical staining for NF, Olig2, and CD68, we have obtained data on neuronal survival, oligodendrocyte survival, and microglial activation, respectively, which were subsequently incorporated into the LQ mathematical model to derive the RBE of protons at varying doses and time points. Our findings reveal that, at the level of neurons and oligodendrocytes, the proton RBE remains below 1.1 (Tables 3 and 4). Notably, for neurons, at the IR2w and IR4w intervals, the RBE of protons increases with dose escalation but decreases at IR6w and IR8w with further dose elevation. For oligodendrocytes, the RBE of protons increases with dose escalation. In terms of microglial activation, the RBE of protons shows an upward trend over dose at IR2W, IR4W, IR8W and downward trend at IR6W. Most RBE values exceeding 1.1 except for RBE10-2 (1.013), RBE20-2 (1.046), RBE30-2 (1.089), RBE10-4 (1.079), RBE40-6 (1.011), RBE10-8 (1.098) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study marked a significant advancement in our understanding of the RBE of protons, challenging the conventional threshold of RBE = 1.1. By meticulously examining the dose- and time-dependent RBE in relation to NF, Olig2, and CD68 markers, we have unveiled a groundbreaking revelation: in the later stages of radiation therapy, proton RBE can indeed fall below 1.1. This discovery not only challenged the prevailing belief in protons’ superior biological efficacy over photons but also underscored the intricate variability of proton therapy’s impact on intracerebral cells.

Building on the foundational studies by Lee and Kim, which underscored the potential of NF as a marker for assessing neuronal damage19,20, our study expanded upon their findings by correlating NF expression with the dose and time-dependent variability of RBE. Dokic and colleagues found a significant reduction in γ-H2AX positive nuclei in the brains of healthy C57BL/6 mice at 7 days post-proton beam IR compared to 1 h, possibly indicating DNA damage repair21. Lohberger et al. suggested that the DNA repair mechanisms activated by proton radiation are more effective than those triggered by photons22. We posit that due to the brain’s self-repair mechanisms, non-apoptotic damage gradually mends over time. Kristen et al. proved that proton and photon therapy both caused impairment of the base-excision repair capabilities of the glial cells but the detrimental effects were less pronounced after proton treatment. We speculate that over time, this self-repair advantage of protons becomes increasingly evident, suggesting that, in the long term, protons unexpectedly confer a protective advantage for brain neurons (RBE < 1.1). Olig2 is a key transcription factor in oligodendrocytes, specifically expressed in normal brain oligodendrocytes23. Radiation induces the loss of type 2 astrocyte precursor cells (O2A), leading to the irreplaceable decline of aging oligodendrocytes, which are essential for myelination, ultimately resulting in brain demyelination and white matter necrosis24,25. Our findings revealed that photon irradiation exerts a stronger inhibitory effect on oligodendrocytes than protons, with the reduction in oligodendrocytes further impacting neuronal function. Following brain irradiation, microglia are rapidly activated, undergoing significant morphological changes and producing an abundance of cytokines and chemokines26. Activated microglia play a crucial role in radiation-induced brain injury by triggering acute and chronic inflammatory responses through the release of a multitude of inflammatory factors27–29. The neurotoxic effects mediated by the excessive inflammatory response of activated microglia promote the onset and progression of cognitive disorders30. CD68 is a marker of activated macrophages31, and Dokic et al. found a 1.75-fold increase in CD68 signaling in the brains of mice 7 days post-proton IR compared to control samples (p < 0.05)21. The activation and proliferation of microglia are core pathological changes throughout the pathology of RIBI. Our data indicated that protons are more effective than photons in stimulating the activation and proliferation of brain macrophages. Why protons cause a more intense immune-inflammatory response yet inflict less damage on neurons and oligodendrocytes warrants further exploration. This may suggest that proton therapy combined with immunotherapy could be more effective than photon-based combinations32,33.

From almost immediately after the first radiation therapy more than 100 years ago, mathematical modeling has played an important role in how we understand and optimize radiation delivery for cancer treatment. The universality of LQ dose-response models in this field cannot be overemphasized1,18. Instead of using cell survival data to derive proton RBE, we have for the first time used in vivo experimental data that reflects the brain environment into the LQ model, meaning that we have integrated complex biological processes into the model to more accurately and robustly parameterize what happens in the brain after radiation exposure.

Our study, while pioneering, acknowledges certain limitations. Firstly, the groundbreaking conclusion we have drawn, indicating a relative biological effectiveness (RBE) of less than 1.1 based on neuronal cells and oligodendrocytes, has thus far been corroborated solely in the context of rabbit cerebrums. There is a pressing necessity for an augmented array of clinical specimens to bolster our findings. Secondly, the applicability of our results, specifically confined to the intracerebral milieu and hinging on the utilization of NF, Olig2, and CD68 as biomarkers, may circumscribe the broadness of our conclusions. Consequently, there emerges a manifest imperative for further investigative endeavors aimed at diversifying the spectrum of biomarkers and substantiating our discoveries within a more expansive clinical framework.

Conclusion

By analyzed the proton RBE of neurons, oligodendrocytes and microglia in the brain with time and dose, which challenged the conventional thresholds of RBE and shedding light on the complexities of proton therapy’s impact, this study lays the groundwork for significant advancements in radiation therapy protocols. Through technological innovation and a detailed analysis of cellular responses, we pave new pathways for enhancing the delineation accuracy of organs at risk and personalization of treatment, with the potential to improve patient care in neuro-oncology and related fields.
