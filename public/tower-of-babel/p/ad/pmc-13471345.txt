Children’s near-death experiences: tales from nurses - a content analysis study
Authors: Poria Moradi, Alireza Abdi, Ameneh Amiri Kolehjoubi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNear-death experiences (NDEs) are rare but profound phenomena that have also been reported in children. However, most existing research has focused primarily on adults, and evidence regarding pediatric NDEs remains limited. This study explored pediatric nurses' observations and accounts of children's NDEs using a qualitative approach.MethodsA conventional qualitative content analysis design was employed. Thirteen nurses working in pediatric oncology and intensive care units of a children's referral hospital in Kermanshah, Iran, were recruited through purposive and snowball sampling. In-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted between May and August 2025. Data were analyzed using the Graneheim and Lundman (2004) approach and managed with MAXQDA 2020 software.ResultsThree main categories emerged from the data: soothing perceptions, encounters with individuals, and emotional reactions. These were further organized into six subcategories: enveloped in light, secure companion, unknown observer, invitation to play, seeking security, and anxiety.ConclusionChildren's NDEs, as reported by nurses, are multidimensional phenomena involving feelings of peace, perceived interactions with figures, and emotional responses. These findings highlight the importance of recognizing the psychological and spiritual dimensions of children's end-of-life experiences and supporting nurses in responding to them with empathy and understanding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04865-9.Keywords: Near-death experience, Content analysis, Children, Nurses

Background

Near-death experiences (NDEs) have long attracted the attention of scientists, philosophers, and the general public. The phenomenon was first documented by Heim in 1892 and gained further recognition following a formal definition proposed by Moody in 1975 [1]. NDEs are profound psychological, conscious, semi-conscious, or memory-based experiences that individuals report when approaching death or during the temporary onset of the dying process, typically occurring in life-threatening situations [2]. Studies have shown that NDEs occur in approximately 15% of survivors of severe illnesses admitted to intensive care units [3], 3% of survivors of traumatic brain injury [4], and 4–8% of the general population considering all causes [5]. Reported features of NDEs include inner peace, out-of-body experiences, traveling through a tunnel or void, life review, perceiving a bright light, entering an otherworldly realm, and interactions with sentient beings [6–8], which Ring suggested may unfold in a five-stage sequence from peace and bodily separation through darkness and light to passage into another realm [9]. The subjective and individual nature of these experiences, along with the lack of a clear framework for them, makes their description and interpretation dependent on personal, cultural, or religious factors, and they are almost always described based on the individual’s religious beliefs [10]. Research conducted to date has addressed many questions concerning the phenomenology of NDEs, such as the situations that commonly trigger them, their shared features, and their subsequent effects. Nevertheless, there is still no single theory that can explain all the features that have been classically described [11]. Most studies conducted in this field have focused on near-death experiences in adults, and research examining these experiences in dying children remains very rare [12]. Morse and colleagues provided the first systematic evidence that children’s near-death experiences share core features with those of adults, including out-of-body sensations and perceiving a bright light [13]. Understanding NDEs in children is particularly important because, unlike adults whose accounts may be shaped by years of cultural and religious conditioning, children’s descriptions are thought to be less biased and more ‘raw’ [14]. However, evidence suggests that children’s NDEs may differ from adults’ in notable ways: while core features such as light, tunnels, and out-of-body sensations appear consistent across age groups, children rarely report life reviews or messages from deceased relatives [14]. These differences highlight the need for dedicated pediatric NDE research. Moreover, NDE-like phenomena have been documented across human history, appearing in ancient religious and philosophical texts across diverse civilizations, further underscoring the universality and significance of these experiences [15]. For pediatric nurses, understanding NDEs is important because they are often the primary witnesses to these events. Caring for dying children is one of the most emotionally challenging aspects of nursing, and nurses need training and support to manage their own reactions and to provide spiritually sensitive care to families [16]. Given the scarcity of direct pediatric NDE research and the reliance on adult retrospective accounts, this study aimed to explore children’s transcendent end-of-life experiences through the lens of pediatric nurses, with the intent of informing spiritually sensitive pediatric nursing care.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a conventional qualitative content analysis approach. The qualitative content analysis approach was chosen due to its ability to uncover hidden, complex, and less well-known dimensions of phenomena, enabling patterns, categories, and underlying meanings to emerge directly from participants’ accounts. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [17–20].

Participants

Participants were 13 nurses working in pediatric intensive care and oncology units at a pediatric referral center in Kermanshah, Iran. Inclusion criteria were: (a) experience of caring for at least one child who had died or experienced cardiopulmonary arrest, (b) ability to participate in an interview, and (c) willingness to participate.

Recruitment

Participants were recruited through purposive sampling, followed by snowball sampling. Senior nurses in the oncology and PICU units were approached first, and then asked to identify colleagues with similar experiences. Recruitment continued until no further eligible individuals could be found and thematic saturation was confirmed.

Data collection

Data were collected between May and August 2025 in private rooms within the participating hospitals. Potential participants were provided with detailed information about the study’s aims and procedures, and those who consented were scheduled for interviews. After obtaining informed consent, in-depth semi-structured face-to-face interviews were conducted by the corresponding author, a doctoral nursing student. Interviews began with the collection of demographic data (e.g., gender, years of work experience, educational background) and continued with open-ended questions (for example: “How do you perceive near-death experiences in children?“), transcribed verbatim on the same day, and lasted approximately 35–45 min each (see Supplementary File 1 for the full interview guide).

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed concurrently with data collection using the Graneheim and Lundman (2004) approach to conventional content analysis [21]. Each transcript was read repeatedly to gain an overall understanding. Meaning units were identified, condensed, and coded; codes were subsequently grouped into categories reflecting nurses’ perceptions of children’s near-death experiences. MAXQDA 2020 software was used to manage and organize data. To enhance analytic rigor, two researchers independently coded the interviews. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, and the final coding framework was agreed upon by the research team.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was addressed using Guba and Lincoln’s criteria [22]. Credibility was enhanced through maximum variation in purposive sampling and member checking by returning preliminary findings to several participants for confirmation. Dependability was supported by maintaining a detailed audit trail documenting all research steps. Transferability was facilitated by providing thick descriptions of the clinical settings and participants’ demographic characteristics. Confirmability was strengthened through reflexive journaling by the primary researcher to minimize the influence of personal biases and preconceptions.

Findings

In this study, 13 nurses working in the oncology and pediatric intensive care units of children’s hospitals in Kermanshah city, Iran, participated. The participants included 3 with a master’s degree and 10 with a bachelor’s degree in nursing. Nine nurses worked in the intensive care units and four in the oncology wards, with work experience ranging from 7 to 18 years (Table 1). A total of 13 near-death experiences were reported from 13 children, including 3 girls and 10 boys, with a mean age of 9 years. The children had been hospitalized with diagnoses such as acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), neuroblastoma, cystic fibrosis, drug overdose, and multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C). Two children reported their experiences after recovering from a coma and survived, while the other 11 children passed away within 3 h to 3 days after reporting the experience. Data analysis yielded three main categories, including soothing perceptions, encounters with individuals, and emotional reactions, along with six subcategories (Table 2).

Main categories and subcategories extracted from the study

Soothing perceptions

This category encompasses the experiences of children during near-death moments, characterized by feelings of calmness, security, and the presence of light or a benevolent figure. Participants observed that these experiences were often reported after a coma or during critical conditions, reflecting a sense of soothing and positive perception in the child. This category includes two subcategories: (1) Enveloped in Light and (2) Secure Companion.

Enveloped in light

Participants reported that some children experienced an intense light that surrounded them completely and appeared infinite, which induced a sense of calm. In both cases, the children had emerged from coma and described the experience in a matter-of-fact, almost serene manner. Participant 12, an ICU nurse, described her patient—a 15-year-old boy with cystic fibrosis who had been intubated and whose consciousness had been low. After extubation and recovery, he spoke about what he had perceived while unconscious:

My patient, a 15-year-old boy with cystic fibrosis, told me when he recovered from the coma, ‘I was in a corridor full of light; I could see nothing else, it was light everywhere.’ (P12)

Participant 10 recounted the experience of a 16-year-old girl hospitalized after a drug overdose and suicide attempt. She had been in a near-comatose state, intubated and on a ventilator, with frequent severe seizures. After her consciousness gradually returned and she was extubated, the nurse asked her whether she remembered anything from the time she was unconscious:

A 16-year-old girl hospitalized due to a drug overdose told me after coming out of the coma, ‘I was in a corridor full of light. There was so much light that nothing else was visible, and the further I went, the more the light increased. (P10)

The nurse recalled that the girl offered this description with a sense of calm detachment, showing no fear or confusion about what she had experienced.

Secure companion

In this subcategory, children reported the presence of a kind or protective figure who conveyed a sense of safety and comfort. The nurses observed that the children appeared calm and emotionally soothed during these episodes, rather than frightened.

Participant 5 described a 6-year-old boy with ALL who was in very serious condition—his face bruised and swollen, blood coming from his ear, and his platelet levels critically low. That evening, when the nurse entered his isolation room to administer a platelet transfusion:

My patient was a 6-year-old boy. When I entered his room to administer medication, he pointed to the lower corner of the room and said, ‘Nurse, do you see that kind lady? She’s standing there; she came to take me with her.’ (P5)

Participant 9, an oncology nurse, recalled a 7- or 8-year-old girl with ALL whose test results were severely abnormal, although clinically she appeared stable. While the nurse was drawing her blood during the evening shift:

My patient was an 8-year-old girl. While I was performing her blood tests, she told me, ‘A kind lady came into my room and told me not to worry or be upset. She said she came to take me to a good place, and I want to go with her.’ (P9)

The nurse noted that the child said this peacefully, with no indication of fear. Her mother, however, began to cry upon hearing it. The child was transferred to the PICU that night and passed away.

Participant 8 cared for a 13-year-old boy with ALL in an isolation room. His father was usually with him, but during a moment when the father was absent:

My patient was alone in her room. She told me, ‘Nurse, when my father isn’t in the room, a very kind man comes and talks to me, saying he came to take me with him. But when my dad is in the room, he doesn’t come.’ (P8)

The nurse noted that, unlike many other children, this boy showed no fear whatsoever when speaking about the “kind man.” He appeared comforted by the visits and spoke about them in a relaxed manner. The nurse chose not to probe further to avoid distressing the father.

Encounter with individuals

This category reflects children’s experiences during near-death moments that involved observing or interacting with other individuals. Participants reported that children perceived the presence of people who sometimes appeared as strangers and other times invited them to play, which influenced the child’s experience. This category includes two subcategories: (1) Unknown Observer and (2) Invitation to Play.

Unknown observer

Participants indicated that some children perceived the presence of individuals who were strangers to them, eliciting curiosity or surprise rather than fear. The nurses themselves could see no one, yet the children insisted on the presence of these figures, often pointing toward specific locations in the room.

Participant 3, who was on night shift alone in the ICU around 3 a.m., heard a 6-year-old boy with Neuroblastoma calling out to her from his isolation room:

I was administering the child’s medications when the child pointed toward the door and said, ‘Nurse, who that man is standing at my door?’ (P3)

The nurse described the child as speaking in a frightened voice, with fear clearly visible on his face. Despite repeated reassurance that no one was there, the child kept pointing at the door and would not calm down until his mother arrived. He passed away the next day.

Participant 4 described a 10-year-old boy who was well-liked by all the nurses for his calm and gentle nature. During a morning shift, while the nurse was administering medication with the boy’s father standing beside the bed:

My patient was a 10-year-old boy admitted with a diagnosis of ALL. His father was sitting by the bed while I was setting up his IV. The boy looked at his father and asked, ‘Dad, who is that man standing in my room?’ His father replied that no one was there, but the boy insisted, saying, ‘A man is standing in my room, watching me.’ (P4)

The nurse recalled that the child was not visibly terrified but persistent and insistent. Both the father and nurse reassured him, and he eventually calmed down. He passed away one or two days later.

Invitation to play

One child reported experiencing the presence of other children who were playing and actively inviting him to join them.

Participant 6 was on night shift and entered an 8- or 9-year-old boy’s room at 10 p.m. to administer medication. The child’s mother was present and directed the nurse’s attention to what her son was saying:

The boy said to me, ‘Look, the children are playing in the yard. See, one of them came behind my window and is saying, “Come play with us too.” (P6)

The nurse looked out the window and saw no one in the hospital yard. The child, however, pointed insistently, first to the yard and then to the window screen, saying one of the children had come right up to the glass.

Emotional reactions

This category represents the emotional responses of children during near-death moments as observed by nurses. Unlike the previous categories, the experiences grouped here were marked by overt fear, anxiety, and a strong need for protection.This category comprises two subcategories: (1) Seeking Security and (2) Anxiety.

Seeking security

Participants reported that children in near-death situations actively sought the presence of a nurse or a familiar person to feel safe. When left alone, these children became profoundly anxious.Participant 7 described a 6- or 7-year-old boy with ALL whose condition had deteriorated. Despite having a catheter and being in poor general condition, he refused to stay in his room:

My patient, a 6-year-old boy with ALL, during his last hospitalization, even though he was not in good general condition, often stayed at the nursing station. I asked him why he didn’t stay in his room, and he said, ‘There’s a man in my room, he keeps telling me he will take me with him. I’m scared; if I go into the room, he will take me. (P7)

The nurse recounted that the boy would sit at the nursing station until 1 or 2 a.m., falling asleep from exhaustion, after which his father would quietly carry him back to his room. He was transferred to the PICU and passed away during that same shift.

Participant 13 reported a strikingly similar pattern with an 8-year-old boy with ALL in an isolation room:

Nurse, please stay in the room with me. When you leave, a man comes in and wants to take me with him, but when you are here, he doesn’t come. (P13)

Anxiety

Nurses observed clear anxiety responses in several children, including intense fear, crying, shouting, and urgent requests to remove the perceived figure.

Participant 2 described a 9-year-old girl with MIS-C who, during a night shift while in the isolation room for dialysis, woke up around 1 a.m. and began shouting. The nurse rushed in:

My patient, a 9-year-old girl in the isolation room, shouted at 1 a.m., ‘The man standing at my door won’t let me sleep. I’m scared; please take him out of the room. (P2)

The nurse emphasized the intensity of the fear on the child’s face—“like a stranger was really in her room.” Despite staying by her side and talking to her, the terror remained visible. She went into cardiac arrest around 4 or 5 a.m. and passed away.

Participant 1 shared the case of an 8- or 9-year-old boy with ALL who, upon being admitted to the isolation room, began crying immediately, saying he did not want to die there because someone wanted to take him. That night, around 11 p.m. to midnight, during the nurse’s checks:

The child looked toward the corner of the room with great fear and said, ‘A man is holding my hand, trying to pull me; don’t let him take me, take me out of this room. (P1)

The nurse described the boy as staring fixedly at the bottom corner of the room, as though watching someone stand there. His mother was crying beside him. The boy went into cardiac and respiratory arrest a few hours later and passed away.

Participant 11 recalled a 6-year-old boy with ALL who, at 6 a.m. during the night shift, told the nurse upon her entering his room:

It was 6 a.m. When I entered my patient’s room to administer medication, and the child told me, ‘Nurse, close the door. There’s a man standing behind the door; he wants to take me with an ambulance. (P11)

The nurse found the boy’s mother already in tears beside him. Despite reassurance, the boy insisted on what he saw. His condition deteriorated shortly afterward, he was transferred to the PICU, and he passed away within one or two days. These findings indicate that near-death experiences can be accompanied by severe anxiety, and children strongly require the presence of a safe and supportive nurse.

Discussion

Research on Near-death experiences has primarily focused on adult populations [23], and studies investigating this phenomenon in children are extremely scarce [12]. Moreover, existing studies in children have largely been retrospective, meaning that adults recall and report NDEs they experienced during childhood [12]. This study similarly approaches the phenomenon through nurses’ perspectives. Parnia and colleagues have emphasized the need for empirical and systematic studies in this area to address the lack of an overarching research framework as well as the absence of precise definitions and terminology [10]. Some of the experiences reported by nurses in the present study—particularly those involving perceived visitors, invitations, and emotional responses to unseen figures—share characteristics with what has been termed ‘bedside visions’ in the literature. However, Thomas and O’Connor have argued that in children, such phenomena should be distinguished from core NDEs, as they identify different extrasensory experiences that children have in and near death, demarcating between experiences during the dying process and those occurring around its edges [12]. The findings of the present study indicate that a temporal distinction was noted: experiences of light were reported immediately following critical events such as coma or cardiac arrest, whereas encounters with figures and observers were more commonly described while the child was conscious and interacting with staff or family. In a case study from India, Purkayastha and Mukherjee reported on a 4-year-old Hindu boy who, after recovering from hypotensive shock due to gastroenteritis and dehydration, described being surrounded by silver and white clouds and feeling as though he had spent months in that state—although his actual period of unconsciousness lasted only one week [24]. This experience reflects the presence of positive and soothing perceptions in children near death and aligns with the “soothing perceptions” category identified in the present study. Additionally, in a case study conducted by Thomas and O’Connor (2024, 2026) examining near-death experiences in children after intensive care, seeing light emerged as a central theme. For instance, a boy who had undergone a lung transplant and experienced three episodes of cardiopulmonary arrest described passing through a spiral-like space and ultimately encountering a bright spiral of light [12]. This observation further supports the prominence of luminous and comforting experiences among pediatric NDEs. These findings correspond with the “Enveloped in Light” subcategory in our study. Furthermore, children in the present study reported experiences of encountering other individuals.These findings align with adult NDEs, as in a study by Parnia on cardiac arrest survivors, participants reported encounters with deceased relatives and mysterious beings [25]. Although the form and interpretation of these interactions differ between children and adults, both groups report experiences of interacting with other beings. The anxiety observed in some children appeared linked to the bedside vision component—particularly unfamiliar or threatening figures—rather than to the core NDE itself. A review of sedation protocols confirmed that ketamine was not part of these patients’ medication regimens; thus, the distress was unlikely to be pharmacologically induced. It is important to consider alternative clinical explanations for some of the reported phenomena. Delirium, hallucinations, and medication-induced perceptual disturbances are common in critically ill children and could, in some cases, mimic or overlap with features described here. However, several aspects of the accounts suggest more than confusional states alone. The experiences were often described as calm, organised, and meaningful rather than fragmented or distressing in the manner typical of delirium. Moreover, in cases such as the child who saw light during coma, the experience occurred at a time when conscious hallucination would be unlikely. Nonetheless, the retrospective and nurse-reported nature of the data limits our ability to definitively rule out such confounders, and future studies would benefit from prospective designs incorporating clinical screening for delirium and medication review. Anxiety and seeking security appeared more closely tied to these bedside visions than to the core NDE itself. This distinction supports the view that children, like adults, experience transcendent phenomena near death; however, their narratives are shaped by developmental stage, emerging as simpler, more concrete, and often expressed through observable behaviours rather than abstract reflection. This suggests that NDEs may have a universal and pervasive nature, not limited by age or cognitive maturity, although the way they are perceived and described differs in children. Given the limited number of direct studies on children, these findings underscore the importance of including children in NDE research. Future studies could employ participatory and creative methods, such as visual interviews, art, and storytelling (Thomas & O’Connor, 2024, 2026), to gain deeper insights into children’s near-death experiences and to establish a scientific framework for their psychological and spiritual support during critical moments.

How might this information affect nursing practice?

Understanding children’s near-death experiences can enhance nurses’ ability to provide developmentally appropriate, emotionally sensitive, and family-centered care during critical illness. The insights from this study help nurses recognize the psychological and spiritual dimensions of NDEs in children, enabling them to respond with greater empathy, reduce fear or confusion, and support both the child and family more effectively. These findings also emphasize the need for supportive environments, improved communication, and interdisciplinary collaboration to ensure that nurses feel prepared to address children’s emotional and existential reactions during life-threatening events. Positioning this study within a spiritual nursing care framework may also help address the holistic needs of children facing life-threatening illness.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample included only 13 children hospitalized in oncology and pediatric intensive care units in Kermanshah city, and therefore may not represent the experiences of all children; generalizability to other regions or countries is limited. Second, near-death experience data were collected through nurses’ reports, and direct interviews with children were not conducted, which may have resulted in incomplete transmission of the children’s actual experiences or potential influence from the nurses’ personal interpretations. Third, all children were in critical conditions and suffering from severe illnesses; thus, the findings may not be generalizable to healthy children or those with less severe conditions. Additionally, the study did not systematically collect data on nurses’ own beliefs and perspectives regarding NDEs. Since nurses acted as intermediaries in reporting children’s experiences, their personal views may have influenced how these accounts were conveyed. Future studies should incorporate this dimension. Finally, while interviews were conducted in 2025, the experiences reported by nurses had occurred at varying points in their careers—some recent, others several years earlier. This may introduce recall bias and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that near-death experiences in children, similar to adults, are multidimensional and rich phenomena encompassing soothing perceptions, interactions with others, and emotional responses. Children reported experiences of bright light, the presence of kind and reassuring individuals, and alterations in the perception of time and space. These experiences suggest that NDEs can have profound psychological and spiritual impacts on children and highlight the need for specialized environmental and caregiving support. Considering the limitations of existing studies, the findings of this research can provide an initial framework for future studies and contribute to the advancement of knowledge on children’s near-death experiences, as well as the development of supportive and spiritual care programs in clinical settings.
