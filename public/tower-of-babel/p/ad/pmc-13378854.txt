The role of physicians in herbal medicine; knowledge, attitudes, and practices: A cross-sectional
Authors: Mohammad Mehdi Haghighat Lari, Minoo Akbarzadeh Morshedi, Mohammad Saeid Moeinfar, Maryam Sadat Hosseini Khabr, Fatemeh Atoof, Zahra Kermani Zadeh Jamkarani, Fatemeh Ahmadi, Niloofar Sedaghatnezhad, Mohsen Taghizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Herbal medicine, a key component of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM), is widely used globally. However, healthcare professionals often lack sufficient knowledge and practical skills in this area, highlighting the need for improved education and evidence-based guidelines. This study aimed to assess physicians’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding herbal medicine.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This cross-sectional study was conducted at Kashan University of Medical Sciences in 2024. A total of 146 physicians, randomly selected from various educational levels, participated. Data were collected using a validated questionnaire assessing KAP related to herbal medicine. Statistical analysis was performed using non-parametric tests in SPSS software SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a significance level of P < 0.05.RESULTS:The mean ± SD age of participants was 31.06 ± 8.58 years. Knowledge scores were low (mean: 9.37 ± 5.86), while attitude scores were moderately high (mean: 26.71 ± 4.91), and practice scores were limited (mean: 5.42 ± 4.37). KAP scores were all significantly associated with age (P < 0.001), and physicians who prescribed herbal medicine had significantly higher knowledge (P < 0.001), attitude (P < 0.001), and practice scores (P < 0.001) compared to non-prescribers. Multivariate regression confirmed age and prescribing behavior as independent predictors of higher KAP scores, while gender and job level were not significant contributors.CONCLUSION:Physicians demonstrated positive attitudes but limited knowledge and practice regarding herbal medicine. These findings emphasize the need to integrate CAM education into medical training and develop evidence-based guidelines to enhance physicians’ competence in prescribing herbal medicine.

Introduction

Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM), encompassing a diverse array of medical systems, practices, and products outside the realm of conventional medicine, has witnessed a remarkable surge in global popularity in recent decades.[123] This trend is particularly pronounced in Iran, where both the general population and healthcare professionals have increasingly embraced CAM as a viable component of healthcare and treatment strategies.[45] The growing preference for CAM is largely fueled by mounting concerns over the adverse effects associated with conventional pharmaceuticals, prompting a shift toward more natural and holistic therapeutic options.[6] In alignment with this global movement, the World Health Organization (WHO) has actively advocated for the integration of CAM into modern medical systems, emphasizing its potential to contribute to the overarching goal of “Health for All.”[7]

Among the various forms of CAM, herbal medicine stands out as one of the most widely utilized modalities, with its use deeply rooted in cultural traditions and contemporary healthcare practices worldwide.[8] Epidemiological studies reveal that herbal medicine is extensively employed across diverse populations, often serving as a primary or adjunctive treatment for a range of health conditions.[9] For instance, approximately 21% of individuals with hypertension report a preference for medicinal herbs, citing perceived safety and efficacy as key factors.[10] Similarly, the use of herbal medicine among pregnant women varies significantly, with prevalence rates ranging from 22.3% to 82.3%, and commonly used herbs, including peppermint, ginger, thyme, chamomile, sage, fenugreek, and green tea.[1112] Notably, 84% of postpartum mothers rely on herbal remedies to address postpartum health concerns, further underscoring the pervasive role of herbal medicine in maternal and child health.[13] Despite its widespread use, a critical gap persists in healthcare professionals’ knowledge and understanding of medicinal herbs, raising concerns about potential herb-drug interactions and the safe integration of herbal remedies into clinical practice.[14]

The knowledge and attitudes of physicians toward herbal medicine have been the subject of extensive research, yielding mixed findings across different geographical and cultural contexts. In Saudi Arabia, for example, studies indicate that some physicians possess a reasonable awareness of the potential adverse effects and drug interactions associated with herbal medicine.[9] However, other investigations highlight significant deficiencies in healthcare professionals’ knowledge regarding herb-drug interactions, particularly in regions where herbal medicine is deeply embedded in traditional healthcare systems.[15] This variability in healthcare professionals’ understanding of medicinal plant products and their interactions with conventional drugs underscores the need for targeted educational interventions and evidence-based guidelines to bridge this knowledge gap.[16]

Despite the growing integration of herbal medicine into healthcare systems worldwide, there remains a paucity of research examining physicians’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding medicinal herbs in Iran. This study was conducted to address this gap by evaluating the perspectives of physicians affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences. This study aimed to assess the KAP of physicians toward herbal medicine, with the goal of informing medical education curricula, enhancing patient care, and promoting evidence-based decision-making in the use of herbal remedies in clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study, conducted in 2024 at Kashan University of Medical Sciences, Iran, assessed physicians’ KAP regarding medicinal herbs.

Setting and participants

The study population included practicing physicians affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences. Physicians were selected from various specialties and general practices, ensuring a representative sample of the healthcare workforce in the region. Inclusion criteria encompassed all physicians across different medical fields and job levels who were actively practicing under the jurisdiction of Kashan University of Medical Sciences during the study period. Physicians were excluded if they were not affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences or if they were unwilling to participate in the study.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the formula for a 95% confidence level, with a standard deviation of 6.17 based on prior knowledge scores from Soltanipour et al.[14] and a margin of error of 1. The final estimated sample size was 146.

Randomization and blinding

A stratified random sampling method was employed to ensure representativeness. A complete list of practicing physicians in Kashan city, including general practitioners and specialists, was obtained from the Medical System Organization. Participants were selected proportionally based on their representation in the healthcare system.

Data collection tool and technique

Demographic tool

A demographic questionnaire was used to collect information on participants’ age, gender, educational level, job position, and years of experience.

KAP questionnaire

The KAP questionnaire used in this study was originally developed and validated by Soltanipour et al.[14] to assess physicians’ perspectives on medicinal herbs. The tool consists of three domains: KAP regarding herbal medicine. The knowledge section includes six domain-specific questions scored as correct (1 point) or incorrect/unanswered (0 points), with total scores ranging from 0 to 30; higher scores indicate superior theoretical understanding. The attitude section comprises eight items on a 5-point Likert scale from completely disagree (1) to completely agree (5), producing scores between 8 and 40, where higher scores reflect more favorable attitudes toward herbal medicine. The practice section assesses real-world application through six questions, using a combination of Likert-type and binary scales, with a maximum total score of 25 points; higher scores denote more systematic and frequent use of herbal remedies in clinical practice. Notably, one attitude item and four practice items (non-Likert) were excluded from the total score calculations to maintain internal consistency and were analyzed separately for qualitative insights. In the original validation by Soltanipour et al.[14], the questionnaire demonstrated a content validity index (CVI) of 0.89 and a content validity ratio (CVR) of 0.92, confirming excellent content validity. The internal consistency reliability, assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, was reported at 0.86, indicating high reliability across domains.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kashan University of Medical Sciences (IR.KAUMS.MEDNT.REC.1403.085). All procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing data.

Statistical and data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois). Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, and variance) were used to summarize data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to assess data normality. Non-parametric tests, including the Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Walli’s test, were used for group comparisons. Multivariate regression analysis was performed to examine associations between variables while adjusting for confounders. The significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 146 physicians participated in the study, with a mean age of 31.06 ± 8.58 years. The sample included 74 males (50.7%) and 72 females (49.3%), comprising 116 general practitioners (79.5%) and 30 specialists (20.5%). Among participants, 61.0% reported prescribing herbal medicine, primarily for 1–4 patients per 20, a statistically significant finding (P = 0.008). The most frequently cited barrier to prescribing herbal remedies was the perceived lack of scientific basis (49.3%), followed by lack of effectiveness and non-standard production. Notably, 74.2% of prescribers reported prescribing herbal medicine to family members, and 75.3% provided patients with information regarding herbal therapies. Statistically significant differences were observed based on job level (P < 0.001) and frequency of herbal prescriptions (P < 0.001). Additionally, chronic conditions emerged as the most common reason for herbal prescriptions (P < 0.001). A significant positive correlation was also observed between knowledge scores and age (P < 0.001), whereas no significant differences in knowledge were found based on gender (P = 0.708) or job level (P = 0.111). Regarding attitude scores, prescribers exhibited significantly higher mean scores (28.23 ± 4.13) than non-prescribers (24.33 ± 5.10), with this difference also reaching statistical significance (Mann-Whitney U test, P < 0.001). Additionally, attitude scores demonstrated a significant positive correlation with age (P = 0.014), while no significant associations were detected with gender (P = 0.175) or job level (P = 0.730). In terms of practice scores, a significant positive correlation with age was noted (P < 0.001); however, no significant differences were found based on gender (P = 0.896) or job level (P = 0.167). Discussion

The current study aimed to assess physicians’ KAP regarding herbal medicine in Kashan, Iran. The findings revealed a significant gap between physicians’ positive attitudes toward herbal medicine and their limited knowledge and practical application. The mean knowledge score was relatively low (9.37/25), while the mean attitude score was positive (26.71/40). However, the mean practice score was low (5.42/20), indicating that despite favorable perceptions, physicians rarely prescribed herbal remedies.

The regression analyses provided additional insights into the factors associated with KAP outcomes. Age emerged as a consistent and significant predictor across all domains, suggesting that older physicians tend to have more experience or exposure to herbal medicine. Physicians who reported prescribing herbal remedies scored significantly higher in knowledge, attitude, and practice domains, even after adjusting for age, gender, and job level. This reinforces the idea that practical engagement with herbal medicine may positively influence physicians’ overall orientation toward it. In addition, no significant differences in knowledge, attitude, or practice were observed between genders or job levels, neither in bivariate comparisons nor multivariate regression models. These findings imply that the gaps in herbal medicine competence are widespread, affecting physicians across demographic and professional strata.

The low knowledge scores observed in this study align with findings from other regions. Ventola et al.[17] reported that nearly half of US physicians had weak or very weak knowledge of herbal medicine, while Soltanipour et al.[14] found that a majority of physicians in Rasht, Iran, lacked adequate knowledge. The current study also found a significant positive correlation between knowledge scores and age, suggesting that older physicians may have acquired more experiential knowledge over time. However, no significant differences were observed based on gender or job level, indicating that the knowledge gap is widespread across demographic and professional groups.

Despite limited knowledge, participants exhibited generally positive attitudes toward herbal medicine, consistent with findings from other studies. Amaeze et al.[15] reported that over 90% of physician students believed in the moderate to high effectiveness of herbal remedies, while Shaito et al.[18] report that a majority of physicians in the US viewed herbal medicine favorably. Similarly, Khan et al.[19] noted that a significant proportion of general practitioners in Pakistan held positive attitudes toward herbal medicine. These findings suggest that healthcare providers recognize the potential benefits of herbal medicine, even in the absence of formal training or standardized guidelines.

However, the positive attitudes observed in this study did not translate into widespread practical application. Only 61% of participants reported prescribing herbal medicine, and most prescribed it to fewer than 20% of their patients. This limited utilization is consistent with findings from Soltanipour et al.,[14] who reported similarly low practice scores among physicians in Iran. (5.02/20). In contrast, studies in the United States and Europe have found higher rates of herbal medicine prescription, though these practices are often supported by established guidelines and training programs. The discrepancy between attitudes and practices may stem from a lack of formal education, insufficient knowledge of herb-drug interactions, and the absence of standardized guidelines for herbal medicine use.[182021]

The findings of this study underscore the need for targeted interventions to bridge the gap between physicians’ positive attitudes and their limited knowledge and practice of herbal medicine. Integrating CAM and herbal medicine training into medical school curricula and continuing professional development programs could enhance physicians’ confidence and competence in prescribing herbal remedies. Additionally, the development of national guidelines, similar to the WHO’s Physician Desk Reference (PDR) or the European Scientific Cooperative on Phytotherapy (ESCOP) standards, could provide physicians with evidence-based resources for safe and effective herbal prescriptions.[22]

These measures are particularly important in light of findings from other regions. For instance, in Lebanon, Makarem et al.[23] found that while physicians and nurses were open to CAM, they lacked the knowledge and confidence to discuss it with patients. Similarly, in Saudi Arabia, Zaidi et al.[24] emphasized the need for public awareness campaigns to educate both healthcare providers and the general public about the safe and effective use of herbal medicine. In addition, Rathore et al.[25] found that structured training programs significantly improved knowledge retention among medical interns and residents, suggesting that similar interventions could be beneficial for herbal medicine education as well.

Strengths and limitations

The current study employed a stratified random sampling method and a validated questionnaire, enhancing the representativeness and reliability of the findings. However, it provides only a snapshot of physicians’ perspectives at a single point in time, limiting the ability to establish causality. Self-reported data may also introduce recall or social desirability bias. Furthermore, the study was conducted within a single medical university, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This study found that physicians in Kashan, Iran, exhibited low knowledge and limited practice of herbal medicine, despite positive attitudes. Age was positively correlated with knowledge and practice, but no significant differences were observed based on gender or job level.

The findings highlight the urgent need for policy interventions to integrate herbal medicine into healthcare systems. The WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy underscores the importance of evidence-based CAM in achieving universal health coverage. Policymakers should prioritize the development of national guidelines, regulatory frameworks, and standardized training programs to ensure the safe and effective use of herbal medicine. Integrating CAM into medical school curricula and continuing professional development programs can enhance physicians’ knowledge and confidence. Additionally, public awareness campaigns can educate both healthcare providers and patients about the benefits and risks of herbal remedies, fostering a more collaborative and informed approach to healthcare.

To address these challenges, the research team recommends incorporating herbal medicine training into medical education, establishing evidence-based clinical guidelines, and conducting periodic workshops for practicing physicians. Future studies should evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions in improving physicians’ KAP regarding herbal medicine, particularly in regions with high cultural acceptance of CAM.
