Sex steroid hormones and atrial electrophysiology: Vive la difference!
Authors: Elise S Bisset, Susan E Howlett
Published: 2024

There is increasing recognition that the manifestations of many diseases differ importantly between the sexes. This is especially true with respect to cardiovascular diseases, where women are at higher risk of developing life-threatening arrhythmias than men of the same age [1]. Women also have longer QT intervals, which reflect a longer ventricular action potential duration (APD). This prolonged QT duration is important as it can predispose towards various cardiac arrhythmias. Since women develop longer QT intervals at puberty, the primary female sex steroid hormone 17β-estradiol has been implicated [1]. These observations, and the presence of receptors for the major sex steroid hormones in the heart, have motived researchers to explore the impact of sex steroid hormones on cardiac electrophysiology in ventricular myocytes and ventricular tissues [1].

Although previous studies have focussed on the effects of these hormones on cardiac electrophysiology in the ventricle, estrogen and androgen receptors are expressed in the atria as well. Despite this, few studies have explored the influence of sex steroid hormones on atrial electrophysiology. This is an important area of investigation as fluctuations in APD and slowing of conduction velocity (CV) can promote common arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation. In this issue of the Journal of Molecular and Cellular Cardiology Plus, James Bell and colleagues investigate sex differences in atrial electrophysiology using state-of-the-art optical mapping techniques [2]. They compare APDs and CVs in left atria isolated from male and female C57BL/6 mice. They also assess the effects of both low and high concentrations of 17β-estradiol and testosterone on atrial electrophysiological responses. They show that, although male and female atria have comparable baseline electrophysiological properties, epicardial repolarization is more variable in females than males. Male and female atria also respond very differently to acute application of sex steroid hormones. While 17β-estradiol prolongs APD and slows CV in both sexes, the impact on APD is greatest in females while the impact on CV is the most pronounced in males. This new work by James Bell and colleagues [2] is important. It provides novel insights into male-female differences in atrial electrophysiology and identifies clear a role for sex steroid hormones in regulating these differences. Evidence that acute exposure to estradiol prolongs APD in both sexes, and that testosterone application prolongs atrial APD in males suggests that estrogens and androgens can promote atrial arrhythmias. The observation that estradiol slows atrial conduction in both sexes - especially in males - suggests that estradiol creates an arrhythmogenic substrate in the atria. While the effects of estradiol on atrial electrophysiology are clearly relevant in females, under certain conditions they may also be an important factor in males. For example, the levels of estradiol may be high in obese men where aromatase, an enzyme found in adipose tissue, converts androgens to estrogens [3]. Vulnerability to atrial arrhythmias also may increase in men and women who are taking sex steroid hormone supplementation for various conditions.

Although this work provides clear evidence for sex differences in atrial electrophysiology and shows that two major sex hormones, 17β-estradiol and testosterone, mediate at least some of these differences, there are some issues to consider. One issue is that atrial repolarizing currents differ appreciably in mice and humans [4]. This may limit the translatability of these findings to human studies. This calls for studies in larger preclinical models such as the rabbit, where atrial currents are more like those seen in humans [4]. Another issue to consider is concentration of sex hormones used. Although low and high concentrations were investigated, the use of a broader range of physiological concentrations would be of interest.

This study raises several intriguing questions. The incidence of arrythmias increases dramatically with age at the same time as circulating levels of sex steroid hormones decline in both men and women. Based on the findings reported here, it is possible that age-associated declines in sex hormones attenuate the development of arrhythmias. On the other hand, intra-cardiac estrogen synthesis increases with age [3], which may increase the susceptibility to arrhythmias in the setting of aging. It is also well established that aging promotes fibrosis and slows conduction in the atria, which both set the stage for the development of atrial arrhythmias in later life [5]. Additional studies that directly explore sex differences in the susceptibility to atrial arrhythmias and the role of sex hormones in both young and old animal models are warranted. Investigating arrythmias directly with tools like electrocardiography would allow determination of the types of atrial arrhythmias (e.g. persistent vs paroxysmal atrial fibrillation). It will be important to directly investigate the roles of both 17β-estradiol and testosterone in atrial arrhythmia susceptibility with protocols such as rapid pacing. Identification of the specific receptors involved in the estradiol effects is also required, and this could involve studies in receptor knockout models. Finally, it will be important to investigate the ionic currents that underlie these critical effects of sex steroid hormones in mice, as well as in larger models like rabbits. This will facilitate the translation of these findings as they apply to men and women at risk of developing atrial arrhythmias as they age.
