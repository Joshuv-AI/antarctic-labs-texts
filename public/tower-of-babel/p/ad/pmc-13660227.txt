Beyond free healthcare: household out-of-pocket expenditure and catastrophic health expenditure among chronic kidney disease patients attending a tertiary care hospital in Sri Lanka
Authors: Wishva Rathnayake, Anuji Gamage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global public health challenge associated with high morbidity, premature mortality, and substantial healthcare costs. In Sri Lanka, CKD is a public health priority due to the high prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease of unknown etiology (CKDu). Although public healthcare services are largely free at the point of delivery, CKD imposes considerable financial pressure on households through out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE). This study assessed the household economic burden among CKD patients attending a tertiary care hospital in Sri Lanka, measured through OOPE and catastrophic health expenditure (CHE).MethodsAn analytical cross-sectional study was conducted from May to June 2019 among patients with CKD attending the renal clinic, ward, and hemodialysis unit of a government tertiary hospital. OOPE was assessed through direct patient interviews using a structured questionnaire developed for this study, capturing direct medical, non-medical, and indirect costs. CHE was defined as OOPE exceeding ≥ 25% and ≥ 40% of total monthly household income, reflecting different levels of financial burden. Costs were collected in Sri Lankan Rupees (LKR) and converted to United States Dollars (USD) using the 2019 average exchange rate (1 USD = 178.8 LKR). Associations between CHE and selected variables were examined using multivariable binary logistic regression.ResultsA total of 272 participants were included (response rate 90.7%). Median monthly OOPE was highest for hemodialysis care (LKR 38,540 [USD 215]), followed by inpatient care (LKR 16,000 [USD 89]) and clinic care (LKR 4,050 [USD 23]). Direct non-medical costs contributed substantially in all three patient groups. At the 25% threshold, CHE occurred in 74.0% (95% CI: 64.3–82.3) of hemodialysis patients, 51.3% (95% CI: 39.7–62.8) of inpatients, and 9.6% (95% CI: 4.5–17.4) of clinic patients; overall CHE was 45.2% (95% CI: 39.2–51.3). At the 40% threshold, overall CHE was 35.7% (95% CI: 30.0–41.7). Higher age and higher income were associated with lower odds of CHE.ConclusionsCKD imposes a substantial economic burden on households in Sri Lanka, particularly among patients receiving hemodialysis and inpatient care. Financial protection strategies should address major non-medical costs, including transport and dietary expenses, while strengthening existing social assistance programs for vulnerable households.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15146-1.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has emerged as a serious public health challenge globally, with nearly 700 million active cases and 1.2 million deaths per year [1, 2]. The estimated prevalence of CKD is 8%–16% worldwide [3]. It is a progressive and irreversible state of renal damage or impaired renal function associated with high morbidity, premature mortality, and significant healthcare costs. It is defined as “decreased kidney function shown by glomerular filtration rate (GFR) of less than 60 mL/min per 1·73 m², or presence of markers of kidney damage, or both, of at least 3 months duration, regardless of the underlying cause” [4].

In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Sri Lanka, the burden of CKD is increasing due to the rising prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, and an ageing population. Moreover, CKD poses a distinct challenge in Sri Lanka due to the emergence of CKD of unknown etiology (CKDu), predominantly affecting young male farmers in specific hotspots in the dry zone agricultural communities [5]. CKDu prevalence in endemic regions in the country has been reported to range between 15.1 and 22.9% [6], with recent estimates indicating an overall CKD prevalence of 10.7% among adults in non-endemic regions [7]. In 2019, diseases of the urinary system, under which CKD is classified, were the fourth leading cause of hospitalizations (1830 per 100,000 population) and the eighth leading cause of hospital deaths (14.4 per 100,000 population) in Sri Lanka [8, 9]. Due to this substantial impact, CKD is considered a public health emergency in Sri Lanka [10].

Chronic kidney disease generates significant financial pressure on healthcare systems as well as patients and their households [11]. Out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE), encompassing direct medical, direct non-medical, and indirect costs [8], reflects the financial burden faced by families of patients with the disease. Households of patients with CKD often experience economic burden due to direct healthcare costs such as medications and investigations, as well as non-medical costs including transportation and informal care, and indirect costs such as productivity losses due to morbidity and mortality [12, 13]. Catastrophic health expenditure (CHE), defined as “out-of-pocket spending for health care that exceeds a certain proportion of a household’s income, with the consequence that households suffer the burden of disease” [14], further reflects the severity of financial impact on households. A high level of CHE indicates significant financial hardship resulting from insufficient financial protection mechanisms [15].

In LMICs, OOPE constitutes a major source of healthcare financing due to limited public health funding, low health insurance coverage, large informal economies, high pharmaceutical costs, and fragmented health systems [16]. In contrast, in high-income countries (HICs), a higher proportion of total health expenditure is financed through public sources, supported by risk-pooling mechanisms and comprehensive insurance systems, offering greater financial protection [16, 17]. As a result, households in LMICs are more vulnerable to financial hardship and catastrophic expenditure when managing chronic conditions such as CKD.

Sri Lanka has a predominantly tax-funded public healthcare system that provides services free at the point of delivery, including care for patients with CKD through government hospitals. The Ministry of Health Sri Lanka delivers healthcare through a structured, tiered system comprising tertiary care hospitals, secondary-level provincial and district facilities, and primary care settings that provide outpatient, limited inpatient, and emergency services. Hemodialysis is the main modality of renal replacement therapy (RRT) for patients with end-stage kidney disease and is primarily available in tertiary care hospitals located in major urban centers [9] and selected secondary-care hospitals. Most state-funded dialysis services are hospital-based, with very few stand-alone dialysis units [9]. Renal clinic (follow-up) care for CKD patients is also predominantly delivered through tertiary and some secondary-care hospitals. Peritoneal dialysis remains underutilized in Sri Lanka as a chronic RRT modality, partly due to limited patient acceptance of self-administered treatment responsibilities [9]. Access to kidney transplantation is limited, with approximately 10 government hospitals across the country providing transplant services [18]. Specialized institutions, including the National Institute for Nephrology Dialysis and Transplantation and the China–Sri Lanka Friendship National Nephrology Hospital, offer advanced diagnostic and therapeutic services.

However, despite these free public healthcare provisions, patients in Sri Lanka still incur substantial OOPE due to pharmaceuticals and consumables, private outpatient consultations, diagnostic and laboratory investigations, as well as travel and transportation, food, lodging, and caregiver-related costs [19, 20]. World Bank estimates for Sri Lanka indicate that OOPE accounted for 44.3% of total health expenditure in 2019, and more recent estimates suggest this has increased to 54.81% in 2023 [21]. These constraints make OOPE a critical issue in the Sri Lankan context, particularly for chronic conditions requiring long-term care such as CKD.

Studies conducted in different LMICs have shown that CKD imposes a substantial economic burden on patients and their families. However, there is limited evidence from Sri Lanka that comprehensively assesses this burden at the household level. Most existing health economic research has focused on the cost of treatment modalities, cost-effectiveness in screening and prevention, or health system costs, leaving a critical gap in understanding the true cost of illness borne by households. This study aimed to address that gap by assessing the household economic burden experienced by CKD patients receiving care at a tertiary healthcare facility in Sri Lanka, located in Kurunegala district, a region affected by CKD [22], by evaluating direct and indirect costs, OOPE incurred, the level of CHE, and identifying socioeconomic factors associated with financial vulnerability.

Methods

Study design and setting

This analytical cross-sectional study was conducted from May to June 2019 to assess the household economic burden among patients with CKD attending the renal clinic, renal wards, and hemodialysis unit at Teaching Hospital Kurunegala, the largest government tertiary care hospital in Kurunegala District, Sri Lanka, one of the districts most affected by CKD [22].

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population comprised adult CKD patients aged 18 years or older who had been diagnosed for at least three months and were receiving regular follow-up care at the renal clinic, admitted to male or female renal wards, or attending the hemodialysis unit for maintenance dialysis. Patients who were critically ill and unable to respond, and those without a family member to provide reliable information were excluded.

Sampling technique and sample size

The sample size was calculated using the formula described by Lwanga and Lemeshow [23], assuming an anticipated prevalence of CHE of 24% [24] and a precision of 5%. After adjusting for a 5% non-response rate, the minimum required sample size was 294. For feasibility and subgroup comparisons, 300 participants were selected, with 100 participants from each of the renal clinic, renal wards, and hemodialysis unit.

Participants were recruited using stratified systematic sampling. The three care settings (renal clinic, renal wards, and hemodialysis unit) were considered as separate strata. Within each stratum, systematic sampling was applied using the corresponding registry as the sampling frame (renal clinic registry, hospital admission registry, and hemodialysis patient registry, respectively). A sampling interval of three was applied, and every third eligible patient was selected following a randomly determined starting point.

Recruitment of participants

Eligible patients were identified from renal clinic, ward, and hemodialysis unit registers and were approached in person during clinic visits, hospital admission, or dialysis sessions. Those who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate.

Data collection tools and procedure

Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire capturing socio-demographic characteristics, disease-related information, and costs incurred for CKD care [Additional File 1]. The questionnaire was developed based on relevant literature and expert input from professionals involved in nephrology and health economics to ensure content relevance and appropriateness. It was pre-tested among CKD patients to assess clarity, comprehensiveness, and feasibility, and necessary modifications were made prior to data collection. Cost components included:

Cost data were collected, specific to the care setting through which the participants were recruited. For, ward patients, only costs related to the current admission were included. For clinic and hemodialysis participants, costs were limited to those respective services. Costs from other concurrent care settings were not combined, thereby preventing double counting. Costs were estimated for a 30-day reference period. Indirect costs related to productivity loss were estimated using the human capital approach based on self-reported income loss due to missed workdays by patients and/or caregivers during the reference period. Total OOPE was calculated by summing all cost components [12]. Costs were collected in Sri Lankan Rupees (LKR) and converted to United States Dollars (USD) using the average exchange rate for 2019 (1 USD = 178.8 LKR), based on Central Bank of Sri Lanka data [25].

Outcome definition

CHE was defined as OOPE exceeding a specified threshold of total monthly household income, total expenditure, or non-subsistence expenditure [26, 27]. Different methodological approaches have been used to estimate CHE, primarily differing according to the denominator used [26]. The choice of denominator and threshold can substantially influence CHE estimates, the population groups identified as financially vulnerable, and comparability across studies.

The budget share approach estimates CHE as a proportion of total household income or expenditure and is widely used because of its methodological simplicity [26]. However, it may underestimate financial hardship among poorer households, as wealthier households are generally better able to absorb high healthcare spending [26]. In contrast, the capacity-to-pay approach deducts basic subsistence expenditure, commonly food expenditure, before calculating CHE, thereby reducing the denominator for low-income households and often identifying a greater burden among poorer populations [26].

The World Health Organization commonly recommends the capacity-to-pay approach using a 40% threshold, while budget share approaches using thresholds such as 10%, 25%, and 40% have frequently been applied in studies from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [26]. In the present study, CHE was assessed using household income-based thresholds of 25% and 40% following the budget share approach. This method was selected because of its methodological simplicity and to facilitate comparison with previous regional studies evaluating the economic burden of CKD care. The use of two thresholds enabled assessment of varying levels of financial hardship, with higher thresholds reflecting greater economic burden.

CHE was calculated at the household level for each participant by dividing total OOPE related to the participant’s respective care setting by monthly household income. As each participant represented a single household and belonged to one primary care category (inpatient, outpatient, or hemodialysis), OOPE was not aggregated across different service settings. Overall CHE was estimated as the proportion of all study participants whose household-level OOPE exceeded each threshold.

For regression analysis, CHE was considered the dependent variable, while age, gender, household income, and duration of disease were included as independent variables.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using JASP (version 0.95.4; JASP Team, University of Amsterdam, The Netherlands, 2025). Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and cost variables. Proportions with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for CHE. Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to assess associations between CHE and selected socio-demographic and disease-related variables, namely age, gender, household income, and duration of disease, based on their potential influence on household financial vulnerability as reported in previous literature. Potential confounding was addressed by including these variables in the regression model to estimate adjusted associations with CHE. p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the Postgraduate Institute of Medicine, University of Colombo, Sri Lanka. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. Participant confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing questionnaires using unique identification numbers. Data were securely stored and accessible only to the research team.

Results

A total of 272 participants were included in the study, comprising patients from the renal clinic, hemodialysis unit, and renal wards, with an overall response rate of 90.7%. Non-response was primarily due to refusal to participate, inability to complete the interview because of illness or discomfort, and unavailability during the data collection period. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 83 years, with a mean age of 56 years (SD ± 12.4). Most participants were male, married, and of Sinhalese ethnicity. Travel distance to the tertiary care center varied across the three patient groups, as reflected by differences in frequency and percentage distribution across distance categories. Socio-demographic profile of the respondents (N = 272)

Most of the participants (63.2%; n = 172) have completed their secondary education. 15% (n = 41) of individuals had G.C.E.A/L or above educational qualifications. Concerning the current work status, 25.4% (n = 69) of them were not participating in the workforce, as they were unable to work. Of the people who were employed (n = 167), 59.3% (n = 99) were farming/self-employed. The median monthly household income was LKR 51,700 [USD 289] (IQR: LKR 46660 [USD 261]). Total 52.2% (n = 142) of clinic, inward and hemodialysis patients, reported a monthly income of LKR 20,000 – LKR 60,000 (USD 112 - USD 336). More than half of the study participants (55.5%) did not have extra income sources.

Considering the disease-related factors, a total of 51.5% of study participants had been receiving care for 1–5 years. The highest number of patients (n = 84; 30.9%) were in stage IV of CKD. Overall, 217 (79.8%) of the sample (N = 272) had at least Diabetes Mellitus or Hypertension as a co-morbidity. A total of 100 (36.8%) patients were diagnosed with both Diabetes and Hypertension. The frequency of admission/visits to the hospital was assessed, and patients had an average of one admission (SD ± 0.661), one clinic visit (SD ± 0.3429), and 8 hemodialysis events (SD ± 1.644) per month. Time spent in each setting to obtain the service was also analyzed. The mean time spent on dialysis was 8.3 h (SD ± 2.847). The mean number of days stayed in the hospital was 7.0 (SD ± 5.603). The mean time spent in the renal clinic was approximately 4.5 h (SD ± 1.872) and, maximum of 9 h.

Out-of-pocket expenditure of CKD patients

OOPE was assessed separately for hemodialysis, inpatient, and clinic care by combining direct medical, direct non-medical, and indirect costs.

Hemodialysis patients reported the highest median monthly OOPE, followed by inpatient and clinic patients. Travel costs contributed substantially to OOPE among hemodialysis patients, while travel and food expenses were major contributors among inpatients. Indirect costs related to productivity losses were also notable among hospitalized patients. Although clinic patients incurred comparatively lower OOPE, food expenses constituted the largest cost component, followed by medication costs, reflecting the purchase of prescribed drugs from external pharmacies. Catastrophic health expenditure of CKD patients

CHE for each setting (Hemodialysis, Ward and clinic) were calculated by dividing the respective total monthly OOPE by average monthly income. The prevalence of CHE showed a clear gradient across care settings, with hemodialysis patients consistently experiencing the highest financial burden, followed by inpatients, and clinic patients reporting the lowest burden at both threshold levels. This pattern was consistent across both the 25% and 40% income thresholds, indicating a persistent disparity in financial vulnerability across service types. Overall Catastrophic Healthcare Expenditure

Based on individual household expenditure-to-income ratios, each participant was classified according to whether their OOPE exceeded 25% or 40% of monthly household income. The overall proportion of participants experiencing CHE across all study groups was 45.2% (95% CI: 39.2–51.3) at the 25% threshold and 35.7% (95% CI: 30.0–41.7) at the 40% threshold.

Factors associated with CHE at 25% threshold

Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis identified age and monthly household income as significant predictors of CHE at the 25% threshold. Increasing age and higher household income categories were associated with lower odds of experiencing CHE. Duration of disease and gender were not significantly associated with CHE. The regression model showed a statistically significant improvement over the null model (χ² = 25.81, p < 0.001) and demonstrated modest explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.122). Discussion

This study demonstrates that CKD imposes a substantial economic burden on Sri Lankan households, despite the provision of largely free public healthcare services. Almost half of all households experienced CHE at the 25% threshold, with a very high burden observed among those requiring hemodialysis, where approximately three in four households exceeded this level. More than half of inpatients also experienced CHE at the same threshold. These findings indicate that CKD remains in a highly impoverishing condition, particularly in its advanced stages.

Direct non-medical costs contributed the largest share of total OOPE across all three care settings in this study, including hemodialysis, inpatient care, and clinic visits, compared with both direct medical costs and indirect costs due to productivity loss. Similar patterns have been reported in previous Sri Lankan studies. A population-based study conducted in Anuradhapura district-Sri Lanka reported, 62% of the OOPE in hospital admission incurred for cost of visiting the patient in hospital by immediate family members and main OOPE of an episode of dialysis was for transport (42.7%) and food (22.2%) [28]. Another Sri Lankan study demonstrated that 92% of households reported some degree of transport-related expenditure, whereas 57% reported spending on medicines, highlighting the greater consistency of transport-related costs among CKD patients [29]. Evidence from CKDu-endemic regions in Sri Lanka further highlights the substantial household economic burden associated with CKD, with non-medical costs such as transport, expenses during hospital visits, special dietary requirements, and long-term caregiving contributing considerably to household expenditure, often exceeding direct medical expenses [30]. Comparable findings have also been reported in regional and international studies. A study done in Kerala, India revealed that direct non-medical cost is substantial among patients undergoing hemodialysis [31]. Similarly, an Italian study conducted among stage 4–5 pre-dialysis CKD patients reported that direct non-medical costs accounted for a substantial proportion (41%) of the total social cost of CKD [32].

Although direct non-medical costs remained the largest contributor to OOPE in clinic-based care, a relatively higher contribution from direct medical costs was also observed, primarily driven by expenditure on medicines. This finding is consistent with previous local research, which reported that expenditure on medicines accounted for a large proportion (37.4%) of costs incurred during clinic visits [28]. This pattern reflects gaps in the regular availability of essential CKD medications in government health facilities, which compels patients to purchase drugs from the private sector even when accessing public outpatient services.

However, evidence from several regional and international studies demonstrates contrasting cost patterns. A study from India has reported that direct medical costs constitute the 79.9% of OOPE among CKD patients, largely due to out-of-pocket payments for medicines [33]. Another cross-sectional study done in India also demonstrated that the majority of costs were direct medical expenses (78.54%) [34]. A study done with CKD stages III – V, non-dialysis patients in Cameroon revealed that direct medical cost accounted for very high percentage of the OOPE (86.4%), compared to direct non-medical cost (7.1%), and indirect cost (6.3%) [35]. These contrasting cost structures likely reflect differences in health system organization, financing mechanisms, and the balance between public and private sector service provision. In settings where households directly bear treatment costs, direct medical expenses tend to predominate, whereas in publicly financed healthcare systems, non-medical and indirect costs may contribute more substantially to household economic burden [19]. Together, these findings highlight the context-specific nature of CKD-related economic burden and underscore the importance of considering health system characteristics when interpreting OOPE patterns.

The CHE incidence observed among patients requiring hemodialysis and inpatient care in this study was markedly higher than that reported among general non-communicable disease (NCD) populations in Sri Lanka. At the 40% threshold, 66% of households receiving hemodialysis and 33.3% of households with hospital admissions experienced CHE. In comparison, a previous Sri Lankan multi-district study among patients with NCD-related hospital admissions reported a CHE incidence of 13.5% at the same threshold [19]. This contrast underscores the disproportionate financial burden imposed by CKD, likely attributable to its chronic nature, progression to renal replacement therapy, and accumulation of recurrent expenses.

Regionally, wide variation has been reported in the proportion of households experiencing CHE among patients with CKD. A study from India among pre-dialysis CKD patients reported that 67.4% of households incurred catastrophic costs when a 10% annual household income threshold was applied [33]. Among patients undergoing hemodialysis in India, the burden was even greater, with CHE reported in 93.4% of households [31]. In contrast, a study conducted in Thailand among patients with CKD stages 3–5, including those receiving dialysis, found that approximately 20% of patients experienced CHE [36]. Evidence from Nigeria also indicates severe financial vulnerability; a cross-sectional study reported an almost universal incidence of CHE, affecting 100% of households at the 10% threshold and 97% at the 40% threshold [37]. These marked differences across settings likely reflect variations in health system financing mechanisms, availability of publicly funded renal services, insurance coverage, and household socioeconomic capacity. In countries where dialysis services, medicines, and hospital care are largely financed through out-of-pocket payments or private healthcare systems, direct medical costs contribute substantially to household expenditure and CHE [38]. In contrast, in publicly financed systems such as Sri Lanka, where core medical services are provided free of charge through the government sector, non-medical expenses including transport, accommodation, caregiving, and income loss may become relatively more important contributors to household financial burden, as reflected in existing evidence [19]. In addition, differences in household income levels, social protection mechanisms, geographical accessibility to health care services, and the availability of decentralized facilities may further explain variations in CHE across settings [39].

In the present study, age and household income were significantly associated with CHE. Interestingly, higher age groups experienced lower levels of CHE. The inverse association with income is consistent with existing evidence demonstrating that low-income households are more vulnerable to financial catastrophe due to limited financial reserves and reduced capacity to absorb health-related shocks [36, 40]. However, our finding of lower CHE among older patients contrasts with several studies that report elderly individuals to be at greater risk of financial hardship because of limited earning capacity and higher dependency ratios [36, 40]. This discrepancy may be partly explained by sociocultural factors in Sri Lanka, where large percentage of elderly parents reside with their adult children within extended family structures [41]. Such living arrangements may provide financial pooling and shared household resources, thereby buffering older patients from direct economic vulnerability. In Sri Lanka, CKD patients also receive informal support from extended family networks, religious organizations, community groups, and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) [42, 43]. Such social support mechanisms may mitigate the direct financial impact of CKD-related expenditure in some households. These contextual and sociocultural factors may influence the observed relationship between age and CHE and should be considered when interpreting financial risk determinants.

The present findings should be interpreted within the context of major socioeconomic and health system changes that occurred in Sri Lanka following data collection. Sri Lanka experienced a severe economic crisis in 2022, accompanied by marked depreciation of the Sri Lankan Rupee against the United States Dollar [44]. The exchange rate increased from approximately LKR 180 per USD in 2019 to more than LKR 360 per USD at the peak of the crisis in 2022, before partially stabilizing around LKR 300 per USD in 2025 [45]. The crisis affected the population as a whole through reductions in household income, employment instability, rising inflation, increased transport and food costs, and disruptions in access to healthcare services, including medicines, and medical consumables [46]. Households affected by CKD were likely to experience an even greater impact because of their dependence on continuous long-term care, regular hospital visits, medicines, and dietary requirements. Consequently, the actual economic burden experienced by CKD households may be substantially greater than the estimates observed in the present study.

At the same time, several policy initiatives have been implemented by the Sri Lankan Ministry of Health and related government agencies to strengthen universal health coverage (UHC) and reduce OOPE among patients with chronic diseases, including CKD. Expansion of hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis services, strengthening of primary healthcare reforms, and initiatives to improve the availability of essential medicines have been introduced in recent years [44]. Moreover, financial assistance programs for selected low-income CKD patients have been strengthened by increasing the allowance up to LKR 10,000 (USD 56) [47]. By 2024, this program reportedly covered more than 39,000 beneficiaries nationwide [47]. Taken together, these post-2019 economic and health system developments suggest that the current burden of CKD-related OOPE and CHE may differ from the estimates reported in this study. While health system reforms and social assistance program may have reduced some components of OOPE, inflation, economic instability, and increased living costs may simultaneously have intensified financial vulnerability among CKD-affected households.

By emphasizing the economic hardship experienced by households affected by CKD, the findings of this study underscore the need for targeted policy actions to reduce the financial burden of CKD care in Sri Lanka. Financial protection measures should extend beyond free clinical services to address major non-medical costs, particularly transport and food-related expenses. Potential strategies include transport subsidies, reimbursement schemes, subsidized patient transport services, and improved access to dialysis and tertiary-care centers.

Although renal diets are routinely provided for hospitalized patients in government hospitals, households in the present study still incurred considerable food-related expenditure during inpatient care, and clinic visits. This may reflect costs related to accompanying caregivers, repeated hospital visits, supplementary meals purchased outside hospital settings, or concerns regarding the adequacy and acceptability of hospital-provided diets. Further research is needed to better understand the drivers of food-related expenditure among CKD households and to identify appropriate nutritional support strategies for patients requiring long-term care. In addition, further decentralization and expansion of hemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis services closer to patients’ communities may help reduce both transport- and food-related expenditure by minimizing prolonged travel, repeated hospital visits, and the need for meals purchased outside the home.

Existing government financial assistance programs for low-income CKD patients should also be strengthened, better targeted, and supported through improved awareness to enhance equitable access. Future universal health coverage strategies should explicitly incorporate non-medical costs when designing financial protection policies for chronic diseases such as CKD.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides detailed household-level evidence on the economic burden of CKD in a tertiary-care public hospital in Sri Lanka, with comprehensive assessment of direct medical, direct non-medical, and indirect costs across inpatient, dialysis, and outpatient care. The use of standard CHE thresholds further strengthens its relevance for health financing and universal health coverage policy.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The study relied on self-reported expenditure data, which may be subject to recall bias. As a single-center study conducted in a tertiary-care setting, findings may not be fully generalizable to all CKD patients in Sri Lanka. In addition, distress financing mechanisms, informal social support, and government financial assistance were not fully captured, potentially leading to underestimation of the true economic burden. The study was also powered primarily to estimate CHE prevalence, limiting the robustness of regression analyses exploring associated factors. Finally, data were collected prior to major post-2019 economic disruptions, and current cost patterns may therefore differ.

Conclusion

Chronic kidney disease imposes a substantial economic burden on households receiving care in this tertiary-care public hospital in Sri Lanka, with a considerable proportion experiencing catastrophic health expenditure, particularly among patients requiring hemodialysis or inpatient care. Despite the provision of free public healthcare, non-medical costs remain a major driver of financial hardship. These findings have important implications for strengthening financial risk protection and informing universal health coverage strategies for chronic disease care.

While the findings provide important insights from a major referral center serving a high-burden CKD population, they may not be fully generalizable to all CKD patients in Sri Lanka. Accordingly, to obtain a more comprehensive and nationally representative understanding of the population-level impact, further multicenter and nationwide studies are required.
