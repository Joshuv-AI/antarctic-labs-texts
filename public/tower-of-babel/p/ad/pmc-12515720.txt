Tuberculous Serpiginous‐Like Choroiditis, Optic Nerve Tuberculoma and Tuberculous Episcleritis: A Case Report
Authors: Dana Nourallah, Farah Nourallah, Iram Tariq, Sondus Alashrfi, Judy Alhakim, Mohammad Obada Alsadi, Rayan Mohammed Abdelwahab Elsadig, Walaa Ali, Raghad Naji, Hind Altag Alteraify Alsiddig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOcular tuberculosis is a rare extrapulmonary manifestation of TB with diverse presentations, posing diagnostic challenges. High suspicion, multimodal imaging, and ancillary tests are crucial for diagnosis. Early initiation of combined antitubercular and corticosteroid therapy can significantly improve visual outcomes and prevent complications, even in the absence of pulmonary TB symptoms.Keywords: episcleritis, extrapulmonary tuberculosis, ocular tuberculosis, optic neuritis

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), a communicable illness spread through the air, is a common public health concern in underdeveloped nations [1]. People who are infected either acquire latent tuberculosis, which is an immune response to antigens but lacks clinical symptoms of active disease, or develop active disease, which typically manifests within 2 years [2].

The body's organs can be impacted by tuberculosis. Ocular tuberculosis (OTB) is an uncommon extrapulmonary variant of tuberculosis that should not be disregarded due to its potential to cause visual impairment in patients. Because of its varied ocular tissue involvement, inconsistent clinical presentations, absence of gold standard testing and diagnostic criteria, and lack of international consensus regarding the best course of treatment, OTB continues to pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [3].

Scleritis, necrotizing scleritis, posterior scleritis, sclerokeratouveitis, optic neuritis, optic disc granuloma, subretinal abscess, orbital cellulitis, choroiditis, uveitis, chorioretinitis, and choroidal granuloma are all examples of ocular tuberculosis (TB) manifestations in various eye tissues. The predominant means of dissemination is haematogenous spread with extrapulmonary or pulmonary origins [1].

Chest x‐rays, high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT), interferon‐gamma release assays (IGRAs), and tuberculin skin tests (TSTs) can all be used to establish prior exposure to Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB). In cases of ocular TB, other methods of detecting active tuberculosis, such as lesion biopsies and culture, are not as effective. For one or more MTB DNA‐coding regions, the most popular method of diagnosis is multiplex, multitargeted polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

When ancillary testing confirms the diagnosis of ocular tuberculosis (TB), a combination of anti‐inflammatory therapy and antitubercular therapy (ATT) is necessary. This diagnosis warrants a high index of suspicion [4]. Treatment consists of corticosteroid therapy administered at different dosages and by different modes concurrent with anti‐TB therapy (ATT). Approximately 84% of patients respond to this type of treatment, which also prevents future recurrences of intraocular inflammation. Ocular imaging, response to ATT, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis of ocular fluid have contributed to tremendous advancements in the diagnosis and treatment of ocular tuberculosis (OTB) over the last 20 years [3]. Below, we present a rare and instructive case of ocular tuberculosis involving the optic nerve, choroid, and episclera in a young woman without pulmonary involvement.

Case Report

A 33‐year‐old South Sudanese (African) female patient with diabetes (compliant with medication) presented with generalized headache, photophobia, and unilateral ocular symptoms—periorbital pain and blurry vision in the left eye—for almost 2 months. She had an extensive travel history back and forth from North Africa (a TB‐endemic area) in the past 6 months; there, she worked in orphanage camps, and undocumented TB exposure was suspected. At her first visit, she did not complain of any systemic symptoms, and the vital signs taken at the clinic were normal. Her corrected vision acuity was 20/20 in the right eye and 20/200 in the left eye, and her left pupil was dilated and unreactive. The intraocular pressure was within the normal range in both eyes, and ocular movement was normal. Initial fundus assessment paired with OCT images of the left eye confirmed severely obscured and elevated optic disc margins, disc oedema with a zero cup/disc ratio, and peripapillary oedema; according to these findings, optic neuritis (papillitis) was strongly suspected (Figure 1). There were no signs of optic disc involvement in the right eye (Figure 1). No abnormalities were observed in the conjunctiva, cornea, iris, or anterior chamber of either eye. No signs of scleritis or vasculitis were detected. Choroidal folds were observed between the macula and optic disc, with nonspecific mild RPE pigmentary atrophic changes in the macula.

Investigations and Treatment

Routine blood tests showed the following results: Hb, 12.6 g/dL; total leukocyte count (TLC), 9.8 × 109/L; neutrophils, 72%; lymphocytes, 18.9%; eosinophils, 0.1%; ESR, 14; and CRP, < 1. Chest x‐ray revealed no evidence of pulmonary tuberculosis. MRI of both orbits, pre‐ and post‐contrast, revealed normal signal intensity and configuration of the globes, scleral margins, and optic discs. Both optic nerves and their sheaths appeared normal in signal, course, and structure, with no focal or diffuse lesions or abnormal enhancement detected. The conal and extra‐conal orbital spaces, including extraocular muscles, tendons, and lacrimal glands, showed a normal appearance without any abnormal signal or enhancement. The surrounding bony structures were intact with no lesions observed. A partially empty sella turcica was noted as an incidental finding (Figure 3). CT brain (pre‐ and post‐contrast) was also unremarkable, excluding any further pathology.

MRI of both orbits (pre‐ and post‐contrast) showing normal anatomy of the globes, optic nerves, extraocular muscles, and surrounding orbital structures. No abnormal lesions or enhancement is observed. Note the incidental finding of a partially empty sella turcica.

Moreover, the visual evoked potentials (VEPs) exhibited latent and prolonged B‐100 waves with low amplitude. For this reason, optic neuritis was suspected, and treatment with a high dose of intravenous methylprednisolone was initiated.

However, on follow‐up, the patient complained of mild shortness of breath (SOB), lethargy, and redness in her left eye. On examination, perilimbal conjunctival congestion and episcleral vessel engorgement were observed. The left pupil became weakly responsive to light. Further assessment via a dilated fundus exam revealed a granuloma on the optic disc of the left eye, coinciding with the previously described elevated disc margins and associated with bilateral, multifocal, yellowish‐white chorioretinal serpiginous lesions and satellite‐shaped pigmentary changes.

Taken together, these findings reinforced the suspicion of an infective etiology, namely, tuberculosis, considering the presence of retinochoroiditis and a granuloma coupled with the patient's extensive travel history. Fluorescein angiography revealed disseminated chorioretinal inflammation with several pigmented atrophic scars in addition to numerous pigmented chorioretinal serpiginous lesions involving the posterior pole (Figure 2). The retinal vessels were normal, with no signs of vasculitis. The QuantiFERON TB gold assay was positive, and HRCT produced supplementary findings, including enlarged, enhanced atypical lymph nodes in the mediastinum and left thoracic outlet and small nodules approximately 5 mm in size in the right upper and middle lobes. Subsequently, the patient's treatment plan was revised, and antituberculosis treatment was administered as follows: isoniazid 150 mg, rifampicin 300 mg, ethambutol 400 mg, pyrazinamide 500 mg, and prednisolone 60 mg tapered over 6 weeks.

Fluorescein angiography images of the left eye showing chorioretinal inflammation with several pigmented atrophic scars and pigmented chorioretinal serpiginous lesions involving the posterior pole.

Differential Diagnosis

Sarcoidosis, Syphilis, and HIV. Extensive investigations were performed to rule out the differential diagnoses of TB. Syphilis and HIV serologies were negative, and serum ACE levels were normal.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The following week, the patient's best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the left eye improved to 20/50. The left pupil responded normally. Furthermore, her symptoms improved, and she did not experience headache, orbital pain, or conjunctival congestion. Fundus examination also demonstrated a decrease in optic disc swelling, RNFL thickness, and the density of chorioretinal lesions. The diagnosis of ocular TB is challenging and should be considered with high levels of suspicion; however, there are some factors that may aid in the diagnosis, such as skin and blood test results, multimodal imaging, calcified ocular mass on imaging, and CNS or extra‐CNS findings that are related to the evidence of TB. Finally, neuroimaging and the initiation of early treatment can help avoid possible vision loss.

Discussion

To our knowledge, the simultaneous occurrence of tuberculous serpiginous‐like choroiditis (Tb‐SLC), optic nerve tuberculoma, and tuberculous episcleritis in one patient with ocular tuberculosis has rarely been reported, especially in the Middle East.

Tuberculosis is a multisystem disease that primarily affects the lungs and is caused by infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Worldwide, TB is the 13th leading cause of death and the second leading infectious killer after COVID‐19 (above AIDS), according to the World Health Organization (WHO). Ocular tuberculosis is an uncommon extrapulmonary tuberculous manifestation that has variable presentations. Its atypical clinical presentation usually delays diagnosis and subsequent treatment.

Although ocular TB (OTB) is an uncommon manifestation of extrapulmonary disease, it should not be underestimated because its potential resultant visual impairment is highly preventable with appropriate treatment [5]. On the other hand, the failure to consider TB in the differential diagnosis of intraocular inflammation may have catastrophic consequences because the immunosuppressive agents often used to manage intraocular inflammation may be fatal for both vision and life in patients with active TB disease [6]. Ocular tuberculosis closely mimics other ocular pathologies, and therefore, a high index of suspicion is required before making a diagnosis [7]. The absence of clinically evident pulmonary TB does not rule out the possibility of ocular TB, as 60% of patients with extrapulmonary TB have no evidence of pulmonary TB, and chest x‐rays are normal in cases of latent TB [8]. In nearly all reported cases, the diagnosis of ocular TB was only presumptive. In most studies, the diagnostic criteria for presumed ocular TB were residence or migration from areas endemic for TB, a history of contact with patients with TB infection, or the presence of suggestive ocular findings [8].

Blurred vision and light sensitivity are the most commonly reported ocular symptoms. Other complaints include flashes, floaters, or eye redness [5]. Patients with posterior segment disease are more likely to be visually symptomatic and seek early medical attention. Notably, lesions in the peripheral fundus may be asymptomatic and escape medical attention [5]. Patients may exhibit a variety of orbital findings, such as pain, proptosis, eyelid swelling, intermittent periorbital swelling, headache, epistaxis, decreased vision, visual field abnormalities, chemosis, Marcus Gunn pupil, epiphora, and increased orbital resistance to retropulsion [9].

An association between lesions resembling classic SC and tuberculosis (TB) was recognized as a potentially distinct clinical entity termed tuberculous serpiginous‐like choroiditis (TB‐SLC) [10]. The differentiation of this tuberculous entity from SC is critical because treatment of the former with immunosuppressive drugs leads to several potential adverse effects, which can have devastating consequences because of the worsening of a concomitant tuberculous infection [11].

In general, the treatment of ocular tuberculosis is the same as that for pulmonary TB. Treatment consists of a four‐drug regimen administered in two phases: rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol daily for 2 months, followed by rifampicin and isoniazid for 4 months. If a patient fails to respond within 3–4 weeks, multidrug resistance should be considered, and TB management should continue under collaboration with an infectious disease specialist [7].

In the management of ocular tuberculosis, corticosteroids play an important adjunctive role rather than serving as stand‐alone therapy. Their primary purpose is to suppress granulomatous inflammation and to minimize ocular tissue damage resulting from delayed‐type hypersensitivity to Mycobacterium tuberculosis antigens. Sharma et al. suggest that when used in low systemic doses for 4–6 weeks in conjunction with multidrug ATT, corticosteroids help limit immune‐mediated tissue injury and support visual recovery. However, corticosteroid monotherapy is contraindicated, as it may promote bacillary proliferation or reactivate latent systemic infection, leading to poor outcomes. This was evident in our patient, who initially received high‐dose intravenous corticosteroids under the presumptive diagnosis of optic neuritis. Her ocular condition subsequently worsened until the initiation of ATT, at which point a clear improvement was observed. This clinical course highlights the necessity of considering ocular TB early in the differential diagnosis of optic nerve and posterior segment inflammation, and underscores that corticosteroids should only be administered in combination with ATT to achieve favorable visual and clinical outcomes [10].
