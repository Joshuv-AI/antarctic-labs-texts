Obstacle crossing when dual-tasking in children with and without developmental coordination disorder
Authors: Zdenek Svoboda, Lucia Bizovska, Tomas Klein, Ludvik Valtr, Kamila Banatova, Reza Abdollahipour, Peter Henry Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the essential motor skills in children’s lives is obstacle crossing. This ability is frequently employed in everyday scenarios, such as stepping over toys or miscellaneous objects on the floor. In a real-life context, the execution of this task is often influenced by concurrent cognitive demands, leading to potential cognitive-motor interference. Its effect could be more apparent in younger children or children with some motor difficulties. Thus, the aim of this study is to assess the obstacle crossing performance and post-obstacle movement under single and dual-task conditions (motor and visual discrimination task) in children with developmental coordination disorder (DCD) and typically developing (TD) children of various ages. A total of 110 children aged from 7 to 12 years (35 DCD, 75 TD) participated in the study. They performed obstacle crossing task under single and two dual-task conditions (simple, complex-inhibit). The results showed significant effect of group only for centre of pressure velocity in medial-lateral direction during post-obstacle stance phase with higher values (worse balance) in DCD children. In addition, children with DCD also showed later propulsion during dual-tasking compared with single locomotor task, while TD children had comparable values for both conditions. The effect of age was significant in all aspects of the task including obstacle crossing performance, landing, dynamic balance and propulsion. Older children compared to younger can be characterized by a more effective strategy for obstacle crossing with smaller braking and faster gait initiation after obstacle crossing.Keywords: Motor difficulties, Dyspraxia, Motor, Cognitive, Task prioritizationSubject terms: Biotechnology, Psychology

Introduction

The ability to perform motor and cognitive tasks simultaneously (dual-tasking) is both exceedingly common and vitally important for many activities of daily life. Interference effects that impact one or both tasks can present safety risks (including falls, collisions, and decision errors) or impair functional performance in school or occupational settings1. The extant literature suggests higher dual-task costs (DTCs) in younger children compared with older children and adolescents2, and in older adults compared with younger3. In younger children, higher costs are associated with both lower cognitive reserves and less automated motor skill execution, both of which are related to immaturities in the development of neural networks that support each individual task and the ability to time share (between tasks) when overlapping neural structures are enlisted2. In older adults, these effects are attributed to age-related degradation of existing neural structures and greater attention to maintain overall gait stability3.

Importantly, young children who have not reached mature levels of gait and cognitive performance show greater dual-task interference on gait-related postural control metrics compared with older children and healthy young adults4. Moreover, typically developing children of school-age (TD) have difficulty maintaining their usual walking fluency while carrying out a concurrent cognitive or motor task, which suggests that walking per se requires a level of cognitive control5.

One can infer that for children with delayed or deviant development of motor skill (Developmental Coordination Disorder—DCD), dual-task interference effects are likely to be much higher than TD. Children with DCD have difficulty coordinating movements and acquiring motor skills in line with age expectations including everyday ones like dressing and grooming, fine-motor skills like handwriting, sport skills that require dynamic balance, and participation in leisure activities6. As well, children with DCD also commonly experience cognitive control deficits, which may compound difficulties in the performance of motor-cognitive dual-tasks7,8.

The very few dual-task studies on DCD to date have tended to support the hypothesis of heightened dual-task interference in these children. In one of the few studies involving locomotor and cognitive tasks, Schott et al.9 showed that children with DCD had higher DTCs on motor outcomes but performed similar to TD on cognitive metrics. Schott argued that the DCD group allocated cognitive resources differently by using an external focus of attention under simple task conditions9. However, attentional focus was not tested directly in this study. It suggests that for children with DCD, deficits in the automatization process may emerge when conscious monitoring of motor skills is hindered by the integration of an additional task that demands attentional resources. In a study of static postural control under dual-task conditions, Tsai et al.10 showed that DCD children with balance problems had profound difficulties maintaining stability when the secondary cognitive task involved oral counting, auditory–verbal reaction time, or auditory–memory. On the other hand, we also see evidence of comparable DTCs for other combinations of motor and cognitive tasks like stationary cycling or manual placement while processing audible words11, Wii Fit ski Slalom task while counting designated sounds7, or simple visual discrimination while standing still12. Hence, under low levels of motor and/or cognitive complexity, dual-task interference effects may not differ between DCD and TD groups13. Similarly, the rate of motor learning and retention on a simple motor task – e.g., rhythmic stepping – can be quite similar for DCD and TD groups14. Conversely, high complexity tends to exacerbate performance costs for DCD8.

One aspect of daily performance that has been ignored in dual-task studies of DCD involves obstacle crossing, an important and pervasive (adaptive) locomotor skill for children, e.g., stepping over play and other objects left on the floor, or stepping over/on curbs and sidings15. The extant literature on the normative development of obstacle crossing while processing a secondary cognitive task4,16,17 is a logical starting point. Here, there is strong evidence of lower dual-task interference in healthy young adults (compared with younger groups) and relatively high interference in young children, particularly on lead leg clearance when crossing an obstacle4; adults do not need alter clearance appreciably when dual-tasking, unlike children. Moreover, Gill et al.16 have observed more subtle age effects. Here, younger children showed more motor variability (stride length, walking velocity), especially under dual-task conditions, compared with older children, suggesting a gradual unfolding of cognitive-motor integration and gait consistency under dual-task constraints. However, no developmental study of dual-tasking has evaluated gait kinematics or kinetics post-obstacle clearance, a phase in movement regarded as critical to gait stability and safety. The only available kinetic data on locomotion in DCD concerns single-task running. Chia et al.18 showed lower knee extensor torque during stance and, as a result, less knee power absorption and ankle power generation. As well, stride length was reduced and stance period prolonged in DCD. With gait abnormalities of this type in DCD, combined with cognitive control issues8, it is likely that relatively high DTCs will be evident on gait metrics in both crossover, and post-obstacle phases.

The broad aim of our study was to examine complex locomotor-cognitive dual-task performance in DCD, one involving obstacle crossing. Specifically, we assessed the kinematics and kinetics of gait during obstacle crossing and post-obstacle, performed under single and dual-task conditions; this was with a view to better understanding gait stability and aspects of propulsion over the entire course of movement which bears on the maintenance of dynamic balance and safety. We were particularly interested in the pattern of dual-tasks effects as a function of the complexity of the secondary cognitive task, in this case, visual discrimination. We predicted significant (cognitive-motor) dual-task deficits in children with DCD at crossover and following landing, reflecting issues with the maintenance of balance and propulsion immediately following a cognitive (secondary) task load.

Methods

Sample

Thirty-five children with DCD (21 male) and 75 TD (38 male), aged 7 to 12 years, participated in this study. Both groups were divided into younger (7 to 9 years old: 21 DCD, 35 TD) and older (10 to 12 years old: 16 DCD, 40 TD) cohorts, the breakdown described briefly in Table 1. Power analysis revealed that a sub-group sample of 16 achieved moderate-to-high power under the assumption of large group effects19.

The project was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Palacký University, and local school authorities. After that, an informed consent was signed by the parents or legal guardians of the children and oral assent was provided by each child. All procedures of this study were conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Children were recruited from four local primary schools and classified as DCD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5-TR)20. Children were screened using the Movement Assessment Battery for Children, Second Edition (MABC-2)21 and were identified as DCD if they scored below the 16th percentile (Criterion A). The impact of motor coordination difficulties on daily activities and academic performance (Criterion B) was evaluated by teachers using the MABC-2 checklist21 with the 16th percentile as a cut-off score. School psychologists reviewed the medical and behavioral records of each child to verify that criteria C and D were met.

Data acquisition

Testing was conducted in the gait lab of Faculty of Physical Culture, Palacký University Olomouc. The test space was a 10-m walkway with a “hurdle” obstacle. The obstacle consisted of three poles, two bases and two connectors for the poles. The poles were 150 cm long. The height of the obstacle was standardised to 30% of leg length for each child and it was placed in the middle of the walkway. There were two force platforms (Kistler Group, Winterthur, Switzerland, sampling rate 1,000 Hz) embedded in the walkway, one in front of and the other immediately behind the obstacle (Fig. 1). An eight-camera Vicon Vantage V5 motion capture system (Oxford Metrics, UK, sampling rate 200 Hz) was used to derive kinematic variables. Before data acquisition, the child was equipped with a microphone (Sennheiser, Wedemark, Germany) and retroreflective markers were placed on the skin according to the Calibrated Anatomical System Technique lower-limb model22 and on the obstacle in order to record its position in measurement space. Foot markers were placed on the dorsal surface of the distal phalanx of the hallux, on the area of the nail bed of the hallux, and on the posterior aspect of the calcaneus.

Experimental set-up with the obstacle and two force plates.

Cognitive task

A secondary visual discrimination task was employed as a cognitive task. Pictures of a bunny or a fox were projected via a data projector on the walkway. Children were tested under two conditions, simple and complex-inhibit.

For the simple condition (projection of bunny), they were asked to report verbally as quickly as possible the side of presentation of each stimulus (left or right). For the complex condition (projection of fox), they were asked to report the opposite side to that presented (complex-inhibit). Outcomes measures were success rate (number of correct answers / total number of stimuli) and mean response time (time between stimulus presentation and initial registration of the vocal response).

Motor task

The locomotor task involved straight-line walking on a path while stepping over an obstacle at its mid-point. Children were asked to continuously walk through the whole walkway while crossing the obstacle placed in the middle. Kinematic variables describing motor task performance included clearance height, step length and step width. Kinetic variables included the velocity of centre of pressure (COP) and peak values of the ground reaction force (GRF) components during specific time intervals (see data processing).

Locomotor-cognitive dual-task

The two dual-task conditions (or combined cognitive and motor tasks) were labelled locomotor-cognitive simple (DTS) and complex-inhibit (DTCI) dual tasks. Children walked on a path and at the final heel strike before the obstacle step over pictures of a bunny or a fox were projected behind the obstacle.

Procedure

The whole testing procedure was divided into three blocks of data acquisition. The first block included the assessment of single cognitive task. It was followed by one block of a single motor task and one block of dual task. After receiving instructions, children were allowed a few practise trials before data acquisition to familiarise themselves with the tests. Sufficient time for rest was included between blocks.

For the first block, single cognitive task was performed. Children stood at the start of the walkway in a comfortable posture and were given 16 continuously appearing pictures for simple and complex-inhibit conditions in a random order. The rate of appearance of conditions was 1:1.

For the second block, single motor task was performed. Before data acquisition, children were asked to perform several trials of walking for familiarisation so that they were able to cross the obstacle and place only the leading foot onto the force platform installed behind the obstacle. No instructions regarding the leading foot were given to children. After familiarisation, five walk trials were performed and recorded. Kinetic data were only considered from the platform behind the obstacle.

For the third block, a combination of tasks was employed in eight trials. The appearance of cognitive task was triggered at the final heel strike before the obstacle step over. The force platform placed in front of the obstacle was used as a trigger device. The rate of appearance of cognitive task conditions was 1:1.

Data processing

The kinematic data were processed using Vicon Nexus 2.10 software, and the 3D coordinates of the foot and obstacle markers were exported in CSV format. The clearance height and step length variables were calculated using Python 3.12. The position of the obstacle was calculated as the mean value of the anteroposterior and vertical positions of both obstacle markers. Clearance height was defined as the instantaneous height of the hallux marker above the obstacle during obstacle crossing. Step length and step width were defined as the anterior-posterior (length) and medial-lateral (width) distance between the hallux markers during last double support phase before crossing the obstacle and first double support phase after crossing the obstacle.

GRF and COP movement data were filtered using a 4th order bidirectional low-pass Butterworth filter with a cut-off frequency of 30 Hz23. The stance phase was considered to be a time interval in which the value of the vertical component of GRF (vGRF) exceeded 20 N. Specific kinetic outcomes, considered as indicators of performance, included the first and second peaks of vGRF, braking and propulsion peaks of the anterior-posterior GRF component and times to reach these peaks (Fig. 2). The first vertical GRF peak represents the impact force during landing immediately after obstacle clearance and reflects the ability to absorb load and control deceleration. The braking peak of the anterior–posterior GRF reflects the deceleration of forward momentum following foot contact, while the propulsion peak and the second vertical GRF peak reflect the re-acceleration of the body and the effectiveness of push-off during gait continuation. Delays in the timing of these peaks indicate prolonged stabilization demands and a more cautious post-obstacle strategy.

Kinetic variables related to landing, braking and propulsion.

COP movement was characterised by the mean velocity in medial-lateral (ML COP velocity) and anterior posterior directions (AP COP velocity) across the stance phase. Although COP is not a direct measure of body sway, its velocity reflects the magnitude and frequency of neuromuscular adjustments required to control posture and forward progression during gait, and therefore is used as an indirect indicator of dynamic postural control24. For statistical analysis, values from all trials in each condition were averaged.

Statistical processing

Data processing was performed using Statistica (version 14, TIBCO software Inc.). All variables showed normal data distribution (confirmed by Kolmogorov-Smirnov test) except success rate for the cognitive task. The effects of the between-subject factors, Group (DCD, TD) and Age (7 to 9, 10 to 12), within-subject factor of Condition (single task, dual-task simple, dual-task complex-inhibit), and group*condition interaction were assessed by three-way mixed Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Interactions reflect the differential effect of condition (single, dual simple and dual complex-inhibit) as a function of Group as such, these effects mirror group differences on dual-task cost metrics. P values smaller 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Effect size was estimated using partial η2 with the following thresholds: 0.01 small, 0.06 medium, and 0.14 large effect. For pairwise comparison of conditions, we used LSD Fisher post hoc tests. Group differences on success rate were evaluated using Mann Whiney U tests and effect size estimates using coefficient r (0.1 small, 0.3 medium and 0.5 large). In addition, an analysis of the effect of gender was performed for all variables where a significant effect of group, age, condition or group*condition interaction was previously found. For this gender comparison Mann-Whitney U test was used in each group (DCD, TD).

Results

Cognitive task

Success rate was comparable between DCD and TD children across all tasks (single simple, single complex-inhibit, dual simple, and dual complex-inhibit) and age groups. In all the cases, mean values were greater than 80%. The only significant difference between groups was found in younger children (DCD 84%, TD 92%) for the single task, simple condition (p = 0.025, r = 0.34).

On response time a significant effect of group (p = 0.017, η2 = 0.052), age (p < 0.001, η2 = 0.226) and condition (p = 0.002, η2 = 0.044) was found. TD children were faster to respond than DCD, and older were faster than younger. For the effect of condition, post hoc tests showed significantly slower response in DTCI compared to all other conditions, but only in TD children (Table 2).

Group effect

The one significant effect for group (DCD vs. TD) was found for ML COP velocity (p = 0.005, η2 = 0.075) indicating higher velocity for DCD relative to TD.

Age effect

There were significant age effects on step length (p < 0.001, η2 = 0.198) and those variables that characterized landing, namely: first vertical peak, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.112; time of first vertical peak, p = 0.009, η2 = 0.061; braking peak, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.126; and AP COP velocity, p = 0.004, η2 = 0.075. In summary, older children had significantly shorter step length, smaller and later force peaks during landing, and greater AP COP velocity than younger children.

Condition effect

Dual-task condition (3: ST, DTS, & DTCI) had a significant effect on obstacle crossing performance (clearance height: p < 0.001, η2 = 0.072; step length: p = 0.015, η2 = 0.039), landing (first vertical peak: p = 0.005, η2 = 0.047; braking peak: p < 0.001, η2 = 0.071), and propulsion (time of propulsion peak: p < 0.001, η2 = 0.077).

Significant Group*Condition interaction was found for Time of propulsion peak (p = 0.001, η2 = 0.063) and AP COP velocity (p = 0.006, η2 = 0.047). Gender effect

Of those parameters that showed significant main effects of group, age, condition, or a group*condition interaction, the effect of gender was significant within the DCD group, only. Specifically, gender differences were identified in clearance height during DTCI (p = 0.037, r = 0.35), time of first vertical peak during both DTS (p = 0.002, r = 0.52) and DTCI (p = 0.031, r = 0.36), as well as the time of propulsion peak during both STM (p = 0.003, r = 0.49) and DTCI (p = 0.022, r = 0.38). Greater values in height clearance and smaller values in time of first vertical peak and propulsion peaks were found in females compared to males.

Discussion

The present study examined how children with DCD and TD peers perform a complex locomotor-cognitive dual-task involving obstacle crossing. We were particularly interested in the impact of the secondary cognitive task on obstacle clearance, and the ability to control landing, balance and propulsion. Informed by multiple-resource sharing theory25, we predicted that the visually-mediated cognitive processing demands would compete for a discrete amount of planning/control resources and, thereby elicit a cost in performance on one or both tasks under dual-task conditions, compared with the single task26. Furthermore, we expected that DTCs would be greater for children with DCD compared with TD because of their well-documented deficits in both motor control and cognitive control7,8. Our comparison of DCD and TD groups showed that children with DCD had comparable gait kinematics during the crossover phase and were able to perform the cognitive task as accurately as their TD peers; however, they needed more time to solve the cognitive task and their post-obstacle kinematics suggested some difficulties controlling the landing and associated propulsion, exacerbated by dual-task constraints. These important findings are consistent with their difficulties in predictive motor control; specifically, a reduced ability to anticipate the consequences of their own action during the critical post-obstacle phase appeared to compromise dynamic stability, necessitating a more tentative landing and propulsion. The following Discussion will elaborate on these performance issues in DCD.

Group-related effects on motor outcomes

Among the variables assessing obstacle clearance and post-obstacle control (landing and propulsion), the primary group-related effect was observed on ML COP velocity, which was significantly elevated in DCD children. Increased ML COP velocity is widely recognized as a key indicator of impaired dynamic balance during locomotion, particularly deficits in frontal plane stability and poor ankle joint control27. Excessive lateral sway during gait not only compromises postural stability but may also increase the energetic cost of locomotion by requiring greater corrective effort to re-establish balance following perturbation27.

Obstacle clearance

In the context of obstacle clearance, both groups of younger children reduced their clearance height in the simple dual-task compared with the single locomotor task. Post hoc analysis of the condition effects (DTS versus DTCI) also showed a longer step length under the DTCI condition for younger children with DCD and older TD, and higher clearance under DTCI for the older DCD group. Interestingly, the added inhibitory control demands of the secondary task did not alter obstacle clearance for either group. This was not consistent with our initial hypothesis about disrupted clearance height with increased cognitive load. Our findings differ to those seen for healthy young adults who show reduced toe-obstacle clearance of the trailing limb during a visuospatial attention task during the approaching phase of obstacle crossing, but increase clearance during a visual Stroop task while approaching and crossing an obstacle28. They attributed this pattern to the increased cognitive demands and working memory load imposed by the Stroop task.

Post-obstacle motor control when dual-tasking

In the post-obstacle phase, children with DCD (esp. younger) showed delayed propulsion and reduced velocity of the centre of pressure in an anterior-posterior direction (AP COP). In contrast, TD children showed increased AP COP velocity during dual-task conditions, although their propulsion remand unaffected. This pattern suggests that TD children may adaptively reduce braking force peak under cognitive load, thereby preserving forward momentum. For children with DCD, the observed reduction in propulsion may reflect a broader deficit in gait automatization10 or challenges in dividing attention7 between motor and cognitive tasks. Similar developmental trends were reported by Boonyong et al.4, who highlighted age-related differences in postural control under dual-task conditions. The delayed propulsion observed in DCD children may result from a strategic allocation of attention to maintenance of postural stability at the expense of re-accelerating forward motion.

The trajectory for obstacle negotiation likely involves anticipatory planning (i.e., forward modelling) initiated before the final heel strike preceding the obstacle. In contrast, the phase immediately following obstacle clearance — when foot placement and propulsion require more online adjustments — may be most susceptible to dual-task interference.

Consistent with this interpretation, Deconinck et al.29 reported that post-obstacle errors in DCD are often caused by trail foot contact. They concluded that children with DCD struggle more with dynamic balance control than with visually guided gait adaptations. Our findings support this view, demonstrating increased AP COP velocity during the first step after the obstacle clearance, suggesting that compensatory adjustments were needed to maintain postural stability.

Age effects

Significant age-related differences were found across both cognitive and motor domains. Older children, compared with younger, showed shorter response time on the cognitive task, indicating improved processing speed. In the locomotor task, they exhibited shorter step lengths, reduced braking forces and a delayed first peak in vertical ground reaction force during landing, as well as higher AP-COP. These findings suggest that older children adopt a more effective strategy for obstacle crossing, characterized by diminished braking and more rapid post-obstacle progression. This age-related progression in movement strategy is consistent with findings by Vallis and McFadyen30, who reported that children and adults employ distinct coordination patterns when executing anticipatory locomotor adjustments tailored to the goal of the locomotor task and the environmental constraints. They also reported that children rely more heavily on visual input than adults during a transient change in travel direction as evidenced by more stable head positioning prior to obstacle circumvention, likely to facilitate enhanced visual sampling during dynamic movement. Furthermore, prior research indicates that with increasing age, children walk more regularly under dual-task conditions26. However, dual-task interference remains more pronounced in children compared to young adults, particularly when navigating obstacles as opposed to walking on level ground4. Finally, substantial evidence suggests that younger children exhibit greater motor variability than older children, with this variability further exacerbated under dual-task conditions16.

Cognitive performance: a novel effect of complexity and prioritisation

With respect to cognitive performance, the absence of significant dual-task interference suggests that both DCD and TD groups allocated sufficient attentional resources to maintain normative cognitive performance under dual-task conditions. Notably, children with DCD exhibited slower response times during a simple visual discrimination task (i.e. identifying the side of stimulus presentation without processing object identity), indicating a cautious or conservative response strategy, even when task demands were low. This response pattern suggests that children with DCD are capable of performing as accurately as TD children under dual-task conditions, while sacrificing speed. A similar pattern has been reported in another study. They found group difference (DCD vs. TD) in single cognitive task (counting task), but no difference under motor-cognitive dual-task conditions7.

Task prioritization may underlie the observed cognitive–motor trade-offs. Both DCD and TD children tend to preserve cognitive performance under dual-task conditions, while differential costs manifest primarily on selected gait metrics8. However, it is important to note that obstacle crossing presents a greater postural challenge than level walking, making gait stability a higher priority, particularly for children with balance impairments4. Previous developmental studies have demonstrated that children tend to prioritize the motor task when their bodily equilibrium is challenged on a demanding dual-task, and reduce their body sway even when instructed to focus more on the cognitive task1. Children with DCD are especially likely to adopt such stability-preserving strategies including reduced walking speed, prolonged double support time and increased reliance on online visual feedback31. In our study, both groups appeared to prioritize speed equally during the more complex cognitive condition, suggesting that under certain circumstances, children with DCD may shift their priority from motor stability in favour of cognitive performance. It follows that we hypothesise that children with DCD may incur increased motor-related errors (especially in the post-obstacle phase) when prioritizing complex cognitive tasks.

Although obstacle crossing is a well-practised and moderately complex motor task for most children, the broader dual-task literature consistently demonstrates that higher levels of task complexity11 and difficulty12 amplify dual-task interference. While no significant group differences emerged in the kinematic variables of obstacle crossing itself, our findings revealed significant main and interaction effects in the post-obstacle phase, specifically on metrics related to dynamic balance and propulsion. These results highlight the need for future studies to examine a broader range of locomotor activities across parametrically varied levels of cognitive and motor complexity to further clarify the mechanisms of dual-task interference in children with and without DCD.

Effect of gender

Although previous literature has reported gender-related differences in motor task performance32, the effects observed in our study were minimal. Significant gender differences emerged only within the DCD group, specifically for clearance height, time to first vertical ground reaction force peak, and time to propulsion peak. Based on our prior findings for these variables, we propose that the balanced proportion of females and males in the older DCD group may have contributed to slightly shorter times to both the first vertical and propulsion peaks across the combined older cohort (i.e., DCD and TD children). This shift may have attenuated the observed differences between younger and older groups, irrespective of diagnostic classification.

Limitations

We identify two main limitations with our study—one sampling related, and the other to do with task representativeness. First, the smaller number of girls in the older DCD group may limit generalizability. However, this ratio of DCD boys and girls among children aged 11 to 15 years seems to be representative for Moravian region of the Czech Republic33. Potential effect of gender is discussed in previous paragraph. Second, the secondary task was presented discretely, which may not fully represent continuous cognitive loads in daily life. We acknowledge that testing under both types of condition (discrete and continuous) would provide a full representation of the types of activity that make up the daily activities of children in their environment—e.g., crossing a pathway or a perceived gap in road traffic while also attending to a secondary visual stimulus or cognitive task. A range of such activities provides a more ecologically valid examination of dual-task competency in children with and without DCD.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrated that children with DCD show specific dual-task deficits in the control of dynamic balance and propulsion during the post-obstacle phase, while successful crossing and cognitive accuracy remain intact. These findings extend current understanding of locomotor-cognitive interaction by showing that attentional load selectively impairs post-obstacle stability in children with DCD. Practically, this suggests that rehabilitation and motor training should target the postural recovery phase of gait under conditions of divided attention to enhance safety and functional mobility in everyday environments.
