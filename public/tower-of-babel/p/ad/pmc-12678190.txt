The psychological implications of forced adoption: A scoping review
Authors: Mary O'Connor, Dearbhla Moroney, Orla Muldoon, Elaine Rogers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Forced Adoption is an under-researched phenomenon with impactful psychological implications for those affected. In the context of an identified gap in empirical research on forced adoption, this scoping review aimed to systematically chart empirical studies and distinct grey literature on the psychological impact of forced adoption on birth mothers and adoptees. The databases PsycINFO, CINAHL, SAGE, Scopus, ProQuest, Sociological Abstracts and Google Scholar were used to screen for operationally defined search terms that align with the research question. Following title and abstract screening (k = 2515) and full-text screening (N = 61), a final selection of 27 studies met the inclusion criteria, emphasising the scarcity of empirical research in this area. The studies reviewed were conducted in the United States (N = 13), the United Kingdom (N = 1), New Zealand (N = 2), Australia (N = 3), Canada (N = 4), Colombia (N = 1), South Korea (N = 1), Ireland (N = 1), and Scotland (N = 1). The findings of this review indicate that the existing evidence base leans more heavily toward studies exploring birth mothers' experiences (n = 18) than studies exploring adoptees' experiences (n = 9) and qualitative studies (N = 23). The psychological implications of forced adoption reported for birth mothers include grief and loss, social stigma and isolation, trauma and emotional distress and lack of autonomy at the time of adoption. Adoptees report challenges navigating subsequent trauma and attachment difficulties, identity loss, lack of belonging and experiences of social isolation and otherness. This review indicates the need for silent voices to be amplified and practices to be critically reviewed. It also emphasises opportunities for methodological and theory-informed advancements.

Introduction

Forced adoption, also known as forced family separation or coerced adoption, refers to the removal of a child from their biological parent/parents (Higgins et al., 2016), often occurring under duress or systemic coercion, resulting in the placement of a child for adoption against the parents' wishes (Albert & Mulzer, 2022), including cross-cultural placements (Vinke, 2020). Historically, pressure and stigma toward unmarried mothers stemmed from societal, cultural, political, and religious beliefs, particularly in communities of lower socioeconomic status (Koffman, 2011). Following periods of conflict, economic instability, and social upheavals, specific groups like unmarried mothers and those facing socioeconomic challenges have faced systemic coercion to relinquish their offspring (Australian Department of Social Services, 2012; Joint Committee on Human Rights, 2022). For example, the literature describes the stigmatisation of illegitimate maternity and how society deemed these ‘wayward’ and ‘deviant’ women as a threat and a social problem (Earner-Byrne, 2022).

Concepts and terminology in the literature within the context of forced adoption can misrepresent and inaccurately portray the experiences of survivors. This can lead to invalidation and re-traumatisation (Scottish Government, 2023a). Several recent studies are now focusing on contested terminology within the context of forced adoption and reflecting on the commanding influence of language on people and communities where stigmatisation at an individual and community level was born out of inaccurate terminology that misconstrued the experience of survivors (McGregor et al., 2022; Devaney et al., 2025). For example, use of the term “home” to refer to mother and baby institutions does not recognise survivors lived experience and can elicit feelings of invalidation; while the term “institution” or “prison” provides a closer description of meaning. Similarly, terminology such as “consent” and “relinquishment” masks the true nature of forced adoption practices and misrepresents survivors' lived experiences (Kenny et al., 2012; Kenny et al., 2015).

Past practices of forced adoption can result in lifelong consequences for those directly involved, particularly for mothers and adoptees, as well as other family members (Higgins, 2010). These consequences were evidenced in both the short and long term across the lifespan, and the limited available research stresses converging outcomes for birthparents and adoptees who experienced forced adoption.

In the short term, birth mothers experienced acute trauma, silencing, disrupted opportunities to bond with their child, social stigma and institutional coercion (Cuthbert & Quartly, 2012; Higgins, 2010) while in the long term, birth mothers reported unresolved grief, enduring mental health difficulties, and fractured maternal identity (Fisher, 2012; Kenny et al., 2012, Kenny et al., 2015). In the short term, forced adoption can create attachment and developmental risks for adoptees, in particular for babies placed in institutional care settings with limited caregiving and attunement (Bowlby, 1969; Howes & Spieker, 2008), while adoptees struggled with confusion relating to personal identity, attachment difficulties in relationships and emotional distress (Meyer et al., 2020). Early experiences of neglect can alter an individual's relational framework and impact their sense of self. For example, inconsistent care can alter how children view themselves and their relationships (Baroncelli et al., 2025).

Cultural displacement can be a significant issue for adoptees, and later in life, this severance from biological and cultural roots can elicit a sense of existential confusion and loneliness (Melina, 2018). Adoptees may find relational and social experiences challenging, where sensitivity to rejection can impact their ability to form healthy and trusting relationships (Lifton, 1994; Olson, 2013; McLamb et al., 2022).

Despite these findings, many adoptees show resilience and creativity in overcoming challenges and navigating their lives (Brodzinsky et al., 2022; Australian Institute of Family Studies, 2019; Gunnar & Reid, 2019).

Forced adoption research has garnered less attention in comparison to other areas of research within the field of adoption, such as research on attachment and adoption or identity and adoption formation (Kenny et al., 2012; Murphy & Kelly, 2020). There appears to be less research conducted due to the sensitivity and complexity of this topic (Melina, 2018). Although Ireland has a complicated history with forced adoption, it is only recently that the government has provided legal recognition through Ireland's Mother and Baby Homes Payment Scheme Act in 2023 (Department of Children, Equality, Disability, Integration and Youth, 2024). Comparable state responses that acknowledged the past wrongdoings include Australia's National Apology for forced adoptions (Gillard, 2013) formal government apologies in Scotland (Scottish Government, 2023b) and also in Wales (Welsh Government, 2023). In 2018, Canada's Senate also recommended a federal apology and reparations (Canada. Parliament. Senate. Standing Senate Committee on Social Affairs, Science and Technology, 2018).

The paucity of research on this topic, particularly the impact of forced adoption on birth mothers and adoptees' psychological, relational and social well-being, remains a challenge in accurately informing future practice and policy. Research has historically emphasised adoptive parents and systems level stakeholders, while birth mothers and adoptive children's voices can be excluded from adoption discourse (Landers, 2023; Morgan et al., 2019). This is especially problematic in cases of forced or involuntary adoption, where power, stigma, and limited reproductive autonomy intersect (Wexler et al., 2023).

A systematic approach to evidence synthesis and identifying research gaps is warranted at this stage of literature development. This scoping review aims to systematically map the current evidence base focusing on forced adoption and the psychological implications for birth mothers and adoptees. This review also aims to capture the key concepts and terminology in the literature to provide clarity on how this topic is represented and understood.

Lastly, this review focused on studies relating to forced adoption where children were relinquished without consent, often within the context of coercive social and institutional pressures. It did not include studies exploring non-consensual adoptions arising from child protection interventions.

The following research questions will guide the review and support in systematically identifying key aspects of evidence, psychological impacts, and conceptual definitions in the literature. Method

A scoping review systematically analyses literature on a broadly defined research question and summarises what has been reported on the research subject (Armstrong et al., 2011; Munn et al., 2018). There was no known existing published systematic or scoping review on this topic. This scoping review was informed by the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for scoping reviews (Peters et al., 2020) and Arksey and O'Malley's (2005) five-stage framework, including (1) formulating the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) charting the data and (5) reporting the findings. A descriptive analytical approach was applied, in line with Arksey and O'Malley (2005) and the Joanna Briggs Institute (Peters et al., 2020). Key study characteristics (aims, design, participants and findings) were systematically charted with categories informed by established psychological frameworks (e.g., attachment theory, trauma theory, stigma theory, identity theory). Findings were then categorised into domains in line with the research questions to guide interpretation of the psychological implications reported. To ensure quality reporting, the study used the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist (Tricco et al., 2018). A written protocol outlining the search strategy, inclusion and exclusion criteria, evidence selection, and synthesis plan guided the review process.

Search strategy

The following electronic databases were searched to identify published articles: PsychInfo, CINAHL, Sage, Scopus, Sociological Abstracts, ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global, and Google Scholar. Search terms were rigorously developed through a preliminary literature review, consultations with academic librarians, and iterative testing to ensure broad coverage and relevance (see Table 1). The search terms included both thesaurus (MeSH) and keyword synonyms for ‘single mother,’ and ‘forced adoption’ using a search strategy developed and piloted in PsycINFO before being refined and expanded for use across all databases. A Google Scholar search using the search terms was also run (screening limited to the first 250 results). Source of evidence selection

Database searches were conducted between March and May 2024. All located citations were compiled and imported into the Rayyan reference management system after the initial searches. Any duplicate entries were removed, and a screening process based on title and abstract and aligned with the review's inclusion criteria was performed within Rayyan by the primary author (MOC). The second reviewer (DM) reviewed 20 % of the identified records. Discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through deliberation. Following the removal of duplicates, 51 studies were retrieved for full-text screening. The PRISMA flowchart reports the process (Page et al., 2021, Fig. 1). Following the process of title and abstract screening, the selection process' rigour was assessed using Cohen's Kappa statistic to measure inter-rater reliability(see Appendix A). Near-perfect agreement (k = 0.835) between the reviewers was calculated for abstract and title screening. For full text screening, moderate agreement (k = 0.44) was calculated and reported between the two reviewers. Conflicts were resolved through discussions with the research team (see Appendix B).

Data reporting & quality review

The first author extracted data using a customised data form created on Microsoft Word. Extracted data from included studies was inserted into the customised data form, which included study findings, study aim, language study methodology, study participants, and reference information (see Appendix C).

The Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP, 2018; see Table 4) qualitative research checklist was used to assess risk of bias and methodological quality. The Mixed Methods Assessment Tool (MMAT; Hong et al., 2018; see Table 5) was applied for mixed methods studies, and an adapted risk of bias and quality assessment tool informed by the case study quality assessment (Baxter & Jack, 2008) for single case studies was utilised (see Table 6).

Findings

Review findings

A descriptive approach was used to examine and compare the evidence by describing critical areas of investigation in empirical research, identifying outcome measures, summarising patterns in the geographic locations of research participants studied, research designs, and reporting the psychological implications of forced adoption on birth mothers and adoptees. Secondary data were extracted from each study and study characteristics were charted, including study aims, study design, participants, and key findings (see Table 3). To answer the review questions and provide an understanding of the types of research conducted on forced adoption, the psychological implications for adoptees and birth mothers, and descriptive analysis was used to explore the data in primary research studies included in this review. Categories were broad and directly derived from the research question, while others were created inductively in response to patterns within the data. Study characteristics

The characteristics of the studies are outlined below to answer the first review question regarding the availability and types of evidence on forced adoption in the literature. A total of 27 papers were included in this review. Out of the included studies, 18 primarily explored birth mothers' perspectives (Castle, 2014; Chambers, 2006; Cole, 2013; Cushman et al., 1993; Edwards, 1995; Edwards, 1999; Fisher, 2012; Harris & Whyte, 1999; Heinemann, 2019; Henney et al., 2007; Kim, 2014; Lauderdale & Boyle, 1994; March, 2015; McNamara et al., 2021; Pengilley, 2003; Samuels, 2013; Wall, 2014; Weinreb & Konstam, 1995) and nine studies primarily on adoptees experiences (Anderson, 2020; Blake et al., 2023; Branco et al., 2022; Dalton et al., 2022; Danzak et al., 2021; DeBetta, 2022; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015; Hampton, 1997; Topfer, 2010). The included studies were conducted in the United States (n = 13; Anderson, 2020; Cushman et al., 1993; Danzak et al., 2021; DeBetta, 2022; Edwards, 1995; Edwards, 1999; Fisher, 2012; Henney et al., 2007; Heinemann, 2019; Lauderdale & Boyle, 1994; Samuels, 2013; Topfer, 2010; Weinreb & Konstam, 1995), Australia (n = 3; Castle, 2014; Cole, 2013; Pengilley, 2003), Canada (n = 4; Chambers, 2006; Hampton, 1997; March, 2015; Wall, 2014), Ireland (n = 1; McNamara et al., 2021), the United Kingdom (n = 1; Dalton et al., 2022), New Zealand (n = 2; Blake et al., 2023; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015), Scotland (n = 1; Harris & Whyte, 1999), Colombia (n = 1; Branco et al., 2022) and South Korea (n = 1; Kim, 2014). The combined sample size of all the included studies consisted of approximately 677 participants. Some sample size data could not be extracted due to the reporting of methodologies. For example, some studies did not specify the exact sample size (Chambers, 2006; Edwards, 1999; Harris & Whyte, 1999; Heinemann, 2019; Pengilley, 2003; Wall, 2014). Two-thirds (67 %) of the studies accounted for birth mothers. In comparison, one third (33 %) explored adoptees' experiences. 13 of the articles included in this review were published in sociological-based journals (Branco et al., 2022; Castle, 2014; Cole, 2013; Cushman et al., 1993; Dalton et al., 2022; DeBetta, 2022; Edwards, 1999; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015; Harris & Whyte, 1999; Henney et al., 2007; Kim, 2014; March, 2015; Weinreb & Konstam, 1995), seven studies were published in interdisciplinary journals such as a nursing journal (Lauderdale & Boyle, 1994) a history journal (Chambers, 2006; Heinemann, 2019), a medical history journal (Wall, 2014), a journal reporting on Indigenous peoples (Blake et al., 2023), a law journal (Samuels, 2013), two journals on qualitative reporting (Anderson, 2020; Danzak et al., 2021), and a transpersonal studies journal (Topfer, 2010). Four studies were non-published dissertations and theses (Cole, 2013; Edwards, 1999; Fisher, 2012; Pengilley, 2003), while one study was published in a psychology-based journal (Hampton, 1997). Out of the sociological-based journals, the most significant proportion were adoption-based journals (Dalton et al., 2022; DeBetta, 2022; Hampton, 1997; Harris & Whyte, 1999).

Key factors relating to forced adoption reported in the literature by birth mothers and adoptees

To address the second research question of the psychological implications for mothers and adoptees who have experienced forced adoption, key aspects and factors relating to forced adoption reported in the literature were explored from both the birth mother's and adoptee's perspectives.

Psychological implications of forced adoption on birth mothers

Grief and loss

All of the studies reported the effects of forced adoption on birth mothers' mental health. Across these studies, 14 studies described the profound experiences of grief and loss (Chambers, 2006; Cole, 2013; Cushman et al., 1993; Edwards, 1995; Fisher, 2012; Heinemann, 2019; Henney et al., 2007; Lauderdale & Boyle, 1994; March, 2015; McNamara et al., 2021; Pengilley, 2003; Samuels, 2013; Wall, 2014; Weinreb & Konstam, 1995) experienced by birth mothers. For example, McNamara et al. (2021) described intense, unresolved grief, while Cushman et al. (1993), Lauderdale and Boyle (1994) Weinreb and Konstam (1995) reported on the sustained grief experienced for birth mothers for many years following the forced separation with their child leading to mental health difficulties such as depression and anxiety. In two studies, the impact of grief and regret was intensified by the societal pressures and lack of support women faced during and after the adoption process (Chambers, 2006; Wall, 2014); however, some studies noted that these effects diminished in response to more inclusive and open adoption practices (Cushman et al., 1993). Cole (2013) described the “pathological grief” that led to suicidal ideation and suicide attempts for some birth mothers.

Trauma symptomology and emotional distress

Six studies reported emotional distress and symptoms of trauma because of forced adoption (Cole, 2013; Edwards, 1995; Fisher, 2012; Henney et al., 2007; Pengilley, 2003; Weinreb & Konstam, 1995). Three studies described trauma symptomology experienced by birth mothers following the loss of their child (Cole, 2013; Edwards, 1995; Pengilley, 2003). For example, Edwards (1995) describes how birth mothers experience flashbacks and depression, while Pengilley (2003) and Cole (2013) reported symptoms among birth mothers such as emotional avoidance and psychogenic amnesia. Fisher (2012) described the long-term implications of forced adoption, noting that birth mothers suffer from chronic depression, substance abuse, and difficulties forming relationships. Cole (2013) described that several mothers were formally diagnosed with post-traumatic stress disorder and mothers reported this starting with the shock of not being allowed to see their baby at birth.

Isolation and social stigma

12 studies discussed the role of social stigma in heightening mother's psychological distress (Chambers, 2006; Cole, 2013; Castle, 2014; Edwards, 1995; Fisher, 2012; Heinemann, 2019; Kim, 2014; Lauderdale & Boyle, 1994; McNamara et al., 2021; Pengilley, 2003; Wall, 2014; Weinreb & Konstam, 1995). Kim (2014) and March (2015) described how societal judgement reinforces the emotional burden and adds to the isolation of single mothers' experiences. Similarly, Henney et al. (2007) and Weinreb and Konstam (1995) reported and described the secrecy and stigma surrounding birth mothers and how this contributes to shame, which leads to silence about their experience of forced adoption. Cole (2013) reported on how birth mothers who lost their babies felt denigrated and shamed by those who worked in the hospital setting, such as social workers and medical professionals. Wall (2014) described how one birth mother was described as “the bad girl” and did not have a single visitor during her hospital stay. Among mothers who kept their babies in this study, it was reported that support from the mother's own parents was perceived as a buffer to the stigma cast on them from institutional stigma.

The illusion of choice

Ten studies described mothers' lack of autonomy in the decision-making process and how this manifested into feelings of powerlessness (Chambers, 2006; Castle, 2014; Cole, 2013; Edwards, 1995; Fisher, 2012; Lauderdale & Boyle, 1994; McNamara et al., 2021; Pengilley, 2003; Wall, 2014; Weinreb & Konstam, 1995). Each of these studies discussed the societal expectations placed upon mothers, which restricted choices. For example, Chambers (2006) described how “too many people have told me that” when referring to a birth mother being influenced to give her baby up. Five studies described the long-term psychological implications of a lack of autonomy in the decision-making process at the time of adoption, such as long-term feelings of helplessness (Castle, 2014), powerlessness (Cole, 2013), unresolved grief and trauma symptoms (Fisher, 2012; McNamara et al., 2021) and self-blame and feelings of isolation (Weinreb & Konstam, 1995).

Coping mechanisms

10 out of 18 included articles discussing birth mothers' experiences reported on coping mechanisms, both positive and negative (Castle, 2014; Cole, 2013; Edwards, 1995; Harris & Whyte, 1999; Henney et al., 2007; Lauderdale & Boyle, 1994; March, 2015; McNamara et al., 2021; Pengilley, 2003; Samuels, 2013). Two studies reported on the positive impact of structured counselling sessions, which allow birth mothers to express unresolved grief and process their “relinquishment” in a supportive environment (Fisher, 2012; Pengilley, 2003), while group settings were noted to have a positive impact on birth mothers, with two studies reporting that peer support allowed birth mothers to connect with others who understood their unique experience (Harris & Whyte, 1999; Henney et al., 2007). Several studies also reported maladaptive coping mechanisms such as alcohol and substance misuse, suicide attempts (Cole, 2013), emotional detachment (March, 2015), avoidance and psychogenic amnesia (Pengilley, 2003) and dissociation, repression and suppression (McNamara et al., 2021).

Psychological implications of forced adoption on adoptees

Identity and belonging

Seven of the nine studies concerning adoptees' experiences focused on the challenges associated with personal identity and belonging (Blake et al., 2023; Branco et al., 2022; Dalton et al., 2022; Danzak et al., 2021; DeBetta, 2022; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015; Topfer, 2010). Of these seven studies, six discussed the phenomenon of cultural displacement and being removed from cultural roots. (Blake et al., 2023; Branco et al., 2022; Danzak et al., 2021; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015; Topfer, 2010). For example, Haenga-Collins and Gibbs (2015) described “walking between the worlds” without a clear sense of belonging to their adoptive or birth family (p.71). Beyond cultural displacement, two studies reported complex relationships with personal belonging and identity (Dalton et al., 2022; DeBetta, 2022).

Social isolation and “otherness”

Five studies reported adoptees' experience of “otherness” (Blake et al., 2023; Branco et al., 2022; Dalton et al., 2022; Danzak et al., 2021;Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015) Physical and cultural differences contributed to feelings of isolation and difference. For example, three studies reported the impact of being visibly different from their family and peers (Branco et al., 2022; Danzak et al., 2021; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015), while Dalton et al. (2022) reported that adoptees experienced never fitting into their adoptive family system . One study reported on biological recognition and the “surrealness” of seeing someone who looked like them (Branco et al., 2022), while Blake et al. (2023) described the confusion surrounding their adoptive heritage and how their skin colour and physical differences left them feeling othered.

Trauma symptomology and attachment difficulties

Out of the nine studies focused on adoptees, six of the studies specifically address trauma symptoms and or attachment difficulties (Anderson, 2020; Blake et al., 2023; Dalton et al., 2022; Danzak et al., 2021; DeBetta, 2022; Topfer, 2010).Three studies reported insecure attachment patterns (Dalton et al., 2022; DeBetta, 2022; Topfer, 2010), while in two studies, adoptees reported a heightened sensitivity to rejection (Anderson, 2020; Danzak et al., 2021). One study reported attachment-related difficulties linked to cultural disconnection (Blake et al., 2023). In relation to trauma, Dalton et al. (2022) described how four out of the five adoptees who participated in their study experienced trauma such as physical, emotional and sexual abuse. Anderson (2020) described experiencing trauma in response to her biological mother's death and used storytelling as a source of healing. DeBetta (2022) described how through writing she was able “to see the many disruptions trauma had caused throughout my life” (p. 127).

Coping mechanisms

Out of the nine studies, six reported mechanisms deemed supportive (Anderson, 2020; Blake et al., 2023; Branco et al., 2022; Dalton et al., 2022; DeBetta, 2022; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015). Three studies reported that the use of cultural reclamation was positive for their well-being (Blake et al., 2023; Branco et al., 2022; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015), while two studies identified the use of personal storytelling as a positive mechanism for healing (Anderson, 2020; DeBetta, 2022). Therapeutic interventions were cited in three studies (Dalton et al., 2022; Danzak et al., 2021; Topfer, 2010), while three studies described the use of peer and community supports (Blake et al., 2023; Danzak et al., 2021; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015). Three studies also reported on the use of harmful coping mechanisms to manage the experience of forced adoption (Blake et al., 2023; Dalton et al., 2022; DeBetta, 2022).In contrast, Two studies described the use of silence (Dalton et al., 2022; DeBetta, 2022) to avoid the implications of adoption, while Blake et al. (2023) reported the use of humour or deflection as a mechanism to manage racial or identity-based microaggressions.

Definitions and concepts of forced adoption in the literature

To answer the final research question, definitions and concepts in the literature were reviewed and reported on.

Varied terminology and framing emerged in the literature on the concept or phenomenon of forced adoption (see Fig. 2). For example, only three studies (Castle, 2014; Cole, 2013; Edwards, 1995) used the term “forced adoption”, while in contrast to this, the term “relinquishment” or “relinquished” featured in 24 out of the 27 studies (Anderson, 2020; Blake et al., 2023; Castle, 2014; Chambers, 2006; Cole, 2013; DeBetta, 2022; Edwards, 1995; Fisher, 2012; Harris & Whyte, 1999; Hampton, 1997; Haenga-Collin & Gibbs, 2015; Heinemann, 2019; Henney et al., 2007; Kim, 2014; Lauderdale & Boyle, 1994; March, 2015; McNamara et al., 2021; Pengilley, 2003; Samuels, 2013; Topfer, 2010; Wall, 2014; Weinreb & Konstam, 1995). Out of these 24 studies, four studies (Castle, 2014; Fisher, 2012;McNamara et al., 2021 ; Pengilley, 2003) challenged the use of the term relinquishment. McNamara et al. (2021) discussed how the term relinquishment does not capture the experiences of coercion and systemic pressures experienced by birth mothers, while Castle (2014) described how the term “relinquishment” implies a voluntary act of willing decision, which contrasts sharply with the realities experienced by birth mothers.Fig. 2Definitions and concepts in the literature.Fig. 2

Definitions and concepts in the literature.

Five studies referenced the terms “coerced” or “coercion” to describe the experiences of birth mothers (Cole, 2013; Edwards, 1995; Fisher, 2012; Heinemann, 2019; Topfer, 2010), while one study used “consent takers” (Cole, 2013). Additionally, four studies (Cole, 2013; Henney et al., 2007; March, 2015; McNamara et al., 2021) included a reference to “lost”, “loss”, or “loss of”, one participant in a study used the term “kidnapped” to describe her experience (Edwards, 1995), three studies used the word “taken” in reference to the loss of a child (Blake et al., 2023; Heinemann, 2019; Lauderdale & Boyle, 1994). Kim (2014) introduced the term “subjectification” to characterise the experience of birth mothers, while Pengilley (2003) used “no decision” and Cole (2013) used the terms “stolen babies” and “stolen generation”.

Psychological theories

Several psychological theories were referenced throughout the studies; however, their application varied. Some theories were discussed in an explicit manner, while other theories were referenced implicitly. These psychological theories provide a valuable framework for the complex experiences of birth mothers and adoptees. Attachment theory was discussed in two studies (Dalton et al., 2022; Fisher, 2012) and provides insight into the emotional impact of early separation with a primary caregiver and the long-term effects for birth mothers and adoptees. While this theory was discussed more explicitly in Dalton et al.'s (2022) study, where the Adult Attachment Interview (AAI) was used, Fisher (2012) acknowledges attachment theory through her discussion in terms of bonding and separation dynamics.

Several studies referenced narrative identity theory (Anderson, 2020; DeBetta, 2022), cultural identity theory (Blake et al., 2023; Danzak et al., 2021; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015) and developmental identity theory (March, 2015). These theories offer an essential insight into how forced adoption practices can impact an individual's sense of self and identity over time and provide an insight into how some birth mothers or adoptees may need to negotiate their sense of self over time. For example, March (2015) described how early relational separation impacts an individual's sense of identity over the lifespan.

Elements of grief and loss theory are referenced implicitly through the lens of adoption openness (Henney et al., 2007), while Weinreb and Konstam (1995) discuss the societal silence and lack of “societal cushioning” surrounding the loss. Concepts such as disenfranchised grief (Doka, 1989), are described more explicitly in two studies (McNamara et al., 2021; Pengilley, 2003), while the concept of ambiguous loss (Boss, 1999) is cited in a number of studies (Lauderdale & Boyle, 1994; McNamara et al., 2021; Weinreb & Konstam, 1995). Grief and loss theories are particularly important for understanding the emotional burden carried by adoptees and birth mothers, particularly disenfranchised grief (Doka, 1989), which explains grief that is unacknowledged or not socially recognised. This often occurred in cases of forced adoption, where social invisibility and silence was a dominant outcome. Similarly ambiguous loss (Boss, 1999) is viewed as one of the most stress evoking types of loss due to the absence of official confirmation, supportive rituals, and the failure to reach resolution (Boss, 2016).

Three studies discuss feminist theory (Hampton, 1997; Pengilley, 2003; Topfer, 2010), with one of these studies focusing on feminist post-Jungian theory (Topfer, 2010). Feminist theory is crucial for its explanation of the gendered experiences and power dynamics involved in forced adoption. The feminist perspective on post-Jungian theory reported in Topfer (2010) explores how forced adoption impacts an individual's identity lens but also disrupts their emotional and psychological well-being through a gendered lens.

The storm and stress model of adolescence (Hall, 1904), which describes adolescence as a time of emotional instability and turmoil was implied implicitly by Wall (2014) to emphasise how medical professionals viewed unmarried mothers as emotionally immature and deficient. Psychoanalytic labelling was also described, whereby young mothers were described as “neurotic” and who had “deficient egos”.

Discussion

This scoping review presents a unique mapping of 27 studies from nine countries (USA, Canada, Colombia, United Kingdom, New Zealand, Australia, Ireland, Scotland, and South Korea), including approximately 577 birth mothers and 101 adoptees. At the same time, data was also extracted from surrender documents from birth mothers. This scoping review answered three review questions: the first about the available evidence in the area of forced adoption, the second about the psychological implications of forced adoption on birth mothers and adoptees and the third about clarifying the key concepts, terminology and language in the area of forced adoption. The data also gives voice to the psychological implications of forced separation. Of the 27 studies included in this study, 23 used qualitative analysis, while four studies used mixed methods.

Qualitative studies provide a level of depth and richness into the experience of birth mothers and adoptees, while also providing space to explore the complexity of individual experience that go beyond statistical data (Agius, 2013). The concentration of qualitative methods is also advantageous in informing therapeutic interventions and advancing psychological knowledge (Chang et al., 2024; Levitt et al., 2022), which is a need within the context of forced adoption. Three case studies were included in this review (Anderson, 2020; DeBetta, 2022; Topfer, 2010). In this review, the deep, contextual understanding of forced adoption, illustrated through case studies (Anderson, 2020; DeBetta, 2022; Topfer, 2010), offered a unique view into each author's experience of forced adoption. Single case studies are suited to and indeed ‘invaluable’ in exploring critical, relevant and exceptional phenomena (Lim, 2024).

While the outcomes of the articles presented a rich and diverse collection of experiences, the following categories were curated. For birth mothers, grief and loss, emotional distress and trauma symptoms, social stigma and isolation, the illusion of choice and coping mechanisms were extracted from the data. For adoptees, cultural displacement, social isolation and “otherness”, trauma symptomology and attachment difficulties and coping mechanisms were identified from the data.

A consistent finding in the data was the loss experienced by birth mothers, which was comparable to bereavement (McNamara et al., 2021). Unlike typical grief or bereavement, several studies reported a feeling of disenfranchised grief by birth mothers, where their role and identity as mothers disappeared without societal or familial acknowledgement (Doka, 1989). Pengilley (2003) described how society's response through comments such as “you will have more children” exacerbated feelings associated with the loss, complicating their ability to process the separation. This supports the broader literature on grief, which describes that socially unsupported grief can compound the grieving process (Worden, 2018).

Birth mothers in these studies also experienced emotional distress and trauma associated with a sense of powerlessness about the loss of their autonomy (Cole, 2013; Heinemann, 2019; McNamara et al., 2021). Forced adoption often involves manipulation or a level of coercion, particularly manipulation by systems or institutions that shape birth mothers' experience of powerlessness and ongoing emotional distress (Higgins et al., 2016). The available literature on trauma would suggest that feelings of powerlessness heighten vulnerability to a complex recovery from trauma and lead to long-term psychological impacts such as depression and anxiety (Seok et al., 2020), while more agency or control throughout a traumatic experience can loosen the grip of trauma and support a healthier recovery (Thompson, 2021). Existing research supports these findings, identifying a range of psychological effects, including grief, identity disruption, anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (Kenny et al., 2012; Rickarby, 1995; Silveira et al., 2020).

This current review suggests a need for bespoke trauma-informed therapeutic resources such as group and individual therapy, which are important for helping birth mothers process and integrate their emotional experiences (Scottish Government, 2023a; Watkins, 2021). However, the specific support needs of those affected by forced adoption remain under-researched (Higgins et al., 2016). Funding difficulties have been cited as a significant barrier to the availability of resources for this population (Scottish Government, 2023b). These findings point to an urgent need for targeted therapeutic interventions, alongside increased awareness of existing support systems to better address the psychological impact of forced adoption. Community psychology initiatives such as advocacy and empowerment training, awareness campaigns that can reduce societal stigma (Grigoropoulos, 2022) and inform and educate the public about the long-term implications of forced adoption and collective initiatives such as healing arts (Scottish Government, 2023b) and storytelling can contribute to and counteract the long-held silences of survivors.

The findings for adoptees underscore the unique psychological challenges they face, particularly in relation to identity, attachment, and cultural belonging. One of the most striking insights from this review is the deep sense of cultural disconnection and the struggle for belonging that many adoptees face. The imagery of being ‘caught between worlds’ illustrates the confusion and lack of a clear place in either their adoptive or biological families (Blake et al., 2023; Branco et al., 2022; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015). This metaphor illustrates the identity struggles and feelings of not fitting in that adoptees often endure. It also echoes earlier studies that show how adoptees can feel alienated and uprooted, largely because their cultural continuity has been disrupted.

Adoptees also grapple with complex attachment difficulties, with studies included in this review reporting on attachment patterns often characterised by insecurity, a heightened fear of rejection, and a fractured sense of belonging (Dalton et al., 2022; DeBetta, 2022). Previous research on adoption and attachment difficulties indicates that adopted adults who display disorganised attachment styles as children can struggle with trauma symptomology and symptoms associated with disorganised attachment styles in adulthood (Karatzias et al., 2022).

While psychological difficulties are reported in the findings, the review also reports on adaptive coping mechanisms. For birth mothers, structured counselling sessions (Fisher, 2012; Pengilley, 2003) and peer support (Harris & Whyte, 1999; Henney et al., 2007) within a group context were conducive to healing. The use of personal storytelling (Anderson, 2020; Topfer, 2010) and reconnecting with cultural roots can be a life-changing experience for adoptees. Adoptees who experienced cultural displacement described reconnecting with their cultural heritage and finding their “Whakapapa” as a turning point on their journey, which instilled a sense of belonging (Blake et al., 2023; Haenga-Collins & Gibbs, 2015). Personal storytelling can be viewed as a symbiotic channel of healing. Anderson (2020) discusses how her story “let her silent voice ring”(p. 3799), and she describes this approach with the hope that it will provide “comfort” to other adoptees and birth mothers. Similarly, Topfer (2010) details how storytelling can break the closed adoption system's silence and helps her connect with others' stories. Personal storytelling potentially has a role in alleviating the hidden trauma experienced by adoptees. Experiences from adoptees in India, where biological connections are deemed superior to adoptive kinship, found that open conversation can elicit change, reinforcing findings from this current scoping review (Puhan, 2024).

Previous research on adoptees' experiences of coping suggests that adoptees who receive support by discussing and understanding their racial and ethnic identity, discussing their adoption experience in a group context and accessing support through tailored counselling services have more positive outcomes (Jones, 2019). Overcoming the problematic impact of early relational disruptions emphasises the role of resilience. While providing early trauma-informed care and stable caregiving environments could help individuals affected by forced adoption build resilience, ultimately improving their mental health and attachment patterns in adulthood (Jampaklay et al., 2024).

The use of language to describe forced adoption in the included studies was incompatible with the reality of the act, often masking the profound loss experienced. Linguistic cruelty, which involves a level of discriminatory intent which demeans, suppresses and marginalises groups overtly or subtly (Dubinsky & Starr, 2022) emerged alongside the practices of forced adoption. Words perpetuate societal judgement and stigma, and language used in the literature to describe survivors of forced adoption, such as “illegitimate”, “fallen woman”, and “unfit”, can serve to dehumanise and categorise individuals (Haslam, 2006). The psychological harm caused by linguistic microaggressions is acknowledged in the literature (Sue et al., 2007). Within the context of forced adoption, there was an emphasis on a child being saved or it was in their “best interest” to have a “real mother” (Cole, 2013). This language served to justify the practices that existed (Dubinsky & Starr, 2022) and in so doing perpetuated self-doubt, shame and cruelty.

Within this review, four studies (Castle, 2014; Fisher, 2012; McNamara et al., 2021; Pengilley, 2003) explicitly challenged the use of inaccurate language concerning experiences of forced adoption. The term “relinquishment” was heavily used; however, it failed to capture the coercion and systemic pressures birth mothers endured. Terminology shapes how experiences are understood and how narratives are perpetuated (Chestnut & Markman, 2018). Misrepresenting these experiences with terms like “relinquishment” can contribute to disenfranchised grief—a type of grief minimised by society—exacerbating mental health challenges and masking the trauma and coercive elements of these adoptions (Anderson, 2020; Doka, 1989). An Irish study on language and representations in institutions such as mother and baby institutions reinforced how terminology fails to reflect the unique lived experiences of birth mothers (McGregor et al., 2022). This gap between language and experience reflects how language in policy and social services often does not align with survivors' emotional and psychological realities. For example, the use of the term “relinquishment” can elicit a feeling of abandonment for adoptees, which can impact the ability to develop secure attachments in the future (Shakiba & Raby, 2021). Viewing these adoptions as forced or coerced reveals systemic injustices, which encourages empathy for birth mothers and enables adoptees to make sense of their heritage and identity.

Adoption as a child welfare intervention

While this review focused on the psychological harms associated with forced adoption, it is important to acknowledge adoption as a potentially positive child welfare intervention. Adoption can offer children safe and nurturing family environments, particularly when compared with institutional care environments (Selwyn & Quinton, 2004; Palacios & Brodzinsky, 2010). Children adopted earlier from deprived environments have similar social, psychological, social and cognitive outcomes when compared to children who live with their family of origin (Gunnar & Reid, 2019). At the same time, however, adoption should not be viewed uncritically. For example, framing adoption solely as a child welfare intervention risks oversimplifying complex experiences and overlooking the broader socio-political context (Newton, 2022).

Limitations

This scoping review charted and categorised the international literature on forced adoption. The inclusion of grey literature, such as the use of non-published dissertations and theses, adds depth to this review and studies that would not be otherwise included helped to form a more complete picture of the current research, which was significant for this study due to its complexity and its stance as an underexplored topic in the empirical literature. However, the review did not comprehensively synthesise government reports, media reports and commentaries. This exclusion may limit or overlook important narratives and insights that could enrich the understanding of forced adoption and its psychological implications.

While the inclusion of grey literature expands the scope of this review, it also welcomes potential biases due to the lack of peer review processes. In saying this, it is essential to consider the role of publication bias and how grey literature can counter this and offer a more comprehensive view of the literature (Paez, 2017).

Differences were observed in relation to the concepts and terminology in the literature.

For example, “mother and baby home” is a recognised term for homes where mothers were residents in Ireland; however, it is not used internationally. To mitigate this difficulty when extrapolating studies based on the research question, the authors sought input from two academic librarians to support accurately defining key search terms.

Despite searching the international literature, the review features a significant concentration of studies from Western countries, particularly the United States. Valuable viewpoints and insights from non-Westernised countries have potentially been prevented from being included due to the inclusion of English-only studies. This introduces a publication bias that risks reinforcing a Western-centric perspective of forced adoption. For example, in post-war South Korea, large-scale international adoptions often placed biological parents in situations where they felt they had no choice (Kim, 2009). At the same time, in China, the one-child policy has led to practices of involuntary forms of adoption (Cai & Feng, 2021). In some Southeast Asian countries, including Indonesia, there are adoption practices based on kinship care and cases of illegal adoption involving coercion (Loibl, 2025).

Implications for practice

Past literature emphasises the need for a public and statewide acknowledgement of past wrongs, harms and injustice caused by forced adoption practices (Higgins et al., 2016; Murphy & Kelly, 2020), while the current review reinforces the need for appropriate state responses. Formal apologies, such as those issued by the Australian Government in 2013 and similar moves in Ireland, have validated some mothers and adoptees, offering a foundation for healing (Scottish Government, 2023a).

Practitioners working with individuals who have experienced forced adoption should prioritise evidence-based, trauma-informed, culturally sensitive practices in their approach. This is inclusive of recognising signs of trauma, creating a safe therapeutic space and being competent in understanding the impact of this on an individual's mental health and wellbeing (Gunnar & Reid, 2019; Kenny et al., 2012).

To alleviate the impact of forced family separation, governments need to prioritise systems that comprise of mental health and social and legal supports (Scottish Government, 2023a). This involves building a structured approach of care that caters to the social, emotional, and practical support needs of the impacted families. It is important to note that terms like “relinquishment” or “surrender” don't truly capture the reality of coerced adoption: the language we use in clinical and policy discussions should be chosen with great care. Practitioners should strive to use language that honour birth mothers' and adoptees' lived experiences and emotions, promoting understanding and empathy in therapeutic and social service contexts (McGregor et al., 2022).

Mental health peer-led support groups have been demonstrated to have a modest but consistent level of efficacy (Smit et al., 2023). This finding implies that peer-led groups may be beneficial for reducing isolation, building emotional support, and facilitating recovery in general.

The focus on westernised studies carries important implications for practice. There is attention needed from mental health practitioners to adopt frameworks that account for cultural variation in experiences of trauma, stigma, and identity. Training for practitioners should therefore embed cultural competence alongside trauma-informed practice, equipping them to respond sensitively across different cultural contexts. There is an important role for policymakers to fund cross-cultural non-westernised studies to address the gaps discussed above. Additionally, policymakers need to establish legal safeguards to prevent coercion in adoption processes, ensuring informed consent is fully protected. Social workers also have a significant role in prioritising practices that ensure adoption related decisions are free from undue pressure or coercion.

Implications for research

The literature available on forced adoption undermines the gravity and significance of this worldwide phenomenon. Future research should be interdisciplinary and focus on the nuanced and impactful idiosyncratic experiences of forced adoption on both adoptees and birth mothers.

Upon evaluating the current research on this topic, a proposed research agenda to advance the current field on forced adoption and amplify survivors' stories would consist of the following actionable steps.

Methodologically, mixed methods studies should be employed because they can extrapolate results to larger populations where the depth and breadth of forced adoption can be captured. While most studies included in this review were assessed to be of sufficient quality, it is crucial that future research places emphasis on ensuring that studies are methodologically sound.

We sought to explore the experiences of birth mothers and adoptees. However, findings from conducting this review highlight the absence of natural fathers within the literature. Due to factors related to societal, political and research bias', natural fathers were often not included in discussions, were often absent, and natural mothers were deemed the primary caregivers who had experienced the loss (Wiley & Baden, 2005). Critical to this point is the maternal centric perspective and the historical issue of gender norms that positioned women front and centre and excluded men from parenting discussions, particularly in relation to unmarried parenthood (Clapton, 2003). Including natural fathers' voices would include an intentional shift and provide a more holistic perspective in relation to forced adoption's impact on all family members. While the study aimed to capture birth mothers' and adoptees' experiences of forced adoption, two-thirds (66 %) of the studies accounted for birth mothers, while approximately one-third explored adoptees' experiences. Given the current imbalance within the literature on adoptees, future research should focus on adoptees' experiences to create a more comprehensive understanding and provide greater depth in the field.

Future research would benefit from exploring cross-cultural perspectives to gain a deeper insight into differing cultural contexts, particularly due to the findings accentuating the impact of cultural displacement and how it impacted participants' lives; collaborative efforts with researchers and survivors in non-westernised contexts could reveal culturally weighted experiences of adoption trauma, which could enrich the global understanding of this phenomenon. Participatory action research (Lenette & Boddy, 2013), whereby individuals from marginalised or underrepresented groups actively engage in research, with a view for their input to be considered and their voices to be heard. This often leads to practical solutions based on the findings and should be considered moving forward within this field.

While current psychological theories such as attachment theory, trauma theory, and Eriksonian frameworks provide a foundation for understanding the fundamental psychological impact of forced separation, trauma, and identity formation, results from this review draw attention to the need for more nuanced, evolutionary models, and inter-disciplinary theory development. For example, while attachment theory addresses the impact of early separation in relation to a child and their primary caregiver, it does not capture the layered and multifaceted adoption trauma that can leak into an individual's experience of societal shame and secrecy, which are critical catalysts for emotional disconnection (Baden, 2016). Understanding survivors' experiences of forced adoption through the lens of social identity theory (Muldoon et al., 2021) could enhance knowledge in relation to how social identity dynamics influence the long-term impact of forced adoption. This approach fits with the experiences of birth mothers and adoptees, which are documented in studies included in this review, and emphasises the profound impact of identity transitions, particularly the loss of valued identities, while coping with the burden of a stigmatised identity, where the onset of social isolation and rejection can occur (Muldoon et al., 2019).

Ensuring research in this area prioritises participatory action will increase the value of survivors' perspectives to help direct and guide research priorities effectively in this area (Beresford et al., 2020). For example, Sweeney (2018) and colleagues emphasise the importance of participant involvement, which sheds light on methodological integrity in qualitative research.

To bring visibility to forced adoption practices, a multi-faceted and considered approach focusing on survivors' narratives is needed. This should prioritise perspectives that have received less attention, such as fathers, siblings and practitioners who were present while these practices were live. Efforts should also focus on advocacy concerning policy change and education for practitioners and the public, such as introducing forced adoption content in professional training for social workers, psychologists and educators.

Implications for policy

Government-led policies that include the provision of long-term mental health supports tailored to the psychological needs of survivors such as specialised counselling services and peer support networks need to be implemented to mitigate the psychological distress reported in this review. The inclusion of systemic therapy approaches that include the whole family, including adoptive families, would ensure more holistic support, addressing intergenerational impacts and fostering healthier relational dynamics.

Child welfare policies and adoption practices should be critically evaluated to ensure that coercive adoption practices are not tolerated from a government and policy level. This review illustrates how linguistic inaccuracies, namely using terms such as relinquishment and surrender, invalidate and mask the true nature of coercive practices. Instead, policy makers need to respect and utilise terms that accurately portray survivors' experiences such as coercive and forced.

An amendment to legislation that prioritises adoptees' access to their birth information needs to be enacted so that all individuals can reconnect with their biological origins if they desire. This fundamental right is restricted across jurisdictions at present. Additionally, survivor-led consultations would ensure that their experiences are highly valued when changes are being made to the creation of laws and support services for survivors of forced adoption.

Conclusion

The psychological implications of forced adoption reported for birth mothers include grief and loss, social stigma and isolation, trauma and emotional distress and lack of autonomy at the time of adoption. Adoptees report challenges navigating subsequent trauma and attachment difficulties, identity loss, lack of belonging and experiences of social isolation and otherness. Forced adoption is a complex, inter-generational, systemic phenomenon, with long last psychological implications.
