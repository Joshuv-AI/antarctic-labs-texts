Examining the Role of Behavioral Health Factors on Inpatient Traumatic Brain Injury Rehabilitation Outcomes Among Service Members and Veterans
Authors: Justin T. McDaniel, Jolie N. Haun, Julie McMahon-Grenz, Tali Schneider, Karen Skop, Sharon Barton, Roberto Sandoval, Kimberly Samson, Risa Nakase-Richardson, Kevin Wells, Mary Jo Pugh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo examine the association between behavioral health factors, specifically pretreatment alcohol and tobacco use, and traumatic brain injury (TBI)-related outcomes in the Intensive Evaluation and Treatment Program (IETP).DesignRetrospective longitudinal cohort design.SettingThe intervention and data collection were conducted at 5 Veterans Health Administration (VHA) sites (Florida, Texas, California, Minnesota, and Virginia).ParticipantsBehavioral health and TBI-related outcome data were collected from N=205 veteran and active-duty service member participants with mild-to-moderate TBI.InterventionsThe VHA IETP delivered interdisciplinary rehabilitation to veterans and service members with a history of mild-to-moderate TBI and related conditions, including posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), pain, impaired function, and adverse neurobehavioral symptoms.Main Outcome MeasuresThe exploratory outcome measures included the Neurobehavioral Symptom Inventory, the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5, the Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Pain Interference scale, and the Mayo-Portland Adaptability Inventory. Pretreatment tobacco and alcohol use behaviors were extracted from patient medical records. Multiple linear regression models were fitted to the data to test our hypotheses.ResultsAlcohol use was not shown to be associated with changes in any of the outcomes; however, tobacco use was associated with attenuated improvement in PTSD symptoms (p=.03).ConclusionsThis study suggests that pretreatment tobacco use may lessen the effects of long-term PTSD rehabilitation among patients with TBI.

Veterans and active-duty military service members experience higher rates of traumatic brain injury (TBI) than civilians.1 TBI, even when diagnosed as mild, is associated with higher rates of downstream posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), chronic pain, and other comorbidities.1 In response to the growing prevalence of TBI, the Veterans Health Administration (VHA) established the Polytrauma System of Care in 2005 to provide tertiary inpatient rehabilitation to service members and veterans (SMs/Vs) at 5 TBI rehabilitation centers. In 2008, the Postdeployment Rehabilitation and Evaluation Program was developed to address the growing need for comprehensive interdisciplinary care for complex mild-to-moderate TBI, particularly for those with co-occurring physical, cognitive, and behavioral health conditions. As demand for this model continued to grow, the Polytrauma System of Care met this need and expanded the model by developing the Intensive Evaluation and Treatment Program (IETP) in 2020.

The Partnered Evaluation Initiative of the IETP was established in 2021 to conduct evidence-based evaluations of the 5 IETP sites2,3 using implementation frameworks such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research4 and the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance framework.5 Given that (1) PTSD outcomes have shown considerable variability in previous analyses,6 and (2) the high prevalence of alcohol and tobacco use among SMs/Vs,7 we sought to examine the association of alcohol and tobacco use with symptom trajectories in the IETP. It has been reported that up to 93% of veterans with an substance use disorder have comorbid mental disorders.8 The strong association between substance use disorder and personal difficulties (interpersonal, professional, medical), sleep disturbances, chronic pain, and other medical conditions may impede healing that accompanies TBI rehabilitation.9,10

Given the disproportionately high prevalence of alcohol and tobacco use among SMs/Vs, particularly those with a TBI and co-occurring PTSD and chronic pain, understanding how these behaviors influence rehabilitation outcomes is critical. IETP has demonstrated improved neurobehavioral, pain interference, and reduced PTSD severity, but we have not yet characterized the extent to which alcohol and tobacco may alter symptom trajectories. This study aimed to examine the aforementioned issue to inform risk stratification and treatment planning and to recommend targeted interventions that maximize long-term outcomes and health benefits.

Methods

Study design and sample

We employed a retrospective, longitudinal, cohort, case-series design in order to examine the association of alcohol and tobacco use with TBI-related outcomes from baseline to discharge in the IETP. This work was conducted within a broader organization-wide, participatory, concurrent mixed-methods quality-improvement effort.3 Oversight was provided by the James A. Haley Veterans’ Hospital Research and Development Committee, which classified the activity as a nonresearch quality-improvement initiative.

Men SMs/Vs with a confirmed history of mild-to-moderate TBI were referred to the IETP by clinicians at military installations, military treatment facilities, or VHA hospitals. Program admission collectively required documentation of a TBI and the presence of ongoing symptoms extending beyond the expected acute recovery window.11

A convenience sample of 50 patients evaluated at each site with mild-to-moderate TBI was included in this quality-improvement project between July 1, 2022, and May 30, 2024. Of the 250 patients enrolled in the IETP, 205 had complete data on alcohol and tobacco use at baseline. Participants were treated for sequelae related to cumulative head trauma. The sample largely consisted of active-duty Special Operations Forces, who are known to experience elevated risk of TBI.12

Measures

Clinicians assisted patients in completing several standardized outcome measures at baseline and at discharge, all of which are valid for patients with TBI. These included the Neurobehavioral Symptom Inventory (NSI),13 the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5),14 the Patient Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Pain Interference scale (PROMIS-PI),15 and the Mayo-Portland Adaptability Inventory (M2PI).16 These instruments are widely used in TBI rehabilitation research because they capture symptoms and functional challenges commonly associated with TBI and are core measures in the IETP.17 For all measures, lower scores reflect fewer symptoms and better overall functioning.

Demographic, military, and clinical characteristics were extracted from electronic health records. Demographic and military variables obtained included age and the number of combat deployments, factors known to influence recovery after TBI. Additionally, IETP staff obtained pretreatment risk information at admission (ie, provider note in the electronic health record) on self-reported alcohol consumption and tobacco use that met criteria for unhealthy alcohol or tobacco use disorder (ie, yes or no).18, 19, 20 Importantly, tobacco and alcohol use were prohibited during treatment in the IETP.

Data analysis

Two sets of linear regression models were estimated to examine the associations between alcohol and tobacco use and change scores of the 4 TBI-related outcomes (NSI, PCL-5, PROMIS-PI, M2PI). The primary independent variable in these models was either alcohol or tobacco use. We controlled for age, the number of deployments, and the baseline score of the dependent variable in each model.

Results

The sample comprised SMs/Vs with a history of TBI (N=205). Most participants reported pretreatment alcohol use (74.19%), whereas approximately one-fifth (20.22%) endorsed pretreatment tobacco use (table 1). The average age of the cohort was 41.18 years (SD, 6.29), and participants reported a mean of 7.15 deployments (SD, 3.30). Across all clinical measures, participants showed substantial improvement from baseline to discharge. Neurobehavioral (NSI) symptom severity decreased from a mean NSI score of 37.82 (SD, 13.53) at baseline to 23.87 (SD, 11.64) at discharge. PTSD symptoms, as measured by the PCL-5, also declined notably from 32.35 (SD, 17.05) at baseline to 21.97 (SD, 15.21) at discharge. Pain-related interference, measured by PROMIS-PI, improved from 18.51 (SD, 5.46) at baseline to 15.55 (SD, 5.17) at discharge. Alcohol use results

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine whether age, number of deployments, alcohol use, and baseline symptom severity were associated with change scores on the NSI, M2PI, PCL-5, and PROMIS-PI. Baseline symptom severity was consistently associated with change scores, such that higher baseline symptom levels were associated with greater reductions in symptoms over time. Specifically, baseline NSI scores significantly predicted NSI change (β=−0.52, p<.001), with the model explaining 36.0% of the variance (F4,181=25.47, p<.001). Similarly, baseline PCL-5 scores were associated with PCL-5 change (β=−0.47, p<.001), explaining 30.1% of the variance (F4,181=19.47, p<.001). Baseline M2PI scores were significantly associated with M2PI change (β=−0.64, p<.001), yielding the strongest overall model fit, accounting for 40.4% of the variance (F4,181=30.62, p<.001). Baseline PROMIS-PI scores were also significantly associated with PROMIS change (β=−0.55, p<.001), explaining 28.8% of the variance (F4,181=18.27, p<.001). Age and alcohol use were not significantly associated with outcomes in any model (p's>.05). The number of deployments was not significantly associated with changes in NSI, PCL-5, M2PI, or PROMIS-PI scores. Tobacco use results

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine whether age, the number of deployments, tobacco use, and baseline symptom severity were associated with changes in the NSI, M2PI, PCL-5, and PROMIS-PI. Across all 4 models, baseline symptom severity was consistently associated with change scores, such that higher baseline scores were associated with greater reductions in symptoms over the course of treatment. Specifically, baseline NSI scores were significantly associated with NSI change (β=−0.52, p<.001), with the model explaining 36.5% of the variance (F4,178=25.57, p<.001). Baseline PCL-5 scores were significantly associated with PCL-5 change (β=−0.47, p<.001), accounting for 31.7% of the variance (F4,178=20.63, p<.001). Baseline M2PI scores were significantly associated with M2PI change (β=−0.61, p<.001), with the model explaining 40.5% of the variance (F4,178=30.26, p<.001). Baseline PROMIS scores were significantly associated with PROMIS change (β=−0.57, p<.001), accounting for 30.3% of the variance (F4,178=19.36, p<.001). Tobacco use was significantly associated with change in PTSD symptoms, such that participants who reported tobacco use demonstrated less improvement in PCL-5 scores than nonusers (β=5.22, p=.028). Tobacco use was not significantly associated with changes in NSI, M2PI, or PROMIS scores. Age and the number of deployments were not significantly associated with any outcome. Discussion

The results of this study show that SMs/Vs participating in the IETP experienced meaningful improvements in their symptoms, regardless of whether they used alcohol or tobacco. On average, participants reported fewer neurobehavioral symptoms, less PTSD distress, reduced pain interference, and better daily functioning at discharge. Alcohol use did not appear to be associated with how much patients improved over time. Although SMs/Vs who reported alcohol use started the program with worse neurobehavioral symptoms, their overall recovery was similar to that of those who did not drink, suggesting that baseline alcohol use at unhealthy levels may not interfere with the benefits of IETP and further supports the IETP requirement to abstain from alcohol during program admission.

While patients who did and did not smoke initiated the program with similar symptom levels, patients who used tobacco exhibited slower change on 1 outcome: PTSD symptoms. Patients who reported smoking at baseline showed attenuated improvement in their PTSD scores compared with nonsmokers, even though their progress on other outcomes, such as neurobehavioral symptoms, pain, and functional ability, was similar. This data suggests that pretreatment tobacco use may be associated with differences in the PTSD-related aspects of TBI rehabilitation.

These findings coincide with prior studies examining the neurological trajectories of patients seeking TBI rehabilitation. Research has consistently shown that individuals with mild TBI tend to recover more quickly when they are not engaged in chronic tobacco use, likely due to smoking’s negative effects on cerebral blood flow, inflammatory processes, and neurocognitive healing.10 Our observation that tobacco use is associated with a slower reduction in PTSD symptoms aligns with broader evidence that smoking can interfere with neurological and psychological recovery. Furthermore, previous studies have also found that alcohol use does not meaningfully influence self-reported TBI rehabilitation outcomes, which may suggest that alcohol does not directly alter the trajectory of symptom improvement in structured treatment settings but may impact long-term outcomes.21 Collectively, these findings support the emerging understanding of how behavioral health may function as a clinically relevant modifier of recovery patterns in patients undergoing intensive rehabilitation for TBI.

The implications of this study extend to TBI rehabilitation in general and treatment of PTSD in particular. Given that pretreatment tobacco use was associated with attenuated improvement in PTSD symptoms during participation in the IETP, patients who smoke and plan to attend inpatient TBI rehabilitation may have an incentive to quit smoking long before admission. Furthermore, providers may have a stronger justification for discussing the potential impact of tobacco use on recovery before rehabilitation begins. Integrating evidence-based smoking cessation interventions into TBI care, such as the contingency management interventions already sponsored within the VHA,22 may assist patients in maximizing the benefits of PTSD-focused interventions. Rehabilitation teams could also screen for tobacco use more systematically and tailor treatment plans to include education on how smoking affects neurological and psychological healing. By proactively embedding cessation support prior to TBI rehabilitation and within the VHA rehabilitation framework, clinicians may enhance long-term outcomes and ensure that patients receive the full therapeutic benefit of programs such as IETP.

Study limitations

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. The sample predominantly consisted of men, veterans, and active-duty service members (especially Special Forces); thus, results may not be generalizable to other TBI and PTSD populations. This study was further limited by the lack of a control group, which may reduce confidence in our understanding of the effects of the IETP on the 4 measured outcomes. Other limitations include the study’s reliance on self-reported outcome measures, which may suffer from social desirability bias or recall bias, and the lack of longitudinal tobacco and alcohol use (which are currently prohibited during treatment in the IETP).

Preinjury substance use may also represent an important unmeasured factor in this study that could influence recovery trajectories after TBI. Prior research indicates that alcohol and tobacco use before TBI are associated with alterations in neuroinflammatory response, cerebral perfusion, stress reactivity, and cognitive reserve, which can persist well beyond acute intoxication or withdrawal periods and potentially shape downstream neurobehavioral and psychiatric outcomes.9 In particular, chronic alcohol exposure prior to injury is associated with impaired neural recovery and greater vulnerability to affective dysregulation after TBI, even when abstinence is achieved during rehabilitation. Similarly, preinjury tobacco use is associated with long-term changes in executive functioning, emotional regulation, and PTSD symptom persistence after brain injury. Although substance use was prohibited during IETP participation, pre-TBI substance exposure may be an important neurobiological or behavioral risk that influences responsiveness to PTSD-focused rehabilitation. This is especially relevant given evidence that substance use prior to injury is associated with greater PTSD chronicity and symptom severity independent of postinjury use. Consequently, the absence of longitudinal substance-use data prior to TBI in this study limits the ability to disentangle whether observed differences in PTSD symptom trajectories, particularly in participants who used tobacco prior to treatment, reflect the effects of preexisting neurobehavioral vulnerability rather than treatment-period behaviors alone. Future studies would benefit from incorporating detailed preinjury substance-use histories to better isolate modifiable risk factors and inform targeted prerehabilitation interventions.

Conclusions

To conclude, this study suggests that while SMs/Vs participating in the IETP experience substantial improvements across neurobehavioral, psychological, pain, and functional outcomes, pretreatment tobacco use may attenuate PTSD symptom reduction during rehabilitation. Pretreatment alcohol use, although associated with worse neurobehavioral symptoms at baseline, did not alter the trajectory of recovery. These findings shed light on the importance of addressing tobacco use before and during TBI and PTSD treatment settings and suggest that integrating smoking cessation (or other tobacco use cessation) support into interdisciplinary rehabilitation may enhance patient outcomes. Additional research is warranted to refine our understanding of how behavioral health factors influence recovery after TBI.
