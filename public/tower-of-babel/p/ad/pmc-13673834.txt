Appendiceal-source Klebsiella pneumoniae iliac pseudoaneurysm with rupture risk: a three-case series highlighting post-EVAR reassessment
Authors: Bingjing Chen, Jingyu Ji, Yiqing Li, Qin Li, Chao Yang, Yujie Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfective native aorto-iliac aneurysms and pseudoaneurysms are uncommon but life-threatening vascular infections. Case 2 involved a distal abdominal aortic infected pseudoaneurysm with rupture near the inferior mesenteric artery origin; persistent peri-aortic fluid after EVAR required laparoscopic hematoma evacuation and lesion resection. Background

Infective native aortic aneurysms are rare vascular infections characterized by microbial destruction of the arterial wall, pseudoaneurysm formation, rapid enlargement, and risk of rupture [1, 2]. When infection involves the abdominal aorta or iliac arteries, the clinical presentation may be nonspecific, including fever, abdominal pain, low back pain, weight loss, leukocytosis, and elevated inflammatory markers [1–3]. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) is central to diagnosis because it can show saccular or irregular aneurysm morphology, peri-aortic or peri-iliac soft-tissue inflammation, gas, fluid collections, abscess, contrast extravasation, and rupture-related changes [2, 3].

Management is difficult because two problems coexist: immediate hemorrhage-risk control and durable infection eradication. Open surgery with radical debridement has traditionally been considered the most definitive approach for suitable patients, but emergency open reconstruction may be poorly tolerated in unstable patients or those with severe inflammation, malnutrition, anemia, sepsis, or major comorbidities [1, 4, 5]. EVAR can rapidly exclude a rupture-prone aneurysm with less immediate physiologic stress, and it has been reported in selected infected aortic aneurysms [6–8].

However, EVAR does not remove infected thrombus, an abscess cavity, contaminated retroperitoneal tissue, or an adjacent source of infection. A satisfactory result at completion angiography therefore does not confirm that the infection has been controlled, and technical success must be followed by deliberate reassessment. Persistent fever, pain, inflammatory-marker elevation, positive cultures, peri-graft fluid or gas, and later device-related complications should prompt a renewed search for residual infection and its source [3, 4, 9].

We present three patients with rupture-prone infective native aorto-iliac aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm in whom emergency EVAR provided initial stabilization but subsequent management was guided by post-EVAR reassessment. Case 1 involved a gas-containing left common iliac pseudoaneurysm associated with Klebsiella pneumoniae infection and later evidence of perforated appendicitis with retroperitoneal contamination, supporting contiguous gastrointestinal or retroperitoneal spread. Cases 2 and 3 involved infective aorto-iliac aneurysms in which the same diagnostic problem arose in different clinical settings.

Case 1

A 66-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes mellitus presented with lower abdominal pain for more than 20 days, which had worsened during the preceding 3 days. On admission, the body temperature was 36.6 °C, heart rate was 80 beats/min, respiratory rate was 20 breaths/min, and blood pressure was 120/75 mmHg. Physical examination showed lower abdominal tenderness without diffuse peritonitis. Initial laboratory testing showed leukocytosis and systemic inflammation: white blood cell count 16.07 × 109/L, neutrophils 14.73 × 109/L, hemoglobin 90 g/L, C-reactive protein (CRP) 183 mg/L, and procalcitonin (PCT) 0.35 ng/mL (Fig. 1). Albumin was 32.1 g/L and fasting glucose was 16.4 mmol/L.

Clinical course and inflammatory-marker trends in Case 1. Trends in C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, white blood cell count, and body temperature are shown across the hospital course. Shaded bands indicate systemic antimicrobial therapy (empirical piperacillin-tazobactam and culture-guided meropenem); cefuroxime sodium was given as intraoperative surgical prophylaxis for the second-stage operation. Dashed vertical lines indicate emergency EVAR and the second-stage laparoscopic appendectomy with retroperitoneal debridement and drainage (postoperative day 6)

CTA demonstrated irregular enlargement of the left common iliac artery, surrounded by heterogeneous low-density inflammatory tissue and multiple gas foci, strongly suggesting an infected left common iliac artery pseudoaneurysm (Fig. 2). Additional findings included pelvic fluid, presacral soft-tissue edema, mild left hydronephrosis, left ureteral inflammatory change, and a thickened bladder wall. Repeat assessment showed progressive aneurysm enlargement with concern for impending rupture. Open debridement and reconstruction were considered very high risk because of infection, anemia, hypoalbuminemia, diabetes, and rupture risk.

Representative CTA findings in Case 1. Preoperative images show a gas-containing peri-iliac inflammatory mass and left common iliac pseudoaneurysm with features of impending rupture. Post-EVAR images show exclusion of the lesion without endoleak. Follow-up images show the residual peri-iliac inflammatory change that prompted source investigation and the subsequent appendiceal and retroperitoneal source control

Emergency endovascular exclusion was therefore performed for rupture-risk control. The procedure included EVAR, iliac covered stent placement, and left internal iliac artery coil embolization. The left internal iliac artery was embolized with two 10-mm and two 8-mm coils. An Endurant II 23-13-124 main body and an Endurant II 16-10-124 iliac limb were deployed to cover the left common iliac lesion. Completion angiography showed satisfactory stent-graft configuration, preserved flow, and no contrast filling of the aorto-iliac aneurysm sac.

Despite technical success, infection remained a concern. Blood anaerobic culture grew Klebsiella pneumoniae. Susceptibility testing showed sensitivity to imipenem, meropenem, ceftazidime, cefoperazone-sulbactam, and ceftazidime-avibactam, while tetracycline resistance was observed. Urine bacterial and fungal cultures were negative. In the documented inpatient course, broad-spectrum piperacillin-tazobactam was given empirically before emergency EVAR, and meropenem was used after EVAR as culture-guided therapy for the Klebsiella pneumoniae infection while persistent infection was being reassessed. Cefuroxime sodium was administered intraoperatively as surgical prophylaxis for the second-stage source-control operation. The patient continued to require close post-EVAR reassessment because endovascular exclusion did not address the source of infection. On postoperative day 6, persistent fever and rising inflammatory markers prompted laparoscopic exploration, which identified perforated appendicitis with periappendiceal purulence and retroperitoneal contamination adjacent to the iliac lesion. Laparoscopic appendectomy with retroperitoneal debridement and drainage was then performed, supporting contiguous appendiceal-source spread to the iliac artery as the most plausible mechanism. This causal pathway was inferred from anatomic proximity, gas-containing peri-iliac inflammation on CTA, operative findings, positive blood culture, and exclusion of a urinary source; molecular strain-level confirmation between the appendiceal focus and blood isolate was not performed, so causality should be interpreted as strongly suggestive rather than definitive. After antimicrobial adjustment and source control, symptoms and inflammatory markers improved, and follow-up CTA showed lesion exclusion without endoleak.

Case 2

A man in his 60s presented with a one-month history of abdominal pain, low back pain, low-grade fever, poor intake, and weight loss of approximately 10 kg. Emergency CTA showed a large distal abdominal aortic pseudoaneurysm with partial thrombosis, heterogeneous density, partial contrast filling, and a rupture site near the origin of the inferior mesenteric artery (Fig. 3). The lesion measured approximately 7.7 × 5.4 cm on cross-sectional imaging, and the aneurysm displaced adjacent bowel anteriorly.

Representative CTA findings in Case 2. Preoperative images show distal abdominal aortic infected pseudoaneurysm and rupture-risk features; follow-up images show peri-aortic fluid prompting laparoscopic source control and subsequent improvement

Given the high risk of further rupture and the patient’s poor general condition, emergency EVAR with an aortic stent-graft and bilateral iliac stents was performed for rapid exclusion. After EVAR, abdominal and back pain improved only partially, and low-grade fever with inflammatory-marker elevation persisted. Repeat CTA showed a peri-aortic fluid collection around the stented segment. Multidisciplinary reassessment favored residual retroperitoneal infection or infected hematoma rather than adequate infection control after EVAR alone.

The patient subsequently underwent laparoscopic retroperitoneal lesion resection with hematoma evacuation. Operative cultures were negative, likely because of previous antibiotic exposure. Antimicrobial therapy was continued across the interval between emergency EVAR and second-stage source control, but the exact total duration was individualized and incompletely documented in the retrospective record. After second-stage source control, fever resolved, inflammatory markers improved, and follow-up imaging showed reduction of the peri-aortic fluid collection.

Case 3

A man in his 50s presented with persistent fever, low back pain, and weight loss. CTA showed an infrarenal infected aortic pseudoaneurysm measuring 48.7 × 47.2 × 48.9 mm, with bilateral common iliac involvement and marked peri-aortic inflammation (Fig. 4). Initial blood cultures were negative. Worsening low back pain suggested progression and impending rupture, and emergency EVAR with iliac limb extension was performed to exclude the lesion and stabilize the patient.

Representative CTA and procedural findings in Case 3. Preoperative images show infrarenal infective pseudoaneurysm with bilateral iliac involvement; follow-up images demonstrate right iliac limb occlusion; retrieved thrombotic material shows neutrophilic inflammatory infiltration

The patient initially improved and was discharged with oral antibiotics. During follow-up, recurrent fever developed. CTA demonstrated occlusion of the right iliac stent limb, and blood culture grew Escherichia coli. This combination raised concern for delayed infection-related or device-related complication rather than isolated mechanical limb occlusion.

Percutaneous thrombectomy was performed. Fibrinous debris was retrieved, and pathological examination showed abundant neutrophilic infiltration, supporting an inflammatory or infectious component within the occluded segment. Fever resolved after thrombectomy combined with antibiotic therapy, repeat blood cultures became negative, and the patient remained symptom-free at approximately one-year follow-up. No recurrent bacteremia, recurrent fever, or new graft-related complication was documented during the available follow-up interval.

Focused narrative literature review

A focused narrative literature search was performed in PubMed, Google Scholar, and Crossref through 1 July 2026 using combinations of the following terms: “infective native aortic aneurysm”, “mycotic aneurysm”, “infected aortic aneurysm”, “iliac artery pseudoaneurysm”, “endovascular aneurysm repair”, “EVAR”, “Klebsiella pneumoniae”, “Enterobacterales”, “appendicitis”, “retroperitoneal abscess”, “FDG-PET/CT”, “Internet of Surgical Things”, “vasa vasorum”, “3D printing”, and “troubleshooting”. We prioritized English-language cohort studies, consensus documents, and case reports directly relevant to endovascular management of abdominal aortic or iliac infective aneurysms and reports involving Gram-negative or gastrointestinal-source infection. The review was narrative and focused, not a systematic review.

Published cohort and case-based reports show that EVAR can be used for selected infective native aortic aneurysms, particularly when patients are unstable or poor candidates for emergency open reconstruction, and reported series of infective native aortic and iliac artery aneurysms describe variable presentation and outcomes [6–8, 10, 11]. These reports should not be interpreted as evidence that endovascular exclusion alone cures infection. Residual infected thrombus, abscess cavities, contaminated retroperitoneal tissue, or adjacent sources may persist after technical exclusion [4, 6, 9].

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis further emphasizes that INAA literature remains heterogeneous in terminology, diagnostic criteria, microbiology, treatment selection, and outcome reporting; it reported a pooled early mortality of 20.3% and reintervention rate of 12%, supporting cautious interpretation of comparisons between open repair and EVAR [12]. A contemporary Malaysian series also highlights regional microbiological variation, including Salmonella and Burkholderia pseudomallei in tropical settings, and reinforces the need to individualize operative strategy, antimicrobial therapy, and follow-up according to pathogen, anatomy, patient condition, and local epidemiology [13].

Several practical considerations arise from these cases. First, rupture-risk control and infection control are related but separate goals. Second, Gram-negative or Enterobacterales infection should prompt evaluation for gastrointestinal, hepatobiliary, urinary, or retroperitoneal sources. Third, when fever, pain, inflammatory markers, bacteremia, peri-graft fluid, gas, or device complications persist after EVAR, clinicians should not assume that the infection has resolved. Discussion

EVAR for rupture control versus infection control

In these patients, EVAR was used to control the immediate risk of rupture rather than to treat the infection. A satisfactory completion angiogram can be reassuring, but infected tissue, septic thrombus, an abscess cavity, and adjacent inflammatory foci remain outside the stent-graft. The key question after emergency EVAR is therefore not only whether the aneurysm has been excluded, but whether the infection has been controlled.

With only three patients, comparative conclusions between endovascular-first and open repair cannot be drawn; the cases instead illustrate when reassessment becomes necessary after initial stabilization.

Patient selection for staged management should therefore be conservative. In these cases, emergency EVAR was chosen because the lesions were rupture-prone and the immediate physiologic burden of open debridement and reconstruction was considered excessive. Staged source control was pursued only when clinical, laboratory, microbiological, or imaging findings suggested that infection persisted despite technical exclusion. This approach should not be generalized to patients who can tolerate definitive open debridement or to those with uncontrolled sepsis, fistula, persistent endograft infection, or progressive peri-graft infection after EVAR.

Appendiceal-source Klebsiella pneumoniae iliac pseudoaneurysm

Case 1 is the most distinctive case in this series. A gas-containing peri-iliac inflammatory mass, Klebsiella pneumoniae infection, and subsequent identification of perforated appendicitis with retroperitoneal contamination support a rare mechanism of contiguous spread to the iliac artery. Appendicitis-associated retroperitoneal contamination and appendicitis-related iliac artery pseudoaneurysm have been reported but remain uncommon, and the left common iliac location is particularly unusual [14, 15]. The inference of appendiceal source was based on anatomic proximity, CT gas and peri-iliac inflammatory change, operative findings of appendiceal perforation and retroperitoneal contamination, and microbiological evidence. However, no molecular homology or strain-level comparison was performed between the appendiceal focus and blood isolate; therefore, the appendiceal source should be regarded as the most plausible causal explanation rather than molecularly proven causality.

This case has practical diagnostic implications. Klebsiella pneumoniae is a recognized, although uncommon, cause of mycotic aneurysm, including abdominal aortic and iliac involvement [16–18]. When Klebsiella pneumoniae or other Enterobacterales are identified in aorto-iliac infection, clinicians should actively search for gastrointestinal, hepatobiliary, urinary, and retroperitoneal sources. Negative urine cultures in Case 1 made a urinary source less likely, while the gas-containing peri-iliac process and appendiceal findings supported gastrointestinal or retroperitoneal contiguous spread.

Post-EVAR reassessment triggers

In clinical practice, discordance between vascular technical success and persistent infection signals should trigger reassessment. These signals include persistent or recurrent fever, ongoing abdominal or back pain, plateauing or rebounding CRP or PCT, leukocytosis, positive blood cultures, enlarging peri-graft fluid, new peri-graft gas, progressive enhancing soft-tissue inflammation, abscess formation, or later device-related complications such as limb occlusion. Case 2 shows that peri-aortic fluid after EVAR may represent infected hematoma or residual retroperitoneal infection requiring evacuation. Case 3 shows that delayed fever with Gram-negative bacteremia and limb occlusion should prompt evaluation for infection-related thrombotic or device complications. The radiological assessment should deliberately compare pre-EVAR, early post-EVAR, and follow-up CTA for sac behavior, peri-graft gas or fluid, adjacent abscess, endoleak, limb patency, and changes in surrounding inflammatory tissue.

Adjunctive technologies and procedural considerations

Broader technological developments may refine post-EVAR surveillance, planning, and education, although they were not used as decision-making tools in the present cases. Internet-of-Surgical-Things concepts may support remote physiologic monitoring, image-guided workflows, and telementoring; in infected aneurysm care, such systems could theoretically help earlier detection of recurrent fever, pain, or functional decline after discharge [19]. Patient-specific 3D printing may improve understanding of complex aorto-iliac anatomy, multidisciplinary planning for source-control procedures, and education around unusual infection pathways [20]. At the same time, device-specific surveillance remains essential: vasa vasorum-related atypical type II endoleak and technical troubleshooting reports after EVAR remind clinicians that sac enlargement, endoleak-like findings, deployment difficulty, or limb complications may reflect mechanical, inflammatory, or infectious processes requiring careful imaging interpretation [21–23]. These technologies are adjuncts to, not substitutes for, source control, antimicrobial management, and longitudinal clinical follow-up.

Surveillance and limitations

Long-term surveillance is essential after EVAR for infected aneurysms because recurrent infection, endograft infection, limb occlusion, and other complications may occur after apparent initial improvement [4, 6, 9]. Follow-up should combine symptom review, temperature assessment, serial inflammatory markers, repeat blood cultures when fever or systemic symptoms recur, and serial CTA. A practical schedule may include early post-source-control imaging, reassessment at 1 to 3 months, repeat imaging at 6 to 12 months, and longer individualized surveillance in patients with persistent inflammatory markers, residual collections, or graft-related concerns. CTA remains central for anatomical surveillance, whereas FDG-PET/CT or leukocyte scintigraphy may be useful in selected patients with equivocal findings or suspected endograft infection [24, 25]. FDG-PET/CT was considered but not performed in these cases, because clinical, microbiological, operative, and CTA findings were sufficient to guide staged source control or thrombectomy.

This report has limitations. It is a small descriptive case series from a single center and cannot establish comparative effectiveness. Microbiological yield was variable and influenced by prior antimicrobial therapy. Not all patients had complete molecular testing, strain comparison, or standardized PET/CT surveillance. Antibiotic choice and duration were individualized, and detailed antimicrobial dosing was not available for all time points. Longer follow-up is particularly needed to evaluate recurrent infection, endograft infection, limb occlusion, reintervention, and survival; therefore, the favorable short-term technical outcomes should not be interpreted as proof of durable cure.

Conclusions

Appendiceal-source Klebsiella pneumoniae iliac pseudoaneurysm is a rare presentation of aorto-iliac vascular infection. In rupture-prone infective native aorto-iliac aneurysms, emergency EVAR may be required to control the immediate risk of hemorrhage, but technical exclusion should not be equated with cure of the infection. Persistent fever, pain, inflammatory-marker elevation, bacteremia, peri-graft fluid or gas, and device-related complications should prompt reassessment for residual infection and for an occult gastrointestinal, retroperitoneal, or urinary source. Further multicenter data with standardized terminology, antimicrobial reporting, imaging protocols, and long-term follow-up are needed before management strategies can be compared reliably.
