MsLEA_3–6 overexpression enhances drought tolerance in Medicago sativa L.
Authors: Wenxue Ma, Xin Wen, Lu Zhao, Shuwei Dong, Dong Luo, Qiang Zhou, Longfa Fang, Wenxian Liu, Zhipeng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLate embryogenesis abundant (LEA) proteins are pivotal mediators of plant abiotic stress adaptation, but their functional roles and regulatory mechanisms in forage crops like Medicago sativa remain unclear. Through a genome-wide analysis of the LEA_3 subgroup in alfalfa, we identified MsLEA_3–6, whose expression is significantly induced by drought. To elucidate its role in drought tolerance, we conducted a comprehensive molecular and physiological characterization of MsLEA_3–6.ResultsPhylogenetic analysis placed MsLEA_3–6 within a conserved clade of drought-responsive LEA proteins. Its promoter harbored stress-related cis-elements, and its expression was upregulated under drought and salinity. Subcellular localization revealed plasma membrane association, while β-glucuronidase GUS staining confirmed ubiquitous expression in the roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and seeds of transgenic Arabidopsis. Overexpression of MsLEA_3–6 enhanced drought tolerance in Arabidopsis (improved root growth and germination under mannitol) and alfalfa (reduced H2O2 and malondialdehyde accumulation, elevated proline/soluble sugars, and attenuated oxidative stress via ROS scavenging). A soil moisture monitoring system demonstrated that MsLEA_3–6 overexpression led to improved photosynthetic performance under drought conditions. Furthermore, the expression of stress/ABA-responsive genes (ABI5, ABF3, NCED5, and NCED9) was altered in the transgenic lines compared with those in the wild-type plants under both normal and drought conditions. Dual-luciferase assays identified MsWRKY71 (MS.gene023126) as a direct binder of the MsLEA_3–6 promoter, forming a stress-responsive regulatory module.ConclusionsOur findings identify MsLEA_3–6 as a functionally conserved, drought-responsive LEA protein that enhances osmotic adjustment and oxidative stress mitigation in transgenic plants, likely through coordinated regulation of ABA signaling and MsWRKY71-mediated transcriptional activation. While the findings support the functional relevance of MsLEA_3–6 under controlled conditions, further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and to validate its utility in improving drought tolerance under field environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07101-9.Keywords: Alfalfa, MsLEA_3–6, WRKY71, Drought tolerance, Antioxidant protection

Introduction

Drought is one of the most severe abiotic stresses limiting global agricultural productivity. It is estimated that over one-third of the world’s land area is affected by drought or semi-arid conditions, posing serious threats to ecological stability, economic development, and food security [1]. Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a widely cultivated perennial legume, plays a pivotal role in livestock forage production, especially in arid and semi-arid regions [2]. However, its shallow root system makes it particularly susceptible to water deficit, often leading to significant reductions in biomass yield and forage quality [3]. Improving drought resistance is therefore a major objective in alfalfa breeding programs, where traits such as dry matter yield, nutritive value, stand persistence, and resilience to biotic and abiotic stresses are key targets [4]. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying drought tolerance and identifying functional genes involved in stress responses are essential for advancing molecular breeding strategies aimed at developing drought-resistant alfalfa cultivars.

Among the molecular candidates for drought tolerance, late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) proteins represent a family of small, highly hydrophilic proteins that enhance stress resistance by stabilizing membranes, scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), and protecting cellular macromolecules [5–7]. In particular, the LEA_3 subgroup has attracted considerable attention due to its highly conserved sequences and strong responsiveness to drought and salinity. For instance, CaLEA1 from pepper modulates abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathways through its LEA_3 domain, contributing to enhanced stress tolerance [8], while wheat LEA_3 genes improve drought resistance in Phellodendron amurense via promotion of stomatal closure [9]. Although LEA_3 proteins have been functionally characterized in several model plants [10], their evolutionary conservation, expression regulation, and mechanistic roles in alfalfa remain unclear. This knowledge gap hampers the effective integration of LEA_3 genes into molecular breeding strategies for improving drought tolerance in alfalfa.

Molecular studies in Medicago species have accelerated since the 1990s. To date, the whole-genome sequencing data of alfalfa have been fully assembled and annotated [11]. Biotechnologies such as transgenics, genomics, and, more recently, gene editing have promised to improve the efficiency of forage breeding [12]. Several studies have demonstrated the feasibility of enhancing abiotic stress tolerance in alfalfa through genetic engineering. For example, MsNTF2L was shown to confer drought resistance by modulating stomatal behavior, promoting cuticular wax accumulation, enhancing antioxidant activity, and improving photosynthetic performance [13]. Similarly, MsLEA1 improves drought and aluminum tolerance via chaperone-like functions that stabilize critical stress-related proteins, including superoxide dismutase and Ms1770 [14]. In addition, genome-wide identification of transcription factor families, such as WRKY, has revealed multiple candidate regulators involved in drought, cold, salt, and ABA-mediated responses [15]. These findings highlight the complex and coordinated genetic networks that underlie abiotic stress responses in alfalfa and provide a solid foundation for the discovery and functional validation of novel stress-responsive genes.

Transcription factors play a pivotal role in orchestrating plant responses to abiotic stress, with the WRKY family emerging as a central regulator in drought and salinity tolerance [16]. In alfalfa, several WRKY genes have been functionally characterized. For instance, MsWRKY33 enhances salt stress tolerance by upregulating antioxidant enzyme genes [17], while MsWRKY11, activated by MsWRKY22, confers drought resistance and regulates lignin biosynthesis [18]. Evidence from other species supports the conserved role of WRKYs in stress adaptation: wheat TaWRKY1-2D interacts with the dehydrin TaDHN3 to promote drought tolerance [19], and in tobacco, WRKY-mediated transcriptional networks co-regulate LEA genes under drought stress [20].

This study elucidates the drought tolerance mechanisms of MsLEA_3–6 in alfalfa through comprehensive functional characterization. By constructing overexpression lines in Arabidopsis and alfalfa, combined with physiological indices (ROS accumulation, osmolytes) and molecular marker genes (ABI5, NCEDs), we revealed that MsLEA_3–6 enhances drought resistance by regulating oxidative homeostasis and the ABA signaling pathway. Systematic promoter analysis via dual-luciferase assays identified critical TF binding sites and their transcriptional activation effects. This study establishes a comprehensive technical framework for LEA_3 gene functional analysis in alfalfa, providing theoretical insights and identifying a candidate gene for drought tolerant breeding.

Results

Phylogenetic and expression profiling of the LEA_3 subgroup in alfalfa

Late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) proteins, particularly those in the LEA_3 subgroup, have been implicated in plant adaptation to abiotic stresses through their proposed role in cellular stabilization during dehydration [21, 22]. To investigate the evolutionary relationship and potential functional diversification of LEA_3 genes in alfalfa, we performed a phylogenetic analysis using full-length protein sequences from 24 alfalfa LEA_3 members, alongside 4 homologs from M. truncatula and 4 from A. thaliana (Fig. 1A). Green dots indicate the Arabidopsis LEA_3 proteins. Blue triangles indicate the M. truncatula LEA_3 proteins. Red dots indicate MsLEA_3–6 and its alleles. Among these, MsLEA_3–6 and its three alleles (MS.gene025712, MS.gene025713, and MS.gene06995) exhibited the highest sequence similarity to the Arabidopsis gene At4g02380. All identified LEA_3 proteins exhibited negative GRAVY values, supporting their hydrophilic nature, which is consistent with their presumed role in desiccation tolerance. Chromosomal localization (Fig. S1) and gene structure analysis (Fig. S2) further supported the evolutionary conservation of this gene family.

Expression profiling under osmotic stress revealed that MsLEA_3–6 was significantly upregulated under mannitol (2.4-fold) and salt (3.5-fold) treatments (Fig. 1B), suggesting its potential involvement in the early drought response pathway. This aligns with reported functions of orthologous genes, such as OsLEA3-1 in rice drought tolerance [23] and AtLEA3 in Arabidopsis seed dehydration tolerance [24].

Promoter analysis of 24 MsLEA_3 genes identified conserved ABRE and G-box cis-elements (Fig. 1C), implicating potential regulation by ABA and light signaling pathways. Notably, the ubiquitous presence of these elements across the subgroup suggests overlapping but not necessarily identical regulatory mechanisms among family members.

Subcellular localization and tissue-specific expression of MsLEA_3–6

To determine the subcellular localization of MsLEA_3–6, a 35 S: MsLEA_3-6-GFP fusion construct was transiently in N. benthamiana. Confocal microscopy analysis indicated that MsLEA_3–6 was predominantly localized to the plasma membrane, and this localization pattern remained stable under 15% PEG-induced osmotic stress (Fig. 2A). This membrane association is consistent with the presence of predicted hydrophobic domains in MsLEA_3–6 (InterPro ID: IPR004864), which may be involved in interactions with membrane components during stress responses [22, 25]. However, the precise molecular function of this localization requires further validation.

(B) The expression of MsLEA_3–6 in various tissues. (C) GUS staining of MsLEA_3–6 pro: GUS transgenic A. thaliana. Bar = 2000 μm. To explore its tissue-specific expression, MsLEA_3–6 transcript levels were quantified in various alfalfa tissues using qRT-PCR. In parallel, transgenic Arabidopsis lines harboring a MsLEA_3–6 promoter-GUS construct were generated to assess spatial expression patterns. Both assays demonstrated that MsLEA_3–6 was broadly expressed in roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and seeds (Fig. 2B, C), with the highest transcript levels observed in stems and flowers under non-stress conditions. GUS activity was particularly strong in root vascular tissues (Fig. 2C), suggesting a potential involvement in water transport and hydraulic regulation, which are known to influence drought responses [26].

To assess the dynamic expression of MsLEA_3–6 under drought-mimicking conditions, GUS staining was performed on seedlings exposed to osmotic stress simulated by 300 mM mannitol at 0, 6, 12, and 24 h. A progressive increase in GUS signal was observed in leaves over time (Fig. 2E), corroborating the temporal expression pattern measured by qRT-PCR(Fig. 2D). This inducible expression suggests that MsLEA_3–6 may participate in the transcriptional response to osmotic stress. The pervasive expression of MsLEA_3–6 aligns with its potential utility in improving whole-plant drought tolerance, particularly in perennial crops like alfalfa [27]. However, agronomic trade-offs (e.g., energy allocation costs) remain to be investigated.

Overexpression of MsLEA_3–6 enhances drought tolerance in Arabidopsis

To assess the functional role of MsLEA_3–6 in drought tolerance, we generated transgenic Arabidopsis lines (OE2, OE6, and OE8) overexpressing MsLEA_3–6 under the constitutive CAMV 35 S promoter. The drought tolerance of these lines was evaluated under both simulated osmotic and soil drought conditions.

On MS medium supplemented with 300 mM mannitol (simulating osmotic stress), 10-day-old OE seedlings exhibited significantly longer primary roots compared with wild-type (Col-0) plants (P < 0.05). This difference was not observed on control MS medium, suggesting that MsLEA_3–6 overexpression specifically contributes to root elongation under water-limited conditions (Fig. 3A-D). Such enhancement may support improved water acquisition under stress [28], although further investigation is required to determine whether this phenotype translates to enhanced field performance. Seed germination assays under 300 mM mannitol also indicated stress sensitivity in both Col-0 and OE lines, but the degree of inhibition was less severe in the OE lines (Fig. 3E–H), implying a partial improvement in early developmental stress tolerance conferred by MsLEA_3–6.

For the soil-based drought assays, 3-week-old plants were subjected to 30 days of water withholding, followed by 7 days of rewatering. Survival rates in the OE lines were significantly higher than those of Col-0 plants. Additionally, chlorophyll content measured using a SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter (Minolta, Japan) revealed consistently higher values in OE lines under both normal and drought conditions (Fig. 3I–L). Nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining showed weaker staining under drought conditions compared with Col-0 (Fig. S3). reactive ROS accumulation (O2⁻ and H2O2) in drought-stressed OE plants than in Col-0.

Taken together, these results suggest that MsLEA_3–6 overexpression may enhance drought resilience in Arabidopsis by improving root growth under osmotic stress, increasing chlorophyll retention, and modulating ROS accumulation under drought. Nonetheless, the mechanistic basis and agronomic relevance of these phenotypes, particularly in alfalfa, require further validation under field or semi-field conditions.

Transgenic alfalfa overexpressing MsLEA_3–6 exhibits enhanced drought resistance

To evaluate the drought resistance conferred by MsLEA_3–6 overexpression, we performed expression analysis on transgenic alfalfa lines generated via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Three independent OE lines showing relatively high transgene expression levels (OE17, OE24, and OE47) were selected for subsequent analyses (Fig. 4A). We conducted both separate-pot and shared-pot soil drought tolerance assays to compare the phenotypic responses of WT and OE lines under water-deficit conditions. In the separate-pot experiment, WT and OE plants were cultivated individually in separate pots, subjected to 32 days of drought and 3 days of rehydration. Under these conditions, OE plants exhibited visibly improved drought resilience compared to WT plants (Fig. 4B). NBT staining revealed a lower intensity of blue coloration in OE leaves compared to WT leaves under drought stress (Fig. 4C, D), suggesting a reduced accumulation of superoxide anions. This observation may indicate an enhanced reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity in the OE lines, although further biochemical assays are required to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms. In the shared-pot drought assay, each pot contained two WT and two OE plants of the same line (WT + OE17, WT + OE24, or WT + OE47), arranged as illustrated in Fig. 4E. After 20 days of drought and 7 days of recovery, OE plants consistently exhibited better survival and less visible damage than adjacent WT plants (Fig. S4), providing additional support for the enhanced drought tolerance phenotype.

Physiological measurements revealed that, under drought stress, OE lines accumulated 27–31% less hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and 17–21% less malondialdehyde (MDA) compared to WT plants (Fig. 4F, G), indicating reduced oxidative damage and membrane lipid peroxidation. Furthermore, OE plants showed significant increases in osmoprotectants, with soluble sugar and proline levels elevated by approximately 1.3-fold and 1.2-fold, respectively (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4E, F). These results align with the known role of LEA proteins in maintaining osmotic homeostasis under abiotic stress conditions [25, 29].

MsLEA_3–6 enhances water retention and photosynthetic performance under prolonged drought

To better understand the potential mechanisms by which MsLEA_3–6 enhances drought tolerance under agronomically relevant conditions, we employed a lysimeter system to evaluate water-use dynamics in wild-type (WT) and overexpression (OE) plants. Each planting unit consisted of a 40 cm diameter, 60 cm height bucket containing three plants of the same genotype. Prior to the initiation of drought treatment, both WT and OE plants exhibited comparable growth vigor and morphology (Fig. 5A). Under progressive drought conditions extending to 115 days without irrigation, OE plants maintained visibly greener foliage and delayed wilting compared to WT plants (Fig. 5B). Upon rehydration for 30 days, continuous soil moisture monitoring revealed that OE plants consumed soil water at a slower rate than WT plants, as reflected by the rate of relative soil water content (RSWC) decline (OE: 6.98–7.09‰ per day; WT: 7.21‰ per day) (Fig. 5C).

Physiological measurements under drought conditions showed that OE lines maintained higher levels of net photosynthetic rate (Pn), transpiration rate (Tr), and water use efficiency (WUE = Pn/Tr) relative to WT controls (Fig. 5D–F). In addition, detached leaf water loss assays revealed that OE plants retained water more effectively than WT plants (Fig. 6), further supporting the hypothesis that MsLEA_3–6 plays a role in limiting water loss.

The integration of gravimetric and physiological data suggests that MsLEA_3–6 may contribute to a more favorable balance between carbon assimilation and water conservation—a trait considered beneficial for forage crop performance under water-limited environments [30]. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that some LEA proteins can stabilize aquaporins [31] and protect photosynthetic components under dehydration stress [13].

MsLEA_3–6 synergizes with ABA signaling to activate drought-responsive genes

To investigate the molecular interplay between MsLEA_3–6 and abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathways during drought stress, we quantified the transcript levels of key genes involved in ABA biosynthesis (NCED5, NCED9) and signaling (ABI5, ABF3) in WT and OE alfalfa plants under both well-watered and drought conditions. Compared to WT, the OE lines consistently exhibited higher expression levels of all four genes across both conditions, with statistically significant increases observed under drought stress (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7). NCED9, which encodes a rate-limiting enzyme in ABA biosynthesis, showed a marked increase in OE plants, suggesting that MsLEA_3–6 may contribute to elevated endogenous ABA accumulation during drought. Similarly, the enhanced expression of ABI5 and ABF3, key transcription factors in ABA signal transduction, points to a possible role of MsLEA_3–6 in modulating ABA-mediated gene regulation.

The expression of stress/ABA-responsive genes in alfalfa WT and OE plants subjected to drought stress, and the relative expression levels of stress/ABA-responsive genes were analyzed in the shoots by RT-qPCR

These results suggest that MsLEA_3–6 may participate in a dual mode of drought response: acting both as a structural protectant, consistent with the hydrophilic properties characteristic of LEA proteins [25], and as a positive modulator of ABA-related gene expression. This potential interaction with the ABA pathway could contribute to the improved drought tolerance observed in OE lines.

However, while the expression data support a correlation between MsLEA_3–6 overexpression and enhanced ABA-related gene activity, the mechanistic basis of this relationship remains to be clarified. Whether MsLEA_3–6 directly influences ABA biosynthesis or signaling, or whether its effect is mediated indirectly through upstream stress-responsive pathways, requires further investigation.

Prediction of transcription factor networks regulating MsLEA_3–6 in alfalfa

To explore the upstream transcriptional regulation of MsLEA_3–6, we used PlantPan 4.0 [32] with Arabidopsis thaliana as the reference genome to predict transcription factor (TF) families potentially binding to the MsLEA_3–6 promoter. The analysis identified several candidate TF families, including bHLH, bZIP, ERF, MYB, and WRKY, which are commonly involved in abiotic stress responses. Based on confidence scores and putative binding motifs, we selected 10 high-confidence TFs (Table S3) for further functional validation using a dual-luciferase (LUC) reporter assay in N. benthamiana leaves. Among these, a WRKY family transcription factor (MS.gene023126, named MsWRKY71) significantly enhanced LUC activity when coexpressed with the MsLEA_3–6 promoter construct (Fig. 8B), indicating a potential regulatory relationship.

Dual-luciferase assay analysis of MsWRKY71-MsLEA_3–6 promoter interaction. (A) Constructs used in dual luciferase reporter gene assay. (B) The interaction between MsWRKY71 and MsLEA_3–6 promoter by LUC assay. (C) The expression of MsWRKY71 under mannitol and salt treatment was analyzed by RT-qPCR

To assess whether MsWRKY71 is stress-inducible, we performed qRT-PCR analysis under drought and salt stress treatments. The expression of MsWRKY71 was significantly upregulated in response to both stresses, with maximum transcript accumulation observed at 12 h after drought treatment and 3 h after salt exposure (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8C). These patterns are consistent with the expression behavior of its Arabidopsis homolog (ATWRKY71, AT1G29860), which has been previously implicated in stress-related transcriptional regulation [33, 34].

Discussion

The LEA_3 protein family has been extensively studied in model plants for its roles in dehydration tolerance, yet its functional diversification and agronomic potential in perennial forage crops such as alfalfa remain unclear. Our study systematically deciphers the evolutionary conservation and novel mechanisms of MsLEA_3–6 in alfalfa drought adaptation, bridging molecular insights with crop improvement strategies.

Plants can protect themselves from damage caused by abiotic stress by inducing the expression of genes encoding protective proteins. The LEA protein-encoding gene family is one such example. According to previous studies in various plant species, LEA proteins are associated with tolerance to abiotic stress [35–37]. By referencing the LEA_3 genes from Arabidopsis and M. truncatula, we identified and characterized 24 genes encoding LEA_3 proteins in alfalfa (Fig. 1A). Physicochemical analysis revealed that most MsLEA genes encode relatively small proteins, which is consistent with findings reported in potato by Chen et al. [38] and in rapeseed by Liang et al. [39]. Previous studies have indicated that the majority of LEA protein members are intrinsically alkaline [40]. Similar characteristics of LEA proteins have also been documented in Arabidopsis thaliana [21], Populus euphratica [41], and potato [38]. These results collectively demonstrate that all MsLEA proteins are hydrophilic. These findings suggest that LEA proteins exhibit pronounced hydrophilicity and evolutionary conservation in higher plants, adopting a fully or partially disordered state and functioning as molecular chaperones to protect plants from dehydration [42, 43]. ABRE, MBS, and G-box cis-acting elements are widely distributed in the promoter regions of MsLEA genes. These regulatory elements are known to provide binding sites for transcription factors such as ABF (a member of the bZIP transcription factor family), BHLH, and ERF, which regulate the transcription of downstream stress-responsive genes [44]. MsLEA_3–6 is constitutively expressed across tissues (Fig. 2B, C), suggesting its role in both developmental and stress-responsive processes. This divergence may reflect adaptive evolution in legumes to cope with recurrent drought in arid grazing systems.

The overexpression of MsLEA_3–6 conferred superior drought tolerance in both Arabidopsis and alfalfa, not only through passive osmoprotection but also through the active modulation of water use efficiency (WUE) and photosynthetic resilience (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). The 1.6-fold higher WUE in transgenic alfalfa (Fig. 5F) aligns with the “more crop per drop” paradigm for sustainable agriculture [45]. Mechanistically, MsLEA_3–6 synergizes with ABA signaling to increase the expression of stress-responsive genes (ABI5, ABF3, and NCEDs; Fig. 7), a type of crosstalk rarely reported for LEA proteins. This dual capacity—membrane protection through intrinsic hydrophilicity combined with ABA-mediated transcriptional priming—explains the sustained drought recovery observed in OE plants (Fig. S4).

The identification of MsWRKY71 as a direct interactor of MsLEA_3–6 through a LUC assay (Fig. 7B) provides compelling evidence for its regulatory role in the alfalfa drought response. These findings corroborate those of prior studies demonstrating that AtWRKY71 regulates ethylene-mediated leaf senescence by directly activating the EIN2, ORE1, and ACS2 genes [46]. Our observation of rapid MsWRKY71 upregulation during drought (peaking at 6 h, Fig. 8C) mirrors these reports and suggests a conserved early stress signaling role. These collective data position MsWRKY71 as a central node in the alfalfa stress adaptation network, potentially coordinating LEA-mediated protection mechanisms through direct protein-protein interactions and transcriptional regulation. Future studies should explore whether this interaction modulates the chaperone activity or subcellular localization of MsLEA_3–6 under stress.

Conclusions

Our results indicate that MsLEA_3–6 enhances drought tolerance in alfalfa by reducing cellular damage, improving water use efficiency, and supporting photosynthetic activity under stress. Its overexpression is associated with elevated expression of ABA biosynthesis and signaling genes, suggesting a role in amplifying drought-responsive pathways. Promoter activation by MsWRKY71 further implies that MsLEA_3–6 is part of a stress-related transcriptional network. While these findings highlight its potential for crop improvement, further studies are needed to confirm regulatory interactions and evaluate performance in field conditions.

Materials and methods

Identification of MsLEA_3 genes and expression pattern analysis

The LEA_3 protein sequences of Arabidopsis thaliana and Medicago truncatula were retrieved from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org) and the Genome Project, respectively. Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L. cv XinJianagDaYe) LEA_3 homologs were identified via BLASTP against the alfalfa genome [10] with an E value cutoff of 1e − 5 via the TBtools BLASTP search tool [47]. An unrooted NJ tree was constructed via MEGA7 with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The MW, pI, and GRAVY values of the MsLEA protein via the EXPASY website (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). The expression profiles of MsLEAs in response to mannitol and salt treatments in the Zhongmu No. 1 transcriptome sequencing database were downloaded from the MODMS website (https://modms.lzu.edu.cn/) [48, 49].

RT‒qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from plant samples via TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). cDNA synthesis was performed with a TransScript All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TransGen Biotech). qPCR was performed via SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme) with a CFX96 Connect Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, USA). The expression levels were standardized to those of MsEF-α and Msactin. For gene expression in WT and OE lines, leaf samples were collected from seedlings at the same time points. To analyze the expression of stress/ABA-responsive genes in drought-stressed alfalfa, the third fully expanded leaves from WT and OE seedlings were collected from both the well-watered control and after 7 days of drought treatment. While drought stress involves complex physiological responses, its primary cellular effect is hyperosmotic stress [50]. We therefore selected mannitol (300 mM) as a non-ionic osmoticum to isolate the osmotic component of drought during germination, excluding confounding factors from salt stress or soil matrix effects [51]. Gene expression profiles were analyzed in shoot tissues harvested at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h post-treatment to capture early drought-responsive regulation. Each treatment included 3 independent replicates. Relative expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method [52].

Subcellular localization analysis

The coding sequence of MsLEA_3–6 was cloned and inserted into the pBI121-GFP vector (35 S: GFP fusion) via Sal I/Spe I restriction sites (primers in Table S2). Seeds of Nicotiana benthamiana were obtained from Coolaber Technology (Beijing, China). Constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens and cultured in LB medium (50 µg/mL kanamycin/gentamycin) to an OD600 ≈ 0.8. N. benthamiana plants, which were grown for 40 days under 16 h light/8 h dark, were infiltrated with Agrobacterium suspensions (OD600 0.4–0.6 in ½ MS buffer containing 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM MES [pH 5.6], and 10 µM acetosyringone). After dark incubation (12 h), infiltrated leaves were returned to normal light for 48–72 h. Subcellular localization was analyzed via fluorescence microscopy and confocal imaging of 1 cm2 leaf sections mounted in water, following established protocols [53].

GUS staining

A 2-kb promoter region upstream of MsLEA_3–6 was cloned and inserted into the pCAMBIA1301 vector. Transgenic Arabidopsis plants were generated via Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation [54]. Wild-type Arabidopsis seeds (Col-0) were purchased from Pujie Biology (Shanghai, China). GUS staining was performed by incubating tissues in X-Gluc solution (1 mM X-glucuronide, 100 mM NaPO4 buffer, pH 7.0) at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by ethanol destaining. Images were captured via a ZEISS V20 stereomicroscope.

Vector construction and plant transformation

For overexpression, the MsLEA_3–6 CDS was ligated into pBIB-BASTA-35 S-GWR-FLAG (35 S: MsLEA_3–6). The constructs were introduced into Arabidopsis (Col-0) via the floral dip method and stably transformed into alfalfa seedlings (genotype Regen SY-4D, a highly efficient genetic transformation material), following the protocol described by Jiang et al. [55]. Alfalfa Regen SY4D was kindly provided by Prof. Zengyu Wang (College of Grassland Science, Qingdao Agricultural University, Qingdao, China). Transgenic lines were confirmed via PCR and RT‒qPCR (primers are listed in Table S2).

Drought stress assays

Arabidopsis: T3 seeds were germinated on MS and transferred to MS medium supplemented with different concentrations of mannitol (0 and 300 mM) 7 days after germination. The root lengths were measured at 10 days. Germination assays of Arabidopsis WT and OE lines were conducted on both standard MS medium and MS supplemented with 300 mM mannitol. Seeds were considered germinated when the radicle protruded ≥ 1 mm. Germination rates were recorded daily at fixed time points until day 10. Soil-grown plants (4-week-old) were deprived of water for 30 days, followed by rehydration for 7 days to assess survival rates, following the protocol described by Luo et al. [10]. For NBT and DAB staining, leaves were collected from plants before drought stress and after 16 days of water withholding and then immediately analyzed via a BCIP/NBT Chromogen Kit (PR1100, Solarbio) and a DAB Substrate Kit (DA1010, Solarbio)

Alfalfa: OE and WT plants were vegetatively propagated from stem cuttings [56] in vermiculite for one month until rooting and then transplanted into a 3:1 (v/v) mixture of peat soil and vermiculite in a controlled greenhouse (25/18°C, 60% RH). Drought tolerance assays were conducted with both shared-pot and separate-pot experimental designs. In the separate-pot trials, WT and OE lines were cultivated in individual pots of identical size, each containing 120 ± 1 g of soil. Phenotypic documentation was performed after 32 days of drought treatment, and rehydration was performed after 3 days. For the shared-pot experiments, each pot contained 400 ± 5 g of soil, and two WT and two OE lines were planted. Phenotypic documentation was conducted after 20 days of drought treatment, followed by rehydration on day 7 postdrought, to quantify the survival rates of both WT and OE lines. Additionally, WT and OE lines were cultivated in a lysimeter (model XHZ-ZS301, Xi’an Xinhuize Measurement and Control Technology Co., Ltd., China) system, with three plants of the same genotype (either WT or OE) per container. The lysimeter measures crop water consumption and water balance by using high-precision load cells to directly weigh soil mass at three points, enabling accurate assessment of transpiration, evaporation, and soil moisture dynamics within the soil–plant–atmosphere continuum (SPAC).

Physiological and photosynthetic measurements

Fully expanded intact leaves (3rd to 5th from the apex) of alfalfa were collected from individual plants for biochemical analyses. Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), malondialdehyde (MDA), soluble sugar, and free proline contents were quantified via commercial assay kits (Comin Biochemical Test Kits, Cominbio, China; catalog numbers: H2O2−2-Y, MDA-2-Y, KT-2-Y, and PRO-2-Y, respectively). All the measurements were conducted at two time points: before and after 14 days of water deficit treatment. Photosynthetic parameters (Pn, Tr, and WUE) were recorded with a LI-6800 portable photosynthesis system (LI-COR Inc., Lincoln, NE, USA). The RSWC was calculated as follows: RSWC = (fresh weight dry weight)/(initial weight dry weight) × 100%.

Dual-luciferase assay

The candidate TFs were subsequently cloned and inserted into the pGreenII 0800 vector, and the MsLEA_3–6 promoter was inserted into pGreenII 62-SK. The constructs were cotransformed into N. benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration (strain GV3101). Five-week-old tobacco plants were selected for Agrobacterium infiltration via larger middle leaves. After being sprayed with water, the plants were kept in darkness at 25 °C for 24 h and then transferred to normal photoperiod conditions for 2‒3 days before being observed via confocal laser scanning microscopy.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed via SPSS 26.0 (IBM). Differences between groups were determined by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD test (P < 0.05). Graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 9.0.
