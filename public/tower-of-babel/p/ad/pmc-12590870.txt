Evaluation of the accuracy of semi-autonomous robotic system versus dynamic navigation in completely edentulous implant surgery: an in vitro study
Authors: Jie Xia, Qingwei Tang, Haiyan Liu, Yuchen Zheng, Linhong Wang, Xulan Yang, Jianying Feng, Fan Yang, Yude Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study compared the accuracy (defined by trueness and precision) of implant placement between a semi-autonomous robotic system (SARS) and a dynamic navigation system (DNS) in completely edentulous models, evaluating the influence of alveolar bone morphology and implant orientation.MethodsThirty-two completely edentulous models were divided by anterior ridge morphology (well-rounded or narrow). Following the All-on-4 protocol, anterior implants were placed vertically, and posterior implants were inserted vertically or at a distal tilt using either SARS or DNS. Planning was conducted virtually, and deviations were assessed postoperatively via CBCT. Mann-Whitney U test and linear mixed model were used for statistical analysis.ResultsAll 128 implants were placed successfully. Platform global deviations were 0.69 ± 0.29 mm (SARS) and 0.72 ± 0.41 mm (DNS); apical global deviations were 0.74 ± 0.27 mm (SARS) and 0.70 ± 0.40 mm (DNS). SARS achieved significantly lower angular deviation (superior trueness) overall (P < .05), particularly in distally tilted implants (P < .001). On well-rounded ridges, SARS showed smaller global and lateral platform deviations (P < .05). On narrow ridges, SARS had larger platform and apical depth deviations (P < .01) but maintained better angular control (P < .01). Precision analysis revealed that SARS achieved superior platform precision under complex conditions (distally tilted implants and narrow ridges), while on well-rounded ridges, it showed higher apical precision and more consistent angular control.ConclusionBoth SARS and DNS demonstrated excellent accuracy in completely edentulous models. Furthermore, the SARS showed superior angular trueness and platform precision in distally tilted implant placement and complex bone morphology.

Introduction

Accurate positioning of dental implants is essential for the functional, esthetic, and long-term stability of the restoration in completely edentulous patients [1, 2]. However, challenges arise from the alveolar ridge resorption, anatomical variations, and the loss of local reference points, all of which make the accurate implant placement more difficult [3].

To achieve accurate positioning, computer-assisted implant surgery (CAIS) has become a crucial tool in completely edentulous arch implant procedures, such as the static guide and dynamic navigation systems. Studies have demonstrated that CAIS significantly improves the accuracy of implant placement in patients with single-tooth loss, partially edentulous arches, and completely edentulous jaws [4–6]. However, despite the clear advantages of CAIS in terms of accuracy, each system presents specific limitations. The static guide is limited by increased manufacturing time, obstruction of the surgical field, a lack of flexibility for intraoperative adjustments, and higher demands on the patient’s mouth opening [7, 8]. In contrast, dynamic navigation provides real-time feedback through optical tracking, offers virtual guidance to ensure high implant placement accuracy, and enables dynamic intraoperative adjustments [9]. Studies have confirmed its accuracy in completely edentulous jaws, especially where anatomical reference points are lacking [10, 11]. It has also demonstrated advantages in placing intentionally tilted implants through effective angular and positional visual guidance [12]. However, dynamic navigation has limitations, including a steep learning curve and the need for frequent visual shifts between the display and the surgical field [13]. Furthermore, due to the lack of physical guidance, dynamic navigation may encounter limitations in maintaining the stability of the drill in patients with complex anatomy or low bone density [14, 15].

Robot-assisted computer-aided implant surgery (r-CAIS) is an emerging technology that is increasingly applied in dental implant procedures. It integrates real-time navigation with precise robotic execution, thereby achieving higher accuracy and flexibility during implant placement [11, 16, 17]. Currently, r-CAIS systems are primarily divided into three categories: fully autonomous robots, semi-autonomous robots, and passive robots [18, 19]. Fully autonomous robots perform the entire procedure based on pre-set programs, reducing human intervention. Although this technology has demonstrated high accuracy in completely edentulous treatment [20], its flexibility and adaptability in complex oral anatomical conditions still require further validation [21]. Passive robots assist surgeons through physical constraints and navigation guidance, with the surgeon maintaining full operational control and without any automatic adjustment capability [22]. Semi-autonomous robots combine the surgeon’s decision-making with the precise execution of the robotic system [23]. The surgeon remains in control of the surgery, while the robot provides real-time feedback and automatic adjustments when necessary [24]. However, despite the promising potential of semi-autonomous robotic technology in dental implantology, its clinical application remains in the early stages of development [25]. Existing research has primarily focused on implant placement in single-tooth or partially edentulous cases [26, 27], while its application in completely edentulous arches or terminal dentitions remains limited, with only a few reported cases [28]. As an emerging technology, semi-autonomous robotic systems require further research to ensure its effectiveness and stability in completely edentulous patients.

Therefore, it remains unclear whether semi-autonomous robotic systems, which reduce human intervention, can achieve superior accuracy compared to dynamic navigation in completely edentulous implant placement. Moreover, it remains uncertain whether this digital technology can maintain its superior accuracy when applied to narrow alveolar ridges and distally tilted implant placement in vitro model experiments. This study aimed to compare the accuracy of the semi-autonomous robotic system (SARS) and the dynamic navigation system (DNS) in implant placement within complex completely edentulous models. The research hypothesis was that SARS could achieve a comparable level of accuracy to dynamic navigation in completely edentulous implant placement.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted at a dental center, using a total of 32 standardized completely edentulous models, including 16 maxillary models and 16 mandibular models. The 32 models were divided into two groups according to the alveolar morphology in the anterior area: the narrow alveolar ridge (width 2 mm) and the well-rounded alveolar ridge (width 5 mm) [29]. The morphological characteristics of the models are illustrated in Fig. 1. These standardized completely edentulous models were purchased online (Chengcheng Dental Model Co., Ltd., Anyang, Henan, China), made of polyurethane, and measured 6.5 × 5.5 × 3.0 cm. Since the data used for the experiment were not derived from clinical cases, the study was granted an ethics exemption. This study was performed according to the checklist for reporting in vitro studies (CRIS guidelines). Sample size calculations were based on prior studies related to alveolar ridge morphology [28]. Due to the lack of relevant references, no independent sample size calculation was conducted for implant orientation. Therefore, subgroup allocation for implant orientation followed the same sample size determined for ridge morphology. Based on α = 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, 15 implants were required per subgroup; to account for potential losses, 16 implants were allocated per subgroup, resulting in 64 implants per group. The experiment was conducted by a skilled surgeon (X.J.) familiar with both dynamic navigation and semi-autonomous robotic techniques. Another surgeon (T.Q.W.), who was responsible for virtual implant planning and accuracy assessment, did not participate in the surgical operations.

Model preparation

To simulate complex clinical conditions, each completely edentulous model received four implants: two in the anterior region and two in the posterior region. The anterior implants were placed according to the alveolar ridge morphology (well-rounded or narrow) [29], while the posterior implants were inserted based on the predefined approach (vertical or distally tilted). All implants were Super Line implants (ø4.0 mm × 12.0 mm; Dentium Co., Ltd., Suwon-si, Gyeonggi-do, Korea). A total of 32 completely edentulous models were randomly assigned to two groups: the SARS group (n = 16) and the DNS group (n = 16). Within each group, models were evenly distributed based on alveolar ridge morphology and implant orientation to ensure balanced sample sizes and comparability.

Robotic-assisted implant surgery protocol

The SARS (semi-autonomous robotic-assisted surgery system, Hangzhou Jianjia Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) is an integrated robotic platform for dental implant placement, developed based on optical navigation and real-time visual feedback. As previously described by Chen et al. [27], the system comprises a surgical cart with a robotic arm, a stereo camera, a touchscreen interface, implant planning software, and an optical tracking system to assist in precise implant guidance.

For model preparation, five registration pins (ø1.9 mm × 8.5 mm) were placed, including two for fixation of the tracking array. During registration, at least four pins were required, and surgery was initiated only when the registration error was < 0.8 mm. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans (Planmeca ProMax, Planmeca Oy, Helsinki, Finland) were performed using the following parameters: 80 kV, 6.0 mA, 15 s scan time, 0.15 mm voxel size, 15-bit grayscale, 0.5 mm focal spot, and 12 × 9 cm field of view. The DICOM files were imported into the planning software (Sutec DHC-DI, V2), and implant positions were digitally planned. The finalized plans were transferred to the SARS system for intraoperative guidance.

Prior to surgery, a calibration protocol employing a spherical-tip probe was executed to validate the robotic arm’s spatial accuracy. The automated adjustment function incorporated continuous optical tracking with real-time feedback on penetration depth (mean tracking error < 0.2 mm), angular control, and safety boundary adherence. Dynamic navigation protocol

The dynamic navigation system (DHC-D12, Digital Healthcare Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) is a computer-assisted platform for dental implant surgery, consisting of a navigation cart, optical tracking device, planning software, and an implant handpiece with infrared reflectors.

Four registration titanium pins (ø2 mm × 9 mm) were placed on the model, and at least three pins were required for registration. Surgery was initiated only when the registration error was < 0.5 mm, ensuring sufficient distance from proposed implant sites and critical anatomical regions. The prepared model underwent CBCT imaging, with acquired volumetric data imported into the surgical planning software for three-dimensional reconstruction. Virtual implant planning was subsequently executed through the haptic interface of the software, followed by initialization of the optical tracking system.

A sterilized implant handpiece with integrated infrared reflectors was coupled to the navigation array, which was rigidly affixed to the models using cortical bone screws. Intraoperative registration was achieved through sequential probe acquisition of the fiducial markers, enabling the navigation system to calculate transformation matrices with a mean target registration error of < 0.2 mm. Based on real-time guidance, the surgeon completed the implant placement. Continuous intraoperative monitoring of osteotomy parameters was implemented, with real-time intraoperative adjustments made to the implant’s three-dimensional position, axial angulation, and insertion depth to achieve consistency with the preoperative plan. Accuracy evaluation

Postoperative CBCT scans were performed using identical parameters to the preoperative scans. The preoperative plan was imported into 3D Slicer (Version 4.13, Harvard, Boston, USA), an open-source software, and the postoperative images were fused for reference based on markers according to the method described by Talmazov G et al. [30]. The software automatically calculated the deviations between the planned implant positions and actual implant placements, including global, depth, and lateral deviations at both the platform and apex, as well as angular deviation (Fig. 4). All measurements were conducted by an independent examiner (T.Q.W.), who was not involved in the surgical procedures. During the examination, the use of numerical codes to identify patients and the application of blinding for group allocation were employed. Additionally, a unified software was utilized for deviation assessment to reduce bias.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (Version 25.0; SPSS Inc.). Descriptive statistics included mean, standard deviation (SD), interquartile range, minimum (Min), and maximum (Max) values. The normality distribution of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to evaluate the consistency of measurements by the same observer at a 24-hour interval. Deviations at the platform, apical, and angular levels were evaluated. According to ISO 5725, trueness was defined as the deviation between planned and actual positions, and precision as the variability among implants based on pairwise distances under identical conditions [31]. Precision was compared between the SARS and DNS groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Given the non-independence of implant data, a linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept was applied to compare the seven deviations between the SARS and DNS groups. Alveolar ridge morphology and implant orientation were included as fixed effects to assess their potential influence. Statistical significance was set at P <.05.

Results

A total of 128 implants were included in this study. A significant difference was found in angular deviation (P <.05), with the SARS group at 1.03 ± 0.56° and the DNS group at 1.29 ± 0.67°. However, no statistical difference was observed in other deviations (Fig. 5). The deviations of global platform and apex in the SARS and DNS groups were 0.69 ± 0.29 mm and 0.72 ± 0.41 mm, respectively, as well as 0.74 ± 0.27 mm and 0.70 ± 0.40 mm (Table 1). To ensure the reliability of measurements, intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis was conducted on 60% of randomly selected samples. The ICC for single measurements was 0.88 (95% CI: 0.86–0.90), and for average measurements it reached 0.94 (95% CI: 0.92–0.95), indicating excellent intra-rater consistency.

In the anterior region with vertical placement, the SARS group demonstrated significantly better platform trueness on well-rounded ridges, with reduced global deviations (0.56 ± 0.26 mm versus 0.90 ± 0.48 mm, P <.05) and lateral deviations (0.39 ± 0.23 mm versus 0.60 ± 0.28 mm, P <.05) compared to DNS group (Fig. 6; Table 3). However, no significant differences were found for platform depth deviation and apical positional trueness (Table 3). Notably, on narrow alveolar ridges, the SARS group demonstrated significantly larger depth deviation at both platform (P <.01) and apex (P <.01) than the DNS group (Fig. 7). Despite this, the SARS group still maintained better angular control than the DNS (0.79 ± 0.46° versus 1.31 ± 0.51°, P <.01) (Fig. 5; Table 3).

Discussion

This study is the first to compare the accuracy between SARS and DNS in completely edentulous implant placement with different alveolar ridge morphologies and different implant orientations. The results indicated that SARS demonstrated better trueness in angular control and higher platform precision under complex conditions than DNS. Thus, the null hypothesis of no difference between the two methods used in completely edentulous implant placement was rejected.

Completely edentulous implant placement presents multiple challenges, including the lack of anatomical landmarks and variations in alveolar ridge morphology, which impose higher accuracy requirements. In such cases, complex alveolar anatomy often necessitated angular adjustments of implant orientation to utilize the limited available bone, particularly in full-arch rehabilitations [32]. Previous studies have demonstrated that DNS achieves reliable accuracy in completely edentulous implant placement and tilted implant procedures [10, 12]. This study further compared the accuracy of the SARS and DNS in completely edentulous models. The results showed that the angular deviation in the SARS group was significantly lower than that in the DNS group (1.03 ± 0.56° versus 1.29 ± 0.67°, P <.05), indicating that SARS could provide superior angular control. In addition, SARS exhibited higher angular precision, suggesting more consistent performance among implants under identical conditions. Although SARS possessed a theoretical advantage in mechanical guidance, no significant differences were observed in most linear deviations between the two systems. This might be attributed to handpiece deflection or system response latency, which could diminish the expected mechanical advantage of SARS. Similar findings have been reported in previous comparative studies of robotic and navigation systems [33]. Overall, both the SARS and the DNS could achieve excellent accuracy in completely edentulous implant placement.

In the posterior region, the SARS system demonstrated significantly better angular trueness than the DNS during distally tilted implant placement. Precision analysis revealed distinct differences between the two systems under different implant orientations. For vertical placement, DNS showed higher apical lateral precision, whereas SARS achieved greater apical depth precision. For distally tilted implants, SARS demonstrated superior angular and platform global precision, while DNS exhibited higher platform and apical depth precision. This difference primarily arises from their mechanical characteristics: the robotic arm of SARS follows a predefined trajectory, effectively minimizing drill deflection and improving depth consistency during vertical placement [34]. However, in complex tilted implantation, its angle-first control strategy maintains the preset trajectory along paths of least resistance reducing angular deviation but potentially increasing depth dispersion at the platform or apex [28]. In contrast, DNS enables real-time visual feedback, allowing surgeons to dynamically compensate for linear deviations caused by variations in bone resistance. Such operator-guided compensation enhances both lateral and depth precision but introduces greater variability in angular stability [35]. Moreover, no significant difference was found between vertical and distally tilted placement in the SARS group, indicating the system’s excellent ability to maintain stable and precise angular control under different implant orientations. Previous studies have indicated that in complex anatomical structures and insufficient bone volume, traditional freehand implant placement is prone to substantial angular deviations [36]. Vrielinck et al. conducted a clinical validation study that utilized CT-based preoperative planning and patient-specific drill guides [37]. They found that, even with static guidance, the average angular deviations for zygomatic and pterygoid implants were 5.14° and 10.18°, respectively. These findings revealed the inherent limitations of static guidance systems in precise angular control during complex implantation. Furthermore, Zhou et al. found that the average angular deviation of the freehand technique in distally tilted implant placement was 12.49°, which was much larger than the 2.64° deviation of the dynamic navigation system, demonstrating the superior accuracy of navigation in angle control [38]. Meng et al. [12] also evaluated the accuracy of DNS in intentionally tilted maxillary implant placement, reporting no significant difference in angular deviation between tilted (3.1° ± 1.0°) and axially placed implants (3.2° ± 1.5°) (P >.05), indicating that DNS provided effective angular control. However, the maximum angular deviation observed in dynamic navigation in some studies can reach 6.7°, which may be attributed to CBCT data inaccuracies, image registration errors, or variations in surgical expertise. This raises concerns about the stability of dynamic navigation in complex completely edentulous implant cases [39, 40]. Compared to vertical implantation approach, tilted implant procedures demand greater accuracy in angular control [28]. With its rigid robotic arm [34], the robotic system can effectively reduce human error and obtain better angular control, which is also proved by our results. Conversely, DNS is more dependent on the surgeon’s technical skill, particularly hand-eye coordination and the interpretation of real-time imaging [35]. During tilted implantation, dynamic navigation may be limited by feedback latency and cumulative operational errors, potentially leading to increased angular deviation [41, 42].

Previous studies have shown that alveolar ridge morphology significantly impacted implant accuracy [43–45]. This study investigated the influence of alveolar ridge morphology on the accuracy of SARS and DNS in the anterior region with vertical implant placement. The results indicated that under complex anatomical conditions, such as narrow alveolar ridges, SARS achieved higher platform precision and angular trueness, similar to its performance in distally tilted implant placement, suggesting superior angular control and positional reproducibility in challenging scenarios. Meanwhile, the robotic system demonstrated significantly lower global and lateral deviations of the platform on the well-rounded alveolar ridge compared to on narrow alveolar ridges in the SARS group. Putra et al. demonstrated that low bone density (D3/D4) and narrow alveolar ridges could increase the angular and depth deviations, especially in computer-guided surgery (CGS) [46], which was consistent with the results of this study. Additionally, research found that the healed alveolar ridges exhibited higher accuracy of implant placement than fresh extraction sites [28]. Dulla et al. further reported that the complexity of alveolar ridge morphology and the inconsistency of bone resorption patterns might reduce the accuracy of computer-assisted implant surgery for completely edentulous patients [47]. Similarly, Cassetta et al. suggested that implant placement in the mandible may exhibit greater deviations due to limited anatomical support, whereas the palatal vault in the maxilla could provide additional stabilization during guided surgery [48]. Interestingly, despite better positional trueness, SARS showed greater angular deviation on well-rounded ridges. This might be attributed to smoother bone surfaces and fewer anatomical references. Miadili et al. noted that indistinct cortical boundaries can compromise angular control in robotic implant placement, which may help explain our findings [49]. Nevertheless, the robotic system still demonstrated significantly superior global and lateral platform trueness as well as apical and angular precision than the navigation system on the well-rounded ridges in this study, suggesting the superior positional stability of SARS in regions with smooth bony contours and indistinct anatomical landmarks. However, on narrow ridges, although the robotic system still achieved better angular control, it exhibited significantly larger deviations in both platform and apical depths. This phenomenon may be attributed to differences in anatomical characteristics and system control strategies. Narrow ridges are often associated with buccolingual asymmetry in bone volume, which increases the susceptibility of the drill to lateral forces and subsequent path deviation during osteotomy [50]. When the drill shows lateral offset, the excellent angle control of the robot arm may instead increase the linear distance deviation [51]. Chen et al. also observed that in anatomically asymmetric sites such as extraction sockets, palatal bone resistance could cause the robotic drill to slightly slide along the bone surface, leading to increased insertion depth [28]. These findings suggest that there may be a balance between the angular control and linear trueness of robotic implant placement in complex anatomical conditions.

The resin models used in this study could not fully replicate clinical conditions (e.g., bone density, saliva, bleeding, limited mouth opening, and patient movement), which may limit the research results. The in-vitro design also did not account for maxilla–mandible differences or other patient-specific variations. Moreover, the two systems employed slightly different registration requirements, which may affect accuracy in clinical practice. Further clinical studies are needed to validate these results and to assess the clinical feasibility of robotic-assisted implant placement.

Conclusion

In this model experiment, both the semi-autonomous robotic system and the dynamic navigation system demonstrated acceptable accuracy in terms of trueness and precision. Furthermore, the robotic system showed superior angular control and greater platform precision than dynamic navigation in distally tilted implant placement and on narrow alveolar ridges. These results offer important experimental evidence supporting the potential application of semi-autonomous robotic technology in dental implant surgery.
