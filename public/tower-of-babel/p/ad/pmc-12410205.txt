Association between wearable sensor signals and expected hormonal changes in pregnancy
Authors: Giulia Milan, Lauren Ariniello, Katie Baca-Motes, Arij Faksh, Jacqueline K Kueper, Jay A Pandit, Tolúwalàṣẹ Àjàyí, Giorgio Quer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe rising maternal health crisis in the United States necessitates innovative approaches to pregnancy monitoring. This observational cohort study aimed to assess whether wearable sensors can effectively track physiological and behavioural changes during pregnancy and examine their associations with pregnancy-related hormonal fluctuations.MethodsThis longitudinal cohort study recruited participants via a bilingual mobile research platform. Eligible participants were aged ≥16 years, pregnant or within eight weeks postpartum, residing in the United States, and consented to share data collected by their own wearable device (Apple, Garmin, or Fitbit). Self-reported survey responses and wearable sensor data were collected from 99 participants who experienced a live birth pregnancy, from three months pre-pregnancy to six months postpartum. Key outcomes included changes in resting heart rate (RHR), physical activity, and sleep patterns, analysed in relation to expected hormonal fluctuations during pregnancy.FindingsIn live birth pregnancies, RHR initially decreased between weeks 5–9, followed by a steady increase until 8–9 weeks before delivery, then declined until birth, dropping below pre-pregnancy levels postpartum before stabilising at six months. Total sleep time increased in the first trimester but decreased throughout the remainder of pregnancy. A strong correlation was observed between RHR fluctuations and pregnancy-induced hormonal changes (R2 = 0.93). Pregnancies ending in adverse outcomes displayed distinct RHR patterns compared to live birth pregnancies.InterpretationThese findings suggest that wearable sensors provide a non-invasive method to monitor pregnancy-related physiological and behavioural changes, which align with hormonal shifts. This study provides population-level insights in live birth pregnancies, and an exploratory analysis of adverse outcomes reflecting the feasibility of recruiting and capturing physiological signals also in these cases. This approach may enable early risk assessment for adverse pregnancy outcomes, including miscarriage and preterm birth.FundingThis work was supported by the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (UM1TR004407) and from the Patrick J. Research in context

Evidence before this study

The increasing prevalence of adverse pregnancy outcomes in the United States underscores the urgent need for innovative solutions to monitor maternal and foetal health.

We searched PubMed for studies on the use of wearable sensors in monitoring pregnancy with keywords like “wearables in pregnancy” and “hormones and wearables in pregnancy”. The search covered publications in the English language up to October 1st, 2024. 203 studies deal with the usage of wearable in pregnancy healthcare. Most of these studies focus on specific conditions in pregnancies, such as monitoring of diabetes during pregnancy or proposing ad-hoc wearable sensors for hypertensive pregnancies. Some studies leveraged commercially available wearable sensors to monitor pregnancies and collect signals during pregnancy, such as sleep and heart rate. Fewer studies collected physiological signals through commercial wearable sensors to evaluate whether some physiological signals, including heart rate variability and resting heart rate, may serve as potential predictor for preterm birth or labour onset. We did not find studies evaluating how the physiological changes collected through wearable sensors are associated with hormonal changes during pregnancy.

Added value of this study

In this study, we investigated whether wearable sensors can effectively monitor physiological and behavioural changes in pregnancy, identifying patterns in resting heart rate, activity, and sleep for live birth pregnancies. We investigated how these changes in pregnancy are associated with pregnancy-induced hormonal changes, since the evidence is lacking in existing studies. Furthermore, we detected differences in the resting heart rate patterns between pregnancies ended in a live birth and those ended with an adverse outcome.

Implications of all the available evidence

Existing evidence in biomedical research has proven how wearables can be used to identify physiological changes and predict adverse outcomes, e.g., in infectious diseases. Our study highlights the potential of monitoring individuals during pregnancy with wearable devices. By detecting physiological changes, we can provide real-time insights into the health of pregnant women. Furthermore, our study opens new possibilities by studying the association between hormone levels and physiological signals and how physiological signals may differ in live birth pregnancies and those that ended with an adverse outcome. Further studies are needed to investigate whether these monitored changes could be used for assessing the risk of miscarriage and early prediction of preterm birth.

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of adverse pregnancy outcomes in the United States underscores the urgent need for innovative solutions to monitor maternal and foetal health.1, 2, 3 Pregnancy complications, including miscarriage and preterm birth, continue to pose significant risks to maternal and child health, demanding more effective ways to monitor and address these outcomes. Additionally, there exist differences in maternal mortality between White and non-White women in the United States. In 2021, the maternal mortality rate for non-Hispanic Black women was 2.6 times higher than their non-Hispanic white women counterparts, being 69.9 per 100,000 live births.4 These concerning trends demand more effective methods to monitor and address pregnancy complications. Innovative approaches, such as integrating digital health technologies and artificial intelligence, are being explored to enhance maternal and foetal health monitoring. These advancements aim to provide timely interventions, improve patient engagement, and ultimately reduce the incidence of adverse outcomes.5

Pregnancy is a dynamic process corresponding to a marked increase in metabolic demand and haemodynamic adaptations that vary by trimester and regress toward normal during the postpartum period.6 Thus, early and regular prenatal care is important for monitoring these changes, while managing existing medical conditions. Major maternal haemodynamic adaptations during pregnancy include increased cardiac output and plasma volume along with a concurrent reduction in systemic vascular resistance, all of which are influenced by hormonal changes that occur during pregnancy.7,8

However, due to systemic barriers with healthcare access and utilisation, including socioeconomic disparities and geographic limitations, consistent in-person prenatal visits are not always feasible for many individuals.9,10 Beyond socioeconomic constraints, many working mothers, regardless of financial status, also face challenges in attending regular prenatal appointments due to employment demands, lack of paid leave, and childcare responsibilities. Furthermore, approximately 30–50% of birthing individuals reside in maternity care deserts, where access to obstetric care is severely limited.11

Remote monitoring and mobile health technology can address these issues. They were introduced in 2014 as a component of perinatal care and their adoption has accelerated in recent years.12, 13, 14, 15 A systematic review confirmed that remote monitoring and mobile health, in combination with increased patient empowerment and home pregnancy care, could lead to better participant satisfaction, increased efficiency, and reduction of pregnancy stress.16 More studies are needed, however, to show the impact of remote monitoring on health outcomes.

Collecting data during pregnancy is key to identifying potential threats to the wellbeing of mothers and their children. In this observational study, we utilised data collected in PowerMom, a bilingual digital research platform, which allows participants to remotely self-assess and report survey data and collect real-world data by integrating digital health technologies, such as wearable sensors, to capture information beyond the traditional, episodic measures during the standard 12–14 prenatal clinic visits. The aim of this project was to investigate whether wearable sensors have the potential to serve as a minimally invasive and widely accessible tool to monitor pregnancy-related physiological and behavioural signals in pregnancies that result in live births. We analysed wearable sensor data spanning from 3 months pre-pregnancy to 6 months after the delivery of the baby, in order to identify common patterns in resting heart rate, physical activity, and sleep. Additionally, we studied the association between wearable signals and expected changes in hormone levels, and we explored differences in physiological signals in pregnancies with adverse outcomes (Fig. 1). While the identified trends are based on data from live birth pregnancies, the adverse outcome analysis is intended as a feasibility assessment to examine whether wearable sensors can capture distinct physiological patterns in such cases. With further prospective work, we will investigate if this technology can be used to predict the onset of labour and estimate the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Methods

Study population

Participants of 16 years of age or older, currently pregnant or within 8-weeks postpartum, living within the United States and territories, and with access to a device that is supported by the PowerMom (https://powermom.scripps.edu/) application were eligible to participate in the study. Participants were recruited through public announcements of the study through multiple sources. All participants were explicitly required to provide informed consent electronically. Participants unable to consent in English or Spanish were excluded from this study. Data was collected from September 15th, 2021, to July 29th, 2024.

Ethics

The protocol for this study was reviewed and approved by the Scripps Office for the Protection of Research Subjects (IRB-21-7738). All individuals participating in the study provided informed consent electronically.

Data collection

Participants were able to complete surveys and given the option to share wearable sensor data through the PowerMom mobile research platform using the MyDataHelps™ app (CareEvolution, Inc.). Upon consent, participants were asked to complete a series of surveys at preset intervals within the PowerMom research platform. After enrolment in the study, they complete a study intake survey to collect demographic information and a “Health and History” survey to provide details on their current and past health status. Throughout the course of their pregnancy, participants are asked to complete a biweekly “Health and Well-being” survey and a bimonthly “Pregnancy Support” survey. Based on self-reported due date, participants are alerted to complete the “Delivery” survey within 4 weeks of their due date, and the “Postpartum” and “Postpartum Mental Health” surveys 6–8 weeks after that date. The data collected in these surveys were used to extract demographic information, general information about the pregnancy, due dates, pre-existing conditions, pregnancy related complications, and information about the delivery and life postpartum. Participants who joined during or after their pregnancy were able to consent to share wearable data from before enrolment, including data before the beginning of their pregnancy, if they were already wearing the device. Data was then securely transferred to Scripps Research for analysis. Wearable sensor data was collected through HealthKit or Fitbit applications from smartphones, smartwatches, and other compatible devices. More information on the design and implementation of the PowerMom digital health platform is available.17

Participants selection

This was an exploratory study, and as such, a formal sample size calculation was not conducted. Participant recruitment was maximised within the constraints of the available funding. Additionally, the sharing of wearable sensors was optional, constraining the sample size for participants included in this analysis. In the original cohort, some participants were initially identified as potentially fraudulent due to aberrations in the e-consent process. Upon reviewing their shared data, information, and completed surveys, we excluded 254 fraudulent participants from the original cohort. Excluding these fraudulent accounts, 5612 participants were enrolled in the study at the time of data collection. More information on how fraudulent accounts were identified is available in the Supplement (Supplementary Methods 1).

Only participants sharing wearable sensor data were considered for this analysis. 697 participants collected wearable sensor data with a device by Apple, Garmin, or Fitbit. Among them, 670 participants specified either their due date, their delivery date, or both. As our focus is to study how hormones relate to signals collected during live birth pregnancies, and how these differentiate with respect to pregnancies with an adverse outcome, we included only participants who reported their pregnancy outcome in a delivery survey. Among the 670 participants, 249 self-reported their pregnancy outcome in the delivery survey. Participants were asked how they completed their pregnancy: if they gave birth, they experienced a stillbirth, or the pregnancy ended otherwise.

When participants collected data both through Fitbit and HealthKit applications, we considered data from the application that reported more data throughout the pregnancy. In our analysis, we only included participants with sufficient data for the analysis. We considered as valid any week for which we had at least two days of wearable sensor data available. Selecting a minimum of two days per week should ensure enough data while maintaining signal stability and minimising gaps in weekly trends. Given that wearable sensor signals can fluctuate due to short-term variations (e.g., daily activity patterns), using at least two days helps reduce noise while still capturing longer-term trends throughout pregnancy. Previous studies have shown that two or more days per week are generally sufficient to estimate reliable signals.18,19

The beginning of the pregnancy date was set to 280 days before the self-reported due date. Participants with R < 50% were excluded (N = 139). For the analysis of RHR signals, 99 participants with live births and 9 participants with adverse outcomes were included. For step count analysis, 99 live birth participants and 9 adverse outcome participants were included. Note that the live birth groups in the RHR and step count analyses are not identical, as one participant had only sufficient RHR data, and another had only sufficient step count data. For sleep signal analysis, 54 participants with live births and 2 participants with adverse outcomes were included (Fig. 2).

Signal processing

Behavioural and physiological data acquired from wearable sensors depends on various factors. Previous work showed limited intra-individual and intra-device variability, validating the consistency of wearable sensor signals collected longitudinally.18,20 However, the intrinsic inter-individual variability of physiological metrics and the multiple purposes of the wearable devices require a careful processing of the signals. Moreover, the difference in hardware and software across manufacturers is an additional source of discrepancies. In this analysis, we mitigated both inter-individual and inter-device variability by analysing signals with respect to a pre-pregnancy baseline and aggregating individual-level data using means and confidence intervals. This approach allowed us to focus on population-level patterns across different pregnancy stages and to capture signal variations rather than absolute values, hence reducing variability caused by individual differences and device heterogeneity.

In our analysis, we focused on the signals that were collected most consistently across devices and appeared most relevant to the scope of our work. We considered three types of wearable sensor data for all participants who experienced a live birth: daily resting heart rate (RHR), number of daily steps (STEPS), and total minutes of daily sleep (SLEEP). Since different devices calculate sleep metrics in different ways, we extracted total minutes of sleep only from Fitbit sensors and included all sleep episodes of the day.

All individual signals were smoothed using a rolling mean of 7 days. Missing days were estimated through linear interpolation. We then computed the daily mean among all participants and the 95% confidence interval (CI) using empirical bootstrapping. Finally, we defined the pre-pregnancy baseline by averaging all pre-pregnancy daily values from the aggregated curve (i.e., the daily mean across all participants), using all data available from 3 months prior to the start of the pregnancy up to the day before pregnancy onset. This single baseline value was then used to shift the sensor curves, allowing for a clearer visualisation of average changes relative to pre-pregnancy levels.

To analyse pregnancy-related changes across different time periods, we aligned data in two ways. For studying early pregnancy, we selected data from 3 months before the start of pregnancy (day = 0) until delivery, with the convention that day 0 is the first day of pregnancy. For studying late pregnancy and postpartum recovery, we selected data from the start of pregnancy to 6 months after delivery, with the convention that day 0 is the day of the delivery, allowing us to assess how signals return to pre-pregnancy baselines. These two alignments, one based on the start of pregnancy and one on the delivery date, are necessary in order to analyse pre-pregnancy, pregnancy and post-partum periods, since there are variations in pregnancy duration across participants (Table 1), and it ensures consistency in analysing transitions between pre-pregnancy, pregnancy, and postpartum periods. This allowed us to have a common reference point, so that data aggregated across participants would not obscure potential trends.

Expected hormone changes in live birth pregnancies

To analyse how the signals collected through wearable sensors changed during pregnancy we evaluated them and their timing in association with expected hormonal changes in pregnancy. The key hormones in pregnancy include human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), progesterone (P), oestrogens like oestrone (E1), oestradiol (E2), and oestriol (E3), relaxin, prolactin, human placental lactogen, oxytocin, and cortisol (C). Production of these hormones play a key role in the successful continuation of the pregnancy, and hormone levels are expected to change at specific timeframes during the pregnancies (Supplementary Methods 2). In order to study the associations between physiological signals and hormones, we performed a detailed review of the literature and analysed hormone levels collected in previous studies for live birth pregnancies. We conducted a targeted PubMed search to identify relevant studies and complemented it with citation search from key review papers, prioritising sources that reported quantitative data (Supplementary Table S1).

Available pregnancy-related hormone levels included progesterone,21,22 oestrogens E1,22 E2,22,23 E3,22 cortisol,24 and hCG.23,25 We did not find values collected consistently during the pregnancy for relaxin and prolactin. We summarised characteristics of study samples from which we extracted the hormone values (Supplementary Table S1). In general, studies reported limited information about sample characteristics. While we could only find studies conducted in different countries with variation in sample characteristics, hormone levels specifically are expected to follow a similar pattern throughout pregnancy, so using average values even if from different populations is expected to provide a clinically meaningful trend to compare wearable sensor data to in this preliminary study. We handled hormone curves based on the availability from the selected study samples.

Cortisol (C), oestrone (E1), oestriol (E3)

We retrieved mean levels for C, E1, E3 in nanomoles per litre (nmol/L), for the study sample population. Cortisol levels were available throughout the entire pregnancy, starting from week 6.24 E1 and E3 levels were available throughout the pregnancy starting from week 14 and 18 respectively.22 The retrieved values were mean values for P, E2 levels in nanomoles per litre (nmol/L).

Progesterone (P), oestradiol (E2), human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)

For P, E2, and hCG, some works collected hormone levels only during the first trimester of the pregnancy, while others collected data only starting from weeks 8–10. In these cases, we merged group level data from two sources, i.e., from different studies, checking that their reported hormone levels were comparable in the overlapped period by calculating the distance between the two sources in terms of their mean squared error (MSE). Available hormone levels from two sources were interpolated to obtain daily values in the overlapping period. To account for differences in scale across hormones, the MSE was normalised by dividing the differences by the maximum value observed for each hormone prior to squaring. Thus, the relative MSE was computed as the mean of the squared differences between the daily values of the two sources in the overlapping days, each scaled by the corresponding hormone's maximum value. All available hormone levels were retrieved from the first source for the first trimester data. Hormone levels from the second source were selected starting from the first unavailable week of the first source. The relative MSEs between the two sources in the overlapping period were small for progesterone (relative MSE = 0.0013), oestradiol (MSE = 0.0003), and hCG (relative MSE = 0.3092). Progesterone (P) levels were retrieved from the first source from week 5 to week 13, and from the second source starting from week 14.21,22 E2 levels were retrieved from the first source from week 4 to week 10, and from the second source starting from week 12.22,23 HCG levels were retrieved from the first source from week 4 to week 10, and from the second source starting from week 11.23,25 The retrieved values were mean values for P, E2 levels in nanomoles per litre (nmol/L). HCG levels were retrieved as mean values for the first trimester and median values for the remaining of the pregnancy across the population of the original studies, in international units per litre (IU/L).

All retrieved hormones

Since levels of hormones in the blood during the normal menstrual cycle are very small compared to the levels reached during pregnancy, all hormone curves were extended to 0 at the day 0 of the pregnancy. As hormone levels in the literature were typically collected weekly or biweekly, hormone curves were interpolated linearly to obtain daily values throughout the pregnancy. Hormone curves were then normalised with min–max scaling technique. The final hormone levels curves matched expected pregnancy-related hormone changes described in clinical literature.26 (Supplementary Figure S1).

Correlation of hormones and wearable sensor data in live birth pregnancies

We first studied the association between changes in wearable sensor data and hormones in pregnancies that resulted in live births. To do so, we first studied the linear correlations between hormones and RHR, steps, and sleep. All individual signals were smoothed using a rolling mean of 7 days. Missing days were estimated through linear interpolation. Each curve was then normalised with min–max scaling. We then computed the linear correlations between each available hormone and each wearable signal curve. Finally, the median and interquartile ranges [Q1, Q3] between each hormone and signal curves were computed.

RHR modelling from hormones with linear regression

Among the collected wearable sensor signals, RHR is directly influenced by hormones changes (Supplementary Methods 2). Hence, we focused on how RHR changes are associated with expected hormone changes. To better assess the relationship between pregnancy hormones and RHR, we used multivariable linear regression to model the expected RHR curve in live birth pregnancies using the expected hormones curves. The expected RHR curve was computed as previously stated. All individual signals were smoothed using a rolling mean of 7 days. Missing days were estimated through linear interpolation. The daily mean among all participants was then computed to obtain the expected RHR curve. The expected RHR curve was normalised with min–max scaling, similarly to the hormone levels curves. Normalised hormone levels were used in a linear equation to estimate the average changes in RHR. The multivariable linear regression model was optimised to maximise the coefficient of determination (R2) score. The model estimated the RHR curve using as variables the hormones E1, E2, E3, P, C, and hCG:

To assess the robustness of these associations, we conducted two additional analyses. First, we ran single-variable (crude) linear regression models regressing daily mean RHR on each hormone individually. Second, we computed Spearman rank correlations between daily mean RHR and each hormone curve, to evaluate associations without assuming linearity or normality.

Signals in adverse outcome vs live birth pregnancies

Among the participants who shared sufficient wearable data and reported their pregnancy outcome, 9 reported an adverse outcome, either a miscarriage, defined as a spontaneous abortion in the first 20 weeks, or a stillbirth, the death of a foetus after 20 weeks of pregnancy. We limited our analysis to RHR, since it showed the strongest correlations with hormone changes and it exhibited the most distinct patterns during pregnancy. We compared RHR signals between pregnancies that ended in adverse outcomes and those that resulted in live births. Because the gestational age at miscarriage was not consistently reported in our study, we opted to compare the individual RHR data in the 9-month period after the start of the pregnancy, even though miscarriages occur earlier in pregnancy. Despite the limited number of participants who reported an adverse outcome, we were able to retrieve summary metrics to highlight differences in RHR patterns between live birth pregnancies and adverse outcome pregnancies. For each participant, we calculated the difference between their average RHR over two-months periods compared to the previous two months. For this metric, all individual RHR signals were smoothed using a rolling mean of 7 days. We divided the pregnancy into the following periods: months 2–3 after the beginning of the pregnancy, months 4–5, months 6–7, months 8–9, and the first two after the expected due date. For each period, we calculated the average RHR for each participant (the bimonthly RHR mean). We then computed the difference between each bimonthly RHR mean and the corresponding bimonthly mean of the preceding two months. We refer to this value as the delta of the bimonthly mean RHR. Finally, for each 2-month period considered, we computed the median and interquartile range [Q1, Q3] of the computed delta of the bimonthly mean RHR and observed the difference between live birth pregnancies and pregnancies with an adverse outcome.

Statistics

Statistical differences for demographics of participants with respect to race/ethnicity, access to care, neighbourhood, and reporting being under anxiety/depression treatment were determined through the Chi-squared test. Statistical differences in demographics of participants with respect to age at enrolment were determined through the two-sample t-test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all comparisons.

For wearable sensor signals, the 95% confidence interval using empirical bootstrapping was computed with 1000 iterations.

For modelling the expected RHR curve in live birth pregnancies using hormones, the multivariable linear regression model used ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, available in the Python package statsmodels (v0.14.0). For each variable, p-values were calculated using the t-test, and 95% confidence intervals were computed to assess the statistical significance and precision of the estimated coefficients.

For assessing the differences in RHR between live birth and adverse outcome pregnancies for all the time periods considered, p-values were computed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Results

Participants demographics

As of 29th July 2024, 5612 participants were enrolled in the PowerMom study. Out of the 697 participants who consented to share wearable data, 108 had sufficient RHR data during their pregnancy, 108 had sufficient STEPS data, and 56 had sufficient SLEEP data. The average age at enrolment of the overall participant cohort of 5612 participants was 31.1 years old (standard deviation; SD = 6.5), and the 108 participants with RHR wearable data included in our analysis were older on average (age 33.9; SD = 5.2; two-sample t test, p < 0.001). Additional differences between all enrolled participants and participants eligible for our RHR analysis were statistically significant for race and ethnicity (Chi-squared test, p = 0.001), neighbourhood (Chi-squared test, p < 0.001), and reported anxiety/depression treatment (Chi-squared test, p = 0.035). We did not find statistically significant differences between enrolled and RHR participants in terms of access to maternity care (Chi-squared test, p = 0.137). No statistically significant differences were found among RHR, STEPS, and SLEEP subsets of participants. The average duration of the pregnancy was 276.2 days (SD = 12.0) for enrolled participants, and 275.2 days (SD = 8.0) for RHR participants (Table 1). Most participants sharing wearable data had singleton pregnancies, except for two participants who had a twin pregnancy. Most enrolled participants who completed the survey about their delivery (N = 873) had live birth pregnancies (93.1%), while 6.9% reported a pregnancy loss, either miscarriage or stillbirth (i.e., an adverse outcome). Among participants selected for the RHR analysis, 99 (91.7%) participants reported a live birth pregnancy, while 9 (8.3%) participants reported an adverse outcome (Table 1).

Physiological signals from wearable sensors in live birth pregnancies

During week 1 in the first trimester of the pregnancy, we observed a decrease in average RHR with respect to pre-pregnancy levels, followed by an increase until week 5. RHR reaches a local peak in the first trimester around week 5 (mean RHR = 4.6 bpm, CI = [3.7, 5.5]). From week 5, we observed a decrease in the average RHR, reaching a minimum around week 9. After week 9, RHR increased during the second and third trimesters. RHR reached a peak (mean RHR = 9.4 bpm higher with respect to pre-pregnancy levels, CI = [8.6, 10.0]), about 8–9 weeks before the delivery, then decreased until the delivery. After the delivery, RHR dropped below pre-pregnancy levels, reaching a minimum after 7 weeks postpartum (mean RHR = −3.3 bpm, CI = [−4.5, −2.0]), stabilising around pre-pregnancy levels only about 6 months post-delivery.

The number of steps decreased during the whole pregnancy with respect to pre-pregnancy levels, reaching its minimum in the first week postpartum, as expected (mean STEPS = −3955.5, CI = [−4656.9, −3409.1], 3 days postpartum). The number of daily steps increased post-delivery, but it remained below pre-pregnancy levels even 6 months postpartum.

The total minutes of sleep increased with respect to the pre-pregnancy levels after week 5, reaching its peak between weeks 8–9, with an average of almost 40 min of additional sleep with respect to pre-pregnancy levels (mean SLEEP = 36.5 min, CI = [27.1, 54.2]). The total minutes of sleep showed a generally decreasing trend in the second and third trimesters. The total minutes of sleep dropped on delivery reaching a minimum in the first week postpartum (mean SLEEP = −51.3, CI = [−65.0, −38.4], 4 days postpartum). The total minutes of sleep remained around 20 min below the pre-pregnancy levels even after 6 months postpartum (Fig. 3) (Supplementary Table S2).

Association between hormones and sensor signals in live birth pregnancies

Expected changes in hormonal levels during pregnancy were correlated with measured wearable signals. RHR curves were positively correlated with oestrone (E1), oestradiol (E2), oestriol (E3), progesterone (P), and cortisol (C). RHR was negatively correlated with human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) (Fig. 4). On the contrary, SLEEP presented mild negative correlations with E1, E2, E3, P, and C, and was positively correlated with hCG. STEPS curves presented a mild negative correlation with E1, E2, E3, P, and C (Supplementary Figure S2).

Model of the relationship between RHR changes and expected hormones in live birth pregnancies

RHR is highly correlated with hormone changes, as confirmed by our previous analysis. Since it is known that many pregnancy-related hormones directly affect heart rate, we focused on RHR to study the associations between physiological changes and expected hormone changes during pregnancy. To showcase the relationships between expected changes in hormonal levels and measured wearable signals, we modelled the expected RHR curve through multivariable linear regression, receiving as input the expected hormone curves during pregnancy. The RHR curve was estimated from E1, E2, E3, P, C, hCG curves with a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.93. Coefficients for each hormone considered were statistically significant (t-test, p-value<0.001), positive for E1 and cortisol, negative for E2, E3, P and hCG (c0 = 0.05, 95% CI = [0.03; 0.08]; cE1 = 1.37, CI = [1.25; 1.49]; cE2 = −0.75, CI = [−0.95; −0.53]; cE3 = −0.45, CI = [−0.59; −0.31]; cP = −0.57, CI = [−0.73; −0.41]; cC = 1.04, CI = [0.94; 1.15]; chCG = −0.16, CI = [−0.20; −0.12]).

The high coefficient of determination of this model for the RHR curve showcased the close relationships between pregnancy hormones and RHR signal. The model followed the non-monotone pattern observed in the first trimester, potentially driven by the hCG peak that corresponds to the relative minimum in RHR at week 9. During the second and third trimesters, the increase in RHR was likely induced by E1, E2, E3, P, and C hormones. The decrease of RHR around the 31st and 32nd week of the pregnancy is not well captured in the model, since the length of each pregnancy is different for different individuals (Supplementary Figure S3). The assumptions of linear regression were assessed. Residual diagnostics indicated violations of normality (Shapiro–Wilk test, p < 0.001), autocorrelation (Durbin–Watson statistic = 0.06), and heteroscedasticity (Breusch–Pagan test, p < 0.001). Additionally, multicollinearity was observed among some hormonal predictors (variance inflation factors, VIF >10).

To evaluate whether these associations were sensitive to modelling assumptions, we conducted two complementary analyses. First, single-variable linear regression models of RHR on each hormone individually showed consistent direction and statistical significance, despite exhibiting similar violations of normality and homoscedasticity (Supplementary Table S3). Second, Spearman rank correlations between the daily mean RHR and each hormone curve yielded strong monotonic associations for most hormones (ρ > 0.80, all p < 1e-65), with the exception of hCG, which showed a weaker negative correlation (ρ = −0.27, p = 4.0e–6), consistent with its distinct temporal pattern early in pregnancy (Supplementary Table S4).

Physiological signals and adverse outcomes

We compared live birth pregnancies (99 participants) with pregnancies with an adverse outcome (9 participants), in terms of the delta of the bimonthly mean RHR. Due to the limited sample size, this analysis is considered preliminary. For live birth pregnancies, the delta of the bimonthly mean of the RHR with respect to the previous 2 months was positive for the entire duration of the pregnancy. In the first two months postpartum, the RHR decreased, resulting in a drop of the delta. For adverse outcome pregnancies, we see a similar positive delta in the months 2–3, while from months 4–5 after the beginning of the pregnancy the delta fluctuated around 0 without relevant changes, potentially representing an earlier termination of the pregnancy (Fig. 5). The differences between the two curves were statistically significant for months 4–5 (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.003), 6–7 (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.001), and the months 10–11 (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.001) (Supplementary Table S5). For pregnancies ending in stillbirth or miscarriage, RHR patterns differed from those observed in live birth pregnancies when examined over a 9-month period. For miscarriage and stillbirth cases, however, the timing of pregnancy loss and delivery was not consistently reported, limiting our ability to precisely map RHR fluctuations in the immediate pre- and post-loss period. While the 9-month window provides a broad comparison, future studies should aim to analyse RHR trends leading up to pregnancy loss with more precise timing data. Additional information on self-reported pre-existing conditions before the pregnancy, complications developed in past pregnancies, and complications and/or conditions diagnosed in the current pregnancy is available for both live birth and adverse outcome pregnancies (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

Our results show that signals collected via wearable sensors follow expected changes in hormonal levels and can detect unique patterns specific to live birth pregnancies, potentially allowing the monitoring of maternal health throughout the pregnancy and postpartum. This work builds on a previous analysis on data collected through the first version of the PowerMom study,27 which presented general trends of resting heart rate, sleep and activity from pregnant women enrolled in the study, however not analysing the associations with hormonal levels.

The impact of hormonal changes during pregnancies was studied in works focused on changes in the brain structure caused by the pregnancy and the correlation between these changes and changes in the endocrine system.28,29 Other works analysed how these hormone levels during normal menstrual cycle are correlated with the changes in physiological signals, such as heart rate and cardiac output.30, 31, 32 We build upon these works to analyse the associations between hormone levels and physiological signals specific to pregnancy. The observed correlations are in line with the evidence that hormones, such as progesterone and oestrogens, are associated with heart rate and heart rate variability changes.33,34 Changes in RHR during pregnancy were observed in the All of Us Research program dataset and other smaller cohorts, obtaining trends that are consistent with the ones presented in this paper.35, 36, 37 Future studies collecting both wearable data and hormone data from the same sample would be valuable to be able to identify more nuanced trends than possible in this initial exploratory study that used external hormone level estimates.

An individual will never experience such high levels of hormones throughout their life except for during pregnancy and postpartum.26,29 Since these hormones play a key role in determining the pregnancy outcome, it is important to understand if these hormonal changes during pregnancy are associated with physiological and behaviour changes that are easy-to-measure, such as an increase in resting heart rate. It has been shown that hormones such as oestrogens31,32 and cortisol38 directly affect heart rate and other peripheral metrics measurable with wearable sensors.39

Before pregnancy, RHR fluctuates as expected during the normal menstrual cycle, as reflected by the signals collected in our study through wearable sensors.33,34 During pregnancy, changes in hormone levels were strongly associated with changes in RHR. Once the pregnancy starts, the production of progesterone and oestrogens is expected to increase starting from 1 to 2 weeks after the last menstrual cycle, which defines the start of the pregnancy. Their levels rise for most of the pregnancy contributing to the increase in RHR during the pregnancy. The increase in RHR is paused and reaches an initial peak around week 5, when hCG levels start rising exponentially (around weeks 4 and 5). RHR then decreases up to week 9, when hCG is expected to reach its maximum peak (around weeks 9 and 10).40 After week 9, while the hCG decreases and all the other pregnancy hormones keep increasing, the RHR increases for the remaining of the pregnancy, until about 9 weeks before delivery. In the last 9 weeks of the pregnancy, we observed a decrease in RHR. In some mammals, parturition is enabled through progesterone withdrawal.41 In humans, while progesterone and oestrogens levels increase throughout the entire pregnancy, the body prepares to parturition by the so-called functional progesterone withdrawal.42 The functional progesterone withdrawal may be reflected by the decrease in RHR towards the end of the pregnancy. Postpartum, most of the key hormones drop dramatically, except for prolactin and oxytocin.26,29 Similarly, RHR dropped dramatically below pre-pregnancy levels, and returned to the pre-pregnancy levels around 6 months postpartum. The timing to reach pre-pregnancy levels varied individually, and it is likely related to the return to the normal menstrual cycle and end of breastfeeding.43 Although the sample size for adverse outcome pregnancies was limited and the precise timing of adverse outcomes was not available, the observed changes in RHR during the 9-month window differed from that of live birth pregnancies, showcasing a different physiology in adverse outcome pregnancies. Further prospective studies are needed to investigate whether this represents a consistent signature, which may allow for early detection of adverse outcomes.

Digital research studies like PowerMom represent the transition from brick-and-mortar research centres towards a remote, direct-to-participant approach that is far more inclusive for participants, even if they live in remote locations or do not have access to research hospitals. These studies are possible thanks to wireless sensors that can communicate continuously 24/7,44,45 and the application of machine learning techniques to process these large datasets with missing and unreliable data.46 The digital clinical trial paradigm has already been successfully adopted in infectious diseases,19,47 while only recently it has been applied to maternal health.13, 14, 15 All these research efforts come with unique challenges to assure privacy and security, that need to be addressed by properly informing individuals before consent, engaging them to become true participants in the clinical trials in which they have been enrolled.48 The digital divide in rural areas, where approximately 22% of residents lack access to high speed Internet, compared to only 1.5% in urban areas, also impacts the feasibility of digital health solutions, which require reliable internet access for effective implementation.49,50 While remote monitoring and mobile health technologies offer promising solutions to bridge these gaps, the lack of internet infrastructure in these areas must be considered when evaluating their feasibility and impact.

The observed associations between RHR changes and hormonal changes suggest that RHR may serve as an early indicator of distress during the pregnancy. For example, observing the lack of pregnancy-induced heart rate increase may be related to abnormal hormone levels in the blood, potentially signalling a risk of miscarriage. While previous preliminary work investigated whether the decrease in RHR can be used as an estimator of labour onset or an early detector of preterm delivery,51,52 larger, prospective studies are needed to confirm whether RHR changes could effectively predict labour onset and assess the risk of miscarriage, as previously hypothesised.39 Once confirmed, these findings will be particularly important for birthing individuals living in maternity care deserts, and for the postpartum population who cannot access maternity care as frequently as needed. This technology will allow remote, continuous, and ubiquitous monitoring during pregnancy, potentially detecting deviations from the expected pattern and enabling early intervention.

The sample size for this study is limited, as many participants did not own a wearable device or did not consent to share data with the PowerMom study. Additionally, while selected participants collected sufficient data from wearable sensors during pregnancy, only 54 (53.4%) participants provided data before the start of their pregnancy. However, the primary analyses of this paper focused on data during pregnancy.

While our study enrolled a diverse cohort of participants in terms of different races and ethnicities, women with wearable sensors in this study who disclosed information about their race and ethnicity are predominantly White. The differences between the enrolled participants in PowerMom and those sharing wearable data were also evident in terms of age, anxiety and depression treatment, and neighbourhood, with participants with wearables being older, more likely to report being under anxiety or depression treatment, and living in an area with better resources and higher socioeconomic status. The usage of wearable sensors may be limited due to their costs. Costs issues and lack of diversity can potentially be addressed by distributing devices to women that are planning to become pregnant and that are consenting to participate in this study.

RHR measurements may be influenced by weight and BMI changes commonly experienced during pregnancy, for which we have incomplete data. Additionally, the stratification of pregnancies into singleton and multiple gestations has not been fully investigated due to insufficient data. Pre-existing medical conditions such as anaemia, hypertension, and infections, as well as medications that can affect RHR or other physiological parameters, have not been accounted for in our analysis. Geographic and environmental factors, which may also influence RHR, were not considered.

Our study included participants using multiple commercial wearable devices (Apple, Garmin, Fitbit), presenting differences across brands that may introduce variability in sensor-derived metrics, due to differences in technologies and algorithms used.

Moreover, some data such as delivery and due dates is self-reported through app-based surveys, thus prone to potential errors and missing information. Sleep metrics were collected from Fitbit devices only, hence the sample size is smaller for these metrics. As participation required access to a compatible wearable device and digital literacy, our findings may not fully generalise to populations with limited access to these technologies.

Since we did not collect blood samples in our study, all hormone levels used in our analysis rely on data collected from other published studies. For some of these studies, the number of participants included in the analysis was not clarified. Hormone levels were also retrieved from studies where data was not available through the whole pregnancy. Since we were able to retrieve sufficient data for only some of the key hormones in pregnancy, further data needs to be collected to investigate the relationships with other hormones, like prolactin, placental lactogen or relaxin. Moreover, the regression analysis using hormones was intended to explore associations between hormone levels and physiological signals, not to make formal predictions or causal inferences. While several assumptions of linear regression were violated, we conducted complementary robustness checks using single-variable models and non-parametric Spearman correlations. These analyses confirmed that the observed associations between hormones and RHR are consistent in direction and strength, even when evaluated with alternative statistical approaches that do not rely on linear regression assumptions. Future work with individual-level, paired hormone and wearable data will be essential to confirm these associations using more robust statistical approaches.

Additionally, the number of participants with adverse outcomes was small (N = 9), limiting our ability to draw robust conclusions about these pregnancies and to observe distinct trends for miscarriage and stillbirth. This analysis should be considered a feasibility assessment for leveraging wearable sensors in the context of adverse pregnancy outcomes, warranting validation in larger cohorts. While RHR trends were analysed over a 9-month period for pregnancies ending in miscarriage and stillbirth, this approach may not be optimal if the pregnancy loss was due to a miscarriage, which occur in the first 20 weeks of pregnancy. The lack of precise data on gestational age at miscarriage and stillbirth limited our ability to analyse perimortem RHR trends. Future studies should focus on refining the timing of physiological data collection relative to pregnancy loss events, including a larger number of individuals with adverse outcomes. One limitation for studying adverse outcomes in PowerMom may be related to the fact that participants who experienced an adverse outcome in the past may be reluctant to join the study. However, while most of the participants selected for the analysis already experienced pregnancy in the past (81.1%), less than half of the selected participants (48.1%) already gave birth in the past. Hence, we were able to capture participants who were pregnant in the past but who did not successfully complete their pregnancy (Table 1). Factors such as access to care, ADI, and other sociodemographic variables could provide additional insights to explain differences between live birth and adverse outcome pregnancies. However, the available data was not sufficient to incorporate these variables to account for differences in RHR trends. Future studies should aim to include larger sample sizes and integrate additional sociodemographic factors to better understand differences between outcomes.

This work provides early evidence that wearable sensor signals are associated with pregnancy-related hormone changes during pregnancy and can be used to monitor physiological and behavioural changes during pregnancy and postpartum. As more participants enrol in our study, future analysis will focus on understanding whether these detected patterns are consistent across subgroups of birthing and postpartum participants, based on age, race and ethnicity, their access to maternity care, geography, and environmental factors, towards a more accurate, precision-based medicine. Future work will incorporate more data and individual-level modelling to better understand physiological variability leading to labour onset, preterm birth, and miscarriage. After large prospective clinical studies, we may demonstrate that changes in RHR are associated with labour onset, potentially opening new directions in risk stratification and early detection of preterm birth and miscarriage. This will be particularly relevant for people living in maternity care deserts, in which prenatal and postnatal care is difficult to access and remotely monitoring may aid in timely intervention and improved outcomes for both birthing people and children.
