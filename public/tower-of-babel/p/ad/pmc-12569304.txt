A rare case of primary transverse colon adenocarcinoma penetrating the abdominal wall
Authors: Boren Tan, Lei Liu, Huiming Wu, Qi Bao, Xiaoming Xu, Hongfeng Cao, Liuhong Wang, Yeting Hu, Jingjing Wu, Jian Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most prevalent malignancy globally and the second leading cause of cancer mortality as of 2020. Its clinical presentation varies by stage, progressing from asymptomatic early disease to advanced symptoms (e.g., rectal bleeding, bowel habit alterations), while unusual manifestations may signal locally advanced disease. Locally advanced CRC is rare, without metastasis representing a critical therapeutic window for curative surgery or neoadjuvant therapy. However, primary locally advanced CRC penetrating the abdominal wall from the intra-abdominal cavity remain unreported. In this report, we describe a rare case of a 78-year-old male who had primary invasive transverse colon adenocarcinoma penetrating the abdominal wall, forming an externally visible mass measuring approximately 9 × 5 cm without lymph node or distinct metastasis. After the pathologically diagnosis of colon adenocarcinoma, the patient received a radical resection surgery combined with abdominal wall reconstruction using a skin flap, followed by six months of capecitabine-based adjuvant chemotherapy. The patient showed no relapse or progression during the one year follow-up after the operation. This rare case highlights the ongoing challenges in managing locally advanced CRC and underscores the potential benefits of active curative treatment strategies for such patients.Keywords: Colorectal, Primary tumor, Penetration, Radical resection surgery, Adenocarcinoma

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most common cancer worldwide [1] and the second most common deadly cancer worldwide as of 2020 [2]. Colorectal cancer presents with markedly different clinical features depending on the stage of disease progression. Early-stage colorectal cancer is often clinically silent, but as the disease progresses, characteristic presenting symptoms may gradually emerge including altered bowel habits, abdominal discomfort, persistent pain and per-rectal bleeding. However, unusual symptoms can also be observed, which might indicate the cancer is locally advanced.

In all CRC cases, locally advanced cases without distant metastases are extremely uncommon, comprising only 5% to 22% of all CRC cases [3]. Locally advanced colorectal cancer lies at the boundary of a “zone of hope,” where precise clinical judgment can make curative outcomes possible. A substantial proportion of patients may achieve complete resection through meticulous surgical intervention, while others may become eligible for curative surgery following tumor down-staging via neoadjuvant chemotherapy, thus attracting considerable attention from high-level clinical and translational research [4–6]. Regarding this, a preliminary assessment of the disease stage based on clinical manifestations is crucial for guiding subsequent treatment strategies. Locally advanced transverse colon tumors often cause perforation or invade adjacent organs, such as peritoneum, stomach, duodenum, upper jejunum, liver and spleen, whereas primary colorectal tumors penetrating the abdominal wall from within the abdominal cavity are extremely rare and have not been reported to date.

Herein, we reported a case of a 78-year-old man with primary penetrating transverse CRC measuring approximately 9 × 5 cm who presented with no lymph node or distinct metastasis. After careful evaluation, the tumor was pathologically diagnosed as well-differentiated adenocarcinoma and then underwent radical resection. In the following one year, the patient showed no evidence of metastasis or local recurrence.

Case presentation

A 78-year-old man was admitted to the hospital with a one-month history of abdominal pain and a visibly large ulcerated mass on the abdominal wall. On admission, the patient presented with symptoms of nausea, melena, and vomiting dark gastric contents 2–3 times per day. Physical examination found a mass approximately 9 × 5 cm in size located in the subxiphoid region of the abdominal wall, with surface ulceration, bleeding, and purulent discharge (Fig. 1A). The surrounding skin tissue showed signs of necrosis, lighted dark or deep bluish-green. In addition, he appeared to be emaciated, with a BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m². His facial skin was pale and lacked elasticity, and his hair was dry, exhibiting noticeable signs of anemia.

The patient proposed a history of sigmoid colon cancer surgery over 20 years ago, with pathology indicating moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma and lymph node metastasis. He underwent 8 cycles of the FOLFOX4 regimen as adjuvant chemotherapy and achieved clinical complete response (CCR). He denied any history of other significant diseases, other surgeries, but reported a family history of malignant gastrointestinal tumors. His mother and brother both passed away due to colorectal cancer.

In hospital, we underwent the abdominal contrast-enhanced CT scan for him, which revealed an irregularly enhanced mass measuring 8.8 × 5.3 × 5.5 cm in size invading the abdominal wall and gastric wall, suspected to be a malignant tumor originating from the transverse colon (Fig. 2A). The CT scan did not detect any enlarged lymph nodes or nodules in the abdomen suggestive of metastasis. A biopsy of the abdominal wall mass was then carried out, and indicated a diagnosis of transverse colon adenocarcinoma, with a Kras p.G12V mutation and microsatellite stability (MSS) molecularly. Consistently, the laboratory tests were examined, and the results exhibited that he had severe anemia with a hemoglobin level of only 54 g/L. A silver lining, however, was that his coagulation function and platelet levels were normal. Considering the patient’s significant tumor burden and the high risk of nutritional depletion and hemorrhagic shock, we performed a complete resection of the mass, the surrounding abdominal wall, and part of the infiltrated gastric wall after blood transfusion (Fig. 1B). After completely removing the tumor, we repaired the defect using the surrounding abdominal wall. (Fig. 1C-D) The excised specimen showed a cauliflower-like protrusion in the intestinal cavity, which invaded and broke through the surrounding abdominal wall and skin (Fig. 1E-F). Intraoperative exploration of the abdominal cavity revealed no evidence of possible metastatic lymph nodes, consistent with the preoperative CT findings. The patient’s postoperative recovery was uneventful, and he was discharged home after correcting all the complications.

CT scanning of the patient before and after surgery. (A) CT enhanced scan revealed a poorly-margined lesion penetrating the abdominal wall with irregular shape and uneven density before surgery. (B) CT plain scan after surgery showed no significant masses and metastatic lymph nodes

Subsequently, histology of the transverse colon showed well-differentiated adenocarcinoma invading and breaching to epidermis and gastric wall with none out of 18 regional lymph nodes involved (Fig. 3A). The colon cancer was classified as pT4bN0, according to the TNM classification of the American Joint Committee on Cancer Eights Edition. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining (Fig. 3B) showed β-catenin (+), CK20 (+), P53 (+), Ki-67 (90%+), MLH1 (+) while BRAF (−). To reduce the potential for recurrence and metastasis, the oncologist recommended that the patient undergo capecitabine chemotherapy for 6 months. We utilized detailed physical examinations and imaging studies (ultrasound and CT) during subsequent follow-up visits to ensure treatment continuity. The patient exhibited no evidence of metastasis or local recurrence after one year of follow-up. During the most recent follow-up, the patient was in stable general condition, able to perform daily activities such as eating and passing stool independently. The surgical wound was healing well, with no significant signs of hyperplasia. The patient’s nutritional status was gradually improving, and the anemia had been corrected.

Discussion

Colorectal cancer is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors worldwide. Nowadays, owing to the advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, the overall survival rates of colorectal cancer have been steadily improving. However, the survival rates can be seriously shortened, mostly in the presence of distant metastasis in the following years [7]. Colorectal cancer is prone to metastasizing to nearby lymph nodes, the omentum, and abdominal organs such as the liver. Metastasis to the abdominal wall is rare and often because of incision site seeding following the resection of the primary colorectal malignant tumor, generally indicating advanced or recurrent disease [8]. Meanwhile, previous studied have also reported the port site metastasis and intraperitoneal tumour dissemination, although the underlying role of laparoscopic surgery on abdominal wall metastasis is not well understood [9]. To date, there have been no reported cases of primary CRC invading and then penetrating abdominal wall.

In this case, we described a moderately differentiated primary transverse colon tumor advanced to and perforated the abdominal wall. This remarkably huge tumor also invaded the adjacent gastric wall but did not cause metastasis to the surrounding draining lymph nodes. Invasion to the abdominal wall may occur by tumor cell implantation, hematogenous or lymphatic pathway, as well as direct invasion [10]. Moreover, clinical and experimental studies have put forward that tumor metastasis can also result from surgical trauma [11]. This patient underwent laparoscopic resection for sigmoid colon cancer over twenty years ago, but no visible involvement was observed at the port site and the fistula scar. Metastasis of colorectal cancer often occurs within five years after the appearance of the primary tumor, with the natural course of the disease typically not exceeding ten years [12], which can not adequately account for this case. More importantly, this cauliflower-like protrusion of the tumor growing out of the intestinal lumen is more likely to be primary rather than a recurrence or metastasis of the incision. Magee T. et al. revealed that injury to skeletal muscle may alter muscle physiology, rendering it more susceptible to metastatic disease at abdominal wall [13]. The patient did not report a clear history of abdominal wall muscle trauma, however notably, the patient had been in a state of malnutrition for an extended period owing to the profound tumor burden, with a BMI of 16.5 kg/m2 upon admission. It seems that the fragile abdominal wall was more susceptible to perforation after tumor invasion because of the low muscle and subcutaneous fat contents. Further observation of a larger population of such cases is required to clarify the precise mechanisms.

Histopathological findings of our patient’s surgical specimens showed proliferation of cancer cells of various morphologies and sizes. And the immunohistochemical staining indicated that tumor nuclei were positive for β-catenin and MLH1. β-catenin are key events in the Wnt signaling pathway, of which the modification and degradation significantly impacts on the proliferation and cell stemness of colon cancer cells [14]. Also, β-catenin dependent pathway can also enhance cell migration, immune escape and metastasis [15, 16]. The immunohistochemistry of the resected tumor revealed MLH1 positivity, consistent with the preoperative biopsy indicating MSS (microsatellite stability). MSS in cancer cells indicates that the patient’s mismatch repair system is intact, although it may imply reduced efficacy of PD-1 therapy.

Stage II (T3-4N0M0) colorectal cancer accounts for approximately 30% of colorectal cancers, with a 5-year survival rate ranging from 70% to 80%. Almost 25% of patients develop distant metastasis, with survival rates similar to those of stage III patients, while the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy post-surgery remains unclear [17]. Part of stage II colorectal cancer patients with high-risk features may benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy to improve survival [18], with pT4 being an independent high-risk factor [19]. Moreover, increasing number of researches revealed that, compared to stage II colorectal cancer patients with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), those with MSS may have higher metastatic potential and a worse prognosis [20, 21]. The patient in our study was comprehensively assessed with a tumor stage of pT4bN0M0, indicating stage II, and MSS. Consequently, we implemented a six-month regimen of capecitabine therapy and conducted timely follow-up to monitor the patient’s subsequent condition after the complete resection of the tumor, to reduce the potential risk of recurrence and metastasis. However, due to the unignorable variability in risk factors and heterogeneity of each patient, larger-scale studies are needed in the future to explore and establish more clinically beneficial consensus for patient management.

Conclusions

We reported the first case of primary transverse colon adenocarcinoma penetrating the abdominal wall without lymph node or distinct metastasis. Despite the locally advanced properties, the patients still have the opportunity to undergo radical surgery to achieve clinical complete response and an ideal prognosis. This case contributes new insights and addresses an existing gap regarding similar instances.
