Immunocompromised patients with viral severe acute respiratory infection in intensive care: an Australia wide, retrospective, observational study
Authors: Priyanka Hastak, Christopher R. Andersen, Win Wah, Sze J. Ng, Andrew A. Udy, Sarah C. Sasson, Aidan Burrell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to compare the clinical characteristics, treatments and outcomes of immunocompromised patients with viral severe acute respiratory infections (SARI), to those of immunocompetent patients admitted to an intensive care unit (ICU) across Australia.MethodsRetrospective analysis of a prospective, nation-wide, observational registry of 46 ICUs from June 2022 to August 2025. Critically ill adults (age ≥ 18) with laboratory-confirmed viral SARI were included. Immunocompromised status was defined by the presence of any one of the following: chronic immunosuppression, malignant neoplasm, AIDS/HIV, or organ transplantation. Associations between immunocompromised status and in-hospital mortality were evaluated using mixed-effects logistic regression models. Competing risks regression (Fine and Gray models) was used to assess the association between immunocompromised status and risk of hospital discharge within 30 days.ResultsAmong 4,703 patients with viral SARI who were admitted to ICUs, immunocompromised patients accounted for 908 (19.3%) cases. Immunocompromised patients were older (median age 67 vs. 62 years), more comorbid (31.4% vs. 22.5% with ≥ 3 comorbidities), and more frequently admitted with COVID-19 (51.1% vs. 34.5%); (p < 0.001 for all). Immunocompromised patients received more treatment, including antivirals (67.3% vs. 57.7%), and corticosteroids (81.7% vs. 72%) and had higher in-hospital mortality (22.7% vs. 13.1%); (p < 0.01 for all). After adjusting for demographics, comorbidities, vaccination status (where available), ICU interventions and treatments, immunocompromised status was independently associated with nearly double the odds of in-hospital mortality (adjusted OR 1.93, 95% CI 1.57–2.37).ConclusionsIn this study, we found that immunocompromised patients accounted for approximately one-fifth of critically ill viral SARI patients and had nearly twice the odds of in-hospital mortality, when compared to those that were immunocompetent. Targeted public health campaigns and improved treatment strategies may be warranted for this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13054-026-06192-6.

Background

Viral severe acute respiratory infection (SARI) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Even outside pandemic years, SARI generates a vast patient, health care and economic impact, with costs exceeding US$500 billion per year [1]. Understanding how high-risk populations, such as the immunocompromised, are affected by viral SARI is crucial to develop preventative health strategies, improve therapeutic approaches and guide public policy.

This is particularly pertinent as the proportion of critically ill patients with immunosuppression has increased over recent years [2–4]. This group of people includes those on long-term steroids and/or immunosuppressive drugs for autoimmune/inflammatory diseases, those with solid organ and haematological malignancies and people who have undergone solid organ transplants. However, despite the public health importance, there are currently limited high-quality nation-wide data regarding the incidence of viral SARI in immunocompromised hosts, common causative pathogens, ICU management and clinical outcomes.

SPRINT SARI Australia is a national surveillance registry that has collected data from patients with viral SARI admitted to ICUs nation-wide from 2020 to 2025. This platform provides an opportunity to systematically examine the burden of viral SARI in immunocompromised patients across Australia. This study aimed to characterise the incidence, demographics, pathogens, co-morbidities, management and outcomes in immunocompromised patients with viral SARI admitted to ICUs and compare them to immunocompetent individuals.

Methods

Study design and setting

Prospectively collected data from the short-period incidence study of severe acute respiratory infection (SPRINT-SARI) Australia were retrospectively analysed [5, 6]. SPRINT-SARI Australia is a multicentre, observational study of patients admitted with SARI and laboratory-confirmed viral respiratory pathogens, (i.e. including COVID-19 and non-COVID-19) in 46 Australian ICUs. Prior to June 2022, only COVID-19 data were collected, but from this timepoint both COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 SARI cases were recoded. For this study, we only included data collected on or after June 1, 2022 and only from sites after they began reporting both COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 viral SARI cases to ensure consistent case ascertainment and reduce biases related to differences in testing and admission patterns during earlier COVID-19 phases.

Study population

We included all adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) admitted to ICUs at participating sites with viral SARI due to laboratory-confirmed respiratory pathogens. Individuals were excluded if they were admitted with incidental infection (defined as a positive PCR but did not receive treatment consistent with SARI), or if data concerning immunosuppression status, ICU admission year, and hospital outcomes, were incomplete. Individuals were excluded if they were admitted with incidental infection (defined as a positive PCR but did not receive treatment consistent with SARI because the identified pathogen was not considered the primary reason for ICU admission). We excluded individuals with non-viral SARI including bacterial, fungal, and parasitic co-infections. Characteristics of excluded patients in Supplementary Table 6.

Variables

Immunocompromised status in the SPRINT-SARI Australia dataset followed the APACHE definition by specific diagnoses or treatments that confer significant immune compromise that includes: (1) Hematologic malignancy (leukaemia, lymphoma); (2) Metastatic cancer; (3) HIV/AIDS with severe immunodeficiency; (4) Recent or active chemotherapy for malignancy; (5) Solid-organ transplant on immunosuppressive therapy; (6) Other ongoing immunosuppressive treatment (e.g., high-dose/long-term corticosteroids, cytotoxic drugs, biologic immunosuppressants) (7, 8). Additional variables extracted for analysis included age, sex, ICU admission year, laboratory-confirmed respiratory pathogen primarily responsible for ICU admission, body mass index (BMI), obesity status defined on the basis of a BMI ≥ 30 or by measurement of abdominal girth, number of comorbidities, vaccination status (where available), duration of mechanical ventilation, use of invasive mechanical ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), renal replacement therapy (RRT), prone positioning, receipt of agents, antibiotics, corticosteroids, and monoclonal antibodies targeting SARS-CoV-2, e.g. Evusheld (tixagevimab/cilgavimab), or IL-6 e.g. tocilizumab during ICU admission, ICU and hospital lengths of stay, ICU discharge location (Supplementary Table 5).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death in hospital at any time during the index hospital admission. The secondary outcome was hospital discharge within 30 days of ICU admission.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographics, admission characteristics, interventions and outcomes were compared between the immunocompromised and non-immunocompromised groups using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, and t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between immunocompromised status and in-hospital mortality. To account for clustering of patients within hospitals, mixed-effects logistic regression models were fitted with hospital site as a random effect. Multivariate models were adjusted for covariates based on baseline differences and established clinical predictors of mortality (variables mentioned above). Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the association between immune-competence and in-hospital mortality stratified by type of respiratory pathogen, invasive mechanical ventilation status, and cause of compromised immunity.

To assess the association between immunocompromised status and the likelihood of hospital discharge within 30 days, competing risks regression models (Fine and Gray) were used to estimate sub-distribution hazard ratios (SHRs) and 95% CIs. Time to event was defined as days from ICU admission to hospital discharge (event) or in-hospital death (competing event). Follow-up was administratively truncated at 30 days after ICU admission. Patients who remained hospitalised beyond 30 days were censored at day 30. For these analyses, in-hospital death within 30 days was treated as a competing event. Robust standard errors clustered at the hospital level were used to account for clustering of patients within sites.

For categorical variables with missing data, missing values were coded as a separate category to allow inclusion of all available cases in multivariable analyses. For continuous variables (age), cases with missing data were excluded from multivariable analyses (< 0.07% missing).

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

Between 1 June 2022 and 4 August 2025, 4,703 patients met the inclusion criteria from 46 sites (see Fig. 1). The immunocompromised cohort comprised 908 patients (19.3%) while 3,795 patients (80.7%) were non-immunocompromised. The majority of immunocompromised patients had chronic immunosuppression or a malignant neoplasm (Supplementary Table 1). The total number of patients in the database increased each year from 2022 to 2024, with the most recent year comprising 2,169/4,703 (46.1%) of the total cohort.

The demographic, admission characteristics, interventions, and outcomes of SARI patients by immunocompromised status are shown in Table 1. The median age was higher in immunocompromised patients (67 years, IQR 57–75) compared with non-immunocompromised patients (62 years, IQR 46–73; p < 0.001). The prevalence of obesity was lower among immunocompromised patients compared with non-immunocompromised patients (11.2% vs. 15.2%, p = 0.01). Immunocompromised patients were more comorbid, with 285 (31.4%) having ≥ 3 comorbidities (e.g. cardiac disease, chronic pulmonary disease, asthma, chronic kidney disease, rheumatologic disorder, liver disease, dementia, chronic neurological disorder, hematologic disease) compared with 855 (22.5%) in the non-immunocompromised group (p < 0.001). Diagnostic categories accounting for an immunocompromised state included chronic immunosuppression (66.3%), malignant neoplasm (52%), organ transplant (12.4%), and HIV/AIDS (2.9%).

Over half of the immunocompromised cohort were admitted with COVID-19 (n = 464, 51.1%), followed by influenza A (20.7%) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) (n = 61, 6.7%). In contrast, non-immunocompromised patients were more evenly distributed with respect to causative pathogen, with 34.5% admitted with COVID-19, 31.3% with influenza A, and numerically greater proportions of influenza B and RSV compared with immunocompromised patients (Table 1). The top five causes of other types of non-COVID viral respiratory pathogens are listed in the Supplementary Materials 1.

With respect to treatments, antiviral use was more frequent among immunocompromised patients compared with non-immunocompromised patients (67.3% vs. 57.7%, p < 0.001). Similarly, antimicrobials (93.9% vs. 91.7%, p = 0.024), corticosteroids (81.7% vs. 72%, p < 0.001), and monoclonal antibodies (5.5% vs. 1.9%, p < 0.001) were administered more frequently to immunocompromised patients; >70% of administered monoclonal antibodies targeted the IL-6 pathway (i.e. toculizumab or sarilumab).

Overall, one-third of patients required mechanical ventilation (33.3%). Immunocompromised patients were less likely to receive this intervention (26.8% vs. 34.8% p < 0.001) and ECMO was used in 9 immunocompromised patients (1%) compared to 84 (2.2%) non-immunocompromised patients. Prone positioning during ICU admission was reported in 2.8% of patients, with no significant difference between groups. Approximately one in ten patients required RRT, with similar rates in both groups (Table 1).

Outcomes

The median ICU length of stay was 4 days (IQR 2–7), which was the same in both groups. The median length of hospital stay was longer in immunocompromised patients (12 days, IQR 6–23) compared with non-immunocompromised patients (10 days, IQR 6–18) Table 1). ICU mortality was higher in immunocompromised patients (14.1% vs. 9.6%, p < 0.001), and hospital mortality was also greater (22.7% vs. 13.1%, p < 0.001).

We performed univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses to identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality (Table 2; Fig. 2). After adjusting for covariates, immunocompromised status was associated with nearly double the odds of mortality in the adjusted model (OR 1.93, 95% CI 1.57–2.37). Increasing age was strongly associated with higher mortality, while female patients had lower odds of in-hospital death (OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.67–0.95). Receipt of invasive mechanical ventilation was strongly associated with greater odds of in-hospital mortality (OR 3.67 95% CI 3.02–4.46). Although univariate analysis suggested decreasing mortality over time, this was not confirmed in the multivariable model.

We further examined the association between immunocompromised status (versus non-immunocompromised status) and in-hospital mortality in different sub-groups. Immunocompromise was associated with greater odds of in-hospital mortality in almost all patient categories (Supplementary Table 2). Subtypes of immunocompromised status demonstrated an increased odds of mortality with chronic immunosuppression (OR 1.83, 95% CI 1.44–2.32) and malignant neoplasm (OR 2.22, 95% CI 1.72–2.87, p < 0.001), whereas no significant associations were identified for patients with solid organ transplantation or HIV/AIDS. (Supplementary Table 1). In sensitivity analyses, the association between immunocompromised status and in-hospital mortality remained consistent, including models excluding COVID-19 vaccination status, ICU interventions and treatment variables, and chronic hematologic disease from the comorbidity count (OR range 1.75–1.95; Supplementary Table 4).

Within 30 days of ICU admission, the cumulative incidence of hospital discharge was lower among immunocompromised patients compared with non-immunocompromised patients, with this trend evident within the first week (Fig. 3). Within 30 days of ICU admission, 3,589 patients (76.3%) were discharged from hospital, 660 (14.0%) died in hospital, and 454 (9.7%) remained hospitalised and were therefore censored at day 30. At day 30, 67.4% of immunocompromised patients had been discharged compared with 78.4% of non-immunocompromised patients (Gray’s test p < 0.001).

Discussion

Immunocompromised patients accounted for nearly 20% of all ICU admissions to SPRINT-SARI sites in Australia, suggesting this group is over-represented given estimates of immunocompromise in the general population is 2–3% [9]. Immunocompromised patients were older and had higher levels of comorbidities, reflecting increased medical complexity. Immunocompromised patients experienced nearly double the odds of in-hospital mortality compared to non-immunocompromised patients even after adjusting for demographics, comorbidities, vaccination status, ICU interventions and treatments. Immunocompromised patients received antiviral therapies more frequently, which likely reflects differences in pathogen distribution and treatment indications, given the higher prevalence of COVID-19 and influenza A infections in this group.

These findings are consistent with previous studies that demonstrated increased mortality among immunocompromised patients with respiratory infections including virus-detected acute respiratory failure, ventilator-associated lower respiratory tract infection and severe COVID-19 in the ICU [10–15]. In our study, immunocompromised patients accounted for 19% of ICU admissions for viral SARI, consistent with previous studies reporting 12% to 30% for severe respiratory infections [10–15].

There are several potential reasons why immunocompromised patients may have higher mortality. Firstly, they may be more vulnerable to different infections – opportunistic, health-care associated and multi-resistant pathogens (viruses, bacteria and fungi) that don’t respond as well to treatment [16]. Second, the immunocompromised status means innate, or adaptive immune responses are likely to be impaired resulting in higher pathogen burden and slower clearance. Thirdly, immunocompromised patients were older and more comorbid, with other diseases that may impact their host response and ability to combat infection, as well as underlying chronic diseases or transplantation, which reduce physiological reserve and increase the risk of ICU-related complications. Interestingly, the rate of invasive mechanical ventilation was lower in the immunocompromised population (despite similar markers of severity at presentation), which may reflect systematic differences in management, including withholding a treatment because of perceived worse prognosis or higher complications, or due to treatment limitations.

Sub-group analyses indicated that the influence of immunocompromise on mortality was consistent across major respiratory pathogens, including COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 (influenza A, RSV, and other types), suggesting that underlying immune dysfunction rather than the specific pathogen was the key driver of poor outcomes. This aligns with a previous study, which reported that mortality did not vary much across types of respiratory virus. We also found that the association between immunocompromise and mortality persisted regardless of invasive mechanical ventilation, consistent with the prior study [17, 18]. Thus, these results reinforce the consistent, adverse influence of impaired immunity in SARI.

Immunocompromised patients had longer hospital stay compared to non-immunocompromised counterparts suggesting higher healthcare resources usage, as previously reported [19–21]. However, the duration of mechanical ventilation and ICU length of stay were similar. Steroids were commonly administered in both groups. As the majority of patients had COVID-19, it is probable such treatment was guided by the respiratory pathogen isolated, rather than immune status alone. Similarly, the greater utilization of IL-6 antagonists in the immunocompromised group, is likely related to the greater proportion of COVID-19 infections.

The strengths of this study are that it is one of the first nation-wide studies to investigate the impact that immunocompromised status has on outcomes of patients admitted to ICU with viral SARI. The study captures data from over 4700 patients, across 46 sites in Australia, ensuring broad geographic representation and generalizability to similar high-income healthcare settings. It spans multiple respiratory virus seasons, providing robust and recent population-level estimates of incidence and outcomes. Our study includes detailed clinical data including causative pathogens, treatments, interventions, and outcomes, enabling comprehensive adjusted analyses to identify independent risk factors for mortality.

However, we acknowledge several limitations. While a broad range of immunocompromised conditions were captured, the SPRINT SARI Australia database did not record detailed information on the degree or duration of immunosuppression, specific immunosuppressive regimens, or recent changes in therapy, all of which may influence outcomes and therefore lacked granularity. We also acknowledge that immunocompromised status encompasses a heterogeneous spectrum of conditions associated with varying mechanisms and degrees of immune dysfunction. The study also lacked detailed information on comorbidity severity, and whether ACE inhibitor / AR2 blocker use reflected current or historical use. Additionally, microbiological testing practices may have varied across sites and over time, potentially affecting pathogen detection rates and the proportion of cases without identified causative organisms. In addition, we included patients with viral infections as the primary cause and excluded patients with secondary and/or opportunistic non-viral infections, which we acknowledge introduces selection bias. Our work was focussed on the relationship between immunocompromised status and the primary viral infection. While we adjusted for multiple confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as frailty, functional status, or goals of care discussions cannot be excluded. Missing categorical data were modelled as separate unknown categories to minimise data loss; however, this approach may introduce bias if missingness was not completely random. There was no illness severity score, such as Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) or Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), as well as no information on non-invasive mechanical ventilation or degree of hypoxia in this study. In addition, details regarding the specific type of chronic immunosuppression or malignant neoplasm were not part of the database collection and there is significant overlap between the immunocompromised categories. We also had a small sample size for HIV-infection with no further information on HIV/AIDS status including CD4 + T cell count or treatment. Notably, transplant subgroups and vaccination data were missing in > 70% of the patient population. Subgroups such as HIV/AIDS, transplant recipients, influenza B and parainfluenza groups were noticeably smaller, and should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive estimates of effect.

This study may be affected by selection bias arising from differences in referral and ICU admission practices. For the same level of symptoms and clinical observations prior to ICU admission, immunocompromised patients are more likely to be referred for hospital care and more likely to be admitted to ICU than immunocompetent patients. ICU admission is generally expected to improve outcomes for admitted patients, and this benefit is unlikely to be lower in immunocompromised individuals. As a result, immunocompromised patients in this ICU cohort may represent a relatively less severe subset of all immunocompromised patients with severe acute respiratory infection. Notably, immunocompromised patients may be admitted at different clinical thresholds than immunocompetent patients, which complicates interpretation of clinical outcomes and mortality. Finally, SPRINT-SARI Australia captures only patients admitted to ICU, thereby vulnerable to selection bias related to ICU admission practices, potentially missing immunocompromised patients who died before ICU admission. ICU admission itself is not a biologically neutral selection process.

Future research should focus on identifying specific immunocompromised subgroups at highest mortality, risk, evaluating novel therapeutic approaches in randomized controlled trials conducted in these populations, and developing risk stratification tools to guide clinical decision-making and resource allocation. Ultimately, improving outcomes for immunocompromised patients with SARI will require a multifaceted approach encompassing enhanced prevention, early recognition, tailored management strategies, and healthcare system preparedness to meet the complex needs of this growing patient population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, immunocompromised patients with viral SARI were relatively common and represent a high-risk population, with significantly elevated mortality rates and resource utilisation compared to non-immunocompromised patients, even in highly resourced settings. Despite advances in supportive care and the availability of targeted therapies, outcomes remain poor in this vulnerable population. These findings underscore the need for further prevention strategies and the development of evidence-based treatment guidelines specifically designed for immunocompromised patients with SARI.
