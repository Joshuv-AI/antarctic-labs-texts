Arthroscopic-Assisted Intraosseous Bioplasty of the Humeral Head for Osteonecrosis
Authors: John W. Yurek, Paul E. Caldwell, Eric E. Peterson, Sara E. Pearson
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Osteonecrosis of the humeral head is an uncommon condition, and treatment options are controversial. The shoulder is the second most common location for osteonecrosis, typically presenting between the second and fifth decades of life. Early diagnosis and treatment are essential because osteonecrosis may progress and lead to significant pain and loss of function. Nonoperative options are limited and are based on addressing the cause of the osteonecrosis. Multiple surgical treatments have been described, and these techniques continue to evolve. Open core decompression of the humeral head has been found to be an effective surgical option to restore blood supply and stimulate new bone formation. The evolution of arthroscopic techniques combined with biological adjuncts allows a minimally invasive approach with potential to accelerate revascularization and bone growth. We describe our technique for arthroscopic-assisted intraosseous bioplasty of the humeral head for the treatment of osteonecrosis.

Osteonecrosis of the humeral head most commonly occurs in men between the second and fifth decades of life.1 Multiple direct and associated risk factors have been shown to increase the risk of this condition, including trauma, radiation, chemotherapy, sickle cell disease, alcohol abuse, tobacco use, long-term systemic corticosteroid administration, and autoimmune diseases.1 The incidence of osteonecrosis of the humeral head is equivalent to that seen in the knee, but this condition is less common than osteonecrosis of the hip.1 Despite the lack of consensus on the pathogenesis of this condition, the disruption of blood supply leads to increased intraosseous pressure and subsequent bone death.2 Without early diagnosis and proper treatment, osteonecrosis compromises the subchondral bone and may progress to collapse of the articular surface, causing significant patient morbidity.

The diagnosis of osteonecrosis of the humeral head can be difficult because initial radiographic findings are often normal and advanced imaging is needed to verify the condition. Magnetic resonance imaging has been found to be 99% sensitive and specific for diagnosing early-stage osteonecrosis.3 The radiographic progression of the disease was originally classified by Cruess4 into 5 stages; this classification has since been simplified into 4 stages by Colegate-Stone et al.5 In stage 1, radiographic and computed tomography findings are normal and the osteonecrosis is only identified on magnetic resonance imaging. In stage 2, focal areas of sclerosis and osteolysis are identified on radiographs but the sphericity of the humeral head remains intact. Humeral head collapse begins in stage 3 with a maintained glenohumeral joint space. Stage 4 indicates that patients have progressed to secondary osteoarthritis with loss of glenohumeral joint space.5 Treatment options are guided by the stage of disease, and early diagnosis allows for less invasive options, whereas in later stages, only joint restoration may be permitted.

Options for conservative management of osteonecrosis of the humeral head are limited and based on treating the cause of the disease and masking the symptoms rather than addressing underlying bony pathology. Activity modification, physical therapy, and administration of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications and injections may help to decrease the symptoms but are ineffective in reversing the process.6 Multiple less invasive techniques have been described to surgically treat early-stage osteonecrosis of the humeral head, but once humeral head collapse occurs, surgical options may be relegated to joint arthroplasty. We advocate early diagnosis and treatment using the technique of arthroscopic-assisted intraosseous bioplasty to take advantage of not only the minimally invasive nature of the procedure but also the biological adjuncts to assist in revascularization and bony remodeling.

Surgical Technique

Our preference is to perform the procedure with the patient in the lateral decubitus position, although the beach-chair position is also adequate (Video 1, Table 1). We initially turn our attention to the harvest of bone marrow aspirate from the ipsilateral anterior iliac crest. The crest is prepared and draped in the standard fashion. The inner and outer tables of the crest are palpated and marked. A No. 11 blade knife is used to puncture the skin, and a fenestrated aspiration trocar with a sharp tip (Arthrex, Naples, FL) is malleted into the iliac crest to a depth of approximately 2 cm. Two heparinized 30-mL syringes (Arthrex) are used to aspirate 60 mL of bone marrow aspirate (Fig 1). We recommend periodically rotating the trocar 90° every 5 mL as well as slightly withdrawing the trocar while aspirating to harvest efficiently. Redirecting the trocar through the same hole may also be necessary to ensure an adequate harvest. During the concentration process, the shoulder is prepared and draped in the standard fashion and the arm is suspended in 10 lb of traction. A standard posterior portal is used to perform a diagnostic arthroscopy of the shoulder with special attention to the humeral articular surface. An anterior portal is established with a 5-mm cannula (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA), and an arthroscopic probe (Arthrex) is used to palpate the humeral head articular surface to inspect for softening or collapse (Fig 2). Once the glenohumeral joint has been evaluated, attention is turned to the subacromial space. The posterior portal is redirected into the subacromial space, and subsequent anterior and lateral portals are established. A thorough bursectomy is carried out with an arthroscopic shaver (Smith & Nephew), and the bursal side of the rotator cuff is inspected. A GraftNet autologous tissue collector (Arthrex) is placed on the back of a Helicut burr (Smith & Nephew) to harvest morselized bone. The burr is then used to perform a standard acromioplasty and distal clavicular excision, while the bone is collected in the GraftNet (Fig 3). Attention is then turned back to the shoulder. The arthroscope is placed into the posterior portal in the shoulder, and an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) guide (Arthrex) is placed through the anterior portal in the shoulder. The ACL guide is placed on the corresponding area of osteonecrosis of the humeral head, and this is confirmed under fluoroscopy (Fig 5). A percutaneous incision along the lateral aspect of the humerus is made with a No. 11 blade knife, and an ACL drill sleeve (Arthrex) is placed on the humerus; the position is confirmed under fluoroscopy. A FlipCutter III drill (Arthrex) is drilled into the humerus to the subchondral bone of the articular surface under fluoroscopy. Care is taken not to violate the articular surface of the humerus with the drill. We advocate using tactile feel rather than power to advance the drill to the subchondral bone. The drill is flipped to 12 mm and rotated by hand to decompress the area of osteonecrosis (Fig 6). The drill is then flipped back, removed from the drill sleeve, and replaced with a 3.5-mm guide pin (Arthrex). The delivery cannula (Arthrex) is advanced over the guide pin, and the position is verified under fluoroscopy. The mixing and delivery syringe is attached to the delivery cannula, and the mixture is delivered to the decompressed area in the humeral head (Fig 7). The portals are closed with a No. 3-0 nylon suture (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ), and a dry sterile dressing is applied, along with a sling. The patient starts physical therapy at 1 week postoperatively with advancements in flexibility and strengthening as tolerated until returning to most activities at 3 months postoperatively. Return to sport can be expected at 4 to 6 months postoperatively.

Discussion

Treatment recommendations for early-stage osteonecrosis of the humeral head continue to evolve, and consensus on the best technique is controversial. Mont et al.7 were the first authors to describe open core decompression of the humeral head for the treatment of osteonecrosis. Their 1993 study described open core decompression in 30 shoulders. Twenty-two patients went on to have good or excellent results, whereas 8 patients subsequently required an arthroplasty procedure within 5 years of the index procedure. The 14 shoulders that were considered in the pre-collapse stage (stage I or II) prior to surgery had good or excellent outcomes. The authors concluded that open core decompression for humeral head osteonecrosis was efficacious for pre-collapse patients and were the first to advocate this technique.7 LaPorte et al.8 expanded on the aforementioned study to include 63 shoulders and found good or excellent outcomes in 30 of 33 patients with stage I or II disease treated with open core decompression (91%). They noted successful results in 16 of 23 stage III patients (70%) and only 1 of 7 stage IV patients (14%) and encouraged core decompression in early-stage patients with poorer results with progressive disease.8 These results were validated in a study by L’Insalata et al.9 that presented worse outcomes with core decompression in patients with stage III disease. In their study, all 4 patients had radiographic progression of osteonecrosis to stage IV, with many shoulders requiring arthroplasty within 3 years of the initial procedure. L’Insalata et al. concluded that core decompression was not a viable option for the treatment of stage III or higher osteonecrosis of the humeral head.

With advancements in surgical technology, arthroscopic-assisted procedures began to emerge for the treatment of humeral head osteonecrosis. Hayes10 was the first author to use arthroscopy for the treatment of humeral head osteonecrosis. In 1989, he described arthroscopic loose body removal and joint debridement in a patient with humeral head collapse, which provided relief of locking symptoms and improvements in range of motion at 20-month follow-up. Hardy et al.11 published a case report on arthroscopic debridement in a patient with bilateral humeral head osteonecrosis. They reported a decrease in pain and an increase in range of motion at 5-month follow-up. Chapman et al.12 were the first authors to describe a technique for arthroscopic-assisted core decompression of the humeral head using an ACL guide to localize the area of osteonecrosis in a patient as a case report. This technique was further described by Dines et al.13 in a small case series in which 75% of patients were pain free at final follow-up. McClatchy et al.14 described their lateral cortical perforation technique that uses an arthroscopic cannula to allow safe and easy instrument passage to perforate the lateral cortex of the humerus and decompress the area of osteonecrosis.

Multiple types of biological augmentation have been described in the treatment of osteonecrosis of the humeral head, without a clear consensus on their efficacy.15 Steffensmeier et al.16 described the use of calcium phosphate synthetic bone graft to fill the area of core decompression, with good results at 8 months postoperatively in their case series. Makihara et al.17 were the first authors to describe the use of bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) injection for the treatment of osteonecrosis of the humeral head; their study included 5 shoulders. The 4 shoulders with stage III disease had good outcomes, whereas the 1 shoulder with stage IV disease went on to require arthroplasty. This technique was modified by Hernigou et al.18 in their prospective, randomized study of 50 patients with a mean 12-year follow-up. Their study comprised a control group that underwent core decompression alone and a treatment group that underwent open core decompression along with BMAC injection. They found that the control group was significantly more likely to progress to arthroplasty when compared with the treatment group of core decompression with BMAC injection (75% vs 8%). Inoue et al.19 and Kawamura et al.20 described the use of vascularized scapular autograft in their case reports and observed good outcomes. The goals of treatment are to diagnose the disease early and prevent progression given that Burrus et al.21 have shown that the risk of various postoperative complications is higher in patients receiving total shoulder arthroplasty for osteonecrosis when compared with controls.

This article describes our technique for arthroscopic-assisted intraosseous bioplasty of the humeral head for the treatment of osteonecrosis. This technique provides multiple advantages over traditional surgical options (Table 3). The bone marrow aspirate can be harvested with the patient in the lateral decubitus position during preparation for shoulder arthroscopy with minimal disruption to surgical preparation. Our approach is minimally invasive, with all surgical steps being performed through standard-sized arthroscopic portals. Arthroscopy assists in not only the inspection of the articular cartilage but also the collection of autologous bone graft. In contrast to many bone graft harvest techniques, our technique uses an arthroscopic burr and GraftNet to harvest from a local site to eliminate morbidity from a separate donor site. This technique also provides morselized bone graft that is an ideal size for mixing and injection with selected biological augmentation. The ACL guide provides intra-articular guidance under arthroscopy and fluoroscopy to assist in localizing the area of humeral head osteonecrosis to increase the accuracy of decompression and injection. Arthroscopy also confirms that the articular surface is not violated during decompression and injection. The use of an adjustable FlipCutter drill (Arthrex) allows for a minimally invasive approach without multiple passes in the bone and for decompression of a greater area than traditional techniques. The use of radiopaque dye in the mixture provides confirmation of delivery into the area of osteonecrosis with the use of fluoroscopy. We believe that the multiple advantages outweigh the disadvantages of increased complexity and cost of the procedure.
