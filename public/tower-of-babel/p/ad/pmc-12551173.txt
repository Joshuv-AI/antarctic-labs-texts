Acute toxicity evaluation of the ethanol extract from Minquartia Guianensis Aubl (Huacapú) leaves: hematological and histopathological analysis in an animal model
Authors: Julia Espinoza-Rivera, Juana Chávez-Flores, Janet Serván-Meléndez, Michelle Lozada-Urbano, Jaime Rosales-Rimache
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe study of medicinal plants and their potential toxicity arises from the need to address health issues and provide immediate therapeutic solutions. The vast diversity of plants worldwide can lead to confusion regarding their use for food or medicinal purposes, sometimes resulting in intoxications that threaten human health. ObjectiveTo assess the acute toxicity of the ethanol extract of Minquartia guianensis Aubl ("huacapú") leaves in Rattus norvegicus Var. "Holtzman".MethodsM. guianensis leaves were collected, dried, ground, and macerated to obtain the ethanol extract. Phytochemical analysis was conducted using staining and precipitation techniques. Acute toxicity was evaluated according to OECD 423 guidelines, administering a single oral dose of 2000 mg/kg to 20 rats, while 10 control rats received distilled water. Animals were monitored for 14 days, after which they were euthanized on day 15. Blood samples were analyzed for hematological parameters, and organs (brain, lung, liver, stomach, kidney, heart, spleen, ovaries, and testes) were examined for histopathological changes.ResultsNo mortality was observed during the experiment. Minimal hematological alterations were detected, but these were not statistically significant. Histopathological evaluation revealed no lesions in any organs.ConclusionThe ethanol extract of M. guianensis Aubl ("huacapú") leaves, at a dose of 2000 mg/kg, demonstrated no acute toxicity.Keywords: Minquartia guianensis aubl “huacapú”, Acute toxicity, Anatomopathological analysis, Hematological analysis

Introduction

Toxicology research on medicinal plants has gained relevance in recent years due to the widespread use of these plants in traditional and alternative medicine [1, 2]. In Amazonian communities, ancestral knowledge has been preserved, particularly regarding the use of plant leaves as alternative treatments. However, many of these plants contain bioactive metabolites that may cause adverse effects (e.g., lesions in the eyes, gastrointestinal and respiratory mucosa, circulatory system, and skin), especially when used empirically and without scientific validation, posing serious health risks [3, 4].

The use of various plant parts, including leaves, root, or bark, has grown significantly because of their accessibility and low cost. However, this increased use may also lead to unintended exposure to potentially toxic compounds, often due to a lack of scientific knowledge [5]. One such plant, Minquartia guianensis Aubl. is widely distributed across Latin America and is known by several local names: acapu, acari, arariuba, and huacapu in Peru; Manu negro in Costa Rica; and man wood in Panama, among others [6]. This species ranges from Nicaragua to Bolivia, including the Guianas, Brazil, and the Peruvian Amazon. It thrives in seasonally flooded forests, such as those in the village of Jenaro Herrera (4º55′S, 73º44′W) [7], as well as in terra firme. Its growth is favored by clay-rich soils high in organic matter, as well as by alluvial soils in flat or moderately sloped areas [8].

In Peru, the Loreto region is renowned for its rich biodiversity and the traditional medicinal uses of native plants [2]. Within this context, Amazonian communities attribute various therapeutic properties to M. guianensis Aubl, commonly referred to as “huacapú”, using it traditionally to treat conditions such as anaemia, malaria, and rheumatism [9]. However, such use may result in exposure to pharmacologically active compounds that can lead to either beneficial effects or toxicity, depending on the dose and preparation method [10].

Recent studies have evaluated natural extracts used in traditional beverages to enhance sexual performance in the Peruvian tropics. Among these, hydrolysable tannins were identified in M. guianensis extracts, which showed potential inhibitory effects on phosphodiesterase-5, suggesting a possible mechanism of action [11]. Additionally, the methanol extract of M. guianensis branches has shown promising biological activities in Artemia salina models, including antimalarial, antibacterial, antifungal, and antiangiogenic effects [12]. In contrast, species such as Ximenia americana Linn (Olacaceae), locally known as “ababuy”, have undergone acute and sub-chronic toxicity testing at high doses (5000 mg/kg), showing no toxic signs, mortality, or organ damage in Wistar rats [13].

Despite its traditional use, there is limited clinical or preclinical information regarding the safety of M. guianensis ingestion among Peruvian Amazonian populations. The present study evaluated the acute toxicity of the ethanol extract of M. guianensis Aubl. (“huacapú”) leaves using live organ models [14]. The experimental design was based on OECD Guideline 423, which requires both macroscopic evaluation—to identify possible changes in the size, color, shape, or texture of the organs (brain, lung, heart, liver, kidneys, stomach, spleen, ovary, and testicle)—and histopathological analysis to detect cellular-level damage (necrosis, inflammation) or tissue-level alterations (edema) [15].

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the acute toxicity of the ethanol extract of M. guianensis Aubl (“huacapú”) leaves in Rattus norvegicus Var. Holtzman, through hematological and histopathological analyses after a single high-dose administration. The findings provide valuable insights into the plant’s chemical properties, solubility, and potential toxicity effects, contributing to its safety evaluation for traditional and potential therapeutic use.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Rattus norvegicus Var. “Holtzman” rats were sourced from the National Research Center for Biological Production (INS) in Chorrillos, Lima.

The total sample size was 30 rats (15 females and 15 males). A subset of 20 rats, comprising 10 females and 10 males, was selected by simple random probability sampling. The control group consisted of 10 rats (5 females and 5 males).

The number of male and female rats recommended for inclusion in an acute toxicity study is typically balanced to ensure comprehensive data on both sexes. According to established guidelines, a minimum of ten rats, comprising five males and five females, should be tested at each of at least three dose levels. This approach allows for the estimation of the median lethal dose (LD50) and the calculation of confidence intervals, provided mortality is observed at or below the highest test dose [16].

The handling of the rats was conducted by the recommendations outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals [17]. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Norbert Wiener Private University on August 11, 2021 (Exp. No.: 901–2021).

Plant species used

The plant species is native to the Americas, with its natural habitat in Amazonian forests. The species was taxonomically identified by a specialist in botanical taxonomy at the Scientific Institution of the Natural World, affiliated with the Museum of Natural History of the Universidad Nacional Mayor de San Marcos. The leaves of M. guianensis Aubl. were collected by the study’s principal investigator in a state-owned area under the jurisdiction of the Santa Cruz District, Alto Amazonas Province, Loreto Department, Peru, with authorization from the Pacasmayo Community. Beltrán-Santiago, Hamilton, MSc, was responsible for the formal identification of M. guianensis Aubl. The studied specimen was deposited in the herbarium of the National University of San Marcos, Lima, Peru, and is publicly accessible under No. 255-USM-2019.

Preparation of the plant species and method of extraction

Fresh M. guianensis leaves were collected from their natural habitat. The leaves were selected, washed with water, and air-dried at room temperature. They were then further dried at 40 °C for 15 days. The dried material was ground and sieved using a 3 mm mesh. The extraction process was performed using the simple maceration method, wherein the powdered material was immersed in 70% ethanol for 7 days, followed by filtration. The resulting extract was subjected to gradual evaporation in an oven to achieve a pasty consistency and stored in an amber flask. Preliminary qualitative phytochemical analysis was subsequently conducted using precipitation and staining techniques [18, 19].

Conditioning of the experimental animal species

The rats had an average weight of 240 ± 10 g for females and 280 ± 10 g for males. They were housed at the Pharmaceutical Research Centre for a 7-day acclimatization period under controlled conditions, including assisted feeding, regular cleaning, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Following acclimatization, the rats were randomly assigned to cages labeled by group and gender. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the institution’s ethics committee, ensuring the welfare and ethical treatment of the animals throughout the experiment.

The acute toxicity assessment

The study was conducted at the Pharmaceutical Research Centre following the OECD 423 guidelines, using a single dose limit of 2000 mg/kg. The rats were fasted for 24 h with access to water, after which they were weighed to calculate the appropriate dose. The ethanol extract was measured using an analytical balance and dissolved in distilled water. The dose was administered orally to each rat in an upright position using a 1 mL syringe fitted with a No. 18 gold steel gastric tube. The rats were observed for 24 h to monitor any lethal effects of the extract, followed by daily assessments of behavior, food consumption, and weight control. Clinical events were monitored throughout a 14-day observation period, monitoring the acute toxicity evaluation of the huacapu leaf extract using the Irwin test for 14 days.

The control group received orally distilled water per 100 g of body weight 1mL.

Rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal administration of sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg), a method approved by the guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association and the Canadian Council on Animal Care [20]. Death was verified by loss of the righting reflex and subsequent cessation of cardiac and respiratory activity. Confirmation of death, requiring specialized training for accurate assessment of vital signs, was performed by Dr. Juana Chávez.

Blood samples

Blood samples (3 mL) were collected via cardiac puncture and transferred into tubes containing anticoagulants. The tubes were gently homogenized for a few seconds to ensure proper mixing of the sample and subsequently sent for hematological analysis. The analysis included the evaluation of the following parameters: erythrocytes, hemoglobin, hematocrit, leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, basophils, and eosinophils. The hematological parameters in rats were measured as follows: the hematocrit ranged from 35 to 45%, and the red blood cell count varied between 7.2 and 9.6 × 10¹²/L. Hemoglobin concentration was found to be between 12 and 18 g/dL. Reticulocytes represented 1.7–21.1% of total red blood cells. The white blood cell count was approximately 14.0 × 10⁹/L. The coagulation time was recorded at 20 s [21].

Anatomopathology

Target organs, including the brain, heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, stomach, spleen, and ovaries, were eviscerated for anatomopathological analysis. Histological techniques were employed, with tissues fixed in 10% formalin to preserve morphological characteristics. The samples underwent dehydration in alcohol, paraffin embedding, and tissue sectioning using a Minot-type microtome to obtain slices 4–7 μm thick. Paraffin was subsequently removed, and the tissues were stained with hematoxylin-eosin. For mounting, drops of resin were applied. Optical microscopy and microphotography were performed on tissue sections from each organ under investigation.

Data analysis

The statistical analysis was made in SPSS version 25. Simple tables were used to present qualitative variables observed in the laboratory. For quantitative variables (e.g., counts), the mean and standard deviation were calculated and represented using bar graphs with 95% confidence intervals. For inferential hypothesis testing, Student’s t-test was applied to normally distributed data (e.g., counts). At the same time, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was employed for ordinal qualitative data (e.g., toxicity levels ranging from normal to severe). We used a significance level of 5% for all statistical analyses.

Results

We described the toxic effect of the ethanol extract of the leaves of M. guianensis, which belongs to the Olacaceae family, used in traditional medicine in Amazonian communities.

Acute toxicity assessment

Oral administration of the ethanol extract at a single dose of 2000 mg/kg body weight did not result in any mortality within 24 h in the experimental groups. Over the 14-day observation period, no signs of toxicity, physiological dysfunction, or behavioral changes were detected. Additionally, there were no significant differences in body weight, food consumption, or water intake between the treated and control groups. (Table 1).

Macroscopic analysis

Macroscopic evaluation showed no significant changes in the relative organ weights of rats treated with the ethanol extract. The external appearance of organs, including the brain, lungs, liver, stomach, kidneys, spleen, ovaries, and testes, was consistent across groups, with all organs displaying smooth surfaces, soft consistency, and normal morphology. Microscopic analysis

General observations

Histological analysis using hematoxylin-eosin staining revealed no structural abnormalities in the organs of rats treated with 2000 mg/kg of the ethanol extract compared to controls. (Fig. 2)

Microscopic morphology in the control and experimental group treated with the ethanol extract of "huacapu" (microscopic magnification: 100X with oil immersion)

Brain

In female rats, mild interstitial edema was noted, suggesting subtle cellular dysfunction, though no lesions were observed. The pia mater, blood vessels, and neuronal cells appeared histologically normal. In male rats, control animals exhibited dark neurons, neutrophil infiltration, and neuronal autolysis with vacuolated neutrophils. The experimental group showed similar autolytic and degenerative changes without significant lesions, with preserved neuron-glia architecture.

Lungs

Female control rats displayed peri-alveolar congestion and emphysema. The experimental group additionally showed peri-bronchitis and peri-bronchial lymphoid hyperplasia, consistent with chronic inflammation. In males, both groups demonstrated emphysematous changes and dilated alveoli, suggesting mild chronic pulmonary alterations.

Liver

In female rats, liver tissues from the control group exhibited a well-preserved structure, including a centrilobular vein, hepatocyte cords, and hepatic sinusoids. In contrast, the experimental group demonstrated hepatocyte hydropic degeneration, which may be associated with a potential bacterial infection. In male rats, liver tissues from both the control and experimental groups retained normal structural integrity, with no notable histopathological alterations observed.

Stomach

Autolysis of superficial mucosal cells was present in both control and treated female rats with no additional structural abnormalities. A similar pattern was noted in males, indicating no extract-related gastric alterations.

Kidneys

In female rats, both groups exhibited hydrophilic degeneration of the tubular epithelium, suggesting cellular dysfunction. In males, the control group showed glomerular and tubular necrosis, while the treated group presented acute tubular nephrosis, with preserved glomeruli and Bowman's capsules.

Heart

Cardiac tissue from both female and male rats exhibited normal morphology. Features such as intercalated discs, striated cardiac fibers, and centrally located nuclei were maintained in both groups, with no evidence of histopathological alterations.

Spleen

In female rats, spleen tissues from the control group demonstrated well-preserved connective tissue, parenchyma, and lymphatic structures, including the periarterial sheath and Malpighian nodules. In males, the control group showed focal hemosiderosis, whereas the experimental group demonstrated preserved architecture without lesions.

Ovaries

In female rats, ovarian tissues from both groups exhibited regular histological features, including well-preserved follicles and corpus luteum, with no signs of degeneration or lesions.

Testes

In male rats, testicular tissues from the control group displayed normal histological features, characterized by well-organized seminiferous tubules and spermatogonia. The experimental group showed similar features, with preserved seminiferous tubules, ongoing spermatogenesis, and no histopathological alterations.

Discussion

Macroscopic and microscopic analyses indicated that the oral administration of ethanol extract of M. guianensis leaves, at a dose of 2000 mg/kg body weight, did not induce significant morphological or histological alterations in the evaluated organs. The few observed changes were compatible with normal physiological processes or mild chronic conditions typical of the species, suggesting an absence of acute toxicity. These findings reinforce the safety profile of the extract under the tested conditions.

There is no information available regarding the toxicity of M. guianensis. However, some studies have reported its use as a medicinal plant in Colombia, albeit without details on the quantities or doses used for treating diseases. Its application is described as being based on empirical practices passed down through generations, with no documented cases of intoxication [22].

M. guianensis exhibits antibacterial properties due to triterpenes (el β-amyrin), steroids, and phenols (methyl gallate) [23]. Its sustainable use may involve the exploration of its bioactive compounds for pharmaceutical applications. Among the medicinal properties, it has shown an effectiveness of 63% on gram (+) bacteria and 70% on gram (-) bacteria [24].

In the assessment of acute toxicity, administration of M. guianensis ethanol extract at a dose of 2000 mg/kg body weight resulted in no mortality throughout the experiment. We did not assess higher doses, as OECD guideline 420 recommends fixed doses of 5, 50, 300, and 2000 mg/kg. No morphological changes were observed in the organs of male or female rats, and body weight progression was similar in both groups. The dose had no notable impact on toxicological parameters overall.

However, this study revealed significant sex-specific alterations in hematological parameters, which are critical markers in toxicological assessments. In female rats treated with the extract, increases in hemoglobin, hematocrit, and leukocyte counts were observed compared to the control group. Conversely, male rats treated with the extract exhibited lower counts of red blood cells, hemoglobin, and monocytes relative to the control group. In contrast, an increase in leukocyte, lymphocyte, neutrophil, and eosinophil counts was noted in male rats compared to the control group. These findings highlight potential sex-related differences in the hematological response to M. guianensis extract, warranting further investigation.

The mean difference of male rats shows the parameters of leukocytes and neutrophils, with significant values concerning the other parameters. In female rats, the parameters red blood cells, haemoglobin, haematocrit, lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils showed significant values (p < 0.005). Given the limited information on the toxicity of M. guianensis, other authors have reported that similar plants, such as Ambrosia arborescens, do not cause significant changes in the hematological parameters of Rattus norvegicus [25]. Another study found the hematological reference parameter of the Wistar strain laboratory rat, in which the authors reported an average hemoglobin of 12–14 for female Rattus and 12–15 for male Rattus [26]. It is important to note that hematological parameters in male and female rats can vary due to a combination of genetic, physiological, and environmental factors. Below are some key reasons why these parameters may differ between sexes and across different rat strains [27–29].

Acute toxicity in rats varies depending on the strain and the toxic agent(s) being used. Acute toxicity assessment often involves evaluating the median lethal dose (LD50) and any pathological changes observed in various organs [30, 31]. Anatomopathological sections were performed on the brain, lung, liver, stomach, kidney, heart, spleen, ovary, and testis. The brains of male and female rats are unaltered. Substances cross blood-brain barriers into the brain by simple diffusion. Some act on neurotransmitters and alter behavioural functions. Headache, convulsions and unconsciousness manifest acute toxicity [32]; however, neither convulsions nor unconsciousness were shown in the rats during observation.

The lungs of female rats in both the control and experimental groups exhibited peri-alveolar vascular congestion and alveolar emphysema. In addition, the experimental group showed peribronchitis, peri-bronchial lymphoid hyperplasia, and congestion, findings commonly associated with chronic inflammatory conditions. Similarly, male rats in the experimental group presented with alveolar emphysema and dilated alveoli, changes potentially linked to bacterial infections and chronic disease processes. The lungs are particularly susceptible to injury from the acute inhalation of toxic substances, as their extensive surface area and direct exposure to environmental agents make them a primary target. The extent of toxic impact often depends on the solubility of the substance in the blood, which influences its absorption, distribution, and subsequent excretion [33].

In the liver of female rats from the control group, the centrilobular vein, hepatocyte cords, and hepatic sinusoids appeared histologically normal, despite the liver being highly susceptible to damage or inflammation due to its central role in the storage, metabolism, and biotransformation of substances absorbed through the portal circulation.

In the stomachs of female rats from both the control and experimental groups, superficial cells exhibited autolysis and loss of cellular characteristics, likely due to cessation of cellular function. The stomach serves as the primary entry point for toxic substances or xenobiotics, which subsequently pass into the small intestine, where absorption occurs rapidly.

Kidney tissues in female rats showed hydropic degeneration of glomeruli and tubular epithelium in both control and treated groups, indicative of disrupted cellular function. The kidney, responsible for filtering blood and concentrating toxins, are highly susceptible to damage due to potential accumulation of toxic agents up to 100 times higher than plasma concentration [34].

In female rats, heart tissues in both the control and experimental groups exhibited normal histological features, including intact intercalated discs, striated cardiac fibers, and centrally located nuclei. The heart is highly susceptible to toxic effects that influence vascular tension and directly impact cardiomyocytes. Toxic stimuli can trigger a cascade of intracellular processes, leading to cell death, necrosis, and, in severe cases, cardiac shock, particularly with exposure to alkaloids [35].

The spleen in female rats from both groups displayed normal histological characteristics. In male rats, the control group showed a normal splenic capsule, fibrous tissue, Malpighian lymphocytes, and focal hemosiderosis (ferric pigment), indicative of iron accumulation, with an otherwise normal structure. In the experimental group, the spleen exhibited dense, unmodeled connective tissue, a well-preserved periarterial lymphatic sheath, Malpighian nodules, and a septum dividing the parenchyma into white and red pulp, with no evidence of lesions. A previous study found no significant changes in the spleen structure of rats exposed to leaf extracts of Alstonia scholaris [36].

The ovary of the female rats in the control group shows the ovarian follicles in a degenerative stage, and the follicle transformed into a corpus luteum, which is histologically normal. In the experimental group, a developing follicle and a transformed follicle without alterations or lesions are observed in the cortex of the ovary. In the ovary, the passage of toxic substances is through the placental membrane, which determines the factors: molecular weight, lipid/water partition coefficient, degree of ionisation or pKa, and binding to proteins, among other factors [37].

The testis of male rats in the control and experimental groups show histologically ordered tubules. The testicle can produce or increase the frequency of negative effects in the offspring. This finding has an impact on the capacity of the male reproductive tract.

Toxicological studies using rats as animal models should consider that findings may vary depending on the rat strain, such as Wistar, Long-Evans, and Sprague-Dawley, due to genetic differences that can influence hematological parameters. Each strain may exhibit unique characteristics affecting hematology, such as erythrocyte size or hemoglobin concentration. Environmental conditions also play a significant role, as different strains may adapt differently to factors such as nutrient availability or exposure to specific contaminants present in various breeding environments, further influencing hematological outcomes [38].

Other studies that have measured toxicity, analysed a polyherbal formulation (NawaTab), used for the treatment of hyperlipidaemia, found that doses of 2000 and 5000 mg/kg bw showed no effect on mortality, weight, clinical signs, or anatomo-morphological examination of the rats. Similarly, no changes were observed in this study, except in the haematological outcome, between females and males. Agyigra I., et al., evaluated the methanol extract of the stems and bark of Ximenia americana Linn (Olacaceae) in Wistar rats, examining its effects of acute and subchronic toxicity. The results showed minimal lesions in the kidneys, lungs and spleen (p > 0.05) [13]. Another study evaluated the acute toxicity of Ambrosia arborescens Mill, and concluded that in the kidney and liver, a mild congestion was observed with doses of 2000 mg/kg in Rattus norvegicus [39].

This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation of acute toxicity was restricted to a single high dose (2000 mg/kg), which may not adequately reflect potential dose-dependent effects or toxicities at lower or higher doses. Second, the study exclusively assessed acute toxicity, without addressing chronic toxicity or long-term effects, both of which are essential for understanding the safety of prolonged use. Third, although Rattus norvegicus is a standard model for toxicity studies, the findings may not be fully translatable to humans due to interspecies differences. Finally, the study did not explore potential biochemical or molecular markers of toxicity, which could offer deeper insights into subtle or early toxic effects. These limitations should be considered in future studies aimed at evaluating the toxicity of M. guianensis.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that the ethanol extract of M. guianensis Aubl (“huacapú”) leaves did not induce acute toxicity when administered as a single oral dose of 2000 mg/kg body weight. Over the 15-day observation period, no significant alterations in body weight or food intake were observed in treated animals. Hematological analysis revealed statistically significant changes in leukocyte and neutrophil counts in male rats, and red blood cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit, lymphocytes, and monocytes in female rats. These findings suggest that M. guianensis leaves are safe for use at the tested dose. However, further studies on chronic and subchronic toxicity are essential to confirm the long-term safety profile of this plant. Also, to develop studies of experimental toxicity and LD50 toxicity, as well as to evaluate the hepatic profile of the liver.
