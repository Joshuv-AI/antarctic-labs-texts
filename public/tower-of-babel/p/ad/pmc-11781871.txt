Macro–re-entry atrial flutter involving the caval side of a persistent Eustachian valve
Authors: Ahmed T. Mokhtar, Ahmed T. Moustafa, Rami Abazid, Aashish Geola, Sarah Blissett, Allan C. Skanes
Published: 2024

Introduction

Atrial arrhythmias remain a leading cause of mortality and morbidity among adults with congenital heart disease (CHD).1,2 This patient population has unique technical challenges due to the wide spectrum of diseases and heterogeneous issues each patient presents to the electrophysiology (EP) laboratory.3 We present a case of a 41-year-old woman with surgically repaired atrial septal defect (ASD) who was referred for symptomatic atrial flutter (AFL) and underwent a technically challenging cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) ablation. The patient required a second procedure due to recurrence, and a redo EP study revealed a macro–re-entry circuit involving the inferior vena cava (IVC) side of the large, persistent Eustachian valve (EV). We report the first case necessitating ablation on the IVC side of the EV leading to termination of AFL.

Case report

A 41-year-old woman with a remote surgically repaired secundum ASD presented with palpitations and exercise intolerance for approximately 2 years after being lost to follow-up. She had her secundum ASD repaired with a pericardial patch when she was 4 years old after being diagnosed at 1 month postpartum. An electrocardiogram demonstrated what appeared to be counterclockwise typical atrial flutter (AFL) and she was subsequently started on anticoagulation therapy (Figure 1A). A transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated preserved biventricular systolic function, along with a hemodynamically significant ASD patch leak (pulmonary-to-systemic flow ratio = 1.6) and predominant left to right shunting. Her records showed that she had an ASD leak that worsened as the right atrium progressively dilated, as seen in serial echocardiograms since 2015, before being lost to follow-up. Given her simple history of uncomplicated CHD, imaging via transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was not performed. She underwent an attempt at fluoroscopic CTI ablation after 3 months of anticoagulation, but this was technically challenging due to the presence of a large, prominent EV that hindered maneuverability of the radiofrequency ablation (RFA) catheter to reach the IVC side of the CTI, despite the use a deflectable sheath. Furthermore, the ablation catheter frequently went into left atrium, given the presence of the large, residual ASD patch leak. She was cardioverted to help with catheter stability, but RFA lesion quality at the IVC side was thought to be questionable due to poor impedance drops during delivery, along with poor catheter reach. A few weeks later, she developed recurrent palpitations and was found to be in recurrent AFL. Subsequent multimodality imaging with TEE and cardiac computed tomography demonstrated a prominent EV (Figure 1B). It was determined that this prominent EV was not part of the ASD, as the patient never developed hypoxia and had no evidence of right to left shunting.

Given this unusual finding, a decision was made to examine the EV and determine whether it involved the AFL circuit. Hence, she was taken back to the EP laboratory for a second attempt at AFL ablation, with the intent to use electroanatomic mapping and intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) to aid in determining the flutter circuit. Using the CARTO 3 System (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA), a DECANAV (Biosense Webster) catheter was used for anatomic mapping and was later placed in the coronary sinus (CS) as a reference. In addition, a SmartTouch SF catheter (Biosense Webster) was used for ablation. Interestingly, a fractionated, diastolic signal was seen on the caval side of the EV (Figure 1C). During electroanatomic mapping with active AFL (cycle length = 288 milliseconds), an early-meets-late signal was found on the caval side of the EV (Figure 2A and 2B). In addition, an in-circuit response on the caval side of the EV (postpacing interval minus tachycardia cycle length equals 17 milliseconds), confirming the presence of a macro–re-entry circuit involving the EV (Figure 2C). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first published case when ablation on the IVC side of the EV leads to termination of a macro–re-entry AFL. This case highlights several challenges seen in patients with CHD undergoing EP procedures. The most obvious challenge was the presence of a large, persistent EV. This hindered the ability of the ablation catheter to reach the IVC end of the CTI and prevented delivery of adequate ablation lesions. The EV is an embryonic remnant of the right valve of the sinus venosus.4,5 The main function of this valve is to direct oxygenated blood from the IVC through the right atrium toward the foramen ovale. The remnant of the valve forms the Eustachian ridge, which extends from the IVC toward the roof of the CS. There is significant variability in the size of the EV, with a spectrum ranging from a thin, regressed ridge to persistence of the entire right-sided sinus venosus, leading to cor triatrium dexter.5 From an EP perspective, the EV forms a line of conduction block that extends from the IVC to the CS, which allows the re-entrant circuit of the CTI-dependent AFLs to propagate in this channel.6 The EV itself and the CTI have both been implicated as rare nonpulmonary vein triggers of AF.7,8

Ancillary tools should be used to aid in ablation with difficult cases. Preprocedural imaging with computed tomography and TEE allows for adequate preprocedural planning and preparation in anticipation of procedural obstacles, especially in CHD.9 ICE is an effective tool for tackling challenging anatomic variants of the CTI and visualizing the EV. In this case, ICE enabled adequate visualization and engagement of the ablation catheter on either side of the EV, allowing effective RFA delivery (Figure 3C and 3D and Supplemental Video 1).

The EV itself may have some relevant myocardial fibers that participate in the macro–re-entry circuit. In our case, several observations confirmed that the EV formed an essential part of the AFL circuit, including the in-circuit response during entrainment, progressive lengthening of the AFL cycle length, and subsequent termination during ablation. These findings suggest the presence of essential atrial myocytes around either side of the EV. Bencsik and colleagues10 reported a case in which the tip of the EV required ablation to achieve termination and bidirectional block.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this the first reported case of successful AFL ablation from the caval side of the EV. The EV can become a major obstacle in right-sided AFL ablations, especially in patients with CHD and large, persistent EVs. In these patients, operators must take into account the critical role of EV as a substrate in atrial arrhythmias and consider it as an ablation target if EP testing supports this.
