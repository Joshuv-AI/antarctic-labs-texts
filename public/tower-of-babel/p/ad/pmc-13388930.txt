Reevaluating the role of geographical regional factors in subjective well-being
Authors: Yeeun Archer Lee, Phyllis Lun, Louis Tay, Felix Cheung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecognizing that economic indicators are insufficient for assessing societal progress, governments are increasingly turning to subjective well-being to inform policies. However, psychological research on well-being has predominantly focused on person-level factors and interventions, often overlooking broader macro-level contexts, such as where individuals live. This study systematically quantifies the extent to which national and sub-national regional conditions explain variation in subjective well-being, using large international (N = 1,667,495, 154 countries, 2009–2024) and U.S. datasets (N = 3,124,648, 1223 counties, 2008–2017). One-fifth of life satisfaction variance (18.96%) is attributable to national-level conditions, while sub-country regions (e.g., counties or states) account for marginal proportions of variance. By contrast, positive and negative affect show a smaller variance attributable to any geographical level. These findings underscore the role of national conditions in promoting well-being and support the use of life satisfaction, rather than affective measures, as a reliable policy indicator.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51302-7.Subject terms: Geography, Geography, Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Economic indicators such as gross domestic product (GDP), unemployment rate, and inflation, have long been used to assess a nation’s prosperity1,2. However, these measures provide only a partial picture of societal progress3, as they do not fully capture the welfare of the nation or the quality of life of its people. In response, governments and intergovernmental organizations have increasingly turned to subjective well-being to guide policies4. Subjective well-being (also referred to as happiness) can be conceptualized and measured as comprising a cognitive evaluation of one’s life as a whole (i.e., life satisfaction) and affective experiences (i.e., frequent positive affect and infrequent negative affect)5. To what extent, then, is subjective well-being attributable to macro-level conditions at different geographical levels? There are two contrasting views.

Psychology, which pioneered the assessment of subjective well-being6,7, has often emphasized individual factors such as personal agency and personal choices5,8. The Sustainable Happiness Model (commonly referred to as the “Happiness Pie Chart” Model) proposes that happiness is largely attributable to person-level factors, combining genetic factors (50%) and intentional activities (40%), with life circumstances (e.g., sociodemographic and ecological factors) contributing only 10%. This model popularized the view that individuals can enhance their own well-being through intentional activities, contributing to the rise of positive psychology interventions9. Although the model’s authors later scaled back on the assertion that person-level factors play a dominant role in shaping happiness, the framework has been interpreted as prioritizing personal agency as a primary route to happiness while comparatively underemphasizing the influence of life circumstances and other structural factors beyond one’s control10. At the same time, some well-being researchers have documented cross-cultural variation and contextual determinants of subjective well-being, such as wealth, underscoring the role of macro-level conditions in shaping life satisfaction and affect5,11. More recently, emerging evidence within behavioural genetics, a related field of psychology, complicates the strictly individualistic interpretation. Gene-environment interaction models suggest that even person-level differences in subjective well-being, including those influenced by genetic factors, are shaped through dynamic interplay with broader environments, such as education, safety, and economic opportunity12. Nevertheless, the emphasis on person-level change remains dominant in psychological science8 and continues to drive the development of self-help and positive activity interventions, which have shown limited evidence for their sustained effectiveness in enhancing subjective well-being on their own13.

In contrast, disciplines such as public health, public policy, and economics have stressed the importance of macro-level conditions, often beyond individual control, as key drivers of well-being14,15. The socio-ecological model, originally developed in psychology and widely adopted by public health and other social science disciplines, describes how health and well-being are shaped by the interactions across multiple levels, including individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and societal conditions16. In turn, subjective well-being can be conceptualized as an overall, bottom-up assessment of satisfaction across these different life domains, with these domains representing viable pathways for well-being invention17. Some societal conditions include economic development and infrastructure (e.g., education opportunities and industrialization), social and human development (e.g., promoting public health), environmental sustainability (e.g., sustainable water and sanitation), and institutional and political stability (e.g., protecting freedom and human rights)18.

Global-scale research has shown that some objective country-level conditions (e.g., GDP per capita, long life expectancy) as well as the aggregated perceptions (e.g., perceived corruption, freedom to make life choices, social support, and generosity) are positively correlated with national averages in life satisfaction19,20. Conversely, major societal disruptions such as prolonged civil war, regional conflict, and financial instability take a toll on the well-being of civilians21–23. From this perspective, policies that target structural conditions, such as financial support (e.g., cash-transfer programs) and welfare programs (e.g., pension and unemployment compensation)24, may be effective in promoting happiness across the socioeconomic spectrum through cascading effects across ecological levels25,26.

Clarifying the magnitude of macro-level influences will not only help adjudicate between these individual-level and broader contextual explanations of well-being but also inform to what extent promoting subjective well-being would be feasible through macro-level changes (e.g., structural or cultural conditions), versus personal effort. While previous research has documented associations between specific macro-level indicators and well-being, this study goes further by quantifying the variation in well-being at various geographical levels. Macro-level influences operate at multiple geographical scales. National contexts reflect broad institutional arrangements, welfare systems, and historical and cultural contexts, whereas subnational regions capture localized economic conditions, built environments, and social contexts that can vary meaningfully within countries. Comparing these levels allows us to assess the relative importance of countries and sub-national regions as determinants of subjective well-being. Estimating how much variation in subjective well-being exists across different geographical levels provides a crucial foundation for developing population-level interventions.

To systematically quantify how subjective well-being varies at the regional level, we use the intra-class correlation (ICC) as a proxy. ICC estimates the proportion of variance in individual responses that can be explained by clustering variables (e.g., countries or subregions within countries), also known as the random effect, in nested datasets27. The ICC estimation could serve as a reliable and intuitive measure to quantify the proportion of variance that lies in clustering units (e.g., countries), relative to within-unit variance and the noise. Figure 1 provides an illustrative example of how ICC helps decipher between- and within-group variance. The two panels show 200 life satisfaction scores (on a 0–10 scale) sampled from five hypothetical countries (A–E). While both panels feature countries with the same mean life satisfaction score across panels, they differ in the spread (i.e., standard deviations) of scores within each country. Therefore, with country as the clustering variable, we would be able to estimate a higher ICC in panel B than panel A, as a greater variance in life satisfaction scores is explained by between-country differences rather than within-country differences.

Few studies have estimated the proportion of variance in subjective well-being attributable to geographical contexts using the ICC. A 5-year global study tangentially showed substantial variability in life evaluation (24%) between countries, compared to small variance in positive and negative emotions (6% and 4%, respectively)28. This study focused on a single level of regional analysis (i.e., national level), making it difficult to directly compare the relative influence of different geographical levels on subjective well-being. A cross-sectional study in the United Kingdom revealed that less than 1% of the variation in life satisfaction and happiness could be attributable to regions or districts, while the majority of variance (84% and 92%, respectively) was observed at the individual level29. These studies showcased the utility of ICC in empirical research and provided initial insights into subject well-being at different geographical levels. However, together their limited geographic scope and temporal coverage reduce the generalizability of their findings across contexts and time.

Our study sets out to extend the existing studies and systematically examine the contribution of national and sub-national geographical levels to subjective well-being using datasets spanning 16 years. We additionally benchmark our findings against annual household income, a conventional economic indicator. Two large-scale, serial cross-sectional datasets are selected for their extensive time and geographical coverage: the international Gallup World Poll (GWP) (2009–2024) which surveyed on average 1,000 adults in 154 countries on an annual basis (Ntotal=3,124,648)30 and the national Gallup Sharecare Well-Being Index (GSWI) (2008–2017) which sampled over 1,000 United States (U.S.) adults daily across 50 states and Washington D.C. (Ntotal=1,667,495)31. The GWP provides geographical variables for countries and the largest sub-country regions, and the GSWI offers US states and counties as geographical levels. We apply multi-level linear regression models to estimate the random effect of nested geographical levels (GWP: largest sub-country regions nested within countries and GSWI: counties nested within states) on subjective well-being indicators: life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect, and annual household income in each dataset30,31. For each outcome, two models were estimated: (1) a random intercept-only model and (2) a model adjusted for demographic factors (age, gender, education level, marital status). The adjusted models for subjective well-being outcomes also included annual household income. These socio-demographic factors were adjusted for because they are known correlates of subjective well-being32 allowing us to estimate geographical clustering under the assumption that countries have comparable demographic compositions. We then estimated the intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) for the proportion of total variance attributable to each geographical level33.

Results

Globally and across 16 years (2009–2024), 18.96%, 95%CI [15.62%, 22.49%] of the variance in life satisfaction was attributable to the country level, while positive affect and negative affect exhibited lower variations accounting for 6.71% (95%CI [5.45%,8.14%]) and 5.00% (95%CI [4.01%,6.15%]) of the total variance (Fig. 2A). In comparison, nearly half of the total variance (43.32%, 95% CI [37.64%, 48.78%]) in annual household income was explained at the country level. While annual household income showed a larger proportion of variance at the country level, life satisfaction also exhibited considerable country-level variance, with less variability in positive affect and negative affect at the same geographical level. The country-level variance in life satisfaction remained substantial across all years, ranging from 14.95 to 25.23% (see the yellow solid line in Fig. 3A).

The variance in life satisfaction remained substantial even after adjusting for individual-level socio-demographic factors (e.g., age, gender, education level, marital status, and annual income), with about 13.55% (95% CI [11.00%, 16.48%]) of the variance still attributable to the country level. The variance in annual income, positive affect, and negative affect did not drop much after the adjustment, with 40.78% (95%CI [34.68%, 46.38%]) of the variance in annual income, 6.78% (95%CI [5.36%,8.39%]) in positive affect, and 5.09% (95%CI [3.98%, 6.31%]) in negative affect remaining at the country level (Table S1, Fig. S1). Additionally, to examine potential regional variation in the ICC of subjective well-being, we conducted exploratory analyses using models stratified by seven global regions defined by the World Happiness Reports19 (Table S2). Country-level ICCs varied appreciably across regions, indicating that overall estimates reflect an average across this heterogeneity rather than a single common level of between-country variance (Table S3).

Next, we moved down the geographical levels and estimated the subjective well-being variance observed at the largest sub-country regional levels in the GWP. The intra-class correlation coefficients from the random-intercept only model for life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect were considerably smaller at the sub-country regional level (1.60% (95%CI [1.49%,1.82%]), 0.82% (95%CI [0.78%,0.95%]), and 1.18% (95%CI [1.12%,1.36%]), respectively) compared to the country level (Fig. 2A). Similarly, the largest sub-country regions contributed only a small fraction (2.32%, 95%CI [2.06%, 2.68%]) of the total variance in annual household income. The variance at the subregional levels did not change much after adjusting for individual-level factors (Table S1). Taken together, these findings suggest that country-level factors have a much greater impact on individuals’ subjective well-being, particularly for life satisfaction, where the effect is more than ten times larger, compared to sub-country regional factors.

Then we zoomed in further and examined the proportion of variance explained at US states and counties using the GSWI dataset (Fig. 2B; Table S4). For life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect, a negligible portion of the total variance was clustered at the state (range=0.09–0.16%) and county (range=0.24–0.52%) levels in the random-intercept only model. As for annual household income, 0.88% and 2.91% of the variance were explained at the state and county levels, respectively in the random-intercept only model. These estimates were consistent with those estimated with the models adjusted for individual-level factors (Table S4).

For robustness checks, we estimated the proportion of variance in the three subjective well-being indicators and household income by year. The country-level variances in all subjective well-being indicators (life satisfaction range= 14.95–25.23%; positive affect range = 4.54–9.10%; negative affect: 3.83–9.31%) exceed variance at sub-country regional levels (life satisfaction range= 2.40–7.18%; positive affect range = 1.50–4.17%; negative affect: 1.96–4.99%) across the years (Fig. 3, Tables S5–S8). In the United States, life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect show minor variances at the state and county levels. County variances (0.32–3.99%) are consistently at least twice of those observed in states (0.03–1.26%) (Tables S9–S12). These patterns persist even after adjusting for sociodemographic factors.

Discussion

The current study systematically compares the relative proportion of variance in subjective well-being attributable to different geographical levels (in the Gallup World Poll: nations and sub-nations; in the Gallup Sharecare Well-Being Index: states and counties of the United States). We show that a substantial proportion of the variance in life satisfaction is attributable to the national level. On average, approximately one-fifth (19.0%) of life satisfaction variance could be attributed to national-level conditions. This was smaller than the 43.3% variability in annual household income at the same geographical level, but still nearly double the 10% figure purported by the “Sustainable Happiness model”6. Relatively, a smaller amount of positive affect and negative affect variability is attributable to the national geographical level (6.7% and 5.0%).

To contextualize the variance in life satisfaction accounted for at the country level, we compared it with the national variance in annual household income. With aggregated data from 2009 to 2024 across 154 countries, we observed that over two-fifths of the variance in annual household income is by country. This magnitude of between-country variance in annual household income is expected, as income is more directly embedded in national economic systems and policies: wages, employment opportunities, and economic welfare are all shaped by national economic developments and policies34,35. The longstanding centrality of economic growth in national development agendas following World War II36, reinforced by the establishment of international financial institutions such as the International Monetary Fund and World Bank, has further strengthened the link between national-level conditions (e.g., increased trade, production, and investment) that translate into household financial outcomes. By contrast, subjective well-being has more recently become a target of national policy. This helps explain why country-level clustering is comparatively weaker but could grow stronger if subjective well-being continues to gain momentum as a social indicator.

Our findings that 19.0% of the variance in life satisfaction is attributable to the country level likely reflects the combined influence of structural, cultural, and contextual conditions. Although this analysis does not identify specific mechanisms, this pattern supports both the relevance of national-level efforts to improve subjective well-being and the substantial scope for policy action. Although the country-level variance in life satisfaction is roughly half of what we observed for annual household income, this relative magnitude is reasonable given the more recent incorporation of subjective well-being into policy agendas. Since the turn of the millennium, national priorities have expanded beyond GDP growth to include human development, with an emphasis on education, access to health care, equity, and poverty reduction37. This shift has been reinforced by initiatives that promote broader assessment of social progress alongside economic indicators, such as the Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission in the late 2000s and the OECD’s High Level Expert Group on the Measurement of Economic Performance and Social Progress in 20183,38. Indeed, the United Nations’ 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development continues to urge the use of broader well-being metrics to complement objective economic indicators39. Greater national-level policy focus on subjective well-being may further increase country-level clustering.

Our findings on life satisfaction challenge the prevailing notion in psychology that subjective well-being is largely a matter of personal control9—a view that may risk over-individualizing well-being issues. Our estimates indicate that the country where an individual resides could account for nearly one-fifth of the variability in life satisfaction, well exceeding the 10% attributed to a broader range of life circumstances (including demographic and regional factors) suggested by the “Sustainable Happiness model”9. It is noteworthy that our findings do not imply that the remaining 81.0% of life satisfaction variability is attributable solely to individual differences; it also includes residual variance due to measurement error and unobserved variables. For the same reason, our estimated variability in life satisfaction at the national level could be interpreted as a lower-bound estimate. Still, given the established reliability of the single-item life satisfaction measure40, we can infer that a considerable portion of this variance is associated with individual-level factors such as personal agency and genetic predispositions. The cross-temporal consistency in our ICC estimates speaks to the robustness of our findings. The sizable and stable proportion of variance in life satisfaction attributable to country-level conditions each year (15.0–25.2%) further affirms the consistent influence of national context and suggests that federal governments and public policies can play a substantial role in shaping population-wide well-being.

Adjusting for key sociodemographic factors (age, gender, education level, marital status, and annual household income) attenuated the proportion of variance in life satisfaction observed at the country level from 18.9 to 13.6%, yet a substantial share remained unexplained. This novel finding suggests that while individual demographic and socioeconomic characteristics account for some of the cross-national differences in well-being (about 5.4%), a larger portion of the variation may reflect broader country-level conditions. Indeed, studies have shown that population-level changes in subjective well-being coincide with nationwide situations and policies. Countries with a stronger social welfare system tend to report higher average well-being and smaller disparities across social groups41,42. Societies characterized by higher levels of social trust and support13 and political progress, such as perceived freedom of choice and low corruption, show higher levels of life satisfaction and happiness19,43. Conversely, in the most volatile national circumstances, people living through protracted conflicts saw a substantial drop in life satisfaction, notwithstanding their individual-level protective factors such as income brackets and education level21,22. Taken together, these findings underscore the influence of broader national context and structural conditions that operate beyond individual socioeconomic status.

The shared variance in individual life satisfaction at the country level may reflect multiple factors operating within national contexts. These include macro-level structural conditions, such as economic development and infrastructure, social and public health policies, political stability, and major country-level events (e.g., war and conflict) that have been shown to covary with national average life satisfaction19–22. At the same time, substantial between-country variance may also arise from other sources. Cultural influences, including norms and display rules surrounding the experience and evaluation of subjective well-being, may shape how individuals respond to items such as the life satisfaction item44. Counties may also differ in population-level psychological characteristics related to subjective well-being, such as personality traits, which show meaningful geographical clustering45. Therefore, country-level variance in subjective well-being may reflect a combination of structural and cultural influences that the current study does not disentangle.

The stark difference in national-level variance between life satisfaction and affect aligns with the well-being literature distinguishing these aspects of subjective well-being. Life satisfaction, reflecting one’s evaluation of their own life, tends to be more closely related to objective life circumstances (e.g., income and education), whereas emotional experiences, such as positive affect and negative affect, tend to be more responsive to day-to-day events, such as engagement in activities and social events46,47. This distinction is further supported by global analyses showing that country-level conditions, including GDP per capita, freedom to make life choices, corruption, and social support, explained a substantially larger share of national differences in life satisfaction (up to 76%), compared to positive affect and negative affect (range = 35–44%)19. These findings suggest that structural conditions and public policies, especially at the national level, may have greater leverage in shaping evaluative well-being than affective states do. Accordingly, life satisfaction may serve as a more policy-relevant indicator of social progress and welfare conditions than emotional experiences.

In contrast, only marginal proportions of subjective well-being and household income were attributed to sub-national regions, such as counties and states in the U.S. or the largest sub-country regions globally. Two plausible interpretations emerge. First, national contexts, including federal institutions, macroeconomic structures, or cultural norms, may exert a stronger influence on broader contextual conditions across regions within countries than regional and or local authorities do. Alternatively, coordinated efforts across levels of government may reduce regional disparities, resulting in relatively small between-region variance. It is also possible that subnational policies respond more flexibly to local conditions than national policies48, and may therefore operate on shorter timescales that are not easily captured in our annual data. Importantly, smaller regional ICCs do not imply that regional conditions are unimportant or that subnational policies lack impact. Rather, they indicate that, on average, between-country differences exceed between-region differences within countries. Aggregate national-level well-being measures can mask meaningful regional disparities; therefore, monitoring subnational indicators remains important for ensuring equity in social progress. Promoting population well-being involves concerted effort and monitoring across multiple geographical levels. Canada offers a working example of such an effort: its federal initiatives guide national budgeting and policy across key domains, while some provinces (e.g., Nova Scotia and Ontario) complement these efforts by engaging communities in well-being surveys and setting region-specific priorities49,50. Coordinated actions across levels of government and agencies can help promote population well-being while accommodating regional differences. Nonetheless, the relative influence of national versus sub-national contexts may vary depending on governance structure (e.g., more federalized versus more centralized systems) and broader sociocultural contexts. Future research examining governance systems would help clarify when and where sub-national variance is expected to be larger or smaller.

Some limitations in our paper bear mentioning. This study aggregated well-being outcomes (i.e., life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect) across countries and years. Although we used well-established measures, such as the life satisfaction item, widely used to assess population-level well-being and societal progress19,51, these measures remain susceptible to cultural differences in interpretation and bias, as cultural contexts may shape how individuals conceptualize and evaluate their own well-being44. The Gallup World Poll implements several safeguards to mitigate cultural biases. Survey items were professionally translated into each country’s dominant conversational languages and reviewed by independent parties30, and trained local interviewers administered the survey verbatim in each country. In addition, positive and negative affect measures were assessed using binary (“Yes” or “No”) items to reduce cultural response biases and enhance cross-national comparability, which is particularly crucial in a global survey spanning over 150 countries52. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that cultural factors may still influence responses to subjective well-being measures, potentially contributing to some of the observed country-level variance. Also, as we observed ceiling effects for positive affect and floor effects for negative affect, aggregating these binary affect items as our outcomes may introduce distributional constraints that could bias variance estimates53. In addition, the present study is correlational and therefore does not allow for direct causal inferences regarding the effects of geographical context or government influence on well-being. However, a growing body of evidence from natural and quasi-experimental policy changes, such as unconditional cash transfers54, reduced working hours55, and housing mobility programs56, provides support for the role of structural policies in influencing population well-being. Finally, while our study quantifies the overall magnitude of regional influences on subjective well-being at different geographical levels, it does not identify which specific macro-level factors drive these differences. Although the present dataset has exceptionally broad country coverage, representing a large proportion of countries worldwide, it does not include sufficiently comprehensive or harmonized measures of country-level conditions across all included countries that allow a systematic decomposition of the observed country-level variance. Future research is needed to isolate structural determinants, such as geo-political stability, social welfare services, or cultural norms, that play influential roles in shaping subjective well-being at the national and regional levels.

Despite traditionally receiving less attention than economic metrics such as gross domestic product, subjective well-being is increasingly recognized as a comprehensive policy indicator. A few, albeit growing, governments and inter-governmental agencies, such as the United Nations Sustainable Development Solutions Network and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, have begun integrating subjective well-being into their agenda to promote societal progress and human flourishing57,58. Psychological science, one of the pioneering disciplines emphasizing the importance of subjective well-being, has built on decades of theoretical and experimental research59 and is well-positioned to contribute further by considering structural conditions alongside prevailing individual-level determinants. Our findings provide a compelling basis for this broader focus. Geographical context, particularly national conditions, accounts for a substantial share of the variance in life satisfaction, even after considering individual-level factors. This highlights the substantial potential to improve subjective well-being by identifying and addressing macro-level conditions, especially given the limited effectiveness of individual-level interventions alone13. At the same time, although not directly tested in this study, a considerable portion of variance in subjective well-being likely reflects individual differences (e.g., genetics and sociodemographic factors), as well as their interactions with geographic contexts 12,16. The pursuit of population-wide well-being is ultimately a matter of both individual and social responsibility, which will benefit from greater integration across disciplines to address macro-level conditions alongside individual-level factors.

To achieve these, we need cross-disciplinary collaborations that use established research methods such as causal inference, natural experiments, and policy testing from epidemiology, public policy, economics, and psychology60. Promoting life satisfaction at scale requires addressing multiple facets of society, including social capital (e.g., community interactions and social trust), geopolitical stability, economic growth and employment, environmental quality (e.g., green spaces, transportation, and pollution), and healthcare systems (e.g., health expenditure and caregiving burden10 in addition to individual-level interventions (e.g., positive activities). In sum, promoting population-wide well-being calls for a multidisciplinary approach and a collective societal effort.

Methods

Data and sampling

The Gallup World Poll (GWP) used face-to-face or telephone interviews to survey at least 1000 non-institutionalized individuals aged ≥ 15 years annually in 2009–2024 over 160 countries, covering over 98% of the world’s adult population (Tables S13–S14)30. The data collected were aggregated at the national and sub-country regional levels. Cross-national comparisons of self-reported well-being may be influenced by cultural differences in response styles and scale use (measurement invariance) and by context-specific reference groups that shape how individuals evaluate their lives. The Gallup World Poll mitigated this concern by employing standardized questionnaires (e.g., anchored, behaviorally simple items for life satisfaction and binary questions for specific affect questions), rigorous translation and back-translation procedures, and probability-based national samples with post-stratification weights to improve cross-national comparability. The Gallup Sharecare Well-Being Index (GSWI) surveyed at least 1,000 American adults aged ≥ 18 years daily using a random-digit-dialing sampling frame in 2008–2017 (Tables S15–S16)31. The weighted GSWI samples were representative of over 95% of the US adult population.

To ensure stable estimation of variance components, we included only subregions with an average of 50 or more observations across all surveyed years in the GWP (N = 1110/2888 or 38.4% of the total number of sampled subregions), and counties with an average of 50 or more observations across all surveyed years in the GSWI (N = 1223/5401 or 22.6% of the total number of sampled counties). This threshold meets the commonly recommended minimum cluster sizes for reliable estimation in multilevel models61. The total number of sub-country regions in each country-year in GWP and the total number of counties in each US state and Washington D.C. per year in GSWI are reported in Tables S14 and S16, respectively. In total, the GWP analytic dataset consisted of 1,667,495 participants from 1110 sub-country regions in 154 countries (Tables S13–S14). The GSWI analytic dataset included 3,124,648 American participants from 1223 counties in 50 US states and the District of Columbia (Tables S15–S16). We incorporated weights provided by the GWP and GSWI to account for sampling and non-response bias and match demographic targets from national censuses31,62. Notable sampling challenges (e.g., population inaccessibility due to conflict) were documented63. As the current study involved secondary data analyses of de-identified data and did not require informed consent from participants and ethical approval at The University of Toronto, where the analyses were conducted.

Variables

Main outcomes

The current study focused on three subjective well-being indicators (life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect) and an economic indicator comparator (annual household income).

In GWP, not all countries were asked about life satisfaction, affect, and annual household income in all years. Countries included each year for each set of analysis are listed in Tables S17–S20. In GSWI, the affect analyses included data from 2008–2013 and 2016 when all affect items were assessed.

Life satisfaction

Life satisfaction was assessed with the well-validated Cantril Self-Anchoring Striving Scale64, where respondents would rate their life on a rung of a ladder: “Please imagine a ladder with steps numbered from 0 at the bottom to 10 at the top. Suppose we say that the top of the ladder represents the best possible life for you, and the bottom of the ladder represents the worst possible life for you. On which step of the ladder would you say you personally feel you stand at this time, assuming that the higher the step the better you feel about your life, and the lower the step the worse you feel about it? Which step comes closest to the way you feel?” Responses of “don’t know” or refusal to answer were classified as missing in the analyses, consistent with conventional practice in the analyses of the data. Less than 2% the GWP and GWSI data were missing across all years when the life satisfaction item was administered.

Positive affect

Positive affect was measured by averaging two item scores that asked whether respondents smiled or experienced enjoyment most of the previous day (“Yes” or “No”). An affirmative response would be considered as a score of 1. Only years when both items were administered were considered in the analyses. Responses of “don’t know” or refusal to answer were classified as missing in the analyses, which totaled 2.58% in GWP and 0.55% in GWSI.

Negative affect

Negative affect was assessed by averaging four binary item scores that asked whether respondents felt worried, sad, angry, and stressed most of the previous day (“Yes” or “No”). An affirmative response would be considered as a score of 1. Similarly to positive affect, only years when all four items of negative affect were administered were considered in the analyses. Responses of “don’t know” or refusal to answer were classified as missing in the analyses, which totaled 3.13% in the GWP and 0.20% in the GWSI.

Annual household income

Annual household income is a long-established metric for evaluating economic well-being across international settings2. GWP applied the World Bank’s purchasing power parity private consumption conversion factor to calculate respondents’ annual household income in international dollars (currently US dollars) to adjust for inflation and ensure cross-national comparability. In GSWI, total annual household income was asked in 12 categories: Under $720, $720 to $5,999, $6,000 to $11,999, $12,000 to $23,999, $24,000 to $35,999, $36,000 to $47,999, $48,000 to $59,999, $60,000 to $89,999, $90,000 to $119,999, $120,000 and over. To convert the responses back to a continuous scale, we chose the median value of each category for respondents in that category. We then applied inflation adjustment to the income variable in GSWI using the World Bank consumer price index65. Log2 transformation of the annual household income variables to facilitate interpretation. Responses of “don’t know” or refusal to answer were treated as missing (nonresponse) in the analyses. Income nonresponse was low in the GWP (less than 4%) but higher in GSWI (21.6%). Nonresponse to income is common in population surveys and may be more prevalent among respondents at both ends of the income distribution66,67. The GSWI weighting procedure included selection probability adjustments, nonresponse adjustments across sampling strata, and post-stratification to align the responding sample with national benchmarks (e.g., region, age, gender, education, race/ethnicity, and population density)31. Although weighting cannot fully eliminate bias associated with income nonresponse, these procedures may mitigate systemic biases in population-level estimates due to missing data.

Sociodemographic variables

Gender

In the GWP, gender was not directly asked but coded based on the interviewer’s assessment of the respondent. The options were male or female. In GSWI, the respondents were asked if they were male or female. In cases where a respondent refused to answer, the interviewer would estimate the gender of the respondent as male or female based on their assessment.

Age

Age was coded in years, with participants aged 99 or older recorded as 99. Responses of “don’t know” or refusal to answer were classified as missing in the analyses.

Education level

The GWP asked participants about their highest completed education level: “completed elementary education or less”, “secondary education and some education beyond secondary education”, “completed four years of education beyond high school” and “received a 4-year college degree”. The GSWI used the following categories for education level: “less than high school diploma”, “high school degree or diploma”, “technical/ vocational school”, “some college”, “college graduate”, and “post-graduate work or degree”. Responses of “don’t know” or refusal to answer were classified as missing in the analyses.

Marital status

In both GWP and GWIS, marital status was coded in the following categories: single/never been married, married, separated, divorced, and widowed. The GSWI included an additional category for domestic partnership/ living with partner (not legally married). Responses of “don’t know” or refusal to answer were classified as missing in the analyses.

Secondary variables

Largest sub-country regions

In the GWP dataset, the largest regions within country geographies are defined by each country’s government or statistics office (e.g., states in the United States and provinces and territories in Canada). The number of sub-country regions per country is listed in Table S14.

United States counties

In the GSWI dataset, counties in each US state were identified by self-reported postal codes and the Federal Information Processing Standards codes. The number of counties per state is listed in Table S16.

Statistical analyses

Due to the descriptive and exploratory nature of our study, we did not pre-register any hypotheses or analyses. All our analytic R code is made available on the project OSF site (https://osf.io/yq8mh/?view_only=979d06deed4e419cb22d8fd3636d6909).

We used multi-level linear regression models to estimate the effect of different geographical levels on subjective well-being indicators (i.e., life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect) and annual household income in the Gallup World Poll and US-based Gallup Sharecare Well-Being Index, respectively30,31. Two sets of multi-level linear regression models were estimated for each outcome: (1) a random-intercept-only model and (2) a model adjusted for age, gender, education level, and marital status. The models for life satisfaction, positive affect, and negative affect additionally adjusted for annual household income. To account for the hierarchical data structure, the random intercepts in the international dataset (GWP) captured the largest sub-country regional level nested within each country, and the random intercepts in the US national dataset (GSWI) estimate the county level nested within each state. Weight was incorporated in each model to account for selection probability, non-response, and post-stratification in the samples. Complete-case analyses were conducted, with entries containing any missing data removed.

Subsequently, we calculated intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs) to estimate the proportion of total variance attributable to each geographical level 24.
