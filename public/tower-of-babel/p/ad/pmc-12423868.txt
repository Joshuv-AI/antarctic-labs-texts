Lipid Extraction from Wet Polymer-Flocculated Chlorella sorokiniana Using Liquefied Dimethyl Ether without Centrifugation and Drying Pretreatment
Authors: Tao Wang, Li Zhu, Kaito Kusumi, Tetsuya Yamamoto, Akiho Yonezawa, Ryosuke Homma, Kenji Shiota, Masaki Takaoka, Kazuyuki Oshita, Hideki Kanda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The energy intensive nature of harvesting microalgae by centrifugation and drying results in a low energy profit ratio for biofuel production from wet microalgae. To address this, microalgal cultures can be flocculated by polymers to reduce energy consumption and avoid centrifugation. Additionally, lipids can be directly extracted from wet microalgae harvested by centrifugation using liquefied dimethyl ether (DME) to reduce energy consumption. In this study, we investigated the lipid extraction from the green alga, Chlorella sorokiniana (C. sorokiniana), flocculated with amphoteric and cationic polymers using liquefied DME, without centrifugation and drying pretreatment. Although the addition of amphoteric polymer did not interfere with lipid extraction by liquefied DME, the amount of lipids extracted by ethanol from flocculated C. sorokiniana decreased. The addition of cationic polymers also interferes with lipid extraction using liquefied DME, chloroform–methanol mixtures, and ethanol. Surface functional group measurements revealed that the amphoteric and cationic polymer flocculants were insoluble in these solvents, remained in the residue, and did not contaminate the extracted lipids. Thus, the amphoteric flocculant KA305BH was found to be effective for lipid extraction with liquefied DME from C. sorokiniana. The proposed approach can facilitate the energy efficient production of biofuel from wet microalgae.

Introduction

Although solar and wind power are becoming increasingly widespread, biofuels are also important for ships and aircraft, where buoyancy and lift are critical, and heavy storage batteries are disadvantageous. In addition, photosynthesis of organic matter , and methane conversion are important for carbon negatives that reduce atmospheric CO2. The use of microalgae with high lipid synthesis capacity is attracting significant attention. Technological prospects have already been achieved in the cultivation of microalgae, including the recovery of nitrogen and phosphorus using secondary sewage treatment water as fertilizer and outdoor mass cultivation. Microalgae are typically unicellular organisms ranging in diameter from a few to several tens of micrometers. Due to their low density relative to water, microalgal cells sediment extremely slowly by gravity alone, often taking several hours to days to separate even partially. Consequently, gravity-based separation methods are generally impractical for efficient biomass recovery. Centrifugation offers a powerful alternative, applying high centrifugal acceleration to significantly enhance the settling velocity of suspended microalgal cells. Based on the principles of Stokes’ law, this technique can rapidly and effectively separate microalgal biomass in a matter of minutes, making it one of the most widely adopted methods in laboratories and industries. The separation process usually takes place under laminar flow conditions to ensure consistent and reliable performance. However, because microalgae are aquatic, those recovered via centrifugal separation are in a muddy state with an extremely high-water content and must be dried before extraction. However, harvesting microalgae from the culture by centrifugation and drying after harvesting are energy intensive, and making biofuel from microalgae has the energy profit ratio less than 1. Even if green electricity is used, the price of the consumed green electricity would be transferred to the price of the microalgae-derived chemical feedstock, which would inevitably increase the cost and be meaningless.

In the case of hexane and chloroform, two conventional organic solvents, lipids cannot be extracted unless the microalgae are dried. Methanol is toxic and causes an azeotrope problem with water when recovered from the extract, unless the algae are dried during pretreatment. In addition, if any of these substances remain in the extraction residue, the residue with a high protein content , cannot be used as fertilizer or livestock feed. To address these problems, liquefied dimethyl ether (DME) has been proposed as an alternative solvent that can extract lipids and carotenoids without drying the microalgae or residues. DME is a fuel that can be efficiently synthesized from biomass without the greenhouse effect. DME can also be synthesized from carbon dioxide via green power in a reductive manner using catalysts. Notably, DME, the simplest ether, is very different from common ethers, such as ethyl ether. Bioassays have confirmed that aqueous solutions of DME at ambient pressure are nontoxic to microorganisms. The European Union has approved DME as an extraction medium, and the US Food and Drug Administration has recognized DME as “generally recognized as safe (GRAS).” Owing to its molecular structure, its normal boiling point is as low as −24.8 °C; therefore, it cannot be used as a liquid solvent unless it is pressurized above its saturated vapor pressure of, for example, 0.59 MPa at 25 °C. As the dipole moment of DME is 1.3 D, it forms weak electrostatic interactions similar to hydrogen bonds and is partially miscible with water in its liquefied state. − Therefore, when liquefied DME is used as an extraction solvent, organic matter can be extracted directly from objects with high water content. In addition, the autoxidation of DME is comparable to that of liquefied petroleum gas, indicating that DME can be handled safely using standard liquefied petroleum gas handling techniques. Therefore, lipids and functional components can be extracted from muddy microalgae with high water content using liquefied DME. ,

Although lipids can be extracted by avoiding drying by using liquefied DME, there remains the other problem that the concentration of microalgae in the culture medium is only about 0.1 wt %; thus, the energy required for centrifugation is enormous. Therefore, to avoid centrifugation, a flocculant can be added to the microalgae prior to centrifugation. Several researchers have used various substances such as flocculants to collect microalgae. However, some substances used for flocculation are toxic and can contaminate the algal biomass. Among flocculants, inorganic chemical flocculants are polyvalent cations, such as aluminum sulfate, ferric chloride, and ferric sulfates, which form polyhydroxy complexes at optimal pH and are used to flocculate microalgae cells, resulting in the neutralization and reduction of the negative surface charge of microalgae cells. , However, the concentration of inorganic flocculants in microalgae at too high a concentration is obviously undesirable for the use of the residue as food or livestock feed. For example, in 2011, the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives set a provisional tolerable weekly intake (PTWI) of aluminum at 2 mg/kg body weight. PTWI is applicable to all aluminum compounds in food, including food additives. Because the amount of inorganic flocculants on microalgae is not negligible, they are highly concentrated in microalgal residues and pose a risk for use as livestock feed or food. Ferric chloride is also an effective flocculant but is toxic and causes abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhea, shock, and collapse when ingested orally. It also irritates the eyes, skin, and respiratory tract.

Polyelectrolytes can be cationic, anionic, or nonionic. Because the surface of microalgae is negatively charged, cationic polyelectrolytes bind and aggregate cells, whereas anionic and nonionic polyelectrolytes cannot form microalgal flocs because of weak electrical interactions. The cohesion of polyelectrolytes depends on properties, such as the charge and functional groups on the microalgae surface, pH of the culture medium, and density of algal cultures. Cationic polyelectrolytes are more effective than metal salts, achieving up to a 35-fold biomass enrichment. Cationic polyelectrolytes with high charge densities are effective flocculants for microalgae harvesting because their effective amount decreases as the molecular weight of the flocculant increases. , Among the polymer-based flocculants used in previous studies, polyacrylamide, chitosan, , poly γ-glutamic acid, and acrylamide-methacrylic acid/ester-acrylic acid copolymer KA305BH have been found to be effective in flocculating Chlorella species. These polymers are harmless when used as livestock feed or can be applied to the soil as fertilizers. Chitosan and poly γ-glutamic acid are also edible. Therefore, these polymers are considered safer than inorganic flocculants when mixed with the residues after lipid extraction. In addition to these polymers, KA305BH, which is used in sewage treatment, was originally designed to be environmentally friendly and does not cause problems when mixed with livestock feed or sown in soil. Among these, KA305BH was found to be particularly effective in a previous study.

However, it is well-known that each organic solvent dissolves a different polymer type. The addition of polymers used as flocculants for microalgae, if dissolved in liquefied DME and mixed with lipids, can have a detrimental effect on the extraction of highly viscous lipids. Conversely, if the polymer is insoluble in liquefied DME, the polymer coating the microalgal surface may prevent the extraction of lipids by the liquefied DME. As these findings are important for future plant scale-ups, in this study, lipids were extracted with liquefied DME from flocculants obtained by adding polymers to microalgae, and the extraction behavior and properties of the extracted lipids were investigated. Owing to the vast combinations of microalgae and polymers, we employed Chlorella sorokiniana (C. sorokiniana, formerly classified as Chlorella vulgaris), which is often used as a model species for microalgae, combined with KA305BH and KP1205BH, which are effective flocculants for C. sorokiniana. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine an issue that has gone completely unnoticed in both the fields of fuels and microalgae culture.

Materials and Methods

Materials

C. sorokiniana slurry used in this study was purchased from Chlorella Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). The C. sorokiniana had a high-water content in BG–11 broth (C3061, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, U.S.A.). The average cell size and ζ-potential were measured using Zetasizer Pro ZSU320 (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Worcestershire, UK), each measuring 4.3 ± 0.8 μm and −33.5 ± 2.0 mV. The flocculants KA305BH and KP1202BH, which are used in Japan for sewage treatment and can be applied to soil, were supplied by Mitsubishi Chemical Corporation (Tokyo, Japan). These included polymers of acrylic acid, acrylamide, dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate methyl chloride quaternary salt, and dimethylaminoethyl methacrylate methyl chloride quaternary salt in different proportions.

However, the monomer ratios were not determined by the manufacturers. According to the manufacturer, the molecular weights of KA305BH and KP1202BH are 7,000,000 and 5,000,000, respectively, with cationicities of 1.05 and 3.27, respectively. In other words, KA305BH is an amphoteric flocculant and KP1202BH is a cationic flocculant. Liquefied

DME was purchased from Tamiya, Inc. (Air Can 420D, Shizuoka, Japan) and used without further purification. For common organics solvent extractions, high-performance liquid chromatography-grade chloroform, methanol, and ethanol were purchased from FujiFilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan.

Liquefied DME Extraction

The DME extraction apparatus used in this study and the operating procedure were based on previous reports. , As shown in Figure , the apparatus consisted of a metal tank (TVS-1, Taiatsu Techno, Saitama, Japan) containing liquefied DME as the solvent, an extraction column (HPG-10-5, capacity 10 mL, inner diameter 11.6 mm × length 190 mm, Taiatsu Techno) for packing microalgae, a collection vessel for lipids and water (HPG-96-3, capacity 96 mL, Taiatsu Techno) connected in series, and valves installed in the middle and at the end of the DME flow.

Schematic of liquefied DME extraction.

The extraction column and collection vessel were pressure-resistant glass covered with a clear polycarbonate cylinder for safety and printed on a volumetric scale. The preparation of the microalgae packed in the column depended on the presence or absence of flocculation, as described in the following subsections. A cellulose filter with a pore size of 0.20 μm (Advantech Toyo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was placed at the outlet of the extraction column to prevent sample from flowing out of the extraction column. The tubing connecting each vessel was made of SUS316 with an inner diameter of 1/16 in.

The inside of the metal tank filled with liquefied DME was heated to 35 ± 1 °C in a water bath to increase the saturated vapor pressure of liquefied DME inside to 0.79 ± 0.02 MPa. However, the pressure of the vessel in which the extracted liquid was collected was low; therefore, liquefied DME was supplied using differential pressure as the driving force. When the supplied liquefied DME passed through the fine tube made of SUS316, noncontact infrared thermometer confirmed that the temperature dropped rapidly to 23.6 ± 1.4 °C due to the large specific surface area of the fine tube. The flow rate of liquefied DME was adjusted based on the volumetric scale of the container from which the extract was collected and the rate of increase of liquefied DME was determined. The rate of increase of liquefied DME was adjusted to 10 ± 1 mL min–1 (= 6.63 g min–1) by manually adjusting a valve on a 1/16 SUS316 tubing in the middle, based on the volume scale of the container from which the liquefied DME extract was collected. Each time the volume of the extracted solution in the collection vessel increased, the vessel was promptly replaced with a new empty collection vessel. After the recovery vessel filled with the extraction solution was disconnected from the apparatus, the end valve was opened, and the pressure was reduced to atmospheric pressure to evaporate the DME. This resulted in an incremental volume of extractant corresponding to each incremental volume of liquefied DME. After extraction, the residue was removed from the extraction column, and after the evaporation of DME, the recovery vessel contained residual water and lipids. The weight of the extracted water was determined as the difference in weight after hot-air drying. Lipids were collected for analysis using a spatula. To confirm the reproducibility, the experiment from extraction to analysis was repeated thrice, and the standard deviations were noted as errors.

Sample Preparation with Flocculants

Two hundred milliliters of BG-11 culture medium was adjusted to 0.1% (w/v) C. sorokiniana concentration in the culture medium, which is a typical culture condition for C. sorokiniana, and 5 mL of 0.2 wt % flocculant (KA305BH or KP1202BH) solutions were mixed to achieve 5% (w/v) flocculant based on C. sorokiniana dry weight. In a previous study, we investigated the flocculation, concentration and dewatering of C. sorokiniana using polymeric flocculants. The highest recovery rate (over 98%) was achieved with a dosage of 5 wt % flocculant. High-molecular-weight, highly cationic flocculants were particularly effective. The culture was stirred at 350 rpm for 5 min, then at 80 rpm for 10 min using a jar tester (Miyamoto MJS-4H, Daido Industries Inc., Osaka, Japan), and allowed to stand for 30 min to allow the flocculant to settle naturally. According to preliminary studies, these conditions resulted in collection efficiencies of 99.3% for KA305BH and 98.4% for KP1202BH, as determined by UV–vis absorbance measurements at 680 nm (Shimadzu UV-1280, Kyoto, Japan). Subsequently, the culture medium was removed from the container and the remaining flocculence was wrapped in polyester filter paper (NBC10/TN-3.3, pore size 10 μm, thread diameter 40 μm, aperture ratio 3.3%, NBC Meshtec Inc. Hino, Japan) and centrifuged at 2100 G (=4330 rpm) for 3 min (H-36α, Kokusan, Saitama, Japan) in a metal mesh basket. The water content of flocculences were 73.3 ± 2.4 wt % (KA305BH) and 62.4 ± 2.5 wt % (KP1202BH), which were determined using a moisture meter (Frontlab, AS ONE Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The water content was determined from the difference between the initial weight and the weight of the dried product when it was heated to 107 °C and remained constant.

In a preliminary study, when 5.00 g of flocculates were filled in an extraction column (7 mL, equivalent to 6.8 cm in thickness), the column was blocked, liquefied DME did not flow, and lipid extraction was not possible because of the sample’s high viscosity. Therefore, samples of a 100:15 (w/w) mixture of flocculent and porous cellulose beads (Viscopearl, AH-2050L, 2 mm diameter, Rengo, Osaka, Japan) were loaded to allow the liquefied DME to flow through the extraction column. The weight of the sample was 5.31 ± 0.10 g (=10 mL), and cotton and colorless glass beads were filled into the cavity of the extraction column.

Sample Preparation without Flocculants

0.1 wt % C. sorokiniana in the BG-11 culture solution was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min (CN-1040, Hsiangtai, Taipei, Taiwan). The water content was 75.4 ± 2.5 wt %. The precipitated algae and viscopearl were mixed in the ratio 100:15 (w/w). Then, 5.42 g (=10 mL) of the sample was added to the extraction column, and cotton and colorless glass beads were added to the cavity of the extraction column.

Common Organic Solvent Extractions

The chloroform–methanol mixture used for Bligh–Dyer extraction is a frequently used solvent for defining the total lipid content because it dissolves both nonpolar and polar components well. A preliminary study showed that flocculants are insoluble in a chloroform–methanol mixture 2:1 (v/v). Ethanol, which is often used to extract lipids and carotenoid, was also used as a solvent for comparison. Carotenoids are high-value-added products found in microalgae, and their coproduction with lipids is important for the future implementation of microalgae-derived biofuels. In other words, the findings regarding ethanol will be useful in the long term. Preliminary studies have indicated that flocculants are insoluble in ethanol.

Sample Preparation and Conditions with Flocculants

First, the flocculate created in Section was hot-air-dried and disrupted using a food mill (IFM-800DG; Iwatani Corporation, Osaka, Japan) for 5 min. Then, 3.00 g of the dried flocculate was mixed with 300 mL of the organic solvent in a beaker. The flocculate was disrupted in the solvent using a homogenizer equipped with a sawtooth generator probe (10 mm outside diameter, Dremel 300 Series, Robert Bosch Tool Corp., IL, USA) at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, and the mixture was stirred in a beaker for 2.5 h using a stirrer. Then, after suction filtration with aspirator through a hydrophilic polytetrafluoroethylene filter paper (Advantec H010A047A, pore size 0.1 μm, Toyo Roshi Kaisha, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), the organic solvent was evaporated using a rotary evaporator (SB-1200, Eyela Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Sample Preparation and Conditions without Flocculants

C. sorokiniana was dried and disrupted in a food mill (IFM-800DG, Iwatani Corporation) for 5 min. Then, 3.00 g of the dry C. sorokiniana was mixed with 300 mL of organic solvent and disrupted again using a homogenizer at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The subsequent protocol was the same as that used for the flocculant.

Analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) Characterization of Lipids and Residues

To determine whether the flocculants were present in the residue or lipids after the extraction operation, the surface functional groups were measured using FT-IR (PerkinElmer Spectrum Two, PerkinElmer Japan Co., Ltd., Yokohama, Japan).

Elemental Components of Lipids and Residues

Elemental analyses (2400 Series II CHNS/O Elemental Analyzer; PerkinElmer Japan Co., Ltd., Yokohama, Japan) were performed to determine the constituent elements of the lipids and residues extracted from the samples to determine the effect of the flocculant on the extracted lipids.

Fatty Acid Components of Lipid

To elucidate the fatty acid components in the extracted lipids, acid-catalyzed esterification was performed using a fatty acid methylation kit (06482-04; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) as a pretreatment for analysis, and impurities were removed using a FEME purification kit (06483–94; Nacalai Tesque) to convert fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs). The FAMEs were analyzed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS; 7890A GC system and 5975C inert XL MSD with triple-axis detector, Agilent Technologies), and a phenylarylene capillary column (HP-5MS; 30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., Agilent Technologies) was used for fractionation to FAMEs. The temperature was programmed to be held at 100 °C for 5 min and then increased to 300 °C at 4 °C min –1. Injector and detector temperatures were set at 250 °C. The fractionation ratio was 1:1 (helium gas flow rate 24 mL min –1, injection volume 1.0 μL). The detected FAME was subjected by comparing the peak area and retention time of the FAME standard (Supelco 37 Component FAME Mix; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) with the mass spectrum. The internal standard tridecanoic acid (C13:0) was used to correct interassay errors. Specifically, a 500-ppm internal standard reagent was prepared by dissolving 50 mg of methyl tridecanoic acid in 100 mL of hexane, and 0.1 mL of this internal standard reagent was added to 4 mg of lipid. After mixing, hexane was completely removed by nitrogen aeration. The NIST mass spectral database was used for the qualitative analysis of C16:2 and C16:3, and the calibration curve of C16:1 was used for quantitative analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or the mean of three replicates. One-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) using the Bonferroni test was used to test for significant differences in extracted lipid amounts, fatty acid compositions, and elemental compositions between solvents with or without flocculant addition. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. ,

Electrophoretic Light Scattering (DLS) Analysis

The particle size of each microalgae and the zeta potential of the culture media were measured using (DLS and electrophoretic light scattering (ELS), respectively (Zetasizer Pro ZSU3205, Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Worcestershire, England). Changes in zeta potential before and after the addition of polymer flocculants to the culture media were compared to investigate the influence of the ionic nature of the flocculants on microalgal aggregation.

Results and Discussion

In the case of C. sorokiniana flocculated with KA305BH, as shown in Figure a, after liquefied DME was passed through the flocculated C. sorokiniana, the residue changed color from deep green to yellowish green. It also shrank, dried, and became powdery. This indicates that liquefied DME penetrated the flocculated C. sorokiniana, and water and pigments were extracted. In contrast, in the case of C. sorokiniana flocculated with KP1202BH, as shown in Figure b, the color of the flocculated C. sorokiniana became partially lighter and the dark green spots of the original sample were mixed. This indicates that liquefied DME did not sufficiently penetrate the flocculated C. sorokiniana.

Photographic images of C. sorokiniana flocculated with (a) KA305BH and 310 (b) KP1202BH in the extraction column after liquefied DME extraction.

In contrast, in the case of KA305BH, liquefied DME turned the flocculant bright green. The extraction was completed when the color of the extract exiting the extraction column changed from light green to clear and colorless for 20 min or more. The total extraction time was approximately 84 min, that is, the total volume of flowing DME was 789 g (1190 mL). Before extraction, both samples, with and without KA305BH, were dark green, as shown on the left side of Figure a and b, whereas the residues after liquefied DME extraction were light green under dry conditions, as shown in the middle of Figure a and b. When this dried residue was removed from the extraction column, it was easily separated from Viscopearl by sieving. Previous studies have shown that liquefied DME extracts not only lipids, but also water, and the dry state of the residues suggests that the sample was in good contact with liquefied DME. The color of the residue also changed to bright green after liquefied DME extraction, suggesting successful extraction.

Photographic images of (a) samples with KA305BH, (b) samples without KA305BH.

The changes in the amounts of water and lipids extracted from the samples with and without flocculants over time are shown in Figure a and b. First, the extraction behavior of water and lipids from the flocculated C. sorokiniana with KA305BH was almost identical to that of the sample to which no flocculant was added, whereas the extraction was clearly inhibited in the flocculated C. sorokiniana with KP1202BH. This was likely due to the cationic nature of KP1202BH, whereas KA305BH was amphoteric. It is reasonable to assume that KA305BH weakly adheres to the negatively charged C. sorokiniana cell surface, whereas KP1202BH firmly adheres because of strong interactions. This strong attachment suggests that KP1202BH, which is insoluble in liquefied DME, partially covered the C. sorokiniana cell surface and inhibited the extraction of water and lipids. However, the weak attachment of KA305BH to the C. sorokiniana cell surface suggests that there were gaps by which liquefied DME could pass.

Changes over time in the amounts of materials extracted from C. sorokiniana using liquefied DME with and without KA305BH and KP1202BH: (a) water content; (b) lipid content. Values represent the mean ± standard deviation from three independent experiments (n = 3).

The zeta potential of the C. sorokiniana culture was measured to be −16.8 ± 0.6 mV. The addition of KP1202BH and KA305BH shifted the zeta potentials to −4.6 ± 0.7 mV and −8.9 ± 0.5 mV, respectively. In general, a decrease in the absolute value of the zeta potential toward zero indicates reduced electrostatic repulsion between particles, thereby promoting their aggregation. Empirically, particles with zeta potentials greater than ±30.0 mV are considered to be well dispersed. Therefore, the cationic flocculant KP1202BH, which brought the zeta potential closest to zero, was more effective in promoting the aggregation of C. sorokiniana cells than the amphoteric flocculant KA305BH.

Because KP1202BH clearly inhibited the extraction of lipids by liquefied DME, whereas KA305BH did not inhibit it at all, a comparison of lipid extraction with conventional organic solvents was made for flocculants made with KA305BH. The results of flocculation with KA305BH combined with extraction using three different solvents, including liquefied DME, are shown in Table and Figure . In the case of liquefied DME extraction, there was no significant difference between the water and lipid extractions with and without KA305BH. For example, the final extracted water amounts were 90.9 ± 3.0% and 93.7 ± 3.4% of the total water content of the samples for those with and without KA305BH, respectively. The final extracted lipid amounts were 17.5 ± 0.9% and 17.1 ± 0.5% of the total lipid amounts of the samples for those with and without KA305BH, respectively. In the early stages of extraction, the amount of extracted substance increased in proportion to the amount of DME; this slope can be explained by the saturated solubility of each substance in liquefied DME. However, at the end of the extraction phase, the slope decreased. This phenomenon can be explained by the inhibition of mass transfer due to diffusion resistance during extraction, or by the constraint of intermolecular interactions between the samples and solute, which reduces the saturation concentration in the solute. , The extraction curves for water and lipids were not perfectly similar in shape, suggesting that lipids were less extractable than water. First, the water surrounding C. sorokiniana encountered liquefied DME and was extracted by the liquefied DME. At the same time, the liquefied DME dissolved in the surrounding water and diffused into the C. sorokiniana cells to dissolve the lipids, suggesting that the osmotic pressure generated between the inside and outside of the C. sorokiniana cells caused the water to move from the inside to the outside of the cells, and the lipids moved with the water. This mechanism explains the differences in the extraction of water and lipids. In the case of chloroform–methanol extractions, total lipid amounts were 15.9 ± 0.4% and 15.6 ± 0.5% of the dry C. sorokiniana with and without KA305BH. There was no statistically significant difference between lipid extraction with and without KA305BH. Although this value was lower than that of liquefied DME extraction, the difference was not statistically significant. In contrast, in the case of ethanol extraction, the total lipid contents were 7.9 ± 0.9% and 11.0 ± 0.7% of the dry C. sorokiniana with and without KA305BH, respectively. These values were significantly lower than those of liquefied DME and chloroform–methanol extractions. Although the addition of KA305BH inhibited lipid extraction, this difference was not statistically significant. Originally, a mixture of chloroform and methanol was used to determine total lipids and not ethanol; therefore, the poor performance of ethanol extraction itself is not surprising, but the fact that the inhibition behavior of KA305BH varied with different solvents is a new finding. This may be related to the relative strengths of the interactions among the solvent, microalgae cells, and flocculants. In other words, if the interaction between the solvent and flocculant is weaker than that between the microalgae cells and flocculant, the flocculant will be attracted to the microalgae cells more than the solvent. This leaves no space for the solvent to penetrate the microalgal surface, thereby inhibiting lipid extraction.

Plant cells are surrounded by a cell wall, which is mainly composed of cellulose. For example, microalgae such as Chlorella have a robust cell wall that is approximately 88 nm thick. As a result, conventional solvent extraction methods, such as chloroform–methanol extraction or ethanol-based solvent combinations, require long processing times and energy-intensive mechanical disruption to extract bioactive compounds such as lipids from cells. As phospholipids in cell membranes are amphipathic molecules, extraction typically requires a mixture of polar and nonpolar organic solvents. In contrast, liquefied DME (dimethyl ether) is used to extract compounds from both wet and dry lipid-rich biomasses without the need for cell disruption. Liquefied DME extraction is particularly effective for extracting lipids from microalgae with biomineralized cell walls, such as the diatoms Chaetoceros gracilis and Pleurochrysis carterae. The “hard” biomineralized cell walls of these microalgae were found not to interfere with lipid extraction by liquefied DME.

In addition, energy-intensive pretreatment steps, such as dewatering of wet microalgae prior to solvent extraction, are often required to increase the extraction efficiency of bioactive compounds from microalgae. In contrast to traditional supercritical solvents, DME shows considerable miscibility and mutual solubility with water. It exhibits high hydrophilicity and dissolves in water up to 35 wt % (0.55 MPa, 25 °C). Conversely, the solubility of water in DME is 7.0 wt % (0.55 MPa, 25 °C). The water to DME ratio can be adjusted by preblending DME with ethanol at approximately 6 wt %. When interacting with water, DME acts as a hydrogen bond acceptor, forming weak hydrogen bonds between the oxygen atoms of DME and water molecules. This property allows DME to efficiently process wet biomass. Depending on the composition of the mixture, water can also act as a cosolvent for water-soluble compounds.

Ethanol, a low molecular weight organic compound with both hydrophilic and lipophilic properties, is well suited for the extraction of bioactive compounds such as phenolic acids, flavonoids and phenolic acid esters. However, in certain regions or cultures, the use of ethanol for food processing is restricted, necessitating the use of alternative solvents. When using ethanol for extraction of highly aqueous samples, it is essential to add benzene to the ethanol–water mixture to prevent contamination by water through azeotrope formation and to increase the volatility of water, thus preserving nonpolar components.

Conventional extraction techniques such as maceration, Soxhlet extraction and decoction are widely used but suffer from significant drawbacks, including long processing times, high solvent and energy consumption, low selectivity and limited efficiency in recovering thermolabile or volatile compounds. These methods often require predrying of aqueous samples, which can lead to degradation of heat-sensitive bioactive. In addition, common solvents such as ethanol can present health, regulatory or cultural challenges that limit their applicability in certain industries. Modern alternativessuch as ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE), microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), enzyme-assisted extraction (EAE), pressurized liquid extraction (PLE) and supercritical fluid extraction (SFE)have overcome these limitations by reducing solvent consumption, speeding up extraction and improving compound preservation. However, these systems often require expensive equipment, complex operating parameters and may not be suitable for all sample types, particularly wet biomass.

In contrast, the liquefied DME extraction method offers a unique combination of simplicity, versatility and sustainability. It allows direct extraction of both polar and nonpolar compoundsincluding lipidsfrom wet biomass without cell disruption or predrying. Its low polarity, amphiphilic nature and high water miscibility (up to 35% w/w) make it highly effective with diverse feedstocks. DME’s low boiling point (−24.8 °C) facilitates rapid solvent removal with minimal thermal degradation or residue. It is also simple to operate and requires less energy than techniques such as SFE or MAE. Recognized as safe by the European Food Safety Authority, Food Standards Australia New Zealand and the US DuPont petitioned the Environmental Protection Agency, DME is biodegradable, noncarcinogenic and environmentally friendly, with a short atmospheric life and no ozone depleting potential. Overall, the liquefied DME method strikes a practical balance between extraction efficiency, environmental safety and operational simplicity, making it a compelling alternative to both traditional and advanced extraction technologies, particularly for processing wet and sensitive biological materials.

In the previously developed microalgal lipid extraction apparatus, liquefied DME is pumped from a storage tank and fed into an extraction column packed with microalgae, where it simultaneously extracts both water and lipids. The liquefied DME is then evaporated using solar heated water and the resulting DME vapor is condensed with cooling water and returned to the storage tank for reuse. A compressor (or vacuum pump) is installed along the vapor path and is activated at the end of the operation to collect any residual DME vapor remaining in the extraction column and evaporator, compress it and return it to the DME storage tank.

The solar-heated water used for DME evaporation and condensation is a carbon-free thermal energy source. The efficiency of converting solar radiation into thermal energy in this system is around 40–60%, which is significantly higher than the ∼20% efficiency typically achieved by photovoltaic systems. This allows solar energy to be used more efficiently to drive the separation process in the extraction apparatus.

The fact that the recovered lipids retain their calorific value is a notable advance over conventional extraction technologies. Traditional methods often involve high thermal loads or drying steps that can degrade energy-dense compounds. In contrast, the DME-based process allows lipids to be recovered with minimal thermal damage, thereby preserving their energy content.

For biodiesel production from microalgae, the accumulation of neutral lipids is more desirable than that of glycolipids or phospholipids. The fatty acids obtained by liquefied DME extraction include saturated fatty acids (SFAs) such as C16:0 and C18:0, monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) such as C16:1 and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) such as C16:2, C16:3, C18:2 and C18:3. The lipid profile obtained from Chlorella indicates a favorable mixture of long chain SFAs and short chain MUFAs or PUFAs, making it a promising feedstock for high quality biodiesel.

Biodiesel typically consists of a mixture of fatty acid esters with carbon chain lengths ranging from C16 to C18. The number of carbon atoms and the degree of saturation in the fatty acid molecules are critical factors that influence the fuel properties of biodiesel. Looking at individual fatty acid ester molecules, properties such as cetane number, melting point, boiling point, calorific value and viscosity tend to increase with increasing chain length. Conversely, these properties generally decrease as the number of double bonds increases.

While long chain SFAs have superior combustion properties due to their high cetane numbers, their high viscosity can hinder effective fuel atomization. In contrast, unsaturated fatty acids have lower viscosity, which can improve atomization, but tend to have lower cetane numbers and lower heating values. In addition, the presence of allylic and bis-allylic positions in unsaturated molecules makes them more susceptible to oxidative degradation, particularly in the presence of atmospheric oxygen. Consequently, a high PUFA content in biodiesel can affect its oxidative stability.

The increased presence of unsaturated fatty acid esters has also been associated with a longer ignition delay, resulting in higher peak pressures and increased heat release rates during combustion. Such properties may contribute to improved combustion efficiency in the premixed phase and result in reduced emissions of pollutants such as CO and hydrocarbons. This improved combustion behavior may also be due to the inherently lower viscosity of unsaturated fatty acids.

Taken together, these findings suggest that biodiesel composed predominantly of a single class of fatty acid (i.e., SFA, MUFA or PUFA) may not exhibit optimal performance. Instead, an ideal biodiesel feedstock should have a balanced composition of long-chain SFAs and short-chain MUFAs or PUFAs to achieve both desirable combustion properties and oxidative stability.

Conclusion

The impact of flocculation on extraction with liquefied DME, which does not require drying as a pretreatment, was verified. C. sorokiniana was harvested using environmentally friendly polymer flocculants, and lipid extraction experiments using liquefied DME and conventional organic solvents were conducted on the flocculated C. sorokiniana. When the cationic flocculant KP1202BH was used, the viscosity of the flocculated C. sorokiniana increased significantly, and when mixed with the porous cellulose spheres, the strong diffusion resistance prevented the liquefied DME from contacting it and inhibiting lipid extraction. In contrast, lipids were extracted from C. sorokiniana flocculated with the amphoteric flocculant KA305BH, and extraction using liquefied DME was not inhibited. When lipids were extracted from C. sorokiniana flocculated KA305BH using conventional chloroform–methanol and ethanol, the amount of extracted lipids was slightly reduced in chloroform–methanol, whereas it was significantly reduced with ethanol. Thus, these results suggest that there is an appropriate combination of solvents, microalgae, and flocculants to effectively extract lipids from microalgae recovered using polymer-based flocculants. The combination of the flocculant KA305BH and lipid extraction with liquefied DME is one of the most effective combinations against C. sorokiniana.
