Identification and validation of hub genes involved in papillary thyroid carcinoma progression
Authors: Zunera Khalid, Khalid Khan, Farah Nazir, Chengbing Yao, Seyed Majid Mousavi Mehmandousti, Yuhua Shang, Humaira Amin, Yixiang Xing, Xikong Pan, Shoubing Zhou, Qingyu Cheng, Tengchuan Jin, Xucai Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPapillary Thyroid Cancer (PTC) is one of the most prevalent endocrine malignancies, and its incidence rate has almost tripled over the past three decades. The current study aimed to identify gene alterations and biomarkers contributing to PTC progression. Based on PTC transcriptome sequencing data, differential gene expression analysis is used to systematically categorize hub genes associated with PTC progression. A total of 1550 overlapping differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in PTC compared to normal thyroid tissues, including 1149 upregulated and 401 downregulated genes. In addition, 3192 DEGs were detected in PTC relative to their normal adjacent tissues (NATs), comprising 1431 upregulated and 1761 downregulated genes. KEGG pathway analysis revealed pathways associated with cancer, proteoglycans in cancer, parathyroid hormone synthesis, and chemical carcinogenesis receptor activity. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis identified FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1 as highly ranked hub genes among the upregulated genes involved in several cancer pathways, including the PID Syndecan-1 pathway, Proteoglycans in cancer, and the CCL18 signaling pathway. The gene expression validation through Reverse Transcription-quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR) further validated the significance of the above findings, demonstrating substantial upregulation of FN1 (7.4-fold increase), SDC1 (5.2-fold increase), and MAPK1 (5.8-fold increase) in papillary thyroid tissues relative to their adjacent normal tissues. Western blot analysis further validated the upregulation of FN1 at the protein level. These findings highlight a strong association between the identified hub genes (FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1) and papillary thyroid cancer, supporting their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Introduction

Thyroid cancer is the most widespread endocrine malignancy worldwide, and its prevalence has increased considerably over the past three decades [1]. The incidence rate is 10.2 per 100,000 women and 3.1 per 100,000 males worldwide. This condition accounts for 5.1% of the estimated female cancer burden [2]. PTC accounts for 70% to 85.9% of the total incidence of all thyroid malignancies. Although PTC is typically non-aggressive and has a promising prognosis, some metastasized lesions cannot be treated with surgery or radioactive iodine [1].

PTC is the most prevalent form of DTC (Differentiated Thyroid Cancer) [3, 4]. Several risk factors, including iodine sufficiency and autoimmune lymphocytic thyroiditis (Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis (HT)) [5], are associated with an increased incidence of PTC. It is a genetically straightforward cancer type with a modest somatic mutation burden on each tumor cell. More than 90% of thyroid cancers contain driver mutations, which provide a selective growth advantage, promoting cancer development [4]. The dominance of thyroid cancer involves the dysregulation of the MAPK (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase) and PI3K/AKT (Phosphatidylinositol 3-Kinase) signaling pathways in its molecular pathogenesis. Such genetic alterations are prevalent in PTC, permanently activating MAPK or PI3K signaling pathways [6].

RNA sequencing has produced many gene expression and NGS (Next-Generation Sequencing) archives and databases. However, the small sample size may result in inflated and unreliable findings. Combining RNA-seq datasets from different data repositories and sources can enhance the statistical reliability and efficiency of the results produced. Meta-analysis can significantly increase statistical power and yield more precise and robust results, thereby facilitating a more accurate evaluation of DEGs [7].

For a more accurate prediction of dysregulated pathways and genes implicated in PTC pathophysiology, we aimed to conduct a transcriptomic meta-analysis with four eligible RNA-seq datasets containing 139 samples to identify DEGs in PTC and their NATs relative to normal thyroid tissues. This approach incorporated literature mining, databases, gene expression, and network analysis. Consequently, this study has systematically identified the genes associated with the progression of PTC, shedding new light on the molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets.

Data collection and dataset information

The search terms used in the GEO (Gene Expression Omnibus) database (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), which is linked with the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), were “Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma” “Human” [Organism] as the keyword. Datasets were included if they met the following criteria: (1) bulk transcriptome profiling (RNA-seq) from human thyroid tissue; (2) contained PTC tumor samples and a non-tumor comparison group (normal thyroid and/or Normal Adjacent Tissue (NAT)) to enable differential expression analysis; (3) had publicly accessible expression matrices (and corresponding platform annotation files where applicable) suitable for downstream normalization and analysis; and (4) provided basic sample metadata allowing classification into Normal, NAT, and PTC groups. Datasets were excluded if they were (1) single-cell studies (2), non-human (3), lacked a control group, or (4) lacked sufficient metadata to define sample groups. The NAT group consisted of histologically non-tumor tissues collected from PTC patients and potentially influenced by the tumor microenvironment. In contrast, normal thyroid samples represented tissues obtained from individuals with no PTC malignancy. A total of 139 samples were downloaded from these datasets: GSE165724 [8], GSE201365 [9], GSE64912 [10], and GSE153659 [11] (Table 1).

RNA-seq data analysis

The primary analyses performed on the RNA-seq data included standard quality control procedures, alignment and mapping of sequencing reads to the reference genome, gene expression quantification, and identification of differentially expressed genes among malignant thyroid samples from PTC patients, NAT, and normal thyroid samples, thereby allowing comparison between true baseline thyroid expression and tumor-adjacent molecular alterations.

Quality control checkpoints

The FASTX-Toolkit-v0.0.14 (https://github.com/agordon/fastq_toolkit and fastp-v0.19.5 (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) were used to estimate the quality criteria of the raw reads. The mapping quality of sequences can be improved by removing nucleotide bases if they become too short [12]. SeqPrep (https://github.com/jstjohn/seqprep) was used to filter original sequencing data for accuracy, and Sickle (https://github.com/najoshi/sickle) was used to remove low-quality reads, eradicate poor-quality bases, and trim adaptor sequences.

Reference genome-based mapping

RNA reads were aligned to the Human (GRCh38.p13) reference genome (http://asia.ensembl.org/Homo_sapiens/Info/Index) using the Hisat2-v2.1.0 alignment program (https://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2/) with the default parameters. The proportion of mapped reads is a significant indicator of alignment quality, referring to sequencing precision and RNA contamination.

Gene expression quantification

The simplest method for expression quantification is to sum the raw counts of mapped reads. The gene and transcript expression levels were estimated using RSEM (RNA-Seq by Expectation Maximization) (https://github.com/deweylab/RSEM) based on TPM (Transcripts Per Million) reads by normalizing sequencing depth and gene length.

Sample correlation analysis and batch effect correction

A Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to determine the direction and magnitude of the relationship between all samples. The study of VENN diagrams revealed the unique and shared genes among the samples. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to identify the gene expression differences between the control and infected experimental conditions. The batch effect arising from the integration of RNA-seq data generated across four sequencing platforms (Illumina HiSeq, Illumina NovaSeq, and BGISEQ-500) was corrected by applying the ComBat algorithm implemented in the sva R package, using the default parameters. It was applied with a parametric empirical Bayes adjustment, using the sequencing platform as the batch variable, while preserving biological condition as the model matrix.

Exploratory plots were generated using NOISeq-v2.18.0 (https://www.Bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/NOISeq.html) and Limma-v3.38.3 (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/limma.html) for the evaluation and visualization of the expression matrix and differential gene expression of the samples.

Differential gene expression analysis

The differential gene expression analysis was conducted to evaluate and compare the DEGs between healthy control groups and infected groups using normal processing and screening conditions. As the present study includes biological replicates from the same species (Homo sapiens), the R package DESeq2 Version 1.24.0 (http://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html) was utilized. The default parameters to screen significantly differentially expressed genes were p-adjust < 0.05 and |log2fc| ≥ 1.

GO and KEGG pathway analysis

To explore the biological function and associated pathways of the DEGs and hub genes, GO analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were executed by ClusterProfiler-v3.6.0 software [13] in R language (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/clusterProfiler.html). Enrichment significance thresholds were set at padj-value < 0.05.

Hub genes identification

PPI network analysis was performed using the STRING (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes) (https://string-db.org/) online database with a default confidence score of 0.4 (medium confidence). This threshold is commonly used to balance network coverage and reliability of predicted interactions. Furthermore, the cytoHubba plugin [14] in Cytoscape (https://cytoscape.org/) [15] was utilized to identify the top 10 hub genes in the PPI network based on the Degree (connectivity) algorithm. Degree centrality was chosen because it reflects the number of direct interactions a protein has within the network, providing a straightforward and widely accepted measure of a gene’s importance in the protein-protein interaction network [15].

Sample collection

Thyroid tissue samples, consisting of six PTC specimens and their six adjacent normal tissues serving as controls (NAT), were collected following a standardized procedure. To ensure the integrity and quality of RNA for subsequent RT-qPCR analysis, each sample was stored in Vazyme RNA Keeper Tissue Stabilizer (R501). The inclusion criteria were based on histopathological confirmation of papillary thyroid cancer and the availability of matched adjacent normal tissue. Surgical resection was performed to obtain both cancerous and adjacent normal thyroid tissues. Tissues were immediately immersed in RNA keeper solution to maintain RNA integrity and prevent degradation.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The collection process adhered to ethical guidelines, and informed consent was obtained from each participant. This study was conducted in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring the protection, rights, and welfare of all involved individuals. Sample collections and experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of the Anhui Cancer Hospital, Hefei, Anhui, China, under approval number 2024-TJK-01.

RNA extraction and cDNA preparation

The RNA extraction process outlined in the E.Z.N.A.® Total RNA Kit I from Omega Biotek (https://www.omegabiotek.com/) was utilized, involving homogenization and lysis steps to liberate RNA from the tissue matrix. The RNA’s quality and quantity were assessed using spectrophotometry (for example, with a NanoDrop instrument) and gel electrophoresis techniques. RNA that exhibited suitable ratios of 260/280 and 260/230, indicative of high purity, was chosen for further experiments. The conversion of the extracted RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA) was achieved using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (+ gDNA wiper) from Vazyme® (https://www.vazymebiotech.com/).

Quantitative PCR

RT-qPCR was performed using synthesized cDNAs with RealQ Plus 2× SYBR Master Mix Green and a Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) LightCycler 96 System, a reliable platform provided by Roche Diagnostics (https://diagnostics.roche.com/). SnapGene (https://www.snapgene.com/) was employed to design primers specific to the targeted gene’s DNA sequence (Table 2). A housekeeping gene, GADPH (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase), was used as an endogenous control [16].

The expression of the top hub genes was further validated across TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) datasets via UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/), which provides direct and easy access to publicly available high-quality TCGA datasets.

FN1 protein extraction and western blot analysis

Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for one h at room temperature. FN1 (Fibronectin-1) protein was detected using a rabbit anti-FN1 primary antibody (Abcam, ab2412) overnight at 4 °C. GAPDH was used as a loading control. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence and imaged with a ChemiDoc system. Molecular weight markers were included (e.g., 250, 150, 100, 75, 50, 37, 25 kDa). FN1 protein levels were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to the GAPDH protein.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis

Portions of the same tumor and adjacent non-cancerous tissues were homogenized in cell lysis buffer from the MolPure® Flash CellTissue Total RNA Kit containing steel beads. RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription. qPCR was performed using FN1-specific primers and GAPDH-specific primers to quantify mRNA expression levels. For each patient, FN1 mRNA in tumor tissue was normalized to GAPDH and compared to the matched adjacent non-cancerous tissue.

Survival analysis and ROC curve

The diagnostic performance of FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1 was evaluated using Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves generated through the SRplot web server https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/plot_basic_one_or_multi_ROC_curve_plot_106_en [17], with the area under the curve (AUC) indicating discriminatory power. Survival analysis was performed using the KM Plotter web server [18] (https://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php?p=termsofuse&ofuse), where PTC patients were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on optimal cut-offs automatically determined by the platform. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated, and survival differences were assessed using the log-rank test, with hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values reported.

Identification of TFs and miRNAs for the top-ranked hub genes

TFs (Transcription Factors) and miRNAs of hub genes existing in TRRUST were computationally predicted using the miRNet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/, version 2.0). TRRUST (Transcriptional Regulatory Relationships Unravelled by Sentence-based Text-mining) is the most extensive, freely available database of literature-curated human TF-target interactions to date, delivering a consistent benchmark for the computational reconstruction of human transcriptional regulatory networks [19]. miRNet is a user-friendly, web-based tool designed to clarify miRNA (microRNA) functions by incorporating user data with the current information through network-based visual analytics [6]. As no experimental validation was performed in this study, all identified miRNA and TF interactions were treated as computationally derived regulatory associations based on curated database information.

Results

We implemented a transcriptome-based approach to evaluate differential gene expression. We identified hub genes potentially associated with PTC progression using RNA-seq datasets of PTC patients and their NAT relatives compared to normal thyroid tissues.

RNA-seq data preprocessing and quality control

RNA-seq datasets were obtained with millions of raw reads and base pairs per sample. Alternatively, clean bases were acquired after quality control and adapter sequence trimming. For each RNA-seq dataset, ∼approximately 98% of the reads were uniquely mapped and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38).p13 (Supplementary Data 1).

Batch effect correction and expression quantification

When it comes to sequencing data, a batch effect can already be seen in two runs at various time points [20]. The principal sources of variance in the data, including batch effects, can be determined using PCA. The primary elements linked to batch effects can therefore be eliminated, while those related to biological variation can be kept. To evaluate and mitigate batch effects arising from the integration of datasets generated on different sequencing platforms, batch correction was performed using the ComBat function. Before correction, samples showed clear clustering according to sequencing platform, indicating significant batch effects. After ComBat correction, PCA revealed a marked reduction in platform-driven clustering, with samples no longer segregating by platform. (Fig. 2). These results demonstrate effective mitigation of batch effects and support the reliability of downstream analyses, including the identification of overlapping differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Identification of Hub Genes from PPI Network

The hub genes are considered central players in the network and are often associated with critical regulatory functions or biological processes [21]. Hub genes are highly connected genes within gene co-expression or protein-protein interaction networks. In contrast, target genes represent genes regulated by a specific transcription factor or other regulatory elements.

We obtained a PPI network comprising overlapping upregulated genes, consisting of 744 nodes and 548 edges, with an average node degree of 1.47 and an enrichment p-value greater than 1.0E-16 for the PTC vs. Normal thyroid tissues dataset. The hub genes identified from upregulated DEGs using the cytoHubba plugin included FN1, SDC1, MAPK1, COL1A1, COL1A2, ACAN, CD44, SDC4, MUC1, and MET. FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1 were the top 3 hub genes (Fig. 8A).

Predicted hub genes are dysregulated in cancer tissues compared to controls

The RT-qPCR results highlighted in the study reveal a significant upregulation of three key genes, FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1, in the thyroid tissue samples from patients with Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma (PTC) compared to their Normal Adjacent Controls (NAT). Specifically, FN1 showed a seven-fold increase (fold change = 7.4) with statistical significance (P = 0.01) (Fig. 9A), indicating a strong association with PTC progression. Similarly, SDC1 and MAPK1 were also significantly upregulated, with fold changes of 5.2 (P = 0.02) (Fig. 9B) and 5.8 (P = 0.04) (Fig. 9C), respectively. These findings support the robustness of the transcriptome analysis performed and suggest that the overexpression of these genes could be pivotal in the PTC progression.

FN1 Protein and mRNA Expression

Western blot analysis showed that FN1 protein expression was significantly higher in PTC tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues in all three patients. FN1 was detected at ~ 220 kDa, and GAPDH at ~ 37 kDa. Densitometry analysis normalized FN1 signals to GAPDH, confirming increased FN1 protein levels in tumor tissues (FN1/GAPDH ratio, p < 0.05) (Fig. 10A; Supplementary Data 4). It was suggested that the FN1 protein was abundantly present in the PTC tissues relative to their normal adjacent controls. Therefore, the Western blot results justify that FN1 expression is upregulated at both the gene and protein levels, suggesting its potential role in papillary thyroid cancer progression.

Furthermore, qPCR results demonstrated that FN1 mRNA expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues across all three patients. Relative FN1 expression, normalized to GAPDH, consistently showed elevation in tumor samples, corroborating the protein-level results (Fig. 10B).

Clinical relevance of Hub genes

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to assess the prognostic significance of FN1, MAPK1, and SDC1 expression in PTC. Patients with high FN1 expression exhibited a markedly reduced overall survival compared to those with low expression, as evidenced by a clear and sustained separation of the survival curves and a statistically significant log-rank p-value. Among the three genes analyzed, FN1 demonstrated the strongest association with adverse clinical outcome (Supplementary Fig. S3_A). Elevated MAPK1 expression was also associated with poorer overall survival, showing a moderate but statistically significant difference between high- and low-expression groups. This suggests that MAPK1 expression contributes to prognostic stratification in PTC, although its impact appears less pronounced than that of FN1 (Supplementary Fig. S3_B). For SDC1, survival analysis revealed a significant difference between expression groups, indicating a prognostic association; however, the extent of curve separation was relatively modest compared to FN1 and MAPK1. This finding suggests that SDC1 may act as a supportive prognostic marker rather than a dominant determinant of survival (Supplementary Fig. S3_C).

To further evaluate the diagnostic relevance of candidate biomarkers identified in PTC, ROC curve analysis was performed for FN1, MAPK1, and SDC1 using expression data. Among the three genes, FN1 demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.963 (Supplementary Fig. S4_A), indicating excellent sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing PTC samples from normal controls. SDC1 also demonstrated strong diagnostic performance, achieving an AUC of 0.826 (Supplementary Fig. S4_B), which supports its utility as a reliable diagnostic indicator. In contrast, MAPK1 showed comparatively limited ability to distinguish, with an AUC of 0.627, suggesting restricted effectiveness as a standalone diagnostic biomarker (Supplementary Fig. S4_C).

miRNA-target and TF regulatory network identification

Using the miRNet database, the six most common miRNAs responsible for regulating the top-ranked targeted hub genes (FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1) were identified computationally (Supplementary Fig. S5_A). The hub genes were regulated by miRNAs, including hsa-miR-143-3p, hsa-miR-9-5p, and hsa-miR-7-5p, indicating their involvement in PTC progression. The miRNA hsa-mir-143-3p may be involved in certain regulatory pathways that regulate the cell cycle and apoptosis, which are pivotal in cancer development. The mutations of this miRNA could affect the tumor suppressor genes or oncogenes involved in PTC progression [22]. Additionally, hsa-mir-9-5p is known to regulate migration and invasion in other cancers, and its role in PTC progression may be similar, potentially affecting metastatic potential [23]. Similarly, hsa-mir-7-5p may affect the signaling pathways that regulate cell growth and differentiation, which are frequently dysregulated in cancerous tissues [24].

A total of 14 TFs were computationally identified for FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1. FN1 has the most complex regulation of the genes discussed, with twelve TFs identified: AR (Androgen Receptor), ATF2 (Activating Transcription Factor 2), and TWIST2 (Supplementary Fig. S5_B). The high betweenness score of 111.5 indicates that FN1 is a key node in the network, as it passes many regulatory signals. MAPK1 had three TFs, including MYC, TWIST1, and SPI1, with a betweenness score = 29. NFKB1 and RELA were the TFs for SDC1, with a score of 0.5.

Discussion

The present study incorporated four eligible RNA-seq datasets from the GEO database to identify DEGs in PTC relative to their NAT and normal thyroid tissues. All the original datasets in the meta-analysis underwent RNA-seq quality control assessment, a crucial step in conducting transcriptome analysis. We screened genes from PTC tissues relative to normal tissues and their adjacent normal tissues using RNA-seq datasets. We screened genes from PTC tissues relative to normal tissues and their adjacent normal tissues using RNA-seq datasets. We identified 1550 DEGs (1149 upregulated and 401 downregulated genes) from PTC tissues relative to normal tissues, while 3192 DEGs (1431 upregulated and 1761 downregulated genes) from PTC tissues relative to their adjacent normal tissues. We have also identified one hub gene module. Among the upregulated DEGs, DCSTAMP and SLC22A31 were highly overexpressed genes among PTC patients relative to normal thyroid tissues. DCSTAMP (Dendrocyte Expressed Seven Transmembrane Protein) is a protein mainly expressed in dendritic cells and essential for the multinucleation and fusion of osteoclasts [25]. Dendritic cell types are infrequent or absent in healthy thyroid tissue, whereas the density of dendritic cells is elevated in thyroid cancer, particularly PTC [9].

PPI network analysis identified FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1 as top hub genes, which were overexpressed in PTC patients compared to normal tissues. FN1 encodes the glycoprotein fibronectin found in the plasma cell surface and extracellular matrix. It participates in cell adhesion and migration during wound healing, embryogenesis, blood coagulation, host defense, cell proliferation, and metastasis [24]. Multiple studies have demonstrated its association with cancer development, including oral squamous cell carcinoma, renal cancer [26], and thyroid cancer [27]. Additionally, FN1 overexpression was substantially associated with lymph node metastasis in the cervical region of PTC patients [28, 29]. Consistent with these studies, our research suggests that FN1 upregulation is associated with a poor prognosis and molecular heterogeneity in PTC. SDC1 (Syndecan-1) is a crucial cell surface adhesion protein essential for maintaining cell morphology and interacting with the adjacent microenvironment [30]. Overexpression and dysregulation of SDC1 lead to cancer development by increasing invasion, cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis, and are linked with relapse via chemoresistance [30]. Overall, RNA-seq studies on thyroid cancer have provided valuable insights into the expression patterns and functional significance of SDC1 [30–32]. Similarly, the MAPK1 gene, also known as ERK2 (Extracellular Signal-Regulated Kinase 2), encodes a protein involved in the MAPK signaling pathway [33]. The dysregulation of the MAPK signaling pathway, including alterations in the MAPK1 gene, has been implicated in thyroid cancer [34]. Consequently, it has become an attractive target for therapeutic intervention in this disease [35]. Through q-PCR analysis, we confirmed the significant upregulation of these genes in the thyroid tissue samples of patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) compared to their normal adjacent controls. These findings validate and strengthen the results obtained through transcriptome analysis, emphasizing the reliability of our gene expression data. The remarkable fold changes and low P-values associated with FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1 underscore their potential roles as key players in the development and progression of PTC. FN1 was prioritized for protein-level validation based on its central role in the PPI network, where it exhibited the highest degree score (ranked 1st; Degree = 108). In addition, RT-qPCR analysis showed that FN1 had the most pronounced differential expression among the identified hub genes, with a statistically significant 7.4-fold upregulation (P = 0.01), further supporting its selection for downstream validation. Accordingly, FN1 expression was examined at the protein level by western blot analysis, and the results were consistent with the RT-qPCR findings, indicating that FN1 is markedly upregulated in PTC and that its elevated expression is associated with poorer patient prognosis. These results also support a recent study via a literature search [36, 37]. However, it is essential to note that these studies may have specific focuses and limitations, and a comprehensive understanding of FN1 in PTC would require considering multiple studies and their collective findings to enhance the reliability of further translational studies. ROC curve analysis further demonstrated the diagnostic potential of these hub genes, with FN1 achieving an excellent AUC of 0.963, SDC1 showing good performance with an AUC of 0.826, and MAPK1 displaying limited accuracy with an AUC of 0.627. Survival analysis using the KM Plotter web server stratified patients into high- and low-expression groups based on optimal cut-offs. FN1 exhibited the most pronounced separation of survival curves, indicating a strong prognostic impact, while MAPK1 showed a moderate but significant association, and SDC1 displayed a supportive trend. Differences in survival were assessed using the log-rank test, with hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values reported, further supporting the clinical relevance of FN1 as both a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker in PTC. However, it is essential to note that these studies may have specific focuses and limitations, and a comprehensive understanding of FN1 in PTC would require considering multiple studies and their collective findings to enhance the reliability of further translational studies. To contextualize our findings, a critical literature search was conducted to compare the current experimental results with prior studies. The elevated expression of FN1 in the current study was consistent with previous findings reported by Sponziello et al., who indicated increased FN1 levels in aggressive PTC, emphasizing its role in promoting tumor cell migration and invasion [27]. Similarly, Geng et al. identified FN1 as a prognostic biomarker that correlates with immune cell infiltration and tumor progression in PTC [37]. Several other studies also suggested that FN1 can contribute to both structural modifications and immune modulation within the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, the upregulation of SDC1 observed in the present research aligned with the findings reported by Bologna-Molina et al., who demonstrated an association between SDC1 expression and extracapsular invasion [38]. Remarkably, their study employed immunohistochemistry to evaluate SDC1 expression. Dezhong Sun et al. [39], Junyi Wang et al. [40], and Linghao Wang et al. [41], reported upregulation of MAPK1 by RT-qPCR, and these findings also aligned with the current study. The MAPK signaling pathway, with MAPK1 as a central component, is recognized for its crucial role in thyroid development and progression. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating that MAPK1 activation promotes cell proliferation and survival in PTC. Notably, although previous research has primarily focused on MAPK1’s independent role in tumor growth, the current research emphasizes its connection with FN1 and SDC1, implying a potential interaction between these genes in regulating the PTC microenvironment and progression.

TFs are important molecules that regulate gene transcription. The human genome encodes approximately 2000 TFs [42]. The study investigated the effect of transcription factors (TFs) on the expression of the top-ranked hub genes (FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1). Fourteen TFs, including AR, ATF2, CEBPA, EGR1, KLF8, NFKB1, PARP1, RELA, SNAI1, SOX17, TWIST1, TWIST2, SPI1 and MYC were identified. AR (Androgen Receptor) plays a significant role in prostate cancer progression [43]. One study reported that altered expression of EGR1/2 (Early Growth Response) affected the proliferation of PTC cells [44]. The REL/NFKB1 transcription factors have been involved in PTC progression [45, 46]. Furthermore, the presence of EGR (Early Growth Response) transcription factors, specifically EGR1 and EGR2, in the identified set is consistent with previous research demonstrating their effect on PTC cell proliferation [44]. EGR TFs have been implicated in various cellular processes, including growth, differentiation, and apoptosis, highlighting their potential significance in PTC pathogenesis. Given their established functions in cellular signaling pathways and immune responses, the participation of the REL/NFKB1 transcription factors is especially intriguing. Notably, these TFs have been linked to the progression of PTC [45, 46], indicating that they may play a role in the complex molecular mechanisms underlying the development of thyroid cancer. Further experimental investigation of the interactions between these TFs and hub genes may reveal novel therapeutic targets for strategies designed to modulate gene expression and slow the progression of PTC.

Although over 90% of all mammalian genomes are transcribed, less than 2% are translated into proteins, and most are transcribed as noncoding RNAs [47]. Recently, the regulatory role of miRNAs has been recognized in several distinct stem cells as they modulate signaling pathways by selectively targeting the molecules involved [48]. The three top-ranked hub genes (FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1) were regulated by miRNAs, including hsa-mir-143-3p, hsa-mir-9-5p, and hsa-mir-7-5p indicating that they may serve a role in PTC progression. The regulatory role of hsa-mir-143-3p has been anticipated in Wnt and MAPK signaling pathways [47]. Hsa-mir-9-5p is downregulated in PTC and significantly targets serine/threonine kinase (BRAF) and B-Raf proto-oncogene [49, 50]. The downregulation of hsa-miR-9-5p has also been reported in Follicular thyroid cancer [51]. In one study, miR-7-5p inhibited colon cancer cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis [52]. It has been demonstrated that miRNAs can be used as biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and treatment due to their tumor-suppressive effects. Consequently, DEGs, hub genes, their associated TFs, and miRNAs identified in the present study may play essential roles in PTC prognosis and progression and could be verified further. These integrated molecular networks offer valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms that drive thyroid tumorigenesis.

The candidate hub genes identified in this study may have potential applications in the prevention and treatment of papillary thyroid carcinoma. Their consistent overexpression across multiple datasets and experimental validation suggest their utility as molecular biomarkers for early detection, disease monitoring, and risk stratification in PTC. From a therapeutic perspective, these genes and their associated regulatory pathways may represent promising targets for future intervention strategies, including pathway-specific inhibitors, gene-silencing approaches, or targeted drug development. Although functional and clinical validation is required to confirm therapeutic efficacy, the present findings provide a foundational framework for future translational research aimed at improving personalized treatment strategies for PTC.

Limitations and future perspectives

Although the present study provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying PTC, several limitations should be acknowledged. While RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed the differential expression of key candidate genes at the mRNA and protein levels, functional validation in physiologically relevant models remains necessary. In vivo studies, such as siRNA-mediated knockdown or overexpression experiments, are required to validate the role of these genes in tumor development and progression. Moreover, in vitro studies using PTC cell lines could provide deeper insights into specific molecular interactions and regulatory pathways. The study was also limited by its small sample size, which prevented subtype-specific or molecularly stratified analyses. Additionally, potential confounding factors, including variability in the distance of adjacent tissue from the tumor margin and the presence of lymphocytic thyroiditis, were not fully controlled. Future work integrating larger patient cohorts and multi-omics datasets would enhance the robustness and translational relevance of these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current study has identified three key hub genes, including FN1, SDC1, and MAPK1, that are significantly overexpressed in papillary thyroid carcinoma tissues compared to normal thyroid tissues. The expression of these hub genes was further validated through RT-qPCR, while western blot analysis confirmed the upregulation of FN1 at the protein level. These findings suggest that oncogenic alterations in PTC may extend beyond the tumor, influencing adjacent normal tissues within the tumor microenvironment, potentially contributing to local invasion and metastatic behavior, which highlights the broader biological impact of tumor-derived signaling on surrounding non-cancerous cells. Collectively, these insights enhance our understanding of PTC progression and may help identify novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Further in vitro and in vivo experiments are needed to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms and clinical relevance of these hub genes in the development and treatment of papillary thyroid cancer.
