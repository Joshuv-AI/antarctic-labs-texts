Dental pulp stem cells maintain epigenetic chromatin architecture remodeling primed by the etiological stimulus of biliary atresia
Authors: Soichiro Sonoda, Liting Yu, Lisha Dai, Yukari Kyumoto-Nakamura, Koichiro Yoshimaru, Ratih Yuniartha, Ying Liu, M Majd Sharifa, Sara Murata, Mohammed Fouad Zakaria, Hiroki Kato, Norihisa Uehara, Satoshi Fukumoto, Haruyoshi Yamaza, Toshiharu Matsuura, Tomoaki Taguchi, Tatsuro Tajiri, Takayoshi Yamaza

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBiliary atresia (BA) is a nongenetic cholangiopathy characterized by biliary obliteration. However, the underlying pathological mechanism remains unclear. We aimed to explore the epigenetic BA pathology by using BA-specific deciduous dental pulp stem cells (BA-SHED), which develop in parallel with cholangiocyte progenitor cells in human embryos.MethodsBA-SHED were isolated from human exfoliated deciduous teeth of patients with BA using the colony-forming unit fibroblast method. After sequential stimulation with cytokines and chemicals in cultured BA-SHED, the in vitro bile duct-forming capacity was analyzed using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and immunofluorescence. Expression of hepatocyte nuclear factor 6 (HNF6) and transforming growth factor beta receptor 2 (TGFBR2) was analyzed using immunoblotting and RT-qPCR. The regulation of chromatin architecture at the HNF6 promoter was analyzed using nuclease-accessible chromatin-qPCR and chromatin immunoprecipitation-qPCR.ResultsBA-SHED showed an inheritable increase in HNF6 levels, resulting in TGFBR2 suppression and deficiency in bile duct formation. BA-SHED also accumulated Brahma and P65 complexes around the HNF6 promoter with chromatin architecture remodeling. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interferon-gamma co-stimulation mimicked the epigenetic signatures of BA-SHED.ConclusionThe present epigenetic memory in BA-SHED implies that BA-SHED imprint bile duct deficiency through TGFBR2 dysregulated by the HNF6 promoter activation epigenetically. Background

Biliary atresia (BA) is a rare congenital cholangiopathy characterized by biliary obliteration and progressive hepatic fibrosis [1]. If untreated, BA will inevitably lead to death due to end-stage liver disease by age 2 years. Kasai hepatoportoenterostomy (KPE) is essential for restoring bile flow in infants with BA with a native liver [2], whereas BA patients often require liver transplantation in a few years post-KPE [2–4]. Notably, the severity of liver fibrosis due to biliary anomalies determines post-KPE outcomes, including jaundice clearance and long-term native liver survival [5, 6]. However, the insufficient understanding of the BA pathology results in a poor prognosis and inadequate treatment for native liver survival [7].

The pathological mechanisms underlying biliary anomalies of extrahepatic and intrahepatic bile duct network in BA remain unclear [3]. Several genes, including adducin 3 (ADD3), cripto, EGF-CFC family member (CRIPTO), nodal growth differentiation factor (NODAL), left-right determination factor (LEFTY), glypican 1 (GPC1), EGF-like fibulin extracellular matrix protein 1 (EFEMP1), ARF GTPase 6 (ARF6), pericentrin (PCNT), kinesin family member 3B (KIF3B), and, tetratricopeptide repeat domain 17 (TTC17), have been reported to be associated with an increased risk of BA [8]. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion of ADD3 or GPC1 in liver organoids resulted in impaired bile duct formation [9]. Although genetic modification, including enhancement of GATA binding protein 6 (GATA6) and reduction of SRY-box transcription factor 17 (SOX17), potentially contribute to the onset of human BA associated with hepatic ductular reaction and gallbladder wall abnormality [10, 11], familial inheritance in BA is contradicted. Pre- and peri-natal inflammation exposures by virus, including cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, and human papillomavirus, and autoimmune reaction are potentially involved in BA ethnology [3, 8, 12]. Inflammation triggers an autoimmune response against overexpressed histocompatibility antigens on the bile duct epithelium, and the infiltrated T cells damage the bile ducts with sclerosis and obliteration [13, 14]. Therefore, the epigenetic modification to liver damage in BA may be imprinted in the BA liver cells, particularly in biliary epithelial cells (BECs) and their progenitors, hepatic progenitor cells (HPCs).

HPCs process that are accompanied by coordinated changes in cytoskeletal proteins, transcription factors, epithelial junction components, and functional transporters during liver development [15–17]. HPCs display a mixed epithelial and progenitor phenotype, characterized by expression of keratin 18 (KRT18), KRT19, epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EPCAM), and low to moderate levels of albumin (ALB) [16, 18]. Their hepatocytic commitment is determined the upregulated expression of ALB and hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 (HNF4), but lost the expression of KRT19 and EPCAM [16, 18, 19]. Meanwhile, their BECs differentiation is marked by sustained expression of KRT19 and EPCAM along with induction of the specialized ductal epithelial architecture-reflecting tight junction and polarity components, such as zonula occluden-1 (ZO-1), claudin-1 (CLDN1), and apical membrane transporter multidrug-resistance-associated protein 2 (MRP2) [16, 20–22]. The pathological findings in the BA livers, along with biliary engorgement, prominent KRT7-positive reactive ductular cells, and HPCs expressing both BEC markers, including KRT19 and hepatocyte nuclear factor 6 (HNF6), and hepatocyte marker, including HNF4, suggest the incorrect control of intrahepatic bile duct architecture formation [15, 23].

Several growth and transcription factors orchestrally control HPCs to develop the biliary network in the liver bud [16, 24]. HNF6 is an essential transcription factor for the differentiation and formation of biliary trees during embryogenesis [25]. Enhanced expression of hnf-6 derives the short and dilated bile duct formation in zebrafish [26]. In Hnf6-knockout mice, transforming growth factor beta (TGFB) signaling was widely up-regulated in liver parenchyma, resulting in the increased abnormal intrahepatic bile duct formation [27]. TGFB signaling arising from the portal mesenchyme determines the biliary fate and luminal ductular modeling of HPCs under HNF6 control [27, 28]. Collectively, the site-dependent gradient of HNF6-TGFB receptor 2 (TGFBR2) axis is considered to regulate the biliary trail formation. However, the participation of the HNF6–TGFBR2 axis in biliary trail anomaly in the BA liver remains unknown.

Tissue cells often receive DNA damage from extracellular stimuli, such as inflammation. If epigenetic DNA damage is inherited by daughter cells through mitosis [29–31], the damage contributes to developing irreversible degenerative changes in tissues and organs of human fetus and infants. Stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED) are a subpopulation of postnatal mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) derived from the dental pulp tissue of deciduous teeth [32, 33]. SHED also displayed fetal neural crest cell (NCC) characteristics [34, 35]. Recently, BA-specific SHED (BA-SHED) were identified and characterized by a deficiency in bile duct formation and DNA hypomethylation [36]. The dental papilla of human tooth germs, which is the embryonic origin of SHED [37], develop parallel to bile duct network development in human embryos [38, 39]. Given the dental papilla of human tooth germs subsequently continues around the perinatal stage, we hypothesized that BA-SHED are exposed to the BA liver inflammation during the BA development and progress. Thus, this study challenges the explanation of the etiology of BA in the epigenetic memory of BA-SHED.

Methods

Detailed descriptions of characterization of BA-SHED, flow cytometry, quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), protein extraction, immunoblotting, immunoprecipitation, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), colorimetric assay, tissue preparation for immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Human subjects

Human deciduous teeth samples were collected as discarded clinical samples from patients with BA (n = 3, 6–8 years old) and healthy pediatric donors (n = 3, 6–8 years old) due to the eruption of their permanent teeth at the Department of Pediatric Dentistry and Special Needs Dentistry of Kyushu University Hospital.

Antibodies

All antibodies used in this study were commercially available. The details of the antibodies are listed in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Animals

Wild-type C57BL/6J mice (male, 8 weeks old) were obtained from Charles River Laboratory Japan (Yokohama, Japan). The animals were provided with free access to sterile water and standard chow (CRF-1LID10; Oriental Yeast, Tokyo, Japan) at around 23 °C with 40–60% humidity under controlled environmental conditions. Mice were group-housed at a density of 3–5 per cage in standard housing with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at Kyushu University. All experiments have been reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Recombinant human cytokines

We purchased recombinant human tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFA; Catalog # 300–01 A) and interferon gamma (IFNG; Catalog # AF-300-02) from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). The cytokines were diluted and kept according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Primary cell culture

Human dental pulp samples were digested with 0.3% collagenase type I (Worthington Biochemicals, Lakewood, NJ, USA) and 0.4% dispase II (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) for 60 min at 37 °C. The obtained single cells were seeded at 1.0 × 103 cells per flask in T-75 culture flasks (Corning, Corning, NY, USA). Floating cells were removed 3 h after seeding, and adherent cells were maintained for 14–16 days in complete growth medium (CGM) at 37 °C and in 5% CO2. Attached colony-forming cells were passaged at 1.5 × 105 cells per dish on 100-mm culture dishes (Corning). The expanded passage 3 cells were used for subsequent experiments. The CGM consisted of 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Equitech-Bio, Kerrville, TX, USA), 100 µM L-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals), 2 mM L-glutamine (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), and premixed antibiotics comprising 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (premixed P/S; Nacalai Tesque) in minimum essential medium alpha (αMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The CGM was changed twice a week. The characterization of the present isolated SHED was performed according to previous reports, using the colony forming unit fibroblasts (CFU-F) method with toluidine blue staining and flow cytometry with specific antibodies against CD105, CD73, CD90, CD34, CD45, CD14, CD19, and human leukocyte antigen DR (HLA-DR) [40, 41]. The mesenchymal multipotency of SHED were analyzed using RT-qPCR with lineage specific genes, including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARG2) and lipoprotein lipase (LPL) for adipocytes, SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9) and collagen type II alpha 1 chain (COL2A1) for chondrocytes, and RUNX family transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) and bone gamma-carboxyglutamate protein (BGLAP) for osteoblasts [40, 41]. For characterizing the NCC phenotype, SHED were analyzed the levels of neural crest cell surface antigens, including CD49D, CD5, CD271, and stage specific antigen 4 (SSEA4), and the expression of neural crest cell specific genes, including KLF transcriptional 4 (KLF4), MYC proto-oncogene, BHLH transcription factor (MYC), nestin (NES), octamer 4 (OCT4), paired box 3 (PAX3), podocalyxin like (PODXL), Snail family transcriptional repressor 1 (SNAI1), SNAI2, and SOX9, using RT-qPCR [34, 42].

Transcriptional activation of HNF6 in SHED

HNF6 expression was transcriptionally activated in healthy donor-derived control SHED (CTRL-SHED) using a CRISPR-dCas9-based system. Passage 1 SHED were seeded at 2 × 105 cells per dish onto 35-mm dishes (Corning) with antibiotic-free CGM. After 24 h, the CGM was replaced with antibiotic-free CGM supplemented with polybrene (5 µg/mL; Nacalai Tesque). The cells were then infected with commercially available HNF-6 lentiviral activation particles (Catalog # sc-401082-LAC; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) at a 1:1,000 dilution and incubated for 18 h at 37 °C, following the recommended manufacturer’s instructions. The infected cells were then incubated with fresh antibiotic-free CGM for 24 h. To select stably activated clones, the cells were incubated for 24 h in antibiotic-free CGM and cultured in CGM supplemented with puromycin dihydrochloride (2 µg/mL; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), hygromycin B (200 µg/mL; Nacalai Tesque), and blasticidin S HCl (3 µg/mL; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals). The medium was replaced every 3–4 days until resistant colonies were identified. The control cells were established by using commercially available control lentiviral activation particles (Catalog # sc-437282; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at a 1:1,000 dilution instead of the target lentiviral activation particles. Each colony was analyzed for stable HNF6 activation using RT-qPCR. The HNF6 transcriptionally activated and non-activated SHED were referred to as taHNF6-SHED and taCTRL-SHED, respectively.

Gene silencing of HNF6 in BA-SHED

HNF6 expression was functionally knockdown in BA-SHED using the small interfering RNA (siRNA) method. BA-SHED were seeded at 2 × 105 cells per dish onto 35-mm dishes (Corning) with antibiotic-free CGM. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with αMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cells were then transfected with commercially available siRNA for HNF6 (siHNF6; Catalog # sc-61254; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at 50 nM and its scrambled control siRNA (siCTRL; Catalog # sc-37007; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at 50 nM using siRNAMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 24 h, following the recommended manufacturer’s instructions. The efficacy of siRNA was confirmed using RT-qPCR and immunoblotting.

Treatment of SHED with IFNG and TNFA

CTRL-SHED were treated with IFNG (100 ng/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and/or TNFA (100 ng/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in CGM for 24 h. The treated cells were subsequently passaged twice. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)-treated CTRL-SHED were used as controls, MOCK-SHED.

In vivo bile duct induction of SHED

SHED were systemically infused into CCl4 mice to assess their in vivo hepatobiliary potency, as reported previously [36, 43]. C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to four groups, including carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-treated group received with MOCK (PBS) (n = 18 [n = 9 × 2 group]), CTRL-SHED (n = 9), BA-SHED (n = 9), MOCK-SHED (n = 9), and TNFA/IFNG-co-stimulated SHED infusion (n = 9). Each group was housed in separated cages (less than 5 mice/cage). CCl4 solution (1.0 mL/kg/body weight [b.w.]; CCl4:olive oil = 1:4 volume/volume; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals) was intraperitoneally injected into mice twice a week for 8 weeks. SHED (1 × 106 cells in 100 µL of PBS per mouse) or PBS (100 µL per mouse), as MOCK, were intrasplenically infused into 4-week-CCl4-injected mice under a general anesthesia by intraperitoneal injection of medetomidine hydrochloride (0.3 mg/kg/b.w; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals), midazolam (4 mg/kg/b.w.; Sandoz, Tokyo, Japan), and butorphanol tartrate (5 mg/kg/b.w.; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals). Animals were treated with buprenorphine (0.02 mg/kg/b.w.; Otsuka Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan), kept on warm pads, and monitored every hour during recovery after the surgical intervention. According to Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines and the National Institutes of Health guide for the care and use of Laboratory animals, the mice developed poor feeding, limb weakness, emaciation, or convulsions were euthanized and not considered in the study. All mice were maintained under immunosuppressant-free conditions throughout the experiments. Serum samples were harvested form the mice 8 weeks after CCl4 administration under the intraperitoneal anesthesia with medetomidine hydrochloride (0.3 mg/kg/b.w; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals), midazolam (4 mg/kg/b.w.; Sandoz), and butorphanol tartrate (5 mg/kg/b.w.; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals). After serum samples were collected, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and the liver tissues were harvested. Serum was used for ELISA and colorimetric assay for detecting human ALB (hALB) and total bilirubin. Liver tissues were used for immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence for detecting KRT18, KRT19, and EPCAM. The number of KRT19-positive ducts were measured using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For in vivo tracking, SHED were labeled with 10 µg/mL XenoLight DiR NIR fluorescent dye (DiR; Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) or PBS. DiR-labeled or PBS-treated SHED (1 × 106 in 100 µL of PBS per mouse, each) were intrasplenically infused into 4-week-CCl4-injected mice under the intraperitoneal anesthesia with medetomidine hydrochloride (0.3 mg/kg/b.w; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals), midazolam (4 mg/kg/b.w.; Sandoz, Tokyo, Japan), and butorphanol tartrate (5 mg/kg/b.w.; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals). Ventral images of the mice were obtained 5 days after infusion under inhalation anesthesia with isoflurane (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals) using an IVIS Lumina III in vivo imaging system (Perkin Elmer) with the Living Image software (Perkin Elmer).

In vitro bile duct induction assay of SHED

SHED (P3; 2.5 × 105 cells per dish) were seeded onto human-fibronectin-coated 100-mm culture dishes (Corning) and maintained in the CGM until they reached confluence. They were cultured in sequential cytokine-containing hepatobiliary medium based on Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing premixed P/S (Nacalai Tesque), as described previously [44]. For HPC induction, SHED were pre-incubated with human epidermal growth factor (20 ng/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and human fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2; 10 ng/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 days. They were then stimulated with FGF2 (10 ng/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific), hepatocyte growth factor (20 ng/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and nicotinamide (5 mmol/L; Merck) for 7 days and then cultured with oncostatin M (20 ng/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific), dexamethasone (1 µmol/L; Merck), and ITS premix (1:100; Corning) for 21 days. To induce cholangiocyte-like cells (CLCs), the cells were subsequently cultured with oncostatin M (20 ng/mL), dexamethasone (1 µmol/L), ITS premix (1:100 L; Corning), and TGFB1 (10 ng/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 7 days. The medium was replaced twice a week. The number of ducts were measured using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Immunofluorescence was performed for detecting HPC-specific markers, including KRT18, ALB, KRT19, and vimentin (VIM), and cholangiocyte-specific markers, including KRT19, MRP2, ZO-1, and F-actin (F-ACT). The expression of HPC- and specific genes was assessed using RT-qPCR for detecting HNF4 for HPCs and KRT19, MRP2, CLDN1, and tight junction protein 1 (TJP1) for CLCs.

Cell death assay

Cultured SHED (0.1 × 106 cells) were suspended in ice-cold 100 µL of Hanks’ balanced salt solution (Nacalai Tasque), and subsequently stained with FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit with 7AAD (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and analyzed using a FACSVerse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The percentage of positive cells was determined using a FACSuite software (BD Biosciences).

Expression of target genes and proteins

Cellular expression of target genes was analyzed using RT-qPCR. Cellular levels of target proteins were analyzed using immunoblotting and immunofluorescence.

Bisulfite pyrosequencing

DNA methylation analysis of the HNF6 gene promoter in SHED was performed using bisulfite pyrosequencing using a PyroMark Q24 platform (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands). Genomic DNA was extracted from SHED using a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) and subjected to bisulfite conversion using an EpiTect Bisulfite Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA was amplified using PCR with specific primers for the CpG island in target regions of the HNF6 gene promoter (− 174 to 22 bp upstream of the transcription start site; Supplementary Table 5) and HotStarTaq DNA Polymerase (Qiagen) on a T-100 thermal cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratory, Hercules, CA, USA) using the following cycling parameters: 96 °C for 10 min; 45 cycles of 96 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 30 s; 72 °C for 15 min; and then held at 4 °C. Controls for methylated and unmethylated DNA were obtained from an EpiTect PCR Control DNA Set (Qiagen). The PCR products were incubated with Streptavidin Sepharose High-Performance Beads (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and treated with NaOH. Single-stranded DNA templates were reacted with PyroMark Gold-Q24 Reagent kit (Qiagen) and sequencing primers (Supplementary Table 5) on a PyroMark Q24 System sequencer (Qiagen). The methylation level at each CpG site was estimated as the percentage of methylated cytosines in each region and averaged for CpG methylation levels for the entire HNF6 promoter region using the PyroMark Q24 Analysis software (Qiagen). All primers used in this sequencing were designed using the PyroMark Assay Design 2.0 software (Qiagen).

Nuclease-accessible chromatin-qPCR (ANAC-qPCR)

HNF6 gene accessibility was analyzed using an EpiQuik Chromatin Accessibility Assay Kit (Epigentek, Farmingdale, NY, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA was extracted from SHED and treated with or without the nuclease mix (Nase). All DNA samples were amplified using qPCR with HNF6-promoter-specific primers on a LightCycler 96 system real-time PCR machine (Roche Applied Science, Babaria, Germany) (Supplementary Table 6). Fold enrichment (FE) was determined as the ratio of the amplification efficiency of the Nse-treated DNA versus the non-Nse-treated DNA for each group using the following formula: FE = 2(Nse Ct–no−Nse Ct) × 100. For control tests of ANCA-qPCR, non-Nase- and Nase-treated DNA samples were amplified using qPCR with internal negative and positive control primers (Epigentek) instead of HNF6-promoter-specific primers.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-qPCR (ChIP-qPCR)

Protein–DNA interactions were analyzed using ChIP-qPCR. ChIP was performed using an EZ-Magna CHIP A/G kit (Merck), following the manufacturer’s instructions. SHED (1 × 106 cells) were fixed with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min to cross-link the nuclear proteins to DNA. Chromatin was extracted using a nuclear lysis buffer (Merck) and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Merck). The nuclear samples were sonicated using a Bioruptor UCD-300 sonicator (Diagenode, Denville, NJ, USA). The complexes were immunoprecipitated at 4 °C overnight with ChIP-grade target-specific antibodies (Supplementary Table 2) using protein-A-conjugated magnetic beads (Merck) to enrich for the protein–DNA complexes of interest. Mouse IgG or rabbit IgG was used as the negative controls for degerming specific protein-DNA interaction, and anti-RNA polymerase II (POLR2; Merck) was used as the technical control antibody. The complexes were eluted to remove proteins, and the purified DNA was used for qPCR with HNF6 promoter-specific primers using a LightCycler 96 system real-time PCR machine (Roche Applied Science) (Supplementary Table 6). The average Ct values (the number of cycles required for the fluorescent signal to cross the threshold) were obtained from triplicate qPCR experiments and used to evaluate the fold difference between the experimental sample and the normalized input. The ΔCt value was calculated using the following formula: ΔCt [normalized ChIP] = Ct [ChIP] – (Ct [input] – log2 [input dilution factor]). The percentage input (%Input) value for each sample was calculated as follows: %Input = 2(–ΔCt [normalized ChIP]). The internal negative control primers (Epigentek) were used as the negative-region controls for validating specific promoter engagement.

Statistical analysis

All tests were performed at least in triplicate. The results obtained from each group were averaged from at least three replicates. The results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using independent two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Multiple group comparisons were performed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the PRISM 9 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

BA-SHED display bile-duct-forming deficiency

BA-SHED displayed similar characteristics for CFU-F formation and MSC-characteristic immunophenotype, including positive for CD105, CD73, CD90 and negative for CD34, CD45, CD14, CD19, and HLA-DR, as CTRL-SHED, using the CFU-F method and flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 1). BA-SHED also exhibited a developmental signature of NCCs (Supplementary Fig. 2). However, BA-SHED displayed lower levels of CD49D, CD56, CD271, and SSEA4 than CTRL-SHED using flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 2A). BA-SHED also exhibited lower expression of KLF4, MYC, NES, OCT4, PAX3, PODXL, SNAI1, SNAI2, and SOX9 than CTRL-SHED using RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 2B). BA-SHED exhibited reduced in vitro multipotency into adipocytes, chondrocytes, and osteoblasts compared to CTRL-SHED, as detected by the expression of adipocyte- (PPARG2 and LPL), chondrocyte- (SOX9 and COL2A1), and osteoblast- (RUNX2 and BGLAP) specific genes using RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 3A), indicating that BA-SHED are subpopulation of MSC along with fetal NCC characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

In the present study, we performed SHED transplantation into CCl4 mice to assess their in vivo hepatobiliary potency according to our previous reports [36, 43]. BA-SHED display in vivo hepatocyte-like cell (HLC) potency, but not CLC potency [36]. Compared to PBS (MOCK)-infused mice, the present BA-SHED transplanted mice showed the fluorescent intensity in the liver region of CCl4-injected mice, as determined by in vivo imaging (Fig. 1A). To evaluate the homing efficiency and functional hepatic differentiation of transplanted cells, we measured serum hALB levels and assessed the localization of human KRT18-positive cells in the periportal area of recipient livers, as determined by ELISA and immunohistochemistry, respectively (Fig. 1B, C). Compared to the CCl4-alone group, BA-SHED transplantation increased serum hALB levels and the distribution of human KRT18-posiive cells in the recipient liver of CCl4-injected mice. However, BA-SHED transplantation did not attenuate the increased serum levels of direct bilirubin (DBil) in CCl4-injected mice, as determined by colorimetry (Fig. 1B). In the BA-SHED transplanted mice, no human KRT19-positive ducts were found in the periportal area of recipient livers, as determined by immunohistochemistry and quantification of KRT19-positive ducts (Fig. 1C, D). BA-SHED-transplanted recipient livers showed a deficiency in the number of in situ KRT19-positive ducts in the periportal area (Fig. 1D). BA-SHED- and CTRL-SHED-transplanted recipient livers contained HPC marker human EPCAM-positive cells (Fig. 1C). These findings suggested that BA-SHED exhibited in vivo potency of HPCs and HLCs, but did not display in vivo bile duct forming capacity in chronically CCl4-injected mouse livers.

When induced under sequential stimulation with hepatogenic cytokines in vitro [35, 44], BA-SHED and CTRL-SHED displayed HPC-like signature, including KRT18/ALB double positive, KRT19-positive, and vimentin (VIM) negative cells and HNF4 expressing cells, as determined by immunofluorescence and RT-qPCR (Fig. 2A, B). These in vivo and in vitro results indicated the HPC-like cell potency of BA-SHED and CTRL-SHED (BA-HPCs and CTRL-HPCs) in the hepatobiliary development process. TGFB1 signaling plays a crucial role in the development and formation of bile ducts [27]. BA-HPCs and CTRL-HPCs were stimulated with TGFB1 under oncostatin M-, dexamethasone-, and ITS premix-induced conditions and analyzed their potency of bile duct formation. Microscopic analysis showed that TGFB1 stimulation did not accelerate ductular structure modeling in BA-HPCs compared with that in CTRL-HPCs (Fig. 2C, D). TGFB1-stimulated CTRL-HPCs formed ductular structures surrounded by KRT19- and MRP2-positive cells, as determined by immunofluorescence (Fig. 2E). The lumen-surrounding cells showed obvious polarity with the tight junction proteins ZO-1/F-ACT (Fig. 2E). Meanwhile, TGFB1-stimulated BA-HPCs expressed KRT19 and MRP2 but did not polarity with ZO1/F-ACT (Fig. 2E). TGFB1-stimulated BA-HPCs showed reduced expression of KRT19, MRP2, CLDN1, and TJP1 compared to TGFB1-stimulated CTRL-HPCs, as determined by RT-qPCR (Fig. 2F). These findings suggest that BA-SHED and BA-HPCs impair the bile duct forming capacity under TGFB1 stimulation.

Increased HNF6 expression mediates TGFBR2 suppression in BA-SHED

HNF6 controls biliary cell fate and biliary tree formation of HPCs via TGFB1-TGFBR2 signaling [27, 28, 45]. We investigated the TGFB1 sensitivity and biliary commitment of BA-HPCs. BA-HPCs showed suppressed TGFBR2 and enhanced HNF6 expression compared to CTRL-HPCs (Fig. 3A). Undifferentiated BA-SHED behaved similarly in TGFBR2 and HNF6 expression to BA-HPCs (Fig. 3B). Immunoblotting and immunofluorescence analyses of TGFBR2 and nuclear HNF6 supported the gene expression results for BA-SHED and CTRL-SHED (Fig. 3C–F). To understand the regulation of the HNF6 promoter by the upstream transcription factors, we performed RT-qPCR for analyzing genes for upstream regulators to activate the HNF6 promoter in hepatic lineage differentiation, including CCAAT enhancer binding protein alpha (CEBPA), CEBPB, hematopoietically expressed homeobox (HHEX), and T-box transcription factor 3 (TBX3) [46–49]. CEBPA, CEBPB, HHEX, and TBX3 showed no significant difference between BA-HPCs and BA-SHED or CTRL-HPCs and CTRL-SHED (Supplementary Fig. 4). Therefore, we hypothesized that the hyper-enhanced HNF6 promoter is directly activated by an epigenetic modification primed independently of the hepatic lineage pathway. We then established CTRL-SHED with transcriptionally activated HNF6 using lentivirus infection, along with its control, taHNF6-SHED and taCTRL-SHED, respectively (Fig. 3G–I). taHNF6-SHED exhibited the downregulation of TGFBR2 gene and protein levels compared to taCTRL-SHED (Fig. 3J–L). Next, we treated BA-SHED using siHNF6 and siCTRL. siHNF6-terated BA-SHED expressed reduced HNF6 gene and protein levels compared to the siCTRL-treated BA-SHED using RT-qPCR and immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). siHNF6-terated BA-SHED exhibited the decreased levels of TGFBR2 gene and protein compared to the siCTRL-treated BA-SHED (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D), indicating that HNF6 control the suppression of TGFBR2 levels in BA-SHED. These findings suggest that abnormally increased HNF6 levels may be epigenetically controlled in BA-SHED.

Tumor necrosis factor alpha and interferon gamma co-stimulation causes biliary deficiency along with increasing HNF6 levels in SHED

Previous studies have considered the involvement of the innate and adaptive immune microenvironments in the epigenetic pathogenesis of BA [3, 8]. In this study, CTRL-SHED were stimulated with 10 or 100 ng/mL TNFA and/or 10 or 100 ng/mL IFNG for 24 h. SHED-co-stimulated with TNFA/IFNG at 100 ng/mL (TI-SHED) displayed increased HNF6 gene and protein expression compared to PBS-treated CTRL-SHED (MOCK-SHED); however, TNFA or IFNG stimulation alone did not (Fig. 4A–C). CTRL-SHED co-stimulated with TNFA and IFNG (TNFA/IFNG) at 10 ng/mL (ti-SHED) showed no significant HNF6 expression compared to MOCK-SHED (Fig. 4A). When TI-SHED were passed, the inherited passage 1 and 2 TI-SHED, TI-SHED-P1 and TI-SHED-P2, sustained the increased HNF6 expression (Fig. 4D). The nuclear HNF6 levels were enhanced in TI-SHED and TI-SHED-P1 compared to those in MOCK-SHED (Fig. 4E, F). TI-SHED displayed decreased TGFBR2 gene and protein expression compared to MOCK-SHED (Fig. 4G–I).

Next, we examined the in vivo and in vitro bile duct formation capabilities of TI-SHED compared to MOCK-SHED. When undifferentiated TI-SHED were infused into CCl4-injected mice, TI-SHED showed in situ homing and addiction of donor-derived HLCs, as indicated by the epi-detection of cell-labeled fluorescence intensity in the recipient liver region and immunological detection of ALB in the recipient serum (Fig. 5A, B). The induced TI-SHED did not form typical ductular structures with ZO1/F-ACT polarity, even though they expressed KRT19 and MRP2, as shown using immunofluorescence (Fig. 5D, E). TNFA/IFNG-co-stimulation contributes to Brahma-mediated chromatin architecture remodeling around the HNF6 promoter in BA-SHED

To investigate the epigenetic modification of enhanced HNF6 expression in BA-SHED, we examined chromatin architecture remodeling at the HNF6 promoter locus (-1200 ~ + 1200) in BA-SHED and TI-SHED compared to that in CTRL-SHED and MOCK-SHED using ANAC-qPCR with 20 paired primers (Supplementary Fig. 6). BA-SHED and TI-SHED showed large fold enrichment scores (chromatin-opened state) for the HNF6 promoter with primers 2–5, 8, and 10 and with primers 1–5 and 7–11, respectively. In contrast, CTRL-SHED and MOCK-SHED showed small fold enrichment scores (chromatin-closed state) (Fig. 6A). Control tests for ANAC-qPCR showed no or significant enrichment scores in BA-SHED and TI-SHED and their control SHED (CTRL-SHED and MOCK-SHED) using internal negative or positive control primers, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7). POLR2 increasingly accumulated at the transcription start point (primers 10 and 11), as determined by ChIP-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 8). However, no significant DNA methylation status nor histone modification marks, including tri-methyl H3K4 (H3K4me3) and acetyl H3K27 (H3K27ac), were found at the chromatin remodeling sites of the HNF6 promoter in BA-SHED, as determined by bisulfite pyrosequencing and ChIP-qPCR (Supplementary Figs. 9, 10).

Brahma (BRM) and Brahma-related gene 1 (BRG1) enhance the transcriptional activity of their target genes due to the catalytic activity of the SWI–SNF complex [50, 51]. BA-SHED and TI-SHED show similar nuclear levels of BRM and BRG1 to those of CTRL-SHED and MOCK-SHED (Fig. 6B–D; Supplementary Figs. 11 A–C). The accumulation of BRM in the HNF6 promoter was significantly increased at the sites targeted by primers 3, 4, 10, and 11 in BA-SHED and by primers 5 and 10 in TI-SHED, as determined by ChIP-qPCR (Fig. 6E). However, no increased accumulation of BRG1 was found in the HNF6 promoter of BA-SHED or TI-SHED compared to that of CTRL-SHED and MOCK-SHED (Supplementary Fig. 11D). These findings indicate that BA-SHED and TI-SHED shared the BRM-accumulated sites of the chromatin remodeled HNF6 promoter.

Nuclear factor kappa B regulates the epigenetic machinery of the HNF6 promoter in BA-SHED

TNFA-stimulated nuclear factor kappa B (NFKB), including P65 and P50 subunits, participates in the epigenetic machinery of oral squamous cell carcinoma cells [52]. BA-SHED showed similar levels of phosphorylated P65, P65, and P105/P50, and nuclear and cytosolic P65 and P50 to CTRL-SHED, as determined by immunoblotting (Supplementary Figs. 13 A, B). Immunoprecipitation-immunoblotting and immunofluorescence showed that BA-SHED and CTRL-SHED displayed nuclear P65 and P50 complexes (Fig. 7A, Supplementary Fig. 13C). Increased accumulation of P65 and P50 in the HNF6 promoter of BA-SHED was found at the sites targeted by primers 4, 6, 10, and 11 sites and those targeted by primers 3–5 sites, respectively, compared to that of CTRL-SHED, as determined by ChIP-qPCR (Fig. 7B). BA-SHED and CTRL-SHED showed nuclear colocalization of BRM or BRG1 with P65 and P50, as determined by immunofluorescence (Fig. 7C, Supplementary Fig. 14A). BRM and P65 (BRM–P65) complexes significantly accumulated at the sites targeted by primers 3, 6, 10, and 11, whereas BRM–P50 complexes were only observed at the primer 4 site, around the HNF6 promoter of BA-SHED compared to that of CTRL-SHED, as determined by ChIP-re-ChIP-qPCR (Fig. 7D). The BRG1–P65 and BRG1–P50 complexes did not bind to the HNF6 promoter (Supplementary Fig. 14B). Negative control tests for P65, P50, BRM-P65/P50, and BRG1-P65/P50 accumulation around the HNF6 promoter in BA-SHED using quantitative ChIP- and ChIP-reChIP-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 15, 16). These findings indicate that the BRM–P65 complex participates in the epigenetic machinery involved in chromatin architecture remodeling in BA-SHED.

Discussion

We first discovered the sustained abnormal hyper-activation of HNF6 causes bile duct deformation due to the suppression of TGFBR2 under TGFB1 stimulation using BA-SHED (Fig. 8). HNF6 levels determine the cell fate of HPCs into BECs by TGFB signaling during liver development [27, 53]. In the present study, transcriptionally activation of HNF6 suppressed TGFBR2 in SHED, meanwhile, HNF6 silencing ameliorated TGFBR2 expression in BA-SHED. HNF6 suppresses Forkhead box protein A1 (FOXA1) by inhibiting TGFB signaling via suppressed TGFBR2 in mouse liver development [28], and deletion of Foxa1 results in biliary deficiency during murine development [54]. Interestingly, taHNF6-SHED exhibited cell death after cholangiogenic induction. Consistent with our findings, the mouse insulin I promoter-enhanced human HNF6 knock-in transgenic mice display specific severe defects of pancreatic beta-cells by programmed cell death process [55]. Moreover, in cholangiocarcinoma cells, HNF6 overexpression suppresses in vitro and in vivo cell proliferation by blocking cyclin G1 and insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor via miR-122 action [56]. These findings suggest that HNF6 governs HPC fate of ductular formation and cell survival via TGFB signaling. Further studies will be necessary to elucidate the participants of the HNF6 in biliary development and bile duct deformation in BA.

We demonstrated the hyperexpression of HNF6 by chromatin architecture remodeling in the HNF6 promoter region, associated with BRM accumulation at the remodeling sites of the HNF6 promoter region compared to BRG1 in TI-SHED. BRG1 and BRM contribute to the development of liver diseases caused by proinflammatory cytokines such as TNFA [57, 58]. Meanwhile, BRG1 and BRM antagonistically regulate cell differentiation depending on their transcriptional specificity [59, 60]. TNFA/IFNG co-stimulation synergistically regulates gene transcriptional activity due to the dominance of BRM and BRG1 in the SWI–SNF complex [61]. These findings suggest that TNFA/IFNG signaling primes the transcriptional activity of the HNF6 promoter, accompanied by chromatin architecture remodeling and BRM accumulation (Fig. 8A).

TNFA stimulation recruits P65 to activate POL2 by forming a complex with its coactivators, such as CREB-binding protein (CBP) and P300, inducing gene expression [62, 63]. P65-recruited BRM is associated with POLR2, subsequently activating target gene promoters [64, 65]. In colonic cell lines and primary human organoids, IFNG recruited BRM allows P65 binding on the BCLG promoter for driving BCLG expression through the synergy between TNFA and IFNG, thereby causing inflammation-associated gut damage [66]. BRM is recruited to the mTOR promoter in a dose-dependent manner of leucine in mouse mammary epithelial cells [67]. In the present study, the TNFA and IFNG co-stimulation induced HNF6 overexpression in CTRL-SHED in a dose-dependent manner, but TNFA or IFNG stimulation alone failed. Therefore, we propose that TNFA and IFNG co-stimuli transduce P65/P50 complex and recruit BRM to establish the chromatin access to both the Rel homology and transactivation domains of the HNF6 promoter in BA-SHED, thereby enhancing HNF6 mRNA synthesis by POLR2 associated with BRM–P65/P50-complex (Fig. 8A).

Generally, cells can input environmental conditions through mitosis along with alterations in gene expression [68–70]. During cellular epigenetic memory, essential chromatin remodeling allows binding-poised POLR2 alter gene expression [71]. TNFA and IFNG signaling epigenetically regulate DNA methylation and histone modification, remodeling chromatin architecture to imprint long-term memory [69, 72]. The present BA-SHED exhibited a similar chromatin architecture remodeling and BRM–P65/P50 complex accumulation at the POLR2, P65, and P50 binding sites in the HNF6 promoter to TI-SHED. However, BA-SHED displayed no significant epigenetic marks, including DNA methylation and histone modification, around the promoter. P65 recruits BRM to the target DNA promoter, meanwhile, BRM tunes the stabilization of NFKB binding to the target DNA promoter [57, 65, 73, 74]. In the present generated taHNF6-SHED, HNF6 expression is artificially maintained through the sustainable activation of P65 to recruit to the target sites within 200 bp upstream of the transcription starting site (TSS) (+ 1) of the HNF6 promoter [75]. These findings suggest that BRM–P65/P50 is recruited to HNF6 promoter and enhance transcriptional activity independently of epigenetic marks. To maintain long-term epigenetic memory across cell divisions, cis- and/or trans-feedback loops allow the preservation of epigenetic information [76]. Trans-feedback loops preserve epigenetic information via diffusible factors, such as proteins or RNA, independently of epigenetic marks. Thus, we hypothesize that an auto-sustained positive feedback loop of BRM–P65/P50 complex may maintain the transcriptional activity of the HNF6 promoter in BA-SHED in the absence of TNFA/IFNG stimulation (Fig. 8A). Since the activity of distal enhancer is also regulated by DNA methylation and histone modification, such as H2AK9ac, H3K27ac, and H3K4me2 [77, 78], it is possible that the chromatin architecture of the HNF6 promoter in BA-SHED is indirectly regulated by epigenetic modification at distal regulatory regions. In TNF-dependent monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 expression, P65-binding on the distal enhancer of the gene triggers the recruitment of CBP and P300, thereby altering the chromatin architecture of the proximal region to activate the general transcription machinery [79]. Further epigenetic studies in the enhancer regions of HNF6 may help to elucidate the inheritance mechanism in BA-SHED.

Previous inflammation experience is permanently imprinted on the neighboring organ/tissue-specific stem cells, including hematopoietic, intestinal, and skin epithelial stem cells, through epigenetic modifications, including DNA and histone methylation, and chromatin accessibility [80–82]. Cytomegalovirus infection around the perinatal stages is a causal factor of BA [83] and is highly associated with elevated levels of TNFA and IFNG in liver tissues of patients with BA [84]. Acute and acute on chronic liver inflammation release multiple cytokines, such as TNFA and IFNG, to blood circulation, thereby causing systemic inflammation in multiple organs/tissues [85]. Thus, BA-SHED may have experienced the BA-inducible liver inflammatory exposure around the perinatal stage, maintaining the inflammation experience of BA etiology (Fig. 8).

Conclusions

In summary, we first demonstrated that BA-SHED maintain the transcriptional activation of the HNF6 promoter associated with the chromatin remodeling via BRM-P65 complex under severe TNFA and IFNG and lack biliary potency via TGFBR2 under TGFB1 stimulation. These results suggest that BA-SHED are a potential modality for expanding our knowledge and innovating for future therapeutic targets in BA research. Thus, this study may propose a model to understand the mechanism of congenital anomalies with the help of the disease-specific SHED.
