Competing structural orders frustrate crystallization in a metallic glass
Authors: Xin Zhang, Fujun Lan, Hongbo Lou, Yuxin Liu, Guangrun Zhong, Saichao Cao, Ke Yang, Zhidan Zeng, Hongwei Sheng, Qiaoshi Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Structural frustration is believed to play a key role in suppressing crystallization and promoting glass formation, yet experimentally quantifying and understanding the structural frustration in glasses remains highly challenging. Here, we systematically investigate the structural evolution of a prototypical metallic glass-former, Cu46Zr42Al7Y5, during its glass transition and crystallization upon heating, using in-situ high-temperature high-energy synchrotron x-ray diffraction in combination with conventional and ultrafast differential scanning calorimetry. By probing the kinetic interplay between glass transition and crystallization over a broad range of heating rates, we identify a heating-rate-dependent crossover in the nature of the first exothermic process: at high rates it is associated with conventional-like crystallization, whereas at low rates it corresponds to a polyamorphic-like amorphous-amorphous transition. Quantitative analysis of the evolving medium- and short-range order reveals that these structural evolution pathways are governed by the competition between locally favored ordering and extended crystal-like ordering, providing direct experimental evidence that such structural competition can frustrate crystallization and stabilize the glassy state. These findings provide fundamental insights into the intricate relationship between structural frustration and structural stability in metallic glasses.

Introduction

One of the central challenges in glass science is to understand how supercooled liquids avoid crystallization and develop dramatically slowed dynamics while exhibiting only subtle changes in average structure at the atomic level through glass transitions1,2. In classical thermodynamic phase transitions, critical slowing down in dynamics is typically associated with the divergence of a characteristic correlation length scale3. Extensive theoretical efforts have also been made to identify relevant structural length scales and order parameters, yet direct experimental evidence connecting such structural correlations to vitrification and devitrification in glass-forming systems remains limited4,5.

It has been well established that the seemingly “disordered” glass structure actually contains intricate local order, reflected by statistically significant deviations from random atomic arrangements at both short-range (nearest-neighbor) or medium-range ( < 1–2 nm) length scales6–8. Structural frustration has been widely proposed as a key concept for understanding how local order governs the glass transition and inhibits crystallization9–13. The frustration, arising from the competition among incompatible structural motifs, promotes slow dynamics and ultimately leads to dynamic arrest during cooling, thereby stabilizing the glassy state14–16. While alternative dynamic frameworks, such as mode-coupling theory, have been proposed to describe the slowdown of relaxation in supercooled liquids, the frustration perspective emphasizes the structural origin of vitrification12,17,18. Several frustration-based models typically invoke interactions among higher-order local structural (many-body correlations) parameters, such as bond-angle distributions, bond-orientational order parameters, and polyhedral connectivity. These higher-order parameters are often used in simulations to identify locally favored structures and quantify their competition with crystal-like ordering19. However, conventional scattering experiments primarily probe averaged two-body correlations, making it challenging to capture the structural correlations often revealed in computer simulations.

Metallic glasses (MGs) provide an ideal platform to investigate these issues, owing to their relatively simple (low-directional) atomic structures, well-characterized glass-forming ability, wide compositional tunability, and rich devitrification pathways. In this work, using MGs as a model atomic glass system, we investigate how competing structural orders influence devitrification pathways under controlled heating. Using in-situ high-energy synchrotron X-ray diffraction (XRD) and both conventional and ultrafast differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), we resolve the evolution of short-range order (SRO) and medium-range order (MRO) across the glass-transition and the first exothermic event over a broad range of heating rates. This approach allows us to reveal a distinct structural pre-ordering in the glass state and a heating-rate dependent crossover between crystalline and polyamorphic ordering, associated with the kinetic interplay between glass transition and crystallization. Via quantitative analysis of changes in SRO and MRO, we establish a concrete experimental link between competing local orders and pathway selection, providing direct evidence that structural competition and frustration can play a critical role in governing devitrification behavior.

Results

A prototype bulk MG, Cu46Zr42Al7Y5, was prepared by melt-spinning. Cu-Zr-based MGs, especially those with Al or other minor additions, exhibit excellent glass-forming ability and rich crystallization pathways, making them among the most extensively studied MG systems to date. The specific composition used in this work lies within a compositional regime where two competing crystalline phases, Cu10Zr7 and CuZr2, are known to coexist20–23. This inherent structural competition makes it an ideal platform for probing structural frustration-driven behaviors in glasses. Thermodynamic behavior was characterized using both a conventional DSC (DSC 8500, Perkin Elmer) and a Flash DSC (Flash DSC 2 + , Mettler Toledo), covering regular and extremely high heating rate ranges, respectively. In-situ high-temperature synchrotron high-energy XRD with a heating rate of 0.167 K/s was conducted at beamline 11-IDC of the Advanced Photon Source (APS), Argonne National Laboratory (ANL), using an x-ray beam with a wavelength of 0.1174 Å. Additional in-situ high-temperature experiments with different heating rates (0.333, 0.5, and 0.667 K/s) were carried out at beamline BL12SW of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF), with an x-ray wavelength of 0.1259 Å. One-dimensional (1D) diffraction patterns I(q) were integrated using the Fit2D software24. The total scattering factor, S(q) (q = 4πsinθ/λ, λ is the x-ray wavelength, and 2θ is the angle between the incident and diffraction x-ray beams), and the reduced pair distribution functions, G(r), were derived from is the X-ray wavelength, and 2I(q) using the PDFgetX3 software package25. More experimental details are provided in Methods.

MGs are thermodynamically metastable, so thermal activation during heating generally induces structural relaxation and may trigger more complex structural transformations. The resulting thermal and structural signatures offer valuable insights into the microscopic mechanisms governing glass stability and crystallization. To facilitate further discussion, we refer to these two different ordering processes revealed by XRD separated by T1 as “pre-ordering” and “ordering”. In a conventional picture, amorphous structural ordering involves reconstruction and rearrangement of SRO and MRO, typically manifested as an increase in density (reflected by a quick increase of qp) and the release of latent heat (exothermic signals in DSC curves)30,31. The absence of any detectable transition signal in both qp and DSC curves indicates that the pre-ordering revealed by XRD in this work is fundamentally different from the conventional amorphous structural ordering associated with relaxation below Tg.

By extrapolating the high-heating-rate data in Fig. 2b, the peak temperature Tp1 at a low heating rate of 0.167 K/s is estimated to be ~640 K. This value surprisingly aligns well with the temperature range associated with the pre-ordering process (between T0 and T1) revealed by XRD in Fig. 3a. This coincidence suggests the structural evolution detected as pre-ordering at low heating rates could be closely related to the process that manifests as the first exothermic peak at high heating rates.

To further examine this correlation, additional DSC analyses were performed. Figure 3b presents the Kissinger plot for Tg_end and Tex_start (as defined in Fig. 2b), illustrating the relationship between the first exothermic peak and the glass transition as a function of heating rates (see Supplementary Table. 1 for the fitting results). At relatively high heating rates, Tg_end lies below Tex_start, as typically expected. However, due to the marked difference in activation energies (reflected by the distinct slopes of the fitted lines in Fig. 3b) between these two events, Tex_start quickly falls below Tg_end at heating rates below ~50 K/s.

Surprisingly, the extrapolated low-heating-rate values of Tex_start and Tg_end derived from DSC measurements converge with the temperatures T0 (pre-ordering) and T1 (ordering) independently determined from XRD at heating rates of 0.167, 0.333, 0.5, and 0.667 K/s (More details on the determination of T0 and T1 in XRD data are provided in Fig. S10). This convergence establishes a direct link between the calorimetric signatures and the structural evolution detected by XRD. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 3c, the so-called “pre-ordering” does not represent an independent structural ordering process that exists across all heating rates studied in this work. Instead, it only exists at relatively low heating rates. Upon reducing the heating rate, a kinetic crossing occurs between the Tex_start and Tg_end, causing crystallization-related structural driving forces to be activated while the system remains under strong glassy kinetic constraints (Tex_start in DSC is manifested as T0 in XRD, when Tex_start < Tg_end), which typically promotes the formation of locally favored glassy order. Under such “constrained” conditions, atomic-scale cooperative motion and long-range diffusion are not yet fully activated, preventing crystallization from proceeding via long-range rearrangement. Consequently, the system can respond only through local structural adjustments, which manifest in XRD as weak ordering features (pre-ordering) in the temperature interval between T0 and T1. This interpretation is supported by the evolution of G(r) shown in Fig. S9: at T0, SRO begins to change significantly in the first peak; in contrast, a clear extension of MRO is only observed above T1. This observation aligns well with our hypothesis that during the pre-ordering stage, the spatial extension of “crystal-like” local order is restricted and cannot readily grow to extended length scales.

With this kinetic framework, our experimental results further demonstrate that the “constrained” pre-ordering occurring in the glassy state can impede or redirect subsequent crystallization pathways (Fig. 2), causing a kinetic crossover from conventional-like crystallization at high heating rates to an abnormal amorphous-to amorphous transition at low heating rates at Tp1. Very recently, Liu et al32 studied the atomic structure evolution related to the first exothermic event (Tp1) at low heating rates of Cu-Zr-based MGs using high-resolution electron microscopy. They revealed that Tp1 is related to the formation of metastable amorphous structures composed of four-membered Frank-Kasper (FK) ring networks. Although these FK-type units resemble the local motifs in crystalline Cu–Zr intermetallic phases, their aperiodic assembly disrupts the establishment of long-range translational symmetry and could promote crystallization frustration. Therefore, these results support our kinetic constrained scenario. In contrast, at high heating rates, the kinetic constrained effect is diminished once T0 moves above Tg_end and merges into Tex_start, consequently, crystalline precipitation is clearly observed after heated to Tp1, with diffraction features mostly consistent with the Cu10Zr7 intermetallic compound phase23 (see Supplementary Fig. 11 for details of Rietveld refinement).

Recent simulation studies on non-classical crystallization pathways show that crystallization in MGs often initiates within regions with pre-existing local structural order, and the nature of these local motifs plays a decisive role in determining subsequent crystal growth33–37. To further examine how pre-ordering influences the development of crystalline order in this MG, we analyzed the evolution of MRO and beyond throughout the entire heating process at the heating rate of 0.167 K/s. Is pre-ordering an emergent property of the kinetic interaction between glassy and crystalline configurations near the glass transition, or does it merely reflect monotonic suppression of crystallization? Regarding these interesting questions, competing hypotheses offer distinct perspectives. Some models suggest continuous structural rearrangement as the system cools down towards the glass transition. In contrast, others posit independent transitions governed by distinct short-range ordering mechanisms like the two-order-parameter model proposed by Tanaka and co-workers42–44. To test these competing hypotheses, we further examined the evolution of SRO throughout the heating process (see Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14 for details of the SRO fitting). In reciprocal space, the second peak of the structure factor S(q) is widely regarded as a signature of SRO45,46. Figure 4b presents the temperature-dependent evolution of the intensity of the second peak and its shoulder, S(q21) and S(q22), in S(q) (see Supplementary Fig. 13 for fitting details). Traditionally, the intensities of the second peak and its shoulder are interpreted as sensitive indicators of SRO degree. As shown in Fig. 4b, the intensity of the second peak increases with temperature after T1, whereas its shoulder peak experiences an accelerated decline after T0.

These opposite trends suggest the coexistence of competing topological motifs in SRO above T0 as well, once pre-ordering sets in, which inevitably contribute to the competing behavior beyond SRO, extended into MRO (Fig. 4a). This competition is expected to significantly influence the evolution of crystalline order and the subsequent growth of crystals (see Supplemental Information for more details of the discussion on the competition). The contrasting evolution of these structural features manifests the intrinsic competition characteristics of structural frustration, consistent with the two-order-parameter framework of glass formation43,44. Rather than being trapped in a single metastable state that undergoes two successive ordering processes, the system experiences two distinct structural transitions at their respective characteristic temperatures. Their overlap induces mutual coupling and gives rise to frustration.

It should be noted that while in-situ synchrotron XRD measurements provide valuable insights into the average structural evolution, the interpretation of 1D PDF data is inherently limited, as multiple distinct microscopic scenarios could potentially yield similar changes in two-body correlation functions. Although our multi-scale kinetic results are internally consistent with the proposed structural competition, future studies employing higher-order structural probes or advanced atomistic simulations would be beneficial to uniquely resolve the complex three-dimensional atomic rearrangements during such non-monotonic evolutions.

In summary, by combining calorimetry over an extended range of heating rates with in-situ synchrotron high-energy XRD, we have experimentally explored the role of structural frustration in governing crystallization behavior in a prototype Cu46Zr42Al7Y5 MG. We uncover a heating-rate-dependent crossover between crystallization-dominated and amorphous-amorphous ordering pathways in the Cu46Zr42Al7Y5 MG. The emergence of unusual pre-ordering in the glass state is revealed to be a consequence of a kinetic interplay of the crystallization and glass transition processes with mismatched activation energies. The imprint of pre-ordering on medium-range correlations and its link with anomalous kinetic behavior consistently suggest that the crystallization pathway can be frustrated by competing structural motifs. Our findings experimentally highlight how structural frustration affects the thermodynamic and kinetic pathways of glass devitrification. Effectively controlling competing structural orders by different thermal treatment over a wide range of heating rates could thus open new avenues for tuning the stability, mechanical properties, and thermal behavior of MGs. Beyond such targeted applications, the mechanisms involving competing structural orders may also underlie the unusual thermal and structural evolution observed during prolonged annealing near Tg (akin to infinitely low rate heating)47,48, it could be even possibly relevant to interpreting the formation of paracrystalline49,50 or nanocrystalline phases51,52.

Method

Sample preparation and characterization

Master ingots with a nominal composition of Cu46Zr42Al7Y5 (at%) were prepared by arc-melting of pure elements under a high-purity argon atmosphere using a Ti ingot as an oxygen getter. The master ingots were re-melted at least four times to ensure chemical homogeneity. Amorphous ribbons were prepared by single-roller melt-spinning under an argon atmosphere. The circumferential velocity of the copper roller is ~40 m/s. The ribbons were ~2 mm in width and ~20 μm in thickness. Their amorphous nature was verified by an in-house x-ray diffractometer (Panalytical-Empyrean, Cu-Ka radiation) and a conventional differential scanning calorimeter (DSC 8500, Perkin Elmer). Flash DSC measurements were carried out by a chip-based commercial instrument (Mettler Toledo Flash DSC 2 + ). The as-prepared and recovered samples were thinned by an Ar ion milling system (Gatan-697) at the liquid-nitrogen temperature for TEM experiments, which were carried out using a Tecnai F20 (FEI) electron microscope operating at 200 kV.

The annealed samples were prepared by heating to the target temperatures at different heating rates (conventional DSC and Flash DSC for heating rates below 6 K/s and above 50 K/s, respectively), followed by rapid quenching ( ~ 5 K/s in conventional DSC and 1000 K/s in Flash DSC) to room temperature. The ex-situ synchrotron XRD was conducted on annealed samples at the beamline BL15U1 of Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF), China. The wavelength of the x-ray beam was 0.6199 Å.

In-situ high-temperature high-energy synchrotron XRD

The evolution of the averaged atomic structure of the samples was monitored by in-situ synchrotron high-energy XRD conducted at the beamline 11-ID-C of Advanced Photon Source (APS), Argonne National Laboratory (ANL), USA. The wavelength of the X-ray beam was 0.1174 Å. The beam size was 0.2 mm × 0.2 mm, defined by slits. Ten layers of ribbon samples were folded up to increase the sample thickness for an enhanced scattering signal-to-noise ratio. Two-dimensional (2D) XRD images were collected by a Perkin Elmer α-Si pixel array detector. A computer-controlled Linkam THM-S600 heating stage was used to vary the temperatures of samples between 289 K and 753 K with a heating rate of 10 K/min. A flowing high-purity argon atmosphere was used to protect samples from oxidation at high temperatures. The exposure time for each pattern was 1.5 s, and each pattern was accumulated ten times.

1D diffraction patterns I(q) were integrated using the Fit2D software24.
