Plasmodium falciparum antimalarial resistance in Angola: a systematic review
Authors: Daniela Matias, Celso Chaves, Ana Micolo Cândido, Joana Morais, Maria Lina Antunes, Jennifer Guler, Fátima Nogueira, Acácio Salamandane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria remains a major health challenge in Angola, where Plasmodium falciparum accounts for most cases and deaths. Although artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) have been the recommended first-line treatments in the country since 2006, the emergence of resistance-associated mutations in Africa raises concerns. We conducted a systematic review of molecular markers of antimalarial drug resistance in Angola (2000–2024) to map their prevalence and distribution at the provincial level. Following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we searched PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science for studies on P. falciparum resistance markers: polymorphisms in pfcrt, pfdhfr, pfdhps, pfmdr1, pfk13, or copy number variation (CNV) in pfmdr1 and pfpm2/3. Data on mutations, prevalence, and geographic distribution was extracted and summarized. 20 studies met our inclusion criteria. The chloroquine-resistant pfcrt CVIET haplotype persisted in northern and coastal provinces, while the SP-resistant pfdhfr/pfdhps IRNGE haplotype was detected in Cabinda and Zaire. No validated pfk13 mutations linked to artemisinin resistance were reported. Pfmdr1 analyses showed declining 86Y and 1246Y mutation frequencies, with wild-type NYD and NFD haplotypes predominating. Low pfmdr1 and pfpm2 CNV prevalence was reported, though recent data indicate emerging amplification in southern Angola. Research was concentrated in Benguela, Luanda, Lunda Sul, and Zaire, leaving large geographic gaps country-wise. Resistance markers in Angola show heterogeneous provincial distribution. Expanded molecular surveillance, particularly in underrepresented provinces and among pregnant women, is critical to safeguard ACT efficacy and guide national malaria control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05939-4.Keywords: Plasmodium falciparum, Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), Angola, Molecular markers, Antimalarial drug resistance

Introduction

Angola, a country in sub-Saharan Africa, accounted for 3.1% of global malaria cases and 2.7% of malaria deaths in 2024 [1]. It is estimated that malaria is responsible for 42% of the deaths in the country [2]. Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) were introduced as treatment for uncomplicated malaria in 2006 [3]. The ACTs currently recommended by the Angolan Ministry of Health are artemether-lumefantrine (AL), artesunate-amodiaquine (ASAQ) and dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine (DHA-PPQ) [4]. Recent reports have outlined the independent emergence of ACT-resistant parasites in sub-Saharan Africa, the region which bears the heaviest P. falciparum burden globally [5–7].

Angola is located on the western coast of sub-Saharan Africa, with three main malaria transmission zones: hyperendemic transmission in the northern/northeastern provinces, stable mesoendemic in the coastal and central provinces and unstable (seasonal) mesoendemic in the south (Fig. 1). There is, however, significant variation in prevalence within each province [8]. Therapeutic efficacy studies in Angola have raised concerns regarding the efficacy of AL in the country: 28-day-PCR corrected efficacies below the 90% threshold set by the WHO have been found in Zaire province in 2013 and 2015 [3, 9], as well as in Lunda Sul in 2019 [10]. ASAQ and DHA-PPQ remain highly effective in the tested Angolan provinces [3, 4, 9, 10].

Given increasing evidence of reduced ACT susceptibility in sub-Saharan Africa [12] and declining AL efficacy in Angola [3, 9], molecular surveillance is essential to detect emerging resistance, track resistance-associated alleles, and guide policies to delay ACT efficacy loss. There are several mutations in P. falciparum genes associated with ACT susceptibility, such as the P. falciparum chloroquine resistance transporter (pfcrt), dihydrofolate reductase (pfdhfr), dihydropteroate synthase (pfdhps), multidrug drug resistance gene 1 (pfmdr1) and Kelch13 protein coding gene (pfk13).

Mutations in pfcrt were identified as the main cause of chloroquine resistance (CQR) [13]. The pfcrt CVIET haplotype (residues 72–76), predominantly found in Asia and Africa, is linked to moderate AQ resistance, while the SVMNT haplotype, which is more frequent in South America, is associated with higher levels of AQ resistance [14–16]. In contrast, lumefantrine (LUM), the partner drug in AL, selects for the wild-type pfcrt CVMNK [17]. Pfcrt mutations have also been shown to cause in vitro PPQ resistance: T93S, H97Y, C101F, F145I, M343L, C350R and G353V [18]. Particularly, F145I was associated with treatment failures and found to be sufficient for PPQ resistance in the absence of pfpm2/3 amplification [19–21].

Resistance to pyrimethamine has been associated with mutations in pfdhfr: N51I, C59R, S108N and I164L [22–24] and resistance to sulfadoxine associated with mutations in pfdhps, particularly S436A, A437G, K540E, and A581G [25–27]. The quintuple mutant IRNGE (pfdhfr 51I, 59R, 108 N + pfdhps 437G, 540E) confers a high level of clinical resistance to sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) and is a strong indicator of SP treatment failure [28, 29]. Given that SP is currently used for intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp-SP) in Angola, surveillance of mutations in these two genes is particularly critical [8].

In pfk13, multiple SNPs are linked to clinical artemisinin resistance (ART-R) in Southeast Asia [5, 6, 30–36], some of which are emerging and spreading in Africa [6]. The clonal expansion of pfk13 R561H and R622I (implicated in ART-R) has been recorded in Ethiopia and Rwanda, respectively [5, 37]. The pfk13 SNP R561H is under special monitoring, because of its rise in frequency in Africa [5].

Pfmdr1 plays a critical role in modulating susceptibility to various antimalarials, including CQ, LUM, AQ, and artemisinin derivatives [38], with N86Y, Y184F, S1034C, N1042D and D1246Y as key mutations [39]. Notably, N86Y decreases susceptibility to CQ and AQ while enhancing susceptibility to LUM, mefloquine (MQ), and DHA [38, 40]. A decline in the prevalence of 86Y has been observed following the adoption of ACTs [41]. AL has been found to select for the NFD haplotype (residues 86/184/1246) [42, 43]. Additionally, increased copy number (copy number variation, CNV) of pfmdr1 has been reported to contribute to increased tolerance to DHA, LUM and MQ [44–46], while decreased copy number has been linked to increased tolerance to PPQ [47, 48]. Similarly, amplification of pfpm2 and pfpm3 is associated with widespread resistance to PPQ in Southeast Asia [21, 47, 49, 50].

In Angola, ACTs such as AL and ASAQ have been widely used since 2006 [3, 4, 51, 52]. DHA-PPQ was introduced before 2013 [9]. Updated data on mutations associated with antimalarial drug resistance in the country remains limited and lacks territorial coverage. Our review aims to characterize the prevalence and distribution of molecular markers of antimalarial resistance in Angola at the province level, based on studies published between 2000 and 2024.

By mapping these markers, we aim to underscore the lack of up-to-date, nationwide data on molecular markers of ACT resistance, thereby enabling the development of targeted interventions to preserve the efficacy of ACTs currently used in Angola.

Materials and methods

Selection of relevant literature

This systematic review was conducted following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) [53, 54]. Briefly, the search descriptors, inclusion/exclusion criteria and databases to search were defined in advance. The databases selected for the search were PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science. After combing through the selected databases, studies were screened by two independent researchers. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher was consulted to solve the dispute. The studies selected for inclusion in the review were submitted to analysis to extract the most important findings and conclusions. Two independent researchers entered data from all included studies into Microsoft Excel, after which divergences were checked and corrected. Subsequently, this data was organized and presented in tables and figures. A total of 80 studies published between January 1 st, 2000 and November 30th, 2024 complied with the inclusion criteria in the title, keywords or summary.

Selection criteria

Primary articles, written in English or Portuguese, reporting original observational data on molecular markers of antimalarial drug resistance (genotype and frequency) in P. falciparum isolates collected in Angola and published in indexed journals (PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science) were considered eligible for this review. Preprints, case reports and commentary articles were excluded. Studies which included > 100 samples collected from individuals traveling to and from Angola were excluded from the main analysis and discussed separately. The search was conducted on all databases using the keywords: “(Angola OR Bengo OR Benguela OR Bié OR Bie OR Cabinda OR Cuando-Cubango OR Cuando OR Cubango OR Cuanza OR Kwanza OR Cunene OR Huambo OR Huíla OR Huila OR Luanda OR Lunda OR Malanje OR Moxico OR Namibe OR Uíge OR Uige OR Zaire) AND (pfmdr1 OR “multidrug resistance 1” OR pfpm2/3 OR pfpm2 OR pfpm3 OR plasmepsin OR pfk13 OR pfkelch13 OR kelch13 OR “Kelch 13” OR pfdhps OR “dihydropteroate synthase” OR pfdhfr OR “dihydrofolate reductase” OR pfcrt OR “chloroquine resistance transporter” OR CNV OR “copy number variation”) AND (“malaria” OR “molecular marker” OR “drug resistance” OR “Plasmodium falciparum” OR ACT OR mutation).

Screening and data extraction

The titles of studies found using the above search criteria were imported to Microsoft Excel in order to remove duplicates and conduct the screening steps. Studies were screened by reading the titles and abstracts and then the full text. Information such as the authorship, year of sample collection, target groups, sample size, gene, polymorphisms and their respective prevalence was gathered and displayed in table form (Table S3–Table S7). A risk-of-bias assessment was performed using ROBINS-I V2, a tool for assessing risk of bias in non-randomized studies [55] (Table S2). Although ROBINS-I was originally developed for intervention studies, it was used in this review in order to apply a single, conservative framework across all non-randomised designs. To minimise misclassification for surveillance-type studies, we focused our judgements on the domains most relevant to cross-sectional sampling (e.g. selection of participants, missing data, and measurement of outcomes) and interpreted the intervention-related domains in a descriptive way. Studies were assessed regarding the seven ROBINS-I domains (D1–D7; Table S2) and then assigned an overall risk-of-bias judgement (low, moderate, serious or critical), following ROBINS-I guidance. The studies included in the review are listed in Table S1.

Data synthesis

The authors initially considered performing a meta-analysis of prevalence estimates. However, substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included studies precluded a meaningful quantitative synthesis. Sources of heterogeneity included: (i) differences in target populations (asymptomatic community surveys, symptomatic patients at health facilities, and treatment failure subsets), (ii) wide variation in sampling year, (iii) differences in genotyping methods and outcome definitions (single codons VS. combined haplotypes; presence/absence VS. full prevalence estimates), and (iv) incomplete overlap in the provinces and genes analysed across studies. These factors violate key assumptions for pooling prevalence data and would be expected to yield misleading summary estimates. We therefore conducted a structured narrative synthesis, stratifying results by province, gene and mutation, without performing a formal meta-analysis.

Results

General characteristics of included studies

A total of 80 studies were identified in all databases searched. Of the studies excluded during the selection process, 35 were duplicates and 24 were excluded after reading the title and abstract. No studies were removed during full-text screening. One study was excluded during the risk-of-bias assessment using ROBINS-I V2, as it was judged to be at critical risk of bias due to substantial selection bias and unclear outcome assessment. The remaining 20 studies had a low or moderate overall risk of bias and were included in the review (Fig. 2). Specifically, studies which only provided genotypic information from a subset of patients who experienced treatment failure after a course of ACTs were evaluated as presenting a moderate risk of bias (Table S2).

The information about the 20 studies included in this review is summarized in Table 1 and Fig. 3 and detailed in the Supplementary Material. All 20 studies included molecular analysis of known antimalarial resistance-associated SNPs in at least one amongst the genes pfcrt [4, 10, 56–61], pfdhfr/pfdhps [51, 56, 57, 60–65], pfk13 [3, 4, 9, 10, 66–69] and pfmdr1 [3, 4, 9, 10, 56–61, 67, 68]. Studies addressing SNPs in pfmdr1 were the most frequent (13/20, 65.0%) followed by pfdhfr/pfdhps (10/20, 50.0%), pfk13 (9/20, 45.0%) and pfcrt (8/20, 40.0%). Four studies (20.0%) discussed the presence of copy number alterations associated with antimalarial resistance in Angola [4, 9, 67, 68]. All studied pfmdr1 CNV, while only one included pfpm2 [4].

Most studies (16/20, 80.0%) included children under 18 years of age [3, 4, 9, 10, 51, 56–58, 60, 61, 63, 64, 67–70], while only six studies (30.0%) included adult participants [56, 58, 59, 63, 64, 68]. Notably, none of the studies included pregnant women. Eleven studies (55.0%) used samples collected in health facilities such as hospitals or clinics [56, 60–64, 66–70], while four (20.0%) used samples collected in the community [51, 57–59]. Another four studies (20.0%) only performed molecular analysis in a subset of samples, namely those from patients who experienced treatment failure after a course of ACT [3, 4, 9, 10] (Tables S3-S7). For these latter studies, the authors interpreted the reported frequencies as being enriched for resistant parasites when compared to the general parasite population. This overview highlights a concentration of studies performed in the Benguela, Luanda, Lunda Sul and Zaire provinces (Fig. 3) and a predominance of research done on samples collected from children (Tables S3–S7).

The geographic distribution of the 20 studies revealed a predominance of research activities concentrated in a few key provinces (Fig. 3). Benguela province was the most frequently represented [3, 4, 10, 57, 62, 67, 70], being included in 7 studies (35.0%), followed by Zaire [3, 4, 9, 10, 67, 70] (6 studies, 30.0%) and Luanda [59, 61, 64, 68, 69] (5 studies, 25.0%). This indicates a substantial focus on the coastal regions of northern Angola (Luanda, Zaire) and central Angola (Benguela). Lunda Sul was represented in 4 studies [4, 10, 67, 70] (19.0%), while seven other provinces (Bengo [58], Cabinda [51, 56], Cuanza Norte [51], Huambo [51, 66], Huíla [63], Malanje [51, 69], and Uíge [9, 51, 60]) were represented in less than 15% of studies each. Notably, seven provinces (Bié, Cuando-Cubango, Cuanza Sul, Cunene, Lunda Norte, Moxico, and Namibe) were not included in any study included in this review (Table 1).

Antimalarial resistance-associated single-nucleotide polymorphisms

pfcrt

Studies addressing pfcrt mutations corresponding to changes in amino acids 72–76 were conducted in seven Angolan provinces (Bengo [58], Benguela [4, 10, 57], Cabinda [56], Luanda [59, 61], Lunda Sul [4, 10], Uíge [60], Zaire [4, 10]), with the CQ-resistant CVIET haplotype having been found in all seven (Table 1, Table S3). Two studies (25.0%) addressed multiple provinces, namely Benguela, Lunda Sul and Zaire [4, 10]. The most recent assessment of the CVIET haplotype revealed a prevalence of 73.0% in 2018 samples from Cabinda [56]. For the remaining provinces with published surveillance data, the most recent CVIET prevalences are: 70.5% in Bengo [58] (2010), 14.7% in Luanda [59] (2007) and 93.9% in Uíge [60] (2004) (Fig. 3). Davlantes et al. [4] found a similarly high frequency of the resistant haplotype in children who experienced treatment failure after a course of either AL, ASAQ or DHA-PPQ in 2017, in the provinces of Benguela and Zaire (83.3% and 73.9%, 5/6 and 17/23, respectively). However, the same study also found that, in Lunda Sul, the CVIET frequency was only 11.1% (1/9), while the CQ-susceptible CVMNK haplotype was found in all samples (Fig. 3, Table S3). Gama et al. was the only paper included in our review which reported the presence of the South American StctVMNT haplotype (73.5%, 75/102), in 2007 Luanda samples [59].

pfdhfr and pfdhps

Studies addressing pfdhfr and pfdhps resistance markers were conducted on ten provinces: Benguela [57, 62, 70], Cabinda [51, 56], Cuanza Norte [51], Huambo [51], Huíla [63], Luanda [61, 64], Lunda Sul [70], Malanje [51], Uíge [51, 60] and Zaire [70] (Table 1). Two studies (20.0%) looked into multiple provinces [51, 70] (Table S4). The SP-resistant quintuple mutant haplotype, IRNGE (pfdhfr 51I, 59R, 108 N + pfdhps 437G, 540E), was found in Cabinda [56] in 2018 (37.5%) and in Zaire [70] in 2019 (10.5%). One Cabinda sample contained the pfdhps A581G mutation, while one Zaire sample completed a sextuple mutant IRNGEG (Fig. 3, Table S4). The most recent assessment of molecular markers in these two genes was conducted by Rosillo et al. in 2019 in the provinces of Benguela, Lunda Sul and Zaire [70]. The study found the pfdhfr 108 N mutation in all samples from the three provinces, while pfdhfr 51I, 59R and pfdhps 437G were highly prevalent (≥ 88.9%, ≥ 62.0% and ≥ 90.0%, respectively). A low prevalence of pfdhps 436 A (≤ 16.8%) and 540E (≤ 14.1%) was found in all provinces, while 431 V, 581G and 613S were found only in Zaire (2.8, 4.7 and 2.8%, respectively). No mutations were found corresponding to pfdhfr amino acids 16 and 164. Ebel et al. found pfdhfr 51I and 108 N as well as pfdhps 437G in all their 32 samples collected from Cabinda in 2018 [56]. Similar tendencies were found by Fortes et al. in 2007 samples collected in Cabinda, Cuanza Norte, Huambo, Malanje and Uíge regarding the prevalences of pfdhfr 51I, 59R and 108 N, as well as pfdhps 437G and 540E [51]. Huíla samples from 2011 showed comparable prevalences, with the exception of reporting the lowest prevalence of pfdhfr 51I in the country by far (46.2%) [63]. Distinctly from the remaining Angolan provinces studied, Gama et al. reported a prevalence of 6.7% (2/30) for 437G in 2007 samples from Luanda [64] (Table S4).

pfk13

Studies assessing polymorphisms in the pfk13 propeller domain associated with ART-R have been conducted in eight provinces: Benguela [3, 4, 10, 67], Cabinda [56], Huambo [66], Luanda [68, 69], Lunda Sul [4, 10, 67], Malanje [69], Uíge [9], Zaire [3, 4, 9, 10, 67] (Table 1). Six studies (66.7%) examined multiple provinces [3, 4, 9, 10, 67, 69] (Table S5). All studies found few to no pfk13 mutations (Fig. 3), none of which part of the WHO validated or candidate mutation list [30]. The synonymous mutation R471R was the most common and found in four provinces: Cabinda [56] (2.0%), Huambo [66] (12.8%), Luanda [69] (unknown frequency) and Malanje [69] (unknown frequency). A578S was found in Huambo [66] (2.6%), Lunda Sul [67] (2.4%) and Zaire [67] (0.5%). R575R was found only in Malanje [69] (2.0%) and T535T (0.5%) only in Zaire [67]. A504V was found in one Zaire patient who experienced AL treatment failure [4] (Table S5).

pfmdr1

Studies of pfmdr1 SNPs associated with antimalarial resistance were identified in seven provinces: Bengo [58], Benguela [3, 4, 10, 57, 67], Cabinda [56], Luanda [59, 61, 68], Lunda Sul [4, 10, 67], Uíge [9, 60] and Zaire [3, 4, 9, 10, 67] (Table 1). Five studies (38.5%) addressed multiple provinces [3, 4, 9, 10, 67]. The majority of these studies (69.2%, 9/13) addressed the frequency of mutations corresponding to changes in pfmdr1 amino acids 84, 184 and 1246 [3, 4, 9, 10, 56–59, 67] (Table S6). The most recently-published study on the topic in Angola found that the wild-type NYD haplotype showed a 55.3% prevalence in 2018 samples from Cabinda, while NFD was present in 36.8% [56]. Another study by Dimbu et al. [10], which genotyped 2019 samples from children in Benguela, Lunda Sul and Zaire who experienced treatment failure after a course of either AL or ASAQ, found that 86Y and 1246Y had extremely low overall frequencies (≤ 4.7%), with 184 F being found in 30.2% of samples. The frequency of 86Y has decreased drastically in Angola in the time period focused on for this review, as can be exemplified by two studies conducted in Luanda: Figueiredo et al. [61] found 86Y in 71.4% of samples in 2008, while Kiaco et al. [68] found it decreased from 21.4 to 15.2% between the years 2011 and 2013. Similarly, Menegon et al. [60] found 86Y in 68.2% of their 2008 Uíge samples (Table S6).

Copy number variation in pfmdr1 and pfpm2

Four studies were found which investigated CNV associated with antimalarial resistance in Angola between 2000 and 2024 [4, 9, 67, 68] (Table 1,Table S7). These were conducted in the provinces of Benguela [4, 67], Luanda [68], Lunda Sul [4, 67], Uíge [9] and Zaire [4, 9, 67]. All four studies analysed pfmdr1 CNV, while one also analysed pfpm2 [4]. One study which assessed cases of AL or DHA-PPQ treatment failure in Uíge and Zaire in 2013 found no evidence of pfmdr1 amplification [9]. Similarly, two other studies carried out in Benguela, Lunda Sul, and Zaire in 2015 and 2017 (the latter in treatment failures only) also reported the absence of increased pfmdr1 copy number [4, 67]. On the other hand, a separate study from Luanda found that 14.0% (13/93) of samples collected between 2011 and 2013 exhibited increased pfmdr1 copy number [68]. A study from our group, published in 2025 (outside the established time period for this review), analysed samples collected in 2023 and confirmed the existence of pfmdr1 CNV in 8.9% (9/101) in Lubango, Huíla, central Angola [71].

Only one study included in our review surveyed pfpm2 CNV, in 2017 cases of treatment failure in the provinces of Benguela, Lunda Sul, and Zaire, and reported no gene amplification [4]. The Huíla study mentioned above found a pfpm2 CNV prevalence of 9.8% [71].

Discussion

The pfcrt CVIET haplotype remains prevalent in multiple Angolan provinces. The persistence of this haplotype after the withdrawal of CQ has been observed in several African malaria-endemic countries [72–74]. However, recently-published results in Angola display a considerable range in frequency: from 11.1% in Lunda Sul (2017, treatment failures only) [4] to 73.0% in Cabinda (2018) [56]. It is possible that differences in the availability of first-line ACTs throughout the country has placed distinct pressures on circulating parasites in each region, given that it has been reported that CVIET confers some resistance to AQ, while LUM selects for wild-type CVMNK [14, 17]. Published results from the neighbouring DRC and Republic of the Congo align with these results from Angola. Recent data show a comparable prevalence to Lunda Sul in the southern DRC province of Lualaba of both the 76 T mutation and the CVIET haplotype [75, 76]. Conversely, the same prevalences remained elevated in cities near Cabinda province-Brazzaville in the Republic of the Congo and Kinshasa in the DRC-in the 2010 s [72, 77]. Both countries showed significant regional variation in pfcrt, not unlike Angola, as well as a decrease in the 76 T and/or CVIET frequencies in the current decade [76, 77].

The Luanda study by Gama et al. [59] was the only one in Angola which found the South American StctVMNT haplotype, also reported in Tanzania [78]. The authors proposed the frequent travel of citizens between Angola and Brazil as a potential cause; however, the same study failed to report pfmdr1 mutations commonly-found in Brazil, S1034C and N1042D [79]. SVMNT may be associated with a lower fitness cost than CVIET [14], which would offer an advantage in a high-transmission setting such as Luanda. Continued surveillance of pfcrt is essential given the use of AQ as part of ASAQ in the region. This review found no studies which analysed the presence of pfcrt mutations outside of the 72–76 region.

The SP-resistant haplotype IRNGE found in the northern provinces of Cabinda (37.5%) and Zaire (10.5%) may suggest possible transboundary gene flow from areas with high SP drug pressure such as the Republic of the Congo and the DRC, both of which also use SP for IPTp [80, 81]. In addition, the Republic of the Congo recommends artesunate-SP for the treatment of uncomplicated malaria in pregnant women [81], while SP is inexpensive and available as a monotherapy in the DRC [82, 83]. The tendency seen around Brazzaville and Kinshasa mostly aligns with the Angolan results: all mutations making up the quintuple mutant IRNGE are highly prevalent except for K540E, which keeps the prevalence of the haplotype low. The pfdhps A581G mutation is rare in the DRC, but was found in 41.8% of samples from Brazzaville in 2021 [76, 80, 84].

IPTp-SP remains effective in areas of moderate SP resistance, but surveillance of associated mutations as well as maternal and neonatal outcomes is critical. The WHO recommends discontinuing IPTp-SP in areas where the prevalence of 540E is > 95% and of 581G is > 10% [85], given their involvement in quintuple and sextuple mutants which cause SP treatment failure. The prevalence of resistant haplotypes is highest in (but not exclusive to) West African countries, where the 540E mutation is most common [29, 86, 87]. Despite the low frequencies of 540E and 581G reported in Angola so far, the presence of all required mutations for the creation of quintuple and sextuple mutants highlights the importance of continued surveillance of mutations associated with SP resistance and evaluation of IPTp-SP effectiveness in the country.

None of the pfk13 mutations found in Angola are part of the lists of validated or candidate markers of ART-R released by the WHO [30]. The A578S mutation has been found in multiple African and Asian countries, but has not yet been linked with ART-R [88–90]. While there are no signs of ART-R in Angola so far, surveillance must continue given the severity of potential ACT treatment failure.

A recent study conducted in Brazzaville reported similar prevalences as Angola for pfmdr1 86Y, 184 F and 1246Y: 6.8%, 27.7% and 0% [91]. The decreased frequencies of 86Y and 1246Y align with the discontinuation of CQ and AQ monotherapy [38, 92]. Y184 is also selected for by CQ and AQ [93], but it persists as the most prevalent 184 allele in Angola. The wild-type NYD was found to be the most common haplotype, followed by NFD. Despite the fitness cost, NFD is selected for in the presence of LUM/AL [42, 94]. These two haplotypes co-exist in the population perhaps due to a combination of pre-ACT selection and current distinct drug pressures by AL and ASAQ. Dimbu et al. [10] raised the alarm regarding AL therapeutic efficacies below the WHO threshold of 90% in Zaire and Lunda Sul on some occasions, which could require changes to recommended malaria treatments in the country. While some concerns were raised regarding the supervision of evening doses and the method for estimating efficacy [95, 96], it is nonetheless crucial to continue monitoring efficacy of commonly-used ACTs in Angola and the prevalence of mutations or haplotypes associated with reduced susceptibility and/or treatment failure.

The findings of low pfmdr1 CNV prevalence (≤ 10%) in Angola are similar to those reported in other African countries such as the DRC [97], Gabon [98, 99], Ghana [99], Kenya [99–101], Sudan [102] and Uganda [103]. Since increased pfmdr1 copy number has been associated with treatment failure or reduced sensitivity to multiple ACTs [100, 102, 104], the detection of elevated pfmdr1 copy numbers in Angola highlights the need for ongoing surveillance. Amplification of pfpm2 was not found in the one study included in our review which assessed it, but was later reported in Huíla with a prevalence of 9.8% [71]. This variation in prevalence aligns with recent findings from other countries in Africa, where pfpm2 CNV frequencies have also varied between, as well as within, countries-ranging from 2.0% in Kenya to 33.9% in Uganda [101, 105] and between 1.1% and 5.7% in the center and south of Mozambique [106–108]. Given the wide geographic distribution and diversity of clinical sites in Africa where increased pfpm2 CNV has been detected, the high frequencies reported in distant locations suggest that pfpm2 amplification may have arisen independently at each site [105]. Drawing on the experience from Southeast Asia-where PPQ resistance (possibly driven by pfpm2 amplification) emerged and spread rapidly, leading to treatment failures with DHA-PPQ [47]-close monitoring of this marker in Angola is essential. No study was found which investigated pfpm3 amplification in Angola.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review provides a robust synthesis of over two decades of antimalarial research in Angola, consolidating data from twenty peer-reviewed studies into a single, cohesive overview. By evaluating multiple genes associated with resistance to various antimalarials, the analysis offers valuable insights into regional resistance patterns at the provincial level. and establishes a strong evidence-based foundation for future public health strategies. The authors chose not to perform a meta-analysis due to significant heterogeneity among the included studies. Similarly, ROBINS-I V2 was used for the risk-of-bias assessment despite not being optimally tailored to pure surveillance or prevalence studies, in order to accommodate the papers’ multiple non-randomised designs. We consider both of these aspects to be limitations of our approach.

Conclusions

Molecular surveillance of Plasmodium falciparum in Angola reveals a markedly heterogeneous resistance landscape, which is severely constrained by data fragmentation. The pfcrt CVIET haplotype persists at high prevalences (reaching 73.0% in Cabinda in 2018) and the pfdhfr/pfdhps IRNGE haplotype is stable in coastal northern provinces. Contrastingly, there is a clear declining trend in the prevalence of pfmdr1 86Y and 1246Y mutations following the nationwide implementation of ACTs. To date, no WHO-validated pfk13 SNPs associated with artemisinin resistance have been identified in the country. However, therapeutic efficacy reports indicating AL treatment outcomes below 90% in provinces such as Zaire and Lunda Sul, coupled with the recent emergence of pfmdr1 and pfpm2 gene amplifications in Luanda and Huíla, respectively, serve as critical warning signals.

Research efforts remain disproportionately concentrated in coastal and northern provinces, leaving seven provinces (Bié, Cuando-Cubango, Cuanza Sul, Cunene, Lunda Norte, Moxico and Namibe) entirely unmonitored. Furthermore, existing data in multiple provinces is largely outdated. To preserve the long-term efficacy of ACTs, it is imperative to expand genomic surveillance across the entire territory and ensure systematic data updates, enabling a public health response rooted in comprehensive and current evidence.
