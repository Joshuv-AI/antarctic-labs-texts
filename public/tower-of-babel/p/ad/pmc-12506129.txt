Analysing the effect of multidisciplinary teams to address preventive healthcare gaps in a rural teaching clinic: a quality improvement project
Authors: Veronica Hill, Brittany Woody, Fahad Irfan Abid, Meaghan Barros, Priya Pohani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Preventive care decreases the risk of illness, disease and death, yet patient adherence to preventive care is low, calculated to be around 5%, and rural residents are suspected to have even lower rates compared with urban patients. Despite continually updated guidelines and recommendations for screenings and preventive care, delivery of care to rural patients is difficult, and a deficit exists. This project aimed to use multidisciplinary teams at a teaching rural health clinic to close five preventive care gaps: lung cancer screening, colon cancer screening, Shingrix vaccination, osteoporosis screening and hepatitis C screening by 10% in the rural community.An electronic medical record (EMR) analysis was used to identify and prioritise preventive care gap deficiencies, and quality improvement methods integrating student physicians were used to integrate and optimise care gap closures into the daily routine of clinic staff.After three intervals of the project period, a 19% increase was achieved in lung cancer screening, a 49% increase in colon cancer screenings and 63% and 9% in osteoporosis and hepatitis C screenings. Additionally, medical providers had a change in practice management as a direct result of intentional attention to preventive care gaps. The Shingrix vaccination project needed to be stopped due to the availability of the vaccine.By using an EMR and a working relationship between staff, nursing, physicians and students, health disparities in a rural community were decreased.

Problem

Preventive care decreases the risk of illness, disease and death, yet patient adherence to preventive care is low, calculated to be around 55%, and rural residents are suspected to have even lower rates compared with urban patients.1 Despite continually updated guidelines and recommendations for screenings and preventive care, delivery of care to rural patients is difficult, and a deficit exists.

In late 2019, a primary care office in rural south Alabama was opened in affiliation with an osteopathic medical school to provide medical students hands-on patient experiences while meeting the needs of rural residents who had not had access to a primary care provider in decades. The town of Ashford, Alabama, USA, has a population of just over 2000 residents and approximately 16% of those residents are at or below the federal poverty line. A primary care office was first opened in Ashford in the early 1900s, but as is common in most rural areas, every primary care provider that practised in Ashford had left the practice to work for a larger health system. This is often due to low wages and physician burnout. In Ashford, this left the residents of Ashford without consistent access to primary care. Without access to care, chronic diseases were untreated, and preventive care was non-existent. In 2013, the Alabama College of Osteopathic Medicine opened, and in 2019, a staff of one physician, two nurse practitioners and one nurse began seeing internal medicine and paediatric patients in the local clinic building. Every month, 15 third-year osteopathic medical students rotated through the clinic for their rural health clinical rotation, providing an opportunity to leverage the additional personnel to address preventive care gaps. As the patients had not had access to care in decades, all of the adult patients had several preventive healthcare gaps.

In primary care, multidisciplinary teams have been shown to increase patient compliance and satisfaction and decrease physician burnout.2 With the unique staffing of this clinic, a quality improvement (QI) project was created aiming to use the multiple healthcare team members at the clinic and develop a sustainable process to address preventive care gaps of the adult patients in the clinic.

Background

Compared with urban residents, rural residents are older, poorer, less educated and participate in more high-risk behaviours.1 Clinical audits have shown low compliance to preventive screenings both nationally and locally, highlighting the need for focused efforts to close the healthcare maintenance gaps for those in rural America.1

QI studies are effective ways systems can analyse and deliver healthcare to improve patient outcomes, including closing preventive care gaps. QI projects help turn data from research into practical approaches that can be used by healthcare staff to improve medical practices. The plan, do, study, act (PDSA) approach provides a methodical procedure to QI studies for researching and implementing effective medical strategies into practice. Over a 3-year time period, a team of physicians, medical students, nurse practitioners and practical nurses developed and refined QI projects to increase United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) screenings and decrease the most common care gaps at the Ashford Clinic. Using the PDSA approach, the team addressed lung cancer screening, colorectal cancer screening, osteoporosis and hepatitis C screening, and immunisation status against shingles.

Lung and bronchus cancers represented the highest incidence of cancer-related deaths in the USA in 2017.3 In 2013, the USPSTF issued a Grade B recommendation advising all patients between the ages of 55 and 80 years old with a 30-pack-year or greater smoking history who have smoked in the last 15 years should receive an annual low-dose CT scan (LDCT). In 2021, the recommendation was updated to include individuals with a 20-pack-year smoking history.3

Colorectal cancer is the second leading cause of cancer deaths in the USA, but the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported that in 2018, only 68.8% of adults aged 50–75 were up to date with colorectal cancer screenings.4 5 Colorectal cancer screenings both allow precancerous polyps to be removed and for cancer to be detected early when treatment is most effective.

The International Osteoporosis Foundation estimates that in 2010, 10.2 million men and women over the age of 65 had osteoporosis in the USA.6 The economic burden of osteoporosis was estimated to be 17 billion US$ in 2005.6 Recognition and treatment of osteoporosis decreases the incidence of osteoporosis fractures, the main consequence of osteoporosis.

There is an estimated 2.4 million people living with hepatitis C in the USA.7 Since 2012, there have been more deaths due to hepatitis C than all 60 of the reportable infectious diseases combined. In rural settings, diagnosing and treatment of acute hepatitis C is especially challenging due to the asymptomatic nature of the acute phase of hepatitis C. In 2018, a total of 3621 cases of acute hepatitis C were reported to the CDC.7 Since many are asymptomatic, patients may not seek care and the actual number of hepatitis C cases is estimated to be closer to 50 300. New treatments are available that may cure over 90% of patients living with hepatitis C, but due to low access to diagnosis and treatment, many patients are left untreated. By increasing screening and identifying the diseases before the onset of symptoms, morbidity and mortality can be reduced.8 If individuals with chronic hepatitis C are identified before the development of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma and fibrosis, 90%–100% of patients can be expected to respond to treatment.

By performing screening exams, we hope to identify malignancies or deficiencies early. So, we can improve prognosis and treatment while educating patient about the importance of preventive care. This is especially important in rural settings where various cultural, educational, socioeconomic and resource barriers limit access to healthcare. It is vital that we identify such health factors that can potentially endanger individuals to prevent serious negative impacts. We thus conducted QI studies to detect lung cancer, colorectal cancer, osteoporosis and vaccination rates in patients seen at the primary care clinic. Our goal is that by conducting these QI studies, we are able to improve patient outcomes and raise awareness about the importance of healthcare prevention and QI studies in the field of medical education.

Measurement

Initial measures chosen to demonstrate QI interventions were quality care gap closures and number of screening tests ordered. Quality care gap closures were measured in Epic, the electronic medical record (EMR) system used in the clinic. It displays a column in each patient’s chart with the quality care gaps easily identifiable in red text. This tool helped providers quickly see which recommended tests had not been ordered for each patient so that they could be discussed and ordered during their visit. The number of screening tests ordered could be measured by doing a query in Epic. This query function allowed providers to efficiently see the number of consents collected and screenings done on patients. Each patient’s consent or decline was collected during their initial visit and subsequent follow-up visits. Data frequency depended on patient visit availability and whether they required a follow-up visit. Patient compliance was not a variable being directly operationalised and measured in the study due to the potential lack of standardisation.

We can assume that the EMR quality care gap closures and query functions are optimally valid. It is assumed that if the order is submitted through the EMR, it is being counted appropriately. Additionally, we can assume that the EMR’s query function works as designed and correctly displays the number of screening tests ordered. In terms of measuring reliability, we can assume that the EMR quality care gap and query features will display the same results whenever they are searched for.

We were able to establish that the observed outcomes were due to our interventions as screening outcomes are directly related to patient consent and compliance and orders placed. Implementing the QI aspects to the project helped increase the number of screening tests ordered and thus made progress in closing the gaps seen in rural patients’ healthcare. Specifically, looking at the results of our baseline measurement, we can see that using the healthcare gap closures and screening tests query in the EMR was beneficial for increasing patient compliance and recommended screening. Compliance rates improved in our initial cycle from 53% (44/82) of patients being non-compliant to 35% (29/84) for LDCT lung screening, demonstrating that our QI interventions were beneficial. To meet the goal of our QI project, we planned to continue the process of closing quality care gaps while expanding these gap closures to additional avenues.

Design

In designing a QI project with the goal of improving the rates of health screening, our initial hypothesis was that the main barrier to the screenings being ordered was providers knowing which patients required particular screenings. Patients nor the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination. As such, the first cycle focused on LDCT scans to screen for lung cancer because of the specific parameters a patient must meet to require the screening. At the time of the cycle, LDCT scans were suggested by the USPSTF to be performed yearly for patients who had at least 30 pack years, had smoked within the last 20 years (including current smokers), and who were between the ages of 55 and 80. LDCT scans were also chosen for the first screening cycle because they were indicated for the smallest cohort of patients. This cycle first identified which patients needed an LDCT by collecting data regarding pack years, smoking status and most recent LDCT. This was accomplished by filtering and creating reports within the EMR using the SlicerDicer tool and comparing the identified patients on the report with the daily schedule. We then implemented a strategy to notify providers when their patients met these criteria and needed to be screened.

In the second round of interventions, after finding the first round successful in improving LDCT rates, we expanded the goal to include two more QI measures. In this round, we focused on improving rates of colorectal cancer screenings and osteoporosis screenings by way of colonoscopy/stool test and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scans, respectively. Unlike cycle 1, being eligible for colorectal cancer screening and osteoporosis screening is based only on the patients’ age and sex, making identifying qualifying patients through the filter and reporting tools within the EMR simpler. It was during this cycle that we introduced ‘screening cards’, which indicated for each patient which screening tests needed to be ordered for that day per the results on the filtered report. The cards provided a quick reference for providers, but creating them was labour intensive. Without the availability of medical students, making the cards might not have been practical.

In the third round of intervention, we focused on hepatitis C screening. This screening had the largest potential cohort because it is indicated for every patient over 18 years old but is only recommended once per patient unlike the other screenings which are done periodically. In this cycle, patients were identified in a similar manner using the filter and reporting function of the EMR, and providers were notified using individualised screening cards. The additional step in this phase involved medical students putting the order for the screening into the EMR after their initial interview with the patient. This required the provider to sign the order before concluding the visit to ensure the lab was completed that day. Because the screening is only done once in a person’s lifetime, the initial compliance rates were high, but the intervention was successful in improving the rates further.

Strategy

Our project goal was to increase screening, accessibility and compliance for our patients in the context of certain healthcare disparities. We focused on five of the recommended CDC health topics in our rural clinic and implemented cyclical strategies to meet our goal.

PDSA 1

In our first cycle, our aim was to increase the number of patients screened for lung cancer using LDCT. We sought to accomplish this by creating questionnaires for patients who are smokers and distributing the information gained from the questionnaires to the providers. This intervention involved creating reminder cards based on the information from the questionnaires that identified patients eligible for LDCT screening. We hypothesised that giving providers a physical reminder card that listed patients eligible for screening would prompt the providers to discuss and order the screenings during patient visits and, in turn, improve the rate of LDCT screenings at the clinic. Medical students were tasked with creating reminder cards for the following clinic day and ensuring they were available to providers during the morning huddle. Providers were instructed to place an LDCT order if, according to the reminder cards, patients were eligible and patients consented. On implementation of this strategy, pre-intervention and post-intervention data on LDCT screening rates were collected over a 3-month period. The results of this first cycle showed an increase in compliance rates by 19%, which exceeded our goal of a 10% increase. However, feedback from providers revealed that while reminder cards prompted them to discuss screenings, they often forgot to place the orders in the EMR. While the reminder cards were effective in prompting providers’ attention, the sustainability of this strategy was limited by the continued reliance on clinician memory and the burden of processing orders manually. Additionally, patient feedback revealed a lot of confusion about what an LDCT was and many were unaware of the recommendation for screening and were resistant to another test due to time and cost constraints.

PDSA 2

Building on the success of our first cycle and learning from our limitations, the second cycle aimed to expand the screening process to include additional care gaps, including: osteoporosis (DEXA), Shingrix vaccine status and colorectal cancer screening. We hypothesised that by targeting multiple care gaps, providers could address a broader range of screening needs. Learning from our limitations from our first cycle, we began to distribute reminder cards at both morning and afternoon huddles to ensure consistent reminders and increase provider engagement. Each card listed the patients’ eligibility for the screenings. Data were again collected before and after the intervention was implemented. The data revealed a slight improvement in colorectal cancer and osteoporosis screenings. Shingrix vaccination rates were not reported because the pharmacy that fulfilled the vaccine stopped stocking the vaccine partway through the project, which led to incomplete data collection. Further, we were informed that the added complexity of managing multiple care gaps led to confusion among providers when determining patient eligibility. The addition of multiple care gaps to the reminder cards also made it difficult for providers to process the reminders, essentially reducing the effectiveness of the system. In addition to the confusion among providers, the complexity of managing three screening processes led to additional paperwork and consent forms being missed, as well as other necessary appointment measures and more confusion for the patients. This project taught us that, although we successfully addressed one health disparity through our previous workflow, the structure of our original strategy was not ideal for targeting multiple health disparities simultaneously.

PDSA 3

The confusion in cycle 2 was likely due to the complexity of managing multiple care gaps simultaneously, leading to errors. To simplify, our goal in cycle 3 was to focus on just one care gap—hepatitis C —and embed screening orders directly into the EMR to increase efficiency and ensure a sustainable approach. To get past the issue of reliance on physical reminder cards, we hypothesised that embedding screening orders into the EMR would reduce the burden on providers and improve the number of orders placed for screenings. In this cycle, we eliminated physical reminder cards and instead had medical students input orders for hepatitis C screening directly into the EMR for each eligible patient. Providers would then be prompted to discuss screenings while signing orders in the EMR, eliminating the burden of relying on memory and physical reminder cards. Data were collected by tracking the number of screenings ordered and completed. We collected pre-data and post-data over the course of 3 months, which allowed us to compare the effectiveness of the new approach. This cycle was highly successful, and we observed a significant improvement in hepatitis C screening rates. Further, providers found this intervention to be much more sustainable because the orders were already placed in the EMR, reducing the need for them to act on memory. In fact, the providers expressed a willingness to continue using this method for other care gaps in the future. The key takeaway from this cycle was the importance of integrating interventions directly into existing workflows, particularly the EMR. This reduced the burden on providers and easily prompted them to discuss the care gaps with patients. Focusing on one care gap at a time was also more effective, as it allowed for a more straightforward execution of the intervention.

Expanding to multiple care gaps in cycle 2 led to confusion and inefficiencies. The reminder cards became overwhelming and providers struggled with addressing the care gaps. Additionally, the paper-based reminder cards did not sufficiently generate immediate action during patient visits, and the patients were still confused about the process and meaning behind the additional screenings. The integration of screening orders directly into the EMR was by far the most effective strategy. It reduced administrative burden and thus allowed providers to implement the necessary screenings more efficiently. Simplifying our focus to one health disparity also made the process more manageable and successful. These cycles underscore the importance of simplifying and adapting our interventions, focusing on one care gap at a time, and integrating the process directly into the EMR. This approach improved provider compliance and has made this intervention both sustainable and scalable.

Results

The success of each intervention was measured in the percent change in compliance rate between before and after the interventions. Over time, the baseline compliance rate for additional metrics increased. Please see figure 1, in cycle 1, rates of LDCT compliance increased by 19.2%, from 46.3% to 65.5%. During this cycle, two LDCT scans yielded abnormal results that required more imaging for follow-up. In cycle 2, DEXA scan compliance increased by 62.5% from 12.2% to 74.7%. In the same cycle, colorectal cancer screening increased from 20% to 69%, yielding an increase of 49%. In cycle 3, hepatitis C screenings increased from 90% to 99%, increasing by 9%. During this cycle, four patients were found to have asymptomatic hepatitis C. Lessons and limitations

This project centred around a multidisciplinary team and relied on prompt communication between team members. Through daily huddles and an introductory workflow presentation, all team members were adequately informed of their roles and responsibilities to ensure a sustainable project. However, while implementing this project, some communication problems occurred due to students’ schedules, resulting in students appearing at the clinic at different times. To overcome this problem, the burden of consenting and ordering screenings was given to students assigned to the clinic that particular day.

The project demonstrated a positive change as the clinicians had student aid in ordering the necessary screenings, allowing for more healthcare gaps to be closed. All members were involved in providing feedback and suggestions to create the basis for this project.

While initially hopeful due to the substantial student resources, some unforeseen limitations occurred that hindered our project progress. This project was initially implemented during the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, which resulted in a limited number of patients coming into the clinic each day. Additionally, the pharmacy that fulfilled the shingles vaccinations stopped stocking the vaccines, which led to incomplete data collection for this healthcare gap. Furthermore, restrictions within the EMR did not allow students to close care gaps in the EMR, so the clinician had to actively close each care gap, which resulted in additional burden on the clinicians.

Another limitation was that our initial plan was to analyse multiple health factors at once, which proved to be too cumbersome. This workflow consisted of medical students notifying the clinicians regarding patient screening which placed additional work on the clinicians who had multiple competing tasks between teaching students and seeing patients. To help alleviate this limitation, the clinicians and third-year students worked towards workflow changes with students inputting the consents and screenings into an EMR for these patients to alert the clinicians about which tests needed to be ordered. This led to a successful third cycle as the responsibility remained with the medical students to order the screenings based on current guidelines.

Many of the patients that participated in this project have not had access to primary care in decades. When medical students were suddenly asking them to have multiple tests performed to catch them up on necessary screenings, they became overwhelmed and often required additional time for education and convincing. The lack of previous care resulted in more healthcare gaps available to be closed by our clinic but also presented a challenge as patients resisted more tests and lab work. We alternatively saw this as an opportunity for students to use their teaching skills to educate the patients. As the patient population grows at the clinic, the number of care gaps and closures will fluctuate more than the time period immediately after the clinic opened when this project took place.

While this new system has helped to increase our compliance rates among patients, we will need to apply these new methods towards additional health disparities to determine if it is applicable to multiple health screenings. A future project could implement these strategies in different clinic settings to analyse the usage of students to demonstrate if an increase in resources subsequently increases compliance rates and decreases outstanding healthcare inequalities.

Conclusion

The QI project achieved its goal of improving the rates at which health screenings are ordered. The investigation into which patients were lacking necessary health screenings illuminated a problem that was certainly improved on through the course of this project. The most useful replications of this project might be in clinics or communities in a similar situation: where primary care had not been widely available for many years. The project may also be helpful, however, in identifying patients who have missed certain screenings in otherwise medically well-served areas. Our data show an increase in screening rates in each of our cycles, and it is therefore worth continuing these strategies to maintain high compliance rates with ordering of health screenings. Even as our order rates approach 100%, patients will need to be continuously evaluated for screenings as they age, as time since their last screening increases and as new patients are seen at the clinic.
