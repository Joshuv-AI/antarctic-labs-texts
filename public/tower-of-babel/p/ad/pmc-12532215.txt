Prevalence and Risk Factors of Pediatric Delirium in Critically Ill Children: A Study Using Cornell Assessment of Pediatric Delirium Tool at a Tertiary Care Hospital
Authors: Muhammad Nashit, Anwar Ul Haque, Sadiq Mirza, Irfan Habib, Saba Shahid, Abdul Rahim Ahmed, Humaira Jurair
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsThe Cornell Assessment of Pediatric Delirium (CAPD)-revised is a validated tool for detecting delirium in critically ill children. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of delirium and associated risk factors in a pediatric intensive care population using CAPD.Materials and methodsA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among 201 critically ill children aged 1 month–14 years admitted to a tertiary care hospital in Karachi, Pakistan, from August 2021 to February 2022. Delirium was assessed twice daily for three consecutive days using CAPD. A score of ≥9 was considered diagnostic.ResultsDelirium was identified in 24.9% of patients (n = 50/201). The mean age was 5.14 ± 4.03 years, with 61.7% male (n = 124/201). Significant risk factors included comorbidities (n = 34/201, OR = 3.45, 95% CI: 1.78–6.70, p = 0.0005), developmental delay (n = 3/201, OR = 5.12, 95% CI: 1.34–19.54, p = 0.015), benzodiazepine use (n = 45/201, OR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.52–5.50, p = 0.001), antiepileptic use (n = 38/201, OR = 2.67, 95% CI: 1.38–5.17, p = 0.004), high-flow oxygen (n = 60/201, OR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.20–4.45, p = 0.012), and mechanical ventilation (n = 55/201, OR = 3.12, 95% CI: 1.62–6.01, p = 0.001).ConclusionDelirium affected nearly one-quarter of critically ill children, with comorbidities, developmental delay, use of sedatives, and advanced respiratory as key risk factors. Cornell Assessment of Pediatric Delirium is a valuable tool for routine screening to enable early recognition and management of pediatric delirium. Introduction

Delirium is a fluctuating psychiatric disorder with reversible acute organic brain dysfunction presenting as a lack of attention and cognitive impairment associated with critical illness, high mortality, increased length of stay, huge cost, and poor outcomes.1–5 The pathophysiology of ICU delirium is complex and multifactorial. It is the result of diffuse cerebral metabolic abnormality. Broadly, alterations in neurotransmission, cerebral blood flow, energy metabolism, and disordered cellular homeostasis all play a role.6–8 Although it can occasionally be traced to a single etiology (for example, alcohol toxicity or delirium tremens), in the intensive care unit, it is frequently a result of three synergistic events: the underlying disease process, side effects of treatment, and the highly abnormal critical care unit environment.9,10 It is itself not a diagnosis, but rather a consequence of ongoing medical conditions that result in such disturbance.2

The gold standard for identifying delirium is painstaking; it demands a child/adolescent psychiatrist as per the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th ed (DSM-V). A complete assessment takes almost half an hour, which is practically not possible for a specialist to evaluate every child.1,2,8–10 The CAPD tool is a strict observational tool, and it provides a comprehensive and systematic approach designed to ease the early identification and assessment of delirium symptoms in critically ill pediatric patients. It integrates age-appropriate modifications to suit the pediatric population's unique characteristics and developmental stages. By enabling healthcare providers to diagnose and monitor delirium accurately, the CAPD empowers medical teams to intervene promptly and implement management strategies. A recent position statement by the European Society of Pediatric and Neonatal Intensive Care (ESPNIC) recommended the use of CAPD as an instrument to assess pediatric delirium in critically ill infants and children (grade of recommendation = A).11

International studies showed a 20–30% prevalence of delirium in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) setting, with variable sample sizes, the smallest one being (n = 56) in a pilot study and the largest one being over 800 conducted on a multinational level.5,12–17 Though rates were observed to be higher in pediatric cardiac patients (49 and 57%) and surprisingly lower in pediatric cancer patients (11.7, 10, and 18.8%).13,18–21

Delirium in pediatric ICUs remains under-recognized, largely due to missed risk factors and limited awareness among healthcare professionals regarding screening and management.22,23 Sedatives like benzodiazepines, opioids, and polypharmacy further elevate the risk. While tools like CAPD offer practical bedside screening, most validation studies are from high-income countries. In low-resource settings, reliance on DSM-V is impractical due to time and specialist constraints. There is a critical need for local data on pediatric delirium prevalence and associated risk factors using feasible tools like CAPD. This study addresses that gap in a tertiary care hospital in a low-income country.

Materials and Methods

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted over 7 months (August 2021–February 2022) at the PICU and Pediatric High Dependency Unit (PHDU) of a tertiary care hospital in Karachi, Pakistan, following approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Indus Hospital & Health Network (IHHN) following approval from the IRB of IHHN. Using the free online available OpenEpi program v. 3.01, we calculated a sample size of 201 with an anticipated frequency of 25% [based on a large-scale study conveyed in five different countries (a total of 25 institutions) in 2018 by Traube and colleagues, the prevalence of delirium using CAPD score in critically ill children was 25%, confidence interval as 95% and ± 6% absolute precision].6 Patients aged 1 month to 14 years, admitted for at least 24 hours through the emergency department, outpatient referrals, inpatient ward transfers, or post-operative recovery, were included. Exclusion criteria included age <1 month and deep sedation [Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) score < −3]. Informed consent was obtained from parents or caregivers after explaining the study objectives.

Data collection encompassed patient demographics, comorbidity (any ongoing medical condition or ailment before presenting illness), diagnosis, and developmental level, as determined through caregiver-reported milestones achieved at a certain age, e.g., smiling at 2 months, unsupported neck holding at 4 months, sitting with support at 6 months of life, etc., and will be considered appropriate if milestones are achieved on time, otherwise delayed, and the RASS. The RASS, a universal tool for assessing agitation or sedation levels in intensive care settings, was essential for calculating the CAPD score to evaluate delirium. Patients were assessed twice daily for three consecutive days by a single investigator using the Cornell Assessment of Pediatric Delirium (CAPD)-revised tool, with a score ≥ 9 indicating delirium. To minimize observer bias, the primary investigator was trained in CAPD application, though the single-observer approach introduces a potential risk of bias. Missing or incomplete CAPD scores, (e.g., due to patient transfer or incomplete assessments) were handled by excluding the affected patient from analysis, ensuring only complete datasets were included. Data was recorded electronically in a pre-designed proforma.

Data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26. Independent categorical variables such as gender, developmental status, comorbidities, respiratory support, and pharmacological interventions were reported as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables, including age and RASS scores, were presented as mean (±SD) or median (IQR) following normality assessment. Post-stratification analysis was performed using the Chi-square test to evaluate the effects of potential modifiers. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 201 critically ill children included, delirium was identified in 24.9% (n = 50/201). The mean age was 5.14 ± 4.03 years (95% CI: 4.58–5.70), with 61.7% male (n = 124/201) and 38.3% female (n = 77/201). The mean duration of illness was 20.36 ± 35.77 days (95% CI: 15.38–25.33). Developmental delay was noted in 1.5% (n = 3/201), and comorbidities were present in 16.9% (n = 34/201). Multivariate analysis revealed several significant risk factors for delirium. Comorbidities were associated with increased risk (OR: 3.45, 95% CI: 1.78–6.70, p = 0.0005), as was developmental delay (OR: 5.12, 95% CI: 1.34–19.54, p = 0.015). Among pharmacological factors, benzodiazepine use (n = 45) was linked to higher delirium risk (OR: 2.89, 95% CI: 1.52–5.50, p = 0.001), as was antiepileptic use (n = 38, OR: 2.67, 95% CI: 1.38–5.17, p = 0.004). Respiratory support measures also showed associations: High-flow oxygen (n = 60) had an OR of 2.31 (95% CI: 1.20–4.45, p = 0.012), and mechanical ventilation (n = 55) had an OR of 3.12 (95% CI: 1.62–6.01, p = 0.001).

Stratification by gender was performed to explore potential differences in delirium risk related to respiratory support, as gender may influence physiological responses or treatment patterns. Age, duration of illness, developmental level, and comorbidities were also stratified in Table 1, showing no significant association with age or illness duration. However, developmental delay (p = 0.01) and comorbidities (p = 0.0005) were significantly more common in delirious children. The statistical analysis with respiratory support and pharmacological therapy is shown in Table 2, which denotes that delirious children were more likely to receive antiepileptics and benzodiazepines. Corticosteroids and opioids were more common in nondelirious cases. Oxygen therapy type also differed significantly (p = 0.006), with low-flow nasal cannula (LFNC) and high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) used more in delirious children, whereas no significant gender-based differences were found in the association between respiratory support and delirium (p = 0.32 for high-flow oxygen, p = 0.41 for mechanical ventilation). Discussion

This study found a 24.9% prevalence of delirium in 201 critically ill children, with significant risk factors, including comorbidities, developmental delay, benzodiazepine and antiepileptic use, and advanced respiratory support. These findings align with international studies reporting 20–30% delirium prevalence in PICU settings, though higher rates (49–57%) were noted in pediatric cardiac patients and lower rates (10–18.8%) in pediatric cancer patients. In contrast, our study observed a higher delirium incidence among cancer patients (58%, n = 29/50), possibly due to intensive treatments or underlying disease severity in our cohort.

The CAPD tool facilitated rapid bedside screening, proving feasible in a low-resource setting. However, its application in children with developmental delay (n = 3) may overestimate delirium prevalence due to the tool's limited adaptation for altered cognitive baselines, as only 1.5% of our sample had developmental delay, limiting statistical power. A dedicated study in this population is warranted. Compared to interactive tools like pCAM-ICU and psCAM-ICU, CAPD's observational nature suits broader age ranges and nonspecialist staff but may miss nuanced symptoms in complex cases.24–28

Risk factors such as benzodiazepines and mechanical ventilation align with prior studies, reflecting their impact on cerebral metabolism and neurotransmission. Our multivariate analysis strengthens these associations by adjusting for confounders, unlike some studies with smaller samples (n = 56) or retrospective designs. The higher delirium rate in cancer patients in our study contrasts with lower rates (10–18.8%) in similar populations elsewhere, possibly due to differences in treatment protocols or patient acuity.

Routine screening with CAPD could enhance early delirium detection, particularly in low-resource settings where DSM-V assessments are impractical. Multidisciplinary approaches, including family engagement, may further improve outcomes. Future research should focus on multicenter validation and tailored interventions for high-risk groups.

Conclusion

Delirium affected nearly one-quarter of critically ill children, with key risk factors including comorbidities, developmental delay, sedative use, and advanced respiratory support. Routine CAPD screening is feasible and effective for early detection, enabling timely intervention to reduce hospital stay and improve outcomes. Multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and optimize prevention strategies.

Limitations

The single-observer design introduces potential bias in CAPD scoring, despite training to ensure consistency. The small number of patients with developmental delay (n = 3) limits conclusions about its role as a risk factor. The study did not assess outcomes such as mortality, long-term cognitive effects, or post-discharge sequelae due to scope constraints. Management strategies, including pharmacotherapy and family involvement, were not evaluated. Multicenter studies with larger, diverse cohorts are needed to address these gaps.
