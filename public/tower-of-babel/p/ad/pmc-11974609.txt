Gelatin methacryloyl biomaterials and strategies for trophoblast research
Authors: Samantha G. Zambuto, Samyuktha S. Kolluru, Brendan A.C. Harley, Michelle L. Oyen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Rising maternal mortality rates in the U.S. are a significant public health issue that must be addressed; however, much of the basic science information required to target pregnancy-related pathologies have not yet been defined. Placental and blastocyst implantation research are challenging to perform in humans because of the early time frame of these processes in pregnancy and limited access to first trimester tissues. As a result, there is a critical need to develop model systems capable of studying these processes in increasing mechanistic detail. With the recent passing of the FDA Modernization Act 2.0 and advances in tissue engineering methods, three-dimensional microphysiological model systems offer an exciting opportunity to model early stages of placentation. Here, we detail the synthesis, characterization, and application of gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel platforms for studying trophoblast behavior in three-dimensional hydrogel systems. Photopolymerization strategies to fabricate GelMA hydrogels render the hydrogels homogeneous in terms of structure and stable under physiological temperatures, allowing for rigorous fabrication of reproducible hydrogel variants. Unlike other natural polymers that have minimal opportunity to tune their properties, GelMA hydrogel properties can be tuned across many axes of variation, including polymer degree of functionalization, gelatin bloom strength, light exposure time and intensity, polymer weight percent, photoinitiator concentration, and physical geometry. In this work, we aim to inspire and instruct the field to utilize GelMA biomaterial strategies for future placental research. With enhanced microphysiological models of pregnancy, we can now generate the basic science information required to address problems in pregnancy.

Introduction

High maternal mortality rates represent an acute challenge to global and national health [1]. In 2015, the United Nations Members States defined 17 Sustainable Development Goals designed to target important global issues. Goal 3 focuses on Good Health and Well-Being and includes the goal of a reduced global rate of maternal morality to 70 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births by 2030 [1]. As of 2020, the global maternal mortality rate is 223 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births and the maternal mortality rate has stagnated in all regions except central and south Asia and Australia and New Zealand [1]. Tragically, most pregnancy-related deaths are preventable [1]. Reducing maternal mortality and pregnancy-related complications requires a multifactorial, interdisciplinary approach. While expansion of healthcare systems is crucial for reducing inequities in maternal and postpartum care, the generation of crucial basic science information on pregnancy and pregnancy-complications is also required to develop advanced therapeutics and technology that can aid in that process.

Pregnancy is often described as a “black box” because implantation, the initial blastocyst attachment to the uterus, has never been observed in humans [2,3]. Implantation occurs approximately 6–12 days post-conception, rendering it impossible to currently study in the body [3]. Animal models allow us to study implantation in real time; however, significant differences between animal and human pregnancy (e.g., anatomy, physiology, number of offspring, etc.) call into question the use of animals to infer information on human pregnancy [4–8]. Developing physiologically relevant models of pregnancy will allow us to better understand the mechanisms of pregnancy so we can address potential causes of maternal mortality in humans.

Microphysiological models of the uteroplacental interface offer the opportunity to study processes of trophoblast invasion and placentation that are intractable or unethical to study in humans. The FDA defines a microphysiological system as a “microscale cell culture platform for in vitro modeling of functional features of a specific tissue or organ of human or animal origin by exposing cells to a microenvironment that mimics the physiological aspects important for their function or pathophysiological condition [9]. This definition includes “mono-cultures, co-cultures of multiple cell types, maintenance of explants derived from tissues/organs, and/or inclusion of organoid cell formation” which capture a variety of tissue engineered constructs including biomaterial-based model systems [9].

With the recent passing of the FDA Modernization Act 2.0 in 2022, establishing the rigor and fidelity of microphysiological models of pregnancy is essential. The FDA Modernization Act 2.0 amended Section 505 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (21 U S C. 355) by replacing “preclinical tests” with “nonclinical tests” [9]. Nonclinical tests are defined by the bill as tests “conduced in vitro, in silico, or in chemico, or a non-human in vivo test that occurs before or during the clinical trial phase of the investigation of the safety and effectiveness of a drug, and may include animal tests, or non-animal or human biology-based test methods, such as cell-based assays, microphysiological systems, or bioprinted or computer models” [9]. This shift is particularly significant given that there are no conventional animal models that replicate the uniqueness of human pregnancy, mainly due to significant physiological and anatomical differences between humans and other species [4,5,7,8]. Hence, as we build microphysiological systems of increasing complexity, it is critical to rigorously model the uterus, placenta, and uteroplacental interface to create accurate and precise models of human pregnancy.

Original microphysiological systems have often focused on design variables such as inclusion of relevant cell types, with a focus on the cellular signals (e.g., secreted cytokines) generated by these systems. However, native tissues display significant heterogeneity, including spatial, temporal, and hierarchical patterns of cell, matrix, and biological signals (e.g., cytokines, hormones, extracellular vesicles). Hence, three-dimensional (3D) biomaterial matrix systems provide the opportunity for microphysiological systems to increasingly replicate additional essential features of the three-dimensional tissue architecture, including spatiotemporal heterogeneity [10]. Eventually, they may offer the opportunity to create enriched culture systems for long-term culture of heterogeneous populations of cells in environments that replicate the material, mechanical, and biological properties of native tissues.

Models of the uteroplacental interface benefit from sophisticated tissue engineering techniques. This dynamic interface between the uterus and placenta is established upon implantation of the blastocyst and continues to remodel and develop over the course of pregnancy, resulting in the formation of the placenta [3,11–13]. The development of in vitro models of the uteroplacental interface will allow for mechanistic studies of early pregnancy and placentation that are currently intractable to perform in humans. An emerging field at the intersection of bioengineering and reproductive biology has pioneered a variety of techniques to create three-dimensional in vitro models of the uteroplacental interface using engineered microphysiological systems [14–17]. For more of a generalized overview of biomaterials used for placental research, we invite the reader to read our recent review on biomaterial approaches for modeling the placenta [18]. For specific studies using gelatin-based biomaterials for modeling the uteroplacental interface, we invite the reader to explore our collective research endeavors that have confirmed that gelatin-based biomaterial systems replicate a variety of key features of the uteroplacental interface, including endometrial and decidual material properties, the endometrial microvasculature, epithelium, and dynamic hormone-responsiveness, and dynamic trophoblast motility patterns [19–26].

In this work, we further explore gelatin-based biomaterials in pregnancy research. The focus of this expert opinion is to highlight the strengths, limitations, current applications, and future opportunities of gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels as biomimetic biomaterial platforms for modeling the human uteroplacental interface. Our objective is to emphasize why GelMA biomaterials provide unique advantages for trophoblast research and to provide detailed instruction on how to synthesize GelMA macromers, fabricate GelMA hydrogels, and perform advanced cell-based assays in GelMA hydrogels. We hope this work will encourage researchers to employ GelMA hydrogel systems in future scientific endeavors related to advancing trophoblast research.

Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA)

Collagen at the uteroplacental interface

Collagen-based biomaterials are highly relevant to placental research due to the presence of collagens at the uteroplacental interface [27–29]. Collagens type I, III, IV, V, and VI are found in the endometrium (the lining of the uterus and site of blastocyst implantation) and decidua and the levels and ratios of these collagens fluctuate over the menstrual cycle and during pregnancy [27–29]. Despite the presence of collagens at the uteroplacental interface, collagen hydrogels are not ideal candidates for biomaterial models. Collagen gels are convenient and biocompatible; however, their major limitation involves the inability to tune hydrogel properties beyond a narrow range [30]. Another major limitation of collagen gels includes instability related to factors such as temperature or presence of enzymes [31]. Collagen gels can be stabilized via crosslinking through a variety of techniques, including chemical (e.g., glutaraldehyde, genipin), physical (e.g., temperature, UV light), and enzymatic crosslinking methods (e.g., microbial transglutaminase); however, some of these techniques can be cytotoxic [31].

Gelatin-based biomaterials

Another natural polymer commonly used in biomedical research is gelatin. Gelatin is a soluble polypeptide mixture of denatured and hydrolyzed collagen that can be derived from a variety of animal tissues, including bones, tendons, and skin (Fig. 1A–B) [32,33]. Although gelatin is mostly derived from collagen types I and III, it can also contain other collagen types [27–29]. Gelatin type A and B are produced through the partial hydrolysis of collagen (Fig. 1B) [34]. Type A gelatin is produced through partial acid hydrolysis of collagen and is commonly derived from porcine, piscine, or avine sources [34]. Type B gelatin is produced through partial alkaline hydrolysis of collagen and is derived from bovine sources [34]. Compared to type A gelatin, type B gelatin contains approximately 65 % more aspartic acid and glutamic acid which results in an increased number of negative charges and has an isoelectric point at pH 5 compared to pH 8.5 for type A [34]. Type A porcine and type B bovine are commonly used in biomedical research applications. Interestingly, piscine derived gelatin contains lower amounts of imino acids and higher amounts of serine and threonine compared to mammalian gelatin, resulting in a lower gelling-melting point and lower gel strength [34].

Many of the strengths of collagen, including convenience, low cost, and biocompatibility, are retained by gelatin. Cells can attach to gelatin via integrin cell binding (RGD: Arg-Gly-Asp) motifs and can remodel gelatin through matrix metalloproteinase sensitive degradation sites [10]. In contrast to collagen which can cause antigenic and immunogenic responses in vivo due to aromatic groups in the collagen tertiary structure, denaturation of the collagen tertiary structure in gelatin does not instigate antigenic and immunogenic responses [35–37].

Although it is possible to generate gelatin hydrogels in a similar process as collagen hydrogels, a major limitation of non-functionalized gelatin is that it is liquid at physiological temperatures due to its low melting point and requires cooling (<20 °C) for crosslinking [38]. Gelatin undergoes physical crosslinking via a coil-helix transition and these physical crosslinks are sensitive to a variety of factors, including temperature, pH, and ionic content [39]. As a result, gelatin hydrogels are thermoreversible and unstable in 37 °C cell culture conditions [39]. To overcome this limitation, chemical crosslinking procedures using reagents such as glutaraldehyde, diisocyantes, carbodiimides, polyepoxy compounds, and acyl azides can be used to stabilize the gelatin hydrogels [39]. Chemical cross-linking of the large number of functional side groups on the gelatin macromers thus enable some tuning of the physiochemical properties of the resultant gelatin hydrogel. However, many of these crosslinking agents are toxic to cells and can induce inflammatory or immunogenic responses, rendering them unideal candidates for stabilizing gelatin hydrogels [38].

Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) synthesis and hydrogel fabrication

Another option for stabilizing gelatin hydrogels involves chemical modification of gelatin macromer itself. Gelatin can also be chemically modified with a variety of reagents, including maleimide [40], ferulic acid [41], and hydroxyphenyl-propionic acid [41].

In 2000, Van Den Bulcke et al. described chemical modification of gelatin using methacrylic anhydride [39]. In this procedure, gelatin is reacted with methacrylic anhydride to produce gelatin methacryloyl which contains a mixture of methacrylamide and methacrylate groups (Fig. 3A). Over a decade later in 2016, Shirahama et al. created a one pot synthesis method, thus simplifying the synthesis procedure and enhancing the controllability of the reaction itself through pH [37].

GelMA can be used to fabricate hydrogels through crosslinking methods, including redox, thermal, and photopolymerization [38,39]. GelMA forms covalent crosslinks via photopolymerization, allowing for milder crosslinking conditions that result in higher viability in encapsulated cells [32]. GelMA can be photopolymerized with ultraviolet (UV) light, visible light, near-infrared light, or lasers and using a variety of photoinitiators (e.g., lithium acylphosphinate (LAP), Irgacure 2959, VA-086, eosin Y, ruthenium and sodium persulfate (Ru/SPS)) [38, 42–44]. We, and others, prefer LAP for UV polymerization because of its excellent curing efficiency that allows for short polymerization times, higher crosslinking efficiency, and lower required concentrations (e.g., 0.1 %) [42,45]. Rapid UV polymerization results in chemical cross-linking of GelMA with little to no physical crosslinking because these systems require long induction periods in order to form structures via helix formation [39]. Physical network formation in GelMA mostly occurs if storing hydrogels at 4 °C after UV curing [39]. The Ru/SPS system for crosslinking with visible light has been demonstrated to have a less adverse effect on viability and metabolic activity of encapsulated cells, higher glycosaminoglycan content and re-differentiation capacity, and greater light penetration compared to LAP and Irgacure [43,44]. A deeper discussion on photocrosslinking parameters and their effects on GelMA hydrogel properties can be found in the following manuscripts [46,47].

Gelatin methacryloyl strengths and limitations

Through the introduction of methacrylamide and methacrylate groups, GelMA mitigates some key limitations of native gelatin (e.g., thermally sensitive, reversible gelation) while also maintaining many benefits (e.g., native degradation and adhesion sites) relative to fully synthetic hydrogels such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) materials that require chemical modification to incorporate cell binding and degradation motifs. The ability to use photopolymerization to fabricate GelMA hydrogels renders the hydrogels relatively homogeneous in terms of structure as well as stable under physiological temperatures unlike gelatin. Together, these changes represent a route for tunable, reproducible hydrogel properties. This is significantly different from other natural polymers such as Matrigel that have minimal tunability and significant batch-to-batch heterogeneity [48,49]. GelMA itself and its resultant hydrogel properties can be tuned through a variety of parameters, including polymer degree of functionalization, gelatin bloom strength, UV exposure time and intensity, polymer weight percent, photoinitiator concentration, and geometry [24] (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

GelMA hydrogels can be further modified via a range of chemistry and materials fabrication techniques (Fig. 3). For example, GelMA hydrogels can be enzymatically coated with relevant ECM biomolecules through enzymatic crosslinking techniques (e.g., microbial transglutaminase) [23] or combined with other biomaterials to create composite hydrogel systems with unique material and mechanical properties (Fig. 3B) [10,50–55]. Methacrylated materials such as hyaluronic acid methacrylate, gellan gum methacrylate, dextran methacrylate, and chondroitin sulfate methacrylate, can readily combined with GelMA through photopolymerization [38,56–64]. Other natural and synthetic polymers such as alginate, silk fibroin, PEG, polyacrylamide, polyaniline, polycaprolactone, can be blended with GelMA to reinforce hydrogel mechanical properties [38]. Combining GelMA with organic nanomaterials (e.g., carbon nanotubes, graphene oxide), inorganic nanomaterials (e.g., silicate, metal, ceramic), or nanoparticles (e.g., liposomes, poly(glycerol sebacate), polycaprolactone), can provide unique electrical and magnetic properties within the composite systems that can be used for advanced bioengineering applications, including medical electronics such as actuators, soft robotics, and drug delivery vehicles [38]. GelMA can also be combined with electrospun fibers or nanofibers to create fiber-reinforced composites with enhanced mechanical properties and unique fiber nanostructures [38].

GelMA hydrogels replicate the microenvironment of many tissue systems, rendering GelMA an excellent choice of biomaterial for numerous applications. In the literature, we have found GelMA hydrogel variants ranging in stiffness from less than 1 kPa to over 1 MPa, which captures a wide range of properties applicable to many tissue and organ systems. We have demonstrated success creating GelMA hydrogel variants for modeling other tissue microenvironments, including glioblastoma [56,57,62–67] and the hematopoietic stem cell niche [40,61, 68–70]. For uterine modeling in particular, GelMA is an excellent biomaterial choice. GelMA hydrogels replicate material properties of the uterus, including the endometrium and decidua (pregnant endometrium), and placenta [18].

Fodera et al. measured the material properties of the uterus, including the endometrium, myometrium, and perimetrium in tissue from non-pregnant patients and third trimester tissue from pregnant patients who underwent cesarean sections and patients with placenta accreta [71]. Using the same nanoindenter model and characterization method as Fodera et al. (Optics11Life, Piuma), we characterized GelMA hydrogel material properties using spherical nanoindentation [24]. We demonstrate that GelMA hydrogel properties are similar to endometrial and decidual material properties, including stiffness and viscoelastic ratio (Table 1) [24,71,72]. Fodera et al. determined that overall uterine stiffness ranged from 102 – 103 Pa and stiffness differed by uterine layer and by pregnancy status [71]. Our GelMA hydrogels fall within the stiffness regime of 102 and can be tuned to replicate stiffer endometrial states, such as pregnancy [24]. Viscoelastic ratio describes the ratio between the equilibrium and instantaneous elastic moduli and describes a material’s degree of viscoelasticity. A viscous fluid has a viscoelastic ratio of 0 compared to an elastic solid which has a viscoelastic ratio of 1. The viscoelastic ratio of all uterine layers was between 0.4 and 0.6 and the viscoelastic ratio of GelMA hydrogels was also within this range [24, 71]. Permeability, the motion of molecules through a material due to physical pore geometry, ranged from 10−17 – 10−15 m2 for all uterine layers and 10−17 for GelMA hydrogels [24,71]. Finally, diffusivity, the motion of molecules through a material over time, ranged from 10−12 – 10−8 m2s−1 and 10−12 for GelMA hydrogels [24,71]. These data are consistent with the findings of Abbas et al. who determined that the decidual basalis was an order of magnitude (103 Pa) stiffer than the decidua parietalis (102 Pa) [72].

A limitation of GelMA is that the immediate surface properties of the hydrogel are highly dependent on the substrate on which they are crosslinked which affects hydrogel properties such as swelling ratio and mechanical properties [32]; however, this limitation. can be mitigated by making thicker hydrogels ≥2 mm [32] Another limitation includes weak mechanical strength; however, advanced biofabrication techniques [10], including electrospinning, 3D bioprinting [42], bio-textile, photolithography, micromolding, microfluidics, and microsphere technology, allow for more complex hydrogel structures with enhanced mechanical properties such as the addition of shear stresses. Additional limitations of GelMA hydrogels include more complex synthesis and hydrogel fabrication procedures that require more specialized equipment and chemistry skills. However, we seek to overcome these limitations by providing detailed experimental protocols and procedures in the subsequent section.

Protocols and procedures

In the Supplemental Information, we provide five detailed protocols on GelMA synthesis, GelMA hydrogel fabrication (acellular, fiber-reinforced hydrogel composites, cell-embedded, spheroid embedded), an extracellular matrix coating technique for GelMA hydrogels, a three-dimensional immunofluorescent staining protocol for GelMA hydrogels, and a nascent protein staining assay. Here, we will provide a brief discussion on these protocols and guide the reader to additional resources.

Based on our experience, synthesizing GelMA in-house is superior to purchasing commercially available GelMA. The synthesis protocol is simple, relatively short (1–2 weeks), and the resultant GelMA is consistent in terms of degree of functionalization, material properties, mechanical properties, and cellular response. Commercially available GelMA is expensive and sometimes can polymerize too quickly, resulting in difficult experimental procedures. In the Supplemental Information, we describe the one pot GelMA synthesis method developed by Shirahama et al. [37] and provide additional insight to aid experimental rigor based on our over a decade of experience working with gelatin-based biomaterials.

Next, we share protocols for creating acellular, fiber-reinforced hydrogel composites, cell-laden, and spheroid-laden GelMA hydrogels. Fiber-reinforced hydrogel composites are of particular interest because they can recapitulate fiber orientation which is relevant for mimicking anisotropic orientation and mechanical properties in the uterus [73]. GelMA hydrogels are versatile for cell culture, and cells can be readily encapsulated in the hydrogels, seeded on top of the hydrogels, or both. Finally, we share two protocols for staining cells within hydrogels: three-dimensional immunofluorescent staining and nascent protein staining. With these protocols, researchers will be able to synthesize GelMA macromers, fabricate GelMA hydrogels, culture cells using these hydrogels, and stain the cells in the hydrogels.

Gelatin methacryloyl applications for trophoblast research

In this section, we direct the reader to various publications that use GelMA hydrogels for trophoblast research. We as well as others have used GelMA as biomimetic materials for modeling trophoblast motility, trophoblast invasion, placentation, and uteroplacental interfacing (Fig. 3C) [19–26,45,74–77]. Table 2 summarizes recent work in GelMA hydrogels. These studies employ a variety of 3D cell culture and biomaterial techniques, including spheroid and multi-cellular cultures and 3D printing.

All the cited studies utilized porcine derived gelatin and majority were high (300) bloom. The degree of functionalization of the GelMA macromers ranged from 30 % to 66 %. Low concentrations (0.1 %–0.2 % w/v) of photoinitiators (LAP or Irgacure 2959) were used to polymerize the hydrogels, although LAP was the most popular selection. For hydrogel fabrication, 3 wt% - 10 wt% concentrations of GelMA were used in the studies: majority of the studies employed a 5 wt% concentration. All reported light sources were UV with most studies reporting the wavelength (λ) as 365 nm. Light exposure time ranged from 30 s to 5 min with 30 s as the most reported polymerization time and 0.09–10 mW cm−2 as the light intensity range. Distance from irradiation light source was not reported; however, it is important to keep a consistent distance between the sample and light source. Measured stiffness values ranged from approximately 100 Pa to 24 kPa. Nonetheless, this range of stiffness recapitulates endometrial and decidual stiffness [78]. We refer the reader to the following references [79–82] for detailed discussion on characterization of hydrogels. Based on our analysis of the compiled studies, the recommended material properties are as follows: ~50 % degree of functionalization GelMA synthesized from 300 bloom porcine gelatin and 5 wt% GelMA hydrogels polymerized with 0.1 % w/v LAP.

Because GelMA can sustain culture of a variety of cell types, most placental and uterine cells can be cultured using these hydrogel systems. The cited studies cultured a variety of trophoblast and endometrial cell sources, including immortalized cell lines (HTR-8/SVneo, BeWo, Swan71, Ishikawa, hTERT immortalized endometrial stromal cells) and primary cells (endometrial and umbilical vein endothelial cells, endometrial epithelial cells). The cited studies report that their GelMA systems recapitulate a variety of key trophoblast and endometrial properties, including trophoblast motility, responsiveness to soluble factors, trophoblast interactions with vasculature, endometrial hormonal response, and native tissue architecture and stiffness. We caution against problematic cell lines such as HTR-8/SVneo [83] which has been shown to contain a mixed population of cells, and we direct the reader to use more widely used trophoblast sources including the Okae et al. trophoblast stem cells [84]. Future studies can also consider advanced stem cell and blastoid/gastruloid models to study placentation and embryonic development [85,86].

Outlook

In this work, we detail the synthesis, characterization, and application of GelMA hydrogel platforms for trophoblast research in three-dimensional hydrogel systems.

GelMA retains the same benefits as native gelatin but adds the capacity to use photopolymerization methods to fabricate compositionally and structurally homogenous hydrogels that remain stable under physiological temperatures through covalent crosslinking. Unlike other natural polymers or extracellular matrix molecules (e.g., collagen, Matrigel) that have minimal tunability, GelMA hydrogel properties can be tuned through a variety of parameters, including polymer degree of functionalization, gelatin bloom strength, light source exposure time and intensity, polymer weight percent, photoinitiator concentration and geometry. Although we highlight the strengths and benefits of using GelMA to model trophoblast behavior and uteroplacental interfacing, GelMA shows promise for other systems in the female reproductive system and body, including the vagina and breast [87].

Looking toward the future with GelMA hydrogel systems, advanced biomedical engineering technologies can be employed to create microphysiological models of pregnancy with significantly increased complexity. For example, GelMA can be polymerized in microfluidic devices which will add additional biological signals to the system, including shear stress from fluid flow. Microfluidic devices have previously been used in trophoblast research [88]; however, future studies would benefit from utilizing highly tunable GelMA-based materials to control more of the physical properties of these microfluidic systems. Furthermore, combining GelMA hydrogels with other materials can enhance the 3D architecture. For example, fiber-reinforced composite hydrogels are a major improvement in hydrogel technology because they more closely mimic the native extracellular matrix by recapitulating collagen fiber architecture and alignment.

With the recent passing of the FDA Modernization Act 2.0 in 2022, establishing the rigor and fidelity of microphysiological models of pregnancy is important and essential. In this work, we hope we inspired and instructed the field to utilize GelMA microphysiological systems to study early pregnancy. Now, with advanced model systems, we can begin generating important data on early pregnancy to target, prevent, and cure pregnancy-related problems to improve healthcare for mothers, babies, and women across the globe. In conclusion, GelMA is a valuable biomaterial for trophoblast research.
