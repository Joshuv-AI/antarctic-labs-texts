Identification of natural FGFR3 inhibitor for glioma using integrated computational and microRNA regulatory analysis
Authors: Shehla Javaid, Nouman Ali, Samiah Shahid, Muhamamd Nouman Majeed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma is an aggressive and treatment-resistant brain tumor with poor prognosis, frequently driven by aberrant activation of receptor tyrosine kinases such as fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3). Genetic alterations including FGFR3–TACC3 fusions and post-transcriptional deregulation mediated by tumor-suppressive microRNAs (miR-99a and miR-100) contribute to sustained FGFR3 signaling, glioma progression, and therapeutic resistance. In this study, a structure-based computational pipeline was employed to identify natural inhibitors targeting FGFR3. A phytochemical library comprising 25 plant-derived compounds was screened using ADME criteria, resulting in seven candidates with favorable gastrointestinal absorption and drug-likeness. Among them, Guggulsterone emerged as the top-ranked compound, exhibiting the highest docking affinity (− 10.1 kcal/mol) toward the FGFR3 kinase domain and forming stable hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions with key active-site residues. The FGFR3 structure used showed high stereochemical quality (ERRAT score: 96.5; 93% residues in favored Ramachandran regions). Molecular dynamics simulations (500 ns) demonstrated stable complex formation, with RMSD convergence after 40 ns, low RMSF values, and consistent radius of gyration (1.96–2.04 nm). Persistent intermolecular hydrogen bonds (two to four) and stable solvent accessibility were observed throughout the simulation. MMGBSA binding free energy calculations predicted a favorable interaction (ΔGtotal = − 30.44 kcal/mol), primarily driven by van der Waals and electrostatic contributions. Pharmacophore analysis revealed two hydrogen bond acceptors and seven hydrophobic features, while density functional theory calculations indicated moderate chemical reactivity (HOMO–LUMO gap: 0.1797 a.u.). Toxicity assessment using ProTox 3.0 classified Guggulsterone as low-toxic (LD50 = 2300 mg/kg, Class 5). Collectively, these in-silico findings suggest Guggulsterone as a promising natural FGFR3-binding scaffold warranting further experimental validation in FGFR3-driven glioma models.

Introduction

Gliomas are among the most aggressive and lethal intracranial neoplasms in the adult population and glioblastoma multiforme (GBM, WHO grade IV) is the most prevalent malignant subset. GBM alone is responsible for approximately half of all primary central nervous system (CNS) malignancies with an incidence of about 4 per 100,000 person-years1. Even with multimodal treatment, outcomes in GBM are dismal; median overall survival is limited to about 15–18 months2. Patients unavoidably develop recurrence and therapeutic failure, highlighting the need for new therapeutic strategies novel therapeutic strategies1,3,4. Clinically, gliomas are often associated with symptoms related to focal cerebral invasion and increased intracranial pressure. Seizures, headache that worsens and neurologic deficits (e.g., cognitive impairment, motor weakness, dysarthria, or visual disturbances) are common initial manifestations. Seizures, headache that is progressive, and neurologic deficits (e.g., cognitive impairment, motor weakness, dysarthria, or visual disturbances) are common initial manifestations5. The current standard of care for high grade glioma consists of maximal surgical resection with adjuvant radiotherapy with concomitant and subsequent temozolomide chemotherapy1,6. Nevertheless, even with this aggressive approach, glioma invariably recurs and the prognosis remains poor, prompting exploration of novel targets and treatment modalities.

One promising new molecular target in glioma is the fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3). FGFR3 is a receptor tyrosine kinase which, when bound to its ligands FGF, dimerizes and becomes auto-phosphorylated to activate downstream signaling pathways and regulate cellular proliferation, survival, migration and angiogenesis of cells in the body1. Genetic alterations in FGFR family members have been implicated in oncogenesis, and indeed approximately 8% of gliomas harbor FGFR aberrations most frequently involving FGFR1 and FGFR31. In the case of glioblastomas, FGFR3 seems to have a pro-tumor effect: FGFR3 is frequently highly expressed in invasive GBM cells, which may indicate a role in infiltrative growth1. Moreover, a subset of gliomas (particularly IDH-wildtype GBM) contains the oncogenic fusion of FGFR3 and TACC3, which is a chromosomal rearrangement that produces a constitutively active FGFR3 kinase. The FGFR3-TACC3 fusion causes uncontrolled FGF signaling and glioma cell growth1. Owing to these observations, FGFR3 is suggested to be a promising therapeutic target, since aberrant activation of FGFR3 may increase glioma progression and inhibition of FGFR signaling has shown efficacy in preclinical models1,7. FGFR inhibitors (for example, small molecules like erdafitinib or pemigatinib) are being studied to shut off this pathway in FGFR-altered tumors1. Targeting FGFR3 could therefore directly reduce tumor cell growth and invasiveness, in patients whose gliomas are reliant on aberrant FGF signaling.

Although FGFR inhibitors have been explored in oncology, most reported inhibitors belong to synthetic tyrosine kinase inhibitor classes, and comparatively few studies have examined natural phytochemicals as potential FGFR3-targeting compounds8,9. In glioma, aberrant FGFR3 signaling, including FGFR3–TACC3 fusion events, contributes to oncogenic activation of downstream pathways such as MAPK and PI3K/AKT, making FGFR3 an attractive therapeutic target10,11. Despite the well-documented anticancer activity of several phytochemicals, their potential to interact directly with the FGFR3 kinase domain has not been extensively explored12. In particular, although Guggulsterone has been reported to modulate multiple oncogenic signaling pathways in different cancers, including NF-κB and PI3K/AKT signaling, its direct interaction with FGFR3 has not previously been investigated to our knowledge13. Therefore, the present study focuses on exploring natural phytochemicals capable of interacting with the FGFR3 catalytic pocket using an integrated computational framework.

Noteworthy, FGFR3 activity in glioma is regulated by non-coding RNAs, in this case microRNAs (miRNAs), which adds an extra layer of regulatory complexity. Under normal physiological conditions, some miRNAs help to keep FGFR3 expression in check14. For example, miR-99a, a brain-enriched tumor suppressor miRNA, binds to the 3’-untranslated region (3’-UTR) of FGFR3 mRNA and blocks its translation. The FGFR3-TACC3 fusion transcript, however, lacks the 3’ UTI (where miR-99a binds) of FGFR3, which then escapes the inhibitory effect of miR-99a and leads to uncontrolled FGFR3 expression, significantly promoting tumor growth38. This mechanism is partly responsible for why FGFR3 fusions are potent oncogenic glioma drivers. In line with the significance of miRNA regulation, miR-100 (a member of miR-99/100 family against FGFR3) is observed to be significantly downregulated in GBM tumors and cell lines15. Restoration of miR-100 levels has tumor suppressive effects: overexpression of miR-100 in GBM cells significantly suppresses their proliferation and migration and even increases their chemosensitivity to alkylating agents15. Crucially, FGFR3 was confirmed to be a direct target of miR-100 in these cells - forced overexpression of FGFR3 can rescue glioma cells from the growth-arrest and apoptosis induced by miR-100, confirming that the anti-tumor effects of miR-100 are, for the most part, mediated via FGFR3 down-regulation15. Thus, myth expression of miRNAs that normally inhibit FGR3 (by way of genomic fusions that deleting miRNA binding sites or by epigenetic silencing of miRNAs) can result in aberrant FGR3 activity and glioma genesis. These insights highlight a critical FGFR3–miRNA axis in glioma and indicate that the restoration or mimicry of such tumor suppressors miRNAs may represent a therapeutic avenue for the indirect targeting of the FGFR3 pathway.

Parallel to these molecular advances, there is a growing interest in medicinal plants as the sources of novel anti- glioma agents. Natural products have been historically pivotal in drug discovery processes, including for oncology, due to their enormous chemical diversity and bioactivity. Indeed, many frontline anticancer drugs (such as paclitaxel, vinca alkaloids and irinotecan) were isolated from plant secondary metabolites. Medicinal plants have been used in traditional medicine, for thousands of years, to treat numerous ailments including tumors, and modern-day science is confirming many of their therapeutic properties16. Commiphora wightii (goggul plant) is one such medicinal herb, which has been highly valued in Ayurveda and other medicinal systems for its health benefits. Extracts of Commiphora resin (“guggul”) are known to have anti- inflammatory, antioxidant, hypolipidemic and even antitumor effects, have been attributed mainly to its active phytosterol component Guggulsterone (GS)16. Guggulsterone is found in the forms of Z- and E-steriodenomers and has become a subject of interest for its wide spectrum of pharmacologic activity in cancer models. In-vitro and in-vivo experiments over recent years have shown that Guggulsterone has the ability to inhibit cancer cell growth and cause apoptosis in various cancers16. Mechanistically, Guggulsterone is quite pleiotropic: reports indicate inhibition of various pro oncogenic signaling pathways (NF-xkB, MAPK/ERK, PI3K/Akt and JAK/STAT) along with manipulation of expression of many key regulators of cell proliferation/survival (such as Cyclin D1, c-Myc, survivin, caspase proteins, anti-apoptotic proteins of the Bcl-2 family)16. By targeting these signaling hubs, Guggulsterone is able to induce cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis in cancer cells and also disrupt processes such as angiogenesis, invasion and metastasis16. Importantly, Guggulsterone’s anticancer activity is not limited to cell culture, and it has demonstrated anticancer activity in animal tumor models and was found to worsen the efficacy of some standard therapies.

In the case of glioma, more recently, several studies have described that Guggulsterone may have chemo-sensization properties. Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is notoriously resistant to chemotherapy (e.g. temozolomide, TMZ) partly attributed to constitutive activation of survival pathways, such as the EGF/PI3K/Akt pathway and the transcription factor NF-kappaB. A 2023 in-vitro and in-vivo study showed significant potentiating effects of Guggulsterone on the anti-glioblastoma efficacy of temozolomide due to down-regulation of the epsilonrykanase receptor (EGFR)/protein kinase 3 (PI3K)/protein kinase A (Akt) signaling pathway and inhibition of mitogen-activated gene expression (NF-kB) activation17. In cultured GBM cells (U87 and U251) Guggulsterone when used with TMZ led to better growth inhibition and apoptosis than TMZ alone, which was accompanied by a decrease in phosphorylated Akk along with a decrease in the expression of genes of the nuclear factor (NF)-kB pathway17. Furthermore, using an orthotopic mouse model of GBM, the addition of Guggulsterone greatly improved the TMZ induced tumor growth delay, and tumor analyses showed that PI3K/Akt/NFotted ones signaling pathways were attenuated in the combination treated group17. Not only are these results important to show that Guggulsterone could be used as an anticancer, but they also hint at a rational approach of using Guggulsterone to overcome therapeutic resistance in glioma by inhibiting key survival signals. Given the fact that FGFR3 signaling converges on many of the same pathways (e.g. MAPK, PI3K-Akt) that Guggulsterone can inhibit, it is intriguing to propose Guggulsterone or its analogues as natural compounds that might directly or indirectly target the FGFR3-driven network in glioma cells16,17.

To accelerate the identification of effective FGFR3 inhibitors, modern drug development increasingly relies on structure-based drug design (SBDD) and computational approaches. The three-dimensional structure of FGFR3 (resolved by X-ray crystallography) can be utilized to virtually screen and optimize compounds that are able to fit into its ATP-binding pocket or allosteric sites, allowing a rational design of selective inhibitors. Such computer-aided drug design (CADD) techniques have revolutionized early-phase drug discovery by enabling researchers to assess thousands of potential drug molecules in-silico before investing in labor-intensive laboratory synthesis and testing18. By calculating ligand–receptor interactions and estimating binding affinities, CADD significantly reduces the time and cost of finding new drug leads and the development of new therapies12. In the last decades, several drug candidates have been identified or optimized thanks to the use of molecular modelling, docking, and dynamics simulations, frequently resulting in better efficacy and selectivity. In the case of glioma, where traditional approaches to high-throughput drug screening are hindered by the blood-brain barrier, as well as by the availability of patient-derived cell lines, an in-silico approach represents a powerful tool for molecule screening (including natural products) capable of binding critical targets such as FGFR3. Structure-based virtual screening of phytochemicals against FGFR3, followed by computational evaluation of their drug-like properties can therefore help identify novel inhibitor scaffolds from nature that might be further developed as anti-glioblastoma agents. Recent computational studies have explored molecular docking and simulation-based strategies to identify potential inhibitors for glioma-associated targets; however, structure-based screening of natural compounds specifically targeting the FGFR3 kinase domain remains relatively limited.

Despite increasing interest in FGFR-targeted therapies, most currently available inhibitors are small-molecule synthetic kinase inhibitors, and the FGFR literature is still dominated by these agents rather than plant-derived FGFR3-directed compounds8,9. Moreover, although Guggulsterone has been widely investigated for anticancer activity through modulation of pathways such as NF-κB, PI3K/Akt, and MAPK, to our knowledge we found no prior report directly investigating its interaction with FGFR313,19. Therefore, the novelty of the present study lies in the systematic identification of a natural FGFR3-binding scaffold using an integrated computational framework combining phytochemical screening, molecular docking, long-timescale molecular dynamics simulations, MMGBSA free-energy analysis, pharmacophore mapping, and microRNA pathway interpretation. By integrating structural drug design with regulatory pathway analysis, this work provides a mechanistic computational hypothesis for FGFR3 targeting by Guggulsterone in glioma, where FGFR3 alterations have recognized biological and therapeutic relevance10,11.

The present study applies an integrated computational strategy to identify potential natural FGFR3-binding compounds for glioma research. We specifically intend to identify phytochemicals that are able to interact with and block FGFR3 and investigate their potential anti-glioblastoma performance through in-silico assessments. To achieve this, a library of bioactive compounds from medicinal plants (attention will be focused on Guggulsterone and related steroidal phytochemicals) will be virtually screened against FGFR3 kinase domain. Top-scoring hits from molecular docking will then undergo additional validation in the form of molecular dynamics simulation, binding free energy calculations and interaction analyses to ascertain their stability and important binding interactions within the FGFR3 active site. By comparing these natural candidates with a known FGFR inhibitor (erdafitinib) in-silico, we aim to priorities a lead compound with favorable binding characteristics as well as drug-, like properties. Ultimately, this structure-based computational study aims to propose a promising natural FGFR3 inhibitor which could suppress glioma growth providing a basis for further study through experimentation and development of novel targeted therapy as a functional suppressor for glioma patients.

Methodology

Retrieval of fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR3) 3D structure and validation

The three-dimensional crystal structure of the human Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor 3 (FGFR3) kinase domain was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB; https://www.rcsb.org) using the identifier 7DHL20,21. This structure represents the catalytic kinase domain of FGFR3 resolved by X-ray crystallography with a resolution of 2.57 Å. The kinase domain contains the ATP-binding pocket responsible for receptor phosphorylation activity and is therefore commonly used in structure-based inhibitor discovery studies targeting FGFR family members. Importantly, oncogenic FGFR3 alterations observed in glioma, including FGFR3–TACC3 fusions, retain an intact kinase domain responsible for aberrant signaling. Therefore, the use of the crystallized FGFR3 kinase domain provides a relevant structural framework for evaluating small-molecule interactions within the catalytic pocket. However, it should be noted that the available crystal structure represents the isolated kinase domain rather than the full-length receptor or fusion protein complex; therefore, structural effects associated with receptor dimerization or FGFR3–TACC3 fusion architecture are not captured in this model.

Before continuing on one that would include subsequent computational analyses, the integrity and stereochemical quality of the protein was evaluated to determine whether it would be suitable. Validation was performed using the SAVES v6.0 server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu), which contains several verification utilities, specifically PROCHECK for Ramachandran plot production and ERRAT for evaluation of non-bonded atomic interactions22,23. These examinations determined that the chosen structure has adequate fidelity in geometry with minimal discrepancies in backbone conformation. This stringent validation ensures the reliability and structural accuracy of the model for further modeling and docking protocols.

PPI networking of FGFR3

In order to differentiate the possibilities of the interaction partner and biological connection of the FGFR3 in the cell network, the protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis was conducted with the string database v12.0 (https://string-db.org/)24,25. This resource draws on a synthesis of experimental data, prediction techniques using computational analyses and text mining evidence to build functional association maps between proteins. The official gene symbol ‘FGFR3’ (Homo sapiens) was used as the query and a severe confidence threshold (> 0.7) was used to limit the dataset to interactions of a significant biological relevance. The resultant network was rendered within the STRING interface that allowed for the identification of co-expressed, co-localized and functionally-related proteins. This analytical step played a key role in understanding the role of FGFR3 in glioma-associated signaling pathways, particularly with regard to its interactions with other kinases and growth factors, which could be responsible for oncogenic progression. Mapping of these associations provided a systems level framework to guide the choice of principal targets and strengthen the mechanistic interpretation of ligand - receptor interactions in the following phases of the experiments.

Pathway analysis of FGFR3 with miRNA and glioma

The functional relationship between Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor 3 (FGFR3) and microRNAs (miRNAs) and glioma development was queried by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. The Glioma pathway (KEGG ID: hsa05214; K05094) was retrieved from The KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/) in order to identify the molecular signaling routes of FGFR3 and its regulatory partners. The pathway map was analyzed in terms of the location of FGFR3 in glioma-related oncogenic cascades, and the downstream impact of FGFR3 on cell proliferation, survival, angiogenesis and differentiation. KEGG pathway visualization was used to identify FGFR3-associated signaling via the family’s pathway via the mitogen-activated kinase (MAPK), phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)-Akt, and Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transduction (gene expression) pathway that are the major cause of glioma pathogenesis.

The regulatory role for tumor suppressor miRNAs, specifically miR-99a and miR-100, were also examined by mapping their experimentally validated targets in the pathway within the nodes of the FGFR3 signaling network via KEGG pathway. The overlap of FGFR3 activation and miRNA signaling regulatory loss between the interaction points was identified and annotated in glioma signaling network (K05094). This analysis presented a pathway-level understanding of how FGFR3 dysregulation, through either overexpression or loss of miRNA inhibition, may amplify oncogenic signaling in glioma, and reveals critical molecular intersections to target therapeutically.

The identification of miRNAs regulating FGFR3 further supports the biological relevance of targeting this receptor in glioma. Because the present study focuses on identifying small molecules capable of interacting with FGFR3, the miRNA regulatory analysis provides complementary pathway-level context highlighting the importance of FGFR3 signaling in glioma progression. Thus, the integration of miRNA network analysis with structure-based ligand screening helps contextualize the potential impact of FGFR3 inhibition within the broader regulatory landscape of glioma-associated signaling pathways.

Retrieval of phytochemicals 3D conformers

Twenty-five naturally occurring phytochemicals that had been previously reported to have anticancer or kinase inhibitory activities were assembled via systematic reviewing the scientific literature (Table 1). The reported biological activities of the selected phytochemicals are summarized in Table 1 to provide context regarding their previously described pharmacological properties. To aid comparative benchmarking, the clinically approved FGFR inhibitor, erdafitinib, was made a reference compound. The three-dimensional conformers of all the selected phytochemicals along with the reference compound were retrieved from PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) which provides experimentally validated molecular structures in standardized formats suitable for computational analysis26,27. For each compound, the PubChem identifier and the natural source of the compound was included in order to ensure traceability and reproducibility of the data set. Retrieved structures were converted into compatible file formats (SDF and PDB) and were subjected to preliminary structural checks that were used to validate the integrity of the atomic coordinates. This phytochemical library was used as a ligand set for the subsequent ADME screening/ molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. Making use of PubChem provided one with the correct stereochemical representation and allowed for a consistent subsequent process of energy minimization and optimization. This step was crucial to define a high-quality molecular dataset to assess natural inhibitors against FGFR3 in glioma.

Screening of phytochemicals by ADME approach

The pharmacokinetic suitability of the selected phytochemicals and control drug Erdafitinib were evaluated by using SwissADME web server (http://www.swissadme.ch/)28,29. For each compound, the canonical SMILES notation was obtained from PubChem and fed into SwissADME, to predict important ADME parameters (Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism and Excretion). The analysis included assessment of gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, lipophilicity (XLOGP3), aqueous solubility and Lipinski’s rule of five and other drug-likeness filters. Only those molecules that had shown favorable oral bioavailability profiles and acceptable physicochemical properties have been shortlisted for further molecular docking studies against FGFR3. The application of SwissADME was critical for prioritizing phytochemicals with reasonable pharmacokinetic behavior, thereby the risk of downstream failures is reduced and the translational aspect of the proposed natural inhibitors is increased in the context of glioma therapeutics.

Prediction of FGFR3 3D structure active sites

The potential ligand-binding pockets of FGFR3 kinase domain were predicted using the PrankWeb software (https://prankweb.cz/) which is a machine-learning based software combined with structural and evolutionary properties for identifying and ranking of putative active sites30,31. The validated 3D structure of FGFR3 (PDB ID 7DHL) was uploaded in PDB format and default parameters were used to obtain a set of predicted pockets together with their respective scores and residue compositions. The binding-pocket that was ranked the best by the PrankWeb was used to define the coordinates and parameters of the docking grid for molecular docking simulations that followed. The application of PrankWeb therefore ensured an unbiased, data- and therefore more precise and reliable identification of a functionally relevant binding cavity and thus more precise and reliable structure-based screening against FGFR3.

Preparation of target protein 3D structure

The three-dimensional model of FGFR3 [PDB ID: 7DHL] was preprocessed before docking in order to assure a biologically relevant and energetically optimized receptor model. All the non-essential heteroatoms (crystallographic water molecule as well as co-crystallized ligands) were removed by using the standard molecular visualization and editing software Biovia Discovery Studio to eliminate potential steric interference for ligand binding32. The resulting structure was subjected to restrained minimization of energy using SwissPDB Viewer to reduce the steric clashes and refine bond geometries while the overall fold of the kinase domain is maintained33. Missing hydrogen atoms were introduced, and the protonation states of ionizable residues were modified to mimic physiological pH (7.4) to create a realistically acquired electrostatic environment in the binding site using the SAMSON Connect suite. This protein preparation step was indispensable in creating a stable and decontaminated receptor conformation that was chemically accurate for reproducible molecular docking and molecular dynamics in order to gain confidence in the predicted binding poses and interaction profiles of the evaluated phytochemicals.

Molecular docking and interaction studies of phytochemicals with FGFR3

Molecular docking was used to estimate the binding affinity and the profile of the interaction between the phytochemicals that were discovered during the screening process with FGFR3 active site. The calculations were performed on AutoDock Vina Extended as incorporated in the SAMSON Connect Suite (https://www.samson-connect.net/) which allows for an in-depth exploration of the conformations of the ligand and identification of energetically favorable binding orientations. Prior to docking, the geometry of each ligand structure was stabilized by energy minimization (1,000 steps) to reduce steric strain34,35. The size of the docking grid box was set at X= 20Å, Y= 20Å, Z= 20Å and box centered at the position X = 0.6Å, Y = − 4.2Å, Z = 10.4 Å from the coordinates of the top-ranked pocket predicted by PrankWeb.

Docking protocol validation was performed by redocking the co-crystallized ligand present in the FGFR3 structure (PDB ID: 7DHL) into the receptor binding pocket using the same docking parameters applied for the phytochemical library. The predicted binding pose was then compared with the crystallographic ligand conformation to ensure correct reproduction of the binding orientation within the ATP-binding pocket. The comparable docking score obtained for the co-crystal ligand confirmed that the docking protocol and grid definition were suitable for evaluating ligand interactions with the FGFR3 catalytic site.

To validate the docking protocol, the co-crystallized ligand present in the FGFR3 structure (PDB ID: 7DHL) was re-docked using PyRx and AutoDock Vina Extended with the same grid parameters and ligand-preparation settings applied to the screened phytochemicals. The redocked pose reproduced binding within the same FGFR3 pocket observed in the crystal structure, supporting the suitability of the docking setup for comparative ligand screening. The obtained docked complexes were then analyzed using Discovery studio visualizer, Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler, PLIP (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/) and PyMOL for the characterization of important hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic bonds and p-p interactions for the binding pocket32,36,37. Hydrogen bond and hydrophobic interactions between the ligand and FGFR3 were identified using standard geometric criteria during post-docking interaction analysis. Hydrogen bonds were considered when the donor–acceptor distance was ≤ 3.5 Å and the donor–hydrogen–acceptor angle was ≥ 120°. Hydrophobic interactions were defined based on nonpolar contacts between ligand and receptor residues within a distance cutoff of approximately 4.0–5.0 Å. This analytical step was essential to find the most promising natural inhibitors that are able to make stable and specific interaction with critical residues of FGFR3. The combination of several docking and visualization platforms was a contributing factor in the robustness and interpretability of the molecular interaction assessment.

Molecular dynamics simulation of lead drug candidate

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to investigate the stability, conformational dynamics and persistence of interactions of the lead phytochemical in the active site of FGFR3 under near-physiological conditions. A FGFR3-ligand complex that was found to be the most promising based on docking affinity and interaction profile was run through an all-atom MD simulation using the OpenMM engine coupled with the AMBER force fields38. The protein was parameterized with the AMBER ff19SB force field while the ligand parameters used were derived using antechamber compatible protocols to ensure proper representation of bonded and non-bonded interactions. The complex was solvated in an explicit SPC water model in an orthorhombic box, allowing a minimum distance of 10 A between the protein–ligand complex and the box boundaries, and neutralized with 0.15 M Na+ /Cl- ions to replicate the ionic strength of physiological conditions39,40. The FGFR3–Guggulsterone complex obtained from molecular docking was subjected to molecular dynamics simulation using the GROMACS simulation package. The system was solvated in a cubic box with explicit water molecules and neutralized by adding appropriate counterions. Energy minimization was first performed to remove steric clashes. The minimized system was then equilibrated in two phases: an NVT ensemble followed by an NPT ensemble. The NVT equilibration was performed for 100 ps to stabilize the temperature of the system, followed by NPT equilibration for 100 ps to stabilize the pressure and density. Temperature was maintained at 300 K using the V-rescale thermostat, while pressure was controlled at 1 bar using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat. A time step of 2 fs was used for numerical integration of the equations of motion. Coordinates were saved every 10 ps for subsequent trajectory analysis. After equilibration, a production molecular dynamics simulation was performed for 500 ns under periodic boundary conditions40.

A production run over a period of time of 500 ns was then run with periodic boundary conditions and suitable constraints applied to hydrogen-involving bonds. Post-simulation trajectory analyses were performed to evaluate the structural stability and dynamic behavior of the FGFR3–ligand complex. Root mean square deviation (RMSD) was calculated using the Cα atoms of the FGFR3 protein to monitor overall structural stability throughout the simulation. Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis was conducted on the Cα atoms of individual residues to assess residue-level flexibility along the protein backbone. The radius of gyration (Rg) was computed to examine the compactness and overall folding stability of the protein structure during the trajectory. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the Cα atoms of the FGFR3 protein to identify dominant collective motions of the protein during the simulation. To focus on intrinsic protein dynamics, the ligand, solvent molecules, and ions were excluded from the PCA calculation. Trajectories were sampled at regular intervals from the production run for all analyses. This MD protocol provided a dynamic and energetically informed evaluation of the complex stability beyond static docking snapshots that reinforces the reliability of the proposed natural inhibitor as a potential FGFR3-targeted anti- glioma candidate40,41.

Binding free-energy estimation was performed using the Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MMGBSA) method on representative snapshots extracted from the MD trajectory after equilibration. where each free-energy term was estimated as: G = EMM + Gsolv - TS.

In the present study, the molecular mechanics term (EMM) included van der Waals and electrostatic contributions, while the solvation free energy (Gsolv) included polar and nonpolar solvation terms42. Because conformational entropy (TS) was not explicitly calculated, the reported MMGBSA energies represent relative binding free-energy estimates rather than absolute experimental binding affinities. Water molecules and counterions were excluded prior to MMGBSA analysis. The calculated energy components included VDWAALS, EEL, EGB, ESURF, ΔGgas, ΔGsolv, and ΔGtotal43.

All trajectory analyses, including RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, solvent accessible surface area, and hydrogen bond analysis, were performed using the saved MD trajectory frames. A single 500 ns production trajectory was generated for the FGFR3–Guggulsterone complex to evaluate structural stability and interaction persistence during the simulation period. Although multiple independent replicas can further improve statistical sampling in molecular dynamics simulations, the present study employed a single long-timescale trajectory to capture the overall conformational stability and interaction profile of the complex.

Pharmacophore characterization and density functional theory analysis

In order to understand the structural and electronic determinants that control the bioactivity of the lead phytochemical, we carried out pharmacophore modeling and quantum mechanical studies. Pharmacophore features were identified by using the Pharmit server (http://pharmit.csb.pitt.edu/), which allows the interactive production and visualization of pharmacophore models based on three-dimensional molecular conformations44. The optimized three-dimensional structure of the lead compound was uploaded and two features such as hydrogen bond donors, hydrogen bond acceptors, hydrophobic centers, aromatic rings and ionizable groups were automatically mapped. This analysis was critical to defining the key physicochemical elements responsible for binding of FGFR3, as well as future optimization of analogue development or structure-based virtual screening campaigns.

For the quantum chemical analysis, Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were performed with Gaussian 09 W software. The geometry of the lead molecule has been optimized at the B3LYP/6-31G(d, p) level of theory to obtain a minimum energy conformation45. Frontier molecular orbitals, including Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital (HOMO) and Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital (LUMO), were calculated in order to determine electronic reactivity and stability. The HOMO-LUMO energy gap (Delta E g) was calculated using ΔEgap= E_LUMO - E_HOMO, which was used as a measure of chemical hardness and possible charge transfer ability. Additionally, Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MEP) mapping was carried out to visualization electron-e density distribution as well as regions prone to attack by electrophilic or nucleophilic agents. By combining pharmacophore and DFT analyses complementary information of the binding behavior, reactivity, and suitability of the lead molecule as a rationally designed natural FGFR3 inhibitor was gained.

Toxicity analysis

The safety profile of the lead phytochemical was evaluated by in-silico toxicity prediction in order to confirm the pharmacological appropriateness of this compound as a potential FGFR3 inhibitor. The evaluation was carried out using the ProTox 3.0 web server (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/index.php?site=home), a powerful web-based platform that uses molecular similarity analysis, pharmacophore fingerprinting as well as machine learning algorithms to predict toxicological endpoints with high precision. The canonical SMILES of the lead compound and the reference drug Erdafitinib were submitted for comparative analysis purpose. The predictions generated by the server included the median lethal dose (LD50), toxicity class and several organ- and pathway-specific toxicities, such as hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity and cardiotoxicity. Additional endpoints that cover carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, immunotoxicity and cytotoxicity were also assessed, as well as putative interactions involving nuclear receptor signaling pathways and stress response network such as AhR, AR, ER and p53. This thorough toxicity profiling is necessary for defining the window of toxicity and reducing off-target risks with natural inhibitors. The utilization of ProTox 3.0 thus offers an efficient and reproducible computational strategy to the safety assessment of phytochemicals before experimental validation to support the early stages of the drug discovery pipeline for glioma-targeted therapeutics.

Results

Retrieval of FGFR3 3D structure and validation

The three-dimensional crystal structure of Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor 3 (FGFR3) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank with the PDB ID, 7DHL. The protein is under the class of transferase, homo sapiens are the source, and it was expressed in Escherichia coli without any mutations (Fig. 1A). The structure was solved, based upon X- ray diffraction having 2.57Å resolution, to ensure the high accuracy and the reliability of the later computational modeling. Comprehensive structural validation was performed which proved that the model of FGFR3 has an excellent quality and is suitable for further analyses. Ramachandran plot shows that 93.0% of residues are in the most favored regions, 6.6% of them fall in additional allowed regions, and only 0.4% of them fall in disallowed regions indicating that it has a high stereochemical consistency (Fig. 1B). The ERRAT overall quality factor of 96.52 also further testifies to the structural precision wherein there are small errors of atomic interactions from non-covalent bonding (Fig. 1C). Collectively, these parameters serve to confirm that the selected structure depicts a stable and biologically relevant conformation of FGFR3, and thus makes it an ideal candidate for molecular docking, dynamics simulations and inhibitor design studies.

PPI networking of FGFR3

A detailed protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed to investigate the functional relationships of FGFR3 in the cellular signaling context relevant to glioma (Fig. 2). The resulting network showed a high degree of interconnectivity, reflective of the large biological role of FGFR3 in growth and survival pathways. The network had 11 nodes and 50 edges, an average node degree of 9.09, and an average local clustering coefficient of 0.949, indicating a close module of functionally related proteins. While the number of expected edges should be 15, the observed value was significantly above this threshold, resulting in a p-value for PPI enrichment of 1.28 × 10-12; thus, the observed interactions were not random but biologically meaningful. Protein–protein interaction analysis was performed to understand the functional network associated with FGFR3 and its involvement in glioma-related signaling pathways. Identifying interacting partners provides biological context for target validation and supports the selection of FGFR3 for subsequent structure-based ligand screening.

Key interactors were FGF1, FGF2, FGF8, FGF9, FGF13, FGF17, FGF18, FGFR2, HRAS and GRB2. These proteins are important regulators of cellular proliferation, differentiation, survival, and migration, which are all highly linked to glioma progression. FGFR3 is a tyrosine-protein kinase receptor responsible for the declaration of signaling cascades in response to a ligand, which in turn regulates cellular apoptosis and differentiation. Among its ligands, FGF2 and FGF9 are prominently involved in glial cell proliferation and tumor angiogenesis, while FGF13 participates in neuronal polarity and also in the activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase, or MAPK, signaling pathway, which is often dysregulated in glioma. In addition, these proteins HRAS (a small GTPase) and GRB2 (an adaptor protein) link FGFR3 activation to downstream RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways, thereby enhancing mitogenic and survival signals that favor glioma cell growth. The PPI network exhibited a high degree of connectivity (functional coherence) in these proteins. The observed enrichment of processes associated with cell growth, migration and neurodevelopment highlights the importance of FGFR3-centric signaling in glioma pathogenesis and presents a mechanistic rationale for targeting this receptor with natural inhibitors.

Screening of phytochemicals by ADME approach

A library containing twenty-five naturally derived phytochemicals, along with clinically authorized FGFR inhibitor Erdafitinib used as reference, was subjected to an in-silico ADME evaluation to evaluate the drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic viability (Table 2). The evaluation included the assessment of gastrointestinal absorptive capacity; blood-brain barrier permeability, lipophilicity, aqueous solubility; and adherence to Lipinski’s Rule of Five which allowed candidate compounds with physiologically favorable pharmacological signatures to be identified. This preratification step ensured that only the molecules that had sufficient bioavailability, and physicochemical integrity, passed on to molecular docking and simulation molecule tasks, thereby reducing the negative consequence of false positive results resulting from poor pharmacokinetics.

Of the twenty-five phytochemicals studied, seven met the predefined ADME benchmarks, including favorable gastrointestinal absorption, favorable lipophilicity, and suitable molecular weight ranges. The qualifying constituents included Caffeic Acid Phenethyl Ester, Guggulsterone, Honokiol, Indole-3-Carbinol, Piperine, Resveratrol and Thymoquinone. Importantly, these compounds showed acceptable oral bioavailability, with many exhibiting blood brain barrier permeability an essential property for future demonstrations of anti-glioma efficacy from effective central nervous system access (Fig. 4A, B).

A pharmacokinetic of the lead candidate (Guggulsterone) versus the comparator (Erdafitinib) revealed wide disparities in absorptive and solubility characteristics (Table 3). Guggulsterone showed strong gastrointestinal absorption which can be an important factor in Glioma and blood-brain barrier permeability but not central nervous system penetrance, whereas Erdafitinib, despite its clinical endorsement, showed no central nervous system penetrance, an important drawback for glioma therapeutics. The lipophilic index (Log P = 4.04) for Guggulsterone was higher than that of Erdafitinib (Log P = 3.29) implying better lipid-bilayer passage. Concerning aqueous solubility, Guggulsterone was adjudged moderately soluble (LogS = -4.26) as compared to Erdafitinib which had poor solubility (LogS = -4.48). Though both of the molecules met Lipinski’s rule, Guggulsterone further had the advantage of not being classified as a P-glycoprotein substrate, which may have increased CNS retention.

Metabolic stability prognostications showed that Guggulsterone has selective inhibition of CYP2C19 and CYP2C9 whilst it is sparing of CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 thereby suggesting reduced propensity to widespread drug - drug interactions. On the other hand, Erdafitinib showed inhibition of a wider range of cytochrome P450 isoforms, indicating higher metabolic complexity. Both entities achieved similar bioavailability scores (0.55), supporting their prospective effectiveness of systemic action. Taken together these data identify Guggulsterone as the superior candidate, combining blood-brain barrier permeation, favorable lipophilicity and beneficial metabolic footprint, positioning Guggulsterone as a compelling natural surrogate to the synthetic FGFR inhibitor in glioma therapeutic paradigms. It should be noted that these ADME and toxicity parameters are computational predictions intended for early-stage screening and do not substitute for experimental pharmacokinetic or toxicological evaluation.

Active sites of FGFR3 3D structure

To determine the most likely ligand-binding cavities in the FGFR3 kinase domain, a detailed active site prediction was performed. Several possible binding pockets were determined and ranked according to probability scores, conservation of residues, and pocket dimensions (Table 4). Among the predicted sites, the highest binding probability score of 0.929 was obtained for Pocket 1, which was made up of 32 amino acid residues with an average conservation value of 1.253, suggesting that it is a well-conserved and functionally important region in the kinase domain (Fig. 5).

The spatial coordinates of the selected binding pocket were determined at X = 0.6227Å, Y = -4.2963Å, Z = 10.4076Å corresponding to the deep cleft region of the FGFR3 catalytic domain. The geometric and energetic properties of this pocket implicated it in the accommodation of small molecules and stabilization of ligand and receptor by hydrophobic and polar interactions. This very conserved region was therefore identified as the primary docking site for all screened ligands and was ensured to focus the molecular docking studies on the catalytically relevant domain of FGFR3. Selecting such a pocket offered a rational basis to guide structure of inhibitors and binding, as well as improve the predictive power of docking and molecular dynamics analyses performed in subsequent stages.

Molecular docking and interaction studies of phytochemicals with FGFR3

The current study used structure-based molecular docking to assess the binding affinities and interaction profiles of seven ADME approved phytochemicals, namely, Caffeic Acid, Phenethyl Ester, Guggulsterone, Honokiol, Indole-3-Carbinol, Piperine, Resveratrol and Thymoquinone, against the active site of FGFR3. The licensed antineoplastic agent Erdafitinib was used as a reference compound for benchmarking. Prior to the docking, the conformations of each ligand were optimized by energy minimization in order to achieve energetically stable conformations. The docking scores provide a qualitative ranking of the screened compounds based on their predicted interactions within the FGFR3 binding pocket rather than a direct quantitative estimate of experimental binding affinity. The docking grid was based on the coordinates of Pocket 1 as found in previous work, thus ensuring that all ligands were always placed in the catalytically important domain.

All tested phytochemicals showed docking scores ranging from − 10.1 to -6.2 kcal/mol in PyRx and from − 8.973 to -6.250 kcal/mol in AutoDock Vina Extended (Table 5). Among the screened phytochemicals, Guggulsterone showed the most favorable score in PyRx (-10.1 kcal/mol), while its score in AutoDock Vina Extended (-8.973 kcal/mol) remained comparable to the co-crystallized ligand (-8.860 kcal/mol) and the reference inhibitor Erdafitinib (-8.683 kcal/mol). Piperine, Resveratrol, and Honokiol also showed comparatively favorable docking scores, whereas Indole-3-Carbinol and Thymoquinone ranked lower. These docking results were used as a qualitative basis for comparative prioritization of compounds for further analysis rather than as direct quantitative measures of binding affinity. Because docking scoring functions provide approximate energetic estimates, small differences in binding scores among compounds were interpreted cautiously and primarily used for comparative ranking of the screened phytochemicals.

The molecular docking pose of Guggulsterone within the FGFR3 binding pocket is shown in Fig. 6A. The two-dimensional interaction map (Fig. 6B) and the three-dimensional interaction diagram (Fig. 6C) illustrate the key residues surrounding the ligand within the FGFR3 binding pocket and highlight the hydrophobic contacts contributing to ligand stabilization. These interaction diagrams represent the binding mode of the lead compound Guggulsterone with FGFR3 and are not derived from the reference inhibitor or co-crystallized ligand. The principal interacting residues included LEU32, VAL40, ALA60, LYS62, ILE93, VAL109, LEU178, and ALA188, indicating good shape complementarity between Guggulsterone and the hydrophobic cavity of the receptor. (Table 6). The interaction analysis identified hydrophobic contacts between Guggulsterone and surrounding FGFR3 residues based on standard geometric distance criteria (Table 6). Comparative docking analysis further showed that the docking score of Guggulsterone was comparable to those obtained for the co-crystallized ligand and the reference inhibitor Erdafitinib, supporting its prioritization for subsequent molecular dynamics analysis (Fig. 7A–C). Because docking scores provide approximate energetic estimates, these values were interpreted qualitatively for comparative ranking rather than as direct measures of experimental binding affinity. Collectively, the docking results identified Guggulsterone as a promising computational FGFR3-binding scaffold for further dynamic and energetic evaluation.

Root Mean Square of Deviation (RMSD): The stability of the FGFR3-Guggulsterone complex over the course of the 500 ns simulation was assessed by measuring the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the protein backbone, ligand and active site residues (Fig. 8A). The RMSD trajectory of the protein backbone showed an initial equilibration phase during the first 40 ns, giving way to a phase involving a gradual stabilization at the distance range 0.18–0.30 nm, suggesting that the system reached structural equilibrium without exhibiting large conformational drift. The RMSD of the active site residues followed the same trend and showed steady fluctuations within the range of 0.12–0.20 nm, thus implied that the binding pocket remained stable during the simulation. The ligament RMSD was kept consistently low throughout the production phase ranging from 0.05 to 0.12 nm, and no indication of positional drift or detachment from the binding cavity was noted. This low, constant value of the ligand RMSD computed against the protein backbone is consistent with the structural rigidity and high binding affinity between Guggulsterone and FGFR3. Overall, the RMSD profile shows that the complex reached equilibrium early in the trajectory and was at conformational stability for the remainder of ~ 460 nanoseconds, thus validating the reliability of the docked pose under dynamic physiologic conditions.

Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF): The residue-wise flexibility of the FGFR3-Guggulsterone complex was determined using RMSF analysis from the 500 ns long trajectory (Fig. 8B). Most residues showed low fluctuations, with mostly 0.05–0.15 nm range, which is characteristic of a globally stable backbone with limited local mobility. Catalytically important residues and those lining the ligand binding pocket exhibited especially restricted movements with displacements below 0.12 nm, consistent with the maintenance of a well-packed, structurally conserved active site during ligand binding. Elevated RMSF values were observed in only a few terminal and loop regions with peaks approaching 0.60–0.65 nm (associated with inherently flexible regions that do not directly control ligand recognition or core kinase stability). The overall RMSF profile is consistent with Guggulsterone binding not causing destabilizing fluctuations in the key functional regions of FGFR3, but rather maintaining a compact and conformationally stable kinase architecture that is favorable for long-lasting inhibitory interaction.

Radius of Gyration (Rg): The compactness of the FGFR3–Guggulsterone complex was investigated by the radius of gyration (Rg) profile over a time lapse of 500 ns (Fig. 8C). Rg values were distributed in a tight range between ca. 1.96 and 2.04 nm, with only small fluctuations during the course of the simulation. A brief period of adjustment, over the first 30–40 ns, was followed by a more or less stable Rg value of 1.99–2.01 nm, suggesting that there was no significant global unfolding or expansion of the protein structure. The maintenance of a narrow range of Rg values along the trajectory shows a reproducibly compact conformation of FGFR3 in the presence of Guggulsterone, providing additional support for ligand binding to stabilize the kinase domain without inducing harmful structural rearrangements.

Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA): Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) analysis was performed to explore solvent exposure and general folding behavior of the FGFR3-Guggulsterone complex during the 500 ns long molecular dynamics simulation (Fig. 8D). The SASA profile varied within the range of 1400–1570 Å2 representing small changes related to natural breathing movements of the protein. Following a first equilibration period of ~ 50 ns, SASA resulting values stabilized at an average of 1460–1480 Å2, indicating that the complex reached a conformationally steady state.

Hydrogen Bond Analysis: Hydrogen bond (H - bond) dynamics were monitored during the entire 500 ns trajectory to assess the persistence of molecular interactions between FGFR3 and Guggulsterone (Fig. 9A). The complex retained an average of two to four intermolecular hydrogen bonds during most of the simulation, with occasional bursts up to five, thus indicating strong and recurring interactions which contribute to the stability of the complex. Intramolecular hydrogen bonds in the protein core were relatively constant (~ 650–700 bonds), indicating that the overall tertiary structure of FGFR3 was maintained. Hydrogen bond analysis revealed that the FGFR3 protein maintained a stable number of intramolecular hydrogen bonds throughout the simulation, indicating structural stability of the receptor. In addition, intermittent protein–ligand hydrogen bonds were observed between Guggulsterone and residues within the FGFR3 binding pocket. The count of sustained hydrogen bonds between ligand and receptor, without any loss of contacts being sustained, show Guggulsterone remained conscientiously anchored within the binding pocket of FGFR3 throughout the simulation, reinforcing the high binding affinity and conformational compatibility of the molecule.

Internal energy profile

The internal energy terms, such as electrostatic (E_el) and van der Waals (E_vdw) interactions were assessed in the 500 ns simulation to understand the energetic components that stabilize the FGFR3-Guggulsterone complex (Fig. 9B). The electrostatic energy varied over a moderate range of -10 to -2 kcal/mol, indicating balanced charge-charge interactions between the ligand and the charged residues within the binding site. The van der Waals energy was consistently lower and was around − 35 to -40 kcal mol-1 on average, thereby emphasizing the dominance of hydrophobic and dispersion forces in stabilizing the complex. The persisting of both energy terms throughout the simulation time with hardly any deviation confirms a stable and energetically favorable interaction landscape for Guggulsterone inside the kinase domain of FGFR3.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) analysis was carried out to identify the critical movements of FGFR3 as it interacts with Guggulsterone (Fig. 9C). The PCA results reflect collective motions of the FGFR3 protein backbone, as the analysis was performed using C-alpha atoms of the receptor. The 3-D scatter plot of the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) during the first 500 ns simulation showed clear conformational clusters, reflecting small scale transitions and a lack of unfolding events. The system showed an initial transition stage of the first 100 ns and convergence to a dominant stable cluster within the rest of the simulation time. This pattern is suggestive of FGFR3 adopting a stable equilibrium conformation upon Guggulsterone binding and is characterized by decreased conformational flexibility and increased rigidity of the kinase core. The compact distribution of points also supports the idea that Guggulsterone causes conformational stabilization, not conformational drift.

Dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM)

The dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis was used to study correlated motions between FGFR3 residues in the presence of Guggulsterone (Fig. 9D). The heat map showed a large portion of positive correlations (0.7–0.9) along the diagonal, corresponding to coordinated movements within secondary structural elements such as a secured alpha helix and beta strands. In contrast, several off -diagonal regions showed mild anti -correlated motions ( -0.3 to -0.5), especially loop and surface exposed segments, reflecting local flexibility without losing structural integrity. These correlated and anti-correlated patterns of movement suggest that the internal communications network of the receptor is intact, and binding of Guggulsterone strengthens cooperative dynamics within the active site region. The DCCM results collectively confirm that the complex shows a balance of internal motion, which maintains dynamic stability that is conducive to sustained inhibitory binding.

Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MMGBSA): End-point binding free energy calculations were performed using the MMGBSA method to evaluate the thermodynamic favorability of the FGFR3–Guggulsterone complex (Table 7). The MMGBSA analysis was used to estimate the relative binding free energy of the FGFR3–Guggulsterone complex over the MD trajectory. The calculated ΔGbinding value suggests favorable energetic stabilization of the complex. However, MMGBSA energies represent approximate relative binding estimates and do not directly correspond to experimentally measured binding affinities. The analysis yielded an average ΔGtotal of − 30.44 ± 2.40 kcal/mol, indicating a thermodynamically favorable association between the ligand and FGFR3. The binding free energy was primarily dominated by van der Waals (− 35.96 kcal/mol) and electrostatic (− 7.05 kcal/mol) interactions, while polar solvation contributed unfavorably, which is typical for ligand binding within hydrophobic kinase pockets. It should be noted that the MMGBSA calculations performed in this study do not explicitly include conformational entropy contributions, which can influence absolute free-energy estimates. Therefore, the reported ΔGbinding values were interpreted comparatively rather than as quantitative predictions of experimental binding affinity. Per-residue energy decomposition analysis was not performed in the present work; however, interaction mapping from docking and molecular dynamics simulations provided complementary structural insight into the key residues contributing to ligand stabilization within the FGFR3 binding pocket.

Collectively, extensive molecular-dynamics simulations supported the extreme structural stability and favorable binding kinetics of the FGFR3-Guggulsterone complex and supported the role of Guggulsterone as a potent, naturally occurring inhibitor candidate. The root-mean-squared displacement (RMSD) and root-mean-squared fluctuation (RMSF) trajectories showed little deviation of the atom and limited residue fluctuation, which represented that the complex reached equilibrium at an early stage and maintained conformational stability during the entire 500 ns simulation. Consistent values of the radius of gyration and the solvent accessible surface area (SASA) further supported a compact and properly folded protein conformation with no evidence of global unfolding or expansion of the surface. Persistent hydrogen bonding involving mostly two to four stable intermolecular hydrogen bonds, along with favorable internal electrostatic and van der Waals contributions, highlighted the robustness and longevity of ligand-receptor interactions. Principal component analysis (PCA) and dynamical cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) mapping revealed concerted residue motions and reduced large-scale conformational flexibility which suggested that Guggulsterone imparts a stabilizing effect upon the kinase domain of FGFR3. Finally, MMGBSA binding free energy analysis predicted a ΔGtotal of − 30.44 kcal/mol, supporting a stable and energetically favorable interaction between Guggulsterone and FGFR3 under dynamic simulation conditions. Collectively, these findings establish Guggulsterone as a dynamically potent, energetically favorable and pharmacologically promising lead compound for therapeutic targeting of FGFR3 in glioma.

Pharmacophore characterization and DFT of Guggulsterone

Pharmacophore characterization of Guggulsterone showed a well-defined set of features that were consistent with its observed binding behavior towards FGFR3. The molecule contains two main hydrogen-bond acceptor sites and several hydrophobic centers, a combination which constitutes a pharmacophore that is well positioned with respect to the conserved residues of the FGFR3 active site (Fig. 10A). The presence of a high concentration of hydrophobic and nonpolar areas underlies the formation of numerous van der Waals interactions inside the kinase pocket, whilst the presence of few but well-defined polar functionalities underlie the establishment of directional hydrogen bonding and electrostatic complementarity. This balanced pharmacophoric profile underlies high affinity binding without build-up polarity which results in benefits for both membrane dissipation and target selectivity in central nervous system related scenarios such as glioma.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations also shed further light on electronic properties responsible for the reactivity and binding potential of Guggulsterone. Geometry optimization analyzed at the B3LYP/6-31G(d, p) level provided stable frontier molecular orbital energies with the HOMO localized over mostly conjugated regions of the steroidal framework and the LUMO over electrophilic centers, favoring feasible charge transfer interactions upon binding. The calculated HOMO and LUMO energy levels (–0.23192 and − 0.05222 atomic units respectively) resulted in a EHOMO – ELUMO = energy gap (ΔEgap) of 0.17970 atomic units, or around 4.9 eV (Fig. 10B). This moderate energy difference suggests a chemically stable but sufficiently reactive molecule with the potential for certain types of noncovalent interactions in the binding site of FGFR3 that would not be highly prone to nonspecific molecular degradation or under reductive or oxidative damage.

The MEP (Molecular Electrostatic Potential) analysis was used to obtain complementary information on the spatial distribution of charge on Guggulsterone (Fig. 10C). Electron-rich regions, manifested by areas of negative potential mainly comprising portions around the oxygen atoms and surrounding pi-bonded frameworks were found as possible sites for hydrogen bond acceptance and electrostatic interactions with positively charged/polar residues in the FGFR3 pocket. Conversely, areas of electron -deficient regions over hydrophobic and aliphatic surfaces corresponded to favorable loci for van der Waals packing in the lipophilic clefts of the kinase domain. The correspondence of these MEP-defined regions and the experimentally inferred interaction hot spots of docking and MD simulations is consistent with the fact that the electronic landscape of Guggulsterone is intrinsically suited to FGFR3 recognition. Altogether, the integrated pharmacophore, DFT and MEP results reinforce Guggulsterone as a rationally supported lead scaffold for selective FGFR3 inhibition in glioma.

Toxicity analysis

Toxicity profiling of Guggulsterone, in comparison with Erdafitinib as reference inhibitor was done using in-silico toxicity prediction platform (ProTox 3.0). The results suggested a relatively favorable profile of safety and tolerability of the natural compound. Guggulsterone had a higher predicted LD50 value of 2300 mg/kg and was scored in toxicity class 5, which indicates low acute toxicity, whereas Erdafitinib had an LD50 of 800 mg/kg and was scored in toxicity class 4, which presents a higher risk of acute toxicity. Both molecules were predicted to be non-hepatotoxic, non-nephrotoxic, and retain activity at the blood-brain-barrier - an important feature for glioma treatment as this organ is difficult to cross. Notably, Guggulsterone was predicted to be non-mutagenic with high confidence and showed a lower chance of clinical toxicity compared with Erdafitinib, as well as less broad-spectrum cytochrome P450 interactions with a favorable metabolic profile (Table 8). Although some stress response- and nuclear receptor-related interactions, such as androgen and estrogen receptor modulation, were identified as interesting targets for investigation, considering Guggulsterone in in-vitro and in-vivo experiments, in the future, the overall computational toxicity evaluation is in favor of Guggulsterone a safer and pharmacologically suitable lead candidate for FGFR3-targeted glioma management than the synthetic control in early-stage computational screening.

Discussion

Glioblastoma (GBM) is an aggressive brain tumor, with notoriously poor prognosis, as the 5 year survival is below 6% despite intensive treatment46. Its resistance to conventional treatments (surgery, temozolomide chemotherapy, and radiation) and recurrent disease are common reasons for the need of new therapeutic strategies. One promising new molecular target is fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3), which is a receptor tyrosine kinase that is aberrantly activated in oncogenic signaling. FGFR3 (mutations or gene fusions) in some gliomas (~ 8% of cases) and may enhance tumor growth and invasion1,6. In particular, the FGFR3-TACC3 fusion found in ~ 3–5% of all GBM causes constitutive FGFR3 activation, which drives malignancy1,6. There is a critical need for targeted inhibitors against FGFR3 in gliomas in order to improve outcomes. However, existing FGFR inhibitors are synthetic small molecules and could be costly and have systemic side effects. We wanted to find natural compounds that inhibit FGFR3, but our hypothesis was that plant-derived phytochemicals could provide a much safer and more accessible therapeutic lead against FGFR3 driven glioma.

Pathway enrichment analysis based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEG) database analysis suggests that fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) is a critical upstream regulator in glioma, which drives the activation of multiple oncogenic signaling cascades, including the mitogen activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/Protein kinase B (PI3K-Akt) pathway, and Janus kinase (JAK) Signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT). The confluence of these signaling axes results in cellular processes pivotal to tumor development, such as proliferation, invasion, angiogenesis, and apoptotic inhibition in malignant gliomas. These pathway associations support the hypothesis that dysregulated FGFR3 signaling helps to maintain the tumor growth and contribute to therapeutic resistance. Importantly, the compilation of microRNA (miRNA) regulatory data shows that deficiency in tumor suppressor miRNAs, such as miR-99a and miR-100, removes an important inhibitory checkpoint on FGFR3 expression. As a result of this, there is a continuous activation of downstream kinases and transcriptional programs linked to malignant transformation. Accordingly, evidence based on KEGG analysis supports a dual pathogenic mechanism in glioma - FGFR3 overexpression alongside miRNA downregulation - that reinforces tumor-promoting signaling loops. In addition to structural analysis, the miRNA regulatory network analysis provides complementary biological context by highlighting regulatory mechanisms controlling FGFR3 signaling in glioma, further supporting the relevance of FGFR3 as a therapeutic target explored in this study. The PPI network analysis further highlights the role of FGFR3 within a broader oncogenic signaling network associated with glioma, thereby reinforcing the biological rationale for targeting this receptor through structure-based ligand discovery.

In this in-silico study we evaluated several phytochemicals and identified a bioactive steroid of Commiphora mukul, Guggulsterone, as a potential lead candidate of FGFR3 inhibitor. Guggulsterone had the highest binding affinity to FGFR3 of the tested compounds, with a predicted docking energy well above the threshold of -8 kcal/mol generally thought to be required for high affinity binding47. By contrast, other screened phytochemicals exhibited comparatively weaker binding their docking scores were less favorable (often > -8 kcal/mol) and they engaged fewer of these key residues. For example, while certain compounds formed one or two hydrogen bonds with FGFR3, Guggulsterone relied at all times on three or more hydrogen bonds within the active site during simulation. This high binding specificity suggests that Guggulsterone could potentially compete with ATP and inhibit the kinase activity of FGFR3, as inhibition with FGFR3 known inhibitors, but with the added bonus of being a naturally derived molecule. Notably, the binding affinity of Guggulsterone in our study was comparable to, or better than, some clinically used FGFR inhibitors in docking studies (e.g., its affinity was close to that of ponatinib and pemigatinib, which are potent FGFR1-3 inhibitors)47. Such robust in-silico performance makes Guggulsterone a promising therapeutic scaffold. Moreover, Guggulsterone is already known to be safe for its pharmacological use as an herbal supplement, which may mean that repurposing it for glioma treatment can avoid some of the problems of toxicity found with synthetic drugs.

Docking was followed by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which provided additional support for Guggulsterone’s favorable interaction with FGFR3. The FGFR3-Guggulsterone complex was stable during 100 ns MD: the root mean squared deviation (RMSD) of the protein-ligand system was converging to a constant value of around 0.3 nm for the whole simulation time, within 10–20 ns of the simulations, and from this point varying very rarely. Importantly, the RMSD of the bound complex was similar to that of the apo-FGFR3, thus suggesting that binding of ligand did not destabilize the conformation of the protein47. In contrast, less potent phytochemicals from our screen tended to lose critical contacts during MD - some exhibited increasing RMSD drifts or complete dissociation from the binding site by ~ 50 ns, highlighting the superior dynamic stability of Guggulsterone. Taken together, these results suggest that not only does Guggulsterone bind FGFR3 tightly initially, but it also forms a well-balanced web of interactions capable of withstanding dynamic motions, a characteristic of a potentially effective inhibitor. This remarkable performance in both docking and MD suggests that Guggulsterone could inhibit FGFR3 signaling, and by extension, also potentially inhibit glioma cell proliferation and survival via FGFR3 signaling. Our work thus provides a compelling computational proof-of-concept for Guggulsterone as a natural FGFR3 inhibitor that sets the stage for its further validation.

Our results are consistent with, and offer novel insight to, the emerging computational research on FGFR3 inhibition. Previous virtual screenings have also indicated natural products as a potential FGFR3 inhibitor. For example, researcher used structure-based virtual screening of a phytochemical library and found two molecules both of which were extracted from plants: cycloartobiloxanthone and desoxylimonin to be the promising FGFR3 binder48. Those compounds had good docking scores and stable FGFR3 interactions during 500 ns MD simulations, similar to our findings with Guggulsterone. The confluence of these results across different libraries of natural compounds and research groups reinforces the concept that bioactive phytochemicals can effectively target FGFR3. Moreover, in a recent in-silico drug discovery study of bladder cancer (where FGFR3 is frequently mutated), several novel small molecules were identified by QSAR modeling, docking, and MD which stably interacted with the ATP-binding site of FGFR348. Notably, while those candidates had slightly less negative docking energies than a benchmark inhibitor (Erdafitinib), they had improved pharmacokinetic properties48. This highlights an important point, which is that high binding affinity, though important, need to be balanced with drug-likeness and selectivity.

In our case Guggulsterone is particularly attractive as a lead because it brings together qualities we see as important: a strong FGFR3 affinity and the potential of a natural compound to have greater biocompatibility. The interaction profile of Guggulsterone is similar to that of known FGFR3 inhibitors; for example, two approved drugs, infigratinib and nintedanib, are known to form multiple hydrogen bonds with the hinge residues of FGFR3 (His615, Arg616, Asp617) and a hydrophobic contact at Leu62448. The thermodynamic stability of the FGFR3–Guggulsterone complex was further supported by MMGBSA binding free energy analysis. The predicted ΔGtotal of − 30.44 kcal/mol suggests a favorable binding interaction, largely driven by van der Waals and electrostatic forces, while polar solvation effects opposed binding as expected. This energetic profile is consistent with the hydrophobic nature of the FGFR3 kinase pocket and aligns with the persistent ligand occupancy, low RMSD, and stable hydrogen bonding observed during molecular dynamics simulations. Importantly, MMGBSA provides a relative estimate of binding favorability rather than absolute inhibitory potency; nevertheless, the magnitude and stability of the predicted free energy support Guggulsterone as a promising FGFR3-binding scaffold suitable for further experimental validation. Guggulsterone’s docking pose involving these very residues indicates that the binding mode may be similar to these ATP-competitive inhibitors. To the best of our knowledge, Guggulsterone has not previously been investigated as a direct FGFR3-binding compound. Therefore, our findings propose a new computational hypothesis suggesting Guggulsterone as a potential FGFR3-interacting scaffold that warrants experimental investigation. This novel insight, with strict validation with computational methods, expands the scope of FGFR3 inhibitor discovery when a nutraceutical compound is brought into the pipeline. In summary, our study not only supports existing in-silico evidence demonstrating the binding capability of natural compounds to FGFR3, but also uniquely identifies a new candidate in the form of Guggulsterone as another potent one with a strong interaction footprint - thus adding to the repertoire of new FGFR3 inhibitors which can further be developed.

From an experimental perspective, the idea of targeting FGFR3 in gliomas is gaining acceptance as a viable therapeutic strategy. FGFR3 overexpression has been associated with invasiveness of GBM cells, and the FGFR3-TACC3 fusion is a known oncogenic driver in diffuse gliomas1. Early clinical evidence for the draggability of this target is encouraging, such as the use of the selective FGFR1-3 inhibitor pemigatinib in a patient with recurrent FGFR3-TACC3 fusion GBM that led to a partial tumor response and improvement of neurological function46. Similarly, the multi-kinase inhibitor anlotinib has been shown to be therapeutically beneficial in a case of GBM carrying an FGFR3 fusion1. These cases highlight the potential to block the FGFR3 signaling pathway and make this translate into actual anti-tumor effects in glioblastoma patients. However, the use of such targeted therapies, which can be effective for these patients, is often hindered by high costs and potential off-target toxicities as well as the blood-brain barrier which can limit drug delivery on the site of the tumor46. Our identification of Guggulsterone provides a compelling alternative or adjunct: as a natural compound with a long history of use in traditional medicine (e.g. for hyperlipidemia), Guggulsterone could provide a more affordable and possibly safer option of FGFR3 inhibition. While the existing FGFR inhibitor compounds are man-made and fine-tuned for potency, they can be single-faced in terms of the biological effects they have on the body, whereas the phytochemicals are often multi-faced in terms of their effects. Guggulsterone in particular is known to interact with multiple cellular pathways e.g., antagonist of farnesoid X receptor (FXR) in cholesterol metabolism and has shown anti-inflammatory effects16. This poly-pharmacology could be advantageous in the setting of GBM, which is a very heterogeneous tumor that commonly engages in parallel survival mechanisms.

Indeed, experimental research in recent years brings into focus the anti-glioma potential of Guggulsterone beyond FGFR3. Researcher showed that Guggulsterone is a significant potentiator of efficacy of Temozolomide (the standard chemo for GBM) in-vitro and in-vivo49. Guggulsterone-treated GBM cells exhibited increased apoptosis and growth inhibition when used in combination with temozolomide, which was mechanistically related to downregulation of the EGFR/PI3K/Akt signaling cascade and suppression of NF-kB activation49. Although the two receptors, EGFR and FGFR3, are different, they share downstream paths, and over-activity of the former is another hallmark of aggressive GBMs. The potential of Guggulsterone to abate the processes of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) pathway indicates that it should be able to tame the oncogenic process that is often in tandem with FGFR3. This means Guggulsterone may have a dual benefit in FGFR3-driven gliomas as well as acting directly on the FGFR3 kinase as our simulations predict, Guggulsterone also dampens down other compensatory pathways such as NF-kB that contribute to tumor growth and resistance to therapy. Such an anti-tumor approach relies on the genome of the broadest possible spectrum is particularly relevant in GBM, in which monotherapy against one target may be compromised by network redundancies. In essence, our computational findings are consistent with and build upon these experimental observations: They suggest that the known anti-glioblastoma effects of Guggulsterone could be partially accounted for by direct targeting of FGFR3, adding an element to its mechanism of action. This cross validation between in-silico predictions and laboratory/animal studies strengthens the case for moving Guggulsterone towards preclinical development as an anti-glioma agent.

Despite the promising findings, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, our conclusions are based on computational modeling and have not been verified by in-vitro or in-vivo validation of the inhibitory activity of Guggulsterone against FGFR3. Docking and MD simulations give valuable information about binding potential and stability but cannot fully capture the complexity of cellular context. For example, excellent binding affinity of a compound in-silico may not translate to true biological efficacy if the compound is not cell-permeable, is metabolized quickly, or fails to reach sufficient amounts at the target site. In addition, although overall hydrogen bond dynamics were monitored during the molecular dynamics simulation, detailed trajectory-level quantification of individual protein-ligand hydrogen bond occupancies and aromatic interaction persistence was not performed in the present study. Such interaction fingerprint analysis could provide further residue-level insight into ligand stabilization and may be included in future work. Despite the comprehensive computational workflow applied in this study, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Molecular dynamics simulations were performed for the FGFR3–Guggulsterone complex to evaluate the dynamic stability and interaction persistence of the top-ranked compound identified from docking analysis. Simulations for the co-crystallized ligand and the reference inhibitor Erdafitinib were not performed in the present work because the primary objective was to assess the stability of the selected phytochemical candidate within the FGFR3 binding pocket following docking-based screening. While comparative simulations with additional ligands could provide further insight into relative dynamic behavior, the extended 500 ns trajectory performed in this study offers substantial information regarding the conformational stability of the identified complex. Future studies may include comparative molecular dynamics simulations with reference inhibitors to further evaluate relative binding dynamics.

In the case of Guggulsterone, one of the things that people worry about is bioavailability and brain penetration. Guggulsterone is moderately soluble in water and is reputedly metabolized; it is unknown whether it is able to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) in therapeutic amount. This is an important problem for any putative GBM drug, and not considered in our simulation study. Second, we have not measured the selectivity of Guggulsterone for FGFR3. Given that Guggulsterone can interact with other receptors (e.g., FXR and potentially other kinases), it could have off-target effects which could either be harmful (toxicity) to the patient or helpful (multi-target anti-cancer actions). Future studies should measure the binding of Guggulsterone to FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR4, and other kinases to ensure that inhibiting FGFR3 does not inadvertently inhibit important normal pathways.

Looking forward, it is recommended to validate and build upon our own findings in several directions. Experimentally, their next step would be to run biochemical assays (like an FGFR3 kinase activity assay) that would prove that Guggulsterone does inhibit FGFR3 phosphorylation activity at relevant levels. Parallel cell-based studies using models of FGFR3-driven gliomas (for example GBM cell lines in which FGFR3-TACC3 fusions are placed under a regulated promoter or adult patient-derived cells in which this genetic change is present) are important. These studies can determine if Guggulsterone causes a decrease in cell viability, proliferation or invasion in a FGFR3-dependent fashion and also identify if it causes apoptosis or cell-cycle arrest. It would also be informative to test combination treatments - for example, Guggulsterone with temozolomide or with existing FGFR inhibitors to see if there is synergistic suppression of glioma growth, capitalizing on the multi-target capability of Guggulsterone observed by researcher49. In addition, the pharmacokinetics and formulations research will be important. If Guggulsterone alone has suboptimal BBB penetration, possibilities to increase BBB penetration, such as chemical derivatization (to enhance lipophilicity or stability), nanoparticles to the brain, or BBB-permeabilizing adjuncts can be explored.

In the present work, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed for the selected ligand to evaluate its electronic properties such as HOMO–LUMO distribution and molecular reactivity descriptors. Quantum calculations on the entire protein–ligand complex were not performed because such analyses require large-scale quantum mechanical computations involving thousands of atoms and are typically carried out using QM/MM or linear-scaling DFT approaches. Therefore, the DFT analysis in this study was limited to ligand-level electronic characterization, while the protein–ligand interactions were evaluated using docking and molecular dynamics simulations.

We also propose to investigate structure-activity relations by screening Guggulsterone analogues or other sterol-based compounds, which could provide derivatives with increased affinity or specificity for FGFR3. Finally, progressing into in-vivo models (orthotopic GBM animal models) would enable evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy and toxicity of Guggulsterone under a whole-organism environment. Such studies could help clarify whether doses for FGFR3 inhibition in the brain are achievable and tolerable. In conclusion, though our efforts highlight Guggulsterone as novel lead compound against FGFR3 with substantial computational evidence, it is necessary to make the transition from virtual predictions to the clinical world by carrying out translational studies. If successful, this line of research could lead the way to a new class of natural product-based therapies for FGFR3-driven gliomas and provide hope for the better management of this devastating disease.

Conclusion

In this study, an integrated computational framework combining molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, binding free-energy estimation, pharmacophore characterization, and microRNA regulatory analysis was applied to explore potential natural compounds capable of interacting with FGFR3, a receptor implicated in glioma signaling. Among the screened phytochemicals, Guggulsterone demonstrated favorable docking scores, stable interaction behavior during a 500 ns molecular dynamics simulation, and supportive binding free-energy estimates from MMGBSA analysis. Interaction analysis indicated that the compound fits well within the FGFR3 binding pocket and forms stabilizing hydrophobic contacts with surrounding residues. The pharmacokinetic prediction and toxicity profiling suggested that Guggulsterone possesses physicochemical properties consistent with drug-like molecules in early-stage computational screening. In addition, the miRNA regulatory network analysis provided biological context supporting the relevance of FGFR3 signaling in glioma-associated regulatory pathways. Overall, the results highlight Guggulsterone as a computationally predicted FGFR3-interacting scaffold that may serve as a basis for further investigation. However, these findings are derived from in silico analyses and should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating step in the drug discovery process. Experimental validation through biochemical assays, cellular studies, and structural characterization will be necessary to confirm the inhibitory potential and therapeutic relevance of the proposed compound.
