Platelet transcriptomic signatures in pediatric brain tumors distinguish cancer from cancer-free control
Authors: Markus Talka, Amanda Holmström, Jonathan Hiekka, Matti Kankainen, Satu Långström, Mia Westerholm-Ormio, Anu M Suominen, Ruth Nousiainen, Pirjo Isohanni, Pia Valle, Elina Välkesalmi, Oskar Saijonmaa, Atte Karppinen, Päivi Koroknay-Pal, Anna Piippo-Karjalainen, Jarno Satopää, Nuutti Vartiainen, Jukka Kanerva, Olli Tynninen, Soili Kytölä, Anna-Kaisa Anttonen, Virve Pentikäinen, Katja Eloranta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalignant pediatric brain tumors remain the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in children. Current diagnostics and monitoring rely on imaging and invasive biopsy, which may not capture tumor heterogeneity. Liquid biopsy-based biomarkers offer a novel, minimally invasive option. Among these, tumor-educated platelets have shown diagnostic value in adult cancers, but their utility in pediatric brain tumors has not been investigated. We analyzed platelet transcriptomes of 73 blood samples from 23 pediatric brain tumor patients, classified as high-grade or low-grade tumor patients, and 25 cancer-free controls. Platelets were isolated, CD45+ depleted, and subjected to RNA sequencing. CD45+ depletion efficiency was assessed using xCell-based leukocyte enrichment scores. Differential gene expression was assessed with DESeq2 and Gene Ontology over-representation analysis. Gene-level discrimination between groups was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic analysis, and a logistic regression model with patient-grouped 5-fold cross-validation was trained to classify high-grade tumor patients versus controls. Platelets from brain tumor patients showed transcriptional remodeling compared to controls, especially pronounced in high-grade tumor patients. We identified 315 and 338 differentially expressed genes in the brain tumor group versus controls and high-grade tumor patients versus control comparisons, respectively, and 9 genes in high-grade tumor patients versus low-grade tumor patients. In low-grade tumor patients versus controls, 30 genes met the significance threshold. Platelet gene expression of high-grade tumor patients showed consistent dysregulation of cancer-associated genes. Gene enrichment analyses highlighted pathways related to cytoskeleton dynamics, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix organization. Multiple genes demonstrated encouraging classification performance, and logistic regression classifier based on selected transcripts achieved an area under the curve of 0.91, sensitivity of 85%, and a specificity of 92% in identifying high-grade tumor patients. This study provides the first evidence that platelets exhibit distinct transcriptomic signatures in pediatric brain tumor patients. Platelet RNA profiles separated high-grade tumor patients from controls, possibly reflecting tumor presence. These findings suggest that platelet transcriptomic profiling may warrant further investigation as a potential minimally invasive biomarker for pediatric brain tumors. The observed transcriptomic alterations and enriched pathways also raise the possibility that platelets participate in tumor-associated biological processes. Background

Around 400,000 children and adolescents under 19 years of age are diagnosed with cancer each year [1]. Brain tumors are the most common solid pediatric malignancies, representing about one-third of all cases. They differ markedly from adult brain tumors in histology and biology [2]. The main pediatric brain tumor types include gliomas, medulloblastomas, ependymomas, and germ cell tumors [3], with the highest incidence between ages 0–6 years [4]. Despite advances in surgery and therapy, brain tumors account for the highest mortality among childhood cancers [5]. Prognosis varies widely: some low-grade tumors have > 90% 5-year survival, whereas, e.g., non-infant high-grade gliomas remain associated with poor outcomes [around 45–50% survival) [6]. This highlights the need for accurate diagnosis to avoid overtreatment, and to ensure that aggressive tumors receive timely, intensive management.

The diagnostic gold standard for pediatric brain tumors is surgical biopsy followed by histological and molecular profiling [7], but this approach has limitations. Biopsies may miss intra-tumoral heterogeneity [8], and in some cases safe access is not possible. Repeated biopsies are not feasible for follow-up, and the current standard-of-care instead relies on imaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology, methods that are emerging but still have limitations. Early biological changes often occur before tumors are radiologically detectable [9, 10], and imaging methods reveal only macroscopic lesions without capturing molecular alterations relevant for progression, relapse, or targeted therapy. In parallel, distinguishing true progression from the so-called pseudo-progression (e.g., signs of radiation or other treatment-induced processes) is also difficult and may lead to overtreatment [11]. Together, these challenges highlight the need for complementary diagnostic tools that enable precise, longitudinal monitoring and earlier detection of tumor development, treatment resistance, or relapse.

Liquid biopsies have recently arisen as an increasingly prominent area of investigation, leveraging different tumor-derived biomolecules and tumor-host interactions measured from bodily fluids, such as blood or CSF [9, 12, 13]. Analysis of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is an emerging approach that has shown promise for detecting several key molecular alterations found in pediatric brain tumors, including BRAF V600E, H3F3A K28M (K27M), SMARCB1, and TP53 mutations [14, 15]. However, ctDNA has been predominantly detectable in CSF, and with only low and scarcely detectable levels present in plasma or serum thereby limiting sampling outside specialized hospital settings [16, 17]. This diagnostic approach holds potential for safer and more frequent follow-up, and it may better capture tumor heterogeneity and minimal residual disease cost-effectively [18, 19]. Within this liquid biopsy landscape, circulating platelets have gained growing interest as a biologically active and readily accessible compartment with diagnostic potential [20]. Platelets are small anucleate fragments derived from megakaryocytes. Although they lack nuclei, they retain and translate RNA. Their RNA repertoire reflects megakaryocyte biology but is continuously reshaped by platelet interactions with other cells and systemic physiological cues. Previous studies indicate that malignant processes can influence platelets to acquire tumor-related transcriptomic profiles, giving rise to so-called tumor-educated platelets (TEPs) [21–23]. Direct interactions between tumor cells and platelets occur by receptor binding, resulting in tumor-induced platelet activation. Additionally, activated platelets can absorb circulating biomolecules, such as proteins, nucleic acids, and extracellular vesicles from other blood or immune-related cells within the tumor microenvironment [22, 24]. The uptake of these tumor-derived and host-derived biomolecules reprograms platelet RNA content and function, forming the basis for their designation as TEPs. These altered platelets have been implicated in several pro-tumorigenic processes, including facilitating angiogenesis and supporting metastatic dissemination [21, 25, 26].

The dynamic remodeling of platelet RNA content and the 10-day platelet lifespan enable platelet transcriptomes to serve as sensitive, near–real-time indicators of disease progression, tumor recurrence, or treatment. Prior studies have demonstrated that TEP profiling enables accurate monitoring of disease status across multiple malignancies, including adult glioblastoma, non-small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, breast cancer, and hepatobiliary cancers [11, 23]. TEP-based assays have shown strong performance in distinguishing progression from pseudo-progression, monitoring actual tumor burden, and recognizing disease relapse [11]. These findings highlight the potential diagnostic and clinical utility of TEPs as a minimally invasive, sensitive, and precise tool for longitudinal cancer surveillance.

To the best of our knowledge, TEPs have not been previously studied in the context of pediatric brain tumors. This study aimed to characterize tumor-induced alterations in platelet gene expression in a pediatric brain tumor patient cohort. Secondly, our objective was to assess the potential of platelet transcriptome signatures in pediatric brain tumor diagnostics.

Patients and methods

Sample collection and platelet isolation

Samples were collected in collaboration with the Helsinki Biobank. A total of 73 blood samples (4–10 mL each) from 23 pediatric brain tumor patients (BTG) and 25 pediatric patients with no known cancer history (control group (CTG)), were collected into EDTA-tubes at the HUS New Children’s hospital (Helsinki, Finland) between August 2023 and November 2024. Among the 23 brain tumor patients, all had a baseline sample, either at primary diagnosis or at relapse, collected before the treatment initiation. Of these, 9 patients also underwent serial sampling (1–9 samples per patient). Tumors were classified by experienced neuropathologists using the 2021 World Health Organization CNS tumor classification system [27]. Based on the classification, we divided brain tumor patients into two groups: high-grade tumor patients (HGT) and low-grade tumor patients (LGT).

All samples were processed within 2 h of collection. The whole blood samples were centrifuged at 120 g × 20 min at room temperature (RT) to separate the platelet-rich plasma (PRP). The PRP was then centrifuged at 360 g × 20 min (RT) to pellet the platelets [23]. The platelet pellet was then resuspended in a solution containing phosphate-buffered saline, pH 7.2, 0.5% bovine serum albumin, and 2 mM EDTA. Subsequently, Miltenyi MACS separator was used with human CD45+ microbeads (cat. 130-045-801) and LD columns (cat. 130-042-901; all from Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) for additional leukocyte depletion as previously described [28]. The CD45+ depleted platelets were pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended in pre-chilled 1-Thioglycerol-Homogenization solution (cat. AS1390, Promega, Madison, Wisconsin) and stored in − 80 °C.

Clinical data

Information on histopathological and molecular diagnoses of the tumors and treatments of the patients was acquired from electronic health records and was processed pseudonymously for study purposes. Additionally, sex and age at sample collection of the patients were recorded. For the control patients, screening of any underlying conditions from the electronic health records was also performed. To minimize potential confounding factors, we excluded patients with inflammatory conditions, genetic syndromes, active infections, immunosuppressive treatments, or other known factors that could substantially affect platelet biology. None of the control patients received corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive medications.

RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

The RNA was extracted from platelet pellets using Promega Maxwell SimplyRNA cells kit (cat. AS1390, Promega, Madison, Wisconsin). The total RNA quality and quantity were assessed using Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer RNA 6000 Pico kit (cat. 5067 − 1513, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California). A total of 1000 picograms of high-quality platelet RNA was used for RNA sequencing library preparation using SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v3 Pico (cat. 634490, Takara Bio USA, Inc., San Jose, California). In cases where less than 1000 picograms were available, the maximum obtainable amount of RNA was used. The quality and quantity of the final libraries were assessed with Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer DNA high-sensitivity kit (cat. 5067 − 4626, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California) and KAPA Library Quantification kit (cat. 07960204001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Finally, libraries were pooled and sequenced using Illumina Novaseq 6000 systems (Illumina, San Diego, California) for 2 × 100 paired-end sequencing. RNA extraction and sequencing quality metrics are shown in Additional Table 1. For the analyses, we generated an average of 44.5 million reads (22.25 million paired-end read-pairs) per sample with an average mapping rate of 96.5%. The base quality along the reads was high, with an average of 96.8% of bases exceeding the Q30-score.

Data processing and differential gene expression analysis

FASTQ files were generated from raw base-call (.bcl) files using Illumina bcl2fastq2 (v.4.3.13). SortMeRNA (v.2.1b) was used to remove rRNA reads from FASTQ files. Trimmomatic (v.0.39) was then used to remove adapter sequences, discard short reads, and trim reads based on base quality scores. Only read pairs in which both mates survived were retained for further processing. The processed high-quality reads were mapped to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using the STAR aligner (v.2.7.10a), with gene annotation from Ensembl release 111. Gene expression quantification was performed using the Rsubread package (v. 2.4.3) within the R statistical environment. The featureCounts tool was utilized to count reads mapping to exons with the following parameters: GTF.featureType = exon, GTF.attrType = gene_id, allowMultiOverlap = TRUE, strandSpecific = 2, isPairedEnd = T, and minMQ = 255. Differential gene expression (DE) analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v. 1.48.1) based on raw count data of one sample per patient excluding the serial samples. For each pairwise comparison, the model was fitted with the study group as the main factor. PC1, derived from the PCA of CD45+ depleted and non-depleted samples, was used as a continuous covariate to account for residual leukocyte-derived signal. Log2 fold change estimates were shrunk using the apeglm method [29]. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and log2 fold change > 1 or < − 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. The top 20 genes by adjusted p-value in the HGT versus CTG comparison and all genes that met the criteria of (FDR) < 0.05 in the HGT versus LGT comparison are visualized as violin plots.

XCell cell type enrichment analysis

For the xCell cell type enrichment analysis [30], raw gene-level counts were normalized to reads per kilobase per million (RPKM) using gene lengths from the matching Ensembl gene annotation and library sizes. The resulting RPKM matrix was uploaded to the xCell web application (https://comphealth.ucsf.edu/app/xcell) using xCell gene signatures N = 64, which returns enrichment scores for immune and stromal cell types. For downstream immune-focused analyses, the xCell matrix was restricted to a prespecified CD45+ cell-type panel with 33 cell types from xCell data: B cells (B-cells, Class-switched memory B-cells, Memory B-cells, naive B-cells, pro B-cells); CD4 T cells (CD4+ T-cells, CD4+ Tcm, CD4+ Tem, CD4+ memory T-cells, CD4+ naive T-cells, Th1 cells, Th2 cells, Tregs); CD8 T cells (CD8+ T-cells, CD8+ Tcm, CD8+ Tem, CD8+ naive T-cells); γδ T cells (Tgd cells); NK/NKT (NK cells, NKT); dendritic cells (DC, aDC, cDC, iDC, pDC); monocytes/macrophages (Monocytes, Macrophages, Macrophages M1, Macrophages M2); granulocytes (Neutrophils, Eosinophils, Basophils); and mast cells (Mast cells). A composite CD45+ score was computed per sample as the sum of the xCell scores. Group differences between CD45+ depleted and non-depleted samples were tested with a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For visualization, a heatmap of the CD45+ panel was generated by z-scoring each cell type across samples, followed by hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distances.

Gene Ontology analyses

Over-representation analysis for Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process terms using all filtered DESeq genes (FDR < 0.05 and log2FC ≥ 1 or ≤ − 1) from the comparison of HGT versus CTG, was performed using the clusterProfiler package with visualization supported by enrichplot [31, 32]. Gene annotation was based on org.Hs.eg.db [33].

Statistical analyses

All further statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0. All figures and visualizations were created in R, using packages including ggplot2, readr, dplyr, ggrepel, stringr, tibble, tidyr, rlang, pheatmap, glmnet, pROC, and EnhancedVolcano.

Welch’s two-sample t-test was used to assess the group differences in RNA concentrations per 1 mL of plasma between brain tumor patients and cancer-free controls. Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlations were computed to evaluate the association between total platelet count and RNA concentration per sample. A linear regression model (RNA concentration ~ total platelet count) was fitted to estimate slope and coefficient of determination (R2). Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlations were computed to evaluate the association between PC1 from the PCA of CD45+ depleted and non-depleted samples and FPKM-normalized CD45 expressions.

Gene-level discrimination was evaluated by ROC analysis using log-transformed counts, with AUC calculated by trapezoidal integration. Sample-wise expression of the top 20 ROC genes was visualized in heatmaps after row-wise z-scoring. Binary classification assessed whether selected TEP marker genes discriminate HGT from CTG. The binary classification score included the raw data from both baseline samples and follow-up samples. RNA-seq data were log10(1 + CPM) normalized, filtered to CTG/HGT samples, and restricted to a predefined set of marker genes (n = 66) identified from a prior ROC analysis (AUC ≥ 0.80) performed on an independent HGT versus CTG comparison without follow-up samples. A patient-grouped 5-fold cross-validation was used with z-scoring, zero-variance filtering, and class-weighting. All preprocessing steps (z-scoring and variance filtering) and model fitting were performed within each training fold, with parameters applied to the corresponding test fold. An elastic-net logistic regression model (α = 0.5) generated test-set P(HGT) at lambda.min, and combined predictions yielded ROC AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. No feature selection was performed within the cross-validation procedure. Gene importance was derived from coefficients of a full-dataset elastic-net model at the optimal penalty.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Plasma samples were collected from 23 pediatric patients with brain tumors (14 male, 9 female; median age at diagnosis 9 years, range 0–17 years) and 25 cancer-free controls (14 male, 12 female; median age at sample collection 11 years, range 1–17 years). Among the brain tumor patients, 15 were diagnosed with high-grade and 8 with low-grade brain tumors based on the assessment of tissue biopsy by histology and molecular profiling or imaging and CSF cytology in 1 case where biopsy was not feasible. The most common brain tumor diagnoses in our cohort included high-grade gliomas, medulloblastomas, low-grade gliomas, and ependymomas. The control samples were collected from patients undergoing elective orthopedic surgeries such as hip surgeries for congenital hip dysplasia, fixations after fractures, or benign bone lesion removals. The control samples were collected at the operation day before the procedure. Screening of underlying conditions or previous malignancies was done for all control patients, and those with confounding underlying conditions or inflammatory diseases were excluded. A total of 73 plasma samples were obtained from brain tumor patients and controls. Out of these, 23 samples were collected from brain tumor patients as follow-up samples during therapy (Table 1). RNA extraction, quality assessment, and correlation with platelet count

RNA was successfully isolated from all plasma samples and evaluated for yield and integrity. CD45+ depleted samples (n = 66) yielded a mean plasma volume of 2.3 ± 0.8 mL (range 0.9–4.0), versus 1.8 ± 0.5 mL (range 1.0–2.5) in non-depleted samples (n = 7). Median RNA concentration was 592.5 pg/µL (Q1–Q3: 134.3–1714.3; range: 7–8840) in the depleted group and 2913 pg/µL (Q1–Q3: 2610–6225; range: 1300–9620) in the non-depleted group. Corresponding means were 1426.5 ± 1926.9 pg/µL and 4500.4 ± 3472.6 pg/µL, respectively. RNA integrity (RIN) was high in both groups: RIN 8.8 ± 0.8 (range 6.4–10.0) in the depleted group and 8.1 ± 0.8 (range 6.8–9.4) in the non-depleted group. To account for differences in plasma input volume, RNA concentrations were normalized to 1 mL of collected plasma prior to comparison. For the comparison, we included only CD45+ depleted samples with available plasma input volume (n = 61). No significant difference was observed between BTG and CTG in RNA concentration (p = 0.71) (Fig. 2A). Detailed RNA extraction metrics are shown in Additional Table 1.

Correlation analyses between RNA concentration and platelet count were restricted to CD45+ depleted samples (n = 51 after excluding samples without platelet counts). Platelet count showed no meaningful association with plasma RNA concentration. A simple linear model explained 3.4% of the variance (R2 = 0.034), and correlations were weak and non-significant (Pearson r = 0.183, p = 0.198; Spearman ρ = 0.194, p = 0.172). As shown in Fig. 2B, datapoints were broadly dispersed with no monotonic trend across platelet counts.

CD45+ depletion reduced leukocyte contamination in samples

To evaluate the CD45+ depletion efficiency, a subset of samples did not undergo CD45+ depletion prior to RNA extraction. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the RNA sequencing data revealed that non-depleted samples clustered separately, indicating that depletion affected overall transcriptomic profiles (Fig. 2C). To further investigate the CD45+ depletion efficiency, a composite CD45+ depletion score was calculated based on xCell scores by summing the enrichment scores across a predefined set of leukocyte signatures and compared between the CD45+ depleted (n = 66) and non-depleted (n = 7) samples. The CD45+ depleted samples showed a markedly lower composite score (median 2.171) than non-depleted samples (median 5.386), corresponding to an approximate 60% reduction in composite score (median difference − 3.215; p = 1.71 × 10−5) (Fig. 2D).

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the xCell enrichment scores of the CD45+ cell-type signatures separated the samples largely by CD45+ depletion status. CD45+ depleted samples grouped broadly into separate branches characterized by lower z-scores across different cell type signatures. In contrast, non-depleted samples grouped together with broadly higher z-scores for the same cell-type features (Fig. 2E).

Consistent with these findings, CD45 gene expression was correlated with the principal component analysis separating depleted and non-depleted samples. Specifically, CD45 expression showed a negative correlation with PC1 by both Pearson (r = − 0.72) and Spearman (ρ = − 0.69) correlation analyses, indicating that samples with higher CD45 expression contributed more to the transcriptomic variation (Fig. 2F). Together, these results support that CD45+ depletion reduced leukocyte-derived transcriptional signal and contributed to the differences in gene expression profiles.

Differential gene expression analysis showed extensive transcriptional differences in platelets between high-grade tumor patients and controls

To explore platelet gene expression differences between the groups, four pairwise comparisons were examined: 1: all brain tumor patients versus controls (BTG vs. CTG), 2: high-grade tumor patients versus controls (HGT versus CTG), 3: high-grade tumor patients versus low-grade tumor patients (HGT vs. LGT), and 4: low-grade tumor patients versus controls (LGT vs. CTG). All group comparisons were performed by using the baseline sample of each study patient without including any serial samples. To account for residual leukocyte-associated transcriptional signal, PC1 derived from the PCA comparing CD45+ depleted and non-depleted samples (Fig. 2C) was included as a covariate in the differential expression analyses. This approach was further supported by the supplementary marker-gene analyses (Additional Figs. 1 and 2). The adjustment was applied to minimize the potential confounding effect of leukocyte-derived transcripts on platelet-specific gene expression profiles.

In the BTG versus CTG comparison, a total of 315 genes were significantly differentially expressed (Additional Fig. 3), including 280 upregulated and 35 downregulated genes (Fig. 3A–C, Additional Table 2). The most upregulated genes were WFDC1, COL1A2, and NNMT, whereas KLRC4, GZMK, and DNAH1 were the most downregulated.

In the HGT versus CTG comparison, 338 genes were significantly altered, of which 284 were upregulated and 54 downregulated (Fig. 3A–C, Additional Table 3). The top upregulated genes included COL1A2, ALDH1A3, and NNMT, while FOXP1-IT1, SAP25, and PABPC1L showed the greatest downregulation.

When comparing samples from HGT versus LGT, only 9 genes were found to be differentially expressed, including 0 upregulated and 9 downregulated genes (Fig. 3A–C, Additional Table 4). The most downregulated genes were RN7SL396P, RN7SL128P, and RN7SL151P. No upregulated genes met the significance threshold in this comparison. Finally, in the LGT versus CTG comparison, 30 genes were differentially expressed, of which 23 were upregulated and 7 downregulated (Fig. 3A–C, Additional Table 5). The most upregulated genes were SERINC2, H2BC12L, and ACTBP9. The most downregulated genes were ADGRG5, KLRC3, and ADA.

A summary of the relationships between all comparisons is presented in the multi-group volcano plot, and Venn diagrams (Fig. 3A–C, Additional Fig. 3). Overall, HGT samples exhibited a greater number of transcriptional alterations compared to LGT and the CTG, suggesting increased transcriptomic alterations in high-grade tumor patients compared to controls.

Individual assessment of the top 20 DE genes by adjusted p-value highlighted significant platelet gene expression differences between HGT and CTG. In the HGT versus CTG comparison, genes such as UNC13B, TAGLN, ETS2, S100A10, PLS3, and SOX18 were markedly upregulated in HGT. None of the presented genes were markedly downregulated in the HGT (Fig. 4A). In the HGT versus LGT comparison, only 9 genes met the criteria of (FDR) < 0.05. None of the genes were upregulated. Downregulation of genes such as RPPH1, CCDC6 RN7SL396P, NCOA4P3, and RN7SL3 was noticed (Fig. 4B).

Discriminative power assessment of robust key platelet transcripts differentiating tumor groups and controls

To evaluate the ability of individual gene expression levels to distinguish between brain tumor patients and controls, ROC/AUC analysis was conducted on all the filtered differentially expressed genes. The assessment revealed 66 platelet-expressed genes with strong discriminatory power (AUC ≥ 0.80) between HGT and CTG. The leading candidates included CA3 (AUC = 0.91), BIRC5 (AUC = 0.90), LAMA5 (AUC = 0.90), ELK1 (AUC = 0.89), and UNC13B (AUC = 0.88) (Fig. 5A). When comparing HGT to LGT, no genes met the criteria of AUC ≥ 0.80.

Binary logistic regression model validated the predictive power of the platelet gene expression signature for high-grade tumor patients

A binomial elastic-net logistic regression model with patient-grouped cross-validation was used to assess whether TEP-derived gene expression could distinguish high-grade tumor patients from cancer-free controls. The model demonstrated encouraging performance within this limited cohort, achieving a cross-validated AUC of 0.91 (Fig. 7A) and visible separation of predicted and true sample labels (Fig. 7B). Out-of-fold performance metrics (Table 2) showed a sensitivity of 85%, a specificity of 92%, and an overall accuracy of 89%, suggesting that the selected genes capture group-level differences in this dataset. Coefficient analysis identified key contributors to the classification, including S100A10, MRPL23, SERINC2, CCNB2, CDC20, and UNC13B (Fig. 7C). Discussion

This study investigated the transcriptomic landscape of TEPs in pediatric brain tumors using sequencing of platelet RNA from HGT, LGT, and CTG patients. In this pilot study, our findings demonstrated the feasibility of platelet-derived RNA profiling in pediatric brain tumors based on plasma collected at diagnosis, during therapy, and during follow-up. By integrating multiple group comparisons and biomarker identification approaches, we showed that platelet RNA profiles may primarily distinguish the presence of malignant brain tumors. Collectively, these results provide novel insights into platelet RNA biology in patients with childhood brain tumors and support the potential of TEP signatures as a minimally invasive biomarker source for malignancy detection and monitoring. Although similar results have been demonstrated in the adult population, as far as we know, this is the first reported study for the use of TEPs in pediatric cancer patients.

Many brain tumor biomarkers fail to enter the bloodstream because of the blood–brain barrier, limiting the clinical utility of several liquid biopsy approaches, including circulating tumor DNA, circulating tumor cells, and extracellular vesicles. In contrast, TEPs can actively interact with brain tumors and transport tumor-derived molecular information across the blood-brain barrier [11]. This ability allows TEPs to capture dynamic tumor signals that are often undetectable with other blood-based liquid biopsy methods, making them an attractive tool for the detection and monitoring of brain tumors [23].

Both platelet count and RNA load vary in response to physiological and pathological processes. To assess the suitability of our samples for downstream transcriptomic analyses, we first evaluated RNA yield and integrity across all samples. Despite the inherent variability in platelet RNA content, the overall RNA quality was high. This indicates that pre-analytical procedures, including rapid sample processing and standardized isolation, preserved RNA integrity. Importantly, platelet count did not correlate with RNA yield, consistent with previous reports in adult cohorts [11]. This finding reinforces that the amount of extractable platelet RNA is influenced more by other factors than platelet count alone, such as the immature platelet fraction and clinical states (e.g., disseminated infections) [34, 35]. Consequently, reliable RNA extraction and downstream analyses are feasible even in samples from thrombocytopenic patients. This is relevant in pediatric oncology, where platelet counts of the patients may fluctuate due to therapy or disease. However, in cytopenic patients, it is important to consider the effect of therapy – related platelet infusions, which could potentially lead to false-negative results in TEP transcriptomic analyses, as the infused platelets may not carry the patient’s tumor-associated RNA signatures. Thus, TEP analysis may only be reliable when the patient’s own bone marrow is recovered enough to rapidly produce new platelets. However, transfused donor platelets may also undergo tumor education after infusion, acquiring tumor-associated transcripts despite their external origin. Nevertheless, there remains a significant gap in knowledge regarding how rapidly platelets can be reprogrammed in vivo, underscoring the need for dedicated experimental studies to address this in the future.

Many previous platelet transcriptomic studies have relied solely on double-centrifugation protocols, which may not fully eliminate residual leukocytes [36–38]. Leukocyte contamination is a possible confounding factor in platelet RNA studies, as even minimal numbers of leukocytes contribute disproportionately to total RNA and can distort transcriptomic readouts [39]. In this study, we included an additional bead-based CD45+ leukocyte depletion step to minimize leukocyte-derived RNA contamination, because bead-based positive selection of platelets might cause direct contact and adhesion of platelets, possibly leading to platelet activation [40]. Our comparison between CD45+ depleted and non-depleted samples confirmed that depletion reduced leukocyte-derived transcripts, as evidenced by significantly lower composite xCell scores in depleted samples. The clustering of non-depleted samples further highlights the substantial transcriptomic shift introduced by leukocyte contamination. These findings suggest the CD45+ depletion approach as an important step for reducing possible leftover leukocyte-derived signal and improving subsequent downstream analyses, particularly in plasma samples with variable leukocyte content. However, because leukocyte depletion is unlikely to be absolute in all samples, residual leukocyte-associated transcriptional signal may still persist after depletion. To account for this, we included PC1 from PCA comparing depleted and non-depleted samples as a covariate in the differential expression analyses to improve the specificity of the detected platelet-associated gene expression changes. This likely improved our results by reducing the influence of residual leukocyte-related variation, thereby improving the detection of platelet-associated signals. The establishment of standardized methods for obtaining ultrapure platelets in clinical settings will be important for the future clinical translation of TEP diagnostics.

Platelets from brain tumor patients exhibited transcriptomic dysregulation when compared with cancer-free controls, suggesting that the presence of a tumor induces systemic platelet reprogramming. Importantly, the number of altered transcripts was the highest in high-grade tumor patient versus cancer-free control patient comparison, suggesting that the extent of platelet remodeling scales with the presence of malignancy. This aligns with previous studies, where non-metastatic tumor patients have been compared to metastatic tumor patients [23]. Interestingly, only a small number of transcriptional differences were observed between low-grade tumor patient samples and control patient samples. This may indicate that low-grade brain tumors exert a weaker systemic influence on platelet RNA remodeling. Alternatively, the low number of detectable differences could reflect the modest sample size, heterogeneity of tumor subtypes, or limited sensitivity of bulk platelet RNA-seq to subtle molecular alterations in LGT. This finding supports the notion that patients with low-grade pediatric tumors share greater transcriptomic similarity of platelets with cancer-free controls, consistent with their slower progression rate and lower systemic activity [41].

Assessment of the most differentially expressed genes in the HGT versus CTG comparison revealed multiple dysregulated transcripts, including WWTR1, YAP1, CCM2L, DAB2IP, ETS2, SOX18, LHX6, ITGB4, TAGLN, PLS3, S100A10, EHD2, RPL35, RPLP0, MRPL23, UNC13B, PPFIBP1, FILIP1L, CA3, and PALMD. While some of these genes have reported roles in cancer biology, others may reflect platelet functional adaptation induced by tumor–host interactions, rather than direct tumor-derived transcripts. Several of these genes are involved in processes relevant to both platelet biology and tumor progression, including cytoskeletal remodeling [42, 43], cell adhesion [44, 45], extracellular matrix interactions [46–48], endothelial regulation and angiogenesis [49, 50], and transcriptional control [51–54], as well as signaling processes and tumor progression [55–60], all of which may contribute to tumor–host crosstalk and systemic disease responses [61].

Notably, WWTR1 and YAP1 are central regulators of mechanotransduction and are activated by extracellular matrix stiffness and tumor microenvironmental cues [62]. These pathways are closely linked to tumor invasion, angiogenesis, and microenvironmental adaptation and are currently under investigation as therapeutic targets in several cancer types [51]. Their dysregulation in platelets may therefore reflect tumor-driven changes in vascular and microenvironmental signaling captured in circulating platelets. In support of our findings of S100A10 being upregulated in the HGT samples, a previous platelet proteome study in early-stage cancer reported increased S100A9, another member of the S100 family, suggesting that dysregulation of S100 family proteins may represent a recurring feature of cancer-associated platelet profiles [63]. Furthermore, previous platelet transcriptomic studies have similarly implicated extracellular matrix-related and cell adhesion-associated processes, suggesting that these pathway-level alterations may be a consistent feature of tumor-associated platelet education [28, 64]. In addition, our observation of dysregulation of angiogenesis is consistent with the role of platelets in regulating tumor angiogenesis [65].

In parallel, discriminative power assessment identified several transcripts, including BIRC5, CDC20, KIF20A, PLK1, H19, MCAM, ELK1, BAG3, PKN3, SERINC2, and CCNB2, that demonstrated promising classification performance between HGT and controls. While some of these genes are classically associated with proliferative tumor biology [66–71], others may reflect tumor-associated signaling pathways [72, 73], endothelial activation [74], or systemic inflammatory responses [73, 75–78]. Importantly, six of these genes [S100A10, MRPL23, SERINC2, CCNB2, CDC20, and UNC13B) demonstrated strong discriminative performance, highlighting the convergence between biological relevance and predictive value.

Consistent with these findings, gene enrichment analysis highlighted pathways related to cytoskeletal organization, extracellular matrix remodeling, cell motility, and angiogenesis. These processes are commonly associated with tumor progression and tissue remodeling [79, 80], suggesting that the observed platelet transcriptomic alterations may reflect systemic responses to tumor–host interactions. The presence of these signatures in platelets, despite their limited transcriptional capacity and primary role in hemostasis, supports the interpretation that they capture tumor-associated biological processes rather than incidental background activity. Future dedicated experimental models will be needed to clarify how platelets acquire and potentially support these processes in pediatric brain tumors.

Together, these findings suggest that platelet transcriptomic alterations in pediatric brain tumor patients likely reflect a combination of tumor-derived signals and host response mechanisms, including vascular, inflammatory, and mechanotransduction-related pathways. The overlap between differential expression, biological plausibility, and discriminative performance further supports the potential of these transcripts as tumor-educated platelet–derived biomarkers for monitoring pediatric brain tumors. Though, further investigation is required to validate these findings and clarify the mechanisms underlying platelet reprogramming in this context.

To explore whether platelet transcriptomic profiles contain discriminative signals between high-grade brain tumor patients and tumor-free control patients, we performed an exploratory classification analysis comparing CTG and HGT samples. Using a binary logistic regression framework, the model achieved a cross-validated sensitivity of 85% and a specificity of 92% within this cohort. These results suggest that platelet-derived transcripts may capture group-level differences. The comparison between results from high-grade tumor patients and cancer-free controls provides a reference for defining baseline platelet transcriptomic profiles in the absence or presence of malignancy. Establishing such baselines is essential for future studies aiming to assess whether platelet RNA profiles normalize following successful treatment, or how they change across different phases of therapy and disease progression. In this context, platelet transcriptomics may offer a dynamic readout of tumor-associated systemic changes, with potential utility not only for detection of a malignant tumor, but also for monitoring treatment response and disease recurrence.

In the individual sample scoring by the validation model, some noteworthy patterns emerged. For some patients, both diagnostic and follow-up samples during therapy were available. Some of the follow-up samples were collected when remission was achieved during therapy. Therefore, these samples should likely group closer to the CTG samples instead of the HGT samples, and that was also noticed in the model classification for a few samples. Such observation affects the accuracy of the model, emphasizing the importance of considering patient metadata when interpreting model outcomes, as clinical context critically influences the reliability of classification results. This also supports the theory of TEP transcriptomes being dynamic and not permanent alterations [81, 82]. Although, it is important to mention that the model demonstrates a proof-of-concept of TEPs in diagnostics and monitoring, and further studies with larger cohorts are necessary for the development of the model.

While this study provides valuable insights into the platelet RNA profiles of pediatric brain tumor patients, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the platelet purity was not directly assessed in the final preparations, and residual leukocyte contamination, therefore, cannot be fully excluded. In future investigations, it will be essential to include additional quality control steps to quantify the potential residual cells and the purity of the isolated platelets. Furthermore, the small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings, a common limitation in pediatric oncology studies when studying rare diseases. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate the results and explore the potential clinical implications of the identified gene expression patterns. The study was also conducted as a single-center study, possibly limiting the generalizability of the patient population and risking institutional bias. In this study, we also studied a large variability of tumors, which may also affect the results. Studies including more narrowly specific tumor diagnoses could improve the precision of analyses. Additionally, broader longitudinal studies may be necessary to further investigate the dynamics of platelet RNA profiles in the context of disease progression and treatment response. Assessing the validation model, the logistic regression analysis was not trained enough for observation of clinical status and treatment results, leading to mislabeling, which affects both the sensitivity and specificity. Although these observations should be kept in mind, this study already provides a promising insight into platelet transcriptomics in pediatric brain tumor patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate a feasible and biologically relevant method of platelet RNA sequencing as a minimally invasive liquid biopsy approach for pediatric brain tumors. Platelet transcriptomes showed potential for separation of patients with high-grade tumors from cancer-free controls, indicating that platelet RNA profiles reflect the presence of a malignant brain tumor. The identified differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways support substantial tumor-driven reprogramming consistent with the concept of tumor-educated platelets. The preliminary classification model further demonstrated the proof-of-concept of TEP-derived signatures, although further optimization and larger datasets are required to enhance its performance. While sample size and biological heterogeneity pose limitations, these findings provide a foundation for the development of TEP-based diagnostics for pediatric brain tumors.
