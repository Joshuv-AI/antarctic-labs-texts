Assessment of humoral immune responses elicited by an inactivated lumpy skin disease vaccine in cattle: a preliminary study
Authors: Amarin RITTIPORNLERTRAK, Tawatchai SINGHLA, Pongpisid KOONYOSYING, Anucha MUENTHAISONG, Kanokwan SANGKAKAM, Thanya VARINRAK, Nisachon APINDA, Boondarika NAMBOOPPHA, Khwanchai KREAUSUKON, Nattawooti STHITMATEE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The control of lumpy skin disease (LSD) is often hampered by adverse effects associated with currently available live attenuated vaccines (LAVs). This preliminary study was therefore designed to determine the immune responses in dairy cattle immunized with either an inactivated LSD vaccine (derived from a local strain) or a commercial LAV. Specifically, five cattle received an inactivated vaccine, and five received a commercial LAV. Humoral immunity was assessed: ELISA titers persisted for over six months in both groups. Furthermore, the virus neutralization test showed no statistical difference in the neutralizing antibody titers between the two groups. These findings confirm that the inactivated vaccine effectively elicited the humoral immune responses and highlight its potential as a promising tool for LSD prevention.

Lumpy skin disease (LSD) is a common viral disease in cattle caused by the lumpy skin disease virus (LSDV), a member of the Capripoxvirus genus [1, 9]. The disease was first documented in Zambia, Africa, in 1929 [8] and has since spread to various regions, including the Middle East, Europe, and Asia [1, 20]. Recent outbreaks in Asia, including its first confirmed case in Thailand in 2021, highlight the urgent need for effective control strategies [1].

LSDV is primarily transmitted through mechanical vectors, such as blood-feeding insects, but can also spread via contaminated bodily fluids and equipment [5, 7, 19]. LSD is characterized by clinical signs such as fever, anorexia, and weight loss, but its most distinctive feature is the development of cutaneous nodules across the body [21]. The World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) recommends vaccination as the most effective prevention strategy, with several methods available for monitoring post-vaccination immunity [22]. The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is a cost-effective and high-throughput tool for screening, while the virus neutralization test (VNT) is considered the gold standard for evaluating immunity due to its superior specificity in detecting neutralizing antibodies [8, 15, 22].

Currently, commercially available vaccines are live attenuated formulations, which can induce adverse post-vaccination reactions, including fever, anorexia, reduced milk production, and localized skin nodules [5]. This has led to an increased interest in the development of inactivated LSD vaccines as a safer alternative [10]. This study aimed to monitor the immune responses in dairy cattle immunized with an in-house inactivated LSD vaccine, derived from a local virus strain, and comparing them to those of a commercially available live attenuated vaccine.

This study utilized the local LSDV strain LSD/THAI/CMU/21/05 (accession number: ON024907), which was isolated from skin scabs and nodules of clinically affected cattle [16]. The virus was isolated and propagated in Madin-Darby Bovine Kidney (MDBK) cells (ATCC code CCL-34, Washington, DC, NW, USA) [4, 8]. Viral particle purification was performed using a sucrose gradient method as previously described [12]. The purified viral particles were then inactivated using a binary ethylenimine (BEI) procedure (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) [12, 23]. The final vaccine preparation contained a dose of 106 TCID50 of the inactivated virus, which was formulated with Montanide ISA 206 oil adjuvant (Seppic, La Garenne-Colombes, Île-de-France, France). All procedures used in this study were approved by the Chiang Mai University Institutional Biosafety Committee (CMU-IBC) (approval number: CMUIBCA-0764012). Post-vaccination clinical monitoring was performed daily for 28 days. Clinical safety was evaluated based on the presence of local adverse reactions (swelling or nodules at the injection site) and systemic signs, including daily rectal temperature measurements to monitor for fever (defined as >39.5°C) and assessment of skin lesions characteristic of LSD.

A total of ten healthy calves, aged 2–4 months, were enrolled in this study. To ensure a well-defined immune status, all subjects underwent a comprehensive health screening and were confirmed to be clinically healthy and free from systemic diseases. Critically, baseline serological testing at Week 0 demonstrated that all calves were seronegative for LSDV antibodies, effectively ruling out prior environmental exposure or the presence of interfering maternal antibodies. Furthermore, the subjects were sourced from two certified dairy farms from a government agency with no history of LSD outbreaks or vaccination for at least two years. Beyond LSD, the animals had no record of recent vaccinations for other major bovine pathogens, and their housing conditions were strictly controlled in an insect-proof facility to prevent any confounding environmental exposures during the experimental period. These criteria were established to effectively rule out the interference of maternal antibodies and prior environmental exposure. The sample size of five animals per group was determined using G*Power version 3.1.9.4, with a significance level of 0.05, a large effect size (d=2.0), and a power of 0.8. The study received ethical approval under protocol number S6/2565. The cattle were divided into two groups: five animals received the 1 mL of inactivated LSD vaccine intramuscularly (IM) at week 0, followed by a booster dose at week 4; the other five animals received a single subcutaneous (SC) dose of the live attenuated vaccine (Lumpyvax®; MSD Animal Health, Bangkok, Thailand) at week 0. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.2.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-tests with statistical significance set at P<0.05. To ensure reproducibility, cattle were randomly assigned to treatment groups using a computer-generated sequence. Bias was further mitigated through a single-blind approach, where laboratory personnel performing VNT and ELISA assays remained blinded to group allocations. Subjects were housed in a climate-controlled, insect-proof facility dairy farm under standardized nutritional and environmental conditions. Over the six-month study, clinical safety was assessed daily. Monitoring focused on adverse events, specifically fever, injection site reactions (e.g., nodules or swelling), and characteristic LSDV skin lesions.

Humoral immune responses were monitored using an in-house indirect ELISA [17]. This in-house indirect ELISA assay was selected over commercial kits because it was specifically developed and validated to detect antibodies against the local LSDV strain (LSD/THAI/CMU/21/05) used in this study. Previous evaluation using a Bayesian approach confirmed its diagnostic robustness for monitoring the immune status of cattle in this region. No observable adverse effects, including fever, skin lesions, or local inflammatory reactions at the injection site, were recorded in any animals from either the inactivated or the live attenuated vaccine groups during the monitoring period. Both vaccine formulations demonstrated a high safety profile under the experimental conditions. Blood samples were collected to monitor health and immune responses. For hematological and biochemical analysis, samples were collected at weeks 0, 4, and 8. The following parameters were assessed: red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (Hgb), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), white blood cell (WBC) count (including neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, and eosinophils), and platelet count. Biochemical parameters included total protein, albumin, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, aspartate transaminase (AST), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) [11, 14]. Post-vaccination monitoring revealed no observable adverse effects in the cattle. The global expansion of LSD underscores the urgent need for safe and effective vaccines to control its spread. The live attenuated Neethling vaccine is the primary control method, its use is associated with significant safety concerns, including fever, reduced milk production, and Neethling disease, characterized by localized or generalized cutaneous nodules [6, 18]. One of our key findings was the cattle in the inactivated vaccine group showed no observable adverse effects, such as fever or skin lesions, which are commonly reported with live attenuated vaccines [18]. This is a crucial advantage, as it enhances animal welfare and prevents the economic losses associated with Neethling disease. In addition, monitoring of hematological and biochemical parameters showed no significant differences between the two vaccinated groups and largely remained within normal ranges, further supporting the vaccine’s safety. Additionally, some elevated leukogram values were observed in both groups, this is likely attributable to pre-existing conditions or stress from the field environment rather than the vaccines themselves.

This preliminary study demonstrates that the inactivated LSD vaccine candidate elicits a robust humoral immune response with trends similar to those of a commercial live attenuated vaccine. Both formulations induced substantial antibody levels, as measured by indirect ELISA, with no statistically significant differences observed between groups during the post-vaccination period. Notably, antibody titers in the inactivated vaccine group remained above the positivity threshold for over six months, indicating a sustained immune response [3, 5, 8, 15]. The observed decline in titers after five months in both cohorts aligns with previous findings and provides a critical basis for future investigations into optimal booster schedules for inactivated LSD vaccines [13]. Despite the encouraging immunological data, the study’s preliminary scope requires that these findings be interpreted with appropriate caution. The sample size of five animals per group was determined, assuming a large effect size based on prior LSDV literature. At an alpha level of 0.05, this cohort size provides a statistical power of 0.80, which is sufficient for detecting major immunological trends while adhering to the ‘3Rs’ principles and European Pharmacopoeia guidelines for pilot animal studies. However, the limited sample size may constrain the statistical power and the generalizability of these results to broader cattle populations. Furthermore, in the absence of a viral challenge experiment, neutralizing antibody titers serve only as a proxy for protection rather than a direct measure of clinical efficacy.

Crucially, the study design utilized different routes of administration and dosing regimens-an intramuscular (IM) prime-boost protocol for the inactivated candidate and a single-dose subcutaneous (SC) administration for the live attenuated vaccine. These protocols were selected to strictly adhere to the manufacturer’s recommendations for the commercial benchmark and the optimized delivery method for the candidate vaccine. While these variations introduce confounding variables-making it difficult to isolate the effects of the formulation from the route or dosing schedule-this design allowed for a ‘real-world’ comparison under intended best-practice conditions. Additionally, although all subjects were confirmed seronegative at baseline, the potential influence of maternal antibodies or prior environmental exposure in young calves cannot be entirely dismissed. Consequently, this study provides preliminary evidence of immunogenicity rather than a definitive demonstration of non-inferiority. Future large-scale trials and challenge-protection studies remain essential to definitively establish the clinical equivalence and long-term safety of this inactivated vaccine candidate.
