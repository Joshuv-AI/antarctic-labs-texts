Latent profiles of death coping competence among nursing interns and associations with death attitudes, death exposure, and death education; a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Xinxue Xi, Jing Le, Tieying Zeng, Chenxi Zhou, Jing Liao, Ying Wang, Jing Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreparing nursing students to cope effectively with patient death is an important component of nursing education, yet little is known about heterogeneity in death coping competence among nursing students.ObjectivesThis study aimed to identify latent profiles of death coping competence among nursing interns and to examine whether death exposure, death attitudes, and death education are associated with profile membership.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 366 final-year nursing students from 15 universities undertaking clinical internships at a tertiary teaching hospital in China. Death coping competence, death attitudes, death exposure, and prior death education were assessed using validated questionnaires and structured items. Latent profile analysis was performed to identify subgroups of death coping competence. Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine predictors of profile membership.ResultsThree profiles of death coping competence were identified: low competence (15.0%), moderate competence (68.3%), and high competence (16.7%). Students who had received formal death education were more likely to belong to the moderate (OR = 1.977, 95% CI [1.068, 3.660], p = 0.030) and high competence profiles (OR = 3.637, 95% CI [1.478, 8.954], p = 0.005). Higher neutral acceptance (OR = 1.234, 95% CI [1.115, 1.365], p < 0.001) and approach acceptance (OR = 1.077, 95% CI [1.026, 1.130], p = 0.003) were associated with increased likelihood of membership in the high competence profile. In contrast, certain forms of death exposure, including witnessing patient death during internship (OR = 0.263, 95% CI [0.099, 0.698], p = 0.007) and attending funerals (OR = 0.318, 95% CI [0.109, 0.933], p = 0.037), were negatively associated with higher competence profiles.ConclusionsDeath coping competence among nursing students is heterogeneous. Structured death education and adaptive death attitudes were associated with higher competence profiles, whereas unstructured exposure to death was not consistently associated with higher competence. Educational strategies integrating death education and psychological preparation may be relevant for supporting nursing students’ preparedness in managing end-of-life care.

Background

Confronting patient death is an inevitable and significant aspect of nursing practice. Nurses’ competence in coping with death is closely associated with the quality of end-of-life care and the psychological well-being of healthcare professionals (Povedano-Jiménez et al., 2021; Sansó et al., 2015). Insufficient competence in this area may compromise patient dignity, limit effective support for families, and contribute to professional distress among nursing staff (Wang et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2020). As future members of the nursing workforce, nursing students’ ability to cope with death reflects both their professional resilience and their potential to deliver high-quality care (Conley, 2023; He et al., 2024). However, many students report feeling unprepared when encountering patient death during clinical placements, which may trigger death anxiety, helplessness, or traumatic stress responses (Dimoula et al., 2019; Elzeiny et al., 2025). Such experiences may threaten students’ psychological well-being and professional identity and result in maladaptive coping responses that ultimately affect patient care (Martín-Parrilla et al., 2025; Sökmen and Koç, 2024). Understanding nursing students’ death coping competence and its determinants is therefore essential.

Death coping competence is commonly conceptualized as a multidimensional construct describing an individual’s ability to manage the psychological, emotional, and behavioral challenges associated with death-related situations (Galiana et al., 2019). Cognitively, it involves knowledge, beliefs, and attitudes toward death; affectively, it includes emotional responses and their regulation when encountering dying patients; behaviorally, it encompasses communication with terminally ill patients and their families, providing support, and mobilizing available resources (Dimoula et al., 2019; Elzeiny et al., 2025). The development of this competence is therefore considered an important component of nursing education and clinical preparation (Martín-Parrilla et al., 2025). Previous research has examined this topic using diverse methodologies. Qualitative studies have explored students’ experiences when first encountering patient death (Tang et al., 2023; Yoong et al., 2023), while mixed-methods research has examined related constructs such as spiritual care competence and death anxiety among nursing interns (Şahan and Kaçmaz, 2023). Randomized controlled trials have also assessed the effectiveness of structured death education interventions in improving students’ coping abilities and attitudes toward death (Duan et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2024).

Despite these contributions, most existing studies have used variable-centered approaches that assume population homogeneity, potentially obscuring individual differences (e.g., Chow et al., 2014; Song et al., 2021). As a result, little is known about whether distinct subgroups of death coping competence exist among nursing students. While prior research has identified overall levels of death coping competence and its associated factors (Wang et al., 2022; Younis et al., 2022), these findings are largely based on population averages. Considering the diversity in students’ backgrounds and experiences, responses to death-related situations are unlikely to be uniform (Edo‐Gual et al., 2014; Köktürk Dalcali and Taş, 2021). It is therefore plausible that distinct subgroups may exist within this population. Identifying latent subgroups will require methods capable of capturing such heterogeneity.

Latent Profile Analysis (LPA), a model-based person-centered statistical technique, enables the identification of unobserved subgroups within a population (Mathew and Doorenbos, 2022). Compared with traditional variable-centered approaches, LPA provides a more nuanced method for examining individual differences in complex psychosocial constructs (Mathew and Doorenbos, 2022). In nursing research, this approach has increasingly been used to explore psychosocial characteristics and professional adaptation among healthcare professionals and students (Feng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024). For example, recent research applying LPA to registered nurses revealed heterogeneous profiles of death coping competence (Li et al., 2025). Similar methods have also identified latent classes among nursing students in areas such as death attitudes, anxiety, and resilience (Mao et al., 2021; Niu et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2024). These findings suggest that nursing students represent a heterogeneous population and support the application of LPA to examine differences in death coping competence.

Previous studies indicate that nursing students’ death coping competence is shaped by multiple interacting factors. These include individual characteristics (e.g., age, gender, personal experiences with death, religious beliefs, empathy, and psychological resilience) (Kim, 2019; Martín-Parrilla et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2023), educational experiences (e.g., exposure to structured death education and opportunities for reflective learning) (Martín-Parrilla et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2023), and clinical environmental factors (e.g., frequency of death exposure during internships, supervision quality, and ward culture regarding death-related discussions) (Elzeiny et al., 2025; Yoong et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022). Together, these factors shape students’ cognitive, emotional, and behavioral responses when confronted with death.

Among them, death exposure, death attitudes, and death education represent three particularly important and interrelated variables. Death exposure, including experiences such as attending funerals or witnessing patient death, is a central component of clinical learning and may exert both positive and negative effects (Eichorst et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2022). In supportive learning environments, moderate exposure can facilitate the integration of theoretical knowledge and clinical skills (Zhou et al., 2022). However, frequent or poorly supported exposure may intensify emotional distress, avoidance, or anxiety (Aradilla-Herrero et al., 2013; Edo-Gual et al., 2015). Death attitudes constitute an internal cognitive - affective framework through which individuals interpret death-related experiences (Aradilla-Herrero et al., 2013; Edo-Gual et al., 2015). Positive acceptance attitudes have been associated with greater adaptive capacity (Edo-Gual et al., 2015; Kim, 2019), whereas fear and avoidance are often linked to poorer psychological adjustment (Aradilla-Herrero et al., 2013; Durmus Iskender and Ulaş Karaahmetoğlu, 2026; Kim, 2019). As a structured educational intervention, death education can provide knowledge and practical skills, foster a more scientific understanding of death, and encourage more constructive attitudes toward end-of-life care (Chiou et al., 2023; Han et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2024). Examining these interrelated factors may therefore help clarify the mechanisms underlying differences in students’ death coping competence.

Accordingly, this study aims to (1) identify latent profiles of death coping competence among final-year nursing students in China using latent profile analysis and (2) examine predictors of profile membership, focusing on death exposure, death attitudes, and death education while controlling for demographic characteristics.

The present study was also informed by coping and stress-adaptation perspectives, which suggest that individuals’ responses to stressful or emotionally challenging experiences are shaped by the interaction between environmental stressors, cognitive appraisal processes, and available coping resources. Within this framework, exposure to patient death may function as a clinical stressor, whereas death attitudes and death education may influence how nursing students interpret, regulate, and adapt to death-related experiences. Applying a person-centered approach may therefore help capture heterogeneity in students’ stress-adaptation patterns and coping preparedness in end-of-life care contexts.

Methods

Study design

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. A structured online questionnaire was used to examine heterogeneity in death coping competence among nursing interns and to explore potential predictors, particularly death exposure, death attitudes, and death education.

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted at a large tertiary teaching hospital located in Wuhan, China, a major national medical center. The hospital has >6000 inpatient beds and approximately 4500 nursing staff and is recognized as one of the leading comprehensive medical institutions in the country. Owing to its high patient volume and advanced medical technologies, the hospital receives >10 million outpatient visits annually, discharges over 320,000 inpatients, and performs >150,000 surgical procedures each year. >85% of surgeries are classified as Grade III or Grade IV in terms of procedural complexity.

Each year, the hospital hosts approximately 350–400 nursing interns from 15 universities and colleges across China. Although participants were recruited from multiple universities, all nursing interns completed their clinical training within the same tertiary teaching hospital. Therefore, the present study should be considered a single-site clustered sample rather than a multicenter study. During their clinical training, students rotate through multiple departments, including the intensive care unit, oncology wards, and surgical departments, where exposure to critically ill patients and end-of-life situations may occur.

A convenience sampling approach was used to recruit final-year nursing students. Participants were eligible if they (1) were undertaking clinical internship training at the study hospital and (2) agreed to participate voluntarily after providing informed consent. The adequacy of the sample size for latent profile analysis (LPA) was assessed according to commonly cited heuristic recommendations (Mathew and Doorenbos, 2022). The final sample consisted of 366 nursing interns, exceeding the commonly suggested minimum of 200 cases for person-centered analyses. In addition, the smallest latent profile identified in the study contained 55 participants, which is above the recommended threshold of 30–50 individuals per class for stable parameter estimation (Park and Yu, 2017).

Measures

General information questionnaire

A self-administered questionnaire was developed based on a review of relevant literature and consultation with subject experts. The instrument collected information on several domains, including sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, ethnicity, religious affiliation, hometown, only-child status, annual household income, and presence of close friends), personal and family health histories (self-rated health status, personal experience of severe illness, and serious illness among family members or close friends), and previous exposure to death. Death exposure included experiences such as attending funerals and witnessing patient death during clinical internships. These variables were assessed using brief self-reported yes/no items intended to capture whether participants had encountered selected death-related experiences. Participants were also asked whether they had previously received formal death education.

Death attitude profile-revised (DAP - R)

Death attitudes were measured using the Death Attitude Profile–Revised (DAP-R), originally developed by Wong et al. (1994) and translated into Chinese by Tang et al. (2014). The scale contains 32 items distributed across five dimensions: Fear of Death (7 items), Death Avoidance (5 items), Neutral Acceptance (5 items), Approach Acceptance (10 items), and Escape Acceptance (5 items). Responses are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Each dimension is scored independently, with higher scores indicating a stronger tendency toward the corresponding attitude. Previous studies have demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity of the Chinese version. Reported Cronbach’s α coefficients range from 0.670 to 0.842. In the present study, the overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.859. Reliability coefficients for the five subscales were 0.943 for Approach Acceptance, 0.917 for Escape Acceptance, 0.911 for Neutral Acceptance, 0.906 for Death Avoidance, and 0.905 for Fear of Death.

Coping with death scale (CDS)

Death coping competence was assessed using the Coping with Death Scale (CDS), originally developed by Bugen (1981) and later introduced to Chinese populations by Li and Shi (2020). The scale consists of 30 items grouped into eight dimensions: Death Acceptance (4 items), Death Processing (4 items), Death Thinking and Expression (5 items), Funeral Processing (4 items), Life Inspection (4 items), Loss Processing (3 items), Ability to Discuss Others’ Death (3 items), and Ability to Discuss One’s Own Death (3 items). Items are rated on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Total scores range from 30 to 210, with higher scores indicating stronger competence in coping with death-related situations. Previous research has demonstrated good psychometric properties for the scale, with an internal consistency coefficient of approximately 0.89 (Brysiewicz and McInerney, 2004; Claxton-Oldfield et al., 2006). In the present study, the overall Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.957. The reliability coefficients for the eight subscales were 0.861 (Death Acceptance), 0.903 (Death Processing), 0.897 (Death Thinking and Expression), 0.903 (Funeral Processing), 0.909 (Life Inspection), 0.886 (Loss Processing), 0.919 (Ability to Discuss Others’ Death), and 0.873 (Ability to Discuss One’s Own Death). Although this value indicates excellent internal consistency, very high Cronbach’s α coefficients may also reflect potential overlap or redundancy among items. Given that the CDS contains 30 conceptually related items across multiple dimensions of death coping competence, the high reliability observed in this study may partly reflect the breadth and interrelated nature of the construct. To preserve dimensional specificity, latent profile analysis was conducted using the eight CDS subscale scores rather than the total score alone.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted in September 2025 after approval was obtained from the hospital nursing administration and the institutional review board. A recruitment announcement describing the study was disseminated through the hospital’s internal communication system. Eligible nursing interns were invited to participate via a QR code linking to an online questionnaire hosted on a secure survey platform (Wenjuanxing). Upon accessing the survey, participants were presented with an electronic informed consent form. Only those who agreed to participate were able to proceed to the questionnaire. Participants were informed that participation was entirely voluntary and that they could discontinue the survey at any time without penalty. Because the questionnaire involved potentially sensitive topics related to death and dying, participants were also advised that they could pause or withdraw from the survey if they experienced emotional discomfort during completion. A total of 392 questionnaires were distributed, and 382 responses were returned. The survey required approximately 10 min to complete. The survey platform was configured to prevent missing responses and to ensure anonymity of submissions. Therefore, no item-level missing data were present in the final dataset, and analyses were conducted using complete-case data. Questionnaires completed in less than two minutes or displaying obvious response patterns were excluded during data screening. After data cleaning, 366 valid questionnaires were retained for analysis, yielding an effective response rate of 95.8%.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and Mplus version 8.3. A two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics, experiences of death exposure (e.g., funeral attendance and witnessing patient death), death education experience, death attitude scores, and Coping with Death Scale scores. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was performed to identify subgroups with distinct patterns of death coping competence among nursing interns. Models specifying one to five latent classes were estimated using the eight CDS dimension scores as continuous indicators. The eight CDS subscale scores, rather than individual item scores, were used as indicators in the latent profile analysis to improve model parsimony, reduce model complexity, and enhance profile stability given the sample size. Although item-level indicators may provide more granular information, the use of theoretically derived subscale scores is consistent with previous person-centered research involving multidimensional psychosocial constructs and allowed clearer interpretation of clinically meaningful competence patterns. Model selection was based on a combination of statistical fit indices and substantive interpretability. Fit indices included the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy values, the Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR), and the Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) (Sen and Cohen, 2024). After determining the optimal latent class solution, chi-square tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to examine differences across classes in demographic variables, death exposure (funeral attendance and witnessing patient death), death education experience, and death attitudes.

Multinomial logistic regression analysis was subsequently performed to identify predictors of latent class membership. Variables included in the multinomial logistic regression model were selected based on both theoretical relevance and significant differences identified in the univariate analyses, rather than solely on statistical significance. Prior to regression analysis, multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and all VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold of 5, indicating no substantial multicollinearity. Assumptions of multinomial logistic regression, including independence of observations and absence of influential outliers, were also examined and considered acceptable. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the moderate number of planned comparisons, formal multiple-comparison corrections (e.g., Bonferroni or false discovery rate adjustment) were not applied; however, we acknowledge that the absence of formal adjustment may increase the risk of type I error. Therefore, the findings were interpreted cautiously, with emphasis placed on effect sizes, confidence intervals, and theoretical consistency rather than statistical significance alone. The class characterized by the lowest level of death coping competence was used as the reference category in the multinomial logistic regression analysis. Because all participants completed clinical training within the same hospital and the study was not designed as a multilevel investigation, clustering effects were not explicitly modeled in the analyses. In addition, participants from individual universities were relatively dispersed across clinical departments, reducing the feasibility and stability of university-level clustering analyses. Effect sizes were reported to facilitate interpretation of practical significance, including Cramer’s V for chi-square tests, η² for ANOVA, and odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for multinomial logistic regression analyses.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the study hospital. All participants provided digital informed consent prior to participation. To ensure confidentiality, survey responses were anonymized using unique identifiers and stored in encrypted, password-protected data systems. Given the emotionally sensitive nature of the study topic, several measures were implemented to minimize potential psychological distress. Participants were informed in advance that the questionnaire included items related to death-related experiences and attitudes. Contact information for psychological counseling services provided by the hospital or affiliated universities was made available to participants upon completion of the survey. No participants reported significant distress or requested psychological intervention during the study period.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 366 nursing students were included in the analysis. Participant characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 20.92 years (SD = 0.785; range 19–24). Most participants were female (83.6%, n = 306) and reported no religious affiliation (99.7%, n = 365). The distribution of hometown background was nearly balanced between urban (50.3%, n = 184) and rural areas (49.7%, n = 182). Most participants were not only children (71.3%, n = 261). Self-rated physical health was generally favorable, with 51.6% (n = 189) reporting good health, 41.0% (n = 150) reporting fair health, and 7.4% (n = 27) reporting poor health. Exposure to death-related experiences varied. The majority of participants had attended a funeral (83.1%, n = 304), while fewer reported participation in patient rescue (3.6%, n = 13) or witnessing patient death during clinical internship (7.4%, n = 27). Death attitudes and death coping competence

Scores for the death attitude dimensions indicated moderate variation across subscales. The highest mean score was observed for approach acceptance (M = 3.30, SD = 0.85), followed by escape acceptance (M = 3.24, SD = 0.93), fear of death (M = 3.16, SD = 0.84), and death avoidance (M = 3.05, SD = 0.92). Neutral acceptance had the lowest mean score (M = 2.18, SD = 0.90).

Death coping competence, measured using the Coping with Death Scale (CDS), ranged from 51.00 to 208.00. The overall mean score was 116.69 (SD = 28.71), corresponding to an average item score of 3.89 (SD = 0.96), suggesting an overall moderate tendency in self-reported death coping competence among nursing interns. The 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile CDS scores were 103, 113, and 124.75, respectively, suggesting a moderately dispersed distribution of death coping competence scores across participants. Preliminary assessment of score distribution showed acceptable normality, with skewness = 0.702 and kurtosis = 0.651, both within commonly accepted ranges for parametric analyses.

Identification of death coping competence profiles

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using the eight CDS subscale scores as indicators. Models specifying one to five latent classes were estimated. Model fit statistics are presented in Table 2. Model fit improved progressively up to the three-profile solution. The three-class model demonstrated significantly improved fit according to both the Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR, P < 0.001) and the Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT, P < 0.001), and showed lower values of the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted BIC compared with the one- and two-class models. Entropy values for the selected three-class model exceeded 0.80, indicating acceptable classification accuracy and adequate separation among the latent profiles. The average posterior probabilities for the Low death coping competence, Moderate death coping competence, and High death coping competence profiles were 0.892, 0.978, and 0.977, respectively, further supporting acceptable classification precision. Although the four-class model produced a slightly lower BIC value than the three-class model, the reduction in BIC was relatively small and was not accompanied by substantial improvement in entropy or profile interpretability. In addition, the four-class solution yielded two relatively small subgroups, suggesting limited stability and practical interpretability of the additional class structure. Although the LMR test remained statistically significant for the four-class model (p = 0.002), model selection was based on a comprehensive evaluation of statistical fit indices, classification quality, class size, and conceptual interpretability rather than reliance on a single indicator. The five-class model showed non-significant results for both LMR (p = 0.238) and BLRT (p = 0.667), further suggesting limited benefit from additional class extraction. Overall, the fit indices demonstrated a pattern of diminishing improvement after the three-class solution, supporting the selection of the three-profile model as the most parsimonious and interpretable representation of the data. Considering statistical fit, class size, and conceptual interpretability, the three-class solution was selected as the optimal model. The three identified profiles were labeled as follows: (1) Low death coping competence (Low DCC): n = 55 (15.0%); (2) Moderate death coping competence (Moderate DCC): n = 250 (68.3%); (3) High death coping competence (High DCC): n = 61 (16.7%). These descriptive profile labels were used consistently across the text, tables, and figures to improve interpretability and readability. Univariate comparisons across latent profiles

Univariate analyses were conducted to examine differences across the three profiles (Table 3). Most demographic characteristics, including age, gender, ethnicity, hometown background, family income, and health status, did not differ significantly across profiles. However, several variables related to death exposure, death education, and death attitudes showed statistically significant differences. Participants differed in rates of funeral attendance (χ² = 6.92, p = 0.031), experience of patient death during internship (χ² = 8.07, p = 0.018), and prior participation in formal death education (χ² = 9.34, p = 0.009). Significant differences were also observed in two death attitude dimensions. Discussion

This study used a person-centered analytical approach to explore heterogeneity in death coping competence among nursing interns undertaking clinical training in China. Three distinct competence profiles, namely low, moderate, and high DCC, were identified, indicating that nursing students differ substantially in their preparedness to cope with death-related situations in clinical practice. Rather than representing a homogeneous cohort, nursing interns appear to possess varying levels of psychological readiness and coping resources when confronted with patient death. By identifying latent profiles of competence, the present study extends previous variable-centered research and provides greater insight into the heterogeneity of students’ preparedness for end-of-life care. Although the identified profiles primarily reflected quantitative differences in overall competence levels rather than sharply distinct qualitative patterns, the person-centred approach enabled identification of clinically meaningful subgroups with differing levels of coping preparedness. In particular, the Low DCC profile may represent a potentially vulnerable subgroup requiring more targeted educational and psychological support, which may not be readily identifiable through population-average analyses alone.

The majority of participants belonged to the moderate competence profile, while smaller proportions were classified as having low or high competence. This pattern suggests that although many students demonstrate a basic level of coping ability, a substantial subgroup may still experience difficulty managing the emotional and professional challenges associated with patient death. Similar observations have been reported in studies of both nursing students and early-career nurses, where encounters with dying patients are often described as one of the most stressful aspects of clinical practice (Michalec et al., 2013; Yoong et al., 2023). By identifying distinct competence profiles rather than relying solely on average scores, the present study offers a more nuanced understanding of students’ readiness for end-of-life care and highlights the need for educational approaches that acknowledge these differences. More specifically, students in the High DCC profile demonstrated consistently higher scores in dimensions related to death processing, death thinking and expression, life inspection, and the ability to discuss death-related issues, suggesting stronger reflective capacity, emotional regulation, and communication preparedness in end-of-life care situations. In contrast, students in the Low DCC profile showed consistently lower scores across these dimensions, indicating potential difficulties in emotionally processing death-related experiences and discussing death-related issues with patients or others.

Among the examined factors, prior exposure to formal death education emerged as the most consistent predictor of higher competence profiles. Students who had received structured death-related education were significantly more likely to belong to both the moderate and high competence groups. This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating that educational interventions focusing on death and dying can improve students’ knowledge, attitudes, and perceived competence in end-of-life care (Chua and Shorey, 2021; Wang et al., 2023). Such structured educational programmes may facilitate competence development through several mechanisms, including providing conceptual knowledge about death and dying, facilitating communication skills training, and offering safe environments for reflection on emotional responses to mortality (Meng et al., 2022; Stylianou and Zembylas, 2020).

The importance of formal death education may be particularly pronounced within the Chinese cultural context. In traditional Chinese culture, discussions about death are often avoided because they are associated with misfortune or emotional discomfort (Tu et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2024). Such cultural avoidance may limit opportunities for nursing students to openly discuss mortality, grief, and end-of-life care before entering clinical practice, potentially reducing their psychological preparedness for patient death. As a result, some students may encounter dying patients or bereaved families with insufficient psychological preparedness. Structured educational programmes addressing death and dying may therefore provide supportive opportunities for students to reflect on their beliefs and emotional responses to death. Recent educational innovations in China, such as narrative-based death education and programmes like the “Peace of Mind Tea House,” further supports the potential value of such approaches in enhancing students’ coping competence and professional preparedness (Duan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). These findings also suggest that nursing curricula in China should incorporate culturally sensitive death education strategies, including reflective discussion, simulation-based learning, and guided debriefing, to facilitate students’ emotional adaptation and professional development in end-of-life care.

Death attitudes were also significantly associated with competence profiles. In particular, neutral acceptance and approach acceptance were positively related to membership in the high competence group. These attitudes reflect a more accepting orientation toward death and may facilitate adaptive psychological responses when confronting dying patients. Previous research has shown that individuals who regard death as a natural life process or attribute existential meaning to death tend to report lower levels of death anxiety and greater psychological resilience (Barnett et al., 2020; He and Li, 2022). One possible explanation is that acceptance-oriented attitudes reduce the perceived psychological threat associated with death, thereby enabling individuals to maintain emotional regulation and professional focus during end-of-life care via allocating more cognitive and emotional resources to patient care (Oker et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2024). These findings suggest that educational strategies aimed at fostering constructive attitudes toward death—through reflective discussion, narrative learning, or experiential education—may play an important role in strengthening students’ coping competence.

Interestingly, certain forms of death exposure were negatively associated with higher competence profiles. Students who had witnessed patient death during clinical practice or attended funerals were less likely to belong to the high competence group. These associations should be interpreted cautiously, given the cross-sectional design. It is possible that students with lower death coping competence perceive exposure more negatively, or that unmeasured factors such as emotional burden, insufficient supervision, or limitations of the binary exposure measures contribute to these patterns. Thus, exposure to death is not necessarily indicative of competence development in the absence of structured support and reflection (Majidipour et al., 2024; Shahzeydi et al., 2025). Qualitative studies have similarly reported that nursing students often experience feelings of helplessness or inadequacy when first encountering patient death, particularly when supervision and emotional support are limited (Heise et al., 2018; Yoong et al., 2023). These findings may also be interpreted from a stress-adaptation perspective, suggesting that exposure to patient death does not necessarily promote adaptive coping unless students possess sufficient psychological resources and educational support to process these experiences constructively.

Cultural factors may further shape these responses. In China, death-related rituals such as funerals are often associated with strong emotional and cultural meanings, which may evoke personal grief rather than professional reflection (Tang et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2022). In contrast, many nursing education programmes in Western contexts frequently incorporate preparatory training, simulation exercises, and structured debriefing following patient deaths to help students interpret and process these experiences (Kannappan, 2025; Phung et al., 2024). Overall, these findings highlight the importance of distinguishing between unstructured exposure and supported experiential learning. Clinical encounters with death may contribute to competence development only when they are accompanied by guidance, reflection, and mentorship, rather than being unstructured or unsupported.

Taken together, the findings have several implications for nursing education. First, the identification of heterogeneous competence profiles suggests that a uniform educational approach may not adequately meet the needs of all students. Tailored interventions may be required to support students with lower levels of coping competence while further developing the skills of those already demonstrating higher competence. For example, students in the Low DCC profile may benefit from additional psychological support, structured reflective activities, and closer mentorship during clinical placements involving end-of-life care. Second, integrating systematic death education into undergraduate nursing curricula may help students acquire both cognitive understanding and emotional preparedness for end-of-life care. Educational strategies such as simulation-based training, reflective writing, and facilitated discussions may help strengthen students’ emotional preparedness and communication competence in end-of-life care. These strategies could be incorporated into both classroom teaching and pre-clinical preparation programmes to help students gradually develop communication skills and emotional coping abilities before encountering patient death in real clinical settings. Finally, clinical instructors and mentors play a critical role in helping students process death-related experiences. Structured debriefing and supportive supervision following patient death may help transform potentially distressing events into meaningful learning experiences. Establishing regular post-event debriefing sessions and faculty-led reflective discussions may further support students’ emotional adaptation and professional identity development during internships.

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. The cross-sectional nature of the study prevents conclusions about causal relationships between the examined factors and competence profiles. Although students were recruited from multiple universities, all participants completed their clinical training within a single tertiary hospital, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other educational or healthcare settings. In addition, because all participants were trained within the same institutional environment, hospital-specific factors such as supervision practices, ward culture, communication norms surrounding death, and opportunities for end-of-life exposure may have influenced students’ coping competence and contributed to contextual clustering effects. Moreover, the use of self-reported measures introduces the possibility of response bias, particularly when addressing sensitive topics such as attitudes toward death. In addition, death exposure was assessed using relatively simple binary self-report items, which did not capture the frequency, intensity, duration, or emotional impact of these experiences. As a result, the observed associations between death exposure and competence profiles should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies should consider using validated multidimensional exposure measures or graded indicators of death-related experiences to better understand how different forms and levels of exposure influence the development of death coping competence. In addition, longitudinal and multi-site studies are needed to clarify how death coping competence evolves over time and how educational interventions may influence this developmental process.

Conclusion

This study identified three distinct profiles of death coping competence among nursing interns, highlighting substantial variability in students’ preparedness for managing death-related situations in clinical practice. Formal death education and adaptive death attitudes were positively associated with higher levels of competence, whereas unstructured exposure to death was not consistently associated with higher competence profiles. These findings suggest that relying solely on incidental clinical experiences may not be adequate for supporting nursing students’ preparedness for end-of-life care. Integrating structured death education, reflective learning opportunities, and supportive supervision within nursing curricula may be relevant for supporting students’ coping competence and emotional preparedness. Future longitudinal and multi-site studies are needed to further examine whether and how educational interventions are associated with changes in death coping competence among nursing students.
