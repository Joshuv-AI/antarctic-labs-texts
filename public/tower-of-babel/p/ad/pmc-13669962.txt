Race and Ethnicity Affect Symptom and Endoscopy Associations in CT‐Confirmed Chronic Rhinosinusitis
Authors: Corey Karp, Mitchell Figueroa, Jaynelle Gao, Joshua S Lin, Zara Mubin, Sophia Albanese, Kevin Hur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo investigate demographic differences in the association between chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) symptoms and endoscopic findings with CT‐confirmed CRS.MethodsConsecutive adult patients presenting to outpatient rhinology clinics with chronic cardinal sinonasal symptoms who underwent nasal endoscopy and a sinus CT scan were included. Demographics, cardinal symptoms, SNOT‐22, and Lund–Kennedy (LK) endoscopic scores were extracted from electronic medical records. Logistic regression was used to assess associations between predictors and CT‐confirmed CRS (CT‐CRS) with prespecified stratified analyses by race and ethnicity.ResultsAmong 314 included patients (mean age 48 ± 17 years, 51% female), 228 met criteria for CT‐CRS. Hyposmia and LK endoscopic scores were associated with CT‐CRS. However, in subgroup analyses, the associations of hyposmia and LK endoscopic scores with CT‐CRS were not significant among Asian and Hispanic patients. Among patients with a negative nasal endoscopy, 62.6% had CT‐CRS. In this subgroup, patient‐reported nasal discharge and the presence of ≥ 2 cardinal symptoms were associated with reduced odds of CT‐CRS.ConclusionsAssociations between symptoms and nasal endoscopy findings with CT‐CRS were not significant among Asian and Hispanic patients, suggesting limitations of symptom and endoscopy‐based assessments in these subgroups. Among patients with a negative nasal endoscopy, symptoms demonstrated limited ability to identify CT‐CRS. These findings suggest CT imaging could be considered earlier in selected clinical contexts.Level of Evidence3Keywords: chronic rhinosinusitis, Lund–Kennedy, Lund–Mackay, nasal endoscopy, sinonasal imaging

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is a complex inflammatory condition that requires both patient‐reported symptoms and objective measures to confirm diagnosis. Current international consensus guidelines emphasize the limited efficacy of cardinal symptoms alone for diagnosing CRS, with reported positive predictive values ranging from 34% to 54% [1, 2, 3], increasing the risk for a false‐positive diagnosis and potentially leading to inappropriate interventions and use of healthcare resources. Recent studies have evaluated other symptom‐based measures such as the 22‐item Sinonasal Outcome Test (SNOT‐22), with the relationship between SNOT‐22 symptom burden and radiographic disease severity yielding mixed results. Some report significant positive correlations between Lund–Mackay (LM) computerized tomography (CT) scores and the nasal and emotional symptom domains of SNOT‐22, while others identify correlations limited to the nasal symptom subscores, with no association observed for total SNOT‐22 scores or for sleep and psychological domains [4, 5]. Systematic reviews have similarly reported a moderate correlation between overall SNOT‐22 and LM scores [6].

In contrast, multiple studies have consistently demonstrated correlations between nasal endoscopy findings and LM scores, supporting the role of nasal endoscopy as an effective first‐line diagnostic tool [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, endoscopy is limited by a relatively high false‐negative rate in diagnosing CRS, with estimates ranging between 35% and 70% due to difficulty assessing the interior of sinuses for disease [2, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Consequently, among objective imaging modalities, CT scoring is regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing and staging CRS, carrying greater weight than subjective symptom assessment and providing superior visualization of sinonasal anatomy compared with nasal endoscopy [7]. However, CT imaging involves exposure to ionizing radiation, with added risks for pediatric patients or in cases requiring repeated imaging [15]. Prior studies have emphasized the clinical utility of CT findings in CRS, specifically highlighting the benefits of using CT with a negative nasal endoscopy and low pre‐CT CRS probability in reducing unnecessary repeat clinic visits and prolonged medical therapy in a cost‐effective manner [16].

Despite these established diagnostic principles, it remains unclear whether the diagnostic predictive value of CRS symptoms and nasal endoscopy findings is consistent across patient populations. Prior diagnostic studies have largely evaluated aggregated cohorts, assuming the predictive value of symptoms is consistent across racial and ethnic groups. A recent systematic review demonstrated overrepresentation of CRS in white and non‐Hispanic populations, with underrepresentation of other races and ethnicities relative to the overall US population [17], demonstrating a need for further research examining the role of demographic disparities on CRS diagnosis.

This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between symptoms and nasal endoscopy findings with CT‐confirmed CRS (CT‐CRS) across different populations. Additionally, given the recognized false‐negative rate of nasal endoscopy in diagnosing CRS, this study examined factors associated with CT‐CRS among patients with negative endoscopic findings. In addressing these questions, this study seeks to inform diagnostic decision‐making and resource utilization in settings with diagnostic uncertainty.

Methods

This was a single‐site retrospective cohort study utilizing the electronic medical records of patients evaluated at a single academic medical center. The study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Southern California. The study population consisted of patients presenting with previously undiagnosed cardinal sinonasal symptoms (nasal obstruction, nasal discharge, facial pain or pressure, and loss of smell) to outpatient rhinology clinics from January 2020 to April 2025. Inclusion criteria included nasal endoscopy on the initial clinic visit, and an available sinus CT scan performed either prior to or after the initial visit in the setting of ≥ 12 weeks of symptoms. Patients with a prior diagnosis of CRS or a history of sinus surgery were excluded. Demographic characteristics, cardinal symptoms, SNOT‐22, and modified Lund–Kennedy (LK) endoscopic scores were abstracted from the medical record. The number of cardinal symptoms was summarized as a “cardinal score,” defined as the total number of reported cardinal CRS symptoms (range 0–4). All sinus CT scans were graded according to the LM scoring criteria [18]. Two independent reviewers provided scores for each CT, with adjudication by a third independent reviewer in cases of scoring discrepancies.

All statistical analyses were performed using R packages. The analytic cohort included consecutive adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with available LM scores. For binary analysis, CT‐CRS was defined by an LM score ≥ 4. Patients with LM scores < 4 comprised the comparison group, reflecting patients with sinonasal symptoms who did not meet radiographic criteria for CT‐CRS regardless of endoscopic findings. To explore whether associations between symptoms, endoscopic findings, and radiographic disease varied by population, additional analytic subgroups were determined based on prespecified characteristics: Asian versus non‐Asian race, and Hispanic versus non‐Hispanic ethnicity.

Baseline characteristics were summarized with means and standard deviations for continuous outcomes, except for LK scores, which were summarized with median and interquartile range (IQR) due to non‐normality. Categorical variables were summarized by total count and proportions [n (%)]. Between‐group comparisons utilized chi‐square testing or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, t‐testing for continuous variables, and Wilcoxon rank‐sum for LK scores. Univariable logistic regression models were used to estimate the association between clinical symptoms, symptom scores, endoscopic scores, and CT‐CRS. Multivariable models were then fitted to evaluate these associations while adjusting for the four cardinal symptoms, total SNOT‐22 score, total LK score, and demographic covariates, including age, gender, race, and ethnicity. Because LK and SNOT‐22 scores were not available for all patients, analyses involving these variables were performed using complete‐case data for each model. A sensitivity analysis was performed to include patients with missing LK and SNOT‐22 data to assess robustness of results. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was defined by a two‐sided p‐value ≤ 0.05.

Results

A total of 314 patients were included in the final analytic cohort (Table 1). The median interval from endoscopy to CT was 16.0 days (IQR 5.0–36.3 days). Of these, 228 (73%) met criteria for CT‐CRS (LM score ≥ 4), while 86 (27%) did not. The mean age of the cohort was 48.3 ± 17.0 years and was not significantly different between CT‐CRS and non‐CRS groups (49.4 ± 17.2 vs. 45.6 ± 16.3 years, p = 0.082). Male sex was more common among patients with CT‐CRS compared with those without (54% vs. 34%, p = 0.002). Patients with CT‐CRS were less likely to be White and more likely to be Asian compared with non‐CRS patients (39% vs. 54% and 29% vs. 18%, respectively, p = 0.043). Hispanic ethnicity was not significantly different between groups (19% in both, p = 0.991). Nasal polyps were observed exclusively among patients with CT‐CRS (10% vs. 0%, p = 0.002). The prevalence of individual cardinal symptoms was similar between patients with and without CT‐CRS, including nasal obstruction (p = 0.977), nasal discharge (p = 0.959), and facial pain or pressure (p = 0.647). Hyposmia was more common among patients with CT‐CRS (42% vs. 29%, p = 0.034). Median LK scores were significantly higher among patients with CT‐CRS (2.0 vs. 1.0, p < 0.001). Of note, LK scores were available for 204 patients and SNOT‐22 scores were available for 271 patients.

In univariable logistic regression analysis, the presence of hyposmia was associated with CT‐CRS (OR 1.77, 95% CI [1.05–3.07], p = 0.035). Other individual cardinal symptoms were not significantly associated with CT‐CRS. Similarly, the presence of two or more cardinal symptoms and total SNOT‐22 score did not demonstrate a significant association with CT‐CRS (Table 2). After adjustment for clinical and demographic covariates, reported hyposmia remained significantly associated with CT‐CRS (OR 1.89, 95% CI [1.08–3.24], p = 0.039). Sensitivity analysis results were consistent with this analysis, with no meaningful changes in effect estimates or statistical significance.

Among patients with negative nasal endoscopy findings (LK = 0), demographic characteristics were similar between those with and without CT‐CRS (Table 3). Patients without CT‐CRS were more likely to be female compared with those with CT‐CRS (65% vs. 51%, p = 0.034). The prevalence of individual cardinal symptoms did not differ significantly between groups, including nasal obstruction (p = 0.256), facial pain or pressure (p = 0.520), and hyposmia (p = 0.411). In contrast, nasal discharge was significantly less commonly reported among patients with CT‐CRS with a negative nasal endoscopy (p = 0.017). Similarly, the presence of two or more cardinal symptoms was significantly less frequent among patients with CT‐CRS (56% vs. 82%, p = 0.011). When stratified by nasal endoscopy status, symptom associations with CT‐CRS differed by endoscopic findings (Table 4). Specifically, among patients with a negative nasal endoscopy, reported nasal discharge was associated with a lower risk of CT‐CRS (OR 0.26, 95% CI [0.07–0.88], p = 0.036).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of patients presenting with previously undiagnosed sinonasal symptoms, higher LK endoscopy scores were strongly associated with CT‐CRS and overall radiographic disease burden as measured by LM scores. This relationship persisted across several models, underscoring the diagnostic utility of nasal endoscopy in the evaluation of suspected CRS. These results are concordant with prior studies comparing diagnostic nasal endoscopy to CT findings in patients with CRS, where LK‐based endoscopy scores demonstrated high diagnostic performance relative to CT, with reported sensitivities exceeding 90% and overall diagnostic accuracy above 85% when nasal endoscopy is compared with CT‐CRS [10, 19]. Taken together, these findings support the reliability of nasal endoscopy as a first‐line diagnostic modality that may reduce unnecessary CT imaging and associated radiation exposure when endoscopic findings are clearly positive. Of note, even with positive nasal endoscopy, CT imaging may still be necessary later in the clinical course for management, especially if there is consideration for surgical intervention.

When examining the diagnostic efficacy of the four cardinal symptoms, reported hyposmia was consistently associated with CT‐CRS. This association was significant in overall analyses and most examined subgroups, except for Asian and Hispanic patients. In contrast, nasal obstruction and facial pain or pressure did not demonstrate consistent associations with CT‐CRS, consistent with international consensus and prior studies which highlight the lack of positive predictive value of these symptoms with CT‐CRS. Given the reported high prevalence of cardinal symptoms among the general US population, these symptoms lack specificity for CRS, and the presence of facial pain has even been found to potentially decrease the likelihood of CRS diagnosis [20]. Specifically, these cardinal symptoms may be related to other neurogenic or allergic causes, thus contributing to their limited predictive ability for CRS. More recent work examined the relationship between individual cardinal symptoms to sinus‐specific CT opacification, demonstrating that loss of smell and taste was specifically correlated with sphenoid and posterior ethmoid drainage on CT [21]. This anatomic pattern points to a plausible explanation for the observed association between olfactory dysfunction and radiographic disease burden. Furthermore, a cross‐sectional study reported approximately two‐thirds of adults who met symptom criteria for CRS reported subjective hyposmia [22], demonstrating the symptom's high prevalence among CRS patients and supporting its role as a clinically informative symptom in diagnostic evaluation.

In contrast, the associations of hyposmia and nasal endoscopy findings associated with CT‐CRS were not present among Asian patients. This suggests CT imaging may warrant earlier consideration when evaluating suspected CRS among Asian patients, as symptoms and endoscopic findings may be less reliable among this group. Additionally, the higher prevalence of CT‐CRS observed among Asian patients in this cohort emphasizes potential differences in disease presentation or diagnostic patterns compared with non‐Asian patients. While later‐stage presentations could possibly contribute to an increased likelihood of meeting radiographic diagnostic criteria, the current data are limited in the assessment of disease duration and existing literature does not clearly support this explanation. Nevertheless, prior studies have reported an increased incidence of mixed tissue inflammation on histologic examination of nasal polyps from Asian American patients with CRS when compared with Caucasians [23]. Future research incorporating longitudinal symptoms data, endoscopic findings, and immunologic or histopathologic characterization is needed to determine how these factors may contribute to differences in symptoms, endoscopic findings, and the overall prevalence of CRS in Asian patients.

The lack of significant associations between hyposmia and LK score with CT‐CRS was also seen among Hispanic patients in the subgroup analysis, suggesting differences in the symptomatic profile of CRS among Hispanic patients as well. In contrast to Asian patients, prior literature examining CRS health disparities has reported more severe baseline symptoms and sinonasal symptom burden among Hispanic patients when compared with non‐Hispanic patients [24]. Additionally, a cohort study examining a South Florida Hispanic population found that Hispanic patients had worse SNOT‐22 scores, a higher likelihood of possessing severe CRS markers, and a longer sinonasal symptom duration [25]. However, these studies did not support the lack of symptom and LK score association with CT‐CRS found among Hispanic patients. Thus, further investigation is required to understand the clinical presentation of CRS among Hispanic patients.

When examining factors associated with CT‐CRS despite negative nasal endoscopy, no individual symptom was associated with imaging findings. This suggests symptoms alone have limited predictability of CT‐CRS when endoscopy is negative. Moreover, patients who reported nasal discharge or two or more cardinal symptoms paradoxically had a lower risk of CT‐CRS after a negative nasal endoscopy. This finding differs from a prior study in which mucopurulent discharge seen on nasal endoscopy was a predictive factor for CT‐CRS [26]. However, our study was specifically examining patient‐reported nasal discharge in the setting of a negative nasal endoscopy rather than a finding on endoscopy, highlighting the critical distinction between symptoms and objective findings. Together, our study's findings suggest that when nasal endoscopy is negative, symptom burden, such as multiple cardinal symptoms and nasal discharge, may reflect non‐CRS etiologies rather than occult CRS. Conversely, patients with fewer cardinal symptoms despite a negative nasal endoscopy may still harbor radiographic disease, underscoring the limited ability of symptoms and a negative nasal endoscopy to exclude CRS and the continued importance of CT imaging in this clinical context. Further research should continue exploring patient characteristics or other predictive factors associated with CT‐CRS when nasal endoscopy is negative.

This study's strengths include the relatively large cohort and substantial representation of Asian and Hispanic subgroups. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, there was limited representation of other racial groups within the cohort, particularly African Americans and Native Americans, limiting generalizability. Second, complete LK (65.0% of cohort) and SNOT‐22 scores (86.3% of cohort) were not available for all patients, which may introduce potential selection bias. Third, the retrospective design precluded standardized symptom assessment scores among the rhinology clinics included in this study. Fourth, the timing between nasal endoscopy and CT imaging was not controlled, with inclusion criteria requiring only for CT imaging to occur around patients' initial visit. Given that the interval between CT and endoscopy was not standardized, interim medical treatment could not reliably be ascertained and may have influenced our findings. Finally, unmeasured confounders such as significant septal deviation, severe turbinate hypertrophy, and other causes of hyposmia may have influenced both symptoms and objective findings, but were not accounted for in the present study. The comparison group was defined radiographically rather than as a clinically defined non‐CRS group, which may have included heterogeneous non‐CRS etiologies with overlapping sinonasal symptoms.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study, the association of hyposmia and nasal endoscopic findings with CT‐CRS was not statistically significant among Asian and Hispanic patients. These findings suggest that symptom and endoscopy‐based paradigms may perform differently across racial and ethnic groups and be less reliable in certain populations, warranting a lower threshold for CT imaging when clinical suspicion persists. Furthermore, the lack of positive symptom associations with CT‐CRS among patients with a negative nasal endoscopy underscores the limited ability of symptoms and nasal endoscopy to identify radiographic disease. Future research should further examine the influence of demographic factors on CRS diagnosis and refine diagnostic strategies to improve accuracy across diverse patient populations.
