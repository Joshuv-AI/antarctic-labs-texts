Investigating Medical Radiation Awareness of Saudi Arabian Cardiologists in Catheterization Labs: A Survey Study
Authors: Abdulmajeed Alotaibi, Raseel Alsallum, Reemas Alotaibi, Ali M. Aldhebaib, Noha Althubaity, Oinam Gokulchandra Singh, Zyad M. Almutlaq, Abdulaziz M. Albaraikan, Winnie Philip, Abrar M. Al-Mutairi, Mostafa Alqarras, Basma Alrowailli, Abdulrahman Alkhulaifi, Dhaifallah Ziyad Alanazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:To assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of interventional cardiologists in Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) regarding occupational radiation safety. Occupational radiation exposure poses significant long-term health risks for interventional cardiologists. Understanding current safety knowledge and practices is essential for guiding improvement strategies.Methods:A multicenter, cross-sectional survey was administered across several cardiac centers in KSA. The survey evaluated radiation safety awareness, use of dosimetry monitoring, and adherence to protective measures among practicing interventional cardiologists.Results:The study identified notable gaps in radiation safety knowledge and considerable inconsistencies in dosimetry monitoring. Despite these shortcomings, participants demonstrated high compliance with fundamental protective practices, particularly the routine use of personal protective equipment.Conclusion:The findings underscore the need for continuous professional development, more stringent institutional radiation safety protocols, and stronger enforcement mechanisms. Enhancing these measures is crucial to minimizing long-term occupational risks for interventional cardiologists and ensuring alignment with international radiation safety standards.

Introduction

The harmful health-related impacts that can be caused by x-ray exposure are strongly evidenced through extensive experimental research and a substantial body of scientific data [1]. The radiation exposure received by the medical staff has grown significantly in the last several decades, especially for those involved in fluoroscopy-guided procedures. Healthcare staff involved in intervention techniques, such as coronary catheterization, are frequently exposed to ionizing radiation that may potentially lead to a high-risk level of immediate and long-term health effects [2,3]. Interventional cardiologists in the catheterization laboratories (cath labs) are vulnerable within these specialties. Their mean accumulated annual radiation dose is estimated to be 2–5 millisieverts (mSv), although this number varies according to procedures performed and adherence to safe operating conditions [4].

Epidemiological evidence suggests that the lifetime mortality from cancer increases by an estimated 0.05 for every 10 mSv of radiation exposure. At the same level of exposure, the risk for nonfatal malignancies is almost twice as high [4]. When compared to the baseline lifetime cancer risk, which is about 1 in 3 people in the general population, these numbers indicate that there may be 1 case of radiation-induced cancer for every 80 to 200 interventional cardiologists over a 25-year career [4,5]. However, there are other health effects associated with exposure to occupational radiation besides carcinogenicity. These effects fall into neurological disorders, ocular conditions like posterior subcapsular cataracts, reproductive health problems, psychological diseases including anxiety, depression, and cognitive dysfunction [6,7,8,9,10]. At the same time, carrying protective gear (thyroid-protective shields and lead aprons) is considered necessary to protect against doses of radiation. However, the continuous use of such devices is associated with spinal degeneration, orthopedic and chronic musculoskeletal pain, all of which affect quality of life and presenteeism in healthcare workers [11,12].

Both healthcare institutions and society at large face the repercussions of these occupational hazards. Radiation-related health shocks impose substantial burdens, including direct medical costs, lost income, and the depletion of skilled labor due to illness or incapacity [13]. One in every 10 cath lab technologists and 1 in 4 cath lab nurses report having experienced negative health effects resulting from exposure to radiation [4]. In addition, studies have indicated that inconsistent utilization of radiation protection measures and non-formal training worsen the problem [14,15].

Against this background, the study aims to assess Saudi cardiologists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to occupational radiation hazards. Using a web-based survey, this study evaluates the following domains: i) knowledge deficiencies, ii) perceived barriers, and iii) current practices. The outcomes of this study will support the establishment of educational programs, the harmonization of safety procedures, and the improvement of dosimetry compliance, which may lead to reducing exposure to radiation, promoting safer work practices.

Methods

This study is a cross-sectional-observational study that was conducted across major Saudi cardiac centers, these centers are specifically within the central, western, and eastern regions. Data collection took place between April 2025 and November 2025 in institutions with active catheterization laboratories, including National Guard Hospitals, King Fahad Medical City, and Armed Forces Hospitals. The study received ethical approval from the King Abdullah International Medical Research Centre Institutional Review Board (NRR25/028/1, approved 22/04/2025).

This study targeted Saudi interventional cardiologists who were routinely exposed to ionizing radiation during invasive cardiac procedures. This study recruited Saudi board-certified or board-eligible cardiologists performing interventional procedures at the time of the study. Incomplete survey responses were also excluded. The survey link was digitally disseminated through the Saudi Cardiology Society, cardiology departments and centers, interventional cardiology online channels and forums, and national professional conferences. The participation was voluntary, and all participants were not forced to continue the survey. Also, the participation was anonymous and uncompensated.

The survey comprised closed-ended questions to evaluate radiation knowledge, safety practices, and utilization of personal protective equipment (PPE) among the targeted cardiologists. The first domain focused on knowledge-based items to assess the understanding of the advantages of collimation, radiographic views such as the left anterior oblique (LAO) cranial projection, recommended calibration intervals for cath lab equipment, the most reliable predictors of radiation exposure, and the recommended occupational dose limits. The second domain evaluated the safety measures and radiation-minimization techniques. Participants reported the frequency with which they used low-dose fluoroscopy modes, applied proper collimation, terminated cine runs promptly, selected appropriate frame rates for fluoroscopy and cine angiography, positioned the image receptor close to the patient's chest, and used high-dose projections such as the LAO cranial view. These items were scored on a 3-point frequency scale: always, occasionally, or never. The third domain assessed PPE utilization and understanding the purpose of protective equipment during catheterization procedures.

The data was collected and stored in a password-protected excel sheet and was accessible to only the research group members. Personal identifiers were collected. Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS version 28 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, United States of America). To summarize participant characteristics and responses, descriptive analysis was performed after the data cleaning and entry. Categorical variables were presented as percentages and frequencies. Continuous variables were expressed as standard deviations and means. The associations between categorical variables such as knowledge items, adherence to radiation safety practices, and demographics were examined using the Chi-square test.

Results

This study involved 88 healthcare professionals who successfully completed the survey. According to the workplace of the participants, the results showed that 36 (40.9%) were affiliated with the National Guard, 37 (42%) with the Armed Forces, and 14 (15.9%) with King Fahad Medical City. Most of the participants specialized in cardiology, 79 (89.8%), followed by a smaller group of radiologists (3.4%). However, few participants (6.8%) did not disclose their specialty (Table 1).

Regarding questions based on knowledge, a number of 69 (78.4%) participants accurately recognized the left anterior oblique cranial (LAO cranial) perspective as the most perilous. Just 15 (17%) individuals accurately recognized the suggested calibration interval for cath lab equipment (every 6 months to 1 year). A number of 27 (30.7%) participants accurately identified dose area product (DAP) as the best measure of radiation exposure. Sixty-nine (78.4%) had knowledge of the global guidelines for occupational radiation dose limits (20 mSv/year averaged over 5 years, with a maximum of 30 mSv in any single year) (Table 2).

Most cath lab personnel consistently followed recommended safety procedures as shown in Table 3. Several participants (76.1%) answered that they always utilized low fluoroscopy mode, while 75% of them always adhered to proper collimation. A percentage of 47.7% and 50% of respondents consistently performed the timely cessation of cine recording and the appropriate choice of frame rates for fluoroscopy and cine angiography (Table 3). Furthermore, 64.8% reported always positioning the image detector closer to the patient's chest to reduce exposure, and 77.3% regularly used the left anterior oblique cranial view when necessary. Regarding techniques used for personal safety, 75% of the participants reported that they always implemented ceiling or table-mounted shielding devices, while 79.5% of the participants reported that they routinely applied thermoluminescent dosimeter (TLD). The percentage of workers who always followed up on dosimeter readings was lower at 68.2% (Fig. 1). The use of personal protective equipment was shown as a well-appreciated practice among the respondents. The majority of respondents (98.9%) recognized the necessity for protective measures during cath lab procedures, signifying a commendable level of knowledge on radiation safety (Fig. 2). These findings would indicate an overall high compliance with protocol and self-protection in the group.

Discussion

The study demonstrated that Saudi interventional cardiologists have a high level of awareness and practice of radiation safety protocols [16]. Most of the participants demonstrated excellent knowledge of dangerous imaging modalities, with 78.4% correctly identifying the LAO cranial view as high-risk [17]. Nevertheless, technical knowledge in some areas was lacking, such as periodic equipment calibration (properly answered only by 17% of respondents) and DAP as the important dosimetry indicator for measuring exposure (recognized properly by only 30.7% of the participants) [18].

For clinical implementation, a large proportion of the respondents frequently applied essential procedural safety measures such as proper collimation (75%) and low fluoroscopy mode (76.1%) [16]. The use rates of dosimeters and shields fixed on tables and ceilings were 79.5% and 75% [17]. However, only 68.2% said they regularly checked dosimeter readings, suggesting that routine monitoring could be improved [19]. Nearly all participants (98.9%, Fig. 1) clearly appreciated personal protective equipment, indicating a strong safety culture. Physical challenges related to the long-term use of personal protective equipment, however, is still a concern, as it can potentially impact long-term occupational well-being and musculoskeletal health [20].

A particular strength of this article is that it deals with an under-theorized issue locally by focusing on a specialized but high-risk occupational group. The study may improve the generalization of the findings within Saudi cardiac centers in different parts of the country. Also, a sufficient clinical practice combined with knowledge provides a general overview of safety in cath labs. However, this study had certain limitations. First, there was limited access to interventional cardiologists due to their busy schedules. Second, the data collected was self-reported, which may be affected by bias. The findings showed a high level of compliance with safety protocols, which is encouraging, but they also highlight gaps that must be filled. Future studies should be designed longitudinally to track changes in radiation safety practices over time, eliminating the limitations of self-reported information. Recruitment strategies may be improved by integrating surveys into professional activities to overcome limited accessibility to interventional cardiologists. Expanding research to other high-exposure specialties could provide a broader understanding of safety culture across clinical settings. Furthermore, assessing structured training programs, awareness initiatives, and hospital policy enforcement will be essential for improving radiation safety practices in cath labs.

In conclusion, this study generally demonstrates a good radiation safety practice and understanding among Saudi interventional cardiologists. Most participants could recognize high-risk imaging modalities and frequently practiced significant preventive measures. However, problems regarding noncompliance with radiation monitoring follow-ups and lack of technical knowledge remain. The physical burden of personal protective equipment could also be considered. This data underscores the need for ongoing education, increased surveillance, and institutional support to promote safety in the cath lab.
