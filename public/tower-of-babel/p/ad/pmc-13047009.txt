Immune checkpoint inhibitor–related myocarditis in patients with thymic epithelial tumors: a retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Haiyan Zeng, Jin kang, Sijia Pu, Chang Dai, Junrong Jiang, Yanlin Chen, Yuhan Chen, Lu Fu, Huiyi Liu, Mengmeng Guo, Hong Yi, Haowei Chen, Wenzhao Zhong, Yumei Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitor-related myocarditis (ICI-M) is increasingly recognized, but real-world data in thymic epithelial tumors (TET) remain limited.ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical outcomes, focusing on ICI-M, in patients with TET receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI).MethodsThis retrospective study examined patients with TET at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital in China from January 2018 to March 2024. We assessed the biomarker changes by comparing the baseline values to peak levels during ICI therapy. Clinical characteristics, disease progression, and outcomes of patients with ICI-M were also analyzed. To investigate the relationships between clinical factors or biomarkers and all-cause mortality, we performed univariable Cox regression analysis.ResultsAmong 31 patients, 7 developed ICI-M, all in the thymoma subgroup (7/12, 58.3%). During a median follow-up of 494 days (262–858), 13 patients died (13/31, 41.9%), including 6 cardiovascular deaths (46.2% of deaths). N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and Creatine kinase (CK) increased significantly during ICI therapy. In univariable Cox analysis, each doubling of CK was associated with higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.22 per doubling, 95% CI 1.01–1.47; P = 0.038).ConclusionsIn this cohort of TET patients receiving ICI therapy, myocarditis occurred frequently in thymoma patients. Overall mortality was substantial in TET patients, with nearly half of the deaths attributable to cardiovascular causes. These findings highlight the need for vigilant cardiovascular monitoring in all TET patients undergoing ICI therapy and warrant confirmation in larger multicenter studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04362-7.Keywords: Cardio-oncology, Myocarditis, Thymic epithelial tumors, Immune checkpoint inhibitor

Introduction

Thymic epithelial tumors (TET), including thymomas and thymic carcinomas, are rare tumors, and for recurrent or metastatic TET, systemic treatment options are limited [1]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) have progressively emerged as a key therapeutic approach in clinical practice [2]. However, TET patients frequently present with immunological features and autoimmune-related comorbidities, complicating the risk–benefit balance of ICI treatment in this population [3, 4]

Recent evidence has heightened awareness of cardiovascular risks in patients with TET from various perspectives. Long-term cohort data in thymic neoplasms have suggested a non-negligible burden of cardiovascular events requiring sustained surveillance, largely in the context of conventional therapies such as anthracyclines and radiotherapy [5]. In parallel, multi-source mechanistic and pharmacovigilance analyses have demonstrated that thymic alterations and immune dysregulation are linked to heightened susceptibility to Immune checkpoint inhibitor-related myocarditis (ICI-M), particularly in thymoma [6]. Meanwhile, the clinical literature in TET still relies heavily on case reports/series and narrative reviews that describe severe presentations and provide important early safety signals [7], however, because denominators are often unclear and follow-up is heterogeneous, these descriptive reports seldom allow reliable estimation of incidence or prognosis. Therefore, real-world evidence regarding the occurrence of ICI-M and cardiovascular outcomes among ICI-treated TET patients remains limited.

Therefore, we conducted a real-world retrospective cohort study of ICI-treated TET patients to characterize ICI-M and to describe cardiovascular outcomes and prognosis during follow-up.

Materials and methods

Study population

This is a single-center retrospective study carried out at the guangdong people’s provincial hospital in Guangzhou, China. We collected data from patients diagnosed with TET at our institute during Jan 2018 ~ March 2024. Patients aged above 18 years old, TET diagnosis was confirmed by histological pathology, and had completed at least one infusion of ICI therapy were included. Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with pre‑existing autoimmune diseases and a history of myocarditis, or no follow-up results were available after the treatment.

Definition and data collection

The diagnostic criteria for ICI-M were based on the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for clinically suspected myocarditis [8]. Immune‑related adverse events (irAEs) were defined according to the clinical practice guidelines of the American Society of Clinical Oncology [9], with all grade I–IV events included. Immune‑related hepatitis was defined as asymptomatic or symptomatic elevations of serum AST and/or ALT above the upper limit of normal (ULN), with or without concomitant bilirubin changes. Myositis is identified by the presence of muscle weakness and/or myalgia, along with elevated muscle enzymes, such as creatine kinase (CK). Baseline data, including demographic characteristics, comorbidities, laboratory examinations, in‐hospital treatments, and medications prescribed at discharge, were collected according to the electronic medical management system of the Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital. Follow‑up was defined from the first ICI dose to death or last contact (censor date: 20 November 2024). All‑cause mortality was ascertained via the Guangdong Provincial Death Certification Management Information System or telephone follow‑up, and the final known survival time was based on the date of the most recent follow‑up.

Biomarkers and ECG data were collected. For myocarditis cases, information of clinical presentation, biomarkers, echocardiograms, echocardiography findings, additional irAEs, and hospital outcomes was gathered.

Clinical factors included anthracycline use, smoking, radiation exposure, cardiovascular risk factors, and cardiac medications.

Cardiovascular risk factors were defined as the presence of at least one of the following: history of ischemic heart disease, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, or stroke.

Cardiac medications were defined as the use of at least one of the following: angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACE inhibitors), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), beta blockers, anti-platelet agents, or statins.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (IQR) according to their distribution and compared using the t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while categorical variables were presented as N(%) and compared using Fisher’s exact test. Missing data were imputed using Predictive Mean Matching (PMM) with 30 iterations.

The cardiac injury biomarkers analyzed included cardiac troponin T (cTnT, pg/mL), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP, pg/mL), creatine kinase (CK, U/L), and creatine kinase-MB isoenzyme (CK-MB, U/L). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH,U/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were also recorded. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals.

We carried out univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis to assess the association between clinical factors, ratio (Δ) of peak to baseline values of these biomarkers, and all-cause mortality.

The results are presented as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05, using a two-tailed test. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). (Table 1)

Results

Patient characteristics

As depicted in Fig. 1, the analysis included 31 patients who received ICI therapy, comprising 20 males (64.52%) and 11 females (35.48%), with a median age of 55 years (44.5–61.0). The median BMI was 22.14 kg/m2 (19.81–23.99). Patients with 12 TM (38.71%) and 19 TC (61.29%) were included in the study. Among these individuals, 12.90% received ICI as monotherapy, while 87.10% received ICI in combination with chemotherapy or other therapies, with a median treatment cycle of 2 (1–3.5). Among these 31 patients, except for 1 case with a history of ischemic heart disease, there were no other significant cardiovascular histories.

Baseline and peak biomarker values before and after immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy

After a median of 2 cycles (1–3.5) of ICI therapy, the peak levels of cardiac injury biomarkers were collected during treatment, showing significant increases compared to baseline values (Table 2). Notably, there was an elevation in cTnT levels following ICI therapy compared to baseline (12.20[5.50–13.20] pg/mL vs. 30.20[8.10–1456.00] pg/mL, P = 0.001), as well as NT‐proBNP (60.40 [52.50–89.20] pg/mL vs. 1149.00[68.10–5696.00] pg/mL, P < 0.001), CK(60.00 [39.00–115.00] U/L vs. 111.00 [45.50–414.00] U/L, P = 0.039). Meanwhile, other biomarkers also showed elevated levels, including AST (21.00 [16.00–23.50] U/L vs. 24.00 [20.00–107.50] U/L, P = 0.005) and ALT (14.00 [11.00–21.50] U/L vs. 19.00 [13.00–146.00] U/L, P = 0.011). However, no significant changes were observed in CK-MB and LDH and eGFR.

Clinical features, treatment and patient outcomes in cases of myocarditis associated with immune checkpoint inhibitors

A total of seven cases of ICI-M occurred after ICI therapy. Individual characteristics and case summaries of patients are presented in sTable 2. Among these patients (all with TM), the median time from checkpoint therapy initiation to clinical myocarditis onset was 15 days (14.00–21.00). Palpitations (57.14%) and muscle weakness (57.14%) were the most common symptom in these patients. Other symptoms included shortness of breath, ptosis, myalgias, and fatigue. Simultaneously, In addition to ICI-M, other irAEs were observed in the 7 patients with ICI-M, including 2 cases of myositis–myocarditis–myasthenia gravis overlap syndrome, as well as other irAEs such as pneumonitis and thyroiditis.

Of the 7 ICI-M patients, 5 had baseline ECGs prior to ICI therapy, all of which showed no significant abnormalities, while 2 patients had no pre-treatment ECG records. After the diagnosis of ICI-M, ECG abnormalities were observed in all 7 patients (100%), with the most common findings being high-grade atrioventricular block (42.86%) and ventricular arrhythmias (57.14%), other included atrial fibrillation (14.29%) and ST segment changes (14.29%). Coronary angiography was performed to evaluate ischemia in 4 patients with severe myocarditis, revealing no significant coronary narrowing. Left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF) ≤ 50% was seen in 3 out of 7 patients. Concerning myocarditis management, all patients received glucocorticoids, mainly with initial intravenous pulses of 500–1000 mg. Other modalities of treatment are detailed in sTable2. During the follow-up period, Among these 7 myocarditis patients, 5 patients died, with 4 deaths attributed to myocarditis complications and 1 due to cancer progression.

These 7 patients underwent serial cardiac injury biomarkers (cTnT, CK, CK-MB, NT-proBNP) measurements after admission for myocarditis, with results illustrated in Fig. 2. As shown, Patients 1, 2, and 4 had cTnT levels exceeding 6000 pg/mL and died within one week of hospitalization. Patient 3 had cTnT levels peaking at around 5000 pg/ml at admission and gradually decreasing to below the upper limit of normal (ULN) over a period of 5 months and died of heart failure later. Additionally, except for the deceased patients (Patients 1, 2, and 4), CK-MB and CK levels in all other patients returned to normal within one month. Prognosis

During follow-up of median 494 days (262–858), 13 patients (41.94%) died from any cause. Among them, 6 patients (46.15%) died from cardiovascular causes, including 4 deaths due to myocarditis-related complications (heart failure, 2 cases of arrhythmia, cardiac arrest), one death due to myocardial infarction, and one death due to arrhythmia. Discussion

TET, as a rare thoracic tumor, has limited cohort studies investigating the cardiotoxicity associated with ICI therapy. However, regular reports of ICI-M in recent case studies and pharmacovigilance alerts highlight the urgent need for a comprehensive investigation. In our study, we analyzed nearly six years of TET data, 31 TET patients treated with ICI were included. The main findings of this study are as follows: First, among 31 ICI-treated TET patients, 7 developed ICI-M, all of whom had thymoma (7/12, 58.3%). Second, during follow-up, 13 patients died (13/31, 41.9%), and 6 deaths were attributed to cardiovascular causes (6/13, 46.2%). Third, among the biomarkers assessed, only dynamic changes in CK during treatment were significantly associated with all-cause mortality in univariable analysis.

Myocardial injury and indicators during ICI treatment in TET

Myocardial injury is a rare occurrence during ICI therapy; its manifestations can range from mild, such as elevated cardiac markers and ECG changes, to severe, such as heart failure and fatal heart block [11]. Biomarkers are early and critical indicators of myocardial injury and play a significant role in diagnosing ICI-M. In our study, patients showed significant elevations in cTnT and NT-ProBNP levels. These results raise concerns about potential cardiotoxic effects during the ICI treatment in TET. Similarly, a significant increase was observed in CK, ALT and AST. This suggests that cardiovascular events in the ICI may coincide with other irAEs. Therefore, strengthening the assessment of baseline levels of these biomarkers and closely monitoring them during treatment is extremely important.

Before ICI therapy, TET-associated myocarditis cases were rarely reported. In a retrospective study, the authors included 1030 post-surgical TM patients, with 13 (about 1.26%) showing autoimmune hepatitis and myocarditis [12]. However, a clinical trial showed following ICI treatment, myocarditis occurred in 5% of thymic carcinoma patients and 43–57% of TM patients [13]. In contrast, non-TET patients have a risk of ICI-induced myocarditis of 1.68% [14]. In our study, among the 12 TM patients treated with ICI, seven developed ICI-M, all myocarditis cases were TM. This is a high incidence rate, which is higher than with previous studies.

Previous studies show that biomarkers, including cTnT, remain elevated for months after diagnosis of ICI-M [15, 16], which seems to reflect the immune system’s persistent activation or chronic myocarditis. Consistently, our study presented a patient with myocarditis who had cTnT levels below the upper limit of normal over a period of 5 months. Previous studies have shown that, compared to other cancers, myocarditis in TET patients occurs earlier after ICI initiation and often results in severe outcomes, such as arrhythmias, myositis, respiratory failure, and death [6]. In our study, nearly 100% of myocarditis along with arrhythmias, particularly ventricular arrhythmia and high-grade AV block, Meanwhile, three patients experienced decreased cardiac function due to immune myocarditis.

Cardiovascular death and prognosis

A large population-based study has reported the risk of cardiovascular death as the highest within the first year following cancer diagnosis [17] and previous studies have shown that the mortality rate of ICI-M was very high, at approximately 47% [11]. In our study, among the 7 patients diagnosed with ICI-M, a total of 5 deaths (71%) occurred. Considering its high mortality rate, accurate prognostication becomes increasingly crucial. Previous studies have shown that an increase in CK during ICI therapy is independently associated with the all-cause mortality [18], which is consistent with our findings. In our study, the majority of patients died within the first year of ICI treatment. Among the 12 thymoma patients, 4 died from complications related to myocarditis. Although no cases of ICI-M were recorded in thymic carcinoma patients, 2 of the deceased thymic carcinoma patients died from arrhythmia and myocardial infarction, respectively.

Immunological mechanisms underlying ICI-M in TET

Although this study is not aimed at elucidating new immunological mechanisms, our findings should be interpreted within the established immunological context specific to TET. Thymoma is associated with defects in immune tolerance, including impaired negative selection, abnormal AIRE expression, and defective regulatory T-cell function, which leads to the escape of autoreactive T-cells into the peripheral circulation, creating a potential state of immune dysregulation [19, 20]. Based on this, ICI (especially PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors) further disrupt the ‘immune checkpoint’ of peripheral immune tolerance, potentially exacerbating pre-existing autoimmune abnormalities [21, 22]. Previous studies have shown that ICI-M and myositis are primarily characterized by T-cell-mediated autoimmunity, with clonal expansion of infiltrating T-cells observed in the affected myocardial and skeletal muscle tissues [21]. These findings suggest a potential cross-reactive immune response targeting muscle-related antigens, such as α-myosin [23]. It remains to be determined whether thymic myoid cells can more specifically trigger pathological autoreactive T-cells with a myocardium-enriched profile (such as α-myosin), thereby explaining the extremely high incidence of ICI-M in TET patients [6]. Therefore, rather than conducting more clinical trials with different therapeutic combinations, it may be more beneficial to focus on studying the mechanisms of resistance to ICI. Similarly, prior to undertaking additional clinical trials, further exploration of the mechanisms of irAEs during ICI treatment in TET is warranted.

Implications and Limitations

Taken together, our findings provide incremental real-world evidence on ICI-M in an underrepresented, high-risk TET population, which has previously been described mainly through case reports and small heterogeneous series. In this cohort of TET patients receiving ICIs, cardiovascular causes appeared to constitute a meaningful component of mortality, underscoring the importance of increased cardio-oncology awareness and standardized monitoring in future multicenter studies. However, this study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective cohort with a limited sample size (reflecting both the rarity of TET and the relatively limited real-world use of ICI in this population), which constrained statistical power and precluded complex multivariable modeling and robust subgroup inference. Second, all ICI-M cases occurred in the thymoma subgroup, whereas no events were observed in the thymic carcinoma subgroup; therefore, we could not reliably estimate or compare the risk of ICI-M in thymic carcinoma, and the absence of observed events should not be interpreted as evidence of no risk. Third, biomarker data were retrospectively collected from routine clinical practice, with non-standardized testing time points and frequencies and substantial missingness for some markers; although imputation was applied, these biomarker analyses should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that among 31 TET patients receiving ICI therapy, myocarditis occurred more frequently in thymoma patients, with 7 out of 12 thymoma patients (58.33%) diagnosed with ICI-M. During a median follow-up of 494 days, approximately half of TET patients died from cardiovascular events, highlighting the need for cardiovascular risk assessment, monitoring, and risk reduction strategies in this population.
