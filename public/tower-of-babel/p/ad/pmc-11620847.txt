Elevated aqueous TNF‐α levels are associated with more severe functional and anatomic findings in eyes with diabetic macular oedema
Authors: Sky Chew, Tuan Tran, Paul Sanfilippo, Lyndell L. Lim, Sukhpal S. Sandhu, Sanjeewa Wickremasinghe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntravitreal ranibizumab for diabetic macular oedema (DMO) has been recently shown to modulate levels of aqueous cytokines. This study investigates the associations between changes in aqueous cytokine levels following intravitreal ranibizumab therapy and the corresponding anatomical and functional changes in the eye.MethodsTwenty‐five patients comprising 30 eyes diagnosed with DMO were prospectively recruited. All eyes received three loading dose ranibizumab injections at baseline, week 4 and week 8, followed by pro re nata treatment based on best‐corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and central macular thickness (CMT) up to week 48. Prior to ranibizumab administration, aqueous samples were collected from all eyes, and subsequent sampling was performed at week 8. Levels of 32 cytokines were assessed at baseline and at week 8.ResultsAt baseline, higher aqueous TNF‐α levels were associated with poorer BCVA (p = 0.033), greater macular volume (p = 0.017) and worse diabetic retinopathy (p = 0.047). Higher levels of IL‐7 were associated with poorer BCVA and greater macular volume (MV). Following treatment with ranibizumab there was a significant correlation with reduction of aqueous TNF‐α and improvements in BCVA and MV, both at 6 months (BCVA [r = −0.558, p = 0.001], MV [r = 0.410, p = 0.024]) and 12‐months (BCVA [r = −0.413, p = 0.023], MV [r = 0.482, p = 0.008]). The change in VEGF concentration following ranibizumab treatment did not correlate with either BCVA or MV improvements (p > 0.05).ConclusionsHigher levels of aqueous TNF‐α and IL‐7 correlated with worse DMO, both anatomically and functionally. Reductions in levels of aqueous TNF‐α, but not VEGF, post ranibizumab treatment were associated with improvement in BCVA and MV.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic macular oedema (DMO) is the leading cause of vision impairment in patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR). 1 It can occur at any stage of DR and results from the breakdown of the blood‐retinal barrier (BRB) with subsequent accumulation of plasma and lipids in the subretinal and intraretinal space. 2 The upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) has been implicated as the primary cause of increased vascular permeability, with many phase 3 studies demonstrating success using intravitreal anti‐VEGF for DMO. 3 , 4 , 5 However, there is increasing evidence that inflammatory mediators also play a role in the pathogenesis of DMO. 6 , 7 Several proinflammatory cytokines, including IL‐6, IL‐12 and TNF‐α, have been postulated to result in the degeneration of endothelial cells and pericytes, both of which are responsible for maintaining the integrity of the BRB. 8

More recently, we have demonstrated that eyes with DMO undergoing ranibizumab treatment had significant reductions in aqueous concentrations of various inflammatory cytokines (IL‐1β, IL‐7, IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐12, IL‐17, MCP‐1, and TNF‐α) in addition to VEGF and soluble VEGF receptor 2. 9 Moreover ranibizumab treatment also appeared to influence the serum levels of cytokines. 10 This suggests that the effectiveness of ranibizumab in treating DMO may be due to a complex interplay between VEGF and inflammatory cytokines. In this analysis we aimed to investigate whether the previously identified cytokines that were shown to be reduced post ranibizumab are associated with anatomical and functional characteristics in eyes with DMO at baseline and also to assess whether the reductions following treatment correlated with improvements in best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and OCT features.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Human Research and Ethics Committee of the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital (RVEEH), Melbourne, Australia. Research adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. A study investigator obtained written informed consent from all participants prior to enrolment in the study. Patients with centre‐involved DMO were recruited from the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital, Melbourne, Australia between September 2015–May 2016 as part of the DIabetic macular oedema: aqueous and Serum Cytokine profiling to determine the Efficacy of RaNibizumab (DISCERN) Study. The methodology of the study has been reported in detail elsewhere. 9 , 10

Study population

Patients with centre‐involving DMO requiring anti‐VEGF therapy were recruited prospectively. Key inclusion criteria included age ≥18 years, BCVA of 17–70 logMAR letters (20/400–20/40), clinically significant macular oedema as defined in the Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS), central macular thickness (CMT) 300 μm or greater as documented on optical coherence tomography (OCT, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), and type 1 or 2 diabetes mellitus. Key exclusion criteria were loss of vision from ocular disease other than DR, intraocular surgery within 6 months before study entry, intravitreal anti‐VEGF injection within 3 months or corticosteroids within 6 months before study entry, argon laser photocoagulation within 3 months before study entry, and previous systemic anti‐VEGF treatment.

Visits and treatment schedule

All study participants received intravitreal ranibizumab (Novartis Pharma AG, Basel, Switzerland) 0.5 mg/0.05‐mL injection 3.5–4.0 mm behind the corneal limbus at baseline, week 4 (6–7 days) and at week 8 (6–7 days). All injections were performed by a retinal specialist under sterile conditions, using a 30‐gauge needle attached to a 1 mL syringe.

From week 12 onward, patients were treated pro re nata (PRN) up to 48 weeks. Ranibizumab injections were continued until stable BCVA was reached (stable vision over two consecutive visits). Treatment could be suspended if either of the following criteria were met: if the BCVA was stable over two consecutive visits and the investigator felt that no further BCVA improvement could be achieved with continued intravitreal injections, or BCVA letter score of 84 (approximate Snellen equivalent 20/20) was observed over two consecutive visits. Injections could be resumed if there was a decrease in BCVA due to DMO progression, confirmed by clinical evaluation and/or OCT or other anatomic and clinical assessments. Patients were then treated at monthly intervals until stable BCVA was reached again.

OCT interpretation

The foveal centred OCT scans provided quantitative measurements of the central macular thickness (CMT) and macular volume (MV) within the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) defined central zone of 500 μm radius. The Spectralis® software automatically obtained the measurements by the in‐built ‘thickness map’ function. Although CMT has evolved as the most commonly used OCT measurement of DMO in both research and clinical practice, features greatly affecting CMT such as large IRC may not accurately reflect changes in MV, generalised macular thickening, disease severity or BCVA. 11 , 12 MV thus provides the most objective measure of macular oedema and significantly avoids variability associated in measuring central retinal thickness within the 1 mm subfield defined by the ETDRS.

Aqueous sampling, processing, and analysis

At baseline and month 2, undiluted aqueous humour samples (0.05–0.1 mL) were collected from participants before they received an intravitreal injection of ranibizumab. All samples were collected via anterior chamber paracentesis at the corneal limbus under sterile conditions, using a 30‐gauge needle with a 1 mL syringe. The specimens were immediately transferred to a sterile plastic tube and stored at −80°C until assayed.

The aqueous sample analysis was performed using a multiplex array assay, based on the Luminex xMAP technology. Levels of 32 different cytokines were measured, using multiplex detection kits from Bio‐Rad (Hercules, CA, USA) and Millipore (Billerica, MA, USA).

Aqueous samples were thawed on ice, sonicated, and then spun at 1000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, samples were diluted 1:4 using the sample diluent buffer provided. Cytokine standards and aqueous humour samples were added to wells (50 lL/well) of a 96‐well plate containing cytokine detection beads and incubated for 30 min. This was carried out at room temperature with the 96‐well plate sealed and placed on an orbital shaker at 300 rpm. After incubation, the plate was washed with 100 lL of washing buffer three times, then detection antibody (25 lL) was added, and the plate was sealed and placed on an orbital shaker at 300 rpm for 30 min. The plate was subsequently washed again in washing buffer and 50 lL streptavidin phycoerythrin was added, with the plate sealed and placed on an orbital shaker at 500 rpm for another 10 min. The plate was then washed with 100 lL of washing buffer three times. The beads were resuspended in 125 lL assay buffer and shaken for 30 s at 1100 rpm. Standard curves were generated with the reference cytokine sample supplied using the Bio‐Plex200 System (software version 5.1.1; Bio‐Rad Laboratories). They were used to calculate the cytokine concentrations in aqueous humour samples. Aqueous humour samples were assayed in duplicate.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 27.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Of the 32 cytokines analysed in the DISCERN study, only the 10 cytokines that met significance after Bonferroni correction (0.05/32 = 0.002) for multiple testing were considered in this analysis.

Paired t‐tests were used to examine the differences in BCVA and CMT prior to and following intravitreal ranibizumab treatment.

For those cytokines that showed significant change from pre‐injection baseline, associations between concentrations of cytokines and BCVA, CMT and macular volume at baseline were assessed with the Spearman rank correlation, rho. We used mixed effects regression models to further investigate these associations over time, thus accounting for the correlations among repeated measures from the same individuals. BCVA was modelled as a continuous outcome with each predictor initially assessed independently and then statistically significant predictors from the univariable models (p < 0.10) were subsequently included in a multivariable model and the fit examined. Likelihood ratio tests were used to compare time modelled as a continuous versus categorical variable with the latter parameterisation providing a better fit. For this analysis we considered findings with a p‐value <0.05 as significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Sixty aqueous humour samples were obtained from 30 eyes of 25 patients. Baseline characteristics of patients are illustrated in Table 1. The mean (standard deviation [SD]) patient age was 63.8 ± 9.6 years (range 41–82 years), with a median (IQR) BCVA of 61.5 (±55–67, range 28–70) LogMAR letters. The mean baseline CMT was 484.5 μm (±134.3) whilst the DR severity according to the ETDRS diabetic retinopathy severity score (DRSS) ranged from 31 to 81; with 15 eyes (51.7%) being classified as mild to moderate non‐proliferative diabetic retinopathy (DRSS Score 31–43), 3 eyes (10.3%) were classified as having moderate non‐proliferative diabetic retinopathy (DRSS 47) and the remainder had severe non‐proliferative DR or treated proliferative DR (12 eyes, 38.0%) and had a DRSS > 47. 33% of eyes had undergone prior retinal laser or intravitreal injections or both (4 eyes had PRP, 9 eyes had macular laser, 10 eyes had prior intravitreal treatment). Of the 10 cytokines of interest (IL‐1β, IL‐7, IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐12, IL‐17, MCP‐1, TNF‐α, VEGF and soluble VEGF receptor 2), there was no association with any baseline cytokine level and gender, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) or systolic blood pressure (p > 0.05). Change in aqueous cytokine concentrations and outcomes following ranibizumab treatment.

After two intravitreal ranibizumab injections there was a statistically significant improvement in BCVA of 7.4 (±6.7 letters, p < 0.001, range −4 to +21) LogMAR letters. Similarly, there was a significant reduction in CMT of −102.9 ± 121.9 μm, p < 0.001. Over the 12 months duration, the mean number of intravitreal injections was 9.5 (range 5–12, SD 2.03). At 6 and 12 months from baseline, VA improved by 0.26 and 0.29 letters, respectively. At baseline, 6 and 12 months, mean MV changed from 11.148 to 9.555 to 9.518, respectively (p = 0.001). The proportion of eyes which responded fully to ranibizumab increased over the course of the study from 46.7% at month 2 to 56.7% at month 6 and 76.7% at month 12.

Table 3 shows the relationship between the 10 cytokines of interest with BCVA and MV changes at 6 and 12 months. Only the reduction in TNF‐α was significantly associated with improvements in BCVA and reductions in MV both at 6 (BCVA [r = −0.56, p = 0.001], MV [r = 0.41, p = 0.02]) and 12 months (BCVA [r = −0.41, p = 0.02], MV [r = 0.48, p < 0.001]). IL‐8 and IL‐10 were inconsistently associated with improvements in BCVA and MV, with IL‐8 being associated with improvements in BCVA at 12 months (r = −0.42, p = 0.02) and IL‐10 being associated with MV reduction at 6 months (r = 0.38, p = 0.04). The change in VEGF concentration following ranibizumab treatment did not correlate with either BCVA or MV improvements at either 6 or 12 months (p > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the correlation between the anatomical and functional severity of DMO prior to and following ranibizumab treatment and aqueous cytokines that were previously shown to be significantly reduced following ranibizumab. 9 Pre‐treatment, we demonstrated that higher aqueous TNF‐α levels were associated with worse BCVA, worse DR and greater MV. Following treatment with intravitreal ranibizumab, eyes with higher baseline aqueous TNF‐α levels and consequently those with the greatest reduction of TNF‐α concentrations from baseline to month‐2 were significantly associated with improvements in BCVA and MV, both at 6‐ and 12‐month intervals. Reductions in IL‐8, IL‐10 and IL‐12 levels post ranibizumab injections were also associated with improvements in either BCVA or MV, but not both. Higher levels of IL‐7 were associated with worse BCVA and MV at baseline. However, there was no correlation between its' reduction and BCVA or MV following intravitreal ranibizumab.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that higher levels of aqueous TNF‐α are associated with worse BCVA, MV and diabetic retinopathy at baseline. Our findings suggest although higher baseline TNF‐α levels are associated with poor anatomical and functional characteristics in eyes with DMO these increased levels do not influence outcomes following anti‐VEGF therapy. Additionally, it would appear that the BCVA and other anatomical improvements in treated eyes are related to a reduction of elevated cytokines such as TNF‐α, perhaps over and beyond that of VEGF inhibition.

TNF‐α is a multi‐functional ubiquitous proinflammatory cytokine that has been implicated in the development of DR. Elevated levels of TNF‐α have been found in the vitreous fluid, 13 plasma, 14 serum, 13 and platelets, 15 of diabetic patients. These elevations in plasma and serum have been associated with worse diabetic retinopathy. 16 Few studies have further correlated the progression of DR with the expression of genes involving TNF‐α, supporting the importance of the TNF‐α signalling pathway in activating and mediating angiogenesis. 17 , 18 , 19 Additionally, correlations between levels of aqueous cytokines and severity of DMO, measured either by macular thickness or BCVA have been previously documented. These include IL‐6, IL‐10 and intracellular adhesion molecule‐1 (ICAM‐1). 20 , 21 In another study, aqueous TNF‐α levels were measured but were not associated with DMO severity. 20 In contrast, Kocabara et al. 22 demonstrated that aqueous and plasma levels of TNF‐α were significantly elevated in DMO, which is consistent with our findings.

TNF‐α has been identified in vascular endothelial and stromal cells of diabetic fibrovascular epiretinal membranes, 23 supporting a link between low grade inflammation and diabetic retinopathy. Chronic exposure to TNF‐alpha appears to reprogram cell‐signalling pathways and establishes long term inflammatory memory. 24 Additionally, TNF‐α further impairs glucose tolerance, which would partially explain its association with worse DMO. 25 In diabetic animal models, it is involved in the breakdown of the blood‐retinal barrier, 26 , 27 and high levels have been shown to induce NADPH oxidase and reactive oxygen species leading to endothelial dysfunction. 28 TNF‐α has also been shown to enhance angiogenesis 29 and is required for VEGF‐induced endothelial hyperpermeability. 30 TNF‐α mediated retinal leukostasis and apoptosis is thought to contribute to DMO by the progressive breakdown in blood‐retina‐barrier (BRB) and the increase in retinal endothelial cell permeability via disruption of tight junction complexes. 27 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 Conversely, inhibition of TNF‐α reduces leukocyte adhesion, ICAM‐1 expression and suppresses blood retinal barrier breakdown. 35

The follow through question would be whether this reduction in aqueous TNF‐α translated to any meaningful improvement in functional and anatomical improvements in DMO. This would partially explain the relative efficacy of intravitreal steroids in treating DMO, 36 as steroids have been shown to suppress TNF‐α expression. 37 Previous studies have shown mixed results when anti‐TNF drugs were used for DMO. Sfikakis et al. 38 , 39 demonstrated an improvement in BCVA and macular oedema, after systemic administration of infliximab, an anti‐TNF monoclonal antibody. However, intravitreal administration of anti‐TNF drugs (infliximab or adalimumab) did not appear to improve DMO. 40 Our study did show a significant correlation between reduction in aqueous TNF‐α and DMO improvement. However, after longitudinal assessment accounting for time this significance was lost. Instead, the improvement was driven by eyes with larger MV and younger people.

Interestingly, reduction in aqueous VEGF levels following ranibizumab treatment did not correlate with improvements in DMO. Whilst vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) upregulation is important in increasing intraretinal vascular permeability, it has increasingly been shown that non‐VEGF‐dependent inflammatory pathways also play a crucial role. VEGF and inflammatory pathways are also intimately connected with increased expression of VEGF leading to upregulation of inflammatory mediators such as TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β. 41 , 42 We can therefore infer that DMO is not solely VEGF driven, and further elucidation of the cascade of interactions between TNF‐alpha and VEGF is required.

The strengths of our study include aqueous sampling of a large range of cytokines, and at two different time periods, before and after administration of ranibizumab. However, we acknowledge several potential limitations including the sampling technique and timing. Ideally, sampling of aqueous cytokines should have been done at 6 and 12 monthly intervals, to provide a more concurrent snapshot of cytokine levels and DMO severity. We felt that this would be unnecessarily invasive and thus unethical as the design of our study did not mandate ranibizumab at specific time points, after the loading dose phase. Second, aqueous sampling rather than vitreous fluid was used as a proxy of posterior segment pathology, again due to it being less invasive. Third, three, monthly injections followed by PRN treatment would likely result in cytokine level variability between patients compared with a monthly regimen throughout the study period. Lastly, we acknowledge that our adjusted multivariable model may be underpowered with the available sample size, to detect effects of interest. However, we felt that it was important to provide a model adjusted for any potential confounding factors.

In conclusion, TNF‐α is the predominant aqueous cytokine associated with BCVA and MV in eyes with DMO. Higher levels of aqueous TNF‐α were also associated with worse diabetic retinopathy. Following treatment with ranibizumab, reductions in aqueous TNF‐α correlated with improvements in BCVA and DMO. Further elucidation of this whole cascade of interactions would assist in novel treatments, possibly in combination with ranibizumab to improve outcomes, especially in the partial or non‐responders.
