Lifelong cognitive reserve as a resilience factor for frailty progression and recovery in later life
Authors: Meng Zhao, Shin J Liau, Xiangyun Ma, Chenkai Wu, Peining Liu, Sijing Peng, Si Chen, Ming Li, Kefang Wang, Yafei Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe cognitive reserve reflects the cumulative cognitive enrichment across the life course and has been linked to resilience against cognitive decline and dementia. However, its role in shaping the dynamic course of physical frailty, including both progression and recovery, remains insufficiently understood. We aimed to examine the associations between lifelong composite cognitive reserve and transitions across frailty states and mortality among older adults in China.MethodsA longitudinal study was conducted using the data from 13,256 older adults from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (mean age: 88.14 years; women: 59.00%; median follow-up duration: 6.00 years). A combined cognitive reserve Z-score was developed using early-life educational attainment, midlife occupational complexity, and late-life leisure activities. Frailty was operationalized using the FRAIL scale and reversion was measured by transitions from frailty at baseline to non-frailty during follow-up. Multi-state Markov models were performed to estimate the effects of the cognitive reserve on transitions among non-frail, pre-frail, frail, and death. Fine–Gray competing risks models were used to estimate the cognitive reserve associated with the risk of frailty and likelihood of reversion.ResultsA higher cognitive reserve was associated with lower hazards of progression from non-frail to pre-frail (HR = 0.95; 95% CI 0.92–0.98), pre-frail to frail (HR = 0.95; 95% CI 0.90–1.00), and from all frailty states to death (HR = 0.91–0.97). A greater cognitive reserve increased the likelihood of recovery from pre-frailty to non-frailty (HR = 1.07; 95% CI 1.03–1.10). Fine–Gray competing risks models showed that a higher cognitive reserve was associated with reduced risk of developing frailty (SHR = 0.97; 95% CI = 0.94–0.99) and increased likelihood of reversion (SHR = 1.28; 95% CI = 1.22–1.33).ConclusionsA higher cognitive reserve is linked to a decreased risk of developing frailty, slower progression, and a greater likelihood of improvement from pre-frailty to non-frailty, suggesting that lifelong cognitive enrichment enhances the physiological reserve beyond cognition. These findings suggest that interventions and policies that support the development of cognitive reserve across the life course may contribute to healthier frailty trajectories in later life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07919-4.Keywords: Cognitive reserve, Resilience, Reversion, Longitudinal design, Mortality, Frailty

Background

Frailty is characterized by diminished physiological reserves across multiple systems and a reduced capacity to respond to internal and external stressors [1]. It is not merely an inevitable consequence of chronological aging; rather, it reflects the cumulative burden of multisystem dysregulation. Frailty substantially increases the susceptibility to disability, falls, hospitalization, and mortality [2–5], posing profound clinical and public health challenges. Importantly, frailty is considered a dynamic rather than a fixed state, existing along a continuum from non-frail to pre-frail and frail [6, 7], with transitions that can occur bidirectionally over time. Although some individuals progress toward more severe states, others exhibit resilience and recovery, highlighting the importance of identifying modifiable factors that shape these bidirectional frailty transitions. Cognitive reserve offers a compelling life-course framework for a potentially modifiable buffer, as it reflects the cumulative benefits of cognitively enriching experiences, including early-life educational attainment, occupational complexity in adulthood, and sustained engagement in leisure activities throughout the lifespan [8].

Cognitive reserve is a complex construct referring to the brain’s ability to preserve cognitive function despite age-related brain changes or decline in brain health [9]. Cognitive reserve has been consistently linked to a slower cognitive decline, delayed dementia onset, and better recovery after neurological insult [10–12]. These findings align with, and have further shaped, the scaffolding theory of aging and cognition [13], which positions cognitive reserve as a central mechanism supporting neural compensation. Emerging evidence, however, suggests that the influence of cognitive reserve may extend beyond the preservation of cognitive function [14, 15]. Growing empirical evidence indicates that the cognitive and physical domains of ageing, including frailty, are closely interrelated [14], sharing common neurobiological mechanisms (e.g., neural efficiency and chronic inflammation) and behavioral pathways (e.g., health-promoting behaviors and social engagement) [3, 8, 10, 16]. Therefore, cognitive reserve may function as a form of accumulated cognitive and social capital that shapes not only cognitive aging but also the physiological reserve, including the risk and reversion of frailty.

Despite these conceptual advances, little is known about whether the cognitive reserve extends its influence beyond cognitive function to affect physiological aging processes, particularly the dynamic progression and recovery of frailty [15, 17]. Although several studies have examined the relationship between cognitive reserve and frailty, many have focused on individual cognitive reserve proxies (e.g., education) or assessed multiple proxies separately, with relatively limited attention given to cognitive reserve as an integrated multidimensional construct [15, 17]. Indeed, its components are interrelated and may cumulatively enhance reserve—for example, higher education often leads to more complex occupations and richer social networks, which together reinforce adaptive capacity. To date, empirical evidence on the association between multidimensional cognitive reserve and frailty in older adults remains limited, particularly regarding the combined and potentially synergistic effects of different cognitive reserve dimensions. Moreover, the reversion of frailty has become a highly intriguing area of research in recent years. However, most current studies employ cross-sectional designs [15, 17], which fail to capture the dynamic relationship between cognitive reserve and frailty. Clarifying this complex relationship is essential, as cognitive reserve is relatively simple to measure, requires minimal training, and can provide valuable insights into frailty risk stratification. Moreover, cognitive reserve is modifiable through life course exposures such as leisure activities [12, 18]. If the role of cognitive reserve in frailty reversal is substantiated, it could open up novel clinical avenues to prevent or reverse frailty in ageing populations.

To address this gap, we conducted a population-based longitudinal cohort study among Chinese older adults to examine the associations between lifelong composite cognitive reserve and transitions across frailty states and mortality. We hypothesized that higher levels of cognitive reserve would be associated with an increased likelihood of frailty recovery and decreased likelihood of frailty progression.

Methods

Study population

The population for this study was derived from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey initiated in 1998. The CLHLS aims to investigate the determinants of healthy longevity among older adults aged 65 years and older in China. The study sample from a population of 985 million people across 22 of China’s 31 mainland provinces, representing approximately 85% of the national population, with eight waves of data released to date (Wave 1, 1998; Wave 2, 2000; Wave 3, 2002; Wave 4, 2005; Wave 5, 2008–2009; Wave 6, 2011–2012; Wave 7, 2014; and Wave 8, 2017–2018). Details regarding the study design, survey methods, and data quality have been previously described [19]. The CLHLS received ethics approval from the Beijing University Institutional Review Board (irb00001052-13074).

We used data from Waves 4–7, during which frailty status and social participation items for assessing cognitive reserve were available. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≤ 65 years, (2) missing values on frailty data at baseline. A total of 21,447 participants were included in the Fine–Gray competing risks models. Of these, 16,050 individuals who were non-frail at baseline were examined for the prospective association between cognitive reserve and frailty, whereas the remaining 5,397 participants who were frail at baseline were evaluated for the prospective association between cognitive reserve and frailty reversion. The participants were further excluded if frailty data were missing for two consecutive assessment waves, leaving 13,256 participants for the multi-state Markov model to investigate the relationship between cognitive reserve and transitions in the frailty status. The flow chart of participant recruitment is presented in Figure S1.

Frailty

Frailty was operationalized using the FRAIL scale, which comprises five components: fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illness, and loss of weight [20, 21]. Further details of the individual components are provided in the Supplementary Methods. One point was assigned for each component if the criterion was met, and zero otherwise, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater frailty. A total score of 0 corresponds to non-frail status, a score of 1–2 to pre-frail, and ≥ 3 to frail [20, 21]. Frailty state transitions were defined based on the observed frailty status at consecutive survey waves. The reversion of frailty was defined as a transition from a more severe frailty state to a less severe state at the subsequent follow-up assessment.

Lifelong cognitive reserve

Cognitive reserve was assessed based on three components: early-life educational attainment, adulthood occupational complexity, and late-life participation in leisure activities [8, 9]. Cognitive reserve was assessed at Wave 4 and treated as a fixed covariate throughout follow-up, thereby minimizing concerns regarding reverse causation or violations of temporality. Educational attainment was measured as the years of schooling. Occupational complexity was determined from the participants’ primary occupation before the age of 60 years and classified according to the Occupational Classification Dictionary [22]. The occupations were categorized into three levels of complexity: high (e.g., professional, technical, managerial, or governmental), medium (e.g., commercial, service, or industrial), and low (e.g., agricultural, forestry, livestock farming, or fishery). Leisure activity participation included eight common activities: housework, outdoor activities, gardening, reading, raising domestic animals, playing cards or mah-jong, watching television or listening to the radio, and engaging in social activities. Each activity was scored from 0 to 4 based on the frequency, with higher scores indicating more frequent participation.

For overall cognitive reserve scoring, the three components were transformed into z-scores, standardizing each to a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one to ensure comparability across different units and scales [8, 23]. Subsequently, the three z-scores were summed to generate a composite cognitive reserve index, with higher values indicating a greater cognitive reserve.

Covariates and mortality

Data on age, sex (male or female), marital status (married/partnered or unmarried, including separated, divorced, widowed or never married), and residence (rural or urban) were collected. Death was ascertained using official death certificates or information provided by the participant’s next of kin, local physician, or resident physician.

Statistical analysis

We conducted statistical inference in three distinct steps. First, a continuous-time multi-state Markov model was used to examine the associations between the composite cognitive reserve and transitions among four frailty states: non-frail, pre-frail, frail, and death. The model captures the dynamic and progressive nature of frailty, allowing both forward and backwards transitions between adjacent states, while treating death as an absorbing state (Fig. 1). The direct transitions between nonadjacent states (e.g., non-frail to frail) were constrained to zero because of their biological implausibility.

Second, to address the potential risk of reverse causality, we established prospective cohorts within a baseline non-frail population and employed Fine–Gray competing risks models to examine the relationship between baseline cognitive reserve and frailty outcomes over time (Analysis 2). Finally, the Fine–Gray competing risks model is a generalization of survival analysis approaches that allow for the simultaneous consideration of multiple potential outcomes, accounting for the possibility that the occurrence of one event (e.g., mortality) may preclude the occurrence of another (e.g., frailty). To further validate the association between cognitive reserve and frailty reversion, we constructed additional prospective cohorts within a baseline frail population, with non-frailty as the outcome, and applied Fine–Gray competing risks models to estimate the impact of cognitive reserve on the likelihood of remaining free from frailty (Analysis 3).

Furthermore, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed to examine the dose–response relationships between cognitive reserve and the risk and reversion of frailty.

We did two sets of sensitivity analyses. First, we categorized cognitive reserve into quintiles based on the score distribution in our sample and assess the potential associations between them. Second, because educational attainment and occupation are not readily amenable to change in old age, a further analysis to examine if participation in more leisure activities in later life would be beneficial.

To address missing data in covariates and cognitive reserve, we applied multiple imputation by chained equations. The results were pooled across 20 imputed datasets following Rubin’s rules to obtain robust estimates [24]. Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.11). A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Transitions between frailty states

The median follow-up duration was 6.00 years (interquartile range [IQR], 5.50–9.00). A total of 1,594 individuals had three frailty-state follow-up assessments, 3,189 individuals had two, and 9,597 individuals had one. This resulted in a total of 35,137 checkups on frailty states. The observed transitions between the frailty states at each follow-up are summarized in Table S2. Overall, 40.1% of the participants experienced deterioration from the non-frail state to pre-frail or frail states at subsequent visits, while 14.43% and 11.38% showed improvement from the pre-frail and frail states, respectively.

The transition intensities between frailty states are presented in Table 2. The estimated transition intensity from non-frail to pre-frail was 0.034 (95% CI: 0.032–0.036), approximately 2.3-fold higher than that from pre-frail to frail (0.015, 95% CI: 0.014–0.016). Among the participants who were frail, the transition intensity for deterioration to mortality (0.037, 95% CI 0.036–0.039) was more than nine times greater than that of non-frail individuals (0.004, 95% CI 0.003–0.005), and three times higher than that of pre-frail individuals (0.012, 95% CI 0.012–0.013).

Sojourn time and total stay of each transient state

The mean sojourn times in the non-frail, pre-frail, and frail states were 26.53, 25.15, and 20.30 months, respectively. The estimated total time spent in each transient state before mortality was 37.98, 35.45, and 11.26 months, respectively.

Probability of frailty state transitions

The probabilities of frailty-state transitions over observation intervals of 1, 2, 3, and 4 years were estimated for individuals with different baseline frailty states (Fig. 1). Among participants who were non-frail at baseline, the estimated probabilities of remaining non-frail, deteriorating to pre-frail, deteriorating to frail, and transitioning to death were 0.655, 0.259, 0.023, and 0.063, respectively, over a 1-year interval; and evolved to 0.329, 0.295, 0.080, and 0.250 within 3 years.

Among participants who were pre-frail at baseline, the probabilities of reversing to non-frail, remaining pre-frail, deteriorating to frail, and transitioning to death were 0.093, 0.646, 0.110, and 0.151, respectively, over a 1-year interval; and evolved to 0.122, 0.333, 0.129, and 0.415 over 3 years.

Among participants who were frail at baseline, the probabilities of reversing to non-frail, recovering to pre-frail, remaining frail, and transitioning to death were 0.006, 0.077, 0.567, and 0.349, respectively, over a 1-year interval; and evolved to 0.020, 0.091, 0.198, and 0.691 over 3 years.

Associations between cognitive reserve and transitions across frailty states and mortality

After adjusting for covariates including age, sex, marital status, and residence, each 1-SD increase in cognitive reserve was associated with a lower likelihood of worsening across several frailty transitions (Table 3): from non-frail to pre-frail (HR = 0.952; 95% CI = 0.922–0.984), from pre-frail to frail (HR = 0.946; 95% CI = 0.899–0.996), from non-frail to death (HR = 0.911; 95% CI = 0.865–0.971), from pre-frail to death (HR = 0.968; 95% CI = 0.944–0.993), and from frail to death (HR = 0.908; 95% CI = 0.887–0.931). Additionally, each 1-SD increase in cognitive reserve was associated with an increased likelihood of improvement from pre-frail to non-frail (HR = 1.066; 95% CI = 1.030–1.104). However, the association between cognitive reserve and the transition from frail to pre-frail was not statistically significant (HR = 0.936; 95% CI = 0.868–1.009).

Multivariable RCS curves showed dose–response relationships between cognitive reserve and the probability of non-frailty or frailty risk (Figure S2). The RCS model suggested a non-linear association between cognitive reserve and the probability of non-frailty (p < 0.001). Furthermore, RCS models revealed a negative linear association between cognitive reserve and the frailty risk, where higher levels of cognitive reserve are linked to a reduced risk of frailty.

Sensitivity analyses using cognitive reserve as a categorical variable showed that a higher cognitive reserve was generally associated with a lower likelihood of frailty risk, with evidence that the association is most apparent at the highest level of cognitive reserve (HR = 0.839, 95% CI: 0.705–0.997).

Additionally, with the Q1 level of cognitive reserve as the reference, its Q2, Q3, Q4 and Q5 levels increased the probability of frailty reversion (SHR = 1.542, 95% CI: 1.056–2.252; SHR = 2.394, 95% CI: 1.686–3.401; SHR = 3.713, 95% CI: 2.674–5.156; SHR = 5.588, 95% CI: 3.982–7.842), respectively (Table S3). Furthermore, a higher cognitive reserve is associated with a reduced risk of developing frailty (SHR = 0.911; 95% CI = 0.859–0.967) and increased likelihood of frailty reversal (SHR = 1.883; 95% CI = 1.691–2.096).

Discussion

This longitudinal study provides novel evidence that a higher cognitive reserve, life-course accumulated across early-life education, occupation in adulthood, and leisure activities in late life, is associated with slower frailty progression, potential frailty reversion, and lower mortality. For older adults, even though educational attainment and occupational complexity are difficult to modify, late-life engagement in leisure activities may still influence transitions of frailty. Furthermore, a dose–response relationship was observed between cognitive reserve and these outcomes. By incorporating a multi-state Markov framework, we captured the bidirectional nature of frailty transitions and demonstrated that cognitive enrichment across the life course contributes to the physiological reserves. These findings extend previous research on cognitive reserve beyond neurocognitive outcomes, suggesting that lifelong cognitive stimulation may strengthen the systemic physiological adaptability and reduce the vulnerability to adverse aging outcomes.

Most existing studies on frailty have focused on its risk factors [3, 25] rather than on protective mechanisms that enable stability to recovery, particularly in the early, reversible stages. Pre-frailty, affecting approximately half of older adults [26], remains poorly studied despite its high prevalence. Addressing pre-frailty is crucial, as progression to more advanced frailty is associated with a significantly lower likelihood of recovery and an increased risk of adverse outcomes, including mortality [27]. Our study demonstrates that a notable proportion of pre-frail individuals can revert to a non-frail state, providing evidence that a higher cognitive reserve may act as a protective factor, facilitating this reversion. These findings suggest that cognitive reserve, traditionally considered a buffer against cognitive decline [10–12], may also enhance physiological reserve and support frailty reversion, with important implications for frailty prevention and management in older adults.

Previous research has established a relationship between cognitive reserve and mortality risk [28, 29]. Our study builds on this by examining transitions across the full spectrum of frailty, including death. Although a few cross-sectional studies have reported associations between cognitive reserve and frailty [15, 17], the evidence from these studies is preliminary. Our longitudinal studies complement these findings [15, 17], which indicated that a higher cognitive reserve is associated with a reduced risk of frailty progression and increased likelihood of reversion.

The underlying mechanisms linking cognitive reserve to frailty are likely multifactorial, involving both neurobiological and behavioral pathways. First, both cognitive reserve and frailty have been implicated in late-life cognitive impairment [2, 10, 12], suggesting shared neurobiological mechanisms. A higher cognitive reserve is associated with preserved white matter integrity, more efficient functional networks, and greater neural adaptability under pathological stress [12, 30]. Frailty is correlated with reduced brain volumes, increased white matter hyperintensities, and disrupted connectivity [31]. Additionally, biological and neuromuscular pathways are crucial in mediating the relationship between cognitive reserve and frailty. Biologically, cognitive decline is associated with dysregulation across multiple systems, including chronic low-grade inflammation, endocrine alterations, and autonomic dysfunction [16, 32], collectively accelerating physiological deterioration and multisystem vulnerability. Concurrently, impaired cognition can compromise motor planning, balance, and coordination, leading to reduced physical activity, slower gait, and loss of muscle strength [33, 34], thereby leading to functional decline and frailty. Furthermore, cognitive reserve may foster healthier lifestyle behaviors—such as social engagement, better nutrition, and sustained physical activity—which are linked to a lower risk of chronic diseases and depression [10, 35], both established risk factors for frailty and mortality.

While causality remains to be definitively established, our findings suggest that enhancing cognitive reserve may help delay frailty progression, reverse frailty, and prolong survival. Assessing cognitive reserve using simple life-course indicators, such as education, occupation, and leisure engagement, may facilitate the early identification of older adults at risk of deterioration and guide timely preventive interventions. Cognitive reserve is modifiable throughout life [10–12, 29]; therefore, strategies that promote continued learning and social activities participation may strengthen both the cognitive and physiological reserves. Integrating cognitive reserve assessment into geriatric screening and community programs could complement existing physical and nutritional interventions, supporting more holistic approaches to healthy aging.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the use of the Markov multi-state model provides less biased estimates than traditional Cox models, offering a detailed, real-world depiction of individuals’ trajectories across the frailty continuum and death. Second, the strong consistency between the multistate transition analysis and prospective analysis further strengthens the reliability of our findings. Third, since cognitive reserve is a dynamic construct shaped by diverse and often interrelated lifetime experiences, our use of a composite cognitive reserve indicator effectively captures the accumulation and interaction of various cognitive reserve-related components. Fourth, the CLHLS database provides a unique opportunity to explore frailty transitions and their association with cognitive reserve owing to its oversampling design of the oldest-old population and multiple follow-up surveys, which provide a non-frail sample of older adults. However, this study has several limitations. First, the population was drawn from an Asian geriatric cohort, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, due to the observational nature of our cohort study, caution is warranted when inferring causality between cognitive reserve and frailty. Third, although we adjusted for multiple covariates, residual confounding, such as genetic susceptibility, may still be present. Forth, because frailty status was assessed only at scheduled follow-up visits, changes occurring between assessments could not be captured. Fifth, the multi-state model assumed time-homogeneous transition intensities. Although this assumption is commonly adopted in continuous-time Markov models when the exact timing of transitions is unknown, frailty transitions may vary over time and according to the duration spent in a given state. Therefore, the estimated transition probabilities should be interpreted as average transition patterns over the follow-up period. Future studies with more frequent assessments are needed to investigate potential time-varying transition dynamics.

Conclusion

Higher cognitive reserve was associated with a lower risk of developing and progressing to frailty, reduced mortality hazards across frailty states, and a greater likelihood of improvement from pre-frailty to non-frailty among older adults. These findings suggest that cognitive enrichment accumulated across the life course through education, occupation, and leisure engagement supports both cognitive and physiological reserve in later life. The results highlight the potential value of incorporating cognitive reserve assessment into routine clinical and public health practice for risk stratification for frailty, and to inform early, multidomain interventions for frailty that integrate cognitive, social, and physical components. Further research should explore targeted strategies to increase cognitive reserve across the life course and evaluate their potential to reduce frailty burden and improve healthy ageing outcomes globally.
