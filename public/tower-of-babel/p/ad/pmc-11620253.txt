Advancing sustainable lubricating oil management: Re-refining techniques, market insights, innovative enhancements, and conversion to fuel
Authors: Md Rahatul Islam Sazzad, Md Mizanur Rahman, Tafsirul Hassan, Abdullah Al Rifat, Abdullah Al Mamun, Abidur Rahman Adib, Redoy Masum Meraz, Minhaz Ahmed
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLubricating oil disposal is considered hazardous waste and has adverse effects on the environment and human health. It is crucial to implement tight measures to regulate and minimize these impacts, or explore opportunities for oil regeneration. The paper offers an extensive review of worldwide demand for lubricating oil and its market share, with a specific emphasis on the viewpoint of Bangladesh. Furthermore, the work presents a concise overview of various re-refining methods, including acid/clay and solvent extraction, and compares them based on physical and chemical attributes such as neutralization number, viscosity, and metal contents. It is also emphasized the implementation of design modifications and the integration of current and newly developed technologies like vaxon, ecohuile, etc. to enhance productivity and durability in terms of economic and environmental aspects, to increase the quality of products with a comparison in chemical composition from FTIR and GC-MS analysis of the samples that are obtained via recycling procedures. The feasibility of using spent motor oil as a fuel with similar properties to diesel is also evaluated based on the findings from tests conducted on engine performance and emission attributes. Using nanoparticles to enhance the characteristics of lubricating oil is very promising, particularly in reconditioning old motor oil, which is currently underexplored. The primary goal of the review is to assist the re-refining industries in improving their diverse range of potential uses.Keywords: Waste lubricating oil, Recycling, Lubricating oil market, Waste management, Sustainability

Introduction

Lubricating oil is a significant byproduct of crude oil, comprising high-molecular-weight paraffins, naphthenes, and aromatics [1]. Lubricating oil is an essential element in machinery and automobile engines, minimizing friction, decreasing wear, safeguarding against corrosion, enhancing sealing, and dissipating heat. Its properties include viscosity, viscosity index (VI), flash point, and pour point, among others. Additives are added to base oils to enhance properties like anti-wear, anti-oxidation, corrosion inhibition, and detergency. Lubricating oils come in a variety of forms, such as mineral, synthetic, semi-synthetic, and biodegradable oils. Mineral oils are obtained from the refining of crude oil and are extensively used owing to their economic efficiency and adequate performance. Synthetic oils are engineered from chemical compounds and offer superior performance, while semi-synthetic oils blend mineral and synthetic oils for a balance between performance and cost. Biodegradable oils are made from natural sources and are environmentally friendly. Applications of lubricating oils include automotive engines, industrial machinery, marine engines, and hydraulic systems. Regular monitoring, proper storage, and proper disposal are essential for optimal performance and longevity of equipment. Proper storage in a cool, dry place and proper disposal of used oil minimizes environmental impact. Lubricating oils are essential for the efficiency and durability of mechanical systems. Non-OECD Asian countries, especially China, possess the largest and most quickly growing value pool. This is due to significant expansion in all sectors, especially road transport, and higher profit margins. In contrast, in Europe and America, the slower growth of the value pool and the decrease in road transport volume require investors to take a more defensive approach. According to predictions, developing markets are expected to see a growth in market value of $8 billion by the year 2035. This increase is expected to happen at a rate of 3 % per year, which is three times faster than the growth rate of industrialized nations. China is the leading contributor to overall growth, but, due to its transition towards advanced formulations and synthetic products, its profit margins are expected to trail behind those of the OECD and other non-OECD countries by a five-year delay [2].

The degradation of lubricating oil is caused by oxidation and contaminants that occur during its use [3]. Degradation refers to alterations in the desired viscosity properties of oil due to molecular structural changes in the lubricating oil, which are produced by cracking, isomerization, and polymerization processes triggered by elevated temperatures during operation [4]. Lubricating oil may get polluted by contaminants including metal particles from wear, gums, moisture, deteriorated additives, varnishes, base oils, asphaltic compounds, and other sediments [5]. Sulfur oxide (SOX) and nitrogen oxide (NOX) are formed from the separation and subsequent oxidation of nitrogen in the air that goes in and the sulfur level of the fuel, respectively. These by-products can affect the performance of lubricants. Subsequently, the amount of water content in the lubricant is mixed with both oxides, resulting in the formation of acids. This technique substantially alters the oil's properties by increasing its pH level and making it corrosive [6,7].

The amount of old lubricating oil disposed of annually from railways, workshops, shipyards, and industrial machinery due to its widespread use is approximately 24 million metric tons [8]. Waste lubricating oil (WLO) is a considerable environmental threat as a toxic pollutants [9,10]. Water-based lubricating oil harms aquatic environments and poses health risks to living beings due to the existence of carcinogenic metals, poly-aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and some other hydrocarbon derivatives [[10], [11], [12]]. Subterranean waste disposal and when fuel is burned to make energy, harmful chemicals are released into the surroundings, presenting significant threats to public health [13]. The substance infiltrates streams or permeates the soil, causing damage to aquatic and soil organisms and releasing noxious odors and toxic vapors [14]. Several methods have been suggested for handling WLO, such as burning, converting it into fuel-like substances via thermal processes, distillation, refining with acid, treating it with clay, reclaiming it by hydrogenation, and recycling. The refining process of WLO is contingent upon the specific base stock and the number of pollutants found within the waste oils [15].

Several studies have shown that WLO may be utilized to create energy and power [16], to produce biodiesel [17], and to produce base oil of lubricants [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. The recycling process for lubricating oil production is expensive due to the significant amount of chemical substances and energy needed. Additionally, the substandard quality of the product is a notable worry [[24], [25], [26]]. While high-quality base oil production was successful, the main problem encountered was the formation of a hazardous byproduct known as acid sludge [27]. The acid/clay treatment process has been used since the earliest days and is the most often used re-refining method. Nonetheless, it presents a serious risk to the environment owing to the generation of large volumes of waste clay and acidic sludge [28,29]. An effective alternate method for addressing this issue is the solvent extraction approach, which employs several solvents and their combinations in appropriate proportions to eliminate unwanted components from spent lubricating oil [30,31]. Another effective technique is vacuum distillation, whereby the lubricant's temperature is elevated to 240 °C at a reduced pressure of 20 mm Hg. The vapors of lubricating oil are then condensed and fractionated into lighter fuels at a temperature of 140 °C [32,33]. While solvent extraction may eliminate around 10–14 % of the pollutants present in WLO, mostly sludge, the base oil still contains specific contaminants like heavy metals and PAHs [34]. The adsorption approach is very efficient in eliminating the remaining pollutants. The procedure of adsorption in WLO treatment aids in the removal of pollutants, particularly PAHs [35], heavy metals [36,37], and color in WLO by capturing them on the adsorbent's active sites [38,39]. Clay and carbon are excellent adsorbents because of their considerable porosity and surface area. Agricultural wastes rich in lignocellulosic material possess a significant amount of carbon and a minimal proportion of inorganic substances, making them very suitable for activated carbon production [40]. Activated carbon derived from lignocellulosic origins has been used to separate gaseous mixtures [41], dye removal [42], PAHs, and heavy metals from oil and aqueous effluent [35,43]. The use of local clay as an adsorbent is possible because of its ability to effectively reduce color and other contamination from oils [22,39].

A considerable number of scholars have explored the compelling potential of reprocessing WLO using diverse methodologies [1,[44], [45], [46]]. These studies looked at how well-revived oil worked compared to fresh oil, showing that it could be a beneficial option that doesn't harm the environment. However, there is a gap in the research that looks into whether used motor oil can be used as a diesel-like fuel [47,48]. This different way of handling used oil could not only help the environment but also be a useful energy source. Furthermore, the use of nanoparticles in lubricants remains largely unexplored. Nanoparticles show great potential for improving the characteristics of motor oil [49]. They might increase wear resistance, viscosity stability with temperature, and overall engine efficiency. Investigating this path may result in the production of next-generation engine oils with improved performance and perhaps fewer emissions. As conclusion, although used oil regeneration research exhibits potential, a dual-pronged strategy may yield even greater advantages. Additional investigation into the utilization of spent oil as a fuel source, in conjunction with studies on lubricants augmented with nanoparticles, has the potential to fundamentally transform the fields of used oil management and engine oil technology. Our transport systems may experience a more sustainable future as a result of this all-encompassing strategy. The primary aim of this paper is to elucidate various techniques for re-refining used lubricating oil, including the acid/clay method with and without clay activation, solvent extraction, hydrotreating, and pyrolysis, while comparing the physical and chemical properties of the treated samples to those of fresh lubricant oil samples. The paper also compares the output properties using different washing agents in the same process. The market share and demand for lubricating oil in Bangladesh and around the world are discussed. This study also discusses current breakthroughs in re-refining methods and the use of nanomaterials to enhance the performance of treated lubricating oil. Furthermore, the usability of used lube oil in engines as diesel-like fuel is also explored.

Lubricating oil standards and codes

Viscosity (SAE - Society of Automotive Engineers) and engine performance (API, ACEA, ILSAC and JASO) are the two main classifications for engine oil, and requirements call for a variety of tests on factors including wear, oxidation, soot management, fuel efficiency, and chemical composition. Three fundamental components make up lubricating oil: polymers, performance additives, and base oil. Furthermore, the API has classified it into five unique groups [50]. The performance of the engine as well as the reduction of pollutants in exhaust gases and fuel consumption depend on the proper lubricant selection.

SAE classification

A vehicle operates at its best when its lubricant has the proper viscosity. Improperly circulating oil might lead to premature wear and failure. However, the same problems arise if the oil is very thick because it cannot pass through the microscopic gaps between moving parts. When choosing the right oil for a given temperature range, SAE viscosity ratings are a useful tool. The SAE now provides eight single active viscosity grades: 8, 12, 16, 20, 30, 40, 50, and 60. Engine oil viscosity is categorized by the SAE using a numerical number that is preceded by the letter "W" for "Winter." For example, at a freezing −20 °C, engine ignition is facilitated by 15W oil. The coldest temperature at which oil can flow adequately to lubricate essential engine components is known as the minimum pumping temperature. Oils with multigrade characteristics, such as SAE 5W-30 and 10W-30, exhibit optimal flow at low temperatures and perform well at elevated temperatures, leading to their widespread usage [51].

API classification

The classification system of API (American Petroleum Institute) Service was created in 1970 by the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) and the SAE to define oil performance levels irrespective of viscosity. In the classification method, "S" indicates spark ignition and "C" indicates compression ignition. The API SN and API SM classes of engine oil service for automobile gasoline engines (Fig. 2(A)) improve fuel efficiency while including the performance attributes of earlier categories (e.g., SH, SJ, SL, etc.). When the API SN category was first created in October 2010, its goals were to give pistons better protection at high temperatures, stricter sludge control, and compatibility with seals [52]. In 2017, the API SN Plus was unveiled as an addition to the API SN specification with the goal of preventing low-speed pre-ignition (LSPI) incidents in gasoline direct engines. The engine oil's antioxidant and detergent systems can affect an individual's vulnerability to LSPIs. For SI engines, the classifications range from the now-obsolete SA, which was introduced in the 1930s, to the latest SP, introduced in 2020. API SP offers improved safeguards against low-speed pre-ignition (LSPI) and timing chain deterioration, along with superior resistance to high-temperature deposits on pistons and turbochargers, and more stringent regulation of sludge and varnish accumulation. The API SP with Resource Conserving adheres to ILSAC GF-6A requirements, providing the advantages of API SP along with enhanced fuel efficiency, superior protection for emission control systems, and compatibility with engines using ethanol-blended fuels up to E85 [53].

Codes of oils for automotive engines. (A) Spark Ignition engine, (B) Compression Ignition engine.

The current API code classifications for diesel engines are CJ-4, CI-4, and CH-4 (Fig. 2(B)) [54]. The latest classification is CJ-4, established in 2006. Lubricants CJ-4 are designed for use in all sectors using diesel fuels containing up to 500 parts per million sulfur (0.05 % by weight). However, using these lubricants with fuels that are higher than 15 parts per million (0.0015 % by weight) of sulfur may shorten the life of exhaust treatment systems and/or increase the need for frequent oil changes. Only in conjunction with other advanced post-treatment systems and particle filters can CJ-4 lubricants function effectively. Improved efficiency requires or ensures the best possible protection [54,55].

Demand and market share of lubricants and regeneration of WLO

Today's lubricants fall into five product categories: metalworking fluids (which include corrosion preventatives), automobile oils, greases, process oils, and industrial oils. Process oils are used as raw ingredients in many different operations; in the rubber sector, they are mostly used as plasticizers. They are only mineral oil products that are obtained by the refining of base oils, which is their sole relationship to lubricants; nonetheless, they frequently manipulate the data regarding lubricant usage. As such, their debate will not be covered in this book.

Since the year 1975, the demand for lubricants has become distinctly independent of the number of registered vehicles and the gross national product. This perspective, which initially suggests a consistent decrease in the quantities of lubricants, fails to adequately convey the importance of the lubricants industry in the present day. In nearly all domains, products now possess a longer lifespan and offer superior performance, resulting in a decrease in the consumption of specific lubricants, but a noticeable increase in specific revenues. This fact is further substantiated by the critical category of engine oils, whose needs were quadrupled owing to extended intervals for oil changes, leading to a fourfold rise in the cost of these lubricants. The efforts to extend the longevity of lubricants are not driven by a desire to decrease lubricant costs. The main goal is to reduce the expenses related to service and maintenance stemming from regular oil changes or re-greasing.

Approximately 50 % of lubricants marketed worldwide ultimately result in contamination of the natural environment. Consequently, every endeavor is undertaken to diminish instances of leakage and evaporation. A prime illustration of this is the emanation of diesel engine particulates, of which one-third is attributed to the evaporation of engine oil. The motivation behind the creation of environmentally conscious lubricants, extensively discussed within this publication, stems from the substantial discharge of lubricants into the ecosystem.

A supplementary motivation to decrease particular consumption is the continuously rising expense of disposal or reutilization of utilized lubricants. However, this once again generates novel requirements for lubricants due to diminished leakage losses, resulting in a reduced need for replenishment and rejuvenation of the utilized oil. Consequently, the novel oils must exhibit commendable resilience to aging.

Amount of used lube oil reprocessed

Only 27 % of the aggregate volume of utilized lubricating oil, which is approximated at 3.8 billion gallons, underwent recycling for the specific objective of generating base oil during the calendar year 2017. In 2016, the Middle East region attained the greatest level of base stock production from WLO, constituting 16 % of the overall output. In the European Union and the United States of America, about 13 % and 12 % of the total base stocks, respectively, are derived from processed used lubricating oil. It is crucial to highlight that the proportion is notably lower for other geographical areas globally. To be specific, the Asia-Pacific region succeeded in generating merely 5 % of its base stocks from reprocessed and utilized lubricating oil, while Latin America and Africa accomplished 9 % and 1 %, respectively [63].

Lubricating oil in the perspective of Bangladesh

In Bangladesh, industrial growth occurs together with agricultural production. The goal of the industrial sector's ongoing expansion and the construction of processing facilities is to satisfy the demand for commodities on a national and worldwide scale. Many mobile components are used by these sectors to streamline their production processes. To guarantee these mobile components operate smoothly and continue to work at a level that is desired, lubricating oil must be used.

Supply and demand

Current data from the National Board of Revenue (NBR) indicate that the yearly lubricant usage in Bangladesh is roughly 160,000 metric tons. Between 2015 and 2017, the engine oil market in the nation grew by about 20 %. This expansion was mostly driven by the heightened use of vehicles and the construction of new power plants. The automobile industry in Bangladesh accounts for 70 % of lubricant use, and the industrial and marine propulsion sectors comprise the remaining 30 %. The growth in motor vehicles in the nation has led to a heightened need for engine oil and lubricants. The recent expansion of the lubricant business may be largely ascribed to developments in the industrial sector, especially within the power sector. Moreover, the cement, steel, and fertilizer sectors substantially contribute to lubricant use. As the industrial sector continues to expand, it is expected that the use of lubricants in this sector will continue to grow. This growth is driven by increasing industrial activities and the need for lubricants in various machinery and equipment within these industries. Even though the volume of lubricating oil used has been growing over time, recycled engine oil production has not kept up. Less than 110 thousand metric tons of lubricating oil was used in Bangladesh during the 2013–14 fiscal year; in only six years, that amount climbed by over 50 %, to over 160 thousand metric tons. Waste lube oil reprocessing in the context of Bangladesh

The preferred approach for managing WLO is regeneration, which entails recovering base oil from the spent oil. Bangladesh now has two legally sanctioned plants for recycling WLO: Min Oils Ltd. in Savar, Dhaka, and Lubreff Bangladesh Ltd. in Chittagong. These facilities collectively produce around 4,000 metric tons of regenerated base oil annually, which accounts for approximately 5 % of the total WLO production in the country. However, concerning unregistered re-refining factories, the Bangladesh Lube Blenders Association (BBA) has revealed concerning information. In various locations of Bangladesh, more than thirty similar facilities are in operation. Despite being completely outlawed in 2009, the majority of these re-refining factories still use the traditional acid-clay method for refining the base oil.

Value chain survey of lubricating oil from source to consumer

As part of this research, Islam et al. [63] did a quick informal survey in and around Dhaka city to get an overview of what happened to the generated WLO. To find out the flow channel of old lubricating oil and its applications, interviews with a variety of retail collectors, workshops, and large-scale purchasers were conducted at various sites. To ascertain the path of value addition for recycled lubricating oil in Bangladesh, informal talks were also held with the retail dealers and users of lubricating oil in the following areas: Polashi, Hazaribagh, Mohammadpur, Katabon, Lalbagh, Gabtali, Amin Bazar, Hemayetpur, Savar, and Lalbagh. In the areas of Mohammadpur, Hemayetpur, Hazaribagh, and Amin Bazar, retail collectors (drum van collectors) were assessed. The major purchasers of WLO in the Mohammadpur region, Gabtoli, SowariGhat, and Amin Bazar, were evaluated.

According to the report, motor workshops obtain old engine oil at no cost when they replace the oil in the vehicles that are serviced at their facilities. Nearly every corner of Dhaka and its environs is home to retail collectors of old lubricant oil. The collectors of used lubricant oil operate using drum vans, and they purchase the used oil from workshops in exchange for a fee. The fee charged by the collectors varies between 20 and 35 Tk/L (Bangladeshi Taka per liter). The workshops located in Gabtoli, Amin Bazar, Savar, and Hemayetpur districts appear to collect a larger quantity of wasted lubricant oil, ranging from 180 to 400 L per month. On the other hand, workshops in Lalbagh, Polashi, Hazaribagh, Mohammadpur, and Katabon regions gather a smaller amount of used lubricant oil, between 20 and 60 L per month. Depending on the particular area, the reciprocal selling and buying prices between the workshops and the drum van collectors also change.

A monthly amount of used lubricant that varies from five drums (1,000 L) to twenty drums (4,000 L) is usually collected by the retail collector. After then, the customer acquires this oil in quantity. The retail collector and the bulk purchaser often agree on a price for buying and selling that is between 25 and 40 BDT/L (Bangladeshi Taka per liter). As for the bulk buyer, their monthly collection of used lubricant oil varies depending on the area within and surrounding the city of Dhaka. The collecting capacity may vary from ten drums (2,000 L) to three hundred drums (60,000 L). The region around Mohammadpur tends to have the highest collection volume, while the area around Amin Bazar has the lowest. The majority of bulk collectors mostly sell their used lubricant to Olium Lube Industries, Himu Oil, and Min Oil, situated in Wrisipara-Gazipur, Hemayetpur, and Kamrangir Char, respectively. Furthermore, they provide used lubricating oil to diverse clientele, including brickfields, molding shops, lathe shops, brick crushers, and store shutters. The price at which the bulk buyer sells the used lubricant oil typically ranges between 35 and 45 BDT/L. Though this is the present state of affairs in and around Dhaka, it can also apply to the entire nation. The whole process is represented in Fig. 9.

Effects of contaminants and the importance of recycling

The contaminants included in WLO pose several risks to human health and the environment [65,66]. When WLO is contaminated with degraded pollutants, additives, water, and sediments, it can become harmful. Inappropriate disposal methods of WLO may negatively impact the environment and human health. The presence of water-soluble materials in lubricants makes them susceptible to seeping into garbage and subsequently polluting groundwater through runoff, leaching, and percolation in landfills with permeable soils. Dumping WLO into rainwater or rivers can also impact water bodies and coastal regions. The excessive presence of WLO poses a threat to the lives of plants and animals, leading to financial losses for the fishing and leisure industries [67]. According to Musemic and Basic [68], inappropriate disposal of WLO may result in substantial and enduring adverse effects on the environment. WLO can prevent essential chemical components, such as oxygen, from entering water sources, thereby endangering the health of various aquatic organisms. Additionally, WLO contains harmful compounds, including PAHs, which are notorious for their carcinogenic properties when ingested or inhaled by living beings. These compounds pose a serious health risk and can lead to the development of cancer. It is therefore crucial to ensure proper disposal methods for WLO to mitigate these environmental and health consequences [69]. Dumping used oil into water forms a thin surface film that obstructs sunlight penetration and hinders the dissolution of oxygen in the water [4]. The eventual result of this negative outcome is the loss or decline of aquatic plants and animals. Furthermore, the WLO may volatilize and contaminate the surrounding atmosphere when subjected to elevated temperatures in the presence of air. The WLO comprises a diverse array of contaminants that pose risks to human health, aquatic organisms, the environment, and includes heavy metals [70]. These contaminants include hydrocarbons, halogen compounds, gums, varnish, and polychlorinated biphenyls. Furthermore, the chance of developing cancer is markedly increased when dangerous components such as aliphatic hydrocarbons and PAHs are present in the WLO [71]. Moreover, WLO has a highly toxic effect on animals, particularly on embryos and infants. Lubricating oil, owing to its bio-persistent characteristics and the inclusion of harmful pollutants such as additives, heavy metals, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), exhibits a considerable threat to the health of wildlife, plants, and aquatic organisms [65,73,74]. According to reports, lubricating oil containing BTEX, PCBs, PAHs, and chlorinated solvents may be linked to the growth of tumors and cancer. Preventing the leakage and inappropriate disposal of lubricating oil is crucial in order to reduce the negative impact on human health and the ecosystem [25,[74], [75], [76]]. The production of WLO is increasing in tandem with the steady rise in the number of cars. Managing and disposing of this trash is indeed becoming more and more important, especially in urban and industrial areas. Numerous countries are presently confronting the problem of environmental contamination resulting from the waste of lubricating oil [78].

In addition to safeguarding the environment, WLO recovery aids in the preservation of a highly prized resource that is nonrenewable [34]. WLO is recognized as a significant energy source on a global scale and is valued for its carbon content. By implementing sustainable practices and promoting recycling and reprocessing of WLO, we can contribute to the conservation of these valuable resources for future generations [79]. When it comes to energy conservation, effective use of WLO conserves priceless renewable resources. Many firms are currently spending a significant amount of money on WLO recovery, exploration, and processing into high-quality lubricating oil. Moreover, WLO generated by clients may be recycled again and again into useful goods like pure fill oil, saving a ton of money and effort [79,80]. Conventional techniques

After using the lubricating oil for a certain period of time, it loses its main characteristics and becomes unusable, requiring new oil to supplant it. The waste oil can be reprocessed to regain the characteristics, for which the oil was being used. There are several re-refining technologies with some merits and demerits.

Acid/clay treatment

Procedure

Used motor oil of a specific grade can be collected from a car engine or from engine repair shops and needs to be stored in a plastic container. Fresh lube oil can be collected from supermarkets or car repair shops. Clay sample is also required for acid/clay treatment and can be found in different areas. The Most commonly used is Kaolinite clay [44], whereas Bentonite can also be used for clay treatment [89].

The clay sample is first ground and mixed with distilled water to make slurry, which is subsequently dehydrated in an oven at 105 °C for 6 h. It is eventually grounded into fine particles (<75 μm mesh sieve).

According to Fig. 12, to create a suspension, acid—mostly sulfuric acid—is combined with pulverized clay. After creating slurry for over 3 h at 200 °C on a magnetic hot plate stirrer, the mixture is transferred to a Buchner funnel to isolate the clay from the acid. After that, the clay is repeatedly cleaned until it reaches a neutral point. After being dried at 105 °C for 8 h, it is again crushed into tiny particles.

Acid/Clay treatment process.

Before the acid clay treatment, the used oil goes through a filtration process to eliminate solid particles such as sand, metal chips, and other contaminants. The components needed to perform this filtering process are a vacuum pump, a Buchner funnel, and filter paper. A rubber stopper is required to close the Buchner funnel, which is attached to the filtering flask and vacuumed using the pump. After filtering, the oil is left to settle for a full day so that any leftover contaminants may separate and sink to the bottom. Furthermore, various chemicals can be employed to reduce the acidity level and alleviate the burden of acid in the oil [32].

To create a homogenous mixture, acid and pretreated oil are combined in a 10:1 ratio. Here, a variety of organic and inorganic acid types can be employed. Sulfuric acid is the most often utilized inorganic acid in this technique and yields better results than any other acid in terms of restoring chemical characteristics. On the other hand, there are certain drawbacks in terms of environmental harm. Consequently, under certain circumstances, additional inorganic acids such as hydrochloric acid, nitric acid, and phosphoric acid are utilized. Formic acid, glacial acetic acid, oxalic acid, and other common organic acids are employed in this method. Although organic acids cannot renew oils more effectively than inorganic acids, they do not pollute the environment to the same extent. After that, the oil sample is moved to a separating funnel and left for a full day to settle. This will cause the acidic sludge to sink to the bottom of the funnel. At the flask's bottom, the sludge is released. The acid-treated oil is passed through a bed of activated clay via a Buchner funnel that is fitted with filter paper and activated clay. The funnel is connected to a vacuum pump. Next, a 10 % NaOH solution is used to neutralize the acidic solution. The regenerated lubricating oil is then decanted from the top for subsequent usage after the treated oil is left motionless for a full day to allow for sedimentation [90]. Chemical properties after acid/clay treatment

To assess the quality and predict the performance of lubricating oil, it is crucial to comprehend various physicochemical properties such as water content, metal content, neutralization number, specific gravity, viscosity, flash point, fire point, pour point, and more. The acceptable values for these chemical attributes of lubricating oil may differ based on the grade of the oil. It is important to keep the water content at a low level; however, it tends to rise after the oil is used for engine lubrication, as indicated in Table S1 (see Supplementary Data). Monitoring and understanding these physicochemical properties, including water content, enable us to gain insights into the quality and performance of the oil, facilitating appropriate maintenance and corrective measures when needed. Studies have indicated that procedures involving acid/clay treatment reduce the water content of lubricating oil. Conversely, the viscosity should be high in a fresh, useable oil sample, and it decreases after a few days of usage. Following the treatment process, the viscosity nearly reaches the level observed in fresh oil. Nevertheless, the specific gravity rises above its standard value after a period of use, necessitating an adjustment. It is evident from various findings that acid/clay treatment is effective in restoring the properties of fresh oil. Furthermore, this treatment method is proficient in eliminating almost all undesirable substances to eliminate sulfur and metal content from used oil.

Solvent extraction

Solvent extraction is an appealing method that may preserve resources by recovering base oil rather than discarding it [5]. From an analytical perspective, the solvent extraction method improves various physicochemical properties of the oil, such as volatility, low-temperature characteristics, oxidation stability, and viscosity index [99]. The significance of extraction-flocculation in reducing environmental consequences lies in its capacity to produce less hazardous and reusable by-products. This characteristic contributes to reducing the overall environmental effects associated with the process.

The extraction-flocculation process generates reusable byproducts that are less hazardous. This method offers a high rate of base oil recovery and minimizes oil losses, making it the most effective option in the current technological and environmental context. It demonstrates both technological and environmental viability, making it a favorable choice [100]. The extraction-flocculation technique lowers the main oil loss from the sludge phase by around 10 %–14 %. This reduction in neat oil loss is comparable to the number of contaminants and additive chemicals found in WLO. This approach conserves vital resources and enhances the overall effectiveness of the recycling process by reducing the loss of base oil [21]. The effectiveness of the extraction procedure is significantly influenced by the solubility of the solvent used [101,102].

In their study, Hamad et al. [26] utilized hydrocarbon solvents derived from liquefied petroleum condensate and stabilized condensate to treat WLO. This method successfully decreased the asphaltene concentration of the treated oil to 0.106 % (w/w), reduced the ash content to 0.108 %, and lowered the carbon residue to 0.315 %, while retaining negligible quantities of hazardous metals. The total oil output had a yield of 79 %. In another analysis conducted by Katiyar and Sattar, various solvents, including MEK, MIBK, 2-propanol, and 1-butanol, were examined. Among these solvents, 1-butanol resulted in the highest amount of sludge formation. MEK, on the other hand, yielded superior outcomes with minimal oil waste. Additionally, the percentage of oil loss decreased as the extraction temperature increased [103]. In their study, Osman et al. employed a combination of three solvents and activated alumina as an adsorbent to facilitate the reclamation of used motor oil. The solvent combination comprised toluene, butanol, and methanol. This particular solvent combination demonstrated impressive efficiency, leading to the highest level of sludge elimination. Furthermore, the highest percentage of sludge removal augmented with an increase in the solvent-to-oil ratio [5]. Hydrotreating

Hydro-treatment is a crucial method used by petroleum-based companies to generate superior fuels. It is an integral step within a comprehensive process that utilizes catalysts and high-pressure hydrogen gas to reduce the sulfur, nitrogen, and metal content present in heavy crude oil [120]. The hydro-treatment process is conducted within the temperature range of 315–425 °C and under pressure ranging from 700 to 20,000 kPa [3]. The process involves several steps: (i) using vacuum distillation to segregate gas oil, base oil, and distillation residues; (ii) heating the waste oil to eliminate water and light chemicals; (iii) subjecting the base oil to a guard bed filtration to eliminate any substances that could hinder the catalytic bed; and (iv) subjecting the resulting base oil to hydro-treatment. This procedure results in the production of a re-refined oil product and is particularly beneficial for the process of hydrogenating unsaturated molecules. Furthermore, this method currently has the same economic benefits as the use of hydrocracking catalysts [121].

When the buildup of the liquid product stops and additional vapor phase product development in the system stops, a visual inspection of the reactor is carried out to confirm if the reaction is finished or not. If there was no longer any visible "sticky" consistency in the carbon material and there was no leftover oil sample inside the reactor, the reaction is deemed to have ended. After the reaction is finished, the fan aids in cooling the reactor while the microwave emitter is turned off. Up until the reactor temperature drops to 80 °C, the N2 stream is maintained. The reactor is then sealed to keep the carbon bed from coming into contact with outside air after being detached from the condensation mechanism.

The quantification of leftover material in the reactor and its contents is achieved by measuring the weight change before and after the reaction process. The yield of the liquid product may be estimated by evaluating the weight increase of the collecting containers and filter. Any mass of the initial sample that cannot be accounted for through residual and liquid product measurements is assumed to have left the system in gaseous form, and the gas production is determined through mass balancing. The data collected for analysis represents the average of three valid duplicate runs conducted under the same experimental conditions. Analytical methods such as gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), and scanning electron microscopy/energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDX) are used to ascertain the chemical composition of the pyrolysis products. These methods provide valuable information about the chemical components present in the pyrolysis products.

Comparison among the conventional re-refining techniques

Among all the conventional re-refining technologies, acid/clay treatment provides the best result in regaining the properties with the lowest production cost and a simple procedure. However, it provides the minimum environmental safety by producing a large amount of acidic sludge as a by-product. On the other hand, solvent extraction is a much more environmentally friendly process, because the solvents can be recycled and it does not produce any harmful by-products. However, the physicochemical properties of the end products after the solvent extraction process do not provide as good a result as acid/clay treatment. Here, hydro-treatment can be a better substitute, but it is a relatively costly technique with relatively low efficiency. Vacuum distillation is uneconomical for industrial use. Though it provides better color, it creates a resinous foul that causes harm to the equipment. Table 6 depicts an overall comparison of the recycling techniques.

Advanced techniques

Despite notable advancements in recent decades, it has been noticed that relying on a single recycling technique may not be enough to meet the desired level of emission control when it comes to removing pollutants from old oil, given its intricate composition [142]. Nevertheless, when integrated, these technologies have been shown to significantly improve therapeutic effectiveness and ecological sustainability. Modern re-refining technology may generate higher-quality base oil compared to historic and conventional processes. Some firms have implemented distinct methods and process conditions in the recycling of spent lubricants, which include a variety of methodologies. Countries such as the USA, European countries, Australia, and Saudi Arabia have established extensive systems for the efficient management of WLO. These countries have also built crucial businesses that are already in operation [143,144]. Globally, there are more than 400 oil re-refining plants with a combined capacity of 1.8 million metric tons annually. These plants use different methods [145]. There are a total of 200 oil recyclers throughout North America. Europe has a total of 28 refining facilities; however, only 17 of them are involved in the manufacturing of base oil. Nevertheless, these procedures require a substantial upfront financial commitment in the form of costly and advanced equipment, together with a crew with exceptional expertise [146,147]. Due to increasing environmental concerns, the conventional acid-clay treatment approach has been supplanted by advanced methods emphasizing solvent extraction and hydro-processing. Numerous advanced industrial methods are being used throughout several European nations to tackle this transition. These processes encompass coupled thermal de-asphalting and hydro-processing (Revivoil), solvent extraction (MRD process) (Interline), solvent extraction and hydro-finishing (Cyclon, Snamprogretti), thin-film evaporation alongside various finishing processes (Echohuile, Vaxon, CEP), hydro-processing (Hy Lube), in addition to thermal de-asphalting and clay finishing. These technologies offer alternative and more environmentally friendly approaches to treating and refining oils and petroleum products [137]. This paper offers a thorough review of the existing waste oil recycling methods used in European nations, drawing on the literature already accessible. It is important to mention that the technologies discussed in this document do not include all the techniques used in Europe. Instead, the focus is on facilities with a capacity above 40,000 metric tons annually, mostly for base oil production.

Hydro-processing technology has the capability to produce high-quality oil regardless of the initial feedstock quality. However, it is susceptible to catalyst poisoning, which is a drawback associated with this method. Conversely, alkali treatment agents such as CEP, Vaxon, Ecohuile, and Interline are incompatible with certain categories of synthetic lubricating oils during the re-refining process. Conversely, solvent extraction treatment is independent of the feedstock's characteristics and may efficiently handle used oil from various origins. The solvent extraction method requires a homogenous reaction mixture to produce high-quality base oil. Both solvent extraction and hydro-processing processes need a significant initial capital investment for advanced and expensive equipment, along with qualified staff. Consequently, integrating the MRD solvent process with hydro-processing might provide a viable method for re-refining spent oil. The MRD solvent method alleviates catalyst poisoning without requiring alkaline treatment, while hydro-processing may provide high-quality base oil irrespective of the feedstock's grade. This section categorizes the technologies into two classes, as detailed below.

With sigle processes

Interline

Interline Hydrocarbon Inc, a company located in Utah, has developed a technique for extracting propane from used clay oil [148]. In 2001, Interline acquired a patent for an enhanced extraction procedure. This approach uses chemicals and catalysts to pre-treat used oil, leading to an enhanced capacity for selectively separating propane [149]. The refined lubricating oil is categorized as API Group I. Nonetheless, the oil product may be categorized as Group II owing to its elevated saturated percentage (91–92 %) and viscosity index (>100) [150]. The interline process yields a lubricating oil with a composition consisting of 79 % [149]. This technique is often considered the most effective method for the waste treatment sector [137].

Hy Lube process

Through catalytic processing of lubricating oil, Universal Oil Products (UOP) developed the Hy Lube technology, which yields sealable lube base oil and processed lube base oils [144]. The pressure and temperature ranges in which the reactor of the Hy Lube unit operates are 60–80 bar and 300–350 °C, respectively [151]. Hydrogenation is used to align the attributes of three distinct base oil products with those of Group II base oil [152]. About 85 % of the lubricating oil may be recovered by the Hy Lube technology from the feedstock's hydrocarbon lube boiling range.

With combined processes

Vaxon process

This technology centers around the utilization of a thin film evaporator to distill oil cuttings in the absence of air pressure. This strategy is often used in Denmark (Kalundborg) and Spain (Barcelona-Tarragona). This process is distinguished from previous re-refining procedures for scrap oil by the use of a vacuum distillation device called the Vacuum Cyclone Flash Evaporator. This unit efficiently minimizes the occurrence of oil cracking. The whole procedure has three components: chemical treatment, solvent extraction, and vacuum distillation. The vacuum distillation equipment efficiently segregates the raffinate into various base oils with differing viscosity classes [143]. The typical yield rate for this process is from 65 % to 70 %.

Ecohuile/sotulub process

The Ecohuile system employed vacuum distillation and acid-clay treatment prior to 2000 [146]. The facility has witnessed enhancements and advancements to implement the Sotulub technique as a consequence of the prohibition on clay adsorption. The Sotulub procedure utilizes high vacuum distillation and an alkali addition (an antipoll agent) to treat old oil. By using this approach, it is feasible to get a yield ranging from 82 % to 92 % [153].

CEP process

Evaporation and thin-film hydroprocessing are employed simultaneously in this method. Based in Hamina, Finland, Chemical Engineering Partners (CEP) is a process technology business with an annual production capacity of 60,000 metric tons, including 42,000 metric tons of base oil. The chemical treatment phase involves adding enough sodium hydroxide to reach a pH of 6.5 or above. A catalytic fixed bed reactor is used to eliminate heteroatoms such as sulfur, nitrogen, chlorine, and oxygenated organic molecules. The next step is to hydro-purify the remaining distillate at a pressure of 90 bar and a temperature of 315 °C [137]. The API Group II base oil criteria are met by using three consecutive hydro-treating reactors to lower the sulfur level to less than 300 ppm and increase the proportion of saturated compounds to more than 95 %. The base oil yield from this method is around 70 %.

Axen/revivoil technology

Axen and Viscolube worked together to create the Revivoil process. Jedlicze in Poland, Huelva in Spain, and Pievefissiraga in Italy are the three nations where the Revivoil method is now being used. The technique involves catalytic hydrogenation performed at elevated pressure. The technique consists of three main steps: hydro-finishing, thermaldeasphalting, and pre-flashing [154]. In this technique, the yield rate is around 72 % [137].

Cyclon process

The Greek industrial facility was established in 1982 with the purpose of implementing the Kinetic Technology International idea. Using this method, the Greek firm Cyclon Hellas can produce 40,000 metric tons per year [137]. Solvent extraction, hydroprocessing, and vacuum distillation comprise this approach. According to the references, the process of hydrogenation causes the production of high-quality base oil with a yield of 72 % [155]. Nevertheless, this process is costly even in the absence of the need for extensively skilled personnel. Due to the use of a distinctive vacuum distillation technique in this KTI process, thermal stress is eliminated by subjecting the material to short bursts of temperatures below 250 °C. As a result, there is no need for any pre-treatment. These factors promote thin-film evaporation. The unit has the capability to process a wide range of feedstock types by using a wiping film evaporator.

Snamprogetti/IFP process

French du Petrole was the innovator of the Snamprogetti/IFP method. The factory using this method produces approximately 84,000 metric tons annually. The procedure incorporates both the hydrogenation and vacuum distillation processes. It consists of two main steps: the first phase is atmospheric distillation, followed by vacuum distillation and propane extraction of the oil in a propane de-asphalting section operating within the temperature range of 75–95 °C. Hydrogenation is employed to improve the color and oxidation stability of the base oil. The yield rate in this process ranges from 74 to 80 % [137].

Other latest technologies

Thin-film evaporation

An internal rotating mechanism is mounted in the evaporator vessel to shorten the feedstock oil's residence time and lessen the impact of hydrocarbon impurities on coking in used oil. In order to stop the development of coke, this process is designed to generate significant amounts of turbulence. Cracking, which is the process of breaking down hydrocarbon molecules, typically occurs when the feedstock oil reaches temperatures exceeding 300 °C [137].

Thermal de-asphalting and clay finishing

Under high vacuum conditions (15 absolute torr), the oil is subjected to distillation in a highly efficient packed column as part of the thermal de-asphalting process. To prevent internal fouling, the system incorporates a high liquid recycling flow rate for cleaning purposes. The refined oil is exclusively treated with bleaching clay as a result of the progressive phase-out of sulfuric acid. Consequently, the consumption of adsorbent clay must increase from 3 % to 8 % to account for the absence of acid. In order to optimize the bleaching process, the oil and clay mixture is heated to a high temperature, cooled, and then transferred to a filter press for additional processing [137].

Various characterization techniques and physicochemical properties

Thermogravimetric analysis

Shara et al. [156] performed research to analyze the thermal stability of three samples. The samples examined in the study consisted of base oil, a polymer additive called ethylene propylene diene monomer, and a composition that includes the polymer additive and a base oil concentration of 8 %. Thermal gravimetric (TG) analysis was performed on these materials, revealing two distinct stages of degradation that characterize the breakdown process. The study's results suggest that the existence of the polymer additive in the base oil causes a change in the onset of thermal degradation, increasing it from 170 °C to 196 °C. In their study, Hussein et al. [112] performed thermogravimetric analysis to evaluate the necessity of dehydration in the extraction-flocculation process. The sample analyzed exhibited mass reduction in three distinct zones. Zone (1) exhibited minimal weight loss, suggesting a restricted presence of water within the temperature range of 102 °C–208 °C. At temperatures ranging from 266 °C to 466 °C, Zone (2) exhibited a substantial decrease in weight, with a maximal weight loss of 93 %. The depletion of the remaining bulk of the sample was indicated by Zone (3), which spans from 460 °C to 560 °C. The physicochemical parameters, including viscosity [157], flash point [158], and fire point [159], are directly influenced by the water content in the refined oil, as determined through thermogravimetric measurement. These qualities are essential in assessing the eminence of the refining procedure used to handle WLO. Abdelkhalik et al. [160] studied the thermal stability of oil using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) in a nitrogen atmosphere. The TGA analysis exhibited that the temperature at which a 17.5 % reduction in weight occurred was very close to the flash point temperature, with a difference of approximately ±10 °C, as determined according to the ASTM D92 standard. In a study conducted by Bredin et al. [161], the thermal properties of soot produced by diesel engines and commercial carbon black were examined using TGA analysis. Several mixtures of soot-in-oil were prepared following the guidelines of the ASTM 5967–08 technique. The findings revealed that there was a notable underestimation of the quantity of soot present in the oil due to the loss of carbon mass during combustion.

Optical microscopy

Optical microscopy allows for the analysis of a digitized picture, which can be used to verify the cleanliness and absence of suspended particles in a sample. This verifies the effectiveness of the method in eliminating insoluble compounds. Filho et al. [20] examined the homogenization and optical microscopy of lubricating oil waste, uncovering many suspended particles (average diameter of 1.0 ± 0.1), which suggests that the remaining oil is unfit for use.

Atomic absorption spectroscopy

Riyanto et al. [123] tested the quality of lubricating oil using an atomic absorption spectrometer (AAS). During the preliminary phase, the concentrations of Ca, Mg, Pb, and Cr decreased. The incorporation of kaolin led to a substantial elevation in the iron (Fe) concentration, increasing it from 36.76 to 90.21 mg/kg. Potassium hydroxide (KOH) was determined to improve the efficacy of alcohol in extracting sludge from waste oil. Utilizing 3 g of KOH resulted in concentrations of Fe, Ca, Mg, Pb, and Cr measuring 74.32, 1.20, 8.83, 62.00, and 0.11 mg/kg, respectively. Applying 3 g of KOH led to a reduction in the concentration of all metals in the oil, except for Fe and Pb, which showed an increase compared to the variations with 1 and 2 g of KOH. Although the concentration of Pb did not meet the ASTM D5185 criteria, the concentration of Fe exceeded it. The increase in metal content could be attributed to the incomplete reactivity of KOH throughout the procedure, which diminishes its effectiveness in assisting butanol in removing sludge. Additionally, insufficient adsorption may contribute to the potential for higher metal concentrations, as metals have low migration from kaolin. In another study carried out by M.U. Dabai and N. Bello, the levels of copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), and lead (Pb) in lubricating oil were measured using atomic absorption spectroscopy. The results of the study exhibited a reduction in the concentration of these metals following treatment with mineral acid (H2SO4/clay) [90]. In their study, Wolak et al. investigated the presence of various heavy metals, including Ni, Cu, Mg, Zn, Pb, Fe, Cr, and Al, in engine oils. They utilized an atomic absorption spectrophotometer for their analysis. Among the oils tested, ME 017,764 oil exhibited the highest concentration of iron, measuring 73.87 mg/kg. Conversely, CE 017,977 and PS 018,799 oils had the smallest increase in iron content, with measurements of 31.22 mg/kg and 47.23 mg/kg, respectively [162]. In their study, Reis et al. employed flame atomic absorption spectrometry to detect the presence of copper, chromium, iron, and lead in lubricating lubricants. Their results revealed a positive correlation between the concentrations of copper (Cu) and iron (Fe), which ranged from 0 to 40 ppm (w/w), as well as between the concentrations of chromium (Cr) and lead (Pb), which ranged from 0 to 15 ppm (w/w) [163]. Carballo et al. developed two environmentally friendly methods, known as the "emulsion" and "three-component solution" methods, for analyzing the presence of V, Cu, Cr, Mo, Ni, Pb, and Ag in WLOs using electrothermal atomic absorption spectrometry (ETAAS). By conducting tests against three reference materials—SRM 1084a, "Wear-Metals in Lubricating Oil," obtained from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), "LO-010698 Lubricating Oil," obtained from the Institute for Interlaboratory Studies, and "Used Oil HU-1," acquired from SCP Science—the efficacy of these methods was confirmed [164]. A study conducted by Nwosu and colleagues compared the concentrations of wear metals in both new and WLO samples. Eight different oil samples were examined. Flame atomic photometry analysis and atomic absorption spectrometry were used to measure the metal concentrations. The specific gravity of the fresh, unused oil was determined by the researchers to be between 0.941 and 0.954. Conversely, the oil samples that were employed exhibited a specific gravity that ranged from 0.923 to 0.932. This suggests that the specific gravity decreased subsequent to the oil's utilization [145].

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

The organic compound composition of an original Ravenol 5W-40 base oil was analyzed by Hamawand et al. using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR). The unused base oil contained aromatics, alkanes, alkynes, alkenes, and alkyl halides, as indicated by the analysis. Additionally, oil samples that had been utilized underwent FTIR analysis. Additional peaks were observed in the spectra of the used oil, suggesting that primary oxidation products had been generated at elevated operating temperatures. The researchers employed an acid/clay procedure to decontaminate one of the used oil samples. The FTIR analysis of the treated used oil revealed the presence of carboxylic acid groups, an aldehyde group, and the ongoing presence of aromatics and alkenes [44]. Hegazi et al. also used FTIR analysis to examine engine oils. The presence of primary oxidation products formed when the oils were subjected to high temperatures during use was indicated by the observation of additional peaks in the FTIR spectra of used oils compared to new oils. Despite the application of formic acid and activated carbon to a single used oil sample, the oil continued to contain certain oxidized components. Likewise, the FTIR spectra of another spent oil sample treated with acetic acid showed extra peaks, demonstrating that primary oxidation products developed at high temperatures were still present after this acid treatment process [165]. Abu-Elella et al. reported additional peaks in the FTIR spectra of spent engine oils compared to fresh oil. This suggested the existence of a variety of primary oxidation products, including carboxylic acids, that are produced at elevated temperatures. Carbonyl-containing degradation products, including lactones, esters, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids, and salts, were identified by a broad peak at 1743.74 cm−1. The presence of aromatic compounds was indicated by peaks at 813.46 cm−1 and 884.53 cm−1. A peak at 1603.88 cm−1 signified nitration products, contributing to the used oil's darker color. The presence of the -OH group indicated water in waste engine oil. Oil recovered using formic acid-clay, however, contained few oxidized chemicals, such as carboxylic acid. In contrast, oil from acetic acid-clay treatment lacked aldehyde or carboxylic acid peaks, such as at 2359.56 cm−1 [94]. Dabai and Bello [90] also observed extra peaks in used oil, which implies the presence of primary oxidation products that are generated at elevated temperatures. Short-chain compounds, which usually emerged through oxidation in motor oil, were represented by peaks at 1707.10 cm−1 and 1802.8 cm−1. These compounds contain ester, ketone, and acid carbonyl groups. There was no detection of carbonyl compounds in oil that underwent sulfuric acid-clay processing. The presence of carboxylic acid, water, glycol contamination, and antioxidant additives was indicated by the presence of some oxidized products in the oil that was treated with oxalic acid. Consequently, the sulfuric acid-treated lubricating oil produced the most favorable outcomes, displaying peaks that were comparable to those of base oil. This was consistent with the results reported by Hamawand et al. [44]. Table S3 (see Supplementary Data) summarizes the FTIR profiles of fresh, waste, and recovered lubricating oil.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry

Dabai and Bello [90] conducted gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis on base oil, spent oil, and treated oil to quantify explicit organic compounds, particularly hydrocarbons and oxidative compounds. The GC-MS analysis of base oil did not reveal any oxidative products or carboxylic acids. However, it did identify 2-methylnaphthalene and hydrocarbons such as undecane, tri-decene, tetradecane, pentadecane, cyclo-pentadecane, hexadecane, heptadecane, octadecane, nonadecane, and eicosane. The GC/MS analysis of spent engine oil identified the presence of new compounds, such as mesitylene, o-cymene, and 2,3-dihydro-4,7-dimethyl-1H-indene. GC-MS analysis of the used oil also detected methoxy-acetic acid 3-tetradecyl ester, along with benzene and naphthalene derivatives, consistent with Dabai and Bello's findings [90]. GC-MS analysis detected no new compounds or carboxylic acids in used oils treated with sulfuric acid. However, hydrocarbons such as dodecane, hexadecane, heptadecane, nonadecane, and eicosane were also present. The GC-MS analysis of used lubricants treated with oxalic acid revealed the presence of benzene-based compounds, including 1,2,4,5-tetramethylbenzene, 2-ethyl-1,4-dimethylbenzene, and 1,2,3-trimethylbenzene. Naphthalene-based compounds, including naphthalene and 1,6-dimethyl-naphthalene, were also identified, along with other hydrocarbons like pentadecane, heptacosane, and heptadecane. BET surface area and XRD

The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method was employed by Sadeek et al. [1] to investigate the surface areas of fresh, wasted, and regenerated catalysts. The catalysts' surface area and other properties were substantially enhanced by the quantity of metal recovered from dissolution. The increased surface areas observed were ascribed to the catalyst pores opening up as a result of the interaction with the leaching agents that were tested. Oxalic acid +5 % Fe(NO3)3 exhibited the highest recovery of metal contaminants among all the leaching agents that were analyzed. Consequently, it showed the greatest surface area and pore volume measurements in comparison to the other leaching treatments. The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) and BET surface areas of a variety of catalysts were analyzed by Bhaskar et al. [124]. The Fe/SiO2 catalyst exhibited a surface area of 270 m2/g, which was significantly greater than that of Fe/SiO2–Al2O3 (250 m2/g) and Fe/Al2O3 (100 m2/g). The Fe2O3 phase was detected in the catalyst pre-reaction through powder XRD analysis. The Fe2O3 crystalline size in the Fe/SiO2 catalyst was the smallest at 7.40 nm, while it was the largest at 15.80 nm in Fe/SiO2– Al2O3. The Fe/Al2O3 catalyst exhibited a crystalline size of 9.20 nm. Using the BET approach, Pongsendana et al. [128] investigated the surface characteristics of the produced mesoporous carbon-supported catalysts, Co-Mo/MCG1 and Co-Mo/MCG2. It was found that the mesoporous carbon support alone had a specific surface area of 687.60 m2/g. The specific surface area of Co/MCG1 rose to 720.88 m2/g and for Co/MCG2 to 727.35 m2/g after cobalt and molybdenum impregnation. The increase in specific surface area after impregnation indicated a homogeneous distribution of cobalt and molybdenum on the mesoporous carbon substrate. Physicochemical properties of recycled lubricating oil

Viscosity

Viscosity refers to the force exerted on a surface by a fluid's changing velocity over a distance [166]. Temperature significantly affects viscosity. SAE viscosity numbers help identify lubricating oils by conveying their recommended viscosity levels for use in automobiles. A higher SAE viscosity number indicates the oil has a higher level of thickness or resistance to flow, meaning it is more viscous compared to an oil with a lower SAE viscosity number. Therefore, if an oil has a higher SAE number, it can be deduced that oil has a greater degree of viscosity [32].

Specific gravity and density

Since oils are produced based on weight but measured based on volume, it is necessary to establish their density [168]. Specific gravity is a measurement that compares the density of a substance to the density of water in the same volume. Knowledge of the temperature at which the density is measured is essential, since density is subject to fluctuations based on temperature [32].

Cloud point and pour point

Paraffinic wax and other oils are subjected to controlled cooling until they reach the cloud point [169]. It is, however, essential to recognize that the pour point does not indicate the temperature at which oil flow halts inside the system. This disparity is crucial for those who use lubricants at low temperatures [32].

Water content

Water can seep into lubricating engine oils through various means, depending on vehicle operating conditions. Sources of minor water contamination include oil cooler leaks, engine cooling system leaks, and ambient condensation accumulating in-vehicle systems over time. The water content of oils should not surpass designated "action" limits (no more than 0.5 %), depending on the grade and application. Harsh driving environments and drainage can lead to oil pollution from water. There is also potential for water from the radiator to impact the engine lubricant. High water levels can reduce oil viscosity by forming emulsions. Excess water contamination may also damage gear teeth and bearings [32].

Flash point

The flash point denotes the minimum temperature at which a liquid may generate sufficient vapors to ignite momentarily upon exposure to an ignition source, as assessed in a controlled setting. A lower flash point corresponds to a higher risk of ignition for liquid materials [170]. Conducting a flash point test on oil can reveal the presence of volatile components and indicate the temperature at which heating the oil will generate enough flammable vapors that, when mixed with air, could potentially ignite or flash [32].

Fire point

Evaluating the fire point and flash point is important when dealing with high-temperature processes involving oils. The fire point, specifically for open cup tests, denotes the temperature at which the oil will persistently combust for a minimum of 5 s after ignition. Determining the fire point builds upon the flash point test by further investigating the temperature at which the oil not only ignites but sustains combustion after the heat source is removed. Therefore, measuring an oil's fire point provides additional insight beyond just its flash point in situations dealing with elevated operating temperatures and ignition risks [32].

Volatility and evaporation

Evaporation is the process where a liquid is converted into a vapor/gas form without changing the molecular composition of its constituents. Volatility describes a liquid's inherent propensity to undergo evaporation. Therefore, a highly volatile liquid readily transforms from a liquid to a vapor phase. When lubricating oils are exposed to elevated temperatures, they may thicken over time and deplete in quantity due to evaporation losses. The degree to which evaporation occurs depends directly on the oil's inherent volatility. Higher volatility in an oil indicates greater chances of evaporation taking place, especially under warm conditions. Thus, volatility serves as a measure of the potential for evaporation losses from a lubricating oil [96].

Thermal stability

Thermal stability denotes a lubricant's capacity to withstand deterioration or alteration of its molecular composition when exposed to elevated temperatures in an oxygen-deprived environment. When mineral oils are heated to high temperatures, they will undergo breakdown reactions that produce methane, ethane, and ethylene gases. Though refining can boost the thermal stability somewhat, adding lubricant additives does not further enhance base oil's inherent resistance to thermal changes. The thermal stability depends on the characteristics of the base oil composition itself and is not augmented by additive inclusion [96].

Neutralization number

The neutralization number denotes the level of acidity or alkalinity in a lubricating oil. If the oil exhibits acidity, the result is termed the Total Acid Number (TAN). For oils with alkalinity, it is called the Total Base Number (TBN). The TAN is used to quantitatively assess the amount of residual acidic compounds in the oil. Meanwhile, the TBN measures the quantity of alkaline additives remaining. TBN thus serves as a surrogate measure of acidity levels, but in the opposite direction, and it is important to maintain a minimum TBN value. In contrast, most oils naturally possess some mild acidity, so the TAN value better characterises these types of lubricants inherently [96].

Carbon residue

Coke or carbon forming propensity refers to the carbonaceous residue remaining after engine oil undergoes thermal decomposition in limited oxygen conditions. Carbon residue testing can also assess the carbonaceous deposits an engine oil may produce in a combustion chamber. However, the amount of carbon residue indicated does not reliably predict actual deposit formation, due to complex interactions between additives and other oil components. For instance, an ash-forming detergent ingredient may elevate the carbon residue measurement of the oil while diminishing deposits. Additionally, interactions between components and sulfuric acid can elevate sulfur concentration in the oil independent of deposit potential. A better correlation has been found between carbon residue, hydrogen to carbon ratio, nitrogen and sulfur content rather than carbon residue alone [171].

Metal content

Engine oil formulations contain heteroatoms in the form of metallic elements measured in parts per million (ppm). Metal concentrations can range from a few hundred to thousands of ppm and tend to rise with higher oil boiling temperatures or lower API gravities. Metals are primarily found in heavier oil residues associated with additive chemicals. Basic or highly basic engine oils contain fewer metals, making them purer grades. Some metals occur naturally in higher amounts in virgin oils as performance-enhancing additives. However, wear of engine bearings, bushings, and additives along with dilution from metal-containing gasoline, introduces many additional metals into in-use engine oils over time [172]. Used motor oil contains two types of metals.

Metal particulate contamination

Engine oil becomes contaminated with metallic particles as oil-coated engine surfaces break down, primarily due to insufficient lubrication, mechanical stress, abrasive wear, and/or corrosion effects. The metallic particles that enter the oil originate from degrading engine components. Their presence in the oil is often characterized based on particle hardness. As the concentration of these metal contaminants rises within the oil, they contribute to accelerated wear of engine parts. Therefore, the introduction of metallic debris into the engine oil occurs as a byproduct of component deterioration, facilitated by various lubrication inadequacies and operational stresses. Higher particulate levels then further exacerbate wear issues.

Elemental (metals)

Oil has a variety of metallic components that are added to improve its effectiveness. Metals included in engine lubricants are considered contaminants that must be entirely eliminated to provide a proper foundation for creating fresh, uncontaminated oil [173]. Copper (Cu) is incorporated into engine oils after use to improve their performance in bearings, valves, and valve guides. Furthermore, the presence of copper in engine oil coolers might be attributed to the combination of certain oil additives [174]. The additive package used to introduce zinc into the base oil consists of substances such as zinc diethyl-dithiophosphate (ZDDP), zinc dithiophosphate, and zinc dialkyl-dithiocarbamates. These additives improve the effectiveness of the lubricants by functioning as antioxidants, corrosion inhibitors, anti-wear agents, detergents, and severe pressure additives.

Nanotechnology in waste lubricating oil processing

Tribology is the examination of managing and controlling wear, friction, and lubrication [175,176]. Energy loss is a significant tribological concern caused by high friction. A research by Holmberg et al. [177] indicates that about one-third of fuel energy is squandered due to frictional losses from engines and other moving components, including gearboxes, brakes, and tires. Their analysis indicated that the use of innovative friction reduction technology in passenger cars might result in an 18 % decrease in friction loss within 5–10 years, yielding savings of 174 billion euros globally, and a 61 % reduction after 15–25 years, resulting in savings of 576 billion euros worldwide. This tendency is also seen in heavy-duty trucks [178]. The improvement of lubricating oil characteristics is critical in terms of protecting equipment from potential harm and lowering energy usage. Nano-additives are a relatively new lubricant additive in the industry. The advantages of using nano-additives include their appropriate dimensions for penetrating contact asperities, heat stability, diverse particle chemistries, and an immediate response rate with the surface, eliminating the induction time required for traditional lubricant additives [[179], [180], [181]]. The attributes of individual nanoparticles, including size, shape, and physicochemical characteristics, improve friction reduction and anti-wear performance [182]. There are five types of nanoparticle additives: metals, metal oxide, metal oxide composite nanoparticles, nano-carbon compounds, and boron-based nanoparticles [183]. The impact of a variety of metal nanoparticles on mineral oil was examined by Padgurskas et al. [184]. The nanoparticles (Fe, Cu, and Co) and their combinations were assessed. The behavior of Fe, Cu, and Co is altered when they are combined with SAE 10 oil. The most effective wear resistance capabilities are demonstrated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of Cu nanoparticles. Additionally, it was confirmed that the utilization of a combination of nanoparticles is more advantageous than their individual use. The impact of Sn and Fe nanoparticles added to multi-alkylated cyclopentanes was investigated by Zhang et al. [185]. MACs have been employed as lubricants in the aerospace industry. The friction, abrasion, and heat in MAC oil were diminished by these nanoparticles. The increased solubility of Fe nanoparticles in steel bass surfaces is the reason for their superior anti-wear properties.

Ashirob used Fe3O4 nanoparticles as catalysts to facilitate the hydrolysis of ester groups found in aged lubricating fluids. Caustic extraction, using Fe3O4 nanoparticles, causes the breaking of ester bonds, resulting in the creation of carboxylate salts and alcohols [186]. Ingole et al. carried out an investigation into the critical friction and wear properties of a blend of rutile and anatase phases of P25 and TiO2. Their results indicated that the coefficient of friction can be reduced and stabilized by a 0.25 wt% injection of TiO2 nanoparticles. The homogeneous development of TiO2 coatings on sliding surfaces may be the cause of this effect. The friction coefficient was marginally increased by P25 [187].

Song et al. [188] utilized the solvothermal method to produce and modify the surface of ZnAl2O4 nanoparticles with oleic acid in just one step. The solvent oil was found to have a restricted size distribution and adequate dispersion. When nanoparticles are changed, they become practically monodispersed in lubricant. The anti-wear and anti-friction properties of oil are significantly enhanced by ZnAl2O4 nanoparticles, with an optimal concentration of 0.1 wt%. In addition, ZnAl2O4 was superior to Al2O3 and ZnO. Popoola et al. [189] incorporated CuO nanoparticles into lubricating oil in quantities of 0, 0.75, and 1.50 wt percent. The tribological characteristics of the oil were evaluated using a ball-on-disc tribometer. The experiments were conducted at varying velocities (150, 200, and 250 rpm) and under three distinct loading conditions (2, 5, and 8 N). The optimal conditions for minimizing wear rate, coefficient of friction (COF), and maximizing flash temperatures were found to be a concentration of 1.1061 wt%, a speed of 217.6768 rpm, and a load of 5.0909 N. The behavior of TiO2 and CuO as nano-additives to lubricating oil was examined by N. Wu et al. [190]. It was discovered that the friction of oil is reduced by the addition of two distinct nanoparticles (CuO outperformed TiO2). Nano-ZnO has garnered significant attention for its extensive surface area, high surface energy, robust adsorption, diffusion, ease of sintering, and low melting point [191]. Furthermore, nano-ZnO is a common metal oxide that is easy to produce. Its addition to base oil would not only enhance the lubricant's tribological properties but also reduce costs. Nevertheless, the dispersion of ZnO in base oil may prove to be a difficult task due to its restricted solubility in oil [192].

ZnAl2O4 nanoparticles considerably improve the anti-wear as well as anti-friction attributes of oil, at an optimal dosage of 0.1 wt%. Furthermore, in contrast to Al2O3 and ZnO, ZnAl2O4 demonstrated better performance [188].Using friction and wear experiments, Wei et al. [193] examined the tribology of Al2O3-TiO2 nanocomposites. The nanocomposites, which were approximately 75 nm in size, demonstrated sufficient stability in base oil. The coefficient of friction was reduced, while the anti-wear effectiveness was enhanced by the incorporation of nanocomposites at only 0.1 wt%. In addition, Al2O3/TiO2 nanoparticles exhibit superior anti-wear and anti-friction properties in comparison to clean Al2O3 or TiO2. Aluminum zirconium was substituted for composite nanoparticles of ZrO2 and SiO2 and used as additives in lubricating oil. Subsequently, the product's functionality was evaluated under a diverse array of concentration percentages and burdens. The incorporation of these modified nanoparticles was demonstrated to significantly enhance their lubricating capabilities. The friction coefficient was reduced by approximately 16.24 % when the base oil was treated with an ideal concentration of 0.1 wt% [194].

Nanocarbon materials are categorized into four primary allotropes: zero-dimensional (0D), one-dimensional (1D), two-dimensional (2D), and three-dimensional (3D). Nanotubes, nanowires, and nanorods, categorized as one-dimensional nanomaterials, possess a multitude of applications [[195], [196], [197]]. The effects of varying volume percentages of fullerene nanoparticles in mineral oil were investigated by Lee et al. [198]. The surface friction temperature and coefficient were determined using disk-on-disk testing apparatus in the investigations. By adjusting variables such as additive fullerene volume fraction and normal forces, these two parameters were determined for unprocessed mineral oils and oils containing nanoparticles. The coefficient of fraction is determined by the volume concentration of fullerene nanoparticles in oil, and the frictional surfaces attrition. Ku et al. [199] evaluated the anti-wear and severe pressure capabilities of fullerene at varying viscosities by employing a disk-on-disk tester. The friction was reduced primarily by the reduction of interactions between metal surfaces as a result of the inclusion of fullerene nanoparticles. Further, it was shown that fullerene is more effective when added to lubricants with a lower viscosity. Ettefaghi et al. [200] conducted a study on the effectiveness of fullerene nanoparticles in 20W50 oil and compared their results to those of other carbon nanomaterials. The planetary ball mill technique was implemented to disperse nanoparticles in oil. The stability of fullerene nanoparticles in oil was determined to be satisfactory, as no precipitation was observed after 720 h. Additionally, it was observed that fullerene exhibited the least amount of thermal conductivity increase among all carbon nanomaterials. The initial investigation of multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWNTs) as an additive was conducted by Chen et al. [201]. Before refluxing them with stearic acid (SA), they subjected MWNTs to sulfuric and nitric acids to enhance their tribological properties. They showed that the tribological behavior of the nanoparticles and the pattern of particle dispersion in oil were both factors in determining the efficacy of nano-lubricants in reducing wear and friction. Ahmadi et al. [202] investigated the impact of MWCNTs on thermal conductivity, flash point, pour point, and viscosity. In order to prevent particles from aggregating and precipitating in oil, they employed a planetary ball mill. They proceeded by increasing the concentration of nanotubes by 0.2 wt%, which led to a 13 % and 3.3 % increase in the oil's flash and pour points, respectively. Nevertheless, the concentration of nanotubes is increasing as a result of this trend. The lubricating properties of oil may be diminished as the quantity of nanotubes increases, potentially as a result of agglomeration. After considering all of these countercurrent effects, they determined that a concentration of 0.1 wt% of MWCNTs in oil may be deemed optimal.

Graphene is a two-dimensional substance that is arranged in a honeycomb lattice and possesses exceptional anti-wear and friction properties. It is also an ideal candidate for lubricating machines due to its acceptable thermal, electrical, mechanical, and optical properties [203,204]. Gaphene was subjected to oleic acid treatment by Zhang et al. [205] in order to enhance its solubility in base oil. They postulated that the wear behavior of the lubricant could be significantly enhanced by the addition of an optimal quantity of graphene. Lin et al. [206] tested a diverse array of modifications to stabilize graphene particles in base oil, such as sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate, stearic acid, dodecyl trimethyl ammonium chloride, oleic acid, sorbitan monooleate, polysorbate, and others. They determined that stearic and oleic acid are the most suitable modifiers among all the compounds. The utilization of modified graphene platelets (MGP) at an optimal concentration of 0.075 wt% as an additive enhances lubricant attrition and load bearing. The friction coefficient of raw base oil or base oil with modified natural flake graphite (MNFG) is higher than that of oil with MGP. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) were employed by Pirachiet et al. to adsorb divalent metal ions, including Cu, Pb, Zn, Mn, Cd, and Co [207]. Nano-sized diamond particles have been shown to function as ball bearings between sliding elements of a machine, despite the fact that micro-scale diamond particles are widely used in industry as a polishing material [208,209].

Boron-based nanoparticles have been investigated because of their load-carrying and anti-wear properties. It is also environmentally safe and thermally stable, making it an excellent choice for use as a lubricant oil compound [208,210]. In comparison to TiO2 nanoparticles, titanium borate nanoparticles exhibit superior friction-reducing and anti-wear properties in base oil [211]. Abdullah et al. used hexagonal boron nitride nanoparticles to diminish the coefficient of friction and scar diameter [212]. The use of nano-cerium borate as a friction modifier is possible [213]. Opia et al. studied organic carbon nanotubes [214,215]. Carbon-based nanotubes, fullerenes, metal oxides, polymers, ceramics, and composites are the most often used types of nano-additives [216]. Zhao et al. [217] conducted a study on the anti-wear and load-carrying properties of nano-calcium borate (NCB) as a lithium lubricant addition. They also investigate the nanoadditive effects of greases. The nanoscale production of calcium borate was achieved through the utilization of the ethanol-supercritical fluid dehydration process. Calcium borate was assessed for its tribological properties as a lubricant addition for lubricating steel-steel contacts using an oscillating reciprocating friction and wear tester (SRV). The diameters of the nanoparticles were assessed using laser-dynamic light scattering (LDLS), and the microstructure of calcium borate particles was investigated using SEM. Nanoparticle concentrations were determined to be directly proportional to the coefficient of friction. The addition of NCB to lithium lubricant at concentrations ranging from 1.5 to 6 % (with an ideal concentration of 6 %) resulted in a reduction in friction coefficient and attrition, rendering nanoparticles suitable for use as friction-reducing agents (friction modifiers).

Based on contemporary technology, the majority of machines function for extended durations under heavy loads, necessitating the use of a suitable addition to control intense pressure and reduce wear. Using nano-additives may improve the longevity of the lubricating oil while it is being used, leading to energy savings. Moreover, the integration of nano-additives into processed lubricating oil has the capacity to improve performance and boost the tribological effect.

Production of diesel-like fuel from WLO

WLO can be recycled to produce fuel oil because it contains hydrocarbon components that give it a relatively high calorific value. One advantage of using reprocessed waste oil as a fuel is that the brake specific fuel consumption when burning it in internal combustion engines is lower compared to conventional fuels. This means less fuel by weight is needed to generate the same brake power output. As a result, utilizing waste oil dramatically reduces fuel costs since smaller quantities are sufficient to produce equivalent engine power [218]. The lower fuel consumption makes reprocessed lube oil a more cost-effective fuel alternative.

The selection of fuel may have a substantial impact on the engine's fuel consumption. The AFO fuel demonstrated a little reduction in specific fuel consumption, ranging between 3 and 4 %. Arpa, Yumrutaş, and Argunhan [220] reported the same pattern when using an alternative fuel derived from recovered waste oil. However, the energy issue necessitates consideration of the fuel's low heating value (LHV). The data unambiguously indicates that the LHV of DFO is inferior to that of AFO [221]. Therefore, despite the differences in fuel consumption rates, the engine's energy efficiency stays almost the same for both kinds of fuel. Prior research has also seen a similar trend with biodiesel fuel, however, its inferior LHV in contrast to conventional diesel fuel has been acknowledged [220]. No significant changes were seen between the two fuels for the other metrics.

Both blends exhibit a significant increase in brake thermal efficiency (BTE) across all loads. The higher energy content and cetane number of the blends, as seen in the table above, may be attributed to this when compared to diesel [219]. The higher calorific value of the recycled oil/diesel blends results in more heat being generated during combustion in the engine chamber, promoting more complete combustion. The improvement in BTE seen across the blended fuels directly correlates to the increasing proportion of recycled oil added to the diesel. Specifically, blends with 20 %, 15 %, and 5 % recycled oil content exhibited BTE increases of around 5 %, 4 %, and 2 %, respectively, over pure diesel operation at full load. Therefore, the calorific value boost translated to an observable gain in combustion effectiveness and engine thermal performance.

It is noteworthy that all fuel blends exhibited a decrease in brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) across varying engine load levels. The downward trend in BSFC is directly correlated with the amount of recycled oil added to the diesel fuel. Specifically, blends containing 20 %, 15 %, and 5 % recycled oil showed BSFC reductions of around 6 %, 5 %, and 2 %, respectively, compared to only diesel fuel at full load. The table illustrates how the elevated calorific value of the mixtures translates to less fuel required for combustion, lowering the BSFC. Therefore, the improved energy content of the blends enhances efficiency by reducing the amount of fuel needed to produce the equivalent power output [219].

Table 10 shows that exhaust gas temperature (EGT) increased across all load levels when using blended fuels compared to only diesel. Specifically, blends containing 20 %, 15 %, and 5 % recycled oil saw EGT rise by approximately 3 %, 2 %, and 1 %, respectively, at full load over pure diesel. Based on the tabulated data, recycled oil clearly has a higher energy content than diesel alone. This greater energy content in the blends promotes more complete combustion in the engine due to the enhanced thermal efficiency. Therefore, the elevated calorific value of the mixtures leads directly to higher exhaust gas temperatures as a result of improved combustion quality when employing the blended fuels [219]. The association between the increasing EGT trend and the amount of recycled oil added to diesel is directly proportional.

The data also demonstrates hydrocarbon (HC) emissions were lower for all blended fuels compared to diesel alone, across the entire load range. Specifically, blends containing 20 %, 15 %, and 5 % recycled oil showed HC reductions of around 18 %, 18 %, and 9 %, respectively, relative to full load diesel operation. This trend can be attributed to the higher energy content of the mixtures, which leads to an associated rise in average combustion chamber temperature. Furthermore, the improved cetane rating of the blends enhances combustion efficiency. The decrease in HC emissions directly correlates with the amount of recycled oil added to the mixtures. Blends containing greater proportions of recycled oil exhibited larger decreases in HC emissions.

NOx emissions decreased for all blended fuels relative to only diesel, across the entire load range tested. The more uniform temperature distribution inside the combustion chamber aided in mitigating NOx formation. Additionally, the ignition characteristics of the mixtures had an influence. Specifically, blends containing 20 %, 15 %, and 5 % recycled oil saw NOx reductions of approximately 3 %, 3 %, and 2 %, respectively, when compared to full diesel load operation. The prolonged ignition delay period of the blended fuels also contributed to decreasing NOx emissions. Therefore, combustion temperature control combined with ignition property modifications helped lower NOx emissions through various mechanisms when recycled oil was integrated into the diesel fuel.

Future prospects

The automobile industry is experiencing rapid growth, which has resulted in an increased quantity of WLO that requires a sustainable solution. Increasingly stringent regulations on emissions are driving the search for methods to lessen dependence on the production of virgin oil. The use of recycled oil provides an option. The re-refining of old oil is becoming an increasingly appealing alternative as researchers work to create processes that are both more effective and less harmful to the environment.

As a result of developments in procedures such as vacuum distillation and the introduction of novel cleaning agents, the amount and quality of base oil that can be recovered from spent oil might be improved. Increasing the amount of automation in recycling operations has the potential to make the process both more efficient and less expensive. It is possible that partnerships between firms that make oil and those who develop technology might speed up innovation in the recycling industry. As environmental concerns and technical progress continue to converge, it is anticipated that recycling old engine oil will play a more significant role in the future.

The recycling of WLO is a crucial area of research with significant environmental and economic implications. Future research can focus on improving the effectiveness, efficiency, and sustainability of used oil recycling processes. Key areas include researching new catalytic materials, utilizing nanoparticles to enhance purification and re-refining processes, exploring environmentally friendly solvents and chemical processes, using advanced numerical simulations to optimize existing re-refining methods, implementing AI and machine learning techniques to predict and optimize re-refining process parameters and outcomes, using advanced analytical techniques like FTIR and GC-MS to monitor and improve the quality of recycled oil, conducting comprehensive life cycle assessments to assess the environmental impact of different recycling processes, advocating for stricter regulations and standards for used oil disposal and recycling, and promoting awareness about the benefits of recycling WLO and encouraging responsible disposal practices among consumers.

Conventional acid clay implementation. and solvent extraction methods employ solvents after WLO regeneration. Recycled oil might be prone to corrosion and wear, reducing the longevity of engines and equipment. Corrosion during the usage of recycled oil requires further investigation.

The extent of polymeric bio-flocculant application in the extraction-flocculation process needs to be studied in the future, along with the development of suitable bio-adsorbents, in order to achieve qualitative and quantitative improvements in re-refined base lubricating oil in accordance with international standards.

Analyzing the feasibility of using re-refined oil as an alternative fuel source, focusing on engine performance, emissions, hardware compatibility, and regulatory compliance, will be an intriguing research field worth exploring.

Investigating potential applications of re-refined oil in various industries beyond automotive lubrication, such as hydraulics, manufacturing, and power generation, can also be considered. Fostering collaboration between industry stakeholders, academia, and government bodies can drive innovation and the adoption of best practices in oil recycling. By focusing on these future research directions, the recycling of WLO can become more efficient, sustainable, and economically viable, significantly reducing the environmental impact and enhancing the overall quality and utility of recycled products.

Conclusion

This paper analyzes global lubricant demand and market share, with a particular emphasis on the Bangladeshi perspective. It discusses various re-refining procedures, including process improvement, and compares lubricating oil qualities with chemical compositions using various methods. The paper also explores the use of nano-additives in lubricating oils and the potential of spent engine oil as a diesel alternative, based on engine performance testing and exhaust gas analysis.

WLO release poses ecosystem harm, necessitating circular economy waste management strategies. Re-refinement is cost-effective and environmentally friendly, addressing waste lubricating oil production, consumption, generation, and recycling. Among the numerous inorganic acids used in the acid-clay treating process, such as sulfuric acid, nitric acid, and hydrochloric acid, H2SO4 treatment improves the properties of refined lubricating oil. The acid-clay process employing organic glacial acetic acid and kaolinite as clay material resulted in refined oil quality that was 95 % similar to fresh oil.

Modern technologies based on solvent extraction, pyrolysis, membranes, and so on are ecologically friendly, but they have high operational and capital costs, low yields, and require highly skilled technicians (as compared to traditional processes), which is a significant disadvantage. Additionally, reducing costs through vacuum distillation and hydro-processing methods presents a significant challenge. The combined treatment approaches have produced excellent results while being ecologically friendly.

Various methods for characterization, such as TGA for the study of the thermal stability of fresh oil, used oil, and water content investigation; FTIR; GC-MS to determine the chemical composition of fresh, used, and regained oil; and AAS, were utilized to assess the metal content present in the used and re-refined lubricating oil. Out of the several modern methods employed in advanced countries, the MRD solvent process (which uses N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone as a solvent) has been shown to be superior since it allows for less catalyst poisoning without the need for alkaline treatment.

Research in tribology has revealed that nanoparticles can improve tribological performance by reducing wear and friction. Many researchers have used costly nanomaterials and created membranes, which are also costly processes. Thus, affordable materials must be investigated in order to make the process cost-effective.

The diesel-like fuel's characteristics, such as density, flash point, viscosity, sulfur content, and heating value, were investigated and determined to be comparable to the values of the diesel fuel examined in this study. The addition of Na2CO3 proved ineffective in terms of adjusting the distillation temperature. As the number of additives increases, the flash point and density rise, but the sulfur content falls. The diesel-like fuel was obtained from 75 % refined waste engine oil or 60 % waste engine oil.
