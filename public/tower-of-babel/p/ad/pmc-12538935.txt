Analysis of human disease burden, determinants of treatment costs and affordability perceptions in the Rwenzori region, Uganda using DALYs, OLS and Bayesian regression
Authors: Edwin Akugizibwe, Issa Ndungo, Kassimu Mpungu, Solomon Mulinzi, James Karahukayo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSub-Saharan Africa faces a substantial burden from both communicable and non-communicable diseases, yet gaps in health data tracking hinder understanding of health impacts and treatment affordability. This study examined disease patterns in the Rwenzori region (2013–2022) using Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) and assessed treatment costs and affordability perceptions through ordinary least squares (OLS) and Bayesian regression. DHIS2 records provided annual disease data, while survey data from 284 households informed analyses of treatment expenses and perceived affordability. Findings indicate that malaria, coughs, and intestinal diseases remain prevalent, with hypertension and cancer increasing in incidence. DALY analysis identified hypertension as the leading contributor to disease burden, followed by pregnancy-related hemorrhage and malaria. OLS regression showed that treatment costs were highest in private facilities, in households managing multiple conditions within a given period, and especially among elderly patients with persistent NCDs, highlighting a strong age–disease interaction (β = 173,013, p = 0.014). Bayesian regression indicated that caring for individuals over 60 (Median = 1.71, 95% CI: [0.54, 2.72]) and households with seven or more members (Median = 0.78, 95% CI: [0.25, 1.29]) exerted the greatest influence on perceived affordability. While distance to healthcare facilities did not directly affect treatment costs, it significantly shaped affordability perceptions. The study underscores the need for policies that improve healthcare affordability for vulnerable populations, including the elderly and large households. Strengthening health information systems and prioritizing preventive care for NCDs are essential. Bayesian approaches provide valuable insights to support evidence-based healthcare planning in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Disease burden, Treatment costs, Affordability perceptions, DALYs, Bayesian regression

Background

Sub-Saharan Africa continues to have a high prevalence of both communicable and non-communicable diseases compared to other regions. However, due to challenges in health data tracking, storage, and analysis [1], the extent of disease burden affecting communities, the associated treatment costs, and affordability perceptions remain inadequately studied.

In Uganda’s Rwenzori region, the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2) records more than 120 disease conditions affecting local populations. Malaria alone accounts for nearly 40% of outpatient visits, while HIV prevalence is estimated at 6.2%, slightly above the national average of 5.4% [2]. Non-communicable diseases are also on the rise: hypertension affects about 26% of adults and diabetes prevalence is estimated at 3%, with many cases undiagnosed [3]. Yet, several tropical and neglected diseases remain under-researched, despite their contribution to morbidity and household health expenditure [4]. Most existing studies focus on single conditions in isolation, making it difficult to obtain a holistic understanding of how communicable and non-communicable diseases jointly shape health outcomes.

Treatment-seeking behavior in the region reflects this complexity. Community members turn to public facilities, private clinics, pharmacies, or traditional healers [5]. Although public health facilities are designed to provide free care, frequent shortages of medicines, equipment, and staff often force patients to pay out of pocket [6]. Private facilities are valued for faster service, though they are seen as costly. Self-medication is also common due to the easy availability of medicines, while traditional medicine remains rooted in cultural beliefs.

Healthcare financing in Uganda is predominantly out-of-pocket, as a national health insurance scheme has not yet been implemented. In the Rwenzori region, households spend a significant share of their income on healthcare, with the poorest families most affected (MoH, 2021). While family and community networks sometimes provide support, comprehensive evidence on the actual costs of treatment and how affordability is perceived across different diseases remains limited.

To address these gaps, this study examines the disease burden in the Rwenzori region and analyzes treatment costs and affordability perceptions by applying Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs), Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), and Bayesian regression methods. Disease burden refers to the impact of a health problem measured by financial cost, mortality, morbidity, or other indicators [7], and the use of DALYs has become increasingly common for prioritizing interventions in public health [8, 9]. By combining routinely collected health system data with community-level survey evidence, this study aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the region’s health challenges and generate evidence to support data-driven healthcare planning and policy.

Methods

Methods for establishing disease prevalence trends

To establish disease prevalence trends in the Rwenzori region, we relied on data obtained from the DHIS2 database of the regional hospital, which aggregates data from all government and private health facilities within the region.

The DHIS2 data captures the number of individuals diagnosed with a specific disease at a given health facility within a particular year. For example, it may show that Health Center A in Sub-County A, District A, Region A recorded 11 cases of “105-CV02-Hypertension” in the year 2020. In the following year, the same health center may show a value of 0 or a blank entry. In this context, 0 is interpreted as no cases being reported and is therefore treated as a valid observation—not as missing data. However, when the data was aggregated to obtain the total number of cases per year for the entire region, some disease categories exhibited noticeable trends across years but had missing values in specific years. Overall, about 9.6% of the dataset contained missing values. These missing values were assumed to be Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) and were imputed by replacing each one with the average of its two adjacent values (preceding and succeeding years).

The database contains records of patients diagnosed with specific diseases over a 10-year period (2013–2022), covering over 120 diseases, which were aggregated into 76 disease causes. The annual prevalence per 100,000 for each disease cause was calculated by summing the total patient records from the DHIS2 dataset for a given year, dividing by the estimated population of the Rwenzori region for that year, and multiplying by 100,000, thus

To assess changes in prevalence, we compared the average prevalence in the latter five years (2018–2022) (Period 2) with that of the former five years (2013–2017) (Period 1). We conducted Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon tests to determine whether there were significant differences in average prevalence between the two periods.

Bayesian regression method to establish factors influencing affordability perceptions

The study employed Bayesian logistic regression to analyze the factors influencing affordability perceptions among community members dealing with various diseases. The survey data obtained was supplemented by key informant interviews conducted at selected health centers within the surveyed districts. To effectively combine data from multiple sources, Bayesian regression approaches were adopted. Bayesian methods are increasingly gaining prominence in health research, as they allow for the integration of diverse real-world data sources such as electronic health records, patient registries, claims data, and other supplementary data sources alongside survey or clinical data.22

Recent discussions have focused on selecting appropriate priors, particularly in terms of non-informative versus weakly or strongly informative priors. Non-informative priors are often flat priors, such as a wide uniform distribution [e.g. )] or a diffuse normal distribution [e.g.8. Another form of non-informative priors is Jeffrey’s Priors, which are based on the Fisher Information Matrix of the likelihood function.

The challenge with non-informative priors lies in two main issues: i) Depending on the range of plausible parameter values, non-informative priors can become strongly informative and lead to skewed posterior results, especially in small data cases. ii) The use of non-informative priors negates the advantages of Bayesian statistics. Specifically, Bayesian estimated parameters and inferences based on non-informative priors will mirror frequentist analyses using standard methods and will suffer from high type I and type M error rates [17].

Recent publications [17, 18] advocate for using informative priors. The most common strategy for selecting informative priors is to draw from earlier studies or expert opinion. Meta-analysis of previous studies can help generate informative priors to be integrated into the new data analysis.

This study employed a Bayesian logistic regression model with affordability (initially measured on a 5-level Likert scale, but converted to binary as affordable vs. not affordable) as the response variable. The predictor variables considered included: income, distance in kilometers, education level, locality, number of people in the household, and income source.

For the priors, a strongly informative Student-t prior was used, based on prior knowledge gathered from informant interviews at selected health centers within the surveyed districts, as well as insights from previous studies [18, 19]. Regression model for predictors of disease burden treatment costs

The results in Fig. 4 indicate that seeking treatment in a private health facility significantly increases annual treatment costs per household (p = 0.004), with an average increase of UGX 76,236 ($21.76) compared to opting for self-medication. The negative coefficient for seeking treatment in a public health facility shows that, despite existing cost-sharing mechanisms, it remains the most affordable option. This is further supported by the fact that 60.5% of respondents selected public treatment, followed by private treatment at 27%, and self-medication at 12.5% as an option they often use to seek treatment for diseases burdening them. Additionally, treating more than one condition within a given period increases costs by UGX 51,177 (p = 0.002).

Considering disease categories, the reference group was patients under 60 years with communicable diseases (CDs). Compared to this baseline, treatment costs were significantly higher for under-60 patients with non-communicable diseases (NCDs) (β = 99,900, p = 0.018) and those with “Other/Rare” diseases (β = 115,796, p = 0.014). Age alone did not significantly affect costs among CD patients (β = 8,315, p = 0.859). However, a significant age–disease interaction was observed for NCDs, with patients over 60 incurring an additional UGX 173,013 in treatment costs (p = 0.038). These findings highlight that treatment costs are disproportionately higher for NCDs, particularly among older patients, underscoring the compounded financial burden of aging and chronic disease.

Notably, distance and household size were not statistically significant predictors in the model, contrary to initial expectations. This finding motivated an examination of whether respondents perceived these factors as important in shaping the affordability of treatment costs. To address this, the subsequent section applies a Bayesian modeling framework to incorporate prior beliefs into the analysis.

Discussion

The study highlights the evolving disease landscape in the Rwenzori region, with both communicable and non-communicable diseases (NCDs) contributing substantially to the overall disease burden. While malaria, coughs, and intestinal infections remain prevalent, non-communicable diseases such as hypertension, cancer, and cardiovascular conditions are showing a significant upward trend (Table 3). These patterns are consistent with previous studies [23, 24], which suggest that without targeted interventions, NCD prevalence may surpass that of communicable diseases in Africa over the coming decades. Concurrently, the marked reduction in diseases such as measles and meningitis indicates that interventions—including public awareness campaigns, improved healthcare infrastructure, vaccinations, and enhanced drug distribution—are yielding positive results (IAHO Report, 2023). Nevertheless, the Rwenzori region and Uganda at large must intensify efforts to reduce malaria and HIV prevalence to meet African Union and WHO targets for disease elimination by 2030 [25]. Countries such as Eswatini, Algeria, and Cape Verde demonstrate that nationwide interventions—such as insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, and improved case management—can facilitate progress toward these goals [26].

Analysis of Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) revealed that hypertension represents the highest disease burden, followed by hemorrhage during pregnancy and malaria. For most conditions, premature mortality (Years of Life Lost) contributed more to the overall burden than morbidity (Years Lived with Disability), consistent with prior findings [27]. Similarly, WHO reports indicate that in Africa, communicable, maternal, perinatal, and nutritional conditions account for 59.1% of DALYs, while NCDs and injuries contribute 30.7% and 10.2%, respectively [28].

Treatment cost analysis using OLS regression showed that private healthcare facilities, multiple disease treatments, and care for individuals over 60 years with persistent NCDs significantly increased treatment costs. Household size and distance to health facilities did not directly influence actual expenses but strongly shaped affordability perceptions. Bayesian regression further confirmed that affordability concerns are driven by private healthcare costs, larger household sizes, and the financial burden of elderly care.

These findings suggest the need for targeted policy interventions, including the expansion of affordable healthcare services for the elderly and promotion of family planning to manage household sizes. Comprehensive data collection is also crucial to accurately assess disease burden and guide resource allocation. Policy recommendations emerging from this study include:

Allocating resources according to DALY-measured disease burden, focusing on high-impact conditions such as hypertension, maternal hemorrhage, and malaria.

Conclusion

This study contributes to international debates on health equity, disease burden monitoring, and healthcare affordability in low- and middle-income countries. Using DALYs, OLS, and Bayesian regression, the study applies robust, data-driven methods to inform health prioritization. Findings highlight the persistent double burden of disease in Sub-Saharan Africa, with rising NCDs such as hypertension alongside enduring infectious diseases like malaria, underscoring the need to strengthen preventive care and integrate NCD management into existing health systems.

The study also identifies disparities in treatment costs and affordability perceptions, particularly affecting vulnerable populations, including large households and the elderly. Cost differentials between private and public facilities, along with the impact of geographic distance on perceived affordability, reinforce the importance of universal health coverage (UHC) and equitable health financing. Overall, these results demonstrate the value of localized disease burden data and advanced analytic methods in guiding health policy and planning in resource-limited settings.

Limitations

This study faced several limitations related to data quality and scope. First, inconsistent and unsystematic recording at individual health centers resulted in gaps and missing values. Second, DHIS2 data were not sufficiently disaggregated by key variables such as age, gender, and other demographics, limiting analytical depth. Third, the DHIS2 system does not capture follow-up information such as recovery rates, time to recovery, or mortality outcomes, constraining the ability to fully assess disease burden and treatment effectiveness. Additionally, reliance on a 12-month household recall period may have introduced recall bias in reported costs and illness episodes, despite mitigation efforts through standardized questionnaires and cross-checks. Future studies should focus on strengthening health information systems, either through enhancements to DHIS2 or by adopting more comprehensive alternatives that capture both individual-level and longitudinal follow-up data.
