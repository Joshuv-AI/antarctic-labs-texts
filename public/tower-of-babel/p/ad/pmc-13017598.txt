Transcriptomic biomarkers related to cardiac disease in childhood cancer survivors: a case-control study
Authors: Naïla Aba, Gwénaël Le Teuff, Brice Fresneau, Serge Koscielny, Shaima Belhechmi, Boris Schwartz, Chiraz El-Fayech, Carole Rubino, Rodrigue S Allodji, Eric Morel, Delphine Dayde, Pierre de la Grange, Ariane Jolly, Noémie Pata-Merci, Camille Cordero, Isabelle Aerts, François Pein, Giao Vu-Bezin, Florent De Vathaire, Nadia Haddy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsCardiotoxic treatments like anthracyclines and heart-directed radiotherapy increase the risk of cardiac diseases (CDs) in childhood cancer survivors (CCSs), but individual differences in CD incidence are not fully understood. This study aims to identify transcriptomic biomarkers associated with CD occurrence after childhood cancer treatment.MethodsA matched case-control study was conducted on a sample of 330 CCS: 165 cases with CD and 165 CD-free controls. The expression of 8557 genes was investigated to select those associated with CD and heart failure (HF), using three stabilization approaches derived for the conditional logistic regression with Lasso (Percentile lasso, Bolasso, and Sublasso). The intersection of the three selected gene sets formed the final selection. The interactions between cancer treatment doses and selected genes were investigated.ResultsOne promising gene, NFE2L2, constituted the final selection, and its expression was lower in cases than in controls [CD: odds ratio (OR) .16, 95% confidence interval (CI) .09–.29; HF: OR .11, 95% CI .03–.37]. No interaction between treatment doses and NFE2L2 expression levels was found in our study. Incorporating NFE2L2 gene expression into prognostic models improved discrimination between cases and controls compared with models based solely on clinical and treatment variables [CD: area under the curve (AUC) .85 vs .66; HF: AUC .87 vs .77].ConclusionsUsing high-dimensional data selection methods has enabled the identification of the gene NFE2L2, associated with CD and HF in CCS. Further research is needed to validate this finding and achieve a better understanding of the biological mechanisms leading to cardiac toxicities and so to develop risk-adapted treatment and surveillance strategies.Keywords: Childhood cancer survivors, Cardiac diseases, Cardiomyopathy, Transcriptome, Gene expression, Case-control, Conditional logistic regression, Lasso

See the editorial comment for this article ‘ NFE2L2 in the spotlight: a transcriptional clue to paediatric cancer treatment cardiotoxicity’, by H. Fonoudi and P.W. Burridge, https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehaf608.

Introduction

Annually, approximately 385 500 children and young adults are diagnosed with cancer worldwide.1 Significant progress in treatments has drastically increased the 5-year overall survival rate for these young patients, to over 80% in the modern era.2,3 However, the future of childhood cancer survivors (CCSs) raises important questions regarding long-term adverse effects. Among these, cardiac diseases (CDs) emerge as the leading non-cancer cause of death among CCS,4 placing them at a sevenfold higher risk of dying from heart disease compared with the general population.5 Furthermore, CCSs face a significantly increased risk of mortality after a major cardiac event.6,7

Nearly 20% of CCS treated with anthracyclines experience cardiotoxic effects, with a risk of cardiac complications 3.5 times higher than those untreated.8 Higher doses, particularly those exceeding 360 mg/m², significantly increase CD risk. Additionally, radiotherapy is associated with developing late-onset CD.9 The incidence of CD is higher in patients receiving high radiation doses7,10,11 with a significantly increased risk for a dose >15 Gy.12,13

Although these treatments are strongly associated with CD, they do not entirely explain the individual variability in the incidence of the pathologies related to CD in CCS treated similarly,14,15 suggesting a potential role of genetic predispositions.11,16 Several studies have also investigated genetic biomarkers, focusing on (i) genetic characteristics of anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity17 and (ii) prognostic markers associated with this cardiotoxicity.18–25 Although several genetic variants were identified in association with CD, no consensus has been reached. Unlike the genome, the transcriptome is highly dynamic and sensitive to xenobiotic exposures, including anticancer treatments that can significantly affect gene expression profiling.26 The gene expression study may provide new insights into the mechanisms of cardiotoxicity in patients treated with anthracyclines and radiotherapy involving the heart. To date, only one study has analysed transcriptomic data of 104 patients identifying genes differentially expressed between CCS with cardiomyopathy and controls.27

This study aims to identify potential transcriptomic biomarkers associated with the occurrence of CD after childhood cancer treatment, using statistical methods developed for high-dimensional data.

Methods

Study design and population

This study is based on data from the French Childhood Cancer Survivors Study (FCCSS), a retrospective cohort of 5-year CCS diagnosed with solid cancer or lymphoma before the age of 21 between 1946 and 2000 in five French cancer centres28,29 (see Supplementary data online, Table S1). Clinical and treatment details were extracted from medical records, including cumulative anthracycline doses. Heart radiation doses were retrospectively estimated, and dose-volume metrics were reconstituted.30 Further data collection details are in prior studies.30,31 The CD diagnosis, including heart failure (HF), valvular disease, ischaemic heart diseases, pericardial disease, and cardiac arrhythmia, was validated based on criteria from the European Society of Cardiology32 and the Framingham studies.33 The CD assessment details are in Appendix 1. Cardiac diseases were graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.03. Venous blood samples were collected between 2011 and 2017 for 1571 patients using two EDTA tubes of 10 mL each (Appendix 2). Of these, 165 CCS who developed a CD constituted the cases. Each case was matched to one CD-free control on gender, age at cancer diagnosis (±3 years), first tumour type, and follow-up duration (each control has a follow-up higher or equal to that of its corresponding case) (Figure 1). The study protocol was approved by a regional ethics committee and by the national committee protecting data confidentiality (Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés), following French regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients, parents, or guardians, in line with national ethics standards.

Flowchart of patient’s inclusion in the study

RNA extraction

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from venous blood, and cultures of lymphocyte cell lines were made. RNA extraction was performed on lymphocyte cells of the 165 cases and their controls using the TRIzol Reagent method (Thermo Fisher, Invitrogen). To determine RNA concentration, a Nanodrop spectrophotometer was used, and the quality of RNA preparations was further assessed using Lab-on-a-chip Bioanalyser 2100 technology (Agilent technologies). The concentration of all RNAs was adjusted and verified with a second measurement on a Nanodrop spectrophotometer.

Gene expression profiling

The transcriptome of the 330 patients was obtained for over 33 000 genes. Analysis of the Affymetrix Human ClariomD Array was conducted by GenoSplice (Paris, France). Only the 8557 genes that passed the quality control were included in the analyses. Appendix 3 and Supplementary data online, Figure S1 describe the different steps involved in gene expression profiling.

Clinical endpoints

The primary endpoint was the occurrence of the first event related to CD, whatever the grade, defining the cases. The secondary endpoint was the occurrence of HF. A sensitivity analysis was performed by restricting to patients with a first grade ≥ 2 event related to CD.

Statistical analysis

To identify potential gene expression biomarkers associated with the clinical endpoints in the setting of high-dimensional data and considering the nature of the design (case-control matching), the Lasso (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) selection procedure adapted to a conditional logistic regression (CLR) was used.34,35 Standardized gene expression biomarkers were included in CLR with Lasso and without covariates adjustment. Three stabilization methods were considered to address the instability of the optimal penalty term (λ) estimation in the Lasso and thus on the biomarkers selection36,37 (see Supplementary data online, Figure S2). The Percentile Lasso approach involves selectingλ^(θ) corresponding to the θth percentile of the distribution of the penalization parameter (λ) derived from m replications of the CLR Lasso. Each execution of the Lasso procedure results in a single calculation of the optimal λ due to the random fold assignment within the cross-validation process.36 Bolasso consists in generating m bootstrap samples from the original data set, on which the CLR Lasso is applied.38 Sublasso is similar to Bolasso but involves generating m subsamples of the original data set, on which the CLR Lasso is applied.39 In Bolasso and Sublasso approaches, a gene is retained if its selection frequency through m repetitions exceeds or equals a predetermined threshold (50%, 60%, 70%, or 80%). Higher is the threshold, lower is the selection of a false positive. The final gene selection is defined as the intersection of the sets of genes resulting from the Percentile Lasso with θ = 95% (recommended parameter) and the Bolasso and Sublasso using a cut-off of 70% (the most restrictive threshold). The selected genes’ effects were also estimated in a CLR adjusted on the year of cancer diagnosis (before 1970, 1970–79, 1980–89, 1990–2000), cumulative anthracycline doses (none, 0–250, 250–360, >360 mg/m²), and mean heart radiation (MHR) doses (none, 0–5, 5–15, >15 Gy). The gene expression biomarkers were analysed as continuous standardized variables since no deviation of the log-linearity hypothesis was observed through the Box–Tidwell test. The correlation and interaction between the selected genes and the two treatment variables were investigated. The added value of the selected genes was quantified through the likelihood ratio statistic, and the discriminative performance was evaluated using the bias-corrected area under the curve (AUC) with its bootstrapped 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated from a CLR40 (Appendix 4). A split-sample internal validation was performed to assess the discriminant performance of the Model 3. It involves randomly dividing a data set into two groups: a training sample and a validation sample. On the training sample, a CLR model is fitted; then from the estimated regression coefficients obtained, the linear predictors are calculated for each patient of the validation set; and finally, an AUC is estimated. These steps are repeated m times. The clogitL1 R package was used for the CLR with Lasso.35 For Percentile Lasso, the distribution of the penalization parameter was estimated from m = 1000 runs. For both Bolasso and Sublasso (from a subsample of 60% of the original data), the resampling number is set to m = 1000. For the split-sample validation, training set comprised 60% of the original set, and validation set 40%. A P-value of .05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R (http://www.r-project.org/).

In vitro analysis

To assess the role of the identified genes in cardiotoxicity, we measured the effect of doxorubicin, an anthracycline, on the expression of the identified genes in cardiomyocytes derived from adult rat ventricles using RT-qPCR. Seven samples of rat cardiomyocytes were exposed for 24 h first to physiological serum (controls), then to two concentrations of doxorubicin: 1 and 10 µM. Doxorubicin concentration and treatment time were chosen according to the literature41,42 and extended to match tumour cell line exposure requirements. EPAC1 expression was used as a positive control due to its known dose-dependent response to doxorubicin, insuring validation of the experiment, including the functionality of the reagents and equipment.43 Gene expression levels across the three anthracycline concentrations were described and compared using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test.

Results

Patient characteristics

The median age at cancer diagnosis was 6 years [interquartile range (IQR) 2–11 years] for both cases and control patients (Table 1). The main types of malignancy were lymphoma (35.8%), neuroblastoma (16.4%), bone sarcoma (13.9%), nephroblastoma (12.1%), and soft tissue sarcoma (10.9%). Heart failure was the most common CD (44.8%), followed by valvular heart diseases (24.8%), cardiac arrhythmia (19.4%), pericardial disease (6.7%), and ischaemic heart disease (4.2%). Cases received significantly higher cumulative anthracycline doses, with 30.9% receiving doses > 360 mg/m² compared with 13.3% of controls (P < .001). Additionally, cases were exposed to higher MHR doses, with 30.3% receiving doses > 15 Gy compared with 17.6% of controls (P = .041).

Gene selection associated with cardiac diseases (primary endpoint)

The gene selection by the three stabilization approaches highlighted the significant reduction in gene numbers when constraints were applied (see Supplementary data online, Table S2). The Percentile Lasso approach selected 45 genes with a 95th percentile constraint. The Bolasso and Sublasso approaches, respectively, identified five and six genes for a selection frequency greater than or equal to 50% (see Supplementary data online, Table S3). The intersection of the gene sets resulting from the three approaches (Figure 2) revealed five differentially expressed genes (NFE2L2, TGIF2-RAB5IF, HK2, HDGFL3, and LSP1) between cases and controls (Figure 2). The HDGFL3, NFE2L2, and TGIF2-RAB5IF genes were underexpressed in cases compared with controls, while the genes LSP1 and HK2 were overexpressed in cases compared with controls. When a cut-off of 70% was applied to Bolasso and Sublasso, the intersection was defined by the NFE2L2 gene alone (see Supplementary data online, Table S3 and Figure S3). Table 2 reports the effect of NFE2L2 estimated by a CLR on CD occurrence adjusted on the year of cancer diagnosis, cumulative anthracycline doses, and MHR doses and shows that an increase in NFE2L2 expression was significantly associated with a reduced CD risk (OR .16, 95% CI .09–.29). Correlations were observed between NFE2L2 gene expression and cumulative anthracycline doses (P = .008) and with MHR doses (P = .009) (see Supplementary data online, Table S4). Patients receiving high-anthracycline doses (>360 mg/m²) showed a lower NFE2L2 gene expression compared with patients not exposed to anthracyclines (P = .005). Additionally, patients exposed to radiation to the heart with doses higher than 15 Gy expressed the gene NFE2L2 less than patients who did not receive radiation to the heart (P = .070) and patients who received <5 Gy (P = .004) (see Supplementary data online, Table S4). No significant interactions were observed between NFE2L2 expression and anthracycline doses or MHR exposure. While the other four biomarkers were finally not selected, their potential interactions with treatment variables (anthracyclines and radiotherapy) were investigated, revealing no significant interactions (see Supplementary data online, Table S5). To quantify the added value of NFE2L2, we compared the likelihood (L) ratio statistic of three models: Model 1 included the year of cancer diagnosis, cumulative anthracycline doses, and MHR doses; Model 2 included the year of cancer diagnosis and NFE2L2; and Model 3 (final model) included the year of cancer diagnosis, cumulative anthracycline doses, MHR doses, and NFE2L2 (Table 3). Adding only NFE2L2 to the year of cancer diagnosis (Model 2, −2logL = 110.9, degrees of freedom= 4) produces a non-significant likelihood ratio test (P = .240) as compared with the final model (−2log L = 102.9, df = 10), contrary to the addition of treatment variables to year of diagnosis (Model 1, −2log L = 197.7, df = 9). This indicates a significant added value of this gene (P < .001). Moreover, the information added by NFE2L2 increased the discriminant ability from AUC = .66 (95% CI .62–.73, Model 1) to .85 (95% CI .80–.89, Model 3). These AUCs remain robust when a corrected estimation of AUC is considered to control for overfitting (Table 3). An internal validation evaluating the performance of Model 3 shows a good AUC on the validation data set (.85, 95% CI .76–.91) (see Supplementary data online, Figure S4). The sensitivity analysis restricted only to grade 2 or higher showed similar results (see Supplementary data online, Table S6).

Gene selection associated with heart failure (secondary endpoint)

From the original matched case-control study (n = 330), a subsample of 148 patients, including 74 cases diagnosed with HF as the first cardiac event and their matched controls, were selected (see Supplementary data online, Table S7). Using a selection procedure similar to the primary endpoint but with a reduced threshold for the Bolasso and Sublasso approaches (50%) due to the limited sample size, NFE2L2 remains the only gene associated with HF (see Supplementary data online, Tables S2 and S3 and Figure S5). NFE2L2 was underexpressed in cases compared with controls (see Supplementary data online, Figure S6) and significantly correlated with the cumulative anthracycline doses (P < .001), so that patients receiving high-anthracycline doses (>360 mg/m²) expressed the NFE2L2 gene less, compared with those not exposed to anthracyclines (P < .001) (see Supplementary data online, Table S5). As for CD, an increasing NFE2L2 expression is associated with a decreased HF risk (OR .11, 95% CI .03–.37) (Table 2). There was no interaction between NFE2L2 gene expression and treatment doses received (data not shown). Similar results to CD outcome for quantifying the added value of NFE2L2 and its discriminant ability are also observed (Table 3).

In vitro analysis

A 24 h treatment with clinically relevant anthracycline doses (10 µM) resulted in a significant decrease in NFE2L2 gene expression in rat cardiomyocytes. Treatment of rat cardiomyocytes with 1 µM of anthracyclines for 24 h did not significantly affect the NFE2L2 gene expression as compared with non-exposed cardiomyocytes (mean difference of −.09, 95% CI −.37–.19; P = .759). However, exposure to a concentration of 10 µM of anthracycline for 24 h decreased NFE2L2 gene expression by .65 units (95% CI .37–.93, P = .002) compared with the non-exposed cardiomyocytes and .74 units (95% CI .46–1.02, P = .003) compared with those exposed to 1 µM of anthracycline (see Supplementary data online, Figure S7 and Table S8). As expected, EPAC1 expression levels decreased after 24 h of doxorubicin exposure, validating our experimental approach (results presented in Supplementary data online, Figure S8 and Table S9).

Discussion

This study reports the independent protective effect of NFE2L2 gene expression associated with CD (adjusted OR .16, 95% CI .09–.29) and with HF (adjusted OR .11, 95% CI .03–.37) for a one-unit increase in NFE2L2 (Structured Graphical Abstract). Including NFE2L2 gene expression in a model comprising the doses of cumulative anthracycline and of the MHR significantly improved its discriminative ability, reaching an AUC of .85 (95% CI .80–.89) for both CD and HF, and suggesting a better consideration of the inter-individual variability in CD occurrence. For comparison, our model without NFE2L2 for modelling HF showed an AUC of .77 (95% CI .68–.84) slightly better than that of the model developed by Chow et al.14 in a previous CCS study (.74), which included demographic variables (sex, age at cancer diagnosis) and cancer treatment characteristics (anthracycline and chest radiotherapy doses).

Previous genetic studies on cardiotoxicity in CCS have shown inconsistent results with different genetic variants.19,20,25 Exploring gene expression, which is more responsive to treatment effects, is of major importance in the understanding of the mechanisms associated with cardiotoxicity.26 To date, only one study has investigated transcriptomic data, identifying 36 genes differentially expressed in anthracycline-exposed CCS implicated in different pathways including iron homeostasis signalling.27 However, no overlapping genes were identified with our study. Two main reasons can be given to explain this: (i) the use of different gene expression analysis technologies and (ii) the methodology used. For the latter, our finding results from the use of three approaches (Percentile Lasso, Bolasso, and Sublasso) derived from the CLR with Lasso35 for analysing data of a matched case-control study in the setting of high-dimensional data. The gene selection, resulting in the intersection of these three selection procedures, enables us to minimize the false positive rate. This strategy has identified five genes, with NFE2L2 emerging as a consistent biomarker across varying thresholds. NFE2L2 encodes for the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), an antioxidant-response protein, suggesting a potential mechanistic link to treatment-induced cardiotoxicity.44 The NRF2 is involved in several cellular functions, including iron homeostasis, one of the main pathways identified in the study of Singh et al.27 As a master regulator of antioxidant responses, NRF2 modulates over 240 genes throughout various biological functions44 and protects cardiac tissues against oxidative stress.44–46 Its role in iron homeostasis and its anti-inflammatory effect may protect against cardiac fibrosis preceding cardiomyopathy44 and the iron deficiency common in CD.44,47 Recent mechanistic studies support our findings on NFE2L2 as a protective factor in anthracycline cardiotoxicity.44,48–51 Deficient expression of NFE2L2 expression increased susceptibility to doxorubicin treatment, by raising its intracellular levels.49,51 And the Nrf2 activation attenuated doxorubicin cardiotoxicity in murine cells.48,50 Moreover, Nrf2 knockout mice treated with doxorubicin exhibited more pronounced necrotic areas and oxidative damage compared with wild-type controls.51 Our study highlighted a decrease in NFE2L2 gene expression in rat cardiomyocytes exposed to clinically relevant doses of anthracyclines, suggesting that its downregulation may be associated with cardiac tissue impairment. It should be noted that the transcriptome in our study was derived from PBMCs, which are clinically easy to obtain and a convenient source of transcriptomic biomarkers. While expression profiles and transcript abundance between PBMCs and myocardial tissue/cardiomyocytes differ, changes in inflammatory and ubiquitously expressed genes in inflammatory conditions often correlate.52 Accordingly, previous studies suggested that modifications of gene expression profiles in PBMCs can represent a signature for cardiac remodelling.53–55 In a model including cancer treatment doses adjusted for the year of cancer diagnosis, treatment variables were significantly associated with CD (see Supplementary data online, Table S10). However, when adding NFE2L2 expression, these variables were no longer significant (Table 2). This suggests a broader involvement of NFE2L2 in CD with an effect of anthracyclines on the occurrence of CD mediated by NFE2L2 expression. This demonstrates the dual nature of the transcriptome, which can be viewed as a reflection of a changing process or a link in a causal path.56 A longitudinal monitoring of NFE2L2 gene expression before and after childhood cancer treatment would allow a better understanding of the evolution of this NFE2L2 expression and its involvement in the onset of CD. Moreover, secondary analysis establishing causal relationship between NFE2L2 gene expression and the occurrence of CD in CCS would be an interesting line of research.

Despite the importance of our findings, some limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the FCCSS cohort introduces potential bias such as in anthracycline dose collection from medical records and in the estimation of heart radiation doses, which were derived from anthropomorphic phantom models and based on data from treatment planning charts. The absence of lifestyle and socio-economic factors, which are confounders influencing the onset of CD, introduces a bias into our model. Moreover, the patients of the case-control study nested within the FCCSS who survived long enough to be sampled are not representative of the entire FCCSS cohort. In fact, the genetic background of these patients who survived a long time and were able to be sampled is probably different from that of patients who died before samplings. The latter group likely carries a genetic susceptibility to more aggressive forms of cancer. To date, to our knowledge, no shared constitutional genetic predispositions have been demonstrated between cancer and CD. Therefore, there is no reason to suspect a potential link between childhood cancer survival and cardiac biomarkers as measured by gene expression. Our work focuses on CDs that occur long after childhood cancer treatment. Our results may be applied only to these long-term survivors, and the fact that their applicability is conditional to long-term survival is not considered as an issue. While cardiac outcomes were medically validated, reducing the risk of misclassification bias compared with self-reported outcomes, there is still a potential bias introduced due to intensified clinical surveillance of patients who received higher doses of anthracyclines and radiation. This closer monitoring may lead to the over-detection of subclinical events (grade ≤ 2) in these patients compared with others. Sensitivity analyses restricted to medically validated grade ≥ 2 events confirmed the robustness of our results. Additionally, the primary outcome comprises various CD with different pathophysiologies, requiring separate analyses for each CD, as done for HF. Such subgroup analyses were limited by the small number of patients in the different categories. In addition, matching was performed using gender, age at cancer diagnosis, first tumour type, and follow-up duration, but not the year of cancer diagnosis. We considered that matching on follow-up duration indirectly resulted in a matching on year of cancer diagnosis. In this study, the number of cases and controls is balanced in the following periods of cancer diagnosis: before 1979, 1980–89, and 1990–2000. Furthermore, the year of primary cancer diagnosis was included in the multivariable analyses and was not statistically significant. Our study being nested in the FCCSS cohort, the subsample used may not fully represent the entire cohort, thus potentially underestimating the AUC for a model including only clinical variables. While internal validation showed promising discriminant ability, establishing international collaborations and developing large-scale biobanks with integrated transcriptomic data in cohorts of CCS will be essential to perform external validation of our findings.

Our findings indicate that CCSs presenting a high expression of the NFE2L2 gene were less likely to have a CD. This result should be validated through a longitudinal monitoring of NFE2L2 gene expression before and after childhood cancer treatment, to provide cardioprotective treatments to patients upstream of the development of CD (as a prognostic biomarker) or during early diagnosis of cardiac dysfunction (as a diagnostic biomarker).

Biological samples collection

A biobank attached to the FCCSS cohort was constructed in order to study the biological impacts of the iatrogenic effects of cancer treatments. Between 2011 and 2017, biological samples were taken from adult patients during routine visits or in medical laboratories. Blood samples were available for 1571 patients from the FCCSS, on which we constructed our matched case-control study. For most CD cases (n = 160), blood sampling was done after the diagnosis of CD, and for the five other cases, blood sampling took place less than 1 year before the CD diagnosis. Finally, for all 165 cases, the median time between diagnosis of cardiac pathology and blood sampling was 12.5 years (IQR 6.1–16.6). Furthermore, the median age at blood sampling for cases [39.7 (IQR 34.4–45.8)] was close to that of controls [41.7 (IQR 34.6–46.7)].

Gene expression profiling

Affymetrix Human ClariomD Array was used to profile the transcriptome of cell lines established from PBMCs, covering 33 367 genes. Array analysis was conducted by GenoSplice (Paris, France). Array data were normalized using quantile normalization using APT command lines (Affymetrix). For quality control, ‘pos_vs_neg_auc’, ‘all_probeset_rle_mean’, ‘%P’ metrics, bacterial spikes, and polyA-control RNAs expression were checked. Background correction was made using the antigenomic probes, and probe selection was made as described previously.57–59 Only probes targeting exons annotated from FAST DB transcripts (v2016_1) were selected in order to focus on well-annotated genes whose mRNA sequences are in public databases.60 Among these selected probes, bad-quality probes (e.g. probes identified by Affymetrix as ‘cross-hybridizing’) and probes with too low an intensity of signal compared with antigenomic background probes with the same GC content were removed from the analysis. Only probes with a detection above background (DABG) P ≤ .05 in at least half of the arrays were considered for statistical analyses. Only genes expressed in at least one compared condition were analysed (i.e. case and control samples). The DABG P-value had to be ≤.05 for at least half of the gene probes to be considered as expressed. In total, 11 986 genes satisfied all these criteria. Additionally, a second filter was applied to ensure that only genes with an average expression of at least 7 (log2 transformed) in cases and controls were included to facilitate the in vitro analysis (RT-qPCR). Finally, 8557 genes were included in the analysis (see Supplementary data online, Figure S1).

Statistical considerations

The Lasso procedure

To identify potential biomarkers associated with the clinical endpoints in the high-dimensional data setting, the Lasso selection procedure is often used, allowing both selection of biomarkers and estimation of their effects.34 This process involves two main steps: first, the calculation of a tuning parameter, known as the regularization or penalization parameter, denoted λ, using k-fold cross-validation (high values of λ result in a small selection of biomarkers since the coefficients of the less important biomarkers are set to 0). The second step involves maximizing the penalized log-likelihood function.34 Several runs of the Lasso algorithm, including the same variables, give different penalization parameters since the k-fold cross-validation is different (k = 5 is used per default), leading to unstable biomarkers selection.

Conditional logistic regression with Lasso penalties

Related to the study design, i.e. the matched case-control study, the Lasso procedure is adapted to the likelihood of a CLR.35

Discriminatory performance

The model’s discriminative performance for a binary outcome is commonly estimated using the AUC metric. In the setting of a case-control design, different approaches are possible to estimate this quantity. They differ in their ways of estimating the linear predictor.

Let Y represent the binary outcome variable (1 = CD case: 0 = CD-free control); X=(X1,…,Xp) denotes the selected biomarkers, year of cancer diagnosis, and/or treatment variables; and Z=(Z1,…,Zq) denotes the matching variables. The matching variables are, here, age at primary cancer diagnosis, type of malignancy, and follow-up after diagnosis.

First approach

Unconditional logistic regression including the selected biomarker, the year of cancer diagnosis, and/or treatment variables (X) adjusted for matching variables (Z) is fitted. We then estimate the regression coefficients related to the intercept (α^), related to the covariates X (β^), and related to the matching variable Z (γ^). The AUC is then calculated using the estimated linear predictor α^+β^X+γ^Z.40,61,62

Second approach

A conditional logistic regression including the selected biomarkers and year of cancer diagnosis and/or treatment variables (X) is fitted. Only the coefficients β^ related to X are estimated (α^=γ^=0). The AUC is then estimated using the linear predictor β^X.61

A bootstrap sampling technique was used to estimate a CI of AUC. Specifically, 500 bootstrap samples were generated by sampling the matched sets with replacement. From the distribution of the AUCs estimated from the 500 bootstrap samples, the .025 and .975 percentiles were used as the 95% CI limits (95% CI).

Note that these two approaches produce an unweighted estimate of AUC, i.e. not considering a possible unrepresentativeness of patients from the case-control compared with the parent cohort. Some methods have been developed to consider a weighted AUC for the nested design framework, such as weighted logistic regression, weighted conditional logistic regression, the two-stage procedure, and the simultaneous procedure.61

For the analysis, the second approach was used to estimate the unweighted AUC of different models in a matched case-control design framework. These models differ in terms of the covariates X: (i) year of cancer diagnosis and treatment variables: categorized cumulative dose of anthracyclines (none, 0–250, 250–360, >360 mg/m²) and categorized MHR doses (none, 0–5, 5–15, >15 Gy); (ii) year of cancer diagnosis and the selected biomarkers alone; and (iii) year of cancer diagnosis, both treatment variables and selected biomarkers. This last model corresponds to the final model. Similar results were produced with the first approach (data not shown).

Bias-corrected area under the curve calculation

To estimate a bias-corrected AUC for both unconditional and conditional logistic regressions described above, a bootstrapping technique was used to estimate the bias due to overfitting termed optimism. When the AUC optimism is estimated, it can be subtracted from the AUC derived from the original sample to obtain a bias-corrected AUC.

From the original data of size n, a bootstrapped sample of size n is constructed. The model is then fitted on the bootstrapped sample and applied without changing to the original sample. The AUC from the bootstrap sample (AUCBootstrap) minus the one computed on the original sample (AUCApparent) is an estimate of optimism. optimism=AUCBootstrap−AUCApparent.

This process is repeated for B = 500 bootstrap replications to obtain an average optimism, which is subtracted from the final model estimated AUC to obtain the bias-corrected AUC.63

The 95% CI of this bias-corrected AUC is obtained by applying the .025 and .975 percentiles to the distribution of the corrected AUCs.

For the analysis, conditional logistic regression was used to estimate the unweighted bias-corrected AUC.

In vitro analysis

The expression of the identified gene (NFE2L2) was measured after the exposition of rat cardiomyocytes to doxorubicin in order to assess the effect of anthracyclines on NFE2L2 expression.

Doxorubicin was obtained from ACCORD (central pharmacy of Institut Gustave Roussy, Villejuif, France) in a physiological serum solution format (2 mg/mL). Male Wistar rats (250–300 g) obtained from Janvier Labs (Saint Berthevin, France) were terminally anaesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (.1 mg/g) and hearts were excised rapidly. Individual ARVMs were obtained by retrograde perfusion of the heart as previously described.64 For enzymatic dissociation, the hearts were perfused at a constant flow of 6 mL min−1 at 37°C for 5 min with a Ca2+-free Ringer solution containing (in mm): NaCl 117, KCl 5.7, NaHCO3 4.4, KH2PO4 1.5, MgCl2 1.7, d-glucose 11.7, Na2-phosphocreatine 10, taurine 20, Hepes 21, pH 7.1. This was followed by a perfusion at 4 mL min−1 for 40 min with the same solution containing 1 mg ml−1 of collagenase A (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) plus 300 µm EGTA and CaCl2 to adjust free Ca2+ concentration to 20 µm. The ventricles and atria were then separated, finely chopped and gently agitated to dissociate individual cells from each tissue. The resulting cell suspension was filtered on gauze and the cells were allowed to settle down. The supernatant was discarded, and cells resuspended four more times with Ringer solution at increasing [Ca2+] from 20 to 300 µm. Freshly isolated cells were suspended in minimal essential medium (MEM: M 4780; Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) containing 1.2 mm [Ca2+] supplemented with 2.5% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cergy-Pontoise, France), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, 20 mm Hepes (pH 7.6). They were plated on 35 mm, laminin-coated (10 mg ml−1) culture dishes at a density of 104 cells per dish and kept at 37°C (.5% CO2). After 1 h, the medium was replaced by 300 µL of FBS-free MEM. Cells were kept 24 h before ongoing 24 h treatment.

Seven samples of rat cardiomyocytes were exposed for 24 h first to physiological serum (controls), then to two concentrations of doxorubicin: 1 and 10 µM. Doxorubicin concentration and treatment time were chosen according to the literature41,42 and extended to match tumour cell lines exposure requirements. Using 1 µM of doxorubicin allowed us to study physiological and clinical cardiotoxic effects of doxorubicin,41 while a concentration of 10 µM is used to simulate the high levels of doxorubicin exposure.42 Exposing cardiomyocytes to 10 µM of doxorubicin induces mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and apoptosis in cardiomyocytes, which are similar effects to the mechanisms observed in patients receiving high cumulative doses of anthracyclines (250–300 mg/m²) that can accumulate in cardiac tissue during chemotherapy.42,65 EPAC1 expression was used as a positive control due to its known dose-dependent response to doxorubicin, ensuring validation of the experiment, including the functionality of the reagents and equipment.43
