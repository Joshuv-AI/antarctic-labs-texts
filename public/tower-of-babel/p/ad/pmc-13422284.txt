Acceptability and feasibility of a Patient Support Coordinator (PSC) model to facilitate serious illness communication in outpatient oncology
Authors: Alston Ng, Su-Min Yeo, Wu Meng Tan, Bernard Chua, Rieka Erina, Shirlyn Hui Shan Neo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConventional serious illness conversations (SICs) are frequently impeded by clinical time constraints and a lack of psychological readiness among patients. To address these barriers, this study evaluates the acceptability and feasibility of a novel Patient Support Coordinator (PSC)-delivered intervention that nests values and goals exploration within a non-directive supportive listening intervention facilitated by a non-clinician. Conceptualized as a preparatory adjunct to clinician-delivered SICs, this approach facilitates the open-ended exploration of patient-defined interests and concerns at a pace that respects individual preferences and coping strategies.MethodsThis single-arm, non-randomized pilot study used a convergent parallel mixed-methods design in outpatient oncology. Acceptability was assessed through Feeling Heard and Understood (FHU) and Client Satisfaction (CSQ) surveys with a predefined threshold ≥ 12/16. Qualitative interviews with patients (n = 21) and oncologists (n = 2) provided data on intervention acceptability and implementation feasibility.ResultsEnrollment (90.3%) and retention (85.7%) rates surpassed feasibility targets. High acceptability was demonstrated by median scores of 13/16 for the FHU (n = 19) and 15/16 for the CSQ (n = 20). While most participants met the FHU threshold, four scored below 12 due to gendered discomfort, mismatched expectations, and perceptions that they were coping well and had no need of supportive listening. Qualitatively, the PSC was described as having provided a safe emotional outlet and humanized care by addressing psychosocial needs that participants felt their treating oncologists had no room to manage. The coordinator’s status as a non-clinician and capacity to demonstrate sensitivity and care were found to facilitate rapport-building, thereby enabling meaning-making and biographical reflection. Clinicians reported minimal workflow disruption and valued the PSC’s ability to surface patient concerns and treatment misconceptions that often remain unvoiced in routine clinic consultations.ConclusionsNesting values-based exploration within supportive listening is a feasible and acceptable framework for oncology care. By providing a non-clinical space for reflection, the PSC model may address common barriers to SIC initiation, enhance the patient care experience, and thus serve as an adjunct to support clinicians in SIC initiation. Background

Individuals with life-limiting illnesses like cancer often experience significant anxieties regarding prognostic uncertainty and complex care decisions [1, 2]. Evidence suggests that values-informed discussions called serious illness conversations (SICs) can alleviate these anxieties; early initiation of these conversations has been demonstrated to promote a sense of peace and hope while strengthening patient-clinician rapport [3–5]. Timely initiation of SICs is also associated with a reduced incidence of burdensome end-of-life care, including fewer hospitalizations and lower rates of chemotherapy in the final weeks of life [6, 7].

Despite these benefits, longitudinal engagement in SICs is frequently hindered by a mix of clinician-level barriers—such as time constraints [1, 8, 9], limited rapport [10, 11], and concerns about causing distress in patients [1, 4, 12]—and patient-related factors, including emotional unreadiness for goals-of-care discussions, preferences for present-focused coping, and the perceived untimeliness of such conversations during periods of clinical stability [13–16]. Taken together, these barriers contribute to an implementation landscape where, even when augmented by behavioral nudges in a study context, SIC engagement for high-risk patients remained below 15% [17]. Consequently, in routine practice, these conversations often happen too late, focus narrowly on resuscitation preferences, and are typically limited to a single discussion at the end of life [6, 18].

To address clinician-level constraints impeding SIC initiation and recognizing the potential distress that SICs may evoke in patients, we developed an intervention which situates the exploration of goals and values within the broader remit of supportive listening. This strategy is informed by the principles of person-centered psychotherapy, which posits that an emotionally supportive environment—characterized by genuineness, acceptance, and empathic understanding—is the primary catalyst of therapeutic change [19]. While often employed as an active control in clinical trials, meta-analytic data suggest that non-directive supportive therapies frequently demonstrate comparable efficacy to structured, skill-based modalities in improving global quality of life and alleviating existential distress [20–22]. This efficacy may be attributed to “common factors” such as the cultivation of therapeutic alliance, respect for patient autonomy, and the facilitation of emotional expression [23].

In contrast to conventional SIC practice where the patients’ goals and priorities are elicited by their primary physicians and with reference to prompts and questions found in the Serious Illness Conversation Guide (SICG), this model of care thus aims to foster unstructured, open-ended conversations that allow patients to explore their immediate interests and concerns at a pace that respects their needs, preferences, and coping strategies. These conversations are facilitated by a Patient Support Coordinator (PSC), a non-clinician with whom patients have no prior relationship and whose role is framed in terms of companionship, resource coordination, and non-judgmental listening. Given its novelty as a potential adjunct to clinician-led serious illness communication, it is unclear if values-based exploration within supportive listening is acceptable to patients and operationally feasible in the context of outpatient oncology.

Study design

This is a single-arm, non-randomized pilot study that uses a convergent parallel mixed-methods design.

Trial registration

The study was retrospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (Trial Registration Number: NCT07428655).

Study setting

This study was conducted in the outpatient care setting at a tertiary cancer center in Singapore with two participating breast oncologists. These oncologists were purposefully approached for participation given their clinical experience and prior training in serious illness communication.

Inclusion criteria

Patients were eligible for recruitment if they are 21 years of age or older, diagnosed with advanced cancer (Stage III or IV) or assessed to be at risk of recurrence by their oncologist, aware of their cancer diagnosis, and able to communicate in English or Mandarin. Individuals incapable of providing informed consent due to cognitive impairment or poorly controlled mental health conditions were excluded.

Recruitment

We aimed to recruit at least 25 individuals via convenience sampling to generate preliminary insights into the utility and feasibility of the PSC model in cancer care. The target sample size was determined with reference to existing research on patient experiences in serious illness communication [4, 13, 24, 25], rather than statistical power for outcome detection.

Following oncologist-confirmed chart reviews by the first author (AN), potential participants were introduced to the PSC during routine consultations. Study procedures were then explained to eligible patients in a separate room. Patients were informed that clinically relevant information would be shared with their oncologists, and that an independent researcher would conduct post-intervention interviews and surveys to understand their experience with the program.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board (IRB 2025 − 1184). All participants provided their written informed consent prior to study participation.

Intervention

The PSC model is a short-term, non-clinician-led intervention where the exploration of SIC topics is embedded flexibly within supportive listening sessions. Session frequency and modality, whether in person or via telephone, are tailored to participant preferences. Following a three-month period of proactive, PSC-initiated check-ins, participants transition into an ad-hoc phase where they may request additional support as needed, as shown in Fig. 1.

Intervention overview

Each session begins with a brief distress screening, which provides a point-of-entry for participants to raise issues of concern for supportive listening or discussion (see Appendix S1 for intervention handbook). The model was conceptualized and iteratively refined by the first author (AN) under the clinical supervision of a senior palliative care physician (SHSN). To prepare for this role, AN received structured training in supportive listening and motivational interviewing with professional therapists, and was instructed in distress screening, symptom management, and care escalation protocols by an experienced palliative care nurse (RE).

Communication between the PSC and the medical team was facilitated through systematic documentation in electronic medical records. At the conclusion of the intervention for each recruited participant, the PSC alerted the treating oncologist to the final consolidated note, which may serve as a primary reference for subsequent oncologist-initiated goals-of-care discussions with their patients. Ad hoc patient requests, clinical concerns, and questions were relayed to the treating oncologists as appropriate to ensure continuous oversight. Basic symptom management queries were addressed by the PSC during the session or resolved post-session following consultation with a palliative care nurse (RE) or the supervising palliative care physician (SHSN).

Data collection and analysis

To assess acceptability, participants completed the four-item Feeling Heard and Understood (FHU) survey (scaled 0–4) and the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ) (scaled 1–4). The threshold of ≥ 12/16 was established a priori by the study team as a logical benchmark for acceptability in the absence of accepted cut-offs for advanced breast cancer populations. This threshold was selected to ensure that the intervention met a high standard of perceived utility: a summed score of 12 corresponds to an item-average of 3, reflecting strong agreement (“Very true”) on the FHU and positive experience (“Mostly satisfied”) on the CSQ. Acceptability was evaluated with reference to the proportion of participants meeting these cut-offs, an approach which facilitates outlier analysis and a systematic identification of barriers to intervention acceptability.

Semi-structured interviews further explored intervention utility, novelty, and views on intervention parameters. Feasibility was assessed using enrolment (target: ≥60%) and retention (target: ≥70%) rates, with targets defined with reference to figures from other early-phase psychosocial interventions for women with breast cancer [26, 27]. Feedback on workflow integration and the utility of PSC-generated insights was also collected from participating clinicians. To reduce response bias, the second author (SMY), who was not involved in the intervention delivery, conducted all interviews.

Data were analyzed using the framework method [28]. AN and SMY independently coded transcripts to develop and iteratively refine an analytical framework, resolving discrepancies through discussion. A coding matrix was used to facilitate systematic comparison of experiences across participant categories. To address potential interpretive bias arising from the first author’s dual role as interventionist/ PSC and analyst, the research team engaged in regular study discussions. Interpretations were further validated by the third author (SHSN) to ensure they remained robustly grounded in the data.

Quantitative feasibility indicators (enrolment and retention rates) were summarized descriptively. Qualitative and quantitative findings were integrated though a convergent parallel design: data from both sources were triangulated to identify areas of complementarity or divergence in participant experiences.

Attrition arose from clinical deterioration (n = 2) and fatigue-related refusal to participate in post-intervention study activities (n = 2). One of the four participants who withdrew from the intervention consented to an interview to share her reasons for doing so. Characteristics of patient participants (n = 21)

The study sample was demographically diverse, encompassing a broad age range (mean age = 64 years, SD = 11) and representation across ethnic, marital, and employment backgrounds. Most of the participants were living with stage IV disease and receiving ongoing maintenance therapy with palliative intent through varying modalities, while two had stage III disease and were in remission at recruitment.

A total of 63 supportive listening sessions were delivered, comprising 35 telephonic and 28 in-person interactions. Participants engaged in a median of 3 sessions (range: 1–5), with all but one completing at least 2 sessions. These interactions lasted a median of 50 min (range: 15–110 min), and the majority (74.6%; n = 47/63) exceeded 45 min in duration.

Complete survey data were obtained for the FHU (n = 19) and CSQ (n = 20). One participant did not complete the FHU, while another was not surveyed given her withdrawal from the intervention. Perceptions of intervention utility: qualitative findings from patient perspectives

Most participants (n = 18) appraised the PSC intervention as highly beneficial and highlighted the utility of a dedicated space for the reflective processing of their cognitive and emotional responses to advanced cancer. The qualitative analysis identified three primary dimensions of value that informed participant perceptions of intervention utility.

Psychological safety and emotional outlet

For many, the intervention provided a safe and dedicated space for them to express their vulnerability and to experience a validation of uncomfortable feelings, something several participants felt was unavailable in their home environment:

“With family, you don’t say much. I talk to (PSC), at least I can tell him many things… Sometimes, when I feel very down or when I think too much, I can talk to him. With family members, it’s harder to communicate. They don’t understand… They tell me, ‘It is like that, stop worrying.’” (Pt01, 70s) [Translation SMY’s].

“With family, you don’t say much. I talk to (PSC), at least I can tell him many things… Sometimes, when I feel very down or when I think too much, I can talk to him. With family members, it’s harder to communicate. They don’t understand… They tell me, ‘It is like that, stop worrying.’” (Pt01, 70s) [Translation SMY’s].

Even among participants who felt well-supported by their loved ones, the opportunity to air grievances provided relief from the perceived burden of “heaping on negativity on (loved ones),” (Pt21) offering an opportunity for participants to vent their doubts without social consequence.

Humanizing the care experience

The intervention appeared to bridge an affective gap in standard oncology care, which participants consistently characterized as focused exclusively on physical pathology. By attending to the non-medical aspects of the self, the PSC model humanized the care experience and addressed psychosocial needs for which the clinic setting appeared to have “no room”:

“Not just the physical aspect is being healed here, it is integrated—the emotional, the mental… Deep inside you, you have the hurt, the scar, the pain – that part (that) is emotional that was not healed… (but) No doctor will talk to you about this… I don’t know if it’s about training, but they have no room for emotions.” (Pt16, 60s).

“I feel that somebody cares for me, willing to listen to me and my nonsense… I thought to extend my time with him also. Ya, worth my time, even though talking to him, I’m really late [for consultation with oncologist] already.” (Pt08, 60s).

The sense “that there’s someone who cares for them” (Pt03) was reinforced by the PSC’s continued availability as a ready source of support, mitigating the perceived alienation in being patients who “just come for treatment” (Pt03). This sentiment was echoed by Pt14, who contrasted the impersonal accessibility of the institutional hotline with the “comfort” and “connection” of having a “direct” and “personal” relationship with the PSC.

Meaning-making and biographical reflection

At times likened to “psychotherapy” or “counseling,” the intervention served to facilitate meaning-making and self-discovery for a subset of participants (n = 7). Pt04, for instance, felt comforted in realizing that “It’s not wrong to feel (the) way” she does, while Pt16 experienced newfound appreciation for herself as she gained clarity on her identity:

“Talking to him is like a mirror, and I realized I can appreciate myself… I dig up who I was when I was a child, and I realized, ‘Yeah, I was a giver, I have been a giver.’ I didn’t realize that until our discussion… It’s something untapped, you know? It’s been there all along.” (Pt16).

“Talking to him is like a mirror, and I realized I can appreciate myself… I dig up who I was when I was a child, and I realized, ‘Yeah, I was a giver, I have been a giver.’ I didn’t realize that until our discussion… It’s something untapped, you know? It’s been there all along.” (Pt16).

Further, reflecting on the illness journey allowed some participants like Pt14 to acknowledge “the big picture,” transforming the injuriousness of their illness experiences into an affirmation of their inner strength:

“It does bring back memories that maybe you don’t want to remember too much. But I think it’s also good because it allows me to reflect… and think, ‘Wow, actually, how did I go through that?’… In fact, (reflecting) sort of made me stronger.’” (Pt14, 50s).

The various benefits raised by participants position supportive listening as a dignity-conserving practice that enhances the relational tenor of cancer care [29]. While participants did not identify goals and values exploration as a specific intervention task or component, the non-directive approach appeared to support spontaneous reflections among participants on their clinical and personal priorities. The feasibility of nesting goals and values exploration within supportive listening was demonstrated by the fact that for all included participants, the PSC successfully documented substantive insights regarding their present priorities, personal values, sources of strength, social support systems, worries, and conceptualizations of quality of life. For a subset of participants who voluntarily shared reflections on future care needs (n = 5), clinical records detailed their preferences regarding prognostic disclosure and end-of-life care.

Facilitators of intervention engagement

Beyond the perceived value of the intervention, our analysis identified factors that facilitated sustained engagement. These drivers of engagement resonated with the humanistic underpinnings of the PSC model and indicated the importance of therapeutic alliance and relational safety in goals and values exploration. Specifically, sustained participation was predicated on two distinct but interrelated factors: the perceived affective quality of genuine care and the unique structural advantage of the coordinator’s dual-outsider status.

Affective quality of genuine care

Central to the participants’ engagement was the felt quality of being cared for, which participants identified as the intervention’s most enduring impact. This relational resonance often transcended specific conversational content, persisting even after details of what was discussed were forgotten. As exemplified by Pt23’s quote, the sense that the PSC embodied a genuine interest in and care for her helped the dialogue feel relaxed and the exploration of values and goals natural:

“I felt he sincerely wanted to help, and so we could chat very naturally… The first time I talked to him, it lasted almost an hour. There was nothing we didn’t talk about.” (Pt23, 90s) [Translation SMY’s].

“I felt he sincerely wanted to help, and so we could chat very naturally… The first time I talked to him, it lasted almost an hour. There was nothing we didn’t talk about.” (Pt23, 90s) [Translation SMY’s].

This felt quality of care appeared to be anchored in what they perceived to be the PSC’s responsive, participant-led approach, which balanced engagement with emotionally heavy topics with providing protective distance from emotionally distressing topics:

“I don’t see in his face that he became bored because it’s heavy… The environment he gave, the setting we have is: Be yourself. It’s not like: We have to achieve this, we have to answer this question.” (Pt16, 50s).

“I don’t see in his face that he became bored because it’s heavy… The environment he gave, the setting we have is: Be yourself. It’s not like: We have to achieve this, we have to answer this question.” (Pt16, 50s).

“When you talk to him, he’s not just following a script… (And it’s) good that he did not (talk about) disease spread. That spread, I don’t dare to think about it… I don’t dare to think about the future.” (Pt10, 70s) [Translation SMY’s].

“When you talk to him, he’s not just following a script… (And it’s) good that he did not (talk about) disease spread. That spread, I don’t dare to think about it… I don’t dare to think about the future.” (Pt10, 70s) [Translation SMY’s].

While a participant-led approach did not consistently yield information on participants’ plans for end-of-life care, its emphasis on relational authenticity appeared to provide a supportive context for the emergence of in-depth insights into participants’ life-worlds. For instance, Pt10 initially presented as being unperturbed by her cancer, attributing her distress solely to the practical burdens of her tuberculosis treatment. By the third session, however, she disclosed her ongoing struggles with accepting the incurability of her cancer, and articulated a reliance on social distraction and present-focused optimism as emotional coping strategies. While the non-directive approach precluded a focused exploration of future goals, the reflections that arose organically in the course of the intervention often offered clinically relevant information about the participants’ communication needs, emotional readiness for prognostic disclosure, coping strategies, (mis)understanding of treatment goals, and sources of distress.

‘Dual-outsider’ status

Complementing the interpersonal dimensions of relational authenticity, the PSC occupied a dual-outsider position that functioned as a structural facilitator of relational safety. While the previous section illustrates how a non-directive, participant-focused approach can surface clinically relevant insights, the success of such disclosures was often predicated on the PSC’s status as a non-clinician uninhibited by medical strictures. This professional distance allowed participants to feel comfortable disclosing their “unorthodox (health-improving) methods” without fear of clinical disapprobation. Crucially, because the PSC lacked the authority to interpret medical results, the intervention was decoupled from the clinical context and thus alleviated prognostic anxieties. The affective contrast between clinical and non-clinical contexts was most visible in the participants’ initial apprehensions about the intervention, with several participants expressing their concern that the oncologist’s introduction of the intervention hinted at imminent deterioration. These anxieties, however, were allayed when the coordinator underscored his lack of medical training.

The PSC’s simultaneous distance from the participants’ social worlds also facilitated self-expression and engagement. Most participants practiced protective buffering, attempting to shield their loved ones from their “pent-up angst” and fears. As a “professional stranger” (Pt14) with confidentiality obligations, the PSC provided a low-stakes environment for such disclosures. This was particularly valued among participants where existing social ties appeared inadequate or inappropriate in addressing illness-related distress or carried risks of gossip:

“Sometimes you need to speak, but not (to a) friend… No doubt (there can be) nice friends, but they don’t know (cancer). Some of them like to spread [news/ information]… I don’t like to talk to them.” (Pt18).

“Sometimes you need to speak, but not (to a) friend… No doubt (there can be) nice friends, but they don’t know (cancer). Some of them like to spread [news/ information]… I don’t like to talk to them.” (Pt18).

The professional distance afforded by the PSC’s ‘outsider’ role thus offered a safe platform for emotional “release” (Pt18) and meaning-making without compromising the social connections participants sought to preserve. For Pt18, the relational safety associated with the PSC’s outsider status appeared to facilitate the disclosure of nocturnal panic attacks, which were triggered by intermittent back pain that reminded her of her cancer and was interpreted as signaling disease progression. As she wished to shield her family from anguish and distress, Pt18 withheld these concerns from her children. The functional utility of relational safety was thus evidenced by the identification of these unspoken needs and the subsequent referral to an advanced practice nurse for symptom management.

Further, the intervention uncovered a previously unacknowledged dimension of anxiety that informed Pt18’s persistent inquiries regarding her prognosis. While she rationalized her information-seeking as a prerequisite for pragmatic planning, the participant eventually disclosed her profound fear of dying in pain—a concern she had likewise kept from people close to her. As an ‘outsider’ confidant, the PSC thus gained privileged insights into the psychological drivers underlying her desire for prognostic certainty and documented the multiple sources of distress that Pt18 faced. These findings suggest that relational safety not only sustained participant engagement but was also helpful in eliciting salient contextual information that may assist clinicians in calibrating their communication approaches and identifying targeted areas for psychosocial support.

Challenges and difficulties raised in patient interviews

While the intervention was largely well-received, some participants reported encountering certain challenges in their interviews.

Gendered positionality

For Pt01 and Pt21, the PSC’s gender identity functioned as a barrier to self-disclosure regarding sensitive physical concerns and body image issues. Pt01, for instance, withheld concerns about her mastectomy, explaining to the female interviewer:

“Because you’re a woman, I can talk to you about it. As for (PSC), I didn’t talk to him about it, because he’s a man. How could I? He doesn’t understand how a woman feels. He doesn’t even know those two things [breasts] are for.” (Pt01) [SMY’s translation].

“Because you’re a woman, I can talk to you about it. As for (PSC), I didn’t talk to him about it, because he’s a man. How could I? He doesn’t understand how a woman feels. He doesn’t even know those two things [breasts] are for.” (Pt01) [SMY’s translation].

Mismatched expectations of expertise

A mismatch between participant needs and the coordinator’s generalist support was reported by two participants. Pt27, who characterized the supportive listening as “basic,” expressed a preference for a specialized provider to assist her in managing her fear of cancer recurrence. This perceived inadequacy was echoed by Pt23, whose desire for treatment-related recommendations to achieve remission was left unfulfilled due to the PSC’s lack of clinical training.

Perceived irrelevance/ misunderstanding of intervention intent

For three participants (Pt13, Pt15, and Pt25), the perceived irrelevance of the intervention stemmed from a misunderstanding of its primary intent. Pt15, for instance, described the PSC’s role as being “to console” patients; Pt13 distanced herself from “those people who need [psychiatric] medication” for serious mental illnesses, whom she considered the primary target audience for supportive listening. Positioning themselves as emotionally resilient and unaffected by their clinical condition, these participants rejected the premise that they needed emotional comfort or validation, and concluded that the intervention was of limited relevance to them.

Modality-specific aversion

Finally, Pt26’s rationale for withdrawal highlights the impact of modality-specific triggers. Despite her initial agreement to telephone-based supportive listening sessions, she eventually withdrew because the medium evoked aversive associations with “very serious” family emergencies. This suggests that associations evoked by modality can override the presumed advantage of convenience, necessitating a more nuanced assessment of participant preferences at study enrolment.

Intervention feasibility: quantitative findings

Benchmarked against other early-phase psychosocial interventions in breast cancer populations [26, 27], this study achieved a high degree of implementation feasibility in outpatient oncology. Of the eligible participants approached for participation, 90.3% (n = 28/31) consented to enrolment, surpassing the ≥ 60% target and indicating robust intervention interest. Further, the model demonstrated strong longitudinal engagement, with a retention rate of 85.7% (n = 24/28) among enrolled participants, likewise exceeding the ≥ 70% target. These figures suggest that the intervention was minimally burdensome to and was sufficiently valued by enrolled participants.

Intervention feasibility: qualitative findings from clinician feedback

Both participating oncologists endorsed the intervention, citing minimal workflow disruption and identifying several perceived benefits. That the clinicians were asked to introduce the PSC intervention to patients was not experienced by either as an inconvenience, but, according to one of the oncologists, conferred strategic oversight, allowing him to align the timing of introduction with specific clinical objectives for each patient.

Beyond perceived time savings that allowed clinicians to focus more fully on treatment-related discussions, the intervention was also viewed as useful in surfacing concerns that may not emerge within routine clinical encounters. One oncologist described an instance in which a significant misunderstanding regarding treatment intentions was revealed through the PSC’s engagement with the patient and her caregiver:

“(The PSC) was able to break the barrier to have the conversation with them… to understand why they have such misconceptions… The patient’s husband did not want to raise his concerns to me directly for fear of me altering the treatment to be suboptimal for the patient… Because of this fear, which was unfounded, I would never have known.” (St01).

“There’s a different signature coming from the treating oncologist than from (the PSC), because if the treating oncologist asks it, there will be some patients who think, ‘If you’re asking me what I would want if things are not going well, does it mean that things are not going well now?’” (St02).

With respect to documentation, both clinicians regarded the PSC’s summaries of patients’ goals and preferences as adequate, relevant, and useful for care planning. The only suggestion raised was to streamline operational processes by granting the PSC direct access to the structured advance care planning (ACP) note in the electronic documentation system, with the proviso that these entries are reviewed and endorsed by the treating oncologist beforehand.

Discussion

Findings from this pilot study suggest that the PSC intervention is highly acceptable and feasible in an outpatient oncology setting, as evinced in high enrolment (90.3%) and retention (85.7%) rates and positive reviews from most participants. Quantitative ratings for FHU (median: 13/16) and CSQ (median: 15/16) were corroborated by participants’ qualitative feedback, with most finding the intervention beneficial in providing emotional comfort, humanizing the care experience, or fostering meaning-making. While FHU ratings fell below the acceptability threshold (≥ 12) for four participants, these scores were primarily associated with specific circumstantial constraints and conceptual misalignments rather than a systemic failure of the intervention model. Taken together, the results lend support to the acceptability of the intervention. Clinician endorsement further underscored the model’s operational feasibility, highlighting its limited impact on workflow and its perceived utility in offering insights that might otherwise remain unavailable.

Our observation that participants did not explicitly identify the articulation of goals and values as a distinct intervention component or study task, despite the documentation of substantive SIC-relevant insights, offers preliminary support for the feasibility of nesting values-based exploration within a non-directive framework of supportive listening. In allowing participants to integrate and reflect on everyday and existential concerns at their own pace, the intervention appears to have supported participants in discovering their own goals and priorities as an organic, processual outgrowth of a supportive relationship. Considered alongside the participants’ characterization of the intervention as a source of psychosocial support, the lack of conscious apprehension of goal-oriented discussion suggests that the PSC model may mitigate the psychological resistance and distress arousal noted in studies of directive SICs [13–16, 30].

The potential value of the model is underscored by the prevalence of psychological and existential distress associated with advanced cancer, with some estimates suggesting about half of all individuals with cancer experience severe or prolonged cancer-related distress [31–33]. Meta-analytic data suggest that post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) affects around 10% of individuals diagnosed with breast cancer, with some studies demonstrating a prevalence as high as 32.3% [34, 35]. While engagement in SIC has been shown to improve anxiety and depression symptoms in patients [36], patient avoidance of and disengagement from distressing topics about end-of-life care may render conventional SICs psychologically inaccessible for some patients and give rise to misunderstandings and mischaracterizations of patient priorities in clinical documentation [16]. The PSC model may thus complement conventional SICs by offering a less intimidating entry point for those with subclinical distress or who perceive clinician-initiated SICs as untimely, familiarizing patients with values-based exploration through longitudinal engagement and relational support ahead of clinician initiation of SICs.

This intervention represents a departure from existing healthcare communication initiatives, which typically involve clinicians engaging their patients in SIC during clinic consultations. Alternative approaches to advance care planning (ACP) see task-shifting or task-sharing, with non-clinical staff providing education on goals of care, encouraging engagement in discussions with physicians [37, 38], or directly completing scripted ACP conversations with patients [39, 40]. In contrast to these approaches, this intervention focuses on fostering psychological safety and offering emotional support, with the clarification of goals and values arising spontaneously through supportive listening and secondary to the patients’ interests, comfort, and preferences. To our knowledge, this study offers a proof-of-concept for a novel adjunct to conventional serious illness communication, with a trained non-clinician serving as the primary facilitator of biographical reflection and values clarification through supportive listening and without the use of structured communication tools or scripted questions.

Given its non-directive delivery by a non-clinician, the absence of prognostic disclosure—traditionally regarded as a foundational element of serious illness communication [41, 42]—demands a conceptual clarification of the model’s role. As opposed to a substitute for clinician-led SICs, we have positioned this intervention as a preparatory adjunct designed to establish a biographical foundation for future decision-making. We propose that the prioritization of competing values—a hallmark of formal, high-quality SICs [42]—is predicated on a patient’s clear understanding of their own stable sources of meaning. By centering on personhood rather than subordinating values exploration to the primacy of prognostic alignment, the PSC model is envisioned to prepare patients for future discussions with their oncologists, drawing on new perspectives and insights to navigate complex trade-offs with their clinicians as their health status evolves and prognostic clarity emerges. At the same time, in yielding insights into its participants’ sources of meaning, strength, and distress, the intervention may also support clinicians in developing a deeper appreciation of their patients’ emotional readiness, psychological needs, and existential concerns. This may in turn mitigate clinical inertia in SIC initiation associated with prognostic uncertainty and worries about causing distress in their patients [30, 43].

Several limitations warrant consideration. The small sample size and single-arm, non-randomized design restrict generalizability to broader oncology populations. Clinician perspectives on implementation feasibility were derived from a small subset of two oncologists whose insights may be influenced by selection bias, given their prior exposure to serious illness communication and high baseline receptivity to the intervention. Oncologists with lower initial buy-in or differing clinical priorities may hold more critical views on the utility of the PSC model, but the finding that the intervention posed minimal disruption to clinical workflows may still translate to the broader clinical practice setting. Additionally, the first author’s dual role as PSC and analyst introduced potential interpretive bias, but this was partially mitigated by using an independent interviewer and collaborative coding. Further, the replicability of the PSC model may be contingent upon the intensity of training and clinical supervision provided, which may not be universally accessible outside of a research context. Finally, this trial was registered retrospectively. Despite efforts to adhere to the original IRB-approved protocol, we excluded results on psychological distress assessed using the Depression, Anxiety, Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) due to low completion rates (n = 6). Feedback from non-respondents revealed an objection to the perceived implication of psychological distress, their reluctance suggesting a potential lack of face validity for the instrument among women with advanced breast cancer in Singapore’s cultural context. No other protocol deviations were noted; all primary outcomes and study procedures remained consistent with the original IRB-approved design.

As the primary objective of this study was to evaluate the feasibility and participant receptivity of the delivery framework, it does not provide evidence of its efficacy in facilitating follow-on clinician-led serious illness conversations or any downstream patient-related impacts. Consequently, the clinical utility of this approach in gathering actionable insights into patient goals of care remains an area for future investigation. Future research should employ comparative designs to evaluate the effectiveness of the PSC model as an adjunct to clinician-delivered SICs versus an unassisted, clinician-led approach, focusing on both the breadth and qualitative depth of documentation and their impact on clinical decision-making and patient-reported outcomes for existential and psychological well-being.

Conclusion

The PSC intervention offered an acceptable and feasible framework for surfacing patient goals and values within a non-directive, supportive listening model. The intervention achieved robust enrollment and retention rates, and received positive appraisals from both patients and clinicians alike. The model may help address common clinical barriers to serious illness communication, with qualitative findings providing preliminary evidence of its potential to enhance the care experience and support the psychological well-being of patients. Further investigation is required to establish its clinical utility at scale.
