Dietary index for gut microbiota and its protective role against osteoporosis: Evidence from NHANES 2007 to 2018
Authors: Rui Feng, Shujuan Xu, Yangqing Cui, Kai Wang, Fan Yang, Qunqun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Distortions in gut microbiota homeostasis may contribute to abnormal bone metabolism and osteoporosis. This study aimed to examine the association between the dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM) and osteoporosis among U.S. adults, identify key dietary components, and develop a clinically applicable risk prediction model incorporating dietary and clinical factors. We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 9065 adults. DI-GM was calculated from the dietary intake data, and osteoporosis was the primary outcome. Weighted multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the association between DI-GM and osteoporosis, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Additionally, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was applied to identify critical dietary components. Finally, a prediction model integrating clinical and nutritional factors was developed and evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Each unit increase in DI-GM reduced the risk of osteoporosis by 10% (adjusted OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.83–0.99). A higher intake of DI-GM-beneficial components was also associated with reduced osteoporosis risk (OR: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.79–0.98). Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator highlighted soy, fermented dairy, and processed meat as the most influential dietary factors; higher intake of soybeans and fermented dairy was associated with a decreased risk of osteoporosis, whereas higher processed meat intake was associated with an increased risk. The prediction model demonstrated good discrimination (area under the curve = 0.757). A higher DI-GM was associated with a reduced risk of osteoporosis, and specific dietary components may drive this association. The proposed model may support individualized dietary risk stratification and prevention strategies for populations with an elevated osteoporosis risk.

1. Introduction

Osteoporosis is a widespread and severe skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mineral density, degradation of bone microstructure, increased bone fragility, and elevated fracture risk.[1] Globally, it affects over 200 million individuals, with annual healthcare costs exceeding USD 13.5 billion in the U.S. alone.[2] In 2019, up to 37 million fragility fractures occurred each year worldwide among individuals aged 55 years and older, equivalent to approximately 70 fractures per minute.[3] Moreover, a prior fragility fracture substantially increases the risk of subsequent fractures.[4] Consequently, prevention and management of osteoporosis has become a critical public health priority for aging populations, aiming to reduce medical burdens, ease healthcare system strain, and improve quality of life.[5]

Recent studies have indicated significant variations in the gut microbial composition between individuals with osteoporosis (including animal models) and healthy populations, with the severity of bone loss being positively linked to microbial changes.[6,7] The dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM), developed by Kase et al, captures the microbiota-related quality of habitual diet and was originally derived and validated in U.S. adults using the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data.[8] Subsequent studies have extended DI-GM’s applicability of DI-GM across populations; notably, Jiang et al validated DI-GM in an older Hong Kong cohort and found that higher scores were associated with better cognitive function.[9] Existing research also associates elevated DI-GM scores with heightened risks of metabolic dysfunction such as stroke, diabetes, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease.[10–12] Evidence linking DI-GM to skeletal health is emerging; Fang et al observed an inverse association with osteoporosis in postmenopausal women, yet the overall literature remains limited.[13] Accordingly, we evaluated the association between DI-GM and osteoporosis in a large, diverse adult cohort to further clarify the diet–microbiota–bone health axis.

Building upon prior research, this study integrates data from the NHANES 2007 to 2018 to explore the potential links between the DI-GM and osteoporosis. This investigation specifically evaluated whether elevated DI-GM scores, which reflect improved dietary patterns, demonstrated a statistically significant correlation with reduced osteoporosis incidence. Advanced machine learning techniques, including least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, were employed to identify critical dietary determinants, followed by the development of a personalized risk assessment framework.

2. Methods

2.1. Data source

The dataset for this research was derived from 5 consecutive cycles (2007–2018) of publicly available records from the NHANES. The study strictly adhered to the STROBE guidelines for observational clinical research and received approval from the National Center for Health Statistics ethics committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The NHANES employs a hybrid sampling strategy that integrates multistage probabilistic and stratified random methods to ensure comprehensive and validated health data collection.

2.2. Study population

This research utilized data from the NHANES spanning 2007 to 2018. In total, 59,842 individuals were included in the study. We excluded 16,799 individuals with absent bone mineral density (BMD) data and 28,542 participants with absent DI-GM data. After these exclusions, 14,051 subjects provided comprehensive data. Furthermore, those aged <20 years (n = 5436) were excluded, resulting in a final cohort of 9065 adults. Participants were categorized into 2 groups: 8876 adults without osteoporosis and 189 individuals with osteoporosis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart depicting the screening process of the NHANES participants from 2007 to 2018. NHANES = National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey.

2.3. Diagnosis of osteoporosis

This study focused on osteoporosis as the primary outcome. BMD was measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry targeting either the first 4 lumbar vertebrae (L1–L4) or bilateral hip joints. A T-score of ‐2.5 or lower at the femoral neck or lumbar spine was used as the diagnostic criterion for osteoporosis. The T-score was calculated by dividing the difference between the participant’s BMD and the mean BMD of a healthy reference population by the standard deviation of the reference group.[14]

2.4. Assessment of DI-GM

We constructed a microbiome-focused dietary index (DI-GM) according to Kase classification framework, which was developed and evaluated in U.S. adults using NHANES data and has since been applied in multiple NHANES analyses.[8,15] Positive contributors included plant-based staples (e.g., legumes and berries), fermented substrates, and fiber-rich grains. Tea subtypes were omitted because of data limitations in the national health surveys. Green tea was excluded because of NHANES’s lack of tea subtype data in the NHANES.[8] The negative factors included processed meat, refined grains, red meat, and fat diets. The DI-GM scores were calculated using NHANES 2007 to 2018 dietary recall data.[8] Scoring criteria: beneficial items were assigned 1 point if consumption met or surpassed sex-specific median levels; otherwise, 0 point was awarded. Detrimental items were scored as 0 if the intake exceeded the median; otherwise, they were scored 1.[16] The total scores ranged from 0 to 13 (beneficial: 0–9; detrimental: 0–4) and were categorized as 0 to 3, 4, 5, and ≥6.[17]

2.5. Covariates

The variables selected for this study were based on previously identified characteristics that are significantly associated with osteoporosis. Demographic features included age, ethnicity, sex, and body mass index (BMI). The socioeconomic dimensions included educational level and marital status. Health-related behavioral variables included smoking history and alcohol consumption. Clinical comorbidity indicators included uric acid, phosphorus, serum calcium, alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), history of fractures, diabetes, and hypertension. Missing data were analyzed using statistical imputation techniques.

2.6. Statistical methods

This study meticulously incorporated NHANES’s sophisticated sampling methodology, employing weighted analytical techniques to enhance the representativeness and reliability of the findings. The NHANES achieves national-level representativeness using a stratified multistage probability sampling approach. In alignment with the National Center for Health Statistics protocols, adjustments for design complexity were implemented using sampling weights (WTMECPRP), pseudo-strata (SDMVSTRA), and pseudo-clusters (SDMVPSU). Participants were first stratified according to osteoporosis status and subsequently grouped into DI-GM quartiles. For normally distributed continuous variables, weighted t tests (2 groups) or analysis of variance (multiple groups) were used. Non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the weighted Mann–Whitney U test (2 groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis test (multiple groups). Categorical variables were evaluated using the weighted Chi-square test. Continuous data were expressed as weighted means ± SD, and categorical data were expressed as weighted frequencies (%).

Weighted multivariate regression models were applied to evaluate the association between the DI-GM and osteoporosis risk. Three sequential adjustment models were constructed: Model 1 controlled for demographic variables (age, sex, and ethnicity); Model 2 incorporated socioeconomic factors (education level, marital status); and Model 3 comprehensively adjusted for lifestyle (smoking, alcohol use, and BMI) and clinical parameters (serum calcium, ALT, AST, uric acid, ALP, BUN, phosphorus, hypertension, fractures, and diabetes). Generalized additive models with smoothing splines were used to explore potential nonlinear relationships, whereas forest plots were used to visualize subgroup consistency. LASSO regression identified key dietary predictors that were integrated into the risk prediction nomogram. Model accuracy was validated using receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve (AUC) metrics, with statistical significance set at P < .05. Analyses were conducted using DecisionLime 1.1, and robustness was ensured using cross-validation.

3.2. Association between DI-GM and osteoporosis

Table 2 revealed that a one-unit increase in the DI-GM score was associated with a 10% reduction in osteoporosis prevalence (odds ratio [OR] 0.90 [95% confidence interval [CI] 0.82–0.98]). After adjusting for covariates, each additional DI-GM point corresponded to a 10% lower risk of osteoporosis (OR 0.90, [95% CI 0.83–0.99]). Notably, participants with DI-GM scores ≥6 exhibited a 45% decreased osteoporosis risk compared to those with lower scores (OR, 0.55 [95% CI 0.36–0.85]). A higher beneficial gut microbiota score significantly reduced the incidence of osteoporosis (OR, 0.88 [95% CI 0.79–0.98]), whereas detrimental microbiota scores showed no statistical association (OR, 0.99 [95% CI 0.86–1.15]). Furthermore, smoothing curve analysis indicated a linear relationship between DI-GM and osteoporosis risk in the overall population (Fig. 2).

The LASSO algorithm was used to identify the dietary components that were most strongly associated with osteoporosis risk. This method enhances model efficiency by shrinking the coefficients of nonessential variables to zero, thereby preserving only the most significant predictors. The analysis included 14 food categories alongside covariates (e.g., age, sex, ethnicity, BMI, smoking, alcohol use, and diabetes) and clinical parameters (serum calcium, ALT, AST, uric acid, ALP, BUN, and phosphorus; Fig. 4). The optimal penalty parameter (lambda) was determined via 10-fold cross-validation to minimize the model deviance (Fig. 5). Key dietary risk factors such as soy products, fermented dairy products, and processed meats were identified.

A predictive visualization tool (nomogram) was constructed to estimate individualized osteoporosis risk by integrating key dietary factors and clinical confounders of osteoporosis. This tool combines nutritional intake data and health metrics to enable personalized risk stratification (Fig. 6). By highlighting the impact of specific dietary patterns on skeletal health, it provides actionable guidance for targeted interventions in high-risk populations operating characteristic curve analysis, and the prediction accuracy of the model was confirmed using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. The AUC of 75.7% demonstrated significant discriminating power between instances with and without osteoporosis (Fig. 7). These findings show that combining clinical and nutritional data is a trustworthy method for determining osteoporosis risk.

4. Discussion

This study examined the NHANES 2007 to 2018 data, comprising 9065 participants, and carefully assessed the correlation between the DI-GM and osteoporosis. Our findings demonstrate that elevated DI-GM scores, especially within the DI-GM ≥6 category and those advantageous to gut flora, correlated with a reduced incidence of osteoporosis. The correlation between DI-GM and osteoporosis persisted consistently in subgroup studies. Subsequent research with LASSO regression revealed that soybeans, fermented dairy products, and processed meat are significant dietary determinants affecting osteoporosis risk. A predictive model that includes these parameters exhibited substantial clinical value, with an AUC of 75.7%. These findings not only corroborate the “gut–bone axis” hypothesis, but also provide empirical evidence that dietary manipulation aimed at the gut microbiota may be an effective strategy for enhancing bone health.

Osteoporosis, which is characterized by imbalanced bone remodeling (excessive resorption by osteoclasts and inadequate formation by osteoblasts), is driven by factors such as estrogen deficiency, oxidative stress, and inflammation.[18] Postmenopausal osteoporosis is characterized by coupled increases in both bone resorption and formation (with resorption predominating) due to estrogen deficiency.[19] Estrogen, parathyroid hormone, inflammatory cytokines, and vitamin D govern bone remodeling, and menopause and aging are the leading causes of osteoporosis. Intestinal flora plays a key role in maintaining bone metabolism homeostasis, which is another crucial physiological system in humans.[20,21] A recent study has indicated that gut bacteria may significantly regulate bone physiology. In a physiological study, serum levels of trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), a gut microbiota-derived metabolite, were measured in 286 postmenopausal women with hip fractures.[22] The findings revealed that elevated TMAO concentrations were significantly associated with an increased risk of hip fractures, suggesting a potential role for TMAO in osteoporosis and fracture pathogenesis in postmenopausal women.[22,23] Bone metabolism and osteoporosis are regulated by the gut microbiota, which is affected by the diversity and amount of nutrients in various foods.[24] For example, high-fat diets, which are harmful according to DI-GM, can stimulate bile acid production, which aids in lipid digestion.[25] However, these acids have negative effects on bacterial cell membranes, indicating that they can exert strong selection pressure on certain groups of microbes.[25,26] As a result, these metabolites related to the gut microbiota have a negative impact on the metabolic regulation of osteoporosis. We found that soybeans, fermented dairy products, and processed meat were major factors in the development of osteoporosis. Previous studies corroborate these results. There are 2 ways in which the bacteria and calcium in fermented dairy products work together to boost bone density.[27] The first thing they do is add to the body’s supply of nutrients that are vital for building bones.[28] Second, these products improve calcium absorption efficiency and promote an anti-inflammatory milieu, which in turn reduces bone resorption by changing the composition of the gut microbiota (for example, by increasing the quantity of Lactobacillus.[29] Similarly, research has shown that isoflavones found in soybeans, such as genistein, can inhibit bone resorption by stimulating osteoblast activity, similar to estrogen.[30] The metabolic conversion of isoflavones, governed by gut bacteria, may play a role in this process.[31] However, processed meat also has high salt content. In postmenopausal women, a high-sodium diet alters calcium metabolism and enhances bone resorption.[32]

Enterodiols (END) and enterolactones (ENL) are mammalian lignans derived from plant lignan precursors via gut microbiota metabolism in the proximal colon.[33] These metabolites were integral to developing the DI-GM from the associations found in the NHANES recall data.[8] END and ENL exhibit multimodal mechanisms for bone protection. Ligands of estrogen receptors (estrogen receptor alpha/beta) demonstrate concentration-dependent activity, and at low concentrations, they act as ER agonists, transactivating bone-protective pathways that stimulate osteoblast activity and inhibit osteoclastogenesis.[34,35] At higher concentrations, they function as competitive antagonists against endogenous estrogens (e.g., estradiol), attenuating estrogen deficiency-mediated bone resorption.[36] Additionally, END/ENL production correlates with increased microbial-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs; e.g., butyrate), which inhibit osteoclast activity and promote mineral absorption.[35] The interplay between gut microbiota and skeletal health involves multiple biological pathways mediated by microbial metabolites.[37,38] Intestinal microbiota produces bioactive compounds, such as SCFAs, indole derivatives, polyamines, and adenosine triphosphate through fermentation, which regulate bone metabolism.[39,40] For example, SCFAs may enhance bone formation by stimulating the secretion of insulin-like growth factor-1 and glucagon-like peptide-1.[41] Dysbiosis-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction elevates systemic lipopolysaccharide levels, leading to metabolic endotoxemia.[42] Animal studies have confirmed that elevated lipopolysaccharide concentrations accelerate bone loss and significantly reduce lumbar bone mineral density, trabecular volume, and vertebral count.[43] In the long term, dietary interventions serve as a safe and effective strategy for modulating gut microbiota and influencing bone metabolism. Thus, based on the “gut-bone axis” theory, promoting dietary knowledge for osteoporosis prevention holds significant value as a common intervention in daily life.[44,45]

This study had several notable strengths. First, this is an inaugural investigation that revealed a significant inverse correlation between DI-GM scores and osteoporosis risk after adjusting for potential confounders, suggesting that higher DI-GM values correlate with progressive risk reduction. Second, the analysis identified a linear dose–response pattern between DI-GM and osteoporosis, underscoring its consistent protective role in maintaining bone health. By leveraging NHANES’s robust quality assurance protocols and representative sampling framework of the NHANES, this study validated these associations within a large, ethnically diverse U.S. adult cohort. Furthermore, this study offers innovative insights into the prevention and management of osteoporosis from the perspective of translational medicine. In clinical practice, the DI-GM score is an effective instrument for guiding dietary adjustments including enhancing soybean product intake, incorporating fermented dairy products, and restricting processed meat consumption. The prediction model developed using LASSO regression (AUC = 75.7%) incorporated dietary and clinical parameters, facilitating the identification of high-risk individuals and formulation of tailored intervention strategies.

However, certain limitations were associated with this study. First, its cross-sectional design made it impossible to draw conclusions about the causative relationship between DI-GM and osteoporosis, and it was impossible to rule out reverse causation. However, the underlying processes must be validated using randomized or prospective experiments. Second, reliance on self-reported dietary data (e.g., 24-hour recalls) may introduce recall bias, although averaging multiple recalls and weighted logistic regression were employed to mitigate this bias. Moreover, green tea and other tea subtypes were excluded from the DI-GM due to data limitations in the NHANES, which does not consistently distinguish between tea types in dietary recall data, and the absence of specific tea subtype data may underestimate the contribution of tea-derived lignans to the gut microbiota and bone metabolism. Finally, the composition of the gut microbiota and its metabolites was not directly examined, which limited the mechanistic insights into the pathways that were considered. Future research should incorporate both metagenomic and metabolomic methods to determine the exact microbial taxa and molecular pathways responsible for the modulation of bone health using DI-GM.

5. Conclusion

This study revealed a significant inverse correlation between the DI-GM, which reflects the dietary quality associated with microbial diversity and osteoporosis risk. We propose adopting a plant-centric, diversified diet rich in dietary fiber, probiotics, and prebiotics, while minimizing the consumption of animal-derived products, ultra-processed foods, and high-fat items to enhance gut microbiota functionality. Given the synergistic roles of diet, gut microbiota, and skeletal health, future preventive strategies that integrate DI-GM-based dietary frameworks are critical for osteoporosis management.
