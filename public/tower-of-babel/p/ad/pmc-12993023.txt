Central medial and paraventricular thalamic afferents to the orbitofrontal cortex have differential effects on reversal learning
Authors: Kathleen M. Tuite, Megan C. Ouellette, Milena Girotti, David A. Morilak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cognitive impairments are prominent in stress-related psychiatric disorders, such as depression and PTSD. While current therapies may improve mood symptoms, many patients experience persistent deficits in cognitive flexibility. One form of cognitive flexibility disrupted in such conditions is reversal learning, dependent on the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). Chronic stress causes OFC dysfunction and reversal learning deficits. However, the circuit mechanisms by which this happens are not understood. We hypothesized that two thalamic afferents to the OFC, the central medial (CM) and paraventricular thalamus (PVT), contribute to successful reversal learning and the detrimental effects of stress, respectively. We showed a decrease in activation of lateral OFC, measured by Fos induction, following reversal learning, and increased activation of both CM and PVT. Chronic unpredictable stress (CUS) impaired reversal learning and increased ΔFosB expression in CM and PVT projections to the OFC. Next, we chemogenetically manipulated each pathway to the OFC in non-stressed and stressed rats. Inhibition of the CM to OFC pathway in non-stressed rats disrupted reversal learning, while activation of this pathway restored reversal learning in stressed rats. By contrast, activation of the PVT to OFC pathway disrupted reversal learning in non-stressed male rats. In stressed males, inhibition of the PVT to OFC pathway ameliorated the detrimental effects of CUS. Effects of PVT to OFC manipulations were absent in females. These results indicate that the CM-OFC pathway promotes successful reversal learning in both sexes, and the PVT-OFC pathway contributes to the detrimental effects of stress on reversal learning in a sex-dependent manner.

Introduction

Cognitive flexibility is the ability to change established thoughts or behaviors based on feedback from the environment. Cognitive flexibility is disrupted in many psychiatric disorders, and few treatments address this symptom, leaving the majority of patients with residual deficits even after achieving remission of their mood symptoms (Kurian et al., 2009; Nomikos, 2019; Shilyansky et al., 2016). Cognitive deficits are thought to exacerbate and maintain psychiatric disorders and, left untreated, lead to a high rate of relapse even in patients receiving treatment (Beck, 2008; Steinert et al., 2014). Chronic stress induces cognitive inflexibility in healthy and clinical populations, which is a risk factor for many psychiatric disorders (Kashdan and Rottenberg, 2010; Levy-Gigi and Richter-Levin, 2014). Therefore, understanding the neurobiological mechanisms underlying cognitive flexibility and its dysregulation by stress could inform the development of strategies to improve therapeutic outcomes for stress-related psychiatric disorders.

One form of cognitive flexibility disrupted in such disorders is reversal learning, the ability to adapt responding in a discrimination task when the reinforcement contingencies are switched, i.e., when the previously “positive” stimulus becomes “negative” and vice versa. This cognitive process is disrupted in obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), major depressive disorder (MDD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and substance use disorders (SUDs) (Bağci et al., 2022; Gotlib and Joormann, 2010; McCarthy et al., 2018; Mukherjee et al., 2020; Remijnse et al., 2013). Reversal learning depends on function of the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) (McAlonan and Brown, 2003), and OFC dysfunction is seen in many psychiatric disorders associated with deficits in this ability (Drevets, 2007; Izquierdo, 2017; Remijnse et al., 2013). Our studies focus on the lateral subregion of the OFC (lOFC), as this subregion is most associated with reversal learning (Izquierdo, 2017).

While there is significant evidence showing that the lOFC is essential for reversal learning, there is little to no information about the circuits involving lOFC that might underly this cognitive process and how these circuits are disrupted by stress. Here, we focus on two thalamic afferents to the lOFC, the central medial (CM) thalamus and the paraventricular thalamus (PVT). Both thalamic nuclei have been shown to project to the lOFC (Vertes et al., 2012; Vertes and Hoover, 2008) but neither have been studied extensively in the context of cognitive flexibility. The CM has largely been studied in the context of sleep, seizures, and arousal (Miller and Ferrendelli, 1990; Saalmann, 2014; Van der Werf et al., 2002). In one study, inhibition of the intralaminar thalamic nuclei, of which the CM is a part, disrupted reversal learning and not associative learning (Kato et al., 2018). Given its projection to the lOFC, its role in arousal, and the fact that CM inhibition affected only reversal learning, it is possible that the CM to lOFC pathway might be disrupted by stress and in stress-related psychiatric disorders. The PVT, on the other hand, has been studied in many contexts including the stress response, stress habituation, reward-based behaviors, and aversive/avoidance behaviors (Bhatnagar et al., 2002; Hsu et al., 2014; McGinty and Otis, 2020; Rowson and Pleil, 2021; Ver Hoeve et al., 2013). One study did show that knocking down the immediate early gene, Arc, in the posterior PVT disrupts cognitive flexbility (Corbett et al., 2022), while most other studies on the PVT focus on the behaviors mentioned above (Choi and McNally, 2017; Engelke et al., 2021; Otis et al., 2019). The posterior subregion of the PVT specifically is thought to play a role in aversive/avoidance behaviors and interacts with the HPA axis to regulate the stress response (Bhatnagar et al., 2002; McGinty and Otis, 2020). Since the posterior PVT is highly stress-responsive and projects to the lOFC, we hypothesized that this nucleus might have a role in mediating the effects of chronic stress on cognitive flexibility.

In this study, to investigate how chronic stress disrupts reversal learning, we manipulated these two thalamic afferent pathways to the lOFC in stressed and non-stressed rats. We hypothesized that inhibition of the CM to lOFC pathway in non-stressed animals would disrupt reversal learning, mimicking the effects of stress, and that activation of this pathway in chronically stressed animals would reverse the detrimental effects of stress. Conversely, we hypothesized that activating the PVT to lOFC pathway in non-stressed animals would disrupt reversal learning, while inhibiting the same pathway in stressed animals would reverse the detrimental effects of stress.

Materials & methods

Animals

A total of 154 male and naturally cycling female Sprague-Dawley rats (Envigo, 225-249 g) were group housed on a 12/12-h light cycle (lights on at 07:00), with same-sex cage mates upon arrival. After 1 week acclimation, they were single-housed and provided with food and water ad libitum. Experiments took place between 08:00-17:00 h, during the lights-on portion of the light cycle. All procedures were in accordance with NIH guidelines and approved by the UTHSCSA Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Reversal learning

Reversal learning depends on the function of the OFC (Birrell and Brown, 2000; McAlonan and Brown, 2003). To assess reversal learning, we used the first three tasks in the Attentional Set-shifting Test (AST), first described by Birrell and Brown (2000), and as previously reported (Adler et al., 2020). Animals were food restricted starting 7 days prior to testing, to 11g/day for males and 7g/day for females, approximately 60-70% of average daily free-feeding intake. Animals were trained and tested in a white plastic arena containing a start gate blocking one third of the arena and 2 terracotta pots separated by a plexiglass divider at the other end. The test procedure consisted of three days (habituation, training, and testing):i.Habituation day: Rats were trained to dig in pots filled with increasing amounts of sawdust to retrieve ½ of a Honey Nut Cheerio reward (General Mills Cereals, Minneapolis, MN, USA).ii.Training day: Rats were trained to locate and retrieve the Cheerio buried in one of two pots differentiated by cues in one of two stimulus dimensions, the material with which the pots were filled or an odor with which they were scented. The first discrimination was based on odors applied to the rims of the pots, which were both filled with sawdust. The second discrimination was based on the texture of the digging media filling two pots that were both unscented.iii.Testing day: Rats were tested on 3 discrimination tasks in sequence (Table 2). The first task was a simple discrimination (SD), in which only one stimulus dimension was presented, and the rats had to learn which of two cues in that dimension signaled the location of the reward. Half the rats started with odor as the informative dimension, half with texture. Next was a compound discrimination (CD), in which the second stimulus dimension was introduced as a distractor, and the same cue in the first dimension was still positive. The third task in the sequence was the reversal learning test (R1), in which the informative dimension was the same, but the valence of the two cues within that dimension were switched, such that the previously positive cue was now negative, and the previously negative cue now signaled the location of the reward. In all discriminations, the smell of the reward was masked by lightly sprinkling the medium in both pots with Cheerio powder. Mastery of a given contingency to a criterion of 6 consecutive correct trials was required to proceed to the next task. Fos immunohistochemistry

To assess activation of neurons during reversal learning, animals were returned to their home cages for 1.5 h after completing the reversal learning test. For comparison, a control group was returned to their home cage after completing the CD task without proceeding to reversal learning. Rats were then anesthetized with a cocktail of ketamine 43 mg/kg, acepromazine 1.4 mg/kg, and xylazine 8.6 mg/kg (in 1 ml/kg, i.m.), and sacrificed via transcardial perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, pH 7.4). Brains were stored in PFA overnight, then transferred to 30% sucrose solution for 48 h for cryoprotection. Brains were frozen in isopentane on dry ice for sectioning on a cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Buffalo Grove, IL). Brains were sectioned at 40 μm through regions of the brain including the lOFC, PVT, CM, and basolateral amygdala. Sections were cut into five vials in sequence, i.e., 5 series of sections separated by 200 μm, and stored at 4 °C in PBS and 0.02% sodium azide. For processing, brains were washed with PBS and a PBS/1% Triton X-100 solution, then fixed with 100% methanol at −20 °C for 5 min. After blocking (1% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100, 5% normal donkey serum in PBS) at room temperature for 1 h, sections were incubated with rabbit anti-Fos mAb (1:500, Synaptic Systems 226 003, Goettingen, Germany) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, sections were incubated in anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated with fluorophore Alexa 488 (1:500, Jackson Immunoresearch 711-545-152) for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then washed in PBS/1% Triton X-100 and transferred to PBS for temporary storage before mounting on Superfrost Plus slides and dried in the dark at room temperature overnight. Coverslips were mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (ThermoFisher Scientific). Images were captured using a 20x objective (Zeiss, Observer.Z1). Fos quantification was performed manually using FIJI (ImageJ) and expressed as number of Fos-positive nuclei/mm2.

Retrograde tracing and ΔFosB immunohistochemistry

Animals received injections of 0.5 μL of the retrograde adeno-associated virus (pENN-AAVrg-hSyn-eGFP-Cre-WPRE-SV40) bilaterally into the lOFC (AP: +3.2, ML: ±2.5, DV: −5.1 mm, (Paxinos and Watson, 2007). Half the animals went through CUS while the other half were handled daily. To allow equivalent time (4 weeks) for viral transport and expression, CUS began 2 weeks after surgery in males and 1 week after surgery in females. Full-length FosB degrades after approximately 12 h, while ΔFosB heterodimerizes with JunD, forming a long-lasting stable complex, (Nestler et al., 2001), so animals were sacrificed via transcardial perfusion 24 h after the end of stress to capture only ΔFosB expression, or 24 h after the last day of handling for the non-stressed animals. Brains were post-fixed, frozen, and sectioned as above. For processing, sections were first incubated in 3% H2O2 for 20-30 min, then boiled in 10 mM citric acid pH 6 and cooled for 30 min. After blocking (1% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100, 5% donkey serum in PBS) at room temperature for 1 h, sections were incubated with rabbit anti-FosB mAb (1:200, Invitrogen, MA5-15056) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the primary antibody was washed off and sections were incubated with a biotinylated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000, Jackson Immunoresearch, 711-065-152) at room temperature for 1 h, then incubated for 30 min in avidin-biotin-HRP (ABC) reagent (Vectastain Elite ABC-HRP kit, PK-6100, Vector Laboratories). After washing, sections were incubated for 3 min in Tyramide Signal Amplification (TSA) probe (Cyanine 5) diluted 1:50 (NEL745001KT, AKOYA Biosciences), then washed. To label the retrograde tracer, sections were incubated with mouse anti-GFP mAb (1:1000, Millipore, MAB3580) overnight at 4 °C, then donkey anti-mouse secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000, Jackson Immunoresearch, 715-545-151). Sections were mounted and imaged as above to analyze colocalization of the retrograde tracer and ΔFosB.

Colocalization analysis

Colocalization analysis was done using a combination of a macro described previously (Moser et al., 2017) and the Coloc2 plugin in FIJI (ImageJ). Briefly, the output of the macro was input into an Excel spreadsheet to calculate the percentage of lOFC projecting neurons that expressed ΔFosB (double-labeled neurons/total GFP + neurons) and the percentage of those expressing ΔFosB that project to lOFC (double-labeled neurons/total ΔFosB + neurons).

Inhibition of the CM→OFC pathway in non-stressed animals

Animals received midline injections of an adeno-associated virus (AAV5) to express either an inhibitory Gi DREADD (pAAV-CamKIIa-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry) or control GFP construct (pAAV-CamKIIa-EGFP), under control of the CamKIIa promoter to target glutamatergic neurons, into the central medial thalamus (AP: −3.2, ML: ±1.09, DV: −6.3 mm at a 10° angle to avoid disruption of the 3rd ventricle and PVT; these correspond to coordinates AP: −3.2, ML: ±0, DV: −6.2 in (Paxinos and Watson, 2007). Guide cannulae (Plastics One) were implanted bilaterally above the lOFC (AP: +3.2, ML: ±2.5, DV: −4.2). At testing, injectors will extend 1 mm past the guide cannulae to reach DV −5.2. Animals recovered for 3-4 weeks following surgery. On days 1 and 2 after the recovery period, rats were habituated to the reversal learning arena and trained on the task. On day 3, a subset of animals went through the SD and CD stages of the reversal learning test, then received bilateral intracerebral injections into the OFC of the DREADD agonist clozapine-N-oxide (CNO, 300 μM, 0.75 μL per side, Tocris Bioscience, 4936) 25 min preceding the reversal learning task. To control for potential confounding effects on learning in general, the remaining subset received CNO injections 25 min before the start of testing, i.e., preceding the SD task. As there were no differences in behavior comparing the two injection times, for all subsequent experiments, CNO injections were made prior to SD. A previous study testing all 6 stages of the AST after intracranial injections of the same concentration of CNO used in our study, given 15 min prior to testing, showed effects on the final stage, i.e., CNO exerted effects for the full duration of the entire test sequence (Donegan et al., 2019), which is far longer than the variability in time to complete the SD and CD stages prior to the reversal stage.

Activation of the CM→OFC pathway in stressed animals

Rats received midline injections of an adeno-associated virus carrying either an excitatory Gq DREADD (pAAV-CaMKII-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry) or a control mCherry construct (pAAV-CaMKII-mCherry) into the CM, and guide cannulae were bilaterally implanted into the lOFC. Animals recovered 1-2 weeks following surgery before beginning CUS as described above. Non-stressed controls were handled daily. The same non-stressed GFP controls from the CM-lOFC inhibition experiment above were used as baseline. Rats were then habituated and trained. On day 3 following CUS or handling, they received bilateral CNO injections into the lOFC 25 min before beginning testing.

Activation of the PVT→OFC pathway in non-stressed animals

Rats received midline injections of an adeno-associated virus carrying either an excitatory Gq DREADD (pAAV-CaMKIIa-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry) or control GFP construct (pAAV-CamKIIa-EGFP), under the CamKIIa promoter to target glutamatergic neurons, into the posterior PVT (AP: −3.0, ML: ±0.7, DV: −5.0 at a 10° angle to avoid disruption of the 3rd ventricle; these correspond to coordinates AP: −3.0, ML: ±0, DV: −4.9 in (Paxinos and Watson, 2007) and guide cannulae were implanted bilaterally above the lOFC. Animals recovered for 3-4 weeks following surgery. On day 3 following recovery all animals received bilateral intracerebral injections of CNO into lOFC 25 min preceding testing on the reversal learning test.

Inhibition of the PVT→OFC pathway in stressed animals

Rats received midline injections of an adeno-associated virus carrying either an inhibitory Gi DREADD or control GFP construct into the posterior PVT with guide cannula bilaterally implanted into the lOFC. Animals recovered for 1-2 weeks following surgery. Following the recovery period animals underwent CUS (2 weeks for males, 3 weeks for females). Non-stressed controls were handled daily. The same non-stressed GFP control group from the PVT-lOFC activation experiment was used as baseline. Animals were then habituated and trained as above. On day 3 following CUS or handling, they received bilateral CNO injections into lOFC 25 min preceding the SD phase of testing.

Statistical analysis

Behavioral data were subject to primary analysis by t-test or ANOVA using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). The Shapiro-Wilk test for normality was first performed to determine use of parametric or non-parametric tests. If the data did not exhibit normality, Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests were performed as appropriate. Additionally, a secondary analysis was then conducted including sex as a factor. When sex-dependent interactions were detected, males and females were then analyzed separately, as above, via t-test or ANOVA, although these experiments were not explicitly powered for separate analyses. Pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Holm-Sidak test. Significance was set in all analyses at p < 0.05. Data are represented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise noted.

Results

Lateral OFC activation decreases following reversal learning

Previous work from our laboratory showed that increased excitatory input to the OFC is detrimental to reversal learning (Adler et al., 2020), so we first assessed changes in OFC activation during reversal learning, a control group of animals stopped testing after the CD phase of the reversal learning test, and another group was tested through reversal learning (n = 15-16 per group). Brains were collected 1.5 h after the completion of testing for Fos immunohistochemistry. The lOFC showed a significant decrease in Fos induction following the reversal stage as compared to animals that only went through CD (t = 3.662, p = 0.0010; Fig. 1A, open symbols represent females throughout), while the medial (mOFC, p = 0.2005) and ventral (vOFC, p = 0.4924) OFC showed no change. This is consistent with the lOFC being the OFC subregion most associated with reversal learning (Izquierdo, 2017). To further characterize the effects of CUS on the brain regions of interest, a separate group of male and female rats (n = 9-10 per group) received bilateral injections of a retrograde-AAV into the lOFC and, after a week of recovery for females and two weeks of recovery for males, underwent the CUS protocol (Fig. 2B). Brains were collected 24 h after the end of stress to perform immunohistochemistry for ΔFosB, a protein that accumulates in response to repeated activation (Nestler et al., 2001). We quantified total retrograde tracer (GFP) expression, total ΔFosB expression, the percentage of retrogradely labeled cells that expressed ΔFosB, and the percentage of ΔFosB+ cells that expressed retrograde tracer (Fig. 2C–E, representative images in Fig. S1). Total GFP expression increased in the CM (t = 2.642, p = 0.0177) and PVT (t = 3.768, p = 0.0017), but not the BLA (t = 1.474, p = 0.1598), in animals that underwent CUS (Fig. 2C–E, first column). Compared to non-stressed controls, CUS significantly increased total ΔFosB expression in the CM (t = 4.679, p = 0.0002), PVT (t = 4.785, p = 0.0002), and BLA (t = 2.595, p = 0.0189) (Fig. 2C–E, second column). In secondary analyses of total ΔFosB, there was no effect of sex in CM (p = 0.16). In PVT, females expressed lower levels of ΔFosB overall than males (main effect, p < 0.0001), but there was no difference in the increase induced by stress (interaction, p = 0.65). In cells specifically projecting to the lOFC, CUS significantly increased ΔFosB expression in the CM (t = 3.218, p = 0.0048) and PVT (t = 2.101, p = 0.049) but not the BLA (t = 0.5891, p = 0.5631) (Fig. 2C–E, third column), indicating that CM and PVT projections to the lOFC are activated by CUS. Secondary analysis revealed no effects of sex in either CM or PVT (p = 0.71 and p = 0.81, respectively). In ΔFosB expressing cells, there was a significant increase in the percentage of these cells that contribute to the lOFC projection in the CM (t = 2.332, p = 0.0315) in stressed animals, but no change in the PVT (t = 1.414, p = 0.1745) or BLA (t = 0.4732, p = 0.6418), indicating that a higher degree of ΔFosB induction occurred in the CM-lOFC projection after CUS.

Activation of the PVT→OFC pathway impairs reversal learning

The PVT is a highly stress responsive region with wide-reaching functional outputs (Hsu et al., 2014; McGinty and Otis, 2020; Otake et al., 2002). Our results show that the PVT projection to the lOFC is activated by chronic stress (Fig. 2D, third column). We then investigated the potential role of this projection in the effect of chronic stress on reversal learning. We first tested the hypothesis that activating the PVT projection to lOFC would recapitulate the effects of stress on reversal learning. Behavior began 3 weeks after surgery (Fig. 5A). Non-stressed animals received injections of a Gq excitatory DREADD or GFP control into the PVT and bilateral cannulae for CNO injection into the lOFC (Fig. 5B; n = 10-13 per group). Selective activation of the PVT input to lOFC via CNO injections directly into the lOFC had no effect on performance in the SD and CD associative learning stages (F1,40 = 0.1345, p = 0.7157, Fig. 5C right), but significantly increased the number of trials required to reach criterion in the reversal learning test (t = 4.497, p = 0.0002, Fig. 5C). Discussion

In this study, we investigated the roles of two midline thalamic inputs to the lateral OFC in reversal learning. We hypothesized a potential role of the central medial thalamic input to the lOFC in promoting reversal learning, and a role of the paraventricular thalamic input to the lOFC in the detrimental effects of chronic stress on reversal learning. Fos induction in the lOFC was decreased during reversal learning while the thalamic CM and PVT nuclei showed increased Fos induction. Following CUS, reversal learning was impaired and the PVT, CM, and BLA all showed elevated ΔFosB expression compared to non-stressed controls. Further, neurons in the PVT and CM that project specifically to the lOFC had increased ΔFosB expression, while BLA cells projecting to the lOFC did not. ΔFosB accumulation indicates chronic activation of neurons in response to stress (Nestler et al., 2001). Inhibition of the CM projection to lOFC disrupted reversal learning, and activation of the same projection after CUS restored reversal learning. The PVT is highly stress responsive and projects to the lOFC (Hsu et al., 2014; Otake et al., 2002; Rowson and Pleil, 2021; Vertes and Hoover, 2008). Thus, we examined a potential role of stress-induced activation of the PVT in the impairment of reversal learning following CUS. Manipulation of the PVT to lOFC pathway had sex-dependent effects on reversal learning. Chemogenetic activation of the PVT in non-stressed animals produced reversal learning deficits only in males, and inhibition of the PVT projection to lOFC in stressed animals reversed the effects of stress, again only in males. Thus, our results show that the CM input to lOFC promotes successful reversal learning, and that stress-induced activation of the PVT input to lOFC likely contributes to the detrimental effects of stress on reversal learning specifically in males.

Several human and rodent studies have shown that increased OFC activity, due to chronic stress or in psychiatric disorders, is associated with impaired cognition overall, and reversal learning in particular (Adler et al., 2020; Beucke et al., 2013; Drevets, 2007; Liston et al., 2006; Patton et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2021). Reversing the aberrant increase in OFC activity following chronic stress restored reversal learning to baseline (Adler et al., 2020; Patton et al., 2017). The current results also suggest that decreased lOFC activation might be necessary for effective reversal learning, as total Fos expression decreased in the lateral subregion of OFC compared to the preceding SD and CD associative learning tasks, perhaps by recruiting inhibitory interneurons in the OFC during reversal learning. Previous studies have shown that recruitment of OFC interneurons is critical for effective reversal learning (Bissonette et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2021). Interneuron density in the OFC decreases during chronic stress (Varga et al., 2017). This loss of interneurons might be part of the mechanism underlying the effects of chronic stress on reversal learning.

We also examined ΔFosB accumulation in chronically stressed animals to determine which regions that project to the lOFC are repeatedly activated by chronic stress. ΔFosB is an isoform of FosB that accumulates in response to repeated presentation of a stimulus, e.g., a substance of abuse or chronic stressors (Nestler et al., 2001). Accumulation of ΔFosB may promote neuronal plasticity, and in certain brain regions, confers stress resilience (Nestler et al., 1999; Vialou et al., 2015). Our results show an increase in ΔFosB in cells projecting from both the CM and posterior PVT to lOFC, indicating both projections experienced stress-induced plasticity. This is similar to our finding in Fig. 1 that both nuclei showed increased Fos induction following reversal learning. The posterior subregion of the PVT responds to aversive stimuli and non-reward, and projects more strongly to the lOFC than other regions of the PVT (McGinty and Otis, 2020; Vertes and Hoover, 2008). Both the CM and PVT utilize glutamate as the primary neurotransmitter (Hsu et al., 2014; Matsumoto et al., 2001), but the PVT produces excitatory responses in the prefrontal cortex while the CM is shown to produce inhibitory responses in the prefrontal cortex and to synchronize neuronal ensembles related to memory and attention (Cruikshank et al., 2012; Saalmann, 2014). Thus, while both regions project to the lOFC and show increased ΔFosB expression after stress, and elevated Fos expression after reversal learning, it is possible they induce different, even opposing responses in the lOFC. The retrograde tracing study additionally showed a significant increase in retrogradely-labeled GFP + cells in both the CM and PVT in animals that underwent CUS. Chronic stress has been shown to increase fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1) expression (Magnaghi et al., 2000), a co-receptor for AAVs, potentially increasing viral uptake and transport. Another possibility is that chronic stress has been shown to increase dendritic arborization in OFC (Adler et al., 2020; Liston et al., 2006), which could perhaps induce axonal sprouting in afferent terminals, allowing for increased virus uptake and transport. Such explanations could possibly account for the increased labeling of cells contributing to the projections from CM and PVT to lOFC. Nonetheless, this does not account for the significant increase in the percentage of cells in those projections that were activated by CUS and expressed ΔFosB.

The results of the current study revealed a novel role for the CM in cognition. Most studies of the CM have focused on its role in wakefulness, sleep, seizures, and arousal (Miller and Ferrendelli, 1990; Mitchell and Dalrymple-Alford, 2005; Saalmann, 2014; Van der Werf et al., 2002). We showed that the CM has a strong projection to the lOFC, consistent with previous reports (Vertes et al., 2012). Chemogenetic inhibition of the CM to lOFC pathway disrupted reversal learning, and activation of this pathway in stressed animals reversed the effects of stress on reversal learning, supporting the hypothesis that the CM promotes cognitive processes mediated by the lOFC. CM projections to the prefrontal cortex have been shown to be involved in synchronization of neural activity (Saalmann, 2014); these projections synchronize neuronal ensembles associated with memory and awareness. There is also evidence to suggest that the CM plays a role in seizure regulation; when CM was taken offline, seizures worsened (Miller and Ferrendelli, 1990), indicating the CM is providing inhibitory tone to control aberrant activity. An electrophysiological study in the intralaminar thalamus, including the CM, found that the CM projects to and primarily activates GABAergic interneurons in the prefrontal cortex (Cruikshank et al., 2012). This indicates that the CM might provide an inhibitory tone to the prefrontal cortex that maintains activity at an optimal level for arousal and cognitive flexibility, without over-synchronizing to the point of inducing a seizure. This is consistent with a previous report that found an inverted-U relationship between CM activity and working memory performance. Low and high levels of deep brain stimulation in this region disrupted working memory, a prefrontal cortex-dependent process, and median stimulation facilitated working memory (Mair and Hembrook, 2008). Thus, the CM input to lOFC may be necessary for arousal and attention via synchronization of neural activity and dampening excessive activity to enable successful reversal learning.

By manipulating the PVT to lOFC pathway, we observed a significant sex difference in its role in mediating the detrimental effects of stress on reversal learning. Activating the PVT to lOFC pathway in non-stressed rats disrupted reversal learning only in males, and we saw the same sex-specific effect of inhibiting the PVT to lOFC projection in reversing the effects of stress on reversal learning. The PVT is activated by both acute and chronic stressors (Bhatnagar et al., 2002, 2003; Bhatnagar and Dallman, 1998). However, activation of the PVT, particularly the posterior PVT, plays a complex role in the stress response. Our DREADD-based manipulations of the PVT were acute, and there is evidence to suggest that the role of the PVT in the effects of stress shifts as stress transitions from acute to chronic (Rowson and Pleil, 2021). Corticosterone acts in the PVT to inhibit the HPA axis response, and chronic activation of the posterior PVT is associated with decreased stress response and anxiety-like behavior (Careaga et al., 2019; Jaferi and Bhatnagar, 2006; Salomé et al., 2004). Thus, chronic or repeated manipulations in the PVT-OFC pathway might resolve the sex differences observed with our acute treatments. The PVT has largely been studied in males and there are few, if any, studies directly comparing the role of PVT in behavior or the stress response in males and females. However, it has been shown that the posterior PVT has a higher threshold for activation in females than in males (Ishii et al., 2018; Rowson and Pleil, 2021; Ver Hoeve et al., 2013), which could explain why our chemogenetic manipulations had no effect in females, although CUS compromised reversal learning in both males and females. Our results suggest that stress-activated PVT input to lOFC may be the route by which CUS impaired reversal learning in males, but this pathway does not appear to play a role in mediating the detrimental effects of stress on reversal learning in females.

We applied CUS for 3 weeks in females, compared to 2 weeks in males, to achieve similar deficits in cognitive flexibility in both sexes. Given that the PVT is highly stress-responsive, it is possible the different timing of CUS in males and females could have contributed to the sex-specific effects of PVT activation observed in this study. However, previous results from our laboratory and other results observed in the present study make this unlikely. We previously reported similar effects on both cognitive set-shifting and on electrical responses evoked in the medial prefrontal cortex by stimulating the mediodorsal thalamic afferent (Paredes et al., 2022a) after 2 and 3 weeks of CUS in males and females, respectively. Further, in the present study, the effects of CUS on reversal learning were comparable in males and females, as were the effects of manipulating the CM. More definitively, the sex-specific effects of PVT manipulations were also observed in non-stressed animals. Together, these results indicate that the differences in effect of PVT manipulations are due to a sex difference in the function of the PVT-lOFC pathway.

Our data suggest that in females, an afferent to the lOFC other than the PVT might be responsible for the detrimental effects of chronic stress on reversal learning. The suggestion that male and female brains may utilize different regions and projections to mediate the effects of stress on cognition is supported by a study reporting similar sex difference in regions mediating the effects of stress on learning, specifically involving the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) (Bangasser and Shors, 2008). They found that the BNST mediated the effects of stress on eyeblink conditioning in males and masculinized females, but not in naturally-cycling females, suggesting that circulating female sex hormones may regulate the function of these brain regions. The PVT projects to the BNST (Hua et al., 2018; Levine et al., 2021), and estrogen receptors are expressed in both regions (Heck and Handa, 2019), indicating that effects of estrogen in altering the function of the PVT in naturally-cycling females may have contributed to the sex differences we observed.

In conclusion, the results of these studies showed decreased Fos induction in the lOFC during reversal learning and increased Fos in two thalamic nuclei that project to the lOFC, the CM and PVT. Chemogenetic inhibition of the CM-OFC pathway disrupted reversal learning, indicating a role for this pathway in promoting successful reversal learning. Chronic stress disrupted reversal learning and induced ΔFosB accumulation in both the CM and PVT cells projecting to the OFC, indicating these projections are repeatedly activated by chronic stress. Activation of the glutamatergic CM input to lOFC in stressed rats restored reversal learning. By contrast, activation of the PVT-OFC pathway in non-stressed rats impaired reversal learning, but only in males, and inactivation of the PVT-OFC pathway reversed the detrimental effects of chronic stress on reversal learning, again only in males, indicating a sex-specific role of the PVT-OFC pathway in the cognitive deficits produced by chronic stress. These results provide new insight into the OFC circuits necessary for optimal reversal learning, and illustrate that they are disrupted by chronic stress in a sex-specific manner. This may help identify mechanisms underlying the differential sensitivity of females and males to specific stressors and potentially identify new targets to treat stress-related psychiatric disorders.
