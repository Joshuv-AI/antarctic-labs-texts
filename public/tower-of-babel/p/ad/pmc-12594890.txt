SHAP-based binarization enhances metataxonomic machine learning with application to gut microbiota of inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Youngro Lee, Jongmo Seo, Barbara Di Camillo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine learning has been increasingly applied to microbiome data for biomarker discovery. However, microbiome datasets are typically high-dimensional, sparse, and correlated, which makes model training challenging and prone to overfitting. Previous studies have also reported that microbiome features exhibit binary-like characteristics, and that binarization does not necessarily reduce predictive performance. This observation motivated our work. Building on this idea, we propose a SHAP-based binarization pipeline. We first trained several machine learning models on raw continuous data and selected the best-performing model (random forest). Using SHAP values derived from the training set, we determined feature-specific thresholds that best separated positive and negative contributions. The dataset was then binarized using these thresholds and new models were trained on the transformed data. We evaluated this approach on gut microbiome abundance data (283 species, 220 genera, 1,569 individuals) to classify inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) versus healthy controls. The SHAP-based binarization consistently improved classification performance and interpretability compared with both continuous data and zero-threshold binarization. The best model’s Matthews correlation coefficient increased from 0.884 to 0.928, with the largest improvements observed in non-tree-based models such as logistic regression and neural networks. SHAP summary plots also revealed clearer feature patterns, and biomarker rankings were more stable. In addition, the pipeline enabled us to identify a concise set of 17 microbial biomarkers associated with IBD. This study introduces a novel approach for microbiome data analysis by explicitly linking binarization thresholds to SHAP-derived feature contributions. Our approach was grounded in the observation of binary-like patterns revealed through SHAP values. Furthermore, although binarization inevitably raises concerns about information loss, our evaluation confirmed improvements not only in predictive performance but also in interpretability and biomarker stability, providing a broader validation of robustness. These findings highlight SHAP-based binarization as an effective strategy for high-dimensional microbiome data, with broad applicability and opportunities for future extension.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24802-1.Keywords: SHAP, Biomarker selection, Microbiome, IBDSubject terms: Biomarkers, Computational biology and bioinformatics

Introduction

Challenges in Microbiome machine learning

In the last decade, advances in Whole Genome Shotgun sequencing (WGS) and 16 S ribosomal RNA sequencing (16 S rDNA-seq) have enabled comprehensive profiling of microbial communities at different taxonomic levels1,2. The resulting count matrix data, representing the relative abundance of taxa within each sample, have been widely used to build machine learning (ML) models for biomarker discovery in diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and colorectal cancer3–7. However, microbial abundance datasets are inherently high-dimensional and sparse, often comprising thousands of correlated features but a limited number of samples8,9. This imbalance makes predictive models prone to overfitting and limits their generalizability.

To address this, many studies have adopted feature filtering or selection methods, such as removing low-variance or low-abundance taxa, or using regularized models like ridge, lasso, and elastic net10. Wrapper-based approaches have also been proposed to identify robust microbial biomarkers11–14, and feature transformation prior to selection has been explored to enhance robustness15. Nevertheless, these methods require repeated computation, may discard biologically relevant taxa, and do not necessarily improve interpretability.

Binary characteristics of microbiome data

Recent literature has suggested that microbial data may inherently exhibit binary-like characteristics. Giliberti et al.16 demonstrated that predictive performance can often be driven by the presence or absence of specific microbial taxa rather than their relative abundance. Similarly, Asher et al.17 and Chang et al.18 showed that models trained on binary presence/absence profiles achieved comparable accuracy to those trained on continuous abundances, while Mainali et al.19 provided a theoretical framework for analyzing microbial co-occurrence using binary matrices. Collectively, these studies indicate that binarization can simplify high-dimensional microbiome data without substantially compromising classification accuracy, suggesting the existence of an underlying binary structure.

Information loss and limitations of existing approaches

Despite these advantages, binarization may also lead to a loss of quantitative information. Giliberti et al. acknowledged this trade-off, noting that the effect appeared negligible for classification performance16. However, this finding primarily pertains to predictive tasks. For mechanistic or ecological analyses, the discarded abundance gradients may still carry biologically meaningful signals. Hu et al.20 further observed that presence–absence transformations can capture associations driven by rare taxa but necessarily omit information about their relative magnitudes. Therefore, while binarization is advantageous in high-dimensional biomarker discovery settings, existing approaches often rely on fixed or empirically chosen thresholds, which limit systematic evaluation of how information loss or threshold choice affects each feature21,22. These considerations highlight the need for a more rigorous, data-driven approach to determine optimal thresholds.

SHAP-based observation and motivation for the proposed method

To address this need, we explored SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis as a model-agnostic tool to investigate whether binary-like patterns could be directly observed from trained ML models. SHAP values, derived from cooperative game theory, attribute the contribution of each feature to a model’s prediction23,24. When visualized across samples after a machine-learning model training, we observed that many microbial features exhibited clear sign changes in SHAP values, suggesting intrinsic thresholds that separate positive and negative contributions to the specific prediction under analysis. Moreover, these thresholds varied across features, indicating that each taxon may possess a distinct decision boundary. Building on this observation, we propose a SHAP-based binarization framework that determines optimal, feature-specific thresholds directly from SHAP distributions.

Overview of the proposed approach and its advantages

In this framework, we first trained several ML models using continuous microbial abundance data and selected the best-performing model (random forest). SHAP values from the training data were then used to determine feature-specific thresholds, which were applied to binarize the dataset for subsequent model retraining (Fig. 1). We evaluated this approach on gut microbiome data (283 species and 220 genera from 1569 individuals) to classify IBD and healthy controls25–27.

The SHAP-based binarization consistently improved predictive performance, interpretability, and biomarker stability compared with both continuous and zero-threshold binarization. Our approach builds on prior conceptual findings regarding binary characteristics of microbiome data and is empirically supported by the observed SHAP patterns, providing methodological and experimental justification for binarization. Although binarization has been associated with potential information loss, our analyses indicate that SHAP-guided thresholding can improve model robustness and interpretability while maintaining biological relevance. This approach offers a practical and broadly applicable framework for high-dimensional microbiome data and may inform the development of related methods in other omics contexts.

Methods

Data preprocessing

Collection of datasets and preprocessing methods referred our previous study in Lee et al.15. Three different abundance metrics of gut microbiome were collected from Qitta25–27 as in Table 1. Each dataset reported the state of IBD in each sample. Trimming (QIIMEq2 1.9.1) and Pick closed-reference OTUs (QIIMEq2 1.9.1)28 were applied. Features with a missing ratio higher than 0.99 were deleted, and each feature value was divided by the geometric mean, according to clr transformation29.

We then merged the 3 datasets considering, from the entire set of features, only the ones common throughout all datasets. In this way, we collected a set of features with 283 taxa at the species level and 220 at the genus level15. Total data were split in training (80%) and test (20%) set in every 5-fold cross validation (Fig. 1). In each 5-fold cross-validation, standard scaling was applied to the training dataset before conducting the machine learning experiments, but only when the experiment was performed on continuous datasets.

In addition to the combined dataset described above, we included an external dataset that shares the same feature space but unfortunately does not provide outcome labels30. Although class labels are not available, the use of this external dataset al.lows us to assess whether the thresholds derived from the training dataset generalize consistently to an independent source of data. The same preprocessing steps were applied, including feature alignment and clr transformation, to ensure comparability with the main dataset.

Prediction models

We utilized the list of prediction models from our prior research15 to evaluate the variance in machine learning performance. Eight distinct prediction models were developed using a 5-fold cross-validation approach for parameter tuning: logistic regression, linear SVM, Random Forest, XGBoost, Perceptron, and MLPs with 1, 2, or 3 hidden layers31. Hyperparameters were tuned every time in each 5-fold cross validation. Tuning was done by 5-fold cross validation of training dataset, selecting the hyperparameter with the best performance. Hyperparameters without grid search were set as the default value in scikit-learn library31. The following sections provide brief details on the different ML methods employed.

Logistic Regression generates prediction scores ranging from 0 to 1 by applying a logistic function to linear regression outputs. This study utilized regularized logistic regression with an L2 penalty, known as Ridge Regression. The lbfgs solver with a tolerance parameter set at 1e-4 optimized the model31. The regularization parameter was adjusted using a grid of values: 2 raised to the power of (– 5, – 3, – 1, 1, 3, 5).

Linear SVM separates data linearly by maximizing the margin between two classes. To prevent overfitting, a squared L2 penalty was applied. The ‘rbf’ kernel was employed, and ‘gamma’ was set to ‘scale’31. The regularization parameter was tuned using a grid ranging from 2^(-5) to 2^15, with steps of 2.

Random Forest constructs multiple decision trees and employs bagging to create an uncorrelated forest of trees for improved prediction. Gini impurity was used to determine splits, with no limit on maximum depth, allowing nodes to expand until all leaves were pure or contained fewer samples than the ‘min_samples_split’ parameter. Both ‘min_samples_split’ and ‘min_samples_leaf’ were set to two, with the number of features for the best split set to the square root of the number of total features. The forest comprised 100 trees.

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a tree-ensemble algorithm using gradient boosting to combine multiple trees. The XGBRegressor was trained with 200 gradient-boosted trees, each having a maximum depth of 20. The training instance subsample ratio and the column subsample ratio for each tree were set as 0.2 and 0.5 for each. The minimum loss reduction for partitioning a leaf node was set to 1. The learning rate was adjusted using a grid of 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.5. The regularization factor alpha (L1 regularization term) was tuned with a grid of 1e-3, 1e-2, 0.1, 1, and 10.

Perceptron is a single-layer neural network that predicts outputs using combinations of input values, weights, and biases. Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) is a feedforward neural network with multiple hidden layers. The LBFGS optimizer, which minimizes the squared error function, was used to optimize the weight variables. ReLU function was used for the activation function. Training stopped once the loss score fell below 1e-4. The learning rate and L2 regularization term were tuned by the same grid of XGBoost. The size of hidden layer was set to the number of features. With this setting, MLPs with 1, 2, or 3 hidden layers were constructed.

Evaluation metrics

To evaluate classification performance, seven different indices were calculated: Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, Positive Predictive Value (PPV), and Negative Predictive Value (NPV).

Among these performance metrics, MCC32 was employed to select the best-performing algorithm due to its ability to handle imbalanced datasets and provide a more comprehensive assessment of model performance and because MCC considers all four quadrants of the confusion matrix, giving a more holistic view of the model’s performance:

The other metrics used are briefly described in what follows: AUC (Area Under ROC Curve) is determined by calculating the area under the ROC curve. This curve is a graph where the x-axis represents the False Positive Rate and the y-axis represents the True Positive Rate. Accuracy signifies the ratio of correct predictions, which is the proportion of accurate predictions among the entire set of samples. Sensitivity and Specificity denote the proportion of correct predictions among positive and negative samples respectively. This is calculated by dividing TP by the total number of positive samples for sensitivity, and TN by the total number of negative samples for specificity. PPV and NPV indicate the proportion of correct predictions among positive and negative predictions respectively. They represent the ratio of TP among the total positive predictions and TN among the total negative predictions.

Data binarization

This procedure was performed after training the best-performing model on continuous data to extract SHAP values, and prior to retraining new models on the binarized datasets.

For each fold in the 5-fold cross-validation, SHAP values were computed from the best-performing algorithm (Random Forest) using the TreeExplainer implementation on the entire training set. Threshold optimization was performed independently within each fold to avoid information leakage, and the resulting thresholds were applied only to the corresponding validation and test subsets.

For each feature, all unique observed values in the training data were used as candidate thresholds θ, preserving the empirical resolution of each variable. At each θ, training samples were divided into two groups according to whether their feature value was greater or less than θ. Here we define Greater_set the set of SHAP values for which the feature value is greater than or equal to θ and Lower_set the set of SHAP values for which the feature value is lower than θ. This improvement was particularly evident when the scores were calculated using the top-ranked features (top 10–50), suggesting that the learned thresholds better captured the most informative taxa.

When ML methods were retrained on binarized data, almost all methods showed a significant improvement in the performance setting the threshold to 0 or to the SHAP based θ (Eq. 2). In total we run 112 comparisons (# of taxonomy level × # of machine learning algorithm × # of performance indexes = 2 × 8 × 7 = 112) and performance after binarization were better with respect to those obtained using the continuous original data in 106 comparison out of 112.

Models retrained using MLP with 1 hidden layer after binarization based on SHAP values were used to analyze the selected features. MLP with 1 hidden layer was chosen since it showed the best performance in every performance index at both species and genus level after binarization (Table 2, Supplementary Tables 1 to 6). Biomarkers were selected at the species level experiment as species level is a deeper taxonomy than genus level.

The number of biomarkers should be decided by considering both model accuracy and generalizability potential. To select a small yet high-performing set of biomarkers, we analyzed the performance pattern based on the number of features, as shown in Fig. 5. Discussion

Outline of the study

In this study, we propose a thresholding method for binarizing microbiome data to be given as input to machine learning methods. Our proposed methodology followed this pipeline: (i) We constructed machine learning models using various algorithms and identified the best-performing one; (ii) For each feature, we determined the SHAP-based threshold calculated using the best performing model on the training dataset exclusively; (iii) We binarized the datasets using the computed optimal thresholds; (iv) Finally, we trained machine learning models on the binarized datasets and selected the best-performing model. The proposed approach brought significant enhancements in both performance and interpretability although simplification through binarization necessarily eliminates information that cannot be represented by a step function.

Details about performance improvement

We compared our results with those obtained using a simple zero thresholding. In general, performance improved more with SHAP-based binarization compared to zero-based binarization. However, the improvement was not as pronounced in sensitivity and NPV compared to other performance metrics. False Negatives (FN) are in the denominator of both sensitivity and NPV, whereas False Positives (FP) in the denominator of specificity and PPV. Therefore, a possible interpretation is that biomarkers needed to distinguish FP from TP are partially missing when using the zero-based binarization.

Comparative performance of tree ensembles and MLPs

It is worth noting that binarization tends to not improve much the performance of tree-based methods whereas all other tested ML methods improved. Upon examining the reasons for the shift in algorithm superiority, we can deduce the strengths of tree ensemble algorithms on the raw dataset. Although the original dataset consisted of continuous values, it exhibited a binomial characteristic16, which might align well with the decision tree algorithm’s ability to split feature values in a categorical manner. On the other hand, MLP and other predictive algorithms used in this paper considered feature values from a continuous perspective, making them more susceptible to overfitting. However, by binarizing the dataset without losing information about the target disease, the risk of overfitting might have been mitigated due to lesser complexity. This advantage of binarization helped to address the weaknesses of MLP and other predictive algorithms when compared to tree ensemble algorithms.

Empirical evaluation of MLP-based thresholds

To further examine whether thresholds derived from MLPs would yield different results, we repeated the pipeline using a 1 hidden-layer MLP. However, the outcomes confirmed our theoretical expectation: (1) the scatter plots (Supplementary Fig. 5a) did not display binary-like separation but rather a smooth quadratic curve; (2) the SHAP summary plots (Supplementary Fig. 5b) provided little interpretability improvement compared with the main pipeline (Fig. 3e); and (3) predictive performance declined across all models (Supplementary Table 9).

This outcome can be understood in light of the mechanism of neural networks, as described in the previous section. Unlike tree ensembles, which explicitly partition the feature space at discrete thresholds, MLPs approximate relationships through continuous weighted summations and activation functions. As a result, even if the underlying data exhibits binary characteristics, MLPs tend to smooth them into continuous curves rather than identify sharp cut-points, leading to quadratic patterns in scatter plots and a lack of improvement in interpretability or predictive performance. From the perspective of binarization, this suggests that tree ensembles may be more compatible with threshold-based transformations than neural networks.

Limits of ML-Based biomarker discovery in biological context

Figure 6 summarizes the 17 candidate biomarkers for IBD identified by our algorithm, and Supplementary Table 8 provides literature references for each taxon. Some biomarkers aligned with previously reported directions, others showed the opposite trend, and a few were not directly linked to IBD in prior studies.

Before interpreting these results from a biological perspective, it is critical to acknowledge that ML methods reflects predictive contributions in a model rather than mechanistic causality. As such, a feature may appear predictive in one direction within the model while experimental studies report the opposite. This does not necessarily imply contradiction but rather reflects the multifactorial nature of biomarker interpretation. For instance, within ML models, the contribution of a feature can shift depending on its interaction with other features, leading to apparent reversals when considered in isolation versus in combination. In addition, discrepancies may stem from biological and experimental contexts, such as cohort-specific factors (geography, diet, treatment), differences in sampling (mucosal vs. stool), or taxonomic resolution (genus vs. species, or strain-level variation).

Consequently, our findings should be regarded as a set of candidates for further biological investigation, not as definitive mechanistic explanations. While a comprehensive mechanistic validation of each taxon is beyond the scope of this methodological paper, we believe providing a concise literature overview still offers value. It helps situate the identified taxa within the broader microbiome–IBD context and enables microbiologists to further explore and validate these candidates.

Future direction

This study has several clear limitations that also suggest meaningful directions for future research.

Although the external dataset analysis demonstrated the consistency of feature-wise thresholds, further validation would be required across independent microbiome datasets. Future work should apply the entire pipeline to cohorts derived from different populations, diseases, and environmental conditions to evaluate whether the proposed SHAP-based binarization maintains robustness under variable settings. In particular, it would be important to assess whether threshold stability and interpretability are preserved even in models with lower predictive performance, where signal-to-noise ratios or data sparsity may limit model reliability51.

While the proposed optimization mitigates information loss compared with conventional binarization, some degree of loss is inevitable when continuous signals are binarized. Future studies could explore hybrid approaches that allow partial relaxation of binary constraints, identifying features or conditions where near-binary but continuous patterns persist, and incorporating such information adaptively into the binarization framework52.
