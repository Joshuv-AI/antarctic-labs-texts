Genomic adaptations of novel halotolerant bacteria from extreme North Greenland
Authors: Miguel Ángel Salinas-García, Anders Priemé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeary Land, northern-most Greenland, is a cold desert region whose soil microbiome remains underexplored. This is the first study to explore the halotolerant soil microbiome of this region with culture-dependent methods. Forty-nine taxonomically diverse bacterial isolates were obtained from dry soil biocrust, surface soil, and permafrost soil. Nine isolates were selected for whole genome sequencing; these genomes showed adaptations to their cold, saline native environment, such as cold shock proteins and a large number of osmoprotectant transporters. The genomes also contained several biosynthetic gene clusters, including a large variety of clusters putatively involved in the production of antimicrobial compounds. Furthermore, most of the sequenced genomes showed low digital DNA–DNA hybridization to their closest relatives, suggesting that they represent novel species. Overall, this study demonstrates the unexplored potential of High Arctic deserts as a source of novel halotolerant bacteria and their secondary metabolites.

Introduction

Peary Land in extreme North Greenland is the northern-most landmass of our planet. The interior of this remote and uninhabited peninsula is characterized by very sparse vegetation, very low precipitation (<250 mm) and very low temperatures (annual mean temperature between −20°C and −15°C) (Mølgaard 1982). Due to the low precipitation, most of Peary Land is ice-free and vegetation is mainly found along the moist northern coast bordering the icy Arctic Ocean and in small depressions in the landscape fed by water from melting snowpack.

Due to the low precipitation and sparse vegetation, the soils in Peary Land are generally thin, alkaline, potentially salt-rich, and poor in carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus (Stimmler et al. 2022) (Table 1). Surface soils of the High Arctic desert of Peary Land tend to harden and crack due to lack of water and evaporation, creating the typical patterns found in deserts (Charlier 1969). In contrast to warm or hot deserts, UV radiation is relatively low, with noon UV index averages of 2–3 in July (Acker and Leptoukh 2007, Hovila et al. 2009).

Permafrost soil remains frozen for at least two consecutive years and is covered by the active layer, i.e. soil that thaws during summer. Despite the frozen conditions, permafrost soils are home to a large diversity of microorganisms (Jansson and Taş 2014). In permafrost and other frozen environments, brine veins form due to salt fractionation (Devoie et al. 2024). In contrast to the bulk permafrost, nutrients and microorganisms can move within these brines, enabling microbial activity. These brines can reach salt concentrations near saturation, but nevertheless provide a liquid environment for microorganisms to inhabit (Steven et al. 2006). Consequently, many organisms isolated from permafrost soils are halophilic or halotolerant (Niederberger et al. 2009, Shcherbakova et al. 2009, Spirina et al. 2017). Permafrost soils are potential analogues of Martian environments, and are thus of interest in astrobiology (Miner et al. 2023); in addition, some studies have used permafrost isolates as models of Martian life (Vishnivetskaya et al. 2003, Nicholson et al. 2013). Furthermore, enzymes and metabolites from permafrost isolates could have commercial applications in, e.g. the food and pharmaceutical industries (Flores-Gallegos et al. 2019, Parvizpour et al. 2021).

Biocrusts are common in many ecosystems, including hot and cold deserts, and have been defined as communities of organisms dwelling on the soil surface. These communities may consist of both primary producers (e.g. cyanobacteria, algae, lichens, and bryophytes) and heterotrophic organisms (e.g. bacteria, fungi, and archaea), and aggregate soil particles because of their filamentous structure, depending on the organism, as well as by secreting extracellular polymeric substances. This bio-engineered aggregation makes biocrusts distinct from physical soil crusts. (Weber et al. 2022). Biocrusts prevent soil erosion and retain moisture—properties relevant in dry regions, where biocrusts play an important role in soil stability (Guida et al. 2023).

Polar desert soils and biocrusts typically experience low water activity for several reasons. Despite low evaporation rates due to low temperatures (Gold and Bliss 1995), salt particles deposited on the surface by the wind may not be washed away due to a lack of precipitation, leading to an accumulation of salt (Geyer et al. 2013). Furthermore, freezing temperatures limit the presence of liquid water, leading to the formation of brines due to salt fractionation.

Microbial life in Peary Land soils require special adaptations to the low temperatures, low water activity and low nutrient conditions; including modifications to cellular membranes, proteins, genetic material, and metabolism (Sarma et al. 2023). The extremophiles that thrive under these conditions are known as psychrophiles or psychrotolerant (organisms that grow below 10°C) and xerotolerant (organisms that can grow at water activity below 0.8, where 1 is pure water) (Lebre et al. 2017). Halophiles are a group of xerotolerants that have evolved to grow under high salt conditions with low water activity (Stevenson et al. 2015). Both psychrotolerant and halotolerant organisms often have common adaptations, such as the accumulation of compatible solutes to maintain osmotic balance; this is why many psychrotolerant organisms are found to be halotolerant at the same time (Devoie et al. 2024). Indeed, halophilic microorganisms have previously been isolated from polar desert soils, such as the McMurdo Valleys in Antarctica (Miller et al. 1983, Miller and Leschine 1984, Antibus et al. 2012), Canadian permafrost (Steven et al. 2007), and brines in polar regions such as Canada (Crisler et al. 2019, Gagnon et al. 2023) and Antarctica (Tregoning et al. 2015). While there are some studies where bacteria were isolated from Greenlandic soils and permafrost (Ganzert et al. 2014), we could only find one study focused on halophiles, in which 10 halophilic bacterial strains were isolated from a glacier moraine in northwestern Greenland (Yukimura et al. 2009). Thus, our work here contributes significantly to the catalogue of known halotolerant and halophilic bacteria inhabiting Greenland’s arctic desert soils and permafrost. The isolates presented here also represent, to our knowledge, the northernmost halotolerant and halophilic bacterial strains isolated to date.

Previous studies have shown that the Greenland Ice Sheet is a promising source of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) (Jaarsma et al. 2023). With this study, we aim to extend this potential to different Peary Land soils, whose microbiome remains underexplored and may harbour novel bacterial isolates with potential applications in biotechnology. We hypothesize that (i) permafrost soil, dry soil biocrust, and surface soil in Peary Land are inhabited by novel culturable halophilic and halotolerant bacteria, (ii) the genomes of these bacteria reveal adaptations to life at low water activity, and (iii) Northern Greenland represents a potential source of secondary bacterial metabolites.

Materials and methods

Sampling sites

Mean air annual temperature (MAT) and precipitation (MAP) values listed here are the average values for the period 1970–2000, obtained from the ERA5 hourly time-series data on single levels dataset (Copernicus Climate Change Service 2025), unless stated otherwise. All samples were stored in sterile 118 ml Whirl-Pak bags (Merck Denmark, Søborg, Denmark) at 4°C after collection.

The Kap København formation (MAT: −16.3°C, MAP: 254 mm; sampled on 26 July 2021 at N82.341460, W21.256961, 160 m above sea level, m.a.s.l.) (Fig. 1a), consists of a 100-m thick succession of shallow marine sediments which were deposited during an interglacial period 2 million years ago. The deposits were later uplifted and exposed subaerially about 1 million years ago, at which time the deposits became permafrost (Fernandez-Guerra et al. 2023). Samples were extracted ca. 4 m below the surface accessed from the side of the formation, which was exposed due to recent erosion (Fig. 1a). First, we removed a few cm of unfrozen soil with a shovel and then we chiselled away ca. 30 cm of frozen material using a hammer and a sterile shovel. From these holes, we chiselled out frozen soil clumps of a minimum of 5 × 5 × 5 cm, which were immediately split open with a sterile chisel to expose surfaces of uncontaminated permafrost. Ten subsamples (~50–100 g each) were chiselled from the exposed surfaces and immediately transferred to sterile bags. The permafrost samples were allowed to thaw at 4°C and transported at the same temperature. Thus, the transportation time served as part of the pre-incubation of the samples before initiating growth on agar plates (see ‘Strain Isolation’). These subsamples were named KK1–KK10.

The area up-stream of Citronen Fjord (Fig. 1b) is desert-like with a MAT of −20.2°C, MAP of 162 mm, and very sparse vegetation (<1% cover) consisting of scattered specimens of Arctic Willow (Salix arctica) and Entire-leaved Mountain Avens (Dryas integrifolia). Here, four samples of dry soil biocrust (~100–200 g each) were obtained from a site devoid of vegetation (28 July 2021, N83.085782, W28.2496755, 28 m.a.s.l.). Four samples of biological soil crust were scooped from the surface using a sterile spoon and transferred to sterile bags. These samples were named CF1–CF4.

Due to its proximity to the Arctic Ocean, Cape Morris Jessup (Fig. 1c) harbours more dense vegetation (5%–15% ground cover) compared to Citronen Fjord. In the ERA5 dataset, Cape Morris Jessup’s MAT was −14.1°C and was MAP is 347 mm for the period 1970–2000. The only weather station in Peary Land, WMO 04301 in Cape Morris Jessup, reported a mean annual temperature of −14°C, mean annual precipitation of 234 mm, and temperature maxima of around 12°C in the summer for the period 2020–2023 (Meteostat). The vegetation is dominated by Purple Saxifrage (Saxifraga oppositifolia) and Arctic Willow. On 27 July 2021, at N83.657236, W33.368495, 5 m.a.s.l., we sampled dry soil biocrust (as described above) and soil from 0 to 4 cm depth by hammering sterile steel corers (4.5 cm diameter) into the soil, both in triplicate. A subsample from each soil core (~100–200 g each) was transferred to sterile bags. These samples were named CMC1–CMC3 for the biocrust samples, and CMS1–CMS3 for the soil samples.

Soil parameters were also measured (Table 1). Conductivity (as a proxy for salinity) and pH were measured from 1:5 and 1:2 soil:water slurries, respectively, as described elsewhere (Geyer et al. 2013). Water content was determined by drying 3–5 g of sample at 105°C for 36 h and measuring weight loss. Soil organic matter was determined by the loss on ignition method by burning dry samples at 450°C for 6 h. We chose this temperature to avoid carbonate decomposition, which happens above 500°C (Kanagaraj et al. 2025). Nevertheless, we note that hydrated minerals that lose water at 450°C may have been present in the samples; thus, this method may have overestimated the amount of organic matter in the samples (Kanagaraj et al. 2025). The conductivity of the samples is comparable to low-to-moderate soils from other polar environments (Geyer et al. 2013, Van Horn et al. 2014).

Strain isolation

The samples were incubated in their sampling bags for 4°C for between 1 and 3 months. This incubation at low temperatures, or cold enrichment, has been shown to increase the number of isolated bacteria from permafrost (Vishnivetskaya et al. 2000). This incubation times includes the travel time from the sample sites in Peary Land to Copenhagen, as well as further storage at 4°C in our laboratories.

After pre-incubation, the samples were plated on agar plates. Under sterile conditions, ~1 g of soil was suspended in 1 ml of phosphate-buffered saline solution supplemented with 10% NaCl. This buffer was chosen to initially select for bacteria that were resistant to shock from high osmolarity. After gentle mixing for 5 min, the samples were briefly centrifuged to remove solid particles from suspension. One hundred microlitres of the supernatant was plated on four different solid media (see below) and left to grow. For the permafrost samples, the supernatant was diluted 100 and 1000 times prior to plating. The plates were incubated at either 4°C, 10°C, or 25°C, and checked weekly and daily, respectively. New colonies were randomly selected based on unique morphologies, plated into fresh agar plates of the same medium, and incubated again. After incubation, a single colony was picked, plated in the same medium, and incubated. This process was repeated several times until only a single type of colony was observed, at which point we considered that the culture was axenic. Single colonies from the axenic plates were transferred into liquid medium of the same kind (except GMA, see below), and incubated overnight. One millilitre of these cultures was mixed with a glycerol stock (20% v/v final concentration) and frozen at −70°C for long-term storage.

All samples were plated in four different types of media. For isolation of halotolerant and halophilic bacteria, three saline media were used. The first was a modified HM medium (Ventosa et al. 1982), referred to in this manuscript HM10 for its 10% w/v content of NaCl, and containing, in g l−1: NaCl (100), MgSO4·7H2O (1), CaCl2·2H2O (0.36), KCl (2), KBr (0.27), FeCl3·6H2O (0.002), NaHCO3 (0.06), Proteose-peptone no. 3 (5), yeast extract (10), glucose (1), and Bacto agar (20, when applicable) adjusted to pH 7.8–8. The second was a medium devised for this study named OHAM (Oligotrophic Halophilic Actinobacteria Medium) containing, in g l−1: soluble starch (20), KNO3 (0.2), NaCl (100), MgSO4·7H2O (0.5), K2HPO4 (0.5), FeSO4·7H2O (0.01), sodium pyruvate (0.05) and Bacto agar (15, when applicable), and a final pH of 7.2. The third was R2A medium (Sigma–Aldrich, Germany) supplemented with 10% NaCl.

In addition, a non-saline medium, Gauze Medium 1 (GM1), was also used, containing in g/l: Soluble starch (20), KNO3 (1), NaCl (0.5), MgSO4·7H2O (0.5), K2HPO4 (0.5), FeSO4·7H2O (0.01), and bacto agar (15, when applicable). Final pH was adjusted to 7.2–7.4. This medium has been used in previous works to isolate bacterial strains from permafrost (Wang et al. 2018). We were unable to make liquid GM1, as the starch precipitated quickly after autoclaving. Thus, strains isolated in this medium were grown in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) to produce −70°C stocks. One strain (CMS1.3) was only able to grow on GM1; to produce a −70°C stock, biomass from a 1–2 day old plate was scraped into a solution of 20% v/v glycerol.

Premade marine agar (MarA, Sigma–Aldrich, Germany) and TSB supplemented with agar (TSA, Sigma–Aldrich, Germany) were used for routine culturing of the isolates after axenic cultures were obtained.

Determination of the effect of NaCl on growth

To test the NaCl tolerance of the selected isolates, 96-well plates were prepared with HM supplemented with different concentrations of NaCl, from 0% (w/v) to 20% in increments of 2.5%. The plates were inoculated with overnight cultures to a constant initial OD600 in triplicate for each combination of strain and NaCl concentration, and blanks were made for each different NaCl concentration. The plates were then incubated at 25°C in a BioTek Synergy H1 Multimode Reader (Agilent, USA) for 5 days with constant rotation and OD measurements every 30 min. Range of growth was determined based on the observed growth in the wells at the end of the experiment. To calculate the effect of NaCl on growth, blanks were subtracted and each replicate was fit to a Gompertz model using the R package growthrates (Petzoldt 2016) version 0.8.4. The resulting parameters were used to calculate the area under the curve using the R package pracma (Borchers 2011) version 2.4.4.

rRNA gene sequencing and analysis

Total DNA was isolated from overnight cultures using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The 16S rRNA gene was then amplified with the PCRBio kit (PCR Biosystems, UK) using the universal primers 27F (5′-AGA GTT TGA TCM TGG CTC AG-3′) and 1492R (5′-ACG GYT ACC TTG TTA CGA CTT-3′) purchased from TAG Copenhagen (www.tagc. com). After the size and quality of the amplicons was assessed with electrophoresis on agarose gel and ethidium bromide staining, the amplified product was sent for Sanger sequencing to Eurofins (Germany) with the same primers, to obtain both forward and reverse reads.

The forward and reverse reads were aligned in MEGA11 (Tamura et al. 2021) using the MUSCLE algorithm (Edgar 2004) to produce the near full-length 16S rRNA gene sequences. Then, BlastN (Sayers et al. 2022) was used to identify the strains to the genus level; this search was restricted to type strain sequences. To study phylogenetic relationships, an alignment file was produced in MEGA11 using the MUSCLE algorithm with the near full-length 16S rRNA gene sequences and the full-length 16S rRNA gene sequences of related type strains. A phylogenetic tree was constructed with this alignment file using IQ-TREE 2 (Minh et al. 2020) with ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) and ultrafast bootstrap (Hoang et al. 2018). Lastly, the tree was drawn with FigTree v1.4.4 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/) and edited using Inkscape v 1.3.2 (https://inkscape.org/).

Genome sequencing and assembly

Total genomic DNA was isolated from overnight cultures using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Short read libraries were prepared using the DNA-Seq Enz kit for MagicPrep (Tecan, USA). The quality of the libraries was checked using a 5200 Fragment Analyzer System (Agilent, USA) before sequencing of the DNA fragments using Miseq2 (Illumina, Germany). The quality of the reads was examined with FastQC v.0.12.0 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and Trimmomatic v0.5.1 (Bolger et al. 2014) was used to remove adapter sequences and low quality reads. Unicycler v0.5.1 (Wick et al. 2017) was used with SPAdes v4.0.0 (Prjibelski et al. 2020) to assemble the genomes. The basic statistics and quality of the assemblies were determined with QUAST v5.2 (Mikheenko et al. 2018), checkM v1.2.3 (Parks et al. 2015), and BUSCO v5.7.1 (Manni et al. 2021). To sequence the genome of Arthrobacter halodurans DSM 21081T, genomic DNA was directly ordered from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ) and sequenced as described above.

Genome analysis

The phylogeny of the assembled genomes was determined with the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS) at DSMZ (tygs.dsmz.de), with nomenclature provided by the List of Prokaryotic Names with Standing in Nomenclature (LPSN) (Meier-Kolthoff and Göker 2019, Meier-Kolthoff et al. 2022). TYGS calculates DNA–DNA-Hybridation (dDDH) distances to the closest type strains of the query genome using the Genome-to-Genome Distance Calculator d4 formula (GGDC formula 2). The strains were assigned to the genus of the closest type strain, and they were assigned to a species if dDDH distance was above 70% (Meier-Kolthoff et al. 2013).

The genomes were annotated with Bakta (Schwengers et al. 2021) and their metabolic pathways were explored by obtaining KEGG Orthology (KO) numbers using the BlastKOALA and Reconstruct tools of the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (Kanehisa et al. 2016). AntiSMASH (Blin et al. 2023) in relaxed mode was used to find BGCs in the assemblies.

Results and discussion

Bacterial isolates

A total of 49 bacterial isolates were obtained from the samples (Table 2). The soil samples from Citronen Fjord biocrust (CF) and Kap København permafrost soil (KK) were the most prolific, while a lower number of isolates was obtained from Cape Morris Jessup soil (CMS) and biocrust (CMC). Most strains isolated in saline media came from Citronen Fjord, which indicates that halotolerant bacteria may be more prevalent there, due to its higher salinity. In addition to bacterial colonies, fungal colonies were observed in plates from all samples. We focused on the bacterial isolates and fungal isolates were not further investigated.

The 17 strains isolated with saline media were capable of growth under a wide range of salt concentrations and showed different responses to salt (Fig. 2). Three of these isolates, KK5.5, CF3.5, and CF4.19 showed optimal growth at 0% w/v NaCl, while other isolates such as CF1.1, CF4.1, CF4.2, or CMS1.3 showed almost or no growth without NaCl. The growth of all isolates was impaired at very high salt concentrations (>12.5% w/v), but eight isolates showed some level of growth at the maximum concentration tested, 20% w/v NaCl. Interestingly, some isolates belonging to the same genus, e.g. Nesterenkonia, showed similar responses to NaCl, while both Arthrobacter isolates, KK5.5 and KK5.7, showed slightly different growth patterns as NaCl concentrations increased. Halophiles are commonly classified as such if their growth optimum is above 2% w/v NaCl, while halotolerant organisms grow optimally below 2% and can tolerate a wide range of salinities (Ollivier et al. 1994). Based on this classification, the tested isolates consisted of a mixture of halotolerant (CF3.5, CF4.19, and KK5.5) and halophiles (the rest of the strains).

rRNA gene sequencing

Based on their 16S rRNA gene sequence, the affiliation of the isolates with known type strains was visualized (Fig. 3). Full details of each isolate and accession numbers are available in Table S1. The isolates mainly belonged to three phyla: Actinomycetota, Bacillota, and Pseudomonadota, while Bacteroidota was only represented by one isolate, Acidovorax sp. CMS1.1 (Fig. 3b). All isolates demonstrated the ability to grow at 4°C. Many of the strains were isolated in saline media, thus demonstrating their halotolerance. These included all the Bacillota, as well as isolates belonging to the genus Nesterenkonia. Some strains isolated in non-saline media also proved to be halotolerant, as they grew on MarA (containing 1.945% w/v NaCl), including most members of Arthrobacter and Pseudomonas sp. KK6.1. However, other isolates, such as Massilia sp. CMS3.1 and Flavobacterium sp. CMS1.2 were unable to grow in medium supplemented with >1% NaCl (data not shown).

The most abundant genera were Arthrobacter, predominantly isolated from permafrost soil, and Streptomyces and Nesterenkonia, obtained from biocrust. Previous studies have shown that these genera, as well as other genera isolated in this study, are commonly found in polar environments. For example, Singh et al. (2017) reported that Arthrobacter and Pseudomonas were the most commonly isolated genera in permafrost soil from Svalbard, Norway, where they also isolated strains belonging to Nesterenkonia, Bacillus, and others. Interestingly, the authors also obtained isolates belonging to Streptomyces, while none of our isolates in this genus were obtained from permafrost soil. Streptomyces and other Actinomycetota are commonly found in arid and desert soils (Bull and Asenjo 2013) as well as polar environments (Abirami et al. 2021), and extremophilic and extremotolerant Streptomyces have drawn attention as potential producers of novel antibacterial compounds and other compounds of interest (Sivalingam et al. 2019). In addition, Nesterenkonia has been isolated from Arctic permafrost soil (Singh et al. 2021), as well as other cold environments, such as soils of Antarctica and the Tibetan plateau (Finore et al. 2016, Dai et al. 2022).

Arthrobacter is commonly found in permafrost soil with both culture-dependent (Hinsa-Leasure et al. 2010, Singh et al. 2017) and culture-independent methods (Shen et al. 2021), the latter of which have shown that Arthrobacter spp. can constitute up to 4% of the total bacterial community (Dsouza et al. 2015). This suggests that Arthrobacter spp. are particularly well adapted to survive in permafrost soil and in the active layer. The Arthrobacter pangenome (Gushgari-Doyle et al. 2022) revealed great potential adaptability, which could explain its prevalence in permafrost soil. The same study showed the halotolerance of several Arthrobacter strains, a similar observation to our Arthrobacter isolates, as many of them could grow on MarA or were directly isolated in saline medium. Based on its prevalence in a variety of environments, some authors have used Arthrobacter as a model to study bacterial adaptations in soil (Chauhan et al. 2018, Shen et al. 2021). In our study, the isolated Arthrobacter strains were initially isolated in either GM1 or OHAM, media with no nitrogen source other than nitrate, and with glycogen as the only carbon source (along with pyruvate in OHAM). Some members of this genus are known to be able to assimilate nitrate and nitrite for growth (He et al. 2020, Liu et al. 2023). Based on our results, GM1 may be an effective selective medium to isolate Arthrobacter strains from permafrost soil.

These results show that we isolated phylogenetically diverse bacteria from all three sites, including both halotolerant and non-halotolerant bacteria, suggesting that halotolerant bacteria are ubiquitous at the sampled sites. The only exception was Cape Morris Jessup soil, from which no halotolerant strains were isolated, though this may have been due to the chosen isolation media.

Genomes and functional analyses of selected isolates

From the 16S rRNA gene-based phylogeny, nine isolates were selected for whole genome analysis (Table 3), of which eight were halotolerant. Massilia sp. CMS3.1 was determined not to be halotolerant, as it was only able to grow in GM1 with <1% NaCl, but was chosen for genome sequencing (a) as a Gram-negative and non-halotolerant representative and (b) to investigate its adaptations to its natural environment as a non-halotolerant bacterium. In the case of Arthrobacter sp. KK5.5, its closest strain was A. halodurans DSM 21081T, whose genome was not available in any database. To confirm the phylogeny of our isolate, we sequenced the genome of A. halodurans DSM 21081T. All genomes showed completeness above 98% and contamination below 2%, which was determined sufficient to proceed to the next part of the analysis. Six of the nine sequenced isolates showed dDDH similarities to their closest relatives below 30%, well below the established threshold of 70% to distinguish between different species (Table 3); this suggests that these six strains belong to potentially novel bacterial species. Indeed, in a different study, we showed that strains CF4.4 and KK5.5 belong to novel species, and proposed the names Nesterenkonia maliinae and Arthrobacter arnarulunnguaqae, respectively (Salinas-García and Priemé 2026).

Adaptations to high salinity and low water activity

All sequenced isolates, except for Massilia sp. CMS3.1, possessed orthologs of genes encoding importers of osmoprotectants such as proline, ectoine, and betaine choline (Fig. 4 and Table S3). Osmoprotectants are small, neutral organic solutes that do not interfere with cellular processes, and can thus be accumulated to mitigate osmotic and low-temperature stress (Pathania et al. 2022). The ability to import osmoprotectants from the environment offers flexibility, allowing bacteria to adapt to fluctuating salinity. This strategy reduces the energy cost associated with de novo synthesis, providing a competitive advantage. Interestingly, the non-halotolerant Massilia sp. CMS3.1 possessed only one ortholog of osmoprotectant transporters (yaaJ), whereas its close relative, Telluria aromaticivorans, has 16 transporters and 9 synthesis-related genes, despite its low salt tolerance (Son et al. 2021). Conversely, Salipaludibacillus neizhouensis CF4.18 had 33 orthologues related to osmoprotectant transport, almost twice the amount of the type strain of its species, revealing large intra-species variation. These findings suggests that halotolerance may not be directly corelated with the copy number of genes related to osmoprotectant synthesis and import.

Another abundant group was orthologues of ion transporters of different types. For example, all strains had between one and ten orthologues of tlyC (Mg2+ exporter), and of atpC (ATP-dependent H+ transporter). Furthermore, all strains—except Massilia sp. CMS3.1–had trkA orthologues (K+ uptake). A. arnarulunnguaqae KK5.5, Oceanobacillus sp. CF4.6, S. neizhouensis CF4.18, and Massilia sp. CMS3.1 had at least one orthologue of nhaAC, a Na+: H+ antiporter. In addition, orthologues of kdpABC (ATP-dependent K+ transporter) were found in N. halotolerans CF4.12 and Massilia sp. CMS1.3. Ion transporters are very common in bacteria and are not necessarily indicative of halotolerance, as evidenced by similar numbers of ion transporter orthologues in non-halotolerant strains (Fig. 4). However, ion transporters may have specific adaptations that aid with halotolerance. For instance, the cyanobacterium Aphanothece halophytica has ion transporters with different ion specificities that allow it to tolerate high salinity and pH (Wutipraditkul et al. 2005).

In addition to ion transporters, the strains’ genomes contained orthologues of regulatory genes that have been associated with halotolerance. These include, e.g. spoT and smpB (found in all strains), rsbV (found in most of the strains), and lon and mtnN (both found in Oceanobacillus sp. CF4.6, S. neizhouensis CF4.18, and Massilia sp. CMS3.1). These genes regulate gene expression and quorum sensing, are upregulated in halotolerant organisms under salt stress conditions, and their deletion impairs salt tolerance (Chen et al. 2017, Chen et al. 2018). The presence of these genes associated with halotolerance (Table S3) suggests that the strains isolated in this study are well equipped to tolerate the salt stress that they may encounter in their native environment.

It is likely that the halotolerant strains isolated here use the ‘salt-out’—rather than the ‘salt-in’—strategy to deal with salt stress, because they had several genes related to osmoprotectant synthesis and transport. This strategy is more flexible, and could help them adapt to sudden changes in salinity, e.g. during freezing or melting events, by quickly synthesizing osmoprotectants, taking them up from the environment, or excreting them (Pikuta et al. 2007).

Sub-zero temperatures can lead to similar stress variables as high salinity. For example, water activity is reduced as ice crystals form, leading to osmotic stress, and brines can form as salt ions are excluded from ice crystals (Iwahana et al. 2021). Thus, the halotolerance-related genes discussed may also aid the isolates against the low temperatures in their native Northern Greenland. In addition, the genomes also contained several orthologues of cold shock proteins (cspA). These proteins have a protective effect against the stresses associated with low temperatures by binding to single stranded nucleic acids and regulating transcription and translation (Horn et al. 2007).

Other metabolic pathways

In addition to the adaptations shown in the previous section, other metabolic pathways, such as those related to nitrogen and sulphur cycling, terpenoid production, or oligotrophy, may further aid in nutrient acquisition and stress responses.

Some of the sequenced genomes contained genes encoding enzymes related to nitrogen and sulphur cycling. S. neizhouensis CF4.18 and Oceanobacillus sp. CF4.6 had a complete assimilatory sulfate reduction pathway, while N. aurantiaca CMS1.6 and N. halotolerans CF4.12 lacked this pathway completely. The rest of the sequenced isolates had an incomplete pathway, lacking the enzymes to reduce sulphate to sulphite. However, all genomes contained transporters for alkanesulfonates, and most genomes contained genes encoding for alkanesulfonate monooxigenases. This alternative source of sulphur may aid against sulphur starvation (Ellis 2011), as the low amount of organic carbon in Peary Land soils could limit the amount of sulphur available from organic matter (Stimmler et al. 2022). However, sulphur may not be a limiting nutrient in the permafrost at Kap København, as it consists mostly of marine deposits, which are typically enriched in sulphur (Funder et al. 1984, Francois 1987).

Nesterenkonia sp. CF4.4, Aeromicrobium sp. CF3.5, Arthrobacter sp. KK5.5, S. neizhouensis CF4.18 and Massilia sp. CMS3.1 had genes encoding for both nitrite and nitrate reductases, while these were not found in the other isolates. Interestingly, Oceanobacillus sp. CF4.6 was the only isolate whose genome contained nirK, which encodes for a nitric oxide (NO)-producing nitrite reductase. The reduction of nitrite to NO is the second step in denitrification after the reduction of nitrate to nitrite. NO is then reduced to nitrous oxide (N2O), which is finally reduced to N2. NO and N2O have significant impact on atmospheric chemistry, as they can induce the formation of tropospheric ozone and form NOX; furthermore, N2O is a potent greenhouse gas. In arid areas, especially in the polar regions, nitrification and denitrification remain poorly understood (Ramond et al. 2022). There are no available studies on the nitrification or denitrification rates, or on the emission of NO and N2O, from Northern Greenland, but ecosystem N losses from Arctic soils through N2O emission are generally small, which may be linked to the N limitation of these soils (Tamm 1991, Elser et al. 2007). Oceanobacillus sp. CF4.6 may play a role in denitrification and ecosystem loss of nitrogen in its native environment, although environmental studies are needed to characterize the soil nitrogen cycle in the High Arctic desert of Northern Greenland.

All the sequenced genomes encoded pathways for the synthesis of terpenoid backbones. Apart from Oceanobacillus sp. CF4.6, whose genome encoded for the mevalonate pathway, the sequenced genomes encoded for the MEP/DOXP pathway for isoprenoid backbone synthesis. Interestingly, the pathway in N. halotolerans CF4.12 is incomplete, as it is missing 2-C-methyl-d-erythritol 4-phosphate cytidylyltransferase (EC 2.7.7.60) encoded by IspD, and 2-C-methyl-d-erythritol 2,4-cyclodiphosphate synthase (EC 4.6.1.12), encoded by IspF (Phillips et al. 2008). Nesterenkonia halotolerants DSM 15474T shows the same missing enzymes in its draft genome. These findings suggest that N. halotolerans may contain undescribed isoforms of these enzymes. The synthesis of terpenoid backbones is an important pathway to produce essential and secondary metabolites like undecaprenol phosphate and carotenoids. In fact, all isolates apart from Oceanobacillus sp. CF4.6 and the two Aeromicrobium sp. had BGCs encoding for terpene-related products, with many of them encoding for carotenoids (see next section). This pathway may be of particular relevance in Nesterenkonia, as all isolates belonging to this genus had a bright red or orange colour, suggesting a significant content of carotenoids. Other strains with noteworthy pigmentation include Massilia sp. CMS1.3 (orange, turning to brown in older colonies), Salipaludibacillus neizhouensis CF4.18 (light brown), and Oceanobacillus sp. CF4.6 (light brown), suggesting that these strains may also produce carotenoids or other pigments.

Lastly, the sequenced genomes contained several orthologues of genes that have been associated with survival in nutrient-poor (oligotrophic) environments. For example, all strains, except the two Aeromicrobium strains, had both AceA and AceB genes, which encode for isocitrate lyase (EC 4.1.3.1) and malate synthase (EC 2.3.3.9), respectively. These enzymes form the glyoxylate shunt, a shortcut in the citrate cycle that skips the decarboxylation steps and allows bacteria to assimilate acetate for growth; this route plays a role in nutrient limitation (Prasad Maharjan et al. 2005) and oxidative stress (Ahn et al. 2016). Methylotrophy (i.e. the ability to utilize substrates with no carbon–carbon bonds, C1 compounds) is a trait that has also been associated with survival in oligotrophic environments (Huang and Shen 2016). Some of our strains showed methylotrophic potential in their genomes. For example, Massilia sp. CMS3.1 could potentially oxidize methanol to CO2 for energy, as its genome encoded for a methanol dehydrogenase (XoXF, EC 1.1.2.10), a glutathione-independent formaldehyde dehydrogenase (FdhA, EC 1.2.1.46), and a formate dehydrogenase (FDH, EC 1.17.1.9). FdhA (but not FDH or XoXF) was also present in the rest of the strains, except for Oceanobacillus sp. CF4.6 and Aeromicrobium sp. CF4.19. Furthermore, all the sequenced strains had FolD, the product of which catalyses early steps in the methyl-tetrahydrofolate linked oxidation pathway, related to C1 metabolism (Sun et al. 2011). Aeromicrobium sp. CF3.5 had the genes coxSML, encoding for a carbon-monoxide: quinone oxidoreductase (EC 1.2.5.3), suggesting that this strain has the potential to scavenge CO, a trace gas, for energy. CO oxidation is a widespread source of energy in oligotrophic environments (Cordero et al. 2019).

The adaptations discussed in this section allow the isolates to sustain activity in their cold, nutrient-poor, and water-scarce environment. Furthermore, most of the sequenced genomes showed very low dDDH percentages towards their closest known relative, suggesting that they represent previously undescribed species. Thus, we fail to reject hypotheses i and ii.

BGCs in the genomes of the sequenced isolates

Secondary metabolites are natural products with diverse chemical structures, which in turn give these compounds a wide range of functions, such as antibiotic, antifungal, or stress-response-related. The enzyme and regulatory genes responsible for the production of these secondary metabolites are often co-located within the genome, creating groups known as BGCs (Chen et al. 2019). Novel BGCs are important targets for natural product discovery (Jensen 2016), and the secondary metabolites they produce serve important ecological functions through intra- and interspecies interactions (Barona-Gómez et al. 2023).

The sequenced genomes were found to contain several BGCs (Fig. 5 and Tables S4-S12), including several with no hits to known BGCs. 32% of the identified BGCs putatively encoded for antimicrobial compounds, such as the lassopeptide paeninodin found in S. neizhouensis CF4.18 and Oceanobacillus sp. CF4.6, ε-Poly-l-lysine synthesis clusters found in N. halotolerans CF4.12 and Nesterenkonia sp. CF4.4, and other BGCs encoding for lactones found in several isolates. Most of these putative antimicrobial clusters encoded for polyketide synthases and non-ribosomal peptide synthases (NRPS). Antimicrobial compounds and antimicrobial resistance are widely distributed in soils, where competition for available space and resources exerts significant selective pressure (Mullis et al. 2019). The production of antimicrobial compounds typically incurs a high metabolic cost; e.g. Schlatter and Kinkel (2015) found trade-offs between efficient growth, antimicrobial resistance, and antimicrobial production in Streptomyces spp., and suggested that a “super-killer” phenotype results in a reduction in niche width. The most common strategy in Streptomyces spp. was the simultaneous production of different kinds of antimicrobial compounds to increase their synergistic effect and slow down the evolution of antibiotic resistance (Schlatter and Kinkel 2015). However, in carbon-poor soils, as is the case in Peary Land, nutrient limitation may present an obstacle for this strategy. On the other hand, nutrient scarcity may increase evolutionary pressure to inhibit potential competitors. Many studies have demonstrated the production of antibacterial and antifungal compounds by bacteria isolated from arid soils (e.g. Saadoun et al. 2008, Nasfi et al. 2018), and even the hyper-arid Atacama desert is a potential source of novel antimicrobials (Bull and Asenjo 2013). Polar desert soils, although not as explored, have also demonstrated this potential (Dorst van et al. 2017). In permafrost, where biomass can be higher and environmental conditions are stable, antibiotic resistance genes are widespread, even in ancient permafrost (Mindlin and Petrova 2017, Gomathinayagam and Muthukaliannan 2024). Although measuring the production of antimicrobial compounds of our isolates was beyond the scope of this study, the diversity of BGCs encoding for antimicrobial compounds found in the sequenced genomes suggest a competitive environment where it may be advantageous to have different antimicrobial strategies available.

Desert soils often have higher pH compared to soils from forests and other biomes. The high pH is caused by accumulation of minerals and salts that are not washed away by rain. Peary Land soil is no exception, with soils at Cape Morris Jesup and Citronen Fjord having pH of 7.8 and 9.2, respectively. At these pH values, the bioavailability of many metals is lowered. Thus, bacteria in desert soils need effective metal-acquiring systems such as siderophores. Siderophores allow for the solubilization of iron essential for the production of, e.g. heme proteins. Both Aeromicrobium isolates, as well as Arthrobacter sp. KK5.5 had BGCs encoding for NRPS-independent siderophores; their genomes also contained orthologs of fepBDG, which encode for Fe-siderophore complex transporters, and both Aeromicrobium isolates additionally had fepC. Notably, N. halotolerans CF4.12 also had a complete set of fepBCDG, while the other two Nesterenkonia isolates had orthologues of fepCG. This absence of siderophore synthesis genes, while transport genes are present, may be due to a variety of causes. One possibility is that these bacteria rely on siderophores produced by other bacteria for iron uptake (Kramer et al. 2020).

Lastly, Massilia sp. CMS3.1 had a BGC whose putative product is a homoserine lactone. While it had no hits to known clusters, it contained, among others, a protein with an acyltransferase domain; this suggests that this BGC may encode for an N-Acyl homoserine lactone, a group of signalling molecules involved in quorum sensing and biofilm formation. This aligns with the possible ability of Massilia sp. CMS3.1 to form biofilms. Indeed, the genome of this isolate contained several genes involved in quorum sensing and biofilm formation, such as orthologues of lasIR and wspBEFR, as well as pilGHIJ, which are involved in type IV pilus formation. Young (<24 h) colonies of Massilia sp. CMS3.1 were orange, soft and easily pickable, but older (especially >48 h) colonies became darker, hard, and difficult to disaggregate, which suggests the formation of a hard extracellular matrix that may be mediated by these genes. Biofilm formation has been shown to be a successful strategy to improve survivability against extreme environmental factors such as temperature and pH (high and low), aridity, and high salinity (Khan and Rao 2023). For example, biofilms are employed by epiphytic bacteria to survive periods of extreme cold and freeze-thaw cycles (Wu et al. 2012).

Other types of BGCs found in the genomes included an ectoine synthesis cluster and terpenoid synthesis clusters, which were discussed in the previous section; the terpenoid clusters encoded mainly for carotenoids.

It is possible that some BGCs were missed by antiSMASH. Indeed, BGC detection on contig-level assemblies can be challenging, as BGCs may be split into different contigs (Meleshko et al. 2019). This is evidenced by a few BGCs that were found at the beginning or the end of a contig, suggesting that these BGCs are incomplete. This, combined with the significant amount of BGCs that had no hits with known BGCs, suggest that Peary Land is indeed a source of potentially novel secondary metabolites, as per hypothesis iii; however, further investigation in vivo will be necessary to fully characterize these secondary metabolites.

Conclusions

We isolated 49 bacteria from three different sites in Peary Land, northern Greenland, representing some of the most northernly bacterial isolates, and the first strains isolated from these areas. We showed the diversity of culturable halotolerant bacteria at the three sites and found potentially novel BGCs that suggest a competitive environment with many antimicrobial compounds present. The isolates showed adaptations to their cold and saline native environments, including a variety of ion transporters and genes related to osmoprotectant synthesis and transport. These findings underscore the importance of osmoprotectants in cold and saline environments and expand our understanding of microbial life in extreme environments. Furthermore, 66% of the genome-sequenced isolates were genetically distant enough from their closest known relatives to potentially represent novel species. Overall, this work not only provides the first insights into the diversity of culturable halotolerant bacteria of northern-most Greenland, but also suggests that the region harbours untapped microbial resources that may hold significant biotechnological value.
