Prevalence of alexithymia and its association with social media addiction among medical students in Pakistan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Muhammad Bilal, Zuhha Tahir, Mudassir Hayat, Ayesha Abdullah, Aun Haider, Muhammad Saad Riasat, Muhammad Afham, Ishaq Oyeshina Sanusi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlexithymia, defined as difficulty in identifying, describing, and expressing emotions, has gained increasing attention in populations exposed to high psychological and academic stress. Medical students represent a particularly vulnerable group due to intensive coursework, emotionally challenging clinical exposure, and the early requirement to adopt professional roles. Existing studies suggest an association between alexithymia and digital use; however, there is limited evidence in Pakistan. This study aims to determine the prevalence of alexithymia among medical students and examine its association with demographic, behavioral, and particularly social media addiction. A cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among undergraduate medical students across multiple medical colleges in Pakistan (n = 417). Data were collected using a self-administered online questionnaire comprising demographic variables, the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20), and the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS). Descriptive statistics were calculated, and associations were assessed using chi-square tests and binary logistic regression analysis. The prevalence of alexithymia was 29.7% (95% CI: 25–34%), with 28.8% classified as possible alexithymia. TAS-20 scores showed a moderate positive correlation with BSMAS scores (r = 0.36, p < 0.001). In multivariable analysis, moderate (aOR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.25–3.53) and high social media addiction (aOR = 4.31, 95% CI: 2.35–7.90) were significantly associated with alexithymia. Physical activity ≥ 3 times per week was also associated with higher odds of alexithymia (aOR = 2.74, 95% CI: 1.26–5.97). Alexithymia is prevalent among medical students in Pakistan and has a strong association with social media addiction. This may indicate a potential relationship between social media use and emotional processing difficulties. Introduction

Alexithymia is a personality construct defined by difficulties in identifying and describing one’s emotions, along with an externally oriented thinking style, and is widely studied in affective science and clinical psychology1 This includes facing challenges in differentiating between emotions, recognizing, describing, and expressing feelings. With increasing awareness and relevance to mental health, it is increasingly recognized among populations exposed to high stress and complex environments. Medical students are part of the group exposed to a demanding environment characterized by intense academic pressures, frequent exposure to emotionally charged clinical scenarios, and a requirement to rapidly transition from learner to caregiver roles2. The pervasive use of social media among young adults has introduced a new domain of behavioral risk. Social media addiction, with excessive and compulsive engagement in online networking, has become an increasing concern3. Some studies have reported that individuals with higher levels of alexithymia may be more prone to problematic digital behaviors4, yet very few studies have specifically examined this link.Early research has demonstrated a significant association between alexithymia and problematic internet use, suggesting that individuals with impaired emotional awareness may be more vulnerable to maladaptive digital behaviors5. Furthermore, alexithymia has been linked with anxiety-spectrum psychopathology and emotional dysregulation, which may contribute to excessive engagement in online environments6. Among medical students, mobile phone addiction has been shown to mediate the effect of alexithymia on learning burnout. Emotionally unaware students may overuse mobile devices as a coping mechanism, thereby exacerbating stress and exhaustion in educational contexts7. Research among medical students indicates that alexithymia acts as a mediating factor between social media addiction and empathy levels, implying that limited emotional awareness may contribute to reduced empathic abilities in individuals, which is a concern for those working in healthcare4. Additionally, a multi-country study reported a significant association between alexithymia and psychological distress, suggesting that emotional processing difficulties may increase vulnerability to maladaptive digital coping behaviors8. Understanding the magnitude of this phenomenon and its correlations could inform interventions aimed at enhancing the emotional awareness and mental well-being of future healthcare workers. To bridge this gap, there is a pressing need to determine the prevalence of alexithymia among medical students and its association with social media addiction alongside other sociodemographic and behavioral factors.

This cross-sectional study aims to address this gap by estimating the connection and prevalence of alexithymia in a medical student population, particularly with social media addiction, to provide evidence that can inform emotional health strategies within medical education.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted among undergraduate medical students enrolled in medical colleges across Pakistan between June 2025 and November 2025, following approval from the relevant institutional ethics committee.

Sample size and technique

The sample size was calculated using the OpenEpi sample size calculator, assuming a prevalence of 49%, a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error. The prevalence estimate was based on prior studies reporting relatively high rates of alexithymia among medical students in similar settings9. A 10% adjustment was added to account for potential non-response, resulting in a final sample size of 420 participants. Three incomplete responses were excluded and 417 participants were analysed. Participants were selected using a non-probability convenience sampling technique.

Study population

The study population comprised medical students from all academic years who voluntarily consented to participate. The age of participants ranged from 18 to 26 years. Inclusion criteria encompassed MBBS students of any year who provided informed consent and completed all sections of the questionnaire, including the alexithymia and social media usage scales. Exclusion criteria consisted of students not currently enrolled in MBBS programs, those from non-medical disciplines, participants with incomplete or missing data, and individuals who refused or withdrew consent.

Data collection

Data collection was performed using a self-administered, web-based questionnaire developed through Google Forms. Confidentiality and anonymity of responses was properly ensured. The questionnaires were administered in English, consisted of three main parts: an informed consent section, demographic information, and two validated scales; (1) the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20) for the assessment of alexithymia10, (2) the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS) for evaluating social media addiction11. Each participant completed the questionnaire independently, and only one submission per respondent was allowed to avoid duplication. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (Ethical Review Committee) of Sargodha Medical College (Ref No: E-009-3761), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS)

The Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS) consists of six items scored on a 5-point Likert scale, yielding a total score ranging from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater levels of problematic social media use11. The scale is commonly interpreted as a continuous measure in the literature12. However, for analytical purposes, participants in the present study were categorized into low (6–18), moderate (19–23), and high (24–30) levels of social media addiction based on score distribution and prior studies suggesting that higher scores (≥ 19) may indicate problematic or addictive social media use11. It is important to note that these categories are not diagnostic thresholds but are used to facilitate comparative analysis.

Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20)

The Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20) developed by Bagby et al.10 was used to assess alexithymia. The validity of online administration of TAS-20 has been supported in previous research13.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics version 27. Descriptive statistics, including means, frequencies, and percentages, were used to summarize sociodemographic and study variables. Although normality tests (Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov) were statistically significant, skewness and kurtosis values were within acceptable limits (± 1). Given the large sample size (n = 417), parametric tests were considered robust to minor deviations from normality. The Chi-square test was performed to assess associations between alexithymia and categorical variables, while binary logistic regression was applied in order to identify significant predictors of alexithymia. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The sample comprised 417 medical students, of whom 55.6% were female and 44.4% male. Most participants were in the second (42.0%) or third year (30.0%) of study; 61.9% were hostel residents. Only 3.8% reported smoking, and 8.9% had a self-reported psychiatric history. The mean ± SD TAS-20 total score was 54.30 ± 11.91 and the mean BSMAS total was 18.27 ± 5.10. Among TAS-20 subscales, the Difficulty Identifying Feelings (DIF) domain showed the highest mean (18.14 ± 6.30). The Externally Oriented Thinking (EOT) subscale exhibited relatively lower variability (SD = 4.15).

The internal consistency of the Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20) total score was good (Cronbach’s α = 0.828). The Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS) demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.764.

Bivariate analyses

1. Categorical predictors

Chi-square tests showed significant associations between social-media-addiction category and alexithymia (χ² = 29.46, df = 2, p < 0.001) and between abuse history and alexithymia (χ² = 4.33, df = 1, p = 0.038).

Other demographic factors—including biological sex of participants, year of study, student category, smoking, psychiatric history, and physical-activity frequency—showed no significant association (p > 0.05). 2. Continuous predictors

Independent t-tests revealed that students classified as alexithymic had significantly higher BSMAS scores compared to non-alexithymic and possibly alexithymic students (20.52 ± 5.04 vs. 17.31 ± 4.82; t = − 6.13, p < 0.001, d = 0.66).

All TAS subscales (DIF, DDF, EOT) were markedly elevated in the alexithymic group (all p < 0.001, large effect sizes).

3. Continuous–continuous correlation

Pearson’s correlation demonstrated a moderate positive relationship between TAS-20 and BSMAS total scores (r = 0.363, p < 0.001), indicating that higher social-media-addiction severity was associated with greater alexithymic traits. The correlation between TAS-20 and BSMAS scores is shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Multivariable logistic regression

Variables with p < 0.10 in bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Additionally, biologically and theoretically relevant variables such as biological sex and year of study were included irrespective of their bivariate significance to control for potential confounding effects. The overall model was statistically significant (Omnibus χ² = 38.93, df = 12, p < 0.001) and demonstrated acceptable fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ² = 7.67, p = 0.47) with a classification accuracy of 71.2% and Nagelkerke R² = 0.13. After adjustment for covariates

Students with moderate social-media addiction had 2.1-fold higher odds of alexithymia (aOR = 2.10, 95% CI 1.25–3.53, p = 0.005). Those with high social-media addiction were 4.3 times more likely to have alexithymia (aOR = 4.31, 95% CI 2.35–7.90, p < 0.001). Physical activity ≥ 3×/week also emerged as a positive predictor (aOR = 2.74, 95% CI 1.26–5.97, p = 0.011). Biological sex of participants, student category, abuse history, and year of study were not significant predictors (p > 0.05).

Receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated acceptable discrimination of the logistic model, with AUC = 0.68 (95% CI 0.62–0.74, p < 0.001). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis is shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

This indicates the model correctly distinguishes alexithymic from non-alexithymic students 68% of the time.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study aimed to find the prevalence of Alexithymia among medical students and its association with different behavioral and demographic factors like biological sex of participants, year of study, student category, smoking, psychiatric history, physical activity, and social media addiction. The overall prevalence of alexithymia in our study was found to be 29.7% while students with possible alexithymia accounted for 28.8% of the sample. This overall prevalence is much lower as compared to the study conducted at King Abdulaziz University in Saudi Arabia (49%)14 and moderately higher than a recent study in Nepal (22.89% )2. On the other hand, it is higher than a study conducted in China15, which documented a prevalence of 15.7% using the same TAS-20 scale. Other studies conducted in the Middle East, including those in Lebanon16 and Jordan17, also predicted lower alexithymia prevalence of 20.8% and 24.6% when compared to our study. Variations across these findings may result from cultural differences, differences in academic stress (more students from preclinical years than clinical years), and the use of different cutoff scores or instruments. In spite of these variations, our findings are consistent with the previous literature, suggesting that alexithymia remains a common concern among medical students globally.

A notable finding of this study was the strong association between alexithymia and social media addiction. Students with high social media addiction had over four times greater odds of having alexithymia, while those with a moderate addiction were twice as likely to have it. This relationship has been consistently noted in prior studies. A study conducted by İlter et al.18 reported that nursing students with higher social media addiction scores showed notably higher levels of alexithymia. Similarly, Martingano et al.19 showed that individuals with alexithymic traits often spend more time on social media and have lower empathy levels. A recent study by Ursoniu S et al.4 also found a positive correlation between alexithymia and social media use among university students. Together, these findings suggest that individuals who have difficulty understanding or expressing their emotions may use social media to cope with emotional distress, which could increase their addiction.

The moderate positive correlation between TAS-20 and BSMAS scores (r = 0.36) observed in our data may indicate that when people lack emotional awareness and regulation, they may be more likely to have uncontrollable online activities. According to the compensatory internet use model, social media usage can help alleviate psychological distress temporarily, but over time reinforces this dependence, which ultimately reduces real-world emotional experiences.

Our study showed no significant difference in the prevalence of alexithymia among males and females, the results being contrary to the studies conducted in China15, which demonstrated that males have a higher percentage of alexithymia than females. Whereas our findings were consistent with the findings from King Abdul Aziz University14 and Nepal2. Also, a study by Mendia J. et al.20 noted gender differences of small magnitude, which were considered insignificant and inconsistent across the samples.However, a study among Pakistani adolescents reported higher alexithymia levels in males compared to females highlighting potential demographic variability21. Emotions are also influenced by cultural values, which may explain the difference in patterns between regions.

In contrast to findings from Saudi Arabia14, which reported a higher incidence of alexithymia in early academic years, attributing this difference to difficulty coping with new stressful academics and a busy schedule, our study did not observe any year-wise variation. This may suggest that medical students face personal, academic, and clinical challenges throughout all the years of their medical education equally, rather than in only early academic years, which can have an influence on how they handle their emotions.

Interestingly, physical activity was identified as a key predictor of alexithymia in our study, with a higher incidence among those students who were involved in regular physical activities three or more times per week. This finding is inconsistent with previous studies that found physically active students less vulnerable to developing alexithymia3,7. This contrasts with prior literature suggesting a protective effect of physical activity. One possible explanation is reverse causality, whereby individuals with alexithymic traits may engage in physical activity as a behavioral coping mechanism rather than deriving emotional benefits from it. Additionally, the measurement of physical activity in this study was limited to frequency and did not account for type, intensity, or social context, which may influence emotional outcomes. Potential confounders such as participation in competitive sports or solitary exercise routines may also contribute to this association. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution and warrants further investigation in longitudinal studies. There were no significant correlations of alexithymia with hostel residence, smoking or psychiatric history in this study. While previous research conducted in Saudi Arabia14 demonstrated higher TAS-20 scores among smokers, there is a possibility that a small proportion of smokers in our study (3.8%) might have reduced statistical power to determine a significant association. Similarly, abuse history showed a significant association with alexithymia in bivariate analysis but was not retained in the multivariate model. These findings are consistent with the fact that emotional dysregulation and maladaptive coping, such as social media overuse, share overlapping pathways, leading to alexithymia traits.

The findings from our study are consistent with recent global trends, which emphasize the association between alexithymia, digital behaviors, and psychological health. Studies across different populations, such as nursing students in Turkey18 and college students in China7, clearly demonstrate the correlation of alexithymia with social media overusage, emotional dysregulation, and lower empathy, suggesting that medical students who are already exposed to academic stress and emotional demands are particularly vulnerable. Excessive social media use, which is used as an attempt to manage emotional distress, could exacerbate emotional dysregulation, further impairing communication and empathy skills, which are essential for future clinicians.

The proportion of medical students in our study with possible alexithymia is also considerably high (28.8%) and needs to be addressed. Individuals in this transitional group may be at increased risk of developing more pronounced alexithymic traits over time, although longitudinal evidence is limited. Early interventions focused on emotional awareness could help stop them from progressing to full alexithymia. Interventions such as mindfulness-based training and emotion recognition skill development have shown promise in improving emotional awareness and reducing maladaptive coping behaviors1.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is that it focuses on a diverse group of medical students from various institutes across Pakistan and used validated, widely used scales (TAS-20 and BSMAS). It also incorporated both bivariate and multivariate approaches, improving internal validity. However, it has some limitations. This study can only show a link between alexithymia and social media use, but cannot determine causality due to its study design, in which we examined data at a single point in time. Furthermore, alexithymia was assessed using self-report measures, which may be subject to bias, as individuals with alexithymia may have limited awareness of their emotional difficulties. Given the use of non-probability convenience sampling, the findings should be interpreted with caution due to potential selection bias and limited generalizability. The relatively low internal consistency observed for the EOT subscale is consistent with previous studies reporting lower reliability for this domain22–24.

Alexithymia is associated with difficulties in emotional awareness and regulation, while excessive social media use may further reinforce these issues by promoting emotional disengagement and lack of interpersonal communication. Routine screening for emotional intelligence and digital overuse, along with proper counselling, may enhance students’ mental well-being. Longitudinal and interventional studies in the future should explore this association and evaluate the effect of emotional regulation in reducing alexithymia.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that alexithymia is prevalent among medical students in Pakistan (29.7%). It establishes a strong and positive interrelation between alexithymia and overuse of social media, while no significant association was found between alexithymia and variables such as biological sex of participants, year of study, hostel residence, smoking, or psychiatric history. These findings urge the need for enhanced emotional awareness and teaching skill building in medical education, which is essential not only for the personal well-being of the students but will also help them grow into more empathetic and emotionally competent physicians.
