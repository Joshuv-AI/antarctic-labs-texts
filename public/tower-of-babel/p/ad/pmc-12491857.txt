Predictive Modeling of Weight Loss and Metabolic Health Outcomes: A Retrospective Predictive Modeling Study
Authors: Rolando Andrade‐Calle, Isabel de la Torre‐Díez, Daniel de Luis‐Román
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundObesity is a chronic, complicated, and progressive disease that significantly affects mortality, quality of life, and overall health in nearly 13% of the adult population worldwide. Thus, solutions like a hypocaloric diet with a Mediterranean diet pattern aim to control this and other metabolic problems.ObjectivesThis study developed and measured the performance of different machine learning (ML) models designed to predict body weight loss and/or metabolic syndrome (MetS) change after a 3‐month hypocaloric diet with a Mediterranean pattern in obesity‐diagnosed patients.MethodsThe data set was provided by a clinical trial of 893 obese patients. Five machine learning architectures were implemented: Logistic Regression, Decision Tree Classifier, Random Forest Classifier, eXtreme Gradient Boosting Classifier (XGBoost), and Support Vector Classifier. Performance metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1‐score, and ROC curve were used to assess the prediction models. The influence of the predictors was also evaluated in every case.ResultsFor body weight loss prediction, Stacking and Random Forest models outperformed the other models, with accuracies of 81.37% and 76.44% and AUC of 86.79% (95% CI: 82.9%–90.4%) and 86.25% (95% CI: 82.3%–89.9%), respectively. For MetS change, Stacking had the best performance, with an accuracy of 85.90% and an AUC of 83.65% (95% CI: 76.9%–89.8%). For the prediction model of body weight loss and MetS change, Stacking was the best algorithm, with an accuracy of 94.74% and an AUC of 95.35% (95% CI: 88.7%–99.9%). Furthermore, variables associated with metabolic and inflammatory markers exhibited stronger correlations with the outcomes.ConclusionMachine learning models, especially ensemble methods like Stacking and XGBoost, effectively predict body weight loss and MetS improvement in obese patients following a Mediterranean diet. Key predictors include age, insulin resistance markers, and inflammatory biomarkers. Integrating these predictive tools can significantly enhance personalized dietary interventions, optimizing treatment outcomes in clinical practice.

Introduction

Obesity is a complex, chronic, and progressive disease that impairs quality of life and increases mortality [1], thereby defines obesity by a body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m². The World Health Organization (WHO) projects that by 2035, overweight and obesity may affect over 4 billion people [2], with nearly 2 billion affected by obesity alone [3]. According to Mili and colleagues [4, 5], currently, the worldwide prevalence among adults is 39% for overweight and 13% for obesity.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS), often coexisting with obesity, involves a combination of abdominal obesity, insulin resistance, dysglycemia, atherogenic dyslipidemia, and hypertension—diagnosed when three of these five criteria are met [6]. MetS increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and stroke [7]. Its global prevalence is approximately 25%, with higher rates in the Eastern Mediterranean and the Americas, and associations with national income levels [8, 9].

The coexistence of obesity and MetS poses an even greater threat to health, as both conditions are linked to increased risks of cancer [4] and musculoskeletal disorders such as osteoarthritis [10]. Therefore, identifying effective, personalized strategies to address both conditions is essential.

The Mediterranean diet (MD) is rich in fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and olive oil, and has been shown to reduce body weight and central adiposity, particularly when combined with caloric restriction and physical activity [11, 12, 13, 14]. Its long‐term adherence is associated with improvements in all components of MetS and enhanced cardiometabolic profiles [12, 15].

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), is increasingly used in clinical nutrition to enhance screening, predict disease risk, and support personalized interventions [16, 17, 18]. These models capture complex interactions between dietary patterns, metabolic responses, and health outcomes [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Recent studies have demonstrated their value in predicting obesity, body composition, and MetS using structured clinical data [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33].

In this study, we aimed to develop and evaluate the performance of various ML models in predicting body weight loss (BWL) following a 3‐month hypocaloric MD in patients with obesity. By using baseline clinical and biochemical data, we sought to provide healthcare professionals with a reliable predictive tool to support decision‐making in personalized dietary interventions.

Dietary Intervention

The hypocaloric diet was designed to provide an energy deficit of approximately 500 kcal/day below each participant′s estimated total energy expenditure for 3 months. The macronutrient distribution followed a Mediterranean pattern: 52% carbohydrates, 23% proteins, and 25% lipids. Fat composition consisted of 50.7% monounsaturated, 38.5% saturated, and 11.8% polyunsaturated fatty acids. The diet included extra virgin olive oil (30 mL/day), three servings of fish and nuts per week, daily vegetables, and four to five servings of fresh fruit. Dietary adherence and progress were monitored biweekly by a registered dietitian.

Data Set Description

The data set for this study was obtained from a clinical trial that enrolled 893 individuals diagnosed with obesity. The study was conducted between February 2017 and February 2023 and was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Hospital Clínico Universitario de Valladolid (approval code: PI 17‐542), as authorized by the Public National Health System of Castilla y León. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. Patients followed a hypocaloric diet with a Mediterranean pattern as part of the intervention to promote BWL.

Participants provided baseline data on vital signs, biochemistry, bioimpedance, anthropometry, and lifestyle habits, including diet and physical activity. Dietary adherence was assessed during follow‐up visits every 4 weeks through 24‐h dietary recalls and interviews conducted by a registered dietitian. Compliance was evaluated using food diaries and records of oral nutritional supplement intake, with adherence defined as ≥ 80% of the prescribed diet.

Dietary data were analyzed using professional software (Dietsource, Nestlé, Geneva, Switzerland), incorporating 2 weekdays and 1 weekend day. Physical activity was self‐reported, and participants were encouraged to engage in aerobic exercise (e.g., walking, running, cycling) at least three times per week for a minimum of 30 min, in line with international guidelines.

Anthropometric Parameters and Arterial Blood Pressure

Anthropometric data were collected at baseline and after 12 weeks using standardized procedures. Waist circumference was measured with a flexible, nonelastic tape placed midway between the lowest rib and the iliac crest. Height was recorded using a calibrated stadiometer. Body weight was measured using digital scales, with participants barefoot and wearing light clothing. Two nonconsecutive measurements were averaged to obtain the final weight. The body weight quotient in kilograms divided by the height in square meters was used to determine the BMI.

Bioelectrical impedance analysis was used to measure total fat mass with an accuracy of 5 g. A calibrated signal generator produced an alternating current of 0.8 mA at 50 kHz (Akern EFG, PI, It) [34].

After the subjects sat for 15 min during the interview, their blood pressure was taken with a sphygmomanometer. Each patient had four measurements made, and the average of the four was used.

Biochemical Parameters

Triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, insulin, glucose, and C‐reactive protein (CRP) levels were among the biochemical measurements acquired using the COBAS INTEGRA 400 analyzer (Roche Diagnostic, Basel, Switzerland). LDL cholesterol was calculated using the Friedewald formula [35]: LDLcholesterol=totalcholesterol−HDLcholesterol−trigycerides5.

Based on these parameters, the homeostasis model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) was calculated using the formula [36]: HOOMA−IR=glucose×insulin22.5.

High‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used to measure hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) (Menarini, Pi, It).

MetS was defined based on the criteria of the Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP III) [37]. To be diagnosed with MetS, a patient had to have at least three of the following criteria: low HDL cholesterol ( < 40 mg/dL in males or < 50 mg/dL in females), elevated systolic or diastolic blood pressure ( > 130/85 mmHg or antihypertensive treatment), elevated triglycerides ( > 150 mg/dL), or treatment for dyslipidemia, elevated fasting glucose ( > 94 cm in males or > 80 cm in females).

Numerous Python libraries have been developed for ML; for this and all the following procedures, Python programming was used, applying a set of techniques and algorithms, including packages such as pandas, numpy, scikit‐learn, matplotlib, and seaborn.

Imputation

Handling missing data are a critical preprocessing step, as incomplete datasets can lead to biased estimates and compromised model performance [38]. The decision to impute specific variables depends on the analytical objective and the chosen imputation method [39]. The goal is not to replace all missing values, but to preserve the underlying data distribution and inter‐variable relationships [40].

Following a systematic review of imputation methods in healthcare, K‐Nearest Neighbors (KNN) was identified as one of the most effective and widely used algorithms for numerical data [41]. KNN imputes missing values by identifying similar instances within the data set, making it suitable for continuous variables regardless of data set size [42].

For categorical variables, the nature of missingness was assessed using Little′s MCAR test. All p‐values exceeded 0.05, suggesting that missing data were Missing Completely at Random (MCAR). Accordingly, mode imputation was applied, following recommendations that this strategy is appropriate and unbiased under MCAR assumptions [43].

Out of 198 total features, 191 had at least one missing value, with 11.17% of the data set being incomplete. After imputation, a curated subset of 117 variables was selected based on data completeness and relevance, and used in subsequent statistical and predictive modeling

The data set comprises a comprehensive set of variables grouped into six main categories: macronutrients, micronutrients, biochemical parameters, body composition, lifestyle, and clinical condition, as seen in Table 1. Macronutrient and micronutrient intake data were collected at baseline and after 12 weeks of intervention, providing detailed information on dietary patterns and nutrient consumption. Similarly, biochemical parameters—such as lipid profile, liver enzymes, glucose metabolism markers, and inflammatory indicators—were measured at both time points to assess metabolic and physiological responses over time. Body composition variables were derived from anthropometric measurements and bioimpedance analysis, allowing for the evaluation of fat mass, muscle mass, and other compartments. Lifestyle factors included smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity levels, which are relevant covariates for metabolic health outcomes.

Data Augmentation

In this study′s three cases, there is a clear imbalance in the outcomes, with a dominant number of failed cases. In Figure 2, it is clear that WL has the greatest number of successful outcomes, with a total of 321 out of 893, whereas MetS change has 115. Finally, only 53 have succeeded in both cases.

Outcomes distribution.

To address class imbalance observed in the outcome variables (Figure 2), we applied the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) exclusively to the training set after the initial 70/30 train‐test split [44, 45]. This approach prevents data leakage and ensures unbiased model evaluation. SMOTE generates synthetic minority class samples by interpolating between existing instances using nearest neighbors. Additionally, class weighting was implemented in classifiers that support it, such as Random Forest and Logistic Regression, to penalize misclassification of the minority class [45]. This combined strategy improved recall and overall robustness without compromising model validity.

Categorical variables were transformed using one‐hot encoding to ensure compatibility with ML algorithms that require numerical input [46, 47]. This method converts categories into binary vectors, preserving data distribution while enabling distance‐based computations in models such as logistic regression or support vector classifiers [48].

A 70/30 split was used for training and testing based on prior evidence supporting this partition for maintaining performance while optimizing computational efficiency [49].

ML Algorithms

Classification is a core application of ML in medicine, enabling the generation of discrete outcome categories [50]. In this study, five supervised ML algorithms were implemented to identify the most effective predictors of clinical response: Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), and Support Vector Classifier (SVC). These algorithms have demonstrated strong performance in various biomedical applications.

LR is a widely used statistical model for binary classification due to its simplicity, interpretability, and applicability across medical domains [51, 52]. DTs offer intuitive classification rules and are computationally efficient, making them useful in diagnostic tasks [53, 54]. RF improves upon DTs through bagging and ensembling, reducing overfitting and enhancing performance, particularly in high‐dimensional datasets [55]. XGB, a gradient boosting method, has shown state‐of‐the‐art performance in many clinical prediction problems [55]. SVCs are effective in modeling nonlinear relationships and finding optimal decision boundaries in complex medical data [55].

We prioritized tree‐based ensemble models (RF, XGB) due to their ability to model nonlinear interactions, robustness to multicollinearity, and ease of interpreting feature importance, qualities essential in clinical research. These models have been successfully applied in obesity and metabolic disorder prediction, supporting their relevance to our study.

Hyperparameter optimization was performed using grid search with five‐fold cross‐validation, maximizing the area under the ROC curve (AUC). Tree‐based models were tuned for the number of estimators and depth, while LR models were optimized for regularization strength. A stacked ensemble was also constructed, using RF and XGB as base learners and LR as the meta‐classifier.

Performance Metrics

In ML, performance metrics are essential for model development, selection, and evaluation. While many classifiers are optimized for accuracy, this metric can be biased toward the majority class in imbalanced datasets. Therefore, alternative metrics such as the F1 score and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) are preferred in these scenarios [56].

Classification performance is typically assessed using base measures—true positives (TP), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN), and true negatives (TN)—which are summarized in a confusion matrix [57]. This 2 × 2 matrix provides a comprehensive view of a model′s predictive performance across both classes, enabling more reliable evaluation [58].

The performance metrics obtained from the confusion matrix and applied in this study are as follows: Accuracy (ACC): Defined as the ratio of correctly predicted observations to the total observations, calculated as: ACC=TP+TNTP+TN+FP+FN. Model performance was assessed using multiple metrics, including AUC, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), F1‐score, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve illustrates each model′s ability to distinguish between classes by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate. The area under the curve (AUC) provides a single summary measure of discriminative performance, where 1.0 indicates perfect classification and 0.5 reflects random guessing [59].

To evaluate AUC uncertainty, we computed 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using bootstrapping (1000 iterations), based on predicted probabilities. Narrower CIs suggest greater robustness, while overlapping intervals imply nonsignificant differences.

Model interpretability was explored through feature importance analysis and correlation‐based feature selection. We selected the 15 most relevant predictors based on the absolute Pearson correlation coefficients with the target variables (BWL, metabolic change, and combined outcome) [60]. To reduce dimensionality and multicollinearity, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied before model training. PCA transforms correlated variables into orthogonal components that preserve most of the variance, improving model stability and interpretability.

All analyses were conducted using Python 3.11 (scikit‐learn 1.2.2, pandas 1.5.3, numpy 1.24.2, matplotlib 3.7.1) and SPSS version 28. Statistical significance was defined as α = 0.05, with all tests two‐sided.

Additionally, the prevalence of MetS declined from 36.95% to 35.16%.

Discussion

This study confirmed the effectiveness of ML models in predicting BWL and MetS improvement following a 3‐month Mediterranean dietary intervention. Ensemble methods—particularly Stacking, RF, and XGB—demonstrated superior predictive performance, aligning with previous research [61, 62, 63, 64]. Our results for BWL prediction, with AUCs reaching 0.98, are comparable to earlier studies that reported high accuracies for RF and XGB (ranging from 89% to 97%), underscoring the robustness of these algorithms in modeling complex clinical outcomes. Furthermore, multi‐categorical approaches have been shown to improve interpretability in clinical settings [65].

In MetS prediction, our findings are consistent with studies reporting AUC values between 0.85 and 0.90 using similar ML approaches [66, 67], reinforcing the value of anthropometric indices, triglycerides, and blood pressure as key predictors. Figure 6a–c illustrate the correlation between clinical variables and outcomes. Age, HOMA‐IR, and body composition consistently emerged as the most influential predictors. Diastolic blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, and muscle mass were particularly relevant for MetS resolution, while BWL was primarily associated with age, HOMA‐IR, insulin, uric acid, and impedance metrics [68, 69].

Feature importance analysis highlighted variables spanning multiple physiological domains. HOMA‐IR reflects insulin resistance, a core feature of MetS and type 2 diabetes, disrupting metabolic homeostasis. Low muscle mass and excess adiposity contribute to metabolic inflexibility and chronic inflammation. Diastolic blood pressure and LDL cholesterol indicate cardiovascular risk, while C‐reactive protein (CRP) serves as a marker of systemic inflammation linked to adipose dysfunction. These results suggest that successful metabolic improvement involves coordinated changes across endocrine, cardiovascular, and immunometabolic systems, beyond simple caloric balance.

The model′s ability to prioritize biologically meaningful predictors supports its interpretability and clinical relevance. By identifying individuals more likely to respond to dietary interventions, these tools can facilitate personalized nutrition strategies and improve care delivery in obesity management.

Despite the encouraging results, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted in a single‐center cohort, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to broader populations with different demographic or genetic profiles. Second, although internal validation was performed through cross‐validation, external validation using independent datasets was not conducted. This hinders the assessment of model reproducibility in real‐world settings. Third, while lifestyle variables such as physical activity, alcohol intake, and smoking were included, other relevant factors—such as psychological status, socioeconomic conditions, and long‐term dietary adherence—were not captured and may influence outcomes. Lastly, although ML models can handle complex interactions, their interpretability may be limited in clinical contexts, posing a challenge for routine application.

Future studies should aim to validate these findings in larger, multi‐center cohorts and incorporate behavioral, environmental, and genomic data to enhance model generalizability and personalization. Longitudinal follow‐up would also provide insights into the long‐term effectiveness of diet‐based interventions on obesity and MetS.

Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrated the efficacy of various ML algorithms in predicting BWL and MetS changes in obese patients undergoing a 3‐month Mediterranean dietary intervention. Among the algorithms assessed, ensemble methods, particularly the Stacking and XGB models, exhibited superior predictive accuracy, achieving outstanding AUC values. Notably, the combined prediction model for BWL and MetS provided the highest predictive performance, underscoring the interconnected nature of these clinical outcomes. The ranked feature importance was further interpreted in the context of known metabolic pathways, highlighting how predictors such as HOMA‐IR, CRP, and LDL cholesterol reflect insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and cardiovascular risk. These findings provide clinically meaningful insight into the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying weight loss response and metabolic improvement.

Further research is essential to validate these predictive models in diverse patient populations, which would strengthen their clinical applicability. Future investigations should integrate genetic, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors to enhance predictive accuracy and facilitate more personalized therapeutic strategies. Additionally, exploring long‐term follow‐up outcomes could provide valuable insights into the sustained benefits of dietary interventions on obesity and MetS.
