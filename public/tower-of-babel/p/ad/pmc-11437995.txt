Effectiveness of indomethacin in preventing Heterotopic Ossification: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Lei Wen, Changshun Chen, Yongcheng Deng, Ge Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeterotopic ossification (HO) is a frequent complication of joint trauma or surgery, commonly occurring after hip replacements, acetabular or other joint injuries, or surgeries. Indomethacin has long been used to prevent HO and is considered the first-line therapy. However, its effectiveness and necessity for HO prevention are still debated due to mixed evidence about its efficacy and potential side effects.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, this systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated randomized controlled trials using the PICO framework. Searches were conducted across PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science. Data were extracted and assessed based on the evidence levels of the selected articles. This study was registered with the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols (INPLASY).ResultsThis analysis included 665 patients, with 347 in the Indomethacin group and 318 in the No Indomethacin group. The outcomes analyzed—HO, Gastrointestinal Side Effects, and Bone Ununion—indicated that indomethacin effectively prevents HO. The meta-analysis revealed that the Indomethacin group experienced a significant reduction in the occurrence of grade I-II HO compared to the No Indomethacin group, but not for grade III-IV HO. Gastrointestinal side effects were notably higher in the Indomethacin group. The incidence of bone nonunion was higher in the Indomethacin group, although not statistically significant.ConclusionsThe meta-analysis suggests that indomethacin is effective in preventing HO, particularly for Brooker grade I-II, rather than Brooker grade III-IV. Special attention should be given to gastrointestinal complications when using indomethacin. Further prospective randomized controlled studies are required to confirm these findings.Keywords: Indomethacin, Heterotopic ossification, Gastrointestinal side effects, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Heterotopic ossification (HO) refers to the abnormal formation of bone in soft tissues and is classified into congenital and acquired types [1, 2]. Congenital HO is often linked to mutations in the ACVR1/ALK2 gene, causing fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP). Acquired HO primarily results from injury or orthopedic surgery [2]. It frequently complicates recovery from trauma or surgery in areas such as the hip [3, 4], acetabulum [5], elbow [6, 7], and other joints and can also occur around limb joints following spinal cord injuries [8, 9]. Severe periarticular HO can significantly restrict joint motion and adversely affect quality of life. The incidence of HO following hip replacement surgery is notably high, ranging from 15–90% [10, 11]. Following acetabular fracture surgery, HO also exhibits a high incidence rate. A meta-analysis by Cantrell et al., which included 5,028 patients, reported that 36% of patients who did not receive preventive treatment developed HO [5]. Kundel et al. reported a 49% incidence of HO after distal humeral surgery [12]. The mechanisms driving HO are not yet fully understood; however, recent studies suggest that damage to skeletal muscle activates BMPs and TGF-β signaling in neutrophils, which promotes the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells and progenitor cells, thereby facilitating HO [13]. Indomethacin has long been the preferred medication for preventing HO [14, 15], with many studies confirming its efficacy in significantly reducing the incidence, particularly of severe HO [16, 17]. Moed et al. reported that indomethacin reduced the occurrence of Brooker class III and IV HO from 56–6% [18]. However, some studies challenge its effectiveness and highlight potential side effects [19], including an increased risk of nonunion in posterior wall fractures [20]. Thus, there remains no consensus on the efficacy and necessity of indomethacin for HO prevention. To objectively assess the effectiveness and side effects of indomethacin in preventing HO, we conducted a comprehensive search for relevant prospective randomized controlled trials and performed a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Materials and methods

Data sources and search strategy

Our systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials adhered to the PICO framework in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. We searched four databases—PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science—using both MeSH terms and their equivalent free terms with the keywords: (Indomethacin) AND (Ossification, Heterotopic) AND (Randomized Controlled Trials as Topic). The initial search was conducted in July 2024, with a follow-up on August 1 to capture any additional studies, extending the search from the inception of each database to the present. The search strategy in the Pubmed database is outlined below: (((“Indomethacin“[Mesh]) OR ((((((((Indometacin) OR (osmosin)) OR (Indocid)) OR (Indomethacin Hydrochloride)) OR (Amuno)) OR (Indocin)) OR (Metindol)) OR (Indomet 140))) AND ((“Ossification, Heterotopic“[Mesh]) OR (((((((Ossification, Ectopic) OR (Ossification, Pathologic)) OR (Ossification, Pathological)) OR (Pathological Ossification)) OR (Pathologic Ossification)) OR (Heterotopic Ossification)) OR (Ectopic Ossification)))) AND ((“Randomized Controlled Trials as Topic“[Mesh]) OR (((Clinical Trials, Randomized) OR (Trials, Randomized Clinical)) OR (Controlled Clinical Trials, Randomized))).

A total of 117 studies were identified, 36 duplicate articles were excluded. Titles and abstracts of 38 studies were screened. Excluding one article for which the full text was not accessible, 43 articles underwent full-text evaluation, and 6 were ultimately included in the analysis. These studies directly compared the incidence of HO in patients who received or did not receive indomethacin. Figure 1 presents the PRISMA flowchart utilized in our study.

PRISMA flow diagram of screening process

To mitigate bias in literature evaluation, two authors (LW and CC) independently searched for and assessed the articles, consulting a third author (GC) in case of disagreement. Our study followed the PICO model, defining the patient (P) as individuals undergoing surgical treatment for hip replacement, acetabular fracture, or elbow injury; the intervention (I) as the administration of indomethacin, categorizing these patients into the Indomethacin group; the comparison (C) as patients who did not receive indomethacin, forming the No Indomethacin group; and the outcomes (O) as the primary measure of the incidence of HO, with secondary outcomes including complications and fracture nonunion rates.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria encompassed patients undergoing hip replacement, acetabular or other joint fractures or dislocations receiving indomethacin or no indomethacin post-surgery, articles written in English, involving human subjects, and published in randomized controlled trials before July 2024, with a minimum radiographic follow-up period of 12 weeks. The exclusion criteria encompassed spinal cord injuries, articles in languages other than English, retrospective studies, case reports, conference proceedings, and systematic reviews.

Quality assessment and data extraction

Two authors (LW and CC) assessed the quality of the included studies with reference to the bias risk assessment criteria of the Cochrane Collaboration Network. In cases of disagreement, a third author (GC) made the decision (Fig. 2a and b). Data extraction was performed independently and cross-verified by the two assessors. Information was tabulated including author details, publication year, number of patients, average age, percentage of male patients, number of patients lost to follow-up, diagnosis and operation, method, and timing of administration, incidence of ectopic ossification, and side effects. This systematic review and meta-analysis were registered with the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols (INPLASY) under the registration number INPLASY202470130.

(a). Risk of bias. (b). Risk of bias summary

Data analysis

The results from six studies were synthesized using RevMan 5.4 and depicted in forest plots. Statistical outcomes for continuous variables were reported as mean differences (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI); for dichotomous variables, they were reported as Relative Risk (RR) and 95% CI. Heterogeneity across the studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and Higgins ' I² statistics. A fixed-effect model was applied when I2 was less than 50%, while a random-effects model was utilized for estimating the effect size under conditions of significant heterogeneity, to reduce potential bias risks. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The sensitivity analysis was conducted using StateSE 16.

HO

Six studies reported HO outcomes for both the Indomethacin and No Indomethacin groups, and meta-analysis revealed significant heterogeneity (I2 = 84%, P<0.0001), Therefore, a random-effects model was applied. Meta-analysis indicated that the incidence of HO was significantly lower in the Indomethacin group [MD = 0.58, 95% CI (0.39, 0.86), P = 0.008] (Fig. 3). Despite the pronounced heterogeneity, a sensitivity analysis (one-by-one elimination method) confirmed the robustness of our findings, as excluding any study did not alter the conclusions (Fig. 4). We further employed a random effects model to conduct subgroup analyses among patients undergoing acetabular fracture and THA surgery, and the results revealed that indomethacin was effective in preventing the occurrence of HO in both subgroups(Fig. 5).

Sensitivity analysis of HO

Forest plot for HO subgroup analyses among patients undergoing acetabular fracture and THA surgery

The Brooker classification is the predominant method for classifying HO [26]. A meta-analysis of three studies using this classification assessed the preventive effect of indomethacin on HO of varying severity. The results for Brooker I-II showed moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.38), prompting the use of a fixed-effect model. The analysis revealed that indomethacin significantly reduced the incidence of Brooker I-II HO compared to the No Indomethacin group [MD = 0.76, 95% CI (0.58, 0.98), P = 0.04] (Fig. 6).

Forest plot for meta-analysis of incidence of Brooker grade I-II HO

The findings related to Brooker III-IV were unexpected. A total of 25 patients in the Indomethacin group developed grade III-IV HO, compared to 28 patients in the No Indomethacin group. Given the minimal heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.99), a fixed-effects model was utilized. The analysis revealed no significant difference in the incidence of Brooker grade III-IV HO between the two groups [MD = 0.80, 95% CI (0.49, 1.33), P = 0.39] (Fig. 7).

Forest plot for meta-analysis of incidence of Brooker grade III-IV HO

Gastrointestinal side effects

Four studies reported on gastrointestinal side effects, involving 200 patients in the Indomethacin group and 188 in the No Indomethacin group. The meta-analysis indicated very low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.86), leading to the adoption of a fixed-effect model. The analysis found that the incidence of gastrointestinal side effects was significantly higher than No Indomethacin group [MD = 9.56, 95% CI (2.36, 38.68), P = 0.002] (Fig. 8). In the Indomethacin group, 19 gastrointestinal adverse events were reported, including one ulcer and two cases of gastrointestinal bleeding.

Forest plot for meta-analysis of gastrointestinal side effects

Bone union

Two studies addressed the occurrence of fracture nonunion. Among 116 patients in the Indomethacin group, 19 experienced nonunions, as opposed to 4 nonunions among 83 patients in the No Indomethacin group. The meta-analysis showed negligible heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.79), and a fixed-effect model was implemented. The results indicated no significant difference in the rate of bone nonunion between the two groups [MD = 2.10, 95% CI (0.84, 5.24), P = 0.11] (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Indomethacin has been widely used to prevent HO, with many studies confirming its effectiveness [16, 27–29]. However, recent research has challenged this view, suggesting that indomethacin does not mitigate HO and may lead to complications [19, 21, 30]. Our meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) affirms the beneficial role of indomethacin in preventing HO. We utilized the widely accepted Brooker classification to assess the severity of HO, categorizing it into Brooker I-II and Brooker III-IV based on the data. The meta-analysis of HO demonstrated significant heterogeneity. Notably, the 95% CI for the study “Anderson 1989” showed minimal overlap with other studies, identifying it as a potential primary source of heterogeneity. To ascertain the meta-analysis’s reliability, we conducted further subgroup analyses for ORIF of acetabular fractures and THA. Sensitivity analyses employed the one-by-one elimination method, yet results remained consistent and stable; thus, these were retained in the final analysis. Interestingly, our results were inconsistent with previous reports [17, 18, 28], indicating that indomethacin effectively reduces the incidence of mild to moderate HO (grade I-II). Conversely, while indomethacin also seems to diminish severe HO (grade III-IV) more effectively than the no indomethacin group, but the difference was not statistically significant. We investigated why indomethacin led to varying incidence rates of HO across different injury severities. A more severe local injury may exacerbate HO incidence [31]. It has been suggested that the timing from injury to surgery in trauma patients may also influence outcomes [32]. Recent research implicates the Kinesin Gene KIF26B in modulating HO severity [33]. However, the severity and clinical significance of post-traumatic ectopic ossification show substantial individual variation, and the underlying mechanisms remain elusive [31]. The studies we included provided limited data, precluding a more thorough analysis. Consequently, further randomized controlled trials are needed for confirmation. However, the meta-analysis suggests that indomethacin is marginally effective in preventing clinically significant HO, aligning generally with findings by Atwan [21] and Karunakar [22].

Historically, indomethacin was regarded as the gold standard for managing HO [14]. However, a recent network meta-analysis focusing on total hip replacements identified celecoxib as the most effective preventive measure, with indomethacin ranking second for grade IV cases [34]. Another study showed that celecoxib had fewer side effects than indomethacin [35]. Migliorini et al. conducted a meta-analysis of 1526 patients, comparing the efficacy of both elective and non-elective NSAIDs in preventing HO post-total hip arthroplasty (THA). They found that both types were effective, though no significant differences were observed between them [36]. An expert opinion from Germany, grounded in Level I evidence, indicated that celecoxib, naproxen, and diclofenac are the most effective options for preventing HO in patients undergoing primary THA [37]. HO commonly occurs after acetabular fracture surgery, especially with posterior or extended approach [38–40]. Our subgroup analysis supports the efficacy of indomethacin in reducing HO occurrences. Radiotherapy has also been proven effective, though its comparison with indomethacin remains contentious. According to Burd et al. [41], both radiotherapy and indomethacin significantly prevent HO following acetabular fracture surgery, yet preferences vary between studies [42]. Unfortunately, our data was insufficient to analyze HO incidence across different surgical techniques for acetabular fractures.

Gastrointestinal side effects are common with Non-Steroidal Antiinflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs), occurring at a rate of approximately 4.7% [43], despite some studies employing drugs that inhibit gastric acid secretion [4]. Our study found that the incidence of gastrointestinal side effects was 9.5% (9/200), which was higher than that reported in previous studies. Our meta-analysis revealed a significantly higher incidence of gastrointestinal side effects in the indomethacin group, including 1 case of an ulcer and 2 cases of gastrointestinal bleeding. In fact, the actual incidence of indomethacin-related complications may be higher, as many patients developed gastrointestinal and other complications, withdrew from the study, and were lost to follow-up, these patients were not included in the final analysis. Therefore, gastrointestinal complications necessitate careful monitoring when using indomethacin. Shortening the duration of medication may be one way to reduce the side effects of indomethacin. Dorn et al. compared the preventive effect of taking indomethacin for 4 days and 8 days on HO, and they reported that the gastrointestinal side effects were only 3.6% (9/249). Currently, most studies have a treatment course of 6 weeks, and their study recommends 8 days as the treatment course. However, this view lacks more study support [44].

Several studies have indicated that nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs negatively impact fracture healing [42, 45] by inhibiting the osteogenic differentiation of osteoblast progenitor cells [46], a finding also applicable to indomethacin. Indomethacin impairs blood flow at the osteotomy site, adversely affects callus formation and Haversian system remodeling and leads to decreased bone mineral content and strength [47]. The analysis of only 2 included studies suggested a higher rate of fracture non-union in the indomethacin group, although the difference was not statistically significant. The available data are insufficient to conclude the effects of indomethacin on fracture healing, underscoring the need for further research.

This systematic review and meta-analysis have several limitations. First, despite incorporating the largest number of randomized controlled studies to date, the overall sample size is still considered small. Additionally, the study included a variety of diseases and fracture types. Although we selected sites prone to HO and excluded patients with spinal cord injuries, the study’s generalizability remains limited, underscoring the need for further high-quality research. Second, functional outcomes in enrolled patients were not evaluated due to limited data availability; Third, the data are inadequate for subgroup analysis of different fracture types and surgical approaches for acetabular fractures. Finally, there is insufficient data to assess the preventive effects of various indomethacin treatment durations on HO. Most studies implemented a 6-week course, but some suggested benefits from shorter treatment durations, which are not conclusive.

Conclusions

Our meta-analysis supports the preventive effect of indomethacin on heterotopic ossification, particularly for Brooker grades I-II. Gastrointestinal complications warrant particular attention when using indomethacin. More prospective randomized controlled studies are essential to validate our findings.
