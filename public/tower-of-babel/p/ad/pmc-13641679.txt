Global epidemiological patterns and disease burden of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated liver disease, and alcohol-associated liver disease
Authors: Pojsakorn Danpanichkul, Matheus Souza, Primrose Tothanarungroj, Aijaz Ahmed, Donghee Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The 2023 multisociety Delphi consensus redefined the nomenclature for steatotic liver disease (SLD) by replacing nonalcoholic fatty liver disease with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and introducing metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated liver disease (MetALD) and alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD). This revised framework is expected to enhance epidemiological surveillance, phenotyping of SLD, and public health interpretation. This review summarizes contemporary literature on the global epidemiology, disease burden, and comparative outcomes of MASLD, MetALD, and ALD. MASLD remains the most prevalent SLD subtype, affecting approximately 30–40% of adults worldwide, with a rising burden over time. MASLD rapidly increases in regions with lower sociodemographic indices. MetALD affects an estimated 2–8% of adults and represents an important overlap phenotype, although its prevalence is likely underestimated due to frequent underreporting of alcohol intake. ALD has a lower prevalence but contributes disproportionately to higher liver-related morbidity and mortality and is reported to have a marked regional variation linked to patterns of alcohol consumption. SLD has emerged as a major global epidemic, with MASLD imposing the greatest population burden and MetALD/ALD disproportionately contributing to severe liver-related outcomes. Across SLD phenotypes, cardiovascular disease is a major cause of death in non-cirrhotic disease, while liver-related outcomes show a gradient associated with alcohol exposure. Significant epidemiologic limitations include heterogeneity in the steatosis assessment methods, limited availability of cardiometabolic criteria, and reliance on self-reported alcohol consumption. Mitigating this growing burden of SLD requires public health policies that incorporate metabolic risk prevention, regulation of alcohol consumption, and early risk stratification.

INTRODUCTION

In 2023, a multisociety consensus by leading hepatology organizations worldwide, including the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD), the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL), and the Latin American Association for the Study of the Liver (ALEH), redefined the classification of fatty liver disease to steatotic liver disease (SLD) by replacing nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). This change was intended not only to reduce stigmatizing terminology but, more importantly, to define the condition using an affirmative biological construct-metabolic dysfunction- rather than by exclusion [1,2]. These modifications have essential implications for epidemiological research and public health, as they enable more precise phenotyping of disease burden across populations and may refine assessments of regional and temporal trends in SLD-related morbidity and mortality. While recent publications from our research group have mainly published US data with focus on burden of SLD subtypes, hepatic fibrosis and long-term outcomes in lean MASLD [3-5], this review article provides a global perspective. This article is a comprehensive review of global epidemiological patterns and disease burden across all three SLD phenotypes (MASLD, metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated liver disease [MetALD], and alcohol-associated liver disease [ALD]) under the new nomenclature defined in 2023 [6,7]. In addition, we incorporated the most recent Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2023 estimates with detailed regional and sociodemographic stratification, and we added the rapidly evolving MetALD literature (including underreporting of alcohol use and the use of biomarkers). We have presented direct comparisons of hepatic and extrahepatic outcomes, illustrating the “SLD burden paradox”, and described updated public policy recommendations tailored to the new classification [8,9]. In summary, this review article provides an updated SLD nomenclature and diagnostic framework, examines the current global epidemiology and disease burden across all subtypes of SLD, and attempts to compare phenotype-specific clinical burden and outcomes. We have tried to address the key methodological limitations, knowledge gaps, and public health priorities for future research and policy.

UPDATED SLD NOMENCLATURE AND CASE DEFINITIONS

SLD is an umbrella term for fatty liver disease identified through imaging, histology, or other evidence of hepatic steatosis, with secondary causes excluded [2]. As shown in Figure 1, MASLD is diagnosed in adults with SLD who also meet at least one cardiometabolic criterion (such as overweight/obesity, prediabetes/type 2 diabetes mellitus [T2DM], hypertension, high triglycerides, and/or low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol), after excluding other causes of steatosis and with alcohol intake below specified thresholds (≤20 g/day for women and ≤30 g/day for men) [2]. Epidemiological research from large population-based cohorts supports this terminological modification, demonstrating that individuals who met the previous definition of NAFLD also meet the updated definition of MASLD [10-12]. This strong concordance suggests that historical longitudinal datasets remain generally applicable for MASLD. The new terminology offers a pathophysiology-based framework that emphasizes the key metabolic mechanisms of liver injury, particularly insulin resistance and systemic inflammation [10,13,14]. MetALD is defined by SLD, the presence of at least one cardiometabolic criterion, and more than significant alcohol consumption (20–50 g/day in women or 30–60 g/day in men) [2]. This overlap category was introduced to illustrate that combined metabolic dysfunction and alcohol exposure may synergistically promote hepatic injury and progression of fibrosis [15]. Consequently, individuals with MetALD may experience more accelerated progression of disease than those with MASLD without significant alcohol intake [16]. Individuals with SLD are classified as having ALD when their alcohol consumption exceeds approximately 50–60 g/day (or the sex-specific 350 g/week for women and 420 g/week for men), regardless of concurrent cardiometabolic risk factors (CMRF) [2]. In addition, individuals without any CMRF but consuming alcohol above 20–30 g/day may also be categorized as having ALD [2]. However, “metabolically healthy” ALD, defined as ALD in the absence of CMRF, appears to be exceptionally rare. A study based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) found that only 0.08% of participants met criteria for ALD in the absence of any CMRF [17]. Similarly, in a Danish study of 446 individuals with excessive alcohol consumption, only one individual (0.2%) had ALD without CMRF [18]. These observations collectively support that excessive alcohol consumption is frequently associated with CMRFs, making ALD without CMRF an unusual phenotype in population-based and clinical cohorts [18]. Classification should remain dynamic as patterns of alcohol consumption change over time [19]. This fluidity reflects evolving exposure to risk factors and underscores the need for continuous, longitudinal reassessment of both alcohol consumption and cardiometabolic criteria in clinical practice and epidemiologic research [19].

GLOBAL EPIDEMIOLOGY OF SLD

The new SLD nomenclature enables a more precise separation of the population-level disease burden into three partially overlapping yet distinct phenotypes that map onto different risk-factor environments across regions of the world [2]. In this setting, the global distribution of MASLD, MetALD, and ALD is shaped by various factors, including the epidemics of obesity and T2DM, patterns and intensities of alcohol consumption, genetic variation, and the demographic structures of different populations [20]. This manuscript is a narrative review of the published literature. Data source presented in this section derive from three complementary but methodologically distinct data sources that are not directly comparable: (1) systematic reviews and meta-analyses based on the results from selected observational cohorts and population-based studies with imaging or histologic confirmation of steatosis; (2) various country- and region-specific prevalence data from population, community, or health checkup-based studies summarized in Figure 2, with detailed individual study-level data provided in Supplementary Table 1; and (3) modeled estimates of prevalence and mortality from the GBD 2023 study, which uses Bayesian meta-regression integrating multiple input data sources, risk factors, and cause-of-death modeling across 204 countries and territories with uncertainty intervals (UI) summarized in Figure 3. For the GBD 2023 estimates presented in Tables 1 and 2, the authors extracted data from the GBD 2023 database and performed additional stratification by sex, World Health Organization (WHO) region, and socio-demographic index (SDI) level. Annual percent change (APC) was calculated by the authors using standard methods: log-linear regression of the natural logarithm of the age-standardized rates against calendar year [21]. Case definitions for SLD subtypes follow the consensus statement from the multisociety Delphi panel. For MASLD- and ALD-related outcomes, GBD modeling incorporates relevant International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes and cause-of-death data, using established Bayesian meta-regression assumptions. Full details on methodology, modeling inputs, sensitivity analyses, and all methodological assumptions are provided in Supplementary Methods. We provided an epidemiological summary of SLD in Figure 2, based on available observational studies (population-, community-, or health checkup-based studies) published after 2024, reflecting the new SLD nomenclature introduced in 2023. Because these maps summarize the available epidemiological data at the country level, the figures should not be interpreted as standardized global estimates, as currently available data are insufficient for this purpose. Differences between countries may reflect heterogeneity in study design, population, diagnostic modalities, and alcohol assessment methods. In addition, we provided detailed individual study data in Supplementary Table 1.

MASLD

Incidence

The incidence and prevalence estimates for MASLD vary widely across studies, likely because of differences in diagnostic methods and the populations examined. In a meta-analysis of 12 studies including 256,757 individuals diagnosed by ultrasonography, a pooled incidence rate of 48.89 (95% confidence interval [CI] 38.49–61.93) per 1,000 person-years was reported [22]. In another meta-analysis of 63 studies comprising 1,201,807 individuals, the incidence rate was 4,612.8 (95% CI 3,931.5–5,294.2) per 100,000 person-years [23]. Incidence was higher in males than in females, and populations with obesity were associated with an approximately threefold higher risk of developing MASLD compared with those without obesity.

According to the GBD 2023 study, the global incidence of MASLD was estimated at 48.61 million new cases in 2023 (95% UI 44.86–52.40 million). The global age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) was recorded at 582.67 per 100,000 (95% UI 535.36–630.51). Between 2001 and 2023, the ASIR exhibited a significant upward trend, with an APC of +0.66% (95% CI 0.63–0.70; P<0.001). Detailed incident numbers and ASIR of MASLD, stratified by sex, WHO region, and SDI level, are summarized in Table 1. While both sexes shared a similar proportion of the global incidence, males exhibited a slightly higher number of new cases, ASIR, and growth rate, accounting for 25.38 million incident cases (95% UI 23.39–27.39 million) and an ASIR of 610.27 per 100,00 (95% UI 561.52–661.47), with an APC of +0.69% (95% CI 0.60–0.78; P<0.001). Females accounted for 23.24 million incident cases (95% UI 21.34–25.17 million) and an ASIR of 552.93 (95% UI 507.38–598.44), with an APC of +0.64% (95% CI 0.55–0.73; P<0.001). In 2023, the Western Pacific region reported the largest number of incident cases at 13.97 million (95% UI 12.87–15.14 million), closely followed by Southeast Asia with 10.61 million cases (95% UI 9.69–11.61 million). The ASIR was disproportionately elevated in the Eastern Mediterranean region, reaching 897.65 per 100,000 (95% UI 833.68–960.77). The most pronounced regional increase in new cases occurred in the Western Pacific region, with an APC of +0.77% (95% CI 0.68–0.86; P<0.001). Positive APC values were sustained throughout other WHO regions worldwide. Stratified by SDI level, the high-SDI countries showed the steepest increases in incidence rates at +0.80% (95% CI 0.74–0.86; P<0.001). The ASIR was highest in the middle-SDI countries at 697.19 per 100,000 (95% UI 641.12–750.97). Consistent with all previous stratifications, APCs for ASIR were positive along the full SDI spectrum.

Prevalence

MASLD is the most common type of SLD worldwide. A meta-analysis by Younossi et al. [22] estimated the global prevalence of MASLD was 30.05% (95% CI 27.88–32.32) based on 92 population-based studies (9,361,716 individuals) conducted between 1990 and 2019. The global pooled prevalence of MASLD increased by approximately 50.4%, increasing from 25.26% (95% CI 21.59–29.33) in 1990–2006 to 38.2% (95% CI 33.72–42.89) in 2016–2019 [22]. Longitudinal analyses using data from the prior GBD study also confirmed a concerning rise in the absolute number of cases, paralleling the growing burden of metabolic syndrome [24]. Projections suggest that the prevalence of MASLD could reach 55.7% among adults by 2040 [25]. Following the updated SLD nomenclature, Ho et al. [26] recently conducted a meta-analysis that included 42 studies (11.28 million individuals) and reported a MASLD prevalence of 33.6% (95% CI 28.1–39.5) in the general population, consistent with prior NAFLD-era data. However, estimates of meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution. This is because there is considerable variability arising from differences in diagnostic methods, alcohol consumption assessment, sampling frameworks, and population characteristics.

The GBD 2023 study estimates the global prevalence of MASLD in 2023 at 1,261.57 million cases (95% UI 1,160.28–1,394.72 million). The global age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) was identified at 14,429.25 per 100,000 (95% UI 13,268.33–15,990.58). Between 2001 and 2023, the ASPR exhibited a significant increasing trend, with an APC of +0.84% (95% CI 0.81–0.88; P<0.001). Detailed prevalence counts and ASPRs of MASLD, stratified by sex, WHO region, and SDI level, are provided in Table 1. Males demonstrated a slightly higher absolute prevalence, ASPR, and growth rates, with 669.52 million cases (95% UI 615.40–740.33 million), an ASPR of 15,616.40 per 100,000 (95% UI 14,349.16–17,263.25), and an APC of +0.88% (95% CI 0.81–0.95; P<0.001). In contrast, females accounted for 592.05 million prevalence cases (95% UI 542.21–653.75 million), an ASPR of 13,245.20 (95% UI 12,132.00–14,692.56), and an APC of +0.81% (95% CI 0.77–0.85; P<0.001). The Western Pacific region reported the highest number of prevalent cases, at 407.98 million (95% UI 374.09–449.94 million), and the most pronounced regional increase, with an APC of +0.92% (95% CI 0.82–1.01; P<0.001). The Eastern Mediterranean region, however, exhibited the highest ASPR at 25,153.63 per 100,000 (95% UI 23,137.73–27,590.33). All WHO regions demonstrated positive APC values (Table 1). ASPRs of MASLD per 100,000 population by country are presented in Figure 3A and Supplementary Table 2. Classified by SDI, the high-SDI countries had the highest number of prevalent cases, at 457.59 million (95% UI 420.58–503.11 million), and the most rapid growth rate, at +0.98% (95% CI 0.90–1.05; P<0.001). ASPR trends remained positive across all SDI countries. Conversely, the middle-SDI countries demonstrated the highest ASPR at 17,704.48 per 100,000 (95% UI 16,280.53–19,541.34) (Table 1).

High-risk populations: Within high-risk groups, MASLD is even more prevalent. Among individuals with T2DM, the pooled global prevalence of MASLD is approximately 65% [27,28], and 70% among those who are overweight or obese [29].

Pediatric populations: In the pediatric population, the prevalence of MASLD is lower, affecting approximately 10%–16% of children [30,31], with higher rates of 40–50% observed in obese children [32]. Pediatric MASLD frequently persists into adulthood [33]. It exhibits several distinct features compared with adult MASLD, including differences in histology, earlier onset, and unique pathophysiologic mechanisms, although both forms share central metabolic pathways and genetic susceptibility [34,35]. A key histologic distinction is the predominance of periportal (zone 1) steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in children, whereas adults more commonly display centrilobular/pericentral (zone 3) patterns [34]. Perinatal and early-life exposures also play a much greater role in pediatric MASLD than in adult-onset disease. Maternal obesity, gestational diabetes, and early nutritional factors may alter metabolic programming, promote mitochondrial dysfunction, and induce epigenetic changes that increase susceptibility later in life [36-38].

Mortality

Fu et al. [39] conducted a meta-analysis of 13 studies, including 595,755 individuals with MASLD, and reported a higher risk of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR] 1.32; 95% CI 1.09–1.59). In a population-based cohort study from Sweden involving patients with biopsy-confirmed MASLD, overall mortality was also higher than in matched controls (adjusted HR 1.93; 95% CI 1.86–2.00) [40]. Mortality risk increased progressively with increasing severity within the MASLD spectrum [40,41]. A systematic review comparing lean and non-lean MASLD found greater all-cause mortality among those with lean MASLD (pooled HR 1.43; 95% CI 1.15–1.77) [3]. In the United States, national mortality data indicate that age-standardized deaths attributed to MASLD were increasing before the COVID-19 pandemic (average quarterly percent change: 2.5%; 95% CI 1.9–2.8%) and increased more rapidly during the pandemic period (7.8%; 95% CI 3.4–9.5%) [42].

The GBD 2023 study estimates that MASLD accounted for 89,760 deaths worldwide in 2023 (95% UI 64,650–120,280), with a global age-standardized death rate (ASDR) of 0.99 per 100,000 (95% UI 0.72–1.32). Unlike the rising trends observed in incidence and prevalence, the global mortality rate due to MASLD declined significantly from 2001 to 2023 (APC –0.26%; 95% CI –0.41 to –0.12; P<0.001). Detailed estimates of absolute deaths and ASDRs, stratified by sex, WHO region, and SDI level, are provided in Table 1. By sex, females had a higher number of deaths, 46,850 (95% UI 34,310–62,560) versus 42,910 in males (95% UI 30,100–58,550).

Mortality declined in both sexes from 2001 to 2023, with APCs of –0.22% in males (95% CI –0.42 to –0.03; P=0.022) and –0.28% in females (95% CI –0.49 to –0.07; P=0.010). Across WHO regions, the Americas had the highest mortality burden, reporting both the largest number of deaths, at 29,160 (95% UI 21,320–38,440), and the highest ASDR, at 2.13 per 100,000 (95% UI 1.56–2.79). The Americans demonstrated the most pronounced increase in mortality across study periods (APC +1.04%; 95% CI 0.82 to 1.27, P<0.001). In contrast, the Western Pacific region had the lowest ASDR at 0.41 per 100,000 (95% UI 0.29–0.56) and the largest decline in mortality (APC –1.11%; 95% CI –1.48 to –0.73; P<0.001). ASDRs of MASLD per 100,000 population by country are presented in Figure 3B and Supplementary Table 2. When stratified by SDI category, the high-middle SDI countries exhibited the highest ASDR (1.17 per 100,000; 95% UI 0.87–1.53). Mortality trends over time were not statistically significant in most SDI countries, except in low-SDI countries, where mortality decreased (APC –0.51; 95% CI –0.69 to –0.33; P<0.001). Overall, temporal patterns were more clearly differentiated by WHO region than by SDI level, suggesting that geographic differences—such as healthcare access, prevention efforts, or underlying population risk—may influence mortality more than socioeconomic development alone.

Box 1. MASLD – key epidemiological summary

· MASLD is the most common SLD subtype and accounts for the largest population burden, with a consistently rising prevalence across meta-analyses and GBD estimates.

· MASLD is associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality, which increases with disease severity and stage.

· Special populations: Approximately 65% prevalence among individuals with T2DM and 70% among overweight/obese adults; approximately 10–16% in children and 40–50% in obese children.

MetALD

Incidence and prevalence

MetALD is situated at an intermediate point on the SLD continuum and affects 2–8% of the adult population. Because MetALD is a newly defined category, its epidemiology, natural history, and prognosis remain characterized by substantial uncertainty, and current estimates should be interpreted cautiously as the evidence base continues to develop. A US population-based study showed that the age-adjusted prevalence of MetALD was 2.2% (95% CI 1.8–2.6; cut-off value of 285 dB/m) and 3.2% (95% CI 2.8–3.5; cutoff value of 263 dB/m) [5]. The prevalence was higher in non-Hispanic White (2.5%), followed by Hispanic (1.7%), non-Hispanic Black (1.4%), and non-Hispanic Asian (0.4%) individuals [5]. Ho et al. [26] pooled data from 15 studies (10,577,146 adults) and estimated the prevalence of MetALD to be 4.1% (95% CI 3.1–5.3) in the general population and 9.4% (95% CI 7.5–11.8) in populations with SLD. Another meta-analysis of 19 studies (4.5 million individuals with SLD) reported an overall pooled prevalence of MetALD of approximately 10% (95% CI 7–13), though estimates varied widely across studies (3–24%) [43]. MetALD was more frequent in males than in females. Evidence on regional patterns is emerging but remains less understood. Preliminary data from the available studies (primarily Ho et al. [26]) suggest that the prevalence of MetALD may be higher in Europe (8.6%) than in the Western Pacific (2.7%) or the Americas (3.9%). This hypothesis-generating observation is consistent with the epidemiology of alcohol intake, which tends to be more prevalent in European settings [44]. Various country- and region-specific prevalence data from population, community, or health-checkup studies are summarized visually in Figure 2B. Larger studies with standardized methods are needed to confirm these patterns. Importantly, all estimates likely underrepresent the true prevalence of MetALD due to systematic underreporting of alcohol intake [45,46]. A recent Danish prospective study of 2,924 participants with CMRFs or a history of heavy alcohol use reported that underestimation of alcohol intake was more common in those with prior excessive drinking (39.5%) than in those without (11.1%) [47]. These findings indicate that MetALD may be more prevalent than currently reported. In addition, the MetALD framework has drawn attention to alcohol underreporting as an important source of misclassification when phenotyping patients across the spectrum of SLD. Estimating MetALD epidemiology for large databases such as the GBD 2023 study or CDC WONDER (Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research) was not possible due to the lack of objective estimates of alcohol consumption and no ICD-10 codes for MetALD [48,49]. Therefore, there remains an unmet need for improved epidemiological characterization worldwide using objective alcohol assessment methods.

Specific populations: Among lean US populations, the prevalence of MASLD was 9.3% (95% CI 7.0–11.6; 4.2 million), and MetALD was 1.3% (95% CI 0.7–2.0; 0.6 million) [4]. In contrast to MASLD, the prevalence of MetALD was similar among individuals with normal glucose tolerance, prediabetes, and diabetes in the US population-based study [50].

Pediatric populations: The global epidemiology and clinical outcomes of MetALD in adolescents remain poorly characterized compared with those in older adults [5,51]. According to NHANES data, the prevalence of MetALD among adolescents in the US is 0.6% [52]. Alcohol-related confounding is substantially less common in children [34]. When pediatric NAFLD cases were reclassified under the MASLD framework, only a small proportion were categorized as MetALD, with slightly higher rates among adolescents [53]. This contrasts with adults, in whom alcohol use contributes to a much larger MetALD subgroup [53].

Mortality

Because the SLD nomenclature is relatively new, mortality and clinical outcomes in MetALD are less well-defined than in ALD and MASLD [2]. In non-cirrhotic patients, liver-related mortality has been reported at 0.19 per 100 person-years for MetALD, corresponding to a markedly higher risk (HR 3.38; 95% CI 3.02–3.78) [54]. A retrospective cohort study based on Veterans Affairs (VA) cohort showed that over a median follow-up of 5.5 years, compared with MASLD, MetALD had a higher incidence of adverse liver outcomes (1.12 vs. 0.61 per 100 person-years; HR 1.56; 95% CI 1.50–1.62) and all-cause mortality (2.74 vs. 2.60 per 100 person-years; HR 1.08; 95% CI 1.05–1.10), but similar incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events [55]. In patients without cirrhosis, the adjusted hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) rates per 100,000 person-years were lowest for MASLD, higher for MetALD, and highest for ALD [56]. The HCC risk in MetALD was intermediate between that of MASLD and ALD [56]. A US population-based study reported that MetALD was associated with a 1.5-fold higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.45; 95% CI 1.08–1.96) compared with MASLD, with a higher hazard during a median follow-up of 27.0 years [57]. Mortality risk appears to rise sharply with disease severity: in a population-based cohort followed for 26.7 years, MetALD with advanced fibrosis had substantially higher mortality than individuals without SLD (HR 1.41 for all-cause mortality and HR 15.04 for liver-related mortality) [58]. Consistent with these findings, a multicenter prospective Korean cohort study showed that MetALD was associated with a roughly threefold increase in all-cause mortality compared with non-SLD controls (adjusted subdistribution HR 3.05; 95% CI 1.08–8.58) [59]. Regarding cause-specific mortality, MetALD was significantly associated with a higher risk of cancer-related mortality (adjusted HR 2.40; 95% CI 1.23–4.66) [60].

Box 2. MetALD – key epidemiological summary

· MetALD is an important overlap phenotype. Estimated to affect 2–8% of adults; pooled estimates suggest 4.1% in the general population and approximately 9–10% among individuals with SLD, with wide variation across studies.

· Generally, intermediate risk between MASLD and ALD for liver-related outcomes and HCC; mortality estimates vary across cohorts but are consistently higher than in MASLD in several studies.

· Special populations: Reported prevalence includes 1.3% in lean US adults and 0.6% in US adolescents; prevalence appears similar across glucose tolerance categories in US population-based data.

· Key limitation: Likely underestimated because of systematic underreporting of alcohol intake; the narrow diagnostic window can lead to frequent misclassification between MASLD, MetALD, and ALD.

ALD

Incidence

According to the GBD 2023 study, the global incidence of ALD in 2023 was estimated to be 419,430 new cases (95% UI 350,820–502,940). GBD estimates are modeled using alcohol-attributable fractions for broader ALD outcomes and may not align perfectly with the strict consensus ALD definition or with clinically defined alcohol-associated cirrhosis in cohort/registry studies. The global ASIR was 4.63 per 100,000 (95% UI 3.88–5.57). In contrast to the rising trends observed in MASLD, the global ALD incidence rate showed a significant downward trend between 2001 and 2023, wi th an APC of –1.63% (95% CI –1.66 to –1.60, P<0.001). Detailed estimates of incident cases and ASIRs, stratified by sex, WHO region, and SDI level, are provided in Table 2. Sex-specific analyses demonstrated a substantial disparity in the incident ALD burden, with males (306,810 cases; 95% UI 258,210–365,680) reporting nearly three times as many incident cases as females (112,620 cases; 95% UI 90,590–138,270). Likewise, the ASIR was markedly higher in males at 7.02 per 100,000 (95% UI 5.92–8.36) than in females at 2.36 (95% UI 1.90–2.92). However, both sexes experienced a decline in incidence rates. Regionally, Europe reported the highest ASIR at 7.87 per 100,000 (95% UI 6.79–9.15). Although APCs were negative across all WHO regions, Southeast Asia experienced the largest decline, with an APC of –2.73% (95% CI –2.90 to –2.57; P<0.001). Among SDI regions, the middle-SDI countries exhibited the highest ASIR of 5.34 per 100,000 (95% UI 4.30–6.61). All SDI countries showed decreasing trends in ALD incidence rates, with the most rapid decline occurring in low-middle SDI countries (APC –2.52%; 95% CI –2.68 to –2.36, P<0.001).

Prevalence

Compared with MASLD, ALD is reported to have a relatively low prevalence in the general population, with pooled estimates typically ranging from 1–4% in contemporary cohorts, although these estimates should be interpreted with caution given the well-documented limitations in case ascertainment. A meta-analysis published in 2023 estimated a pooled prevalence of ALD of 3.5% (95% CI 2.0–6.0) in unselected adults (10 studies; 163,819 individuals) [61]. According to SLD nomenclature, a recent meta-analysis reported a prevalence estimate of ALD of 2.2% (95% CI 1.5–3.1) in the general population (9 studies; 391,139 individuals) [26]. Importantly, these relatively low reported prevalence estimates must be reconciled with the substantial methodological limitations acknowledged elsewhere in this review. Despite the lack of consolidated global data on regional patterns, available evidence suggests that the prevalence of ALD parallels regional alcohol consumption patterns [62]. Importantly, similar to the issues associated with underestimation of MetALD, the prevalence estimates for ALD are likely to be substantially conservative due to systematic underreporting of alcohol intake, social stigma associated with heavy drinking, and inaccuracies in administrative coding. Consequently, the true burden of ALD is almost certainly underestimated in population-based and GBD data. In particular, a higher burden of ALD and its complications has been observed in settings with greater per-capita intake and binge drinking [63]. The GBD 2023 study estimated the global prevalence of ALD in 2023 at 3.15 million cases (95% UI 2.65–3.83 million), with a global ASPR of 34.58 per 100,000 (95% UI 29.12–41.69). These GBD-modeled estimates may be likely to be considerably lower than estimates of the true burden given the same constraints of under-detection of subclinical disease and inaccuracies in ICD-10 coding. Consistent with the incidence rate, the ASPR decreased significantly worldwide from 2001 to 2023, with an APC of –1.36% (95% CI –1.39 to –1.34; P<0.001). Estimates of prevalent cases and ASPRs, stratified by sex, WHO region, and SDI level, are provided in Table 2. As with incidence rates, there is a pronounced disparity in the prevalence of ALD, with males reporting nearly twice the number of prevalent cases compared to females, at 2.07 million (95% UI 1.76–2.47 million) and 1.08 million (95% UI 0.88–1.34 million), respectively. Likewise, the ASPR was markedly higher in males (47.00 per 100,000; 95% UI 39.96–55.55) than in females (22.69; 95% UI 18.57–28.27). Notably, females experienced a more rapid decline in prevalence rates from 2001 to 2023, with an APC of –1.50% (95% CI –1.54 to –1.47; P<0.001), compared with males, with an APC of –1.31% (95% CI –1.35 to –1.26; P<0.001). Regionally, Europe had the highest ASPR, at 67.47 per 100,000 (95% UI 57.51–79.32). While APCs were negative across all WHO regions, Southeast Asia experienced the most substantial decrease in prevalence (APC –2.14%; 95% CI –2.31 to –1.97; P<0.001). ASPRs of ALD per 100,000 population by country are presented in Figure 2C and Supplementary Table 3. The highest ASPR was found in the high-SDI countries at 40.39 per 100,000 (95% UI: 34.38–48.47). All SDI countries exhibited downward trends in ALD prevalence, with the most rapid decline occurring in low-middle-SDI countries (APC –2.11%; 95% CI –2.27 to –1.95; P<0.001). The burden of ALD is decreasing disproportionately in lower-SDI countries [64]. Economic growth, which increases disposable income and facilitates greater alcohol consumption, largely fuels this pattern. The resulting shift is often compounded by insufficient alcohol-control policies and limited public health prevention efforts [65].

High-risk populations: Females appear more susceptible to alcohol-related cirrhosis than males at equivalent intake levels [66,67]. For example, consuming 14–28 drinks/week is associated with a 2.3-fold higher cirrhosis risk compared to abstinent individuals, and females consistently show higher relative risks (RR) than males across intake strata [68]. At very high consumption (≥5 drinks/day), cirrhosis risk increased steeply in females (RR 12.44, 95% CI 6.65–23.27 for 5–6 drinks/day; RR 24.58, 95% CI 14.77–40.90 for ≥7 drinks/day) compared with males (RR 3.80, 95% CI 0.85–17.02; RR 6.93, 95% CI 1.07–44.99, respectively) [67]. The burden of ALD is also increasing among females [63,69]. Hispanic populations demonstrate higher susceptibility to ALD [70,71]. Native American communities experience disproportionately high mortality from ALD [72]. Metabolic factors further increase the risk of progression of ALD. Insulin resistance strongly predicts advanced fibrosis (odds ratio 3.04; 95% CI 1.90–4.87) [73]. Among heavy drinkers, obesity raises the risk for liver disease (1.5-fold) and death (2.2-fold) [66]; Diabetes is also strongly associated with the risk of ALD and is more prevalent among cirrhosis cases than controls [74]. In addition, smoking more than 16 cigarettes/day is associated with roughly a 1.6-fold higher risk of ALD-related cirrhosis [66].

Pediatric populations: Although the burden of ALD remains lower in adolescents and young adults compared to older populations, the global prevalence of ALD in adolescents and young adults has markedly increased over the past two decades [75,76]. Prevalence of ALD increased sharply with age, increasing 19-fold from ages 15–19 to 20–24 and a further 2.5-fold from ages 20–24 to 25–29 [75]. In many regions, the prevalence of ALD increased across all adolescent and young adult age groups, with approximately three-quarters of countries and territories reporting rising ALD incidence rates in these populations [75]. In the United States, the annual incidence of alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH) among adolescents and young adults increased from 2.43 per 100,000 person-years in 2002 to 6.82 in 2021, corresponding to an average annual increase of 8% [77]. Adolescents and young adults with ALD tend to experience poorer long-term outcomes than older adults, including increasing hospitalization rates, earlier progression to cirrhosis, higher rates of decompensation, and rising mortality, especially among young females [69,77-80].

Mortality

GBD estimates indicate that ALD caused 308,800 (95% UI 259,340–358,190) deaths in 2023. The mortality in ALD is highly stage- and severity-dependent: overall 5-year mortality exceeds 50%, and in severe AH, it can reach 20–40% within 3 months [81,82]. Outcomes are largely determined by disease stage, ongoing alcohol use, and treatment response. In symptomatic AH, 1-year mortality is 30% [68]. Among patients with severe AH, 3-month mortality ranges from 20% to 50%, rising to 70% in corticosteroid non-responders. Long-term prognosis is closely linked to abstinence, with a 10-year survival of 88% in abstinent patients compared to 73% in those who relapse [68,83]. In biopsy-proven ALD, mortality risk is nearly five times higher than in the general population, with 5-year cumulative mortality of 40.9% compared with 5.8% in matched controls [84]. Among patients with cirrhosis, annual mortality is approximately 8% in both compensated and decompensated cirrhosis, and 5-year mortality reaches 52.5% across the overall ALD cohort [84]. Several limitations may impact the estimates of ALD-related mortality, with the likelihood of underestimation even more pronounced than for MetALD. These limitations include widespread systematic underreporting of alcohol consumption (particularly among the heaviest drinkers who qualify for the ALD category), inaccuracies in ICD-10 coding and death-certificate documentation (which frequently fail to capture alcohol as the contributing cause or misclassify cases with ALD), stigma-related underdiagnosis of early/subclinical disease, and inconsistent quality of registry data [85,86]. Consequently, the incidence, prevalence, and mortality of ALD reported in this review article should be interpreted with caution and are likely to be considerably lower than estimates of the true burden of ALD [87,88]. Additional weaknesses include limited detection of early or asymptomatic ALD, less reliable administrative coding for AH, lack of individual-level data on alcohol intake, incomplete adjustment for key comorbidities, particularly cardiometabolic risk factors, and the frequent reliance on small cohorts or studies with short follow-up [89,90].

GBD estimates for ALD-related mortality are based on modeled alcohol-attributable liver disease and cancer using ICD codes and risk attribution. This should be distinguished from narrower consensus-defined ALD or clinically confirmed alcohol-associated cirrhosis and HCC in cohort and registry studies. The global ASDR was at 3.39 per 100,000 (95% UI 2.85–3.93). Notably, the absolute mortality burden of ALD remains disproportionately high compared to MASLD (308,800 vs. 89,760 deaths, respectively), although MASLD exhibits a vastly higher global incidence (48.61 million vs. 419,430 new cases) and prevalence (1,261.57 million vs. 3.15 million total cases). Mirroring trends in incidence and prevalence, the ASDR decreased significantly worldwide between 2001 and 2023, with an APC of –1.62% (95% CI –1.73 to –1.50; P<0.001). Detailed estimates of death numbers and ASDRs, stratified by sex, WHO region, and SDI level, are summarized in Table 2. Sex-specific analyses revealed a significant disparity in ALD-related mortality. Males accounted for 238,910 deaths (95% UI 199,140–279,930) and an ASDR of 5.47 per 100,000 (95% UI 4.59–6.40), whereas females accounted for 69,890 deaths (95% UI 56,030–83,330) and an ASDR of 1.45 (95% UI 1.17–1.73). The decline in death rates over the study period was more pronounced among females (APC –1.81%; 95% CI –1.95 to –1.67; P<0.001) than males (APC –1.56%; 95% CI –1.68 to –1.44; P<0.001). At the regional level, Europe reported the highest ASDR, consistent with its incidence and prevalence patterns, with 5.78 per 100,000 (95% UI 5.01–6.53). While APCs were negative across all WHO regions, the most substantial decline was observed in the Eastern Mediterranean (APC –2.60%; 95% CI –3.02 to –2.19; P<0.001). ASDRs of ALD per 100,000 population by country are presented in Figure 3D and Supplementary Table 3. Stratified by SDI, the highest ASDR was identified in the low-SDI countries at 4.53 per 100,000 (95% UI 3.38–5.84). Mortality declined across the entire spectrum of SDI, with the most rapid reduction noted in low-middle SDI countries (APC –2.04%; 95% CI –2.31 to –1.76; P<0.001).

Box 3. ALD – key epidemiological summary

· ALD has lower population prevalence than MASLD but contributes disproportionately to liver-related morbidity and mortality, with a clear dose-response gradient by alcohol exposure.

· Special populations: Increasing burden among adolescents and young adults; females are more susceptible to alcohol-related liver injury at equivalent intake; metabolic cofactors further increase risk.

· Key limitation: Substantial underestimation due to self-report bias, ICD coding inaccuracies, under-detection of early or asymptomatic disease, and death-certificate under-attribution.

LIVER AND EXTRAHEPATIC BURDEN ACROSS SUBTYPES OF SLD

Among non-cirrhotic individuals with SLD, cardiovascular disease (CVD) and extrahepatic malignancy are the dominant causes of death across all subtypes of SLD [54]. CVD is frequent among patients with SLD [55,91,92]. In a large Veterans Health Administration cohort, all subtypes of SLD were associated with higher cardiovascular risk than no SLD, and the 10-year cumulative incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events was nearly identical across MASLD, MetALD, and ALD, converging at approximately 14% [55]. This pattern is supported by a systematic review and meta-analysis of 11.58 million individuals, in which cardiovascular event rates were comparable between MetALD and MASLD [93]. However, population-based data suggest more heterogeneity. In a Korean community cohort, cardiovascular risk rose stepwise from MASLD (HR 1.27; 95% CI 1.12–1.45) to MetALD (HR 1.88; 95% CI 1.33–2.65) [94]. Another nationwide Korean study also showed a graded rise in CVD risk across subtypes of SLD, increasing from MASLD (subdistribution HR 1.19; 95% CI 1.15–1.24) to MetALD (HR 1.28; 95% CI 1.20–1.36) and ALD (HR 1.29; 95% CI 1.18–1.41) compared to no SLD [95]. The VA cohort primarily consisted of older males (92%, mean age 59.5) and was composed mainly of US Veterans, in contrast to the Korean cohorts, which were community-based populations with different baseline cardiovascular risk profiles and longer follow-up periods [55,94,95]. The Veterans Health Administration cohort utilized AUDIT-C for a more granular assessment of drinking patterns, whereas the Korean studies relied on self-reported alcohol intake thresholds, which are more susceptible to recall and cultural reporting bias [55,94,95]. Higher rates of adverse liver-related events in the US cohort (HR 1.56, 95% CI 1.50–1.62 for MetALD; HR 2.33, 95% CI 2.20–2.47 for ALD) may have introduced competing risks, thereby reducing the observed incidence of cardiovascular events [55]. A recent meta-analysis of 17.6 million individuals found increased cardiovascular risk across all SLD subtypes compared to individuals without SLD, but did not determine consistent differences between subtypes, highlighting heterogeneity [96].

SLD has been linked to a higher risk of malignancy [3,97]. In a 2025 meta-analysis of 24 cohort studies (11.8 million individuals), MetALD was associated with a slightly higher risk of extrahepatic cancers than MASLD (HR 1.03; 95% CI 1.01–1.06), although the absolute difference was minimal [93]. A nationwide study from Japan reported a similar pattern, indicating that gastrointestinal cancer risk rose with greater alcohol intake across SLD subtypes, with MetALD and ALD representing greater risks of esophageal, gastric, and colorectal cancers than MASLD [98]. Collectively, these findings suggest that alcohol exposure confers an incremental cancer risk beyond baseline metabolic risk. Notably, extrahepatic cancers are considerably more common than HCC in MASLD (10.58 vs. 1.25 per 1,000 person-years) [99]. Although direct comparison between ALD and MASLD for overall extrahepatic cancer is limited, a large US VA study found similar 10-year cumulative incidences of death from extrahepatic cancer among non-cirrhotic patients: 7.5% (MASLD), 7.4% (MetALD), and 7.4% (ALD) [54].

Liver-related outcomes diverge substantially by alcohol exposure, with a clear gradient consistent with a dose–response pattern (ALD>MetALD>MASLD) [55,96]. This is also confirmed by a meta-analysis of 24 cohort studies involving 11,575,558 individuals (9.80 million with MASLD and 1.77 million with MetALD) that found MetALD was associated with higher risks of liver-related events, HCC, and extrahepatic cancer incidence than MASLD [93]. This represented the first comprehensive meta-analysis and the largest dataset directly comparing these outcomes between MASLD and MetALD populations, providing evidence supporting the new nomenclature that distinguishes them as separate clinical entities. Notably, a multicenter propensity score–matched study found that patients with ALD and CMRF were also at increased risk of liver-related outcomes, particularly major adverse liver outcomes and AH [100]. The concept of the “SLD burden paradox” has emerged in the context [101]. Although MASLD is the most prevalent subtype of SLD worldwide, MetALD and ALD contribute disproportionately to liver-related morbidity and mortality [101]. This discrepancy suggests that prevalence alone does not adequately capture the true public health burden of SLD and highlights the need to interpret epidemiological trends in a subtype-specific manner across regions.

Box 4. Liver and extrahepatic burden across SLD subtypes

· SLD burden paradox: MASLD is the most frequent contributor to population burden, whereas MetALD and ALD contribute to a disproportionate share of severe liver-related morbidity and mortality.

· Extrahepatic outcomes: CVD and extrahepatic malignancy are dominant causes of death in non-cirrhotic SLD across subtypes. Alcohol exposure appears to confer incremental cancer risk beyond metabolic risk, with GI cancer risk generally higher in MetALD/ALD than in MASLD.

· Heterogeneity across cohorts, including US Veterans versus Korean community studies, likely reflects differences in alcohol assessment, baseline population risk, and competing risks; this supports subtype-specific interpretation of SLD epidemiology.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Across all SLD categories, objective evidence of SLD was required, most commonly derived from hepatic ultrasound or transient elastography with controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) in large epidemiologic cohorts. MRI-PDFF offers superior quantification but is rarely available in large epidemiologic studies [24,102]. Without imaging, researchers may rely on serum-based indices, but these indirect approaches carry a substantial risk of misclassification [103]. For MASLD and MetALD, SLD must be coupled with proven CMRFs [2,3]. Contemporary epidemiologic studies, therefore, define MASLD by combining a serum-based panel for steatosis with at least one CMRF. Some recent studies have attempted to reconcile legacy NAFLD estimates with MASLD [10,104]; nonetheless, this conversion is fundamentally questionable in the absence of comprehensive metabolic data [1,105]. On the other hand, measuring accurate alcohol consumption remains the greatest challenge for the precise classification of both MetALD and ALD. Because MetALD has a narrow diagnostic window, modest reporting errors can shift individuals between categories. ALD classification is further complicated by the requirement for high consumption thresholds, cultural stigma, lack of proper coding, and erroneous documentation on death certificates. These biases result in systematic underestimation of alcohol-related phenotypes across the SLD spectrum. 86,106 Estimates presented across studies differ substantially due to design, population, and methodology that affect subtype classification and reliability or reproducibility. Diagnostic modality is a major source of variation. Ultrasound and transient elastography with CAP are commonly employed but less accurate than MRI-PDFF, whereas serum-based indices carry a high risk of misclassification. Assessment of alcohol use relies heavily on self-report, introducing systematic underestimation, while objective biomarkers are rarely used [107,108]. Inconsistent application of cardiometabolic criteria and inaccuracies in ICD coding further complicate accurate phenotyping. Objective alcohol biomarkers (e.g., phosphatidylethanol) can help increase the accuracy of diagnosis in subjects with ALD and have been shown to reclassify substantial numbers of individuals, potentially resulting in a three- to four-fold higher estimated prevalence of both MetALD and ALD compared to self-reported cases alone [45,109,110]. The new terminology, therefore, points out the urgent need for standardized, objective assessment of alcohol use in future epidemiologic studies and surveillance systems. The most robust findings across sources are the high and rising prevalence of MASLD (consistent across GBD and imaging-based meta-analyses), the significant underestimation of alcohol-associated phenotypes due to reporting and coding biases, and the clear dose-response gradient in liver-related outcomes (ALD>MetALD>MASLD) observed in large comparative cohort studies. GBD provides valuable standardized global trends, while imaging-based meta-analyses and well-characterized cohort studies offer the most reliable evidence for clinical conclusions and the SLD burden paradox [107]. Future studies with standardized, objective methods will further refine these estimates.

PUBLIC POLICY AGAINST SLD

Most risk factors for SLD, including unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, concurrent cardiometabolic abnormalities, and alcohol consumption, are modifiable and therefore suitable targets for public health prevention [111-113]. Effective strategies should integrate SLD into existing noncommunicable disease frameworks by addressing shared upstream determinants through coordinated policies that promote healthier diets, reduce sugar and alcohol consumption, and increase physical activity [113,114]. Yet in many settings, SLD remains underrecognized and is often missing from national obesity, diabetes, and non-communicable disease action plans [113]. A concerted global effort is needed to raise the profile of SLD on national health agendas.

Because SLD is often clinically silent until advanced stages, early detection in high-risk individuals is critical [68,115]. Routine primary-care screening for adults with significant alcohol consumption or CMRFs can use noninvasive fibrosis panels to identify those at risk for significant fibrosis [116,117]. As these biomarkers become more available and sensitive to early liver injury, they can help clinicians treat high-risk SLD before cirrhosis develops. A comprehensive public health strategy for battling the growing epidemic of SLD requires standardized alcohol assessment with objective biomarkers, harmonized ICD coding for MetALD, integration of SLD screening into existing programs for metabolic disease and addiction, and development of region-specific prevention strategies based on local alcohol consumption patterns and SDI levels. Healthcare systems may improve detection using shared-care pathways, in which primary care assesses risk and refers high-risk patients to hepatology for transient elastography and, where necessary, biopsy. Clear referral criteria and targeted training can help address specialist shortages [118]. Incorporating SLD screening into diabetes and metabolic clinics within national chronic disease programs would further strengthen integrated care. Lifestyle modification remains the cornerstone of SLD management. There is substantial evidence that weight loss and alcohol abstinence slow the progression of disease and, in some patients, even improve steatohepatitis and fibrosis [66,119]. Health services for managing weight and programs for alcohol abstinence should be accessible across both primary care and specialty settings [120]. Health systems can prepare by developing multidisciplinary care pathways and ensuring that patients with advanced fibrosis are efficiently identified and linked to specialist care when new FDA-approved therapies become available [121].

To effectively control SLD, it requires integration into broader healthcare system interventions. A public health strategy can incorporate liver health into existing programs for addiction and cardiometabolic abnormality-related conditions [122,123], supported by clear practice guidelines and care pathways that enable primary care to manage noncomplicated SLD and specify triggers for specialist referral. Task-shifting, which trains clinicians to screen, risk-stratify, and deliver first-line management, can extend services to underserved areas while easing specialist workload [118].

CONCLUSION

SLD has become a major and fast-growing health problem around the world. Although MASLD is the most common worldwide, MetALD and ALD disproportionately lead to advanced liver disease, liver-related complications, and liver-related death. This indicates that prevalence in itself is not a measure of actual clinical burden. This disparity is a reminder that SLD is not a one-size-fits-all proposition. Development of epidemiologic, clinical, and health care policy frameworks addressing cardiometabolic abnormalities and alcohol use. To reduce future SLD healthcare burden, integrated efforts are needed: 1) prevention of cardiometabolic risk, 2) implementation of strict alcohol-control policies with objective biomarkers, 3) earlier identification of high-risk individuals, 4) harmonized coding for MetALD, 5) seamless integration with existing metabolic disease and addiction programs, and 6) region-specific prevention strategies tailored to local epidemiology and SDI levels. These priorities will lead to more accurate phenotyping, improved resource allocation, and a meaningful reduction in SLD-related morbidity and mortality globally.
