Do maize chromosomes contribute to genetic variance in proportion to their size in elite populations?
Authors: Inés Rebollo, Rex Bernardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantitative trait loci (QTLs) are often assumed to be uniformly distributed across the genome in modeling and simulation studies. Our aim was to assess whether the contribution of each chromosome to genetic variance (V G) is proportional to its length in maize (Zea mays L.). We estimated per‐chromosome V G from genomewide marker effects and linkage map positions in eight testcross populations, and we tested whether the slope of the regression of the observed V G on expected V G was different from b = 1. Our results indicated that a chromosome's contribution to V G is generally proportional to its size, supporting the common model of QTLs being uniformly distributed across the genome. In particular, the b value was not statistically different from 1 in about 80% of the population trait combinations. However, 1–2 chromosomes often contributed disproportionately to V G even when b did not differ from 1. The results indicated that these exceptions arose from the preponderance of either coupling linkages, which produced a higher‐than‐expected contribution, or repulsion linkages, which produced a lower‐than‐expected contribution. Because these exceptions resulted from combinations of alleles rather than the effects of alleles by themselves, maize breeding strategies will likely continue to focus on preserving or creating favorable combinations of existing QTL alleles rather than finding copies of individual chromosomes that are hotspots for QTLs with large effects.

INTRODUCTION

The contribution of a given chromosome to trait variation depends on the genes found on the chromosome. Certain chromosomes are known to have genes with large effects. For example, major glutenin genes on chromosomes 1A, 1B, and 1D in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) strongly affect dough quality (Payne et al., 1980). But for traits such as grain yield in maize (Zea mays L.), major genes arguably no longer contribute to genetic variation in elite germplasm because such genes have been fixed either during the domestication process or during multiple generations of intense selection in inbred and hybrid development. How minor‐effect loci underlying quantitative traits are distributed across the genome in elite germplasm is largely unknown or unreported.

In modeling and simulation studies, quantitative trait loci (QTLs) are often assumed to be uniformly distributed across the genome (Ali et al., 2020; Bernardo, 1999; Gaynor et al., 2017; Meyer & Birney, 2018). While the effects of QTLs may vary according to an assumed distribution, the contribution of each chromosome to trait variation is generally assumed proportional to its length. Empirical evidence supporting this assumption includes a rice (Oryza sativa L.) QTL meta‐analysis that showed a positive correlation between the number of QTLs on each chromosome and chromosome length (Khahani et al., 2021). Similarly, in both teosinte (Zea mays ssp. parviglumis) and a maize landrace, the mean proportion of variance explained by each chromosome for 18 domestication traits was highly correlated with chromosome length (Chen et al., 2020).

In contrast, in a study where genetic variation among maize inbreds was partitioned by chromosome across five genetic backgrounds, copies of a given chromosome found in specific inbreds had notably large or small effects for anthesis date, plant height, and protein concentration (Bernardo & Thompson, 2016). These findings suggested that a chromosome's size is not always indicative of its effect on the trait means of maize inbreds. This study by Bernardo and Thompson (2016) focused on the mean effect of each chromosome rather than on the genetic variance contributed by each chromosome.

Understanding whether some chromosomes contribute disproportionately to genetic variance would open avenues for emphasizing specific chromosomes in genotyping and prediction, lead to more realistic simulation studies, guide targeted recombination strategies (Bernardo, 2017; Brandariz & Bernardo, 2019; Cameron & Bernardo, 2023; Ru & Bernardo, 2019; Sadhu et al., 2016), and aid the design or use of chromosome‐substitution libraries (Wehrhahn & Allard, 1965). Our aim in this study was to assess whether the contribution of each chromosome to genetic variance is proportional to its length in elite maize populations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data

We used phenotypic and marker data for four maize biparental crosses (A/B, C/B, C/D, E/F) from the AgReliant Genetics breeding program. A full description of the populations and data processing methods is found in Krchov et al. (2015). In brief, each population included 130–242 doubled haploid (DH) lines that were crossed to two inbred testers, which were designated as T1, T2, T3, and T4. The eight testcross populations were evaluated in up to 18 year‐location combinations in the US Corn Belt. The measured traits included grain yield (t ha−1 at 150 g H2O kg−1), grain moisture (g kg−1), test weight (g L−1), plant height (cm), and ear height (cm). Testcross phenotypic data were adjusted according to the incomplete block design used at each location to obtain best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) per location. Across‐environment BLUEs were subsequently calculated. Variance components were estimated with testcross and environment effects treated as random.

The DNA marker data consisted of 3072 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) generated using the Illumina iScan platform (Illumina Inc.). Within each population, SNPs with >10% missing values, >5% heterozygosity, or minor allele frequency < 0.1 were excluded. Missing marker data were imputed via the most correlated SNP (Bernardo, 2020a). Linkage maps for each population were constructed using QTL IciMapping version 4.2 (Meng et al., 2015). When multiple markers mapped to the same position, only one was retained. Between 354 and 754 SNPs were retained per population, with chromosome map lengths ranging from 93.9 to 432.7 cM (Table 1). Parental haplotypes were imputed by threading the most common haplotypes across a sliding window of two consecutive markers within each population. Marker effects were estimated for each population via ridge regression‐best linear unbiased prediction (RR‐BLUP), with the shrinkage factor being equal to N eff(1 − H)/H, where N eff was the effective number of independent tests (Li & Ji, 2005) and H was the estimate of entry‐mean heritability. The RR‐BLUP marker effects were calculated using a modified version of the RRBLUP software (Bernardo, 2020a).

Per‐chromosome genetic variance estimation and analysis

For each population–trait combination, we estimated the per‐chromosome genetic variance (observed V G) using the marker effect and map positions of each SNP as: (1) VG=∑j,k1+F2Djkajak where j and k indexed loci within a chromosome, F was the inbreeding coefficient (F = 1 for DH lines), Djk was the linkage disequilibrium coefficient between loci j and k, aj was the homozygote effect of the minor allele at locus j, and ak was the homozygote effect of the minor allele at locus k. The Djk was calculated as 0.25(1 − 2rjk ), where rjk was the Haldane recombination coefficient between loci j and k. Equation (1) represents an extension of formulas, derived by Osthushenrich et al. (2017), to estimate the variance within a recombinant inbred line population from marker effects. Our extension generalizes the approach to populations with any level of inbreeding and is equivalent to eq. (5.16a) for F = 0 in Lynch and Walsh (1998). Further details of Equation (1) are in Rebollo Panuncio (2025).

The total V G was obtained by summing the per‐chromosome observed V G values. The per‐chromosome expected V G was calculated assuming proportionality to chromosome length as l c/L multiplied by the total V G, where lc was the size (in cM) of chromosome c, and L was the total size of the linkage map in the biparental cross. We then conducted linear regression analyses to test whether the observed V G per chromosome deviated from the expected V G. The expected V G was used as the X variable, and the observed V G was used as the Y variable. Given that the slope (b) should be equal to 1.0 if the observed V G is equal to the expected V G, the null hypothesis of b = 1 was tested for significance (p = 0.05) via a t‐test. When the slope was significantly different from 1, we concluded that the observed per‐chromosome V G was not proportional to chromosome length, and no further analysis was performed.

When the slope did not significantly differ from 1, we conducted outlier analyses to identify individual chromosomes that contributed disproportionately to the observed V G. We first calculated residuals as the difference between the observed and expected V G. We then tested whether each residual was significantly different from 0 (p = 0.05) using the variance of the remaining residuals in a t‐test. This procedure was akin to the standard outlier‐detection approach that uses a z‐test, and we instead used a t‐test to account for the limited degrees of freedom with only 10 maize chromosomes. After removing residuals significantly different from 0 (i.e., outliers), the residual testing procedure was repeated once to detect additional outliers.

Within‐locus variance and between‐loci covariance

The observed V G was partitioned into two components: within‐locus variance, V G(W); and between‐loci covariance, 2Cov. The V G(W) was obtained by limiting Equation (1) to j = k cases, whereas 2Cov was obtained by limiting Equation (1) to j ≠ k cases. The V G(W) and 2Cov were expressed as percentages of the observed V G.

RESULTS

Slopes from the regression of the observed V G on the expected V G ranged from −0.15 to 1.85 (mean of 0.78) for grain yield; from −1.24 to 1.11 (mean of 0.08) for grain moisture; from −0.76 to 0.99 (mean of −0.08) for test weight; from −1.34 to 0.35 (mean of −0.35) for plant height; and from −0.21 to 1.23 (mean of 0.41) for ear height (Figures 1 and 2 and Figures S1–S4). Only six out of 35 population–trait combinations had slopes significantly different from 1 (Figure 1). Plant height had slopes significantly different from 1 in three populations: A/B(T1), C/D(T1), and E/F(T1). Grain yield had slopes significantly different from 1 in populations E/F(T1) and E/F(T4), and test weight had a slope significantly different from 1 only in E/F(T4). For grain moisture and ear height, none of the populations showed slopes significantly different from 1. The populations with the most slopes significantly different from 1 were E/F(T1) and E/F(T4), with each population having two traits with a slope significantly different from 1.

Across all populations, the number of significant residuals, their magnitude, and the percentage they represented of the expected V G varied by trait (Table 2). For grain yield, the seven significant residuals ranged from 0.71 to 7.38 t2 ha− 2 and represented 91%–281% of the expected V G. Grain moisture had 11 significant residuals that ranged from −11.75 to 27.29 g2 kg− 2 and represented −80% to 395% of the expected V G. Test weight had four significant residuals that ranged from 4.62 to 38.66 g2 L− 2 and represented 304%–386% of the expected V G. Plant height had three significant residuals that ranged from 0.83 to 4.06 cm2 and represented 221%–235% of the expected V G. Ear height had nine residuals that ranged from −2.82 to 7.33 cm2 and represented −78%–409% of the expected V G. Out of 34 significant residuals detected, 30 (88%) had a larger‐than‐expected contribution, whereas four residuals (12%) had a lower‐than‐expected contribution.

Seven out of the eight populations had at least one significant residual. The population with the highest number of significant residuals was A/B(T2) with a total of eight: two for grain moisture, test weight, and plant height, and one for grain yield and ear height. Populations C/B(T2) and C/D(T1) each had six significant residuals, C/B(T1) had five, A/B(T1) and E/F(T1) had four, and C/D(T3) had one.

Within the same biparental cross, some chromosomes consistently had significant residuals across multiple traits, testers, or both. The most notable case was chromosome 4 in biparental cross A/B, which had a significant residual for the following: grain moisture, test weight, and ear height with both testers; grain yield with T1; and plant height with T2. In population A/B(T2), chromosome 6 had significant residuals for grain moisture, test weight, and plant height. In biparental cross C/B, chromosome 4 had significant residuals for grain moisture with both testers, for test weight with T2, and for plant height with T1. In this same biparental cross, chromosome 9 had a significant residual for ear height with both testers. In population C/D(T1), chromosome 7 had a significant residual for grain yield and grain moisture.

The percentage of V G due to within‐locus variance, V G(W), ranged from 11% to 302% (mean of 79%) for grain yield; from 12% to 422% (mean of 95%) for grain moisture; from 10% to 331% (mean of 56%) for test weight; from 10% to 163% (mean of 71%) for plant height; and from 10% to 244% (mean of 61%) for ear height. The percentage of V G due to between‐loci covariance, 2Cov, was the complement of V G(W). For the chromosomes with significant residuals, V G(W) was between 10% and 337% of V G (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

Our results indicated that a chromosome's contribution to genetic variance (V G) is generally proportional to its size. In about 80% of the population–trait combinations, the slope of the regression of observed V G on expected V G was not significantly different from 1. This supported the common model of QTLs being uniformly distributed across the genome. However, we also found that some chromosomes contributed disproportionally to V G. In about 80% of the population–trait combinations where the slope of the regression of the observed V G on the expected V G supported a uniform distribution of the QTLs, one or two chromosomes showed outlier contributions to V G.

Suppose two non‐epistatic loci, i and j, control a trait. The V G is equal to Vi + Vj + 2Cov ij , where Vi is the genetic variance at locus i, Vj is the genetic variance at locus j, and Cov ij is the genetic covariance between the two loci. The within‐locus variance, V G(W), is equal to Vi + Vj , whereas what is denoted by 2Cov in this study is equal to 2Cov ij . The most obvious cause of a high V G on a chromosome is the presence of large‐effect QTLs, such as the glutenin genes in wheat (Payne et al., 1980), which would lead to a high V G(W). While variation was observed among the RR‐BLUP marker effects in each trait population–trait combination, none of the marker effects was inordinately large (see Data Availability Statement). This lack of large marker effects may have been due in part to the use of RR‐BLUP, which leads to a uniform shrinkage of marker effects (Meuwissen et al., 2001). On the other hand, plots of RR‐BLUP marker effects still show peaks close to major QTLs (Bernardo, 2020b, p. 265). We also surmise, as mentioned in Section 1, that long‐term selection would have already led to fixation at any major QTLs for yield, moisture, test weight, plant height, and ear height in elite maize inbreds in a commercial breeding program. If major QTL are known for a given trait, fitting their effects as fixed instead of random is recommended (Bernardo, 2014).

A second cause of a high V G on a chromosome is strong coupling linkage between QTLs, which generates a positive 2Cov (Lynch & Walsh, 1998). In contrast, repulsion linkage produces a negative 2Cov, reducing V G (Lynch & Walsh, 1998) and leading to a phenomenon called “hidden genetic variance” (Lynch & Gabriel, 1983). Instances of V G(W) accounting for >100% of the total V G were due to a large negative covariance. Consider grain moisture in the C/B(T1) population. For chromosome 2, V G(W) was 2.9, 2Cov was −2.0, the total V G was 0.9, and V G(W) as a percentage of the total V G was 337% (Table 2).

Repulsion and coupling linkages also lead to different values associated with the two parental homologs for the trait of interest. To illustrate, suppose the effects of the contrasting alleles are 1 and −1 at each of two linked QTLs. Coupling linkage leads to a value of 2 for the + + parental homolog and a value of −2 for the − parental homolog. In contrast, repulsion linkage leads to a value of 0 for the + − and − + parental homologs. The values of the parental homologs are therefore expected to be similar with repulsion linkage and divergent with coupling linkage.

The results suggested that the outliers and negative slopes for the observed V G were largely caused by coupling linkages in the elite maize populations. For example, in the A/B(T2) population, chromosomes 4 and 6 showed significantly higher‐than‐expected V G for grain moisture. Chromosome 6 was the smallest in the A/B linkage map (thus having the lowest expected V G), whereas chromosome 4 was the fourth smallest. A large portion of their V G arose from a positive 2Cov: the V G(W) accounted for only 12%–15% of the V G on these chromosomes, versus >36% for the other chromosomes. The preponderance of coupling linkages on chromosomes 4 and 6 was reflected by the divergent values of the parental homologs: 6.28 g kg− 1 for chromosome 4 and 4.75 g kg− 1 for chromosome 6, versus 0.04–2.87 g kg− 1 for the other chromosomes.

Outliers with a lower‐than‐expected contribution to V G can be explained in a similar way. For example, chromosome 8 in the E/F(T1) population had a significant residual for ear height, contributing 78% less to V G than expected. Chromosome 8 was the second largest chromosome in the E/F linkage map, leading to a high expected V G. However, for ear height in E/F(T1), chromosome 8 had the highest proportion of V G explained by V G(W) (80% vs. 41% on average for the rest of the chromosomes) and the third‐lowest difference in the values of the parental homologs (0.56 cm).

Slopes significantly different from 1, including negative slopes, can be explained in the same manner, with some short chromosomes showing a higher‐than‐expected V G and some longer chromosomes showing a lower‐than‐expected V G. Consider plant height in A/B(T1), for which the estimated slope was b = −1.3. Chromosomes 6 and 4, which were both short, had a higher‐than‐expected contribution to V G. Chromosome 6 was 107.7 cM long and had an observed V G that was 473% of the expected V G, whereas chromosome 4 was 132.3 cM long and had an observed V G that was 158% of the expected V G. These chromosomes also had the lowest V G(W) (24% for chromosome 6 and 39% for chromosome 4, vs. >77% for the other chromosomes) and the highest difference between parental chromosome values (5.28 cm for chromosome 6 and 4.23 cm for chromosome 4). In contrast, chromosome 1, the longest chromosome (301.0 cM), had an observed V G that was 66% of the expected V G, the highest contribution of V G(W) (156%, with the 2CovG being negative), and below‐average parental difference (1.47 cm). This pattern of higher‐than‐expected V G in short chromosomes and lower‐than‐expected V G in long chromosomes also explained the negative slopes observed in other population–trait combinations with slopes significantly different from 1.

Estimating per‐chromosome V G is straightforward in a biparental cross because population structure is absent and linkage equilibrium is expected between loci on different chromosomes. In contrast, association mapping panels are characterized by population structure and linkage disequilibrium between loci on different chromosomes (Flint‐Garcia et al., 2003; Zhu et al., 2008), and both factors need to be considered when estimating V G from genomewide marker effects (Lehermeier et al., 2017). While we have studied the proportionality between per‐chromosome V G and chromosome size, the chromosome sizes herein were linkage map sizes (cM) rather than physical map sizes (kb). The sizes of linkage maps and physical maps are imperfectly correlated (Kong et al., 2004; Myers et al., 2005) because recombination rates vary across genomic regions (Choulet et al., 2014; Kong et al., 2004; McVean et al., 2004; Myers et al., 2005). The size of a given chromosome in cM is expected to differ among biparental crosses that vary in the level of polymorphism between the parental inbreds.

Because differences between the observed and expected V G resulted from the arrangement of alleles rather than having alleles with inordinately large contributions, maize breeding strategies should focus on preserving or creating favorable allele combinations on homologous chromosomes rather than targeting individual chromosomes as QTL hotspots. Copies of chromosomes with favorable allele combinations can be introgressed through the development of chromosome‐substitution lines (Bernardo & Thompson, 2016; Wehrhahn & Allard, 1965). Targeted recombination, a strategy currently under development (Kouranov et al., 2022), can be used to directly create favorable allele combinations. We speculate that because only 1–2 crossovers per chromosome occur during the creation of DH lines (Smith et al., 2008), their widespread use in maize breeding allows a balance between the preservation of favorable allele combinations and the selection of favorable allele combinations. Per‐chromosome variances can be used when parents are selected by simulating the performance of their progeny (Bernardo, 2014; Chung & Liao, 2022; Mohammadi et al., 2015). It remains to be seen whether per‐chromosome variances would improve the accuracy of genomewide prediction.
