Molecular-Scale Geometric Design: Zigzag-Structured Intrinsically Stretchable Polymer Semiconductors
Authors: Mingliang Zhu, Zhihao Shao, Yifan Li, Zihan Xiong, Zhao Yang, Jinyang Chen, Wenkang Shi, Chengyu Wang, Yangshuang Bian, Zhiyuan Zhao, Yunlong Guo, Yunqi Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrienting intelligence and multifunction, stretchable semiconductors are of great significance in constructing next-generation human-friendly wearable electronic devices. Nevertheless, rendering semiconducting polymers mechanical stretchability without compromising intrinsic electrical performance remains a major challenge. Combining geometry-innovated inorganic systems and structure-tailored organic semiconductors, a molecular-scale geometric design strategy is proposed to obtain high-performance intrinsically stretchable polymer semiconductors. Originating from the linear regioregular conjugated polymer and corresponding para-modified near-linear counterpart, a series of zigzag-structured semiconducting polymers are developed with diverse ortho-type and meta-type kinking units quantitatively incorporated. They showcase huge edges in realizing stretchability enhancement for conformational transition, likewise with long-range π-aggregation and short-range torsion disorder taking effect. Assisted by additional heteroatom embedment and flexible alkyl-chain attachment, mechanical stretchability and carrier mobility could afford a two-way promotion. Among zigzag-structured species, o-OC8–5% with the initial field-effect mobility up to 1.92 cm2 V–1 s–1 still delivers 1.43 and 1.37 cm2 V–1 s–1 under 100% strain with charge transport parallel and perpendicular to the stretching direction, respectively, accompanied by outstanding performance retention and cyclic stability. This molecular design strategy contributes to an in-depth exploration of prospective intrinsically stretchable semiconductors for cutting-edge electronic devices.

Introduction

Soft electronics has gained the rapid rise and received great favor during the past decade.1−4 In contrast to conventional rigid counterparts, flexible, conformal, and stretchable electronic devices could sustain greater external strain (like bending, twisting, and stretching) and gradually march toward multifunction under the background of excellent electrical and mechanical properties.5−7 In the past decade, the progress in flexible and stretchable field-effect transistors (FETs),8−10 solar cells,11,12 light-emitting diodes,13−15 and driving circuits16−18 has been mainly attributed to interdisciplinary integration and efforts in the fields of material science, device physics, and electronic information. Generally, as the basic objective, maintaining the initial functionality as much as possible under mechanical strain is of great significance for practical applications.

To realize mechanical stretchability, material innovations and structural design are two common scenarios.3,19−21 The former focuses on developing novel materials with intrinsic flexibility and reorganizing multicomponent systems. During the multicomponent rearrangement, individual composites tend to form interpenetrating networks composed of nanowires, nanoflakes, nanotubes, etc., undertaking mechanical deformation and specific physiochemical properties.22−25 Unfortunately, there exists the common trade-off relationship between improved mechanical stretchability and undermined original performance.26,27 Constructed on traditional rigid materials, the latter could adjust to external stimuli toward device-level stretchability.3,28,29 After transforming into macroscopic bulking-wrinkles, serpentines, interconnecting island bridges, three-dimensional architecture structures, and so forth processed from microfabrication techniques (Scheme 1), they could typically realize performance retention.2,30,31 However, high cost, complex processing, and low-throughput preparation are all huge hurdles to commercialization. To draw on edges from two strategies, conducting molecular-level geometric design might explore a brand-new pathway to developing intrinsically stretchable materials.

Zigzag-Structured Stretchable Polymer Semiconductors, Initiating from Structure-Innovated Stretchable Inorganic Electronics and Molecular-Scale Geometric Design

Superior to inorganic and small-molecule counterparts, polymer semiconductors would bear the brunt of realizing potential applications in next-generation wearable and implantable electronics.32−34 In addition to large-scale manufacturing and compatibility to multiple solution-processing technologies, their structural tunability, multilevel construction (from atom to molecule to polymer chain to condensed thin film) and intrinsic flexibility also play critical roles in realizing mechanical stretchability (Scheme 1).33,35−37 Up to date, stretchable FETs based on conjugated polymers have made certain headway.19,27,38 It has been widely recognized that inserting nonconjugation flexible spacers attached by hydrogen bonding, coordination bonding, etc., noncovalent secondary bonds could introduce dynamic interactions to dissipate external strains.9,10,39,40 However, breaking backbone conjugation would hinder intramolecular charge transfer and thus decrease carrier mobility. Recently developed pseudoregular all-conjugated polymer semiconductors are expected to circumvent the trade-off between mechanical and electrical performance.8,41,42 For instance, Zhang et al. and Shih et al. introduce noncentrosymmetric spiro-fluorene units and nonlinear dibromobenzene isomers into polymer backbones, respectively, realizing stretchability enhancement without compromising carrier mobility.8,43 These well-designed conjugated spacers and corresponding terpolymers broaden new research thought on developing novel stretchable high-mobility polymer semiconductors, but problems in method universality, material scalability, and stretchability improvement space all face huge challenges. It is currently acknowledged that undermining thin-film overall crystallinity while maintaining short-range ordered packing could preserve high mobility with mechanical stretchability effectively enhanced.19,42−45 In addition to pondering over modified molecular segments, polymer-chain overall conformation and backbone customized extension based on molecular-level geometric design, which has never been considered, might possess vast potentials. In other words, structure-innovated molecular design is likely to realize high-mobility intrinsically stretchable polymer semiconductors.

Inspired by architecture-innovated stretchable inorganic electronics and structure-adjustable organic semiconductors, a series of conjugated polymers with zigzag-geometric features were designed in this work (Scheme 1). Geometric characteristics of target polymers were thoroughly demonstrated via theoretical calculation, optical spectroscopy, and synchrotron radiation, followed by comprehensive exploration in polymer-chain conformation, (pre)aggregation behavior, film-state microstructure, and mechanical and electrical properties. Distinguished from the linear regioregular poly((thiophene-flanked-diketopyrrolopyrrole)-alt-bithiophene) P(DPP-BT) and the commonly reported near-linear terpolymer, all zigzag-structured semiconducting polymers could exhibit superior crack-onset-strain (COS) levels, with the highest value up to 160%, indicating that conformational transition of conjugated polymers played a decisive role in stretchability enhancement. In addition to the bending conformation and backbone distortion existing in meta-type modified polymers, ortho-series (o-series) analogues would also be regulated by intramolecular steric hindrance. Notably, although they both delivered comparable ductility possibly due to saturation in the amorphous phase induced by zigzag-bending (<120°), o-series polymers were nevertheless important for comprehensive performance improvement and functional applications. Furthermore, carrier mobilities were measured in FETs to evaluate their electrical properties, wherein retained conjugation length depending on the modification ratio (5 or 10%) and conformational transition as well as torsion disorder induced by kinking unit type (o-type or m-type) determined the final device performance. Overall, 10% modified analogues and o-series species were in the inferior position. Under the assistance of hydrogen-bonding interactions and flexible alkyl-chain attachment, molecular-scale structural design could endow polymer semiconductors with better stretchability and promoted mobility, with the balance between long-range π-aggregation (favorable) and short-range torsion disorder (adverse). In other words, mechanical stretchability and electrical mobility were possible to simultaneously improve for polymer semiconductors by quantitatively introducing well-designed zigzag-like modification units. This was the comprehensive result deriving from backbone conformation, intermolecular stacking, and film-state microstructure. The geometric design strategy provides an effective methodology to design high-mobility intrinsically stretchable polymer semiconductors.

Results and Discussion

Based on the reference conjugated polymer P(DPP-BT), a range of zigzag-structured conjugated polymers were synthesized with the commonly reported para-type near-linear counterpart p-10% designed for comparison (Figure 1a). In this system, ortho-isomerized (o, o-F, o-OMe, and o-OC8) and meta-isomerized (m, m-OC10C12) bithiophene-benzene units were quantitatively embedded into the polymer backbone, probably with additional noncovalent interactions introduced and flexible alkyl chains attached (Figure 1b,c). Specific synthetic routes and definite structural characterizations of designed kinking units are shown in the Supporting Information and Schemes S1–S5. Notably, given intramolecular structural arrangement and intermolecular reorganization, we adopted bithiophene-benzene derivatives rather than individual benzene series as key building blocks and bending kinks, with the aim of avoiding unnecessary steric hindrance caused by alkyl side chains and reactivity discrepancy.

In p-10% and m-10%, we could observe obvious gelation during the polymerization process, perhaps induced by aggregation-enhanced precipitation and severe chain entanglement. Stemming from the model polymer m-10%, m-OC10C12–5% and m-OC10C12–10% were synthesized based on the (2-decyltetradecyl)oxy-polished segment, wherein these three polymers overall presented an ∼120° bending conformation. Flexible alkoxy chains tended to orient well away from the polymer backbone and thus increased the free volume of amorphous regions without interrupting intramolecular carrier transport. Additionally, different from other comonomer linkers presenting as white powders or flakes, monomer m was a lightweight white flocculent matter, and monomer m-OC10C12 was a transparent oily substance. These phenomena indicated that m and m-OC10C12 exhibited weaker intermolecular interactions. Unfortunately, we failed to obtain m-5% primarily due to reduction in proportion of solubilizing alkyl chains, and local π-aggregation caused by near-planar kinking unit m would further weaken solution processability. m-10% was successfully delivered, presumably because the conformational transition gained the upper hand for its 10% modification ratio. Subsequently, to introduce weak secondary interactions and ensure symmetry-induced order, deriving from the parent o-5% and o-10%, fluorine atom (for o-F-5% and o-F-10%) and methoxy-group (for o-OMe-5% and o-OMe-10%) modified linking units were incorporated to construct zigzag-structured polymer semiconductors. In addition, dioctyloxy-appended analogues (o-OC8–5% and o-OC8–10%) were also designed for enhancing solubility, tuning microstructural morphology, and thus optimizing comprehensive performance.

Target polymers were all synthesized by conventional Stille polycondensation in satisfactory yields (Scheme S6 and Table S1), with structures confirmed by high-temperature 1H NMR spectra. From the reference polymer P(DPP-BT), we could attribute typical resonance peaks to respective segments qualitatively, including alkyl chains (0.5–1.5 ppm), amides (∼4.0 ppm), and thiophenes in different subregions. Different from small molecules, limited to intramolecular coupling and intermolecular interactions, these conjugated polymers often possessed halo peaks and thus brought challenges to quantitative integration for determining particular structures and modification proportion. Fortunately, we could also capture characteristic peaks to qualitatively proof these polymers (Figure S1). The increment in flexible alkyl chains would increase the proportion of chain-terminated methyl groups (0.7–1.0 ppm). Owing to lack of atom or functional-group substitution in the modification unit, o-5% and o-10% delivered additional halo peaks at 7.1–7.5 ppm, which derived from four adjacent hydrogen atoms in o-bithiophene-benzenes. Oxygen-adjacent methylene-group resonances existing in m-O10C12–5%, m-O10C12–10%, o-OC8–5%, and o-OC8–5% could enhance and broaden amide-related peaks (3.7–4.1 ppm). Evidently, introduction of methoxy groups for o-OMe-5% and o-OMe-10% brought about corresponding resonance sharp peaks around 3.7 ppm. All the abovementioned could further support structural characteristics of designed zigzag-structured polymer semiconductors, in that relative intensity and chemical shifts were in good agreement with modification proportions and intrinsic features of introduced small-molecule units.

Subsequently, the proportion of modification linker was determined by elemental analysis (EA) according to the carbon atom as summarized in Table S2, in that introducing zigzag-structured bending units would decrease carbon element content due to the decrement in bulky alkyl chains attached on DPP units. The modification ratios from EA quantification were 10.7% for p-10% and 11.0, 6.3, and 14.3% for m-10%, m-OC10C12–5%, and m-OC10C12–10%, respectively. In o-series zigzag-structured polymers, the proportions of linking units were 8.2, 15.9, 7.7, 11.4, 7.8, 9.8, 7.7, and 13.6% for o-5%, o-10%, o-F-5%, o-F-10%, o-OMe-5%, o-OMe-10%, o-OC8–5%, and o-OC8–10%, respectively. The number-averaged molecular weight (Mn) and dispersity (Đ) were determined by high-temperature gel permeation chromatography, as shown in Figures S2–S14. The above two parameters significantly influenced electrical and mechanical properties of semiconducting polymers.36,41 These conjugated polymers all possessed similar Mn values around 100 kDa with Đ values less than 3. Among them, m-OC10C12–5% and m-OC10C12–10% had higher Mn due to the bulky alkyl-chain attachment. Generally, reasonable modification ratios and comparable parameters in Mn ensured meaningful and referable comparisons of their properties.

All conjugated polymers were thermally stable with decomposition temperatures (at 5% weight loss) of over 400 °C, as observed in thermogravimetric analysis curves (Figure S15). In addition, no evident exothermic and endothermic transition peaks could be captured within the measured temperature range in differential scanning calorimetry curves (Figure S16), which was not unusual for conjugated polymer semiconductors.41,42 Next, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) energy levels were estimated by cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements, as presented in Figure S17. These polymers all exhibited evident oxidation peaks and negligible reduction peaks, indicating that they tended to yield p-type hole transport behaviors in FETs. Compared with P(DPP-BT), zigzag-structured polymers showed slightly higher HOMOs by 0–0.15 eV, due to the decrement in proportion of electron-deficient DPP units. Generally, all series of materials delivered typical p-type characteristics with HOMO energy levels around −5.3 eV (Table S3), expectedly forming good contact with gold electrodes.34,46

We then conducted density functional theory (DFT) calculations to figure out chemical configurations of these regional zigzag-structured bending linkers, toward further speculations of overall polymer-backbone conformations in solution and solid states. Detailed results of optimized backbone geometries at designed zigzag-structured nodes could be found in Figures S18–S25, with critical parameters summarized in Table 1. Briefly, geometry-featured linking units were inserted in the middle of four repeating units (thiophene-flanked diketopyrrolopyrrole covalently connected by bithiophene) with all flexible alkyl chains simplified as methyl groups, which were optimized as basic units at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level of DFT. The twisting angle between central benzene and flanked thiophene in the kinking unit was defined as torsion angle I (TA-I), with torsion angle II (TA-II) pointing to the angle between the bending unit and the adjacent repeating unit. As is commonly recognized, P(DPP-BT) adopted the planar linear conformation. p-10% as the conventional species would maintain a near-linear structure with graded backbone distortion near bending nodes. For meta-series polymers, they were prone to bend polymer chains to ∼120° along the backbone axis, with TA-I and TA-II around 25 and 12°, respectively. Notably, excluding interference from zigzag-bending induced by m and m-OC10C12, they delivered similar torsion disorder and an equivalent effect in disruption of π-aggregation to p-10%. For o-series polymers, they all adopted evident zigzag-structured conformations with a bending angle of around 60° along polymer backbones. Influenced by intramolecular steric hindrance in adjacent thiophenes of o, o-F, o-OMe, and o-OC8 units, they all presented TA-I and TA-II around 50 and 20°, respectively, far larger than those of p-10% and m-series analogues. Notably, the electronegative fluorine atom tended to alleviate TA-1, providing possibilities for enhancing localized interactions and dissipating strain energy for additional hydrogen bonding. In addition, the insertion of slight kinking units hardly influenced HOMO energy levels, as observed in CV measurements. In zigzag-structured polymers, backbone conformations were driven by bending angles and backbone-torsion angles in designed modification units for m-series and o-series polymers, and repulsive effects between adjacent thiophenes should also be taken into account in deliberating over the latter.

Deriving from this, the blueprint of fusing molecular-level geometric features into intrinsically stretchable material design is drawn (Figure 2). Zigzag-structured conjugated polymers employ nearly planar structures in regioregular segments, ideally accompanied by long-range π-aggregation and short-range chain bending as well as distorting near kinking nodes. Upon film forming, polymer chains are prone to self-assemble into extensive ordered aggregation with regional twisting and kinking. Furthermore, desirable microstructures enable high-performance intrinsically stretchable polymer semiconductors, wherein long-range ordered aggregates are disrupted by short-range torsion disorder with undermined overall crystallinity. Notably, alkyl-chain attachment and heteroatom embedment would not influence overall backbone orientation and twisting degree, presumably with positive feedbacks in passivating grain boundaries, decreasing tensile modulus and dissipating external strains.36,41,47

Optical spectroscopy has evolved as a powerful tool to investigate kinetic and thermodynamic behaviors of conjugated polymers.48,49 UV–vis absorption spectra were used to characterize and then explore solution-state preaggregation (Figure 3a–e) and film-state stacking (Figure S26) of zigzag-structured polymer semiconductors, with critical parameters summarized in Table S3. They all possessed π–π* transition peaks around 450 nm and typical internal charge transfer (ICT) peaks (0–0 and 0–1 peaks) from 650 to 850 nm. In the solution state, P(DPP-BT) exhibited the highest degree of preaggregation and ordered intermolecular packing, with an evident red-shift absorption peak and higher I0–0/I0–1 value than those of the other polymers. Upon increasing the content of kinking units, 0–0 absorption intensities would gradually decrease, largely due to interrupted intermolecular ordered packing. With the bending angle decreasing, from P(DPP-BT) to p-10% to m-10% to o-10%, the absorption peaks gradually blue-shifted with 0–1 shoulder peaks gradually weakened and blurred. In m-series zigzag-structured polymers, quantitative modification could undermine intermolecular solution-state preaggregation. Although ordered interchain stacking was maintained, additional bulky flexible side chains would further suppress intermolecular aggregation, due to ambiguous 0–1 absorption peaks in the ICT region for m-OC10C12–5% and m-OC10C12–10%. Similarly, these phenomena could also be observed in the o-series zigzag-structured polymers. With the incorporation of bending units and increment in modification proportion, overall absorption peaks would blue-shift and broaden, accompanied by weakened 0–1 absorption shoulder peaks.

Next, temperature-dependent UV–vis absorption spectra of model polymers P(DPP-BT), p-10%, m-10%, o-10%, and o-5% were collected to identify conformational transition (Figure 3f–j). When increasing solution temperatures, the evident decrement in ratios of I0–0/I0–1 and blue-shifts in absorption wavelength were observed for them, and the traditional order–disorder transition model could well describe it.49 In this aggregate-collapse process, absorption peaks gradually blue-shifted and concomitantly undermined, indicating the deplanarization with distorted π-aggregation and reduced effective conjugation length (ECL).50,51 When approaching saturation over critical temperatures, single featureless and broad absorption peaks sprang up with weakened intensity induced by nearly completely disordered chain conformations. In Figure 3k, in accordance with the order–disorder transition model and DFT calculations, the degree of aggregation αagg (the absorption–intensity ratio of the specific temperature to 20 °C) gradually diminished with elevated solution temperatures, and the tendency of deviating from the linear trend and approaching disaggregation–saturation gradually increased in the order of p-10%, P(DPP-BT), m-10%, o-5%, and o-10%. Additionally, the existence and proportion of ordered aggregates determined the critical temperature and disaggregation efficiency, transforming from ordered arrangement to collapsed chains.

For annealed thin films (Figure S26), in spite of zigzag-linkage introduction and linear-conformation breakage, these geometry-featured conjugated polymers mostly retained the original characteristics of the reference polymer P(DPP-BT). Except for m-OC10C12–5% and m-OC10C12–10%, zigzag-structured polymers with a 10% modification ratio exhibited similar absorption spectra to P(DPP-BT), and 5% series with longer ECLs would present stronger 0–0 peaks and ordered intermolecular packing. For the latter, the achieved balance between long-range π-aggregation and short-range bending as well as torsion disorder mainly contributed to it. From m-10% to m-OC10C12–10% to m-OC10C12–5%, the trend on the intensity ratio of I0–0/I0–1 afforded a gradient increase, and we speculated that m-OC10C12 attached to the bulky alkyl-chain could enhance intermolecular interactions and promote backbone ordered packing. Regardless of structure-featured junctions, quantitative and appropriate modification basically maintained ordered packing with regional π-aggregation enhanced in annealed thin films.

Furthermore, wavelengths of maximum absorption peaks in solution (at room temperature and 80 °C) and film states were extracted and are summarized in Figure 3l. In addition to the aforementioned, we observed that o-5% showed a similar absorption wavelength in solution at 80 °C to p-10% and m-10%, yielding the evident red-shift relative to o-10%. This phenomenon for relaxed polymer chains indicated that the former three polymers possessed longer ECLs and less tortional disorder, which was in good agreement with the lower modification ratio or approximate conformation. Originating from here, upon film formation followed by thermal annealing, extensive π–π aggregates would take place for P(DPP-BT), p-10%, and o-5%, but bending conformation and backbone distortion hindered equivalent stacking and induced blue-shifts for m-10% and o-10%. The above indicated that P(DPP-BT) with the maximum ECL and π-aggregation located at the most thermodynamically stable state, tending to absorb more heat and cause more enthalpy change toward disordered chains. Incorporating zigzag-structured units and increasing the modification ratio both could introduce the amorphous phase and enhance thermodynamic disorder, wherein conformational bending, aggregation distortion, and intramolecular steric hindrance all played critical roles.

To explore the effect of this zigzag-structured geometry design on polymer thin-film microstructures and stacking characteristics, grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) measurements were conducted, with two-dimensional (2D) scattering patterns (Figure 4a–f and Figure S27) and one-dimensional scattering profiles along in-plane (IP) (Figure S28) as well as out-of-plane (OOP) (Figure 4g) directions shown. In addition, as presented in Tables S4 and S5, lamellar and π–π staking distances were extracted to determine their discrepancies in stacking behaviors. All polymers exhibited evident edge-on orientation, with all-level OOP (h00) lamellar scattering peaks and IP (010) π–π diffraction peaks. Apart from o-10% and m-10% (ending at the OOP (400) scattering), the other polymers all yielded ordered edge-on orientation with lamellar scattering peaks up to (500). Therefore, conformational transition induced by zigzag-structured linking units would weaken polymer-chain aggregation and thus undermine film crystallinity, whereas heteroatom modification and alkyl-chain attachment could further regulate intermolecular interactions and enhance ordered stacking. In addition, the control polymer P(DPP-BT) showed the largest lamellar stacking distance, indicating that insertion of kinking units would decrease the proportion of flexible-chain enriched DPP units toward direct π–π contact and thus favor dense packing between π-aggregates. As for the interchain π–π stacking, when compared with P(DPP-BT), m-series zigzag-structured polymers delivered shorter stacking distances and thus possibly more efficient intermolecular carrier transport. This phenomenon suggested that meta-type modification units could further promote interchain contact and percolation in m-series semiconducting polymers. For o-series polymers with double regulation of steric hindrance and chain conformation as discussed above, they were prone to present longer π–π stacking distances for intra- and intermolecular double resistance. Although o-5% and o-OC8–5% adopted a denser stacking than their 10%-modified analogues, o-F-5% and o-OMe-5% showed longer π–π stacking distances. This implied that inserting and increasing ortho-type modification units would weaken interchain π–π stacking, but heteroatom modification could reverse this situation.

To determine the influence of introducing zigzag-structured kinking units on crystalline behaviors and film-state ordering of target polymers, critical parameters of crystalline coherence length (CCL) and relative degree of crystallinity (rDoC) values were extracted from the OOP (200) scattering peaks in 2D-GIWAXS patterns, as shown in Figure 4h,i and summarized in Table S6. Normalized pole figures drawn from OOP (200) diffraction peaks are shown in Figure S29, and rDoCs were estimated based on the same scattering intensity and normalized film thickness with P(DPP-BT) set as the standard.52 Zigzag-structured geometric design could undermine the film crystallinity toward more amorphous regions, and o-series polymers indeed exhibited lower rDoCs primarily due to additional restriction in intramolecular steric hindrance. For these polymers, irrespective of smaller crystallites, potential π–π contact and heteroatom modification would come to prominence toward improved film crystallinity. Therefore, in o-series polymers, 10%-modified species showed rDoCs to be higher than those of the 5%-series analogues. Notably, excessive alkyl-chain attachment would introduce more amorphous regions and undermine film crystallinity, as observed in m-OC10C12- and o-OC8-polished polymers. Unexpectedly, m-OC10C12–5% yielded slightly higher rDoC than P(DPP-BT), which was in agreement with the appropriate alkyl-chain attachment and denser π–π stacking. As for CCLs, P(DPP-BT) afforded the largest value, indicating that it possessed larger crystalline aggregates. When quantitatively incorporating kinking units, zigzag-structured polymers tended to decrease crystallite sizes with good consistency, followed by further reduction when increasing the modification ratios. As for inferior CCLs observed in p-10% and m-10%, we speculated that overly rigid backbones and strong preaggregation kinetically hindered long-range ordered crystallization toward short-range crystalline aggregates.

Based on DFT calculations, UV–vis absorption spectra, and 2D-GIWAXS analysis, the influence of zigzag-structured kinking units on solution-state preaggregation, solid-state aggregation, and thin-film microstructures could be observed. The summaries are as follows: (i) there is no necessary relationship between crystallite size (CCLs) and film overall crystallinity (rDoCs) for zigzag-structured polymers; (ii) except (near-)linear P(DPP-BT) and p-10%, bending conformation and backbone distortion dominate m-series-conjugated polymers, and o-series analogues could also be regulated by additional intramolecular steric hindrance; (iii) the crystallite size is mainly influenced by chain conformation and insertion ratio, but regional crystallinity would be finely tuned by backbone modification; (iv) heteroatom modification and alkyl-chain attachment could further modulate polymer-chain aggregation and film crystallinity.

To further characterize film surface morphologies of these polymer semiconductors, atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were conducted, with AFM height images shown in Figure S30. Among them, near-linear p-10% yielded the highest surface roughness with the root mean square (RMS) value up to 1.43 nm, higher than that of P(DPP-BT), primarily due to the introduction of bare p-bithiophene-benzene free of alkyl chains and thus enhanced π–π stacking toward increased aggregation. In zigzag-structured polymers, m-10% and o-F-5% exhibited similar RMS values and comparable roughness to P(DPP-BT), generally in virtue of strengthened π–π contact for the former and hydrogen-bonding reinforcement for the latter. Other modified semiconducting polymers all delivered smoother and uniform surface morphologies, and the incorporation of geometric-design linking units would weaken polymer-chain aggregation and induce amorphous regions. This might decrease deep carrier traps around grain boundaries and thus enhance carrier mobilities.36,53,54

In order to investigate the mechanical properties of these semiconducting polymers and study the effect of zigzag-structured geometric design on mechanical stretchability, COS values were used to evaluate the stretchability of these polymers. Crack forming and propagating along the stretching direction would hinder carrier transport and degrade device performance.8,41 The film-on-elastomer method was used to capture film-crack formation under strain (Figure 5a). Corresponding optical microscopy (OM) images are shown in Figures S31–S43, with COS values summarized in Figure 5e. Linear P(DPP-BT) and near-linear terpolymer p-10% showed inferior COS levels below 60% (Figure 5b,c), which resulted from their higher crystallinity and strong tendency to aggregate. Upon conducting the zigzag-structured geometric design, mechanical stretchability of these semiconducting polymers could be significantly enhanced. Except o-5% with the COS of 90%, the other zigzag-structured polymers all exhibited COSs over 100%. Among them, 10%-modified species possessed better mechanical stretchability than 5%-series analogues, which implied that the conformational transition of conjugated polymers had significant influences on thin-film mechanical stretchability. Notably, m-OC10C12–10% yielded the excellent COS value up to 160% (Figure 5d), and m-OC10C12–5% unexpectedly delivered the COS of 120% in spite of its comparable crystallinity to P(DPP-BT). After referring to m-10%, in addition to polymer backbone geometric design, their superior mechanical stretchability resulted from appropriate modification ratio, local π-aggregation caused by near-planar kinking unit m-OC10C12, and microstructural modulation endowed by bulky alkyl-chain attachment. Additionally, the incorporation of hydrogen bonding could also enhance film ductility, in that extra noncovalent interactions would sustain stress and dissipate external strain.10,55 Therefore, benefiting from noncovalent cross-linking sites induced by additional fluorine atoms and appropriate modification ratio as well as thin-film microstructure, the COS of o-F-10% reached 130%. Furthermore, COS levels and mechanical stretchability agreed well with corresponding CCLs, rDoCs, and film morphologies. Generally, weakened aggregation, lower crystallinity, additional hydrogen-bonding introduction, and smoother surfaces could contribute to stretchability enhancement. Notably, although o-series zigzag-structured semiconducting polymers were dually modulated by chain conformation and intramolecular steric hindrance, m-10% showed comparable stretchability to the other 10%-modified zigzag-structured analogues. This phenomenon indicated that stress-buffering regions induced by zigzag-bending geometric design might approach to saturation.

Next, tensile moduli of these polymer semiconductors were estimated via the buckling-based metrology to evaluate their mechanical properties.8,19 The measurement details and calculation methodology are summarized in Supporting Information, Figure S44 and Table S7. Linear P(DPP-BT) and near-linear p-10% showed higher modulus levels (around 900 MPa) than those of the other zigzag-structured polymers, agreeing well with their strong aggregation, high crystallinity, and rough morphologies. Among them, m-OC10C12–5% and m-OC10C12–10% featured low tensile modulus (505.2 and 459.1 MPa, respectively) and high stretchability-resistant property, mainly due to their zigzag-bending backbones and bulky alkyl-chain attachment. These results were consistent with previous assertions, and zigzag-structured geometric design could weaken intermolecular interactions, increase chain kinking and entanglement, introduce more amorphous regions, and smooth film surfaces toward enhanced stretchability. These combined effects enabled reduction in tensile modulus, allowing to dissipate strain energy toward mobility retention under loading.40,56,57

To investigate the influence of this molecular-scale geometric design on electrical properties, we first fabricated thin-film transistors based on these semiconducting polymers with the bottom-gate/bottom-contact (BGBC) configuration to characterize their carrier mobilities. Representative transfer and output curves are shown in Figures S45–S57, and extracted device parameters (mobility, current on/off ratio, and threshold voltage) are summarized in Figure 5g and Table S8. Notably, all mobility values were extracted from the saturation regions. Inferior to the reference polymer P(DPP-BT) of 1.19 cm2 V–1 s–1, all 10%-modified species exhibited similar or lower mobilities. This indicated that conformational transition and backbone distortion would destroy intermolecular stacking and hinder intramolecular carrier transport, which had more severe influences on ortho-type counterparts. Among them, o-10% and o-OMe-10% yielded the lowest mobilities of 0.78 and 0.80 cm2 V–1 s–1, respectively. Fortunately, fluorine-atom modification and dioctyloxy-chain appendment could modulate intermolecular interactions and modify film morphologies, and particularly o-OC8–10% presented similar mobility to P(DPP-BT). Furthermore, under the background of longer ECLs and thus more efficient carrier transport for 5%-modified analogues, o-OC8–5% afforded the highest mobility up to 1.92 cm2 V–1 s–1, and m-OC10C12–5% also exhibited the excellent mobility of 1.40 cm2 V–1 s–1. Consistently, they both exhibited superior charge transport to P(DPP-BT) in top-gate/bottom-contact-type FETs, as shown in Figure S58. This phenomenon could be ascribed to the following three reasons: in the optimal 5% modification ratio, (i) there exists the balance between bending conformation accompanied by torsion disorder and long-range π-aggregation with regional direct π–π contact, wherein the former is adverse to carrier transport and the latter is prone to developing continuous conductive channels; (ii) retention in CCL or rDoC plays a critical role in efficient carrier transport, and CCL is strongly associated with ECL; (iii) additional flexible chains contribute to mobility enhancement due to favorable polymer-chain assembling and film-state modulation.

Generally, although decreasing the bending angle toward enhanced stretchability with carrier mobility weakened/retained might reach the saturation threshold around 120°, introducing heteroatom/alkyl-chain modification or incorporating ortho-isomerized units in the appropriate modification ratio would open up much wider space for realizing two-way promotion. Mechanical stretchability and electrical mobility are possible to be simultaneously improved by introducing zigzag-like modification units, and this is the comprehensive result after fully considering backbone conformation, intermolecular stacking, and film-state microstructures. As shown in Figure 5f,h, when compared with representative works in existing literatures (Table S9), these developed zigzag-structured semiconductors possess the outstanding comprehensive performance in mechanical and electrical performance.

Furthermore, morphological evolution, crystallite reorientation, and crystallinity variation of stretched thin films under different strains were characterized by AFM (Figures S59–S63), polarized UV–vis absorption spectroscopy (Figure S64), and grazing incidence small-angle X-ray scattering (GISAXS)58,59 (Figures S65–S69), respectively. Dichroic ratios (Figure 6a) were extracted from polarized absorption spectra to investigate strain-induced alignment of polymer chains,8,10 and degrees of RMS (Figure 6b) of stretched thin films under different strains were used to evaluate thin-film surface situation and crack formation.8,42 In-depth analysis of microstructural transformation could not only explain strain energy dissipation and superior mechanical stretchability of zigzag-structured polymers but also explore corresponding capabilities of charge-transport retention when subjected to external strain. Considering the representation of samples, we focused on zigzag-structured species m-OC10C12–10% (with the best stretchability), as well as m-OC10C12–5% and o-OC8–5% (optimal samples with good mechanical stretchability and carrier mobility). P(DPP-BT) and p-10% as conventional (near-)linear polymer semiconductors were selected for comparison. Upon suffering from strain, P(DPP-BT) and p-10% experienced gradual crystallite breakage and thus thin-film cracks, due to their strong aggregation, high crystallinity, and high tensile moduli. Contrarily, zigzag-structured species delivered smooth morphology, excellent crystallite reorientation, and crystallinity retention under strain. Notably, induced regional crystallization in the early stage of stretching (50 and 75% strains) could be observed for o-OC8–5%. Although m-OC10C12–5%, m-OC10C12–10%, and o-OC8–5% would undergo partial crystallite weakening under 100% strain, microcrystallite reorganization and absence of microcracks in thin films simultaneously ensured their superior stretchability and effective charge-transport pathways. Therefore, our proposed geometric design strategy was expected to realize high-performance intrinsically stretchable polymer semiconductors with excellent performance retention under strain.

Next, to confirm the effectiveness of the proposed zigzag-structured geometric design, bottom-gate/top-contact-type (BGTC-type) transistors were fabricated to measure strain-dependent carrier mobilities and evaluate mechanical durability of semiconducting polymers. The detailed fabrication procedure is shown in Figure 7a. Stretched films under different stains were transferred onto polystyrene-block-poly(ethylene-ran-butylene)-block-polystyrene (SEBS)-modified SiO2/Si substrates, wherein the insertion of ∼25 nm SEBS toward the dual dielectric layer primarily facilitated intact transfer and compact contact between semiconductor and dielectric layers, thereby passivating interfacial traps. In addition to the reference polymer P(DPP-BT), o-OC8–5% with the optimal comprehensive performance was selected to conduct further characterizations. Representative transfer and output curves are shown in Figures S70 and S71, and extracted device parameters (mobility, current on/off ratio, and threshold voltage) are summarized in Table S10. Obtained transfer curves were nearly ideal in SEBS-modified FETs,54 wherein P(DPP-BT) and o-OC8–5% exhibited the highest mobilities up to 1.13 and 1.86 cm2 V–1 s–1, respectively, approaching levels in BGBC-type transistors. Furthermore, carrier mobilities were measured under different strains (25, 50, 75, and 100%) with charge transport parallel or perpendicular to the stretching direction. Resulting transfer and output curves are shown in Figures S72–S75, with average mobilities and retention ratios summarized in Tables S11 and S12. After a slight increase in carrier mobility along the parallel stretching direction under 25% strain generally for crystallite realignment, P(DPP-BT) underwent a sharp decline induced by large-scale crack formation and propagation. Carrier mobilities dropped by two orders of magnitude under 100% strain along both stretching directions. In comparison, o-OC8–5% delivered excellent capability to maintain carrier transport under external strains. Benefiting from its moderate rDoC and reasonable crystallite size, it experienced slow and gradual mobility degradation (Figure 7b,e), retaining 1.43 and 1.37 cm2 V–1 s–1, respectively, under 100% strain along parallel and perpendicular stretching directions (Figure 7c and f, respectively). This result agreed well with it that o-OC8–5% induced crystallite orientation and regional crystallization enhancement with a smooth surface well retained under strain, especially along the direction parallel to the stretching.

For practical applications, cyclic stability under repeated stretching is another assessment criterion for intrinsically stretchable polymer semiconductors; next, multiple stretching cycles under 25% were conducted to evaluate their mechanical durability. Carrier mobilities were measured after stretching cycles (100, 200, 500, and 1000 cycles) with charge transport parallel or perpendicular to the stretching direction. Corresponding transfer and output curves are shown in Figures S76–S79, with average mobilities and retention ratios summarized in Tables S13 and S14. Due to the fully rigid backbone, dense π–π stacking, and film-state large crystallites, P(DPP-BT) exhibited poor strain tolerance, and it suffered from the abrupt decrement under stretching cycles, wherein carrier mobilities dropped to 0.072 and 0.16 cm2 V–1 s–1, respectively, along parallel and perpendicular stretching directions after 1000 cycles (Figure 7d). For o-OC8–5%, benefiting from its well-designed chain conformation, appropriate crystalline scale, and lower elastic modulus, it afforded better cyclic stability with decent mobility decline. From the initial ∼1.7 cm2 V–1 s–1, the polymer also yielded excellent carrier mobilities over 1.10 cm2 V–1 s–1, with retention ratios above 65% along two stretching directions after 1000 cycles (Figure 7g). On the whole, in addition to the higher mobility, the zigzag-structured polymer o-OC8–5% possessed superior mechanical durability and cyclic stability to the reference polymer P(DPP-BT).

Conclusions

In summary, ortho-isomerized (o, o-F, o-OMe and o-OC8) and meta-isomerized (m, m-OC10C12) bithiophene-benzene units are quantitatively incorporated into the regioregular backbone of P(DPP-BT), with a series of zigzag-structured polymer semiconductors synthesized. Correspondingly, backbone conformation, aggregation behavior, thin-film microstructure, and mechanical and electrical properties are deeply explored. This geometric design strategy has advantaged superiority in enhancing the intrinsic stretchability of conjugated polymers. Based on an appropriate modification ratio, molecular-scale structural design could endow polymer semiconductors with better mechanical and electrical properties under the assistance of heteroatom embedment and alkyl-chain attachment. In addition to bending-conformation modulation, long-range π-aggregation and short-range torsion disorder both play critical roles in the resulting comprehensive performance. Superior to the reference polymer P(DPP-BT), zigzag-structured o-OC8–5% yields a high COS value of 110%, with the highest field-effect carrier mobility up to 1.92 cm2 V–1 s–1. It could afford 1.43 and 1.37 cm2 V–1 s–1 under 100% strain with charge transport parallel and perpendicular to the stretching direction, respectively. In addition, even after suffering from cyclic stretching over 1000 cycles under 25% strain, o-OC8–5% also maintains above 1.10 cm2 V–1 s–1 along both stretching directions. This design strategy provides important guidelines to developing next-generation high-performance intrinsically stretchable polymer semiconductors.
