Optimizing gamma irradiation for genotype-specific enhancement of pollen performance and haploid induction in cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.)
Authors: M. Ebadi Hasan Abadi, Z. Ghahremani, A. Borzouei, B. Naserian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of pure homozygous lines is a critical objective in plant breeding, yet conventional methodologies for achieving this goal are time-consuming. Double haploid (DH) technology suggests a powerful technique for producing pure lines with homozygosity stabilization within a single generation. This study aimed to optimize haploid induction protocols and evaluated the effects of gamma-irradiated pollen (300, 400, 500 Gy, Co⁶⁰) on three cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) genotypes (Dastjerdi, Beith Alpha, Super Dominus). Functional traits and pollen grain morphology, embryogenesis and fruit properties were evaluated under two pollination treatments (T1: simultaneous with irradiation; T2: one-day delay). The results showed that, dose of 300 Gy positively stimulated germination, pollen tube growth and haploid embryogenesis, while higher doses adversely affected pollen viability and wall thickness. Among the genotypes, ‘Dastjerdi’ exhibited superior tolerance to gamma radiation, likely attributable to its enhanced antioxidant capacity and efficient DNA repair mechanisms. Regarding pollination timing, simultaneous irradiation (T1) demonstrated higher efficacy compared to the delayed approach (T2). Strong positive correlations were identified between embryogenesis and pollen functional traits. No significant relationships were observed between pollen traits and fruit characteristics, indicating independent regulatory mechanisms. In conclusion, this study indicates that determining the optimal dose and correct pollination timing each genotype to improve DH production efficiency within cucumber breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40441-6.

Introduction

Homozygous inbred lines are essential as parental lines for genetic analysis and breeding programs in agricultural crops. Additionally, homozygous lines contribute to the development of strategies for overcoming abiotic stressors such as drought1, and they are used on progressing F1 hybrid varieties with desirable agricultural characteristics in the seed industry. To achieve highly homozygous parental lines, classical breeding requires several generations of inbreeding (typically 6–8 generations), which makes the process time-consuming and labor-intensive. Moreover, inbreeding may lead to varying levels of selfing depression throughout the breeding cycle2. Therefore, obtaining superior parental lines through haploid methods is crucial. However, haploid production represented an outstanding advancement as it could generate pure lines in a single generation and thus improve selection efficiency. However, spontaneous haploid induction evidence is a rare event3, so haploid and doubled haploid (DH) technology are highly valuable because it enables the fixation of traits in a completely homozygous state within a single generation. These homozygous inbred lines can be generated through the induction of doubled haploids (DHs) using gynogenesis and androgenesis approaches in many vegetable crops4. With in vitro haploid and doubled hoploid techniques significant time, labor, and cost savings are achieved, while the constant introduction new varieties to the market is also accelerated5. These homozygous individuals (DH) plants offer valuable advantages for both fundamental research and practical applications, such as creating mapping populations, developing inbred lines, and diversifying genetic variability6,7.

Cucumber (Cucumis sativus L. 2n = 2x = 14), as a member of the Cucurbitaceous family, is a highly significant vegetable crop cultivated globally in diverse agro climatic conditions, including open fields and green house. As an outbreeding crop without inbreeding depression, widely due to its unique breeding behavior and relatively smaller genome size (372 Mbp) serves as an excellent model crop, was used in vegetable crop breeding and functional genomics research8,9. Resistance to pathogens such as zucchini yellow mosaic virus (ZYVMV) and cucumber downy mildew, which affect fruit quality and yield, is governed by recessive genes. Hence, utilizing haploids in breeding is of most importance for effectively addressing these challenges10. Parthenogenesis (irradiated pollen technique) is the most commonly used technique to obtain haploid plants in the Cucurbitaceae family11.

The irradiated pollen technique, originally applied in petunia, has been successfully used among cucurbit crops, including cucumber15,16, watermelon12, melon13,14, squash and Cucurbita maxima Duch17, and other cucurbits such as Cucurbita pepo20,21. In cucumber, this technique is actively used to obtain pure lines for the production of F1 hybrid varieties18,19.

Irradiated pollen can also stimulate parthenogenic embryos and produce haploid plants in cucumber and squash, which can be integrated into breeding programs{Ermetin, 2023 #7}. Development of haploids in cucumber, using pollen irradiated with gamma rays approach holds greater potential than other androgenesis methods, because of their simple application, good penetration, reproducibility, and low disposal (lethal) problems19.

The primary goal of gamma irradiation is to produce genetically inactive pollen (with eliminated generative cells) that can germinate on the stigma, grow, reach the embryo sac, and stimulate haploid embryo development. The irradiated pollen is genetically inactive but germinable which can stimulate the division of the egg cell and induce parthenogenesis17,24. This method requires immature embryo rescue under in vitro conditions. Pollen grains, are haploid sensitive to mutation factors like gamma irradiation. Pollination with irradiated pollen is one of the standard methods to induce in situ parthenogenesis to get haploid plants. In this method, pollen germination occurs on stigma, and the pollen tube elongates to reach the embryo sac. The first studies investigated the effects of irradiation on pollen germination and pollen tube elongation26. Although the factors affecting haploid plant production with irradiated pollen are similar to other haploidization techniques, they show differences. Among the different factors influencing the efficiency of the progress, the irradiation dose is especially important27.

High-dose irradiation causes chromatin fragmentation in the nuclei of male gametes, and abnormal seed development, such as parthenogenesis and autonomous endosperm formation, has been observed after pollination of irradiated pollen grains28. However, these views agree that pollination with irradiated pollen mainly consists of cytological abnormalities during mitotic divisions during pollen tube formation and deviations in the normal sexual fertilization process26. The effects of ionizing radiation on pollen grains have been extensively studied, and pollen grains are generally considered highly resistant to radiation Extremely high doses of irradiation are required to inhibit pollen germination and pollen tube elongation29. Some factors, such as genotype, irradiation doses limit the effectiveness of haploidy frequency using the irradiated pollen technique30. Therefore, determination effects of radiation doses on pollen behavior are one of the most important factors in in situ parthenogenesis. The patterns of fundamental changes in the biological characteristics of pollination and fertility in pollen activity were revealed by observing the surface and internal structures of pollen. Therefore, optimization of the irradiated pollen technique in cucumber is essential to increase the efficiency of plant breeding10.

The objective of the present study was to evaluate the effects of different gamma irradiation doses (Co 60), capacity different genotypes pollen and time of irradiation and pollination on key reproductive traits including pollen grain germination, tube development, surface, diameter, wall thickness, induce haploid plants followed by in vitro embryo rescue and fruit characteristics derived from gamma-irradiated pollen.

Material and method

Plant materials and growth conditions

The present study was conducted in the greenhouse and plant tissue culture laboratory of the “Agricultural Research School, Nuclear Sciences and Technology Research Institute, Karaj, Iran” in 2024 and 2025. Three genotypes Cucumis sativus L. ‘Dastjerdi’ (an internal sample), ‘Beith Alpha (BA)’ and ‘Super Dominus (SD)’ were used as pollinators and different commercial varieties as female parents. Two commercial cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) hybrids were obtained from international seed companies. The first hybrid was “Beith Alpha” (Lot No. 10801.15, Rossen Seeds, Holland), and the second hybrid was “Super Dominus” (Batch No. 0191975277, Seminis, USA). In addition, one local genotype of cucumber was collected from “Dastjerd/Isfahan, Iran”, which is traditionally cultivated in that area. The local genotype was propagated, maintained, and authenticated in the research greenhouse. All three genotypes were specifically selected because of their stable male flower production outdoor, which was essential for the objectives of this study and grown under identical greenhouse conditions to ensure comparability of the results.

Seeds were grown in polycarbonate greenhouse conditions with a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C during the day and 20 ± 2 °C during the night, humidity of 75% and a photoperiod of 16 h/8 h day/night23. Meanwhile, all plants received standard cultivation practices, mineral soil fertilization, regular pruning and disease and pest management during both growth periods. This study investigated the effects of gamma radiation doses, pollinator genotypes and time on pollen grain characteristics and embryogenesis. For the time treatment, opened male flowers were irradiated on the day of flower collection and were either pollinated on the same day and placed on the culture medium (T1). Male flower buds, whose petals were yellowish green, were placed on the culture medium and pollinated the day after irradiation (T2), (Fig. 1). Also, in this experiment, the correlation between pollen and fruit characteristics obtained from gamma-irradiated pollen and embryogenesis rate was investigated. To evaluate the effects of treatments on germination and pollen tube growth, evaluations were performed 24 h after cultivation in vitro. Flow cytometry technique was used to confirm the haploid status of the obtained embryos.

Preparing flower buds, irradiation

Female flowers were covered with special bags to prevent unwanted pollination. Petals of male flowers were carefully separated using a scalpel and placed in a Petri dish for irradiation. Irradiation was performed using a Co60 gamma ray source with intensities of 300, 400 and 500 Gy with an irradiation intensity of 115 Gy/h. For the control group, pollen without irradiation was placed on solid culture medium. After irradiation, anthers of T2 treatment were incubated overnight in a desiccator at 24 °C and 75% relative humidity to facilitate the bursting of pollen sacs and were placed on the culture medium and pollinated the next day.

Assessment of pollen characteristics

To evaluate the effects of radiation and anther maturation stage at T1 and T2 on the induction of embryogenesis, T1 pollen of all three genotypes was plated on culture medium on the same day and T2 pollen on the following day. The culture medium was prepared containing 15% sucrose, 0.01% w/v boric acid, 1 mM calcium nitrate, and 0.3% agar at pH = 6. To sterilize the culture medium, it was autoclaved at 120 °C for 30 min31. Pollen grains were spread on solid culture medium and incubated at 24 ± 2 °C for 24 h. To measure pollen traits, three square Sect. (1 × 1 mm) were randomly cut from the culture medium of each Petri dish and placed on microscope slides. The samples were stained with Giemsa and the slides were coverslipped. Five areas were randomly selected from each slide and analyzed. Germinated pollen grains were counted under a light microscope (Euromex bScope series) at 40x magnification. The germination experiment was repeated twice in three Petri dishes for each replicate. From each area, elongated pollen tubes that were at least three times their diameter were photographed, and pollen tube length, pollen grain surface area, diameter, and wall thickness were measured using ImageJ software (Fig. 2).

Pollination with irradiated pollen and evaluation of fruit traits

Unopened female flowers were covered early in the morning one day before pollination and pollinated the next day with irradiated pollen (300, 400, 500 Gy gamma rays) at doses of T1 and T2. Then, female flowers were covered with paper bags to prevent contamination with external pollen, labeled and recorded. After 21–28 days after pollination, fruits were harvested. Fruit weight, length and diameter were measured to investigate the correlation with embryogenesis. Embryos obtained from seeds were cultured in vitro for further analysis.

Embryo rescue

To prevent contamination, the harvested fruits were surface sterilized with 70% ethanol. The seeds were extracted and surface sterilized by immersion in a diluted commercial bleach solution (sodium hypochlorite 5% w/v) for 10 min and then rinsed three times in sterile distilled water for 10 min.

The sterilized seeds were placed in Petri dishes containing 25 ml of E20 liquid culture medium supplemented with 3% sucrose and 0.01 mg/L indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) at pH 6.0 for 3 weeks44.

The Petri dishes were then sealed with parafilm and placed in a growth chamber with a constant temperature of 24 °C, a 16-h photoperiod and a light intensity of 30 µmol m2s− 1. After 3 weeks, healthy, non-necrotic embryos were mechanically extracted from the seeds and cultured in Petri dishes containing MS (Murashige and Skoog) supplemented with 0.5 mg/L naphthalene acetic acid (NAA), 5 mg/L zeatin, 0.2 mg/L GA3, 250 mg/L PVP, 30 g/L sucrose, and 3–4 g/L agar, pH 5.9. The isolated embryos were incubated in a growth chamber at 25 ± 2°C and a photoperiod of 16 h light followed by 8 h darkness45.

Ploidy level assessment

The ploidy level of the seedlings was determined using flow cytometry. For ploidy analysis, the reference control was used as an external standard. The reference control and unknown ploidy samples were evaluated using a flow cytometer (BD Accuri C6, USA) equipped with a 552 nm laser and a 585/42 Band Pass filter. Approximately 1000–3000 nuclei were collected and analyzed for each sample. The detector settings were adjusted using the diploid control and the same settings were run for the samples. The results were displayed as single-parameter histograms with the G0/G1 peak on a linear scale. The fluorescence intensity (median value) of G0/G1 for the reference control and samples was recorded. The CV of the G0/G1 peaks for the reference controls and samples was less than 5%. Statistical analysis

Pollen tube length, pollen germination percentage and morphological traits including pollen grain area, diameter and thickness of genotypes were evaluated at different radiation doses and time treatments. Statistical analysis was performed using R data analysis software (R Core Team, 2024). Significant differences among treatments were determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test at p ≤ 0.0547.

Results

The ANOVA results in Table 1 reveal that pollen donor genotype significantly affected all traits studied (p ≤ 0.01), indicating notable genetic variation in pollen performance and its impact on fruit development. Gamma irradiation influenced pollen tube length (p ≤ 0.01), fruit length (p ≤ 0.05), and fruit diameter (p ≤ 0.05), but had no significant effect on pollen germination or fruit weight. This suggests irradiation altered pollen elongation and some fruit traits while having limited or inconsistent impact on germination and fruit mass. Time factors (T1 vs. T2) strongly affected pollen traits (p ≤ 0.01) but had minimal influence on fruit traits, except when interacting with genotype. Overall, pollen attributes like germination and tube length showed high sensitivity to variables and interactions, whereas fruit traits were less responsive.

Pollen germination rate

According to the analysis of variance (Table 1), gamma dose alone had no statistically significant effect on germination percentage (p < 0.05), and based on the comparison of means, germination percentage was relatively stable among doses, changing from 51.41% at 400 Gy to 56.65% at 500 Gy without any significant difference (Table 2). While genotype (p < 0.01), time (p < 0.01) and interaction of treatments (G × D, G × T, D × T, G × D × T) had a significant effect on cucumber pollen germination. Among the genotypes, ‘Dastagerdi’ showed the highest germination percentage (63.04%), which was significantly better than BA (53.36%) and SD (49.41%). Furthermore, T1 pollen had a significantly higher germination rate (75.77%) compared to T2 (24.76%), highlighting the importance of timing in pollen viability. Interaction effects showed that pollen of Dastajerdi irradiated with 400 Gy maintained a high germination rate (98.9%). Conversely, SD was more sensitive at 300 Gy and showed the most severe reduction (1% germination). Dastajerdi had a higher germination rate when pollinated at T2 among other genotype (Fig. 3). At the 300 Gy dose T1, germination was also relatively high (about 80.83%), and it decreased slightly at the 400 Gy (79.67%) and 500 Gy (79.17%) doses. However, a severe reduction occurred at the 400 Gy dose at T1 (Fig. 3). The three-way interaction between radiation dose, time and pollen genotype significantly affected pollen germination, and the cultivar Dastagerdi irradiated at a dose of 400 Gy and at time T1 showed a significantly high germination rate (98.9%). In contrast, the cultivar SD irradiated at a dose of 300 Gy and at time T2 showed the lowest germination (1%), indicating the combined negative effects of genotype, radiation and time sensitivity (Fig. 4).

Pollen tube length

Pollen tube length was significantly affected by all main factors (p < 0.01), and the interaction effects of treatments (Table 1). With increasing radiation dose gradually pollen tube length decreased, with the control group (0 Gy) showing the longest mean tube length (84.74 μm) followed by the 300 Gy treatment (73.42 μm). A significant reduction occurred at 400 Gy (32.95 μm), indicating a strong inhibitory effect of some doses (Table 2). Among the genotypes, pollen tube length of BA showed significantly longer (71.61 μm) followed by Dastjerdi (57.28 μm), while SD had the lowest performance (56.94 μm) but were statistically similar. These differences highlight genotype-specific responses in pollen viability and pollen tube elongation (Table 2). Time also had a strong and consistent effect on pollen tube length. T1 pollen produced significantly longer tubes compared to T2 pollen (82.51 μm and 41.37 μm, respectively). This suggests that delaying pollination after gamma irradiation impairs pollen tube elongation. All two- and three-way interactions, except interaction between time and pollen genotype, also reached statistical significance (p < 0.01), indicating that tube growth was strongly responsive to the combined effects of genetic background, radiation exposure, and pollination time (Table 1). BA showed greater pollen tube growth at 500 Gy and outperformed SD and Dastjerdi at high dose conditions. Dastjerdi genotype especially at lower doses showed moderate pollen tube length (57.28 μm). The genotypes showed different responses to pollination time. Dastjerdi and SD produced significantly shorter pollen tubes at T2. In contrast, Dastjerdi maintained better performance at T1 (Fig. 3). The interaction between gamma dose and pollination time (p < 0.01) showed that the most severe reduction in pollen tube growth occurred at dose of 500 Gy at time T2. In the three-way interaction of treatments, cultivar SD at a dose of 300 Gy and time T1 showed the least tube growth (0.95 μm). It is noteworthy that cultivars BA and Dastajerdi at a dose of 500 Gy and time T2 achieved significant pollen tube length (125.6 μm and 109.18 μm, respectively), indicating resilience under the conditions of pollination on the same day at high dose. These results highlight genotype-specific temporal sensitivity to radiation.

Pollen grain surface area

The results of analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed that both genotype and gamma dose played a significant role in the variation of pollen surface area, while time alone did not have a significant effect on this trait (Table 1). Regarding the effect of gamma radiation dose, the 300 Gy treatment had the highest mean pollen surface area (49.62 μm²), which was statistically comparable to the control group (47.06 μm²). In contrast, the 400 Gy and 500 Gy doses had significantly lower pollen surface area (39.19 μm² and 36.34 μm², respectively) (Table 2). These results indicate that high doses have a directional effect on pollen grain growth. According to Duncan’s multiple range test, the Dastjerdi genotype showed the highest pollen surface area (47.12 μm²), which was significantly better than the BA (42.84 μm²) and SD (39.21 μm²) genotypes. This indicates a strong genetic potential in Dastjerdi to maintain pollen viability under radiation stress (Table 2). The significant differences in mean pollen grain surface among genotypes highlight the genetic contribution to pollen structure. Significant two-way interactions were observed between treatments (P < 0.01), indicating that the response of pollen grain surface area to radiation and time was genotype-dependent. Different genotypes showed different sensitivities to different radiation levels, such that Dastjerdi cultivar performed better at a dose of 300 Gy, and SD cultivar showed a significant decrease at higher doses. Also, the interaction of gamma dose and time suggests that the negative effects of radiation may be exacerbated at T2 (Fig. 5). In addition, the three-way interaction between genotype× gamma dose× and time was also significant at the 1% level, indicating the complex interaction of these factors in shaping the pollen grain surface. Each genotype showed a unique response pattern at different doses and time points, reflecting the multifactorial nature of physiological adaptation. Specifically, the Dastjerdi genotype showed the highest pollen surface at 300 Gy and time point T1, while SD shows the lowest value at 500 Gy and T2.

Pollen grain diameter

The results of analysis of variance (Table 1) show that genotype and gamma radiation dose at the 1% probability level and time treatment, although to a lesser extent (at the 5% probability level), independently had a significant effect on changes in pollen grain diameter. Among the genotypes, Dastjerdi showed the largest pollen grain diameter with an average of 7.71 μm and was placed in the statistical group “a”. The BA and SD genotypes, with an average diameter of 6.80 μm and 6.99 μm, respectively, were placed in the same group and showed a significant difference from Dastjerdi (Table 2). These results indicate that Dastjerdi performs better than other genotypes in terms of pollen grain diameter. Regarding the effect of gamma radiation dose, the control treatment (0 Gy) recorded the highest mean diameter (7.90 μm) followed by the 300 Gy dose (7.76 μm) with a small difference, both belonging to the same statistical group and not significantly different from each other. In contrast, doses of 400 Gy and 500 Gy led to a significant reduction in diameter (6.80 μm and 6.50 μm), placing them in statistical groups “b” and “c”, respectively (Table 2). This trend indicates a dose-dependent inhibitory effect of gamma radiation on pollen grain diameter, with higher doses leading to a significant reduction. The interaction effects of the experimental treatments were all significant at the 1% level (Table 1). These interactions highlight the different responses of genotypes to different radiation doses and sampling time, and reveal the strong effect of time and dose in combination with genotype. The interaction of genotype and time showed that SD maintained the highest mean diameter at time point T1, while a significant decrease was observed at T2, especially in the SD genotype (Fig. 5). In the interaction of dose and time, higher radiation doses (400 and 500 Gy) at T2 resulted in a sharp decrease in pollen grain diameter (Fig. 5). The three-way interaction of genotype × gamma dose × time was also significant at the 1% level, indicating a synergistic effect of these factors on pollen grain diameter. In this interaction, Dastjerdi showed the highest diameter at low radiation doses (0 and 300 Gy) at T1, while BA showed the lowest diameter at 500 Gy at T2. These findings indicate that Dastjerdi is more resistant to radiation and time stress, while BA is more sensitive and performs poorly under such conditions.

Pollen grain wall thickness

Analysis of variance showed that the main effects of genotype, gamma radiation dose, and time all played a role in pollen wall structure at the 1% probability level (P < 0.01) (Table 1). According to Duncan’s multiple range test, the Dastjerdi genotype showed the highest pollen wall thickness (0.55μm), which significantly outperformed the other genotypes. The BA genotype ranked second with a mean thickness of 0.45 μm, while the SD genotype showed the lowest (0.32 μm) (Table 2). Regarding the effect of gamma radiation dose, the 300 Gy treatment had the highest pollen wall thickness (0.54 μm) (Table 2). The 400 Gy dose (0.47 μm) was statistically similar to the control group (0.41 μm), while the 500 Gy dose produced the lowest thickness (0.33 μm), which was significantly lower than the other doses. This decreasing trend in wall thickness with increasing radiation dose, which mirrors the pattern observed in pollen grain diameter, suggests an inhibitory effect of higher doses on cell wall development. The interaction between genotype and gamma radiation dose (P < 0.01) indicates that genotypic responses to radiation were not uniform; each genotype performed better at certain doses. In contrast, the interaction between genotype and time was not significant, indicating that temporal changes in pollen wall thickness were relatively independent of genotype. The interaction between dose and time was also significant at the 5% probability level (P < 0.05), emphasizing the role of time in moderating the effects of gamma radiation (Fig. 5). In addition, the three-way interaction between genotype, radiation dose, and time was highly significant (P < 0.01), indicating a complex interaction between these factors in determining pollen wall thickness and that pollen stability after irradiation is a genetic trait. For example, Dastjerdi was more stable even in the T2 condition, while SD was very sensitive.

Fruit characteristics derived from gamma-irradiated pollen

The results showed that pollen behavior was more sensitive to experimental treatments than fruit traits. Significant main effects of genotype (p < 0.01) and gamma dose (p < 0.05) were observed on fruit length, while time had no significant effect. Genotype had a significant effect on all fruit traits, especially weight and diameter (Table 1). Fruits pollinated with “BA” pollen had significantly smaller diameters than fruits pollinated with other genotypes. In contrast, gamma dose had a more limited effect, only significantly affecting fruit length (p < 0.05) and fruit diameter (p < 0.05). Among the interaction effects, only genotype × time were significant (p < 0.05), indicating that fruit length is primarily determined by genetic identity, and its interaction with pollination time and radiation levels plays a minor role. For fruit weight, only genotype and its interaction with time (G × T) had significant effects (p < 0.01), while gamma dose, time and other interactions were not significant (Table 1). This indicates that genotypic performance and its response to time play a more pivotal role in determining fruit biomass than radiation alone. Although time did not independently have a significant effect on any of the fruit traits, its interaction with genotype (G × T) significantly affected fruit growth. The cultivar Dastajerdi consistently produced heavier and longer fruits when pollinated at T1, while BA and SD performed better at T2 and produced longer and heavier fruits. Overall, fruit length and weight were moderated by genotype × time interaction, indicating genotype-dependent time effects and highlighting the dominant role of genetic background and radiation dose on this trait, with time playing a relatively minor role.

Haploidy levels were confirmed using flow cytometry, based on nuclear DNA content of regenerated plantlets derived from in vitro embryo rescue, in comparison to diploid control plants (2x) (Fig. 6). The histogram peaks for haploid and diploid individuals were clearly distinguishable, confirming the ploidy levels of regenerated lines32.

Correlation between embryo formation and traits

Approximately 21,000 seeds were obtained from fruits produced by pollination with gamma-irradiated pollen. Due to unequal experimental materials, embryogenesis results under treatment are presented only as percentages (%) (Fig. 7). The results showed that embryogenesis was significantly correlated with the radiation dose and the genotype of the pollinator. In general, the number of induced embryos decreased significantly with increasing radiation dose. Most of the recovered embryos were derived from the Dastjerdi genotype, and the highest embryogenesis (1.65%) was observed in Dastjerdi pollen irradiated with 300 Gy and pollinated at time T2. This indicates that moderate irradiation in combination with the appropriate genotype can increase the efficiency of haploid production. Based on Fig. 8, correlation analysis between reproductive and fruit-related traits revealed significant relationships that provide insights into the factors governing the induction of haploid embryos under gamma irradiation. The percentage of haploid embryo formation (Emb%) showed a strong and positive correlation with pollen germination (P.G; r = 0.94, p < 0.01) and pollen tube length (P.L; r = 0.82, p < 0.01). Furthermore, pollen germination was positively correlated with pollen tube length (r = 0.79, p < 0.01), highlighting the interdependence of these two functional traits. Conversely, pollen grain morphological traits, such as pollen grain surface area (P.S) and pollen grain diameter (P.D), showed significant negative correlations with Emb% (r = -0.54, p < 0.05) and pollen tube length (r = -0.73, p < 0.01), respectively. Similarly, pollen wall thickness (P.W) showed positive correlations with pollen grain diameter (r = 0.76, p < 0.01) and surface area (r = 0.54, p < 0.05), but was negatively correlated with germination capacity and tube elongation. Regarding fruit traits, fruit weight (F.W) had a very strong positive correlation with fruit diameter (F.D; r = 0.97, p < 0.01) and fruit length (F.L; r = 0.76, p < 0.01). Similarly, fruit diameter and fruit length were positively correlated (r = 0.63, p < 0.05). However, the correlation between fruit traits and pollen-related traits was weak and non-significant, indicating the relative independence of reproductive efficiency at the gametophytic level and fruit development at the sporophytic level.

Discussion

The present study provides clear evidence of genotypic differences in cucumber plant response to gamma irradiation, especially in relation to pollen grain functional traits such as germination, pollen tube growth, and embryo formation, which were unique to each genotype. The findings showed that increasing gamma irradiation dose caused a decrease in pollen tube length, indicating an inhibitory effect of high doses on pollen growth. Despite this decreasing trend, a slight increase in pollen tube length was observed at a dose of 500 Gy, indicating that even when the germination rate remained constant, high-dose irradiation can disrupt pollen tube growth. The highest mean pollen surfacearea and wall thickness were recorded in the 300 Gy treatment, while the 500 Gy dose caused a significant decrease in these indices. This decrease, which is also similar to the pattern of pollen grain diameter, indicates the negative effects of high doses on cell wall development. The findings showed that a dose of 300 Gy, in addition to increasing germination and pollen tube growth, activated the haploid embryogenesis process without the need for complete fertilization. However, this response did not follow a linear pattern with increasing radiation dose, and higher doses (400–500 Gy) exerted inhibitory effects on wall structure and pollen tube growth. This pattern is consistent with the concept of “radiation hormesis”, a phenomenon in which low to moderate doses of radiation stimulate biological functions and activate adaptive mechanisms, while high doses lead to irreversible cellular damage33. These observations are consistent with previous reports in cucumber and other cucurbits and confirm the dual role of radiation in stimulating or suppressing reproductive processes14,34,35.

The Dastjerdi genotype, with higher germination percentage and pollen surface area, was more resistant to radiation, indicating the effective role of genetics in maintaining pollen viability (Fig. 9). In contrast, the SD genotype showed high sensitivity to radiation and had poorer performance in germination and pollen tube growth. The BA genotype also produced the longest pollen tube. These differences indicate the effect of genetic variation on pollen performance under stress and indicate the strong genetic potential of Dastjerdi pollen to maintain viability under radiation (Fig. 9). Pollen from the T1 treatment produced longer pollen tubes than T2 treatment, indicating the negative effect of delaying pollination after gamma irradiation on pollen tube growth. This difference in performance highlights the role of time in maintaining pollen potency and coordinating gametophytic viability with ovule growth36. The results of this study showed that the reduction in pollen tube length of sensitive cucumber genotypes at high doses (400–500 Gy) reflects the negative effects of radiation on pollen structure and physiological function, impaired nutrient transport, reduced moisture content, and changes in the cell wall. These effects, as reported by Serrano et al.37, indicate a delicate balance between stimulatory and inhibitory responses that depend on radiation intensity and species characteristics.

Yiğit et al.38 reported that gamma radiation affects pollen viability, germination and pollen tube growth in apple. They did not, however, report direct measurements of enzyme activities. High-dose γ-irradiation has been shown to negatively affect pollen physiological performance in cucurbit crops by reducing pollen germination and tube growth due to disruptions in cellular metabolism. This inhibitory response varies among genotypes and reflects differences in radiation tolerance within Cucumis sativus and related cucurbit species. The first evidence supporting this mechanism in reproductive tissues was reported by Georgieva and Atanassov48. Their foundational work on enzymatic responses is strongly supported by modern research, which confirms that gamma radiation triggers genotype-specific alterations in key antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, and catalase, directly linking their activity to observed variations in fertility and radiosensitivity49. More recent investigations provide further confirmation of these genotype-specific sensitivity patterns and fertility impairments under γ-irradiation stress. Therefore, the reductions in pollen germination and tube elongation observed here likely represent genotype-dependent stress signaling processes triggered by γ-irradiation, although the underlying biochemical pathways should be confirmed using targeted enzymatic and molecular assays in future studies.

Our results suggest that the local genotype Dastjerdi exhibits higher tolerance to gamma radiation and time-related stress, maintaining pollen viability and embryogenesis rates, whereas the commercial variety Beith Alpha (BA) appears more sensitive, showing reduced performance under the same conditions. These observations align with previous studies reporting genotype-dependent stress tolerance in Cucumis sativus44.

The greatest reduction in pollen tube growth was observed at a dose of 500 Gy T2. Also, high doses (400 and 500 Gy) in T2 caused a significant decrease in pollen grain diameter, indicating the vulnerability of critical developmental stages to intense radiation. The observed effects of γ-irradiation on pollen viability and tube growth are consistent with well-documented mechanisms involving reduced pollen water content and impaired carbohydrate metabolism, both of which are critically dependent on cytoplasmic hydration50. Experimental evidence suggests that pollen metabolic maturation plays a key role in embryogenesis success, and the timing of male flower harvest can be effective in maintaining pollen viability and increasing embryo induction efficiency. The interaction of genotype and time with the maximum mean square (2572.0) showed that the temporal changes in pollen traits were strongly influenced by genotype. At T2, the BA and SD T2 genotypes had significantly shorter diameter, while the Dastjerdi cultivar maintained a more favorable yield at both times, especially at T2. These differences indicate the different physiological sensitivity of the genotypes to time and its role in moderating the effects of gamma radiation, which is probably related to the developmental stages and metabolic status of the pollen. The triple interaction of genotype, radiation dose, and time had a significant effect on pollen yield, such that each genotype showed a unique pattern under different conditions. SD cultivar had the lowest pollen tube growth at 300 Gy and T1, while BA and Dastjerdi had significant tube length at 500 Gy and T1, indicating their greater tolerance to T1 and high dose conditions. Dastjerdi also maintained the highest pollen surface and diameter at 300 Gy and T1, while BA performed poorly at 500 Gy and T2. Treatments such as Dastjerdi×300×T2, SD × 300×T1, and BA×500×T1 were effective in expressing pollen traits, while combinations such as BA×500×T2 and Dastjerdi×Control×T2 had a limited role. These results indicate that T1 may increase reproductive success by reducing gametophytic damage and better coordinating with egg development. Pollen stability after irradiation appears to be a genetic trait, possibly related to DNA repair capacity, membrane stability, and antioxidant responses17,39.

The strong positive correlation between the percentage of haploid embryo formation by germination and pollen tube length indicated that early pollen function plays a direct role in the success of haploid induction. This finding is consistent with molecular studies in cucumber40that the expression of sugar transporters such as CsHT1 enhances the efficient transport of sugars, pollen tube growth, and ultimately fertilization success. Therefore, the metabolic maturation of pollen is considered a key factor in the efficiency of haploid embryogenesis. Also, environmental factors such as moisture, oxygen, CO₂, and temperature have a significant impact on pollen viability41. The findings of Stephan (2021)29also confirm that ionizing radiation affects pollen tube growth and nuclear function, and that pollen tube elongation is more strongly associated with embryogenesis than with germination rate, which is consistent with our findings. The observed decrease in pollen wall thickness with increasing radiation dose, paralleling the reduction in pollen grain diameter, suggests that higher gamma radiation doses may inhibit cell wall development. Similar inhibitory effects of radiation on cell wall formation have been reported in Cucurbitaceae10,44.

In contrast, pollen morphological traits such as pollen surface area, diameter, and wall thickness showed a negative correlation with embryogenesis percentage. This is consistent with pollenological findings in Cucurbita pepo, where during maturation the thickness and surface protrusions of the exine increase and can act as a mechanical barrier to pollen tube exit42. Such changes are likely part of the normal pollen maturation process, but under certain conditions, they can be associated with reduced pollen tube growth efficiency, explaining the negative correlation between pollen grain area and pollen tube length in this study.

In general, increasing the dose of gamma radiation caused a decrease in the number of induced embryos, with the medium dose (300 Gy) having the highest embryogenesis rate. In contrast, high doses in sensitive genotypes may disrupt developmental processes and reduce fertility. The Dastjerdi genotype performed better than BA and SD at this dose and showed a stronger correlation between embryogenesis and pollen germination percentage. In contrast, the SD genotype showed a greater decrease in the correlation between pollen tube length and embryogenesis at a dose of 500 Gy, indicating the role of genetic differences in radiation tolerance and response. These findings emphasize that combining the appropriate dose of radiation with the genotype can increase the efficiency of haploid production. Previous studies have also suggested a range of 200 to 400 Gy for optimizing the parthenogenetic response43. Also, the irradiation time is an important factor, because irradiation at the T2 stage can disrupt critical cellular events in sensitive genotypes.

On the other hand, the weak correlation between pollen characteristics and fruit traits (length, diameter, weight) suggests that pollen quality does not determine fruit traits and that pistil tissue conditions play a more important role in this process. Dastjerdi cultivar produced fruits with greater weight and length at T1, while BA and SD performed better at T2. Fruit length and weight were affected by the interaction of genotype and time; in particular, fruit length was more dependent on genetic structure, and the role of time and irradiation was secondary. In the case of weight, only genotype and its interaction with time were significant, and irradiation dose did not have a statistically significant effect. Therefore, the genotype response to time plays a more key role in determining fruit biomass, and although gamma irradiation has a significant effect on pollen yield, its effects on fruit morphological traits are mostly insignificant.

These differences indicate that a single protocol cannot be proposed for all genotypes and that optimization and selection of the appropriate genotype × dose × time combination plays a decisive role in embryo regeneration and haploid induction efficiency.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that gamma irradiation had significant but complex, genotype and time-dependent effects on cucumber pollen performance. The optimal dose of 300 Gy was identified as the dose that enhanced in vitro pollen germination, pollen tube growth, and subsequent haploid embryo formation in specific genotypes that exhibited responsiveness. In contrast, lower and higher doses generally exhibited reduced efficacy or even deleterious effects. These results indicate the presence of significant genotype × irradiation dose × pollination time interactions. Pollen physiological parameters (particularly in vitro germination and tube growth rate and percentage) were found to be more reliable predictors of embryogenic potential than pollen morphological/structural traits, which played a secondary or inconsistent role. Despite the ongoing promise and relative simplicity of utilizing gamma-irradiated pollen as a tool for haploid induction in cucumber, the present study demonstrated that the optimal dose must be ascertained for each genotype, given the varying radio-sensitivities exhibited by pollen donors.
