Survey dataset of self-reported gaming preferences, Bartle test responses, and immersive tendencies among 175 IT students and teachers

Objective

Player modelling in games has developed along several complementary methodological lines. Questionnaire-based motivation models examine why players report engaging with games, as illustrated by validated motivation scales and broader gaming-motive inventories [1, 2]. Player experience and immersion research focuses on how interactive systems are experienced, including the functional and psychosocial consequences of play and individual differences in immersion-related tendencies [3, 4]. A third line of work infers player states, engagement patterns, or profiles from behavioural telemetry, gameplay logs, and in-game event data [5, 6]. These approaches capture different evidential layers of player characterisation: motivation questionnaires describe declared psychological orientations, immersion instruments capture absorption, involvement and attention-focusing tendencies, and telemetry-based models provide evidence of observed interaction.

Existing questionnaire resources show that player motivation and player experience are often measured with distinct instrument families. Motivation-oriented work includes Bartle’s typological model [7], Yee’s Online Gaming Motivation Scale [1], the Gaming Motivation Inventory [2], the Videogaming Motives Questionnaire [8], and the Motivation to Play Scale [9]. Experience- and satisfaction-oriented instruments include the Player Experience Inventory [3] and GUESS-18 [10], while questionnaire repositories such as the PX Questionnaires Repository [11] document a broader range of instruments used in games user research. Open datasets are also available for specific questionnaire-based purposes, such as the PXI validation dataset [12], and datasets linking gaming motivation with gaming-disorder indicators [13].

The present dataset complements these resources by linking demographic variables, self-reported weekly gaming time, preferred play mode, genre preferences, Bartle Test of Gamer Psychology responses [14], and ITQ-18 items rated on a seven-point scale [15] in a single item-level table. The item-level structure supports alternative scoring schemes, aggregate and item-level analyses, measurement-oriented work, and exploratory clustering or profiling. It also enables analyses of how Bartle-derived motivational profiles relate to ITQ-18 responses, self-reported weekly playtime, preferred play mode, and genre preferences. The dataset therefore offers reuse opportunities across several areas. In game studies, it can support analyses of how declared player motivations relate to immersive tendencies and self-reported gaming preferences. In educational technology and immersive learning research, it can help characterise IT-oriented learners before the design or evaluation of game-based and interactive learning activities. In recommender-system and adaptive-interface research, its variables can serve as questionnaire-based user features for exploratory studies of preference-aware grouping, content recommendation, or interface personalisation.

Data description

The target sample consisted of students enrolled in information technology programs and computer science educators. The dataset comprises a single CSV file (one row per participant) with n = 175 and 64 variables capturing three blocks:

Demographics and self-reported gaming preferences describe who the respondent is and how they report playing games: age, gender, total weekly gaming time, weekly gaming time excluding mobile devices, preferred play mode, binary-coded genre indicators, and the most preferred genre. This block supports comparisons between respondent groups and analyses of associations between declared gaming preferences, playtime estimates, motivational orientation, and immersive tendency.

The Bartle Test of Gamer Psychology (B1–B30) captures players’ motivational orientations using a two-alternative forced-choice format. Each item compares two types of players, and the response is recorded as a single capital letter: A (Achiever), S (Socializer), E (Explorer), or K (Killer). The pairs being compared vary across items, and exactly one option is selected for each item, so these fields contain no missing values. Overall, item-level choices indicate why players report engaging and the relative importance of different motivational traits [14].

The Immersive Tendencies Questionnaire (ITQ1–ITQ18) measures predisposition to immersion in games and media, encompassing concentration, absorption, and emotional reactivity. These items allow analysis of whether gamers who are more prone to immersion prefer different genres or play for more extended periods. The ITQ-18 is a widely used 18-item short form developed by the original authors of the instrument. Each statement is rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1–7); responses are numeric, do not contain free text, and have no missing values [15].

Data were collected via an online questionnaire administered to university students and IT teachers. Recruitment took place during university courses and workshops on game development and virtual environments. The instrument combined the three components described above and was administered across two time windows to mitigate fatigue; all items used closed-response formats (no free text). The survey was self-paced and implemented in the LMS Moodle (completed on personal computers and smartphones using standard web browsers). The form allowed participants to confirm or decline the use of their responses for research at any time; five opt-outs were recorded in individual sections of the questionnaire, and these cases were excluded from the data set. All retained records were subsequently anonymized.

Responses were exported to CSV (UTF-8) and underwent basic validation: range checks for Likert items (1–7), domain checks for forced-choice options (A/S/E/K), and 0/1 validation for binary indicators. Records with incomplete mandatory blocks were excluded before export, resulting in a complete rectangular dataset with no missing cells. Categorical values were harmonised, and variable names standardised.

Appendix A contains the full text of our original questionnaire items for demographics and self-reported gaming preferences and playtime. For the Bartle Test of Gamer Psychology [14] (implemented by Andreasen & Downey [16]) and the Immersive Tendencies Questionnaire [15] (Witmer and Singer), we do not reproduce the verbatim item texts due to unclear licensing; therefore, we provide only non-verbatim content labels aligned with each item. Readers may consult the cited sources for the original wordings.

The file survey_item_labels_nonverbatim_bartle_itq18, mirrors the Appendix A content. Limitations

The dataset was collected from 175 participants at a single institution, specifically IT students and computer science teachers recruited during classes and workshops. This sampling frame makes the dataset most appropriate for analyses of comparable academic, IT-oriented, or technology-oriented cohorts. The sample is likely to differ from the broader gaming population in technical background, familiarity with digital systems, exposure to game-development or virtual-environment topics, gaming habits, and possibly motivation–immersion profiles. Consequently, the distributions observed in this dataset should be interpreted as cohort-specific characteristics rather than prevalence estimates for the general population of gamers.

The dataset is derived from a self-administered questionnaire and therefore reflects subjectively reported rather than observed behaviour. Estimates of weekly gaming time and genre preferences may be affected by recall bias, social desirability bias, and general measurement error. Because objective behavioural records were not collected, independent verification of respondent reports through gameplay logs, telemetry, or other criterion measures is not possible.

Genre variables are recorded as binary indicators with a single “most-loved” selection. The dataset includes no per-genre intensity or frequency measures, no detailed platform information beyond the mobile versus non-mobile split, and no session-level data. The play_* fields should therefore be interpreted as stated preference indicators, not as evidence of actual play frequency or observed in-game behaviour.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study collected anonymous questionnaire data from voluntary participants. Participants were informed at the outset about the study purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, and the anonymization of their responses. Written consent was waived, but electronic consent with an opt-out option was implemented in accordance with ethical guidelines. Each questionnaire contained a mandatory question about consent to the scientific processing of the participant’s responses (Yes/No). Only responses with an explicit Yes were included in the data set. If No was selected, all responses from that participant were excluded from the research data set. No personally identifiable information was collected or retained. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Constantine the Philosopher in Nitra (approval number UKF/225/2024/191013:002). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.
