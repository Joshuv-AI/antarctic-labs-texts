Healthcare provider perceptions of a pediatric fruit and vegetable prescription program: a qualitative study
Authors: Amy Saxe-Custack, Bridget Farmer, Sarah Egan, Kimberly Pulka, Brittany Tayler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExposure to pediatric fruit and vegetable prescription programs (FVPP) is associated with improvements in food security, dietary intake, and clinical markers of health in youth. However, an investigation of experiences with these programs from physicians and staff responsible for implementation is missing. This study was a qualitative investigation of provider and staff experiences with a pediatric FVPP that provided one $15 produce prescription to patients during pediatric visits.MethodsResearchers conducted focus groups across four primary care clinics that offered the pediatric FVPP for at least four years. Focus group recordings were transcribed for textual analysis. Using thematic analysis, researchers examined qualitative data to identify patterns across transcripts and formulate emerging themes. Researchers concluded when data saturation was reached.ResultsEight focus groups across four clinics were conducted with a total of 40 participants (n = 20 healthcare providers, n = 20 staff). The following recurrent themes emerged: (i) reliance on community-based organization; (ii) operationalizing the FVPP; (iii) clinic benefits; (iv) program challenges; and (v) program suggestions.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that providers and staff who implemented the current pediatric FVPP perceived the program to have numerous benefits, including improved no-show rates. Findings inform national efforts to establish standards that guide development, implementation, and expansion of produce prescription programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01317-0.Keywords: fruits and vegetables, nutrition incentives, produce prescription, pediatrics, children, nutrition, qualitative

Background

Childhood represents a critical period of growth when individuals develop lifelong dietary habits. [1–3] Given the numerous and widespread health-promoting effects of fruits and vegetables, [4–10] recommendations have been made for enhanced public policies designed to promote dietary interventions that increase fruit and vegetable intake. [11] Still, most children and adolescents living in the United States (US) fall short of meeting dietary recommendations for nutrient-rich fruits and vegetables. [11–13] Recently, fruit and vegetable prescription programs (FVPPs), which are frequently used as a treatment-focused strategy for adults with diet-related chronic health conditions, have broadened their focus to include pediatric patients. These pediatric programs are designed to remove barriers to accessing fresh produce while encouraging children and adolescents to develop dietary patterns that promote health and prevent disease. [14–18] Although pediatric FVPPs vary in design and procedures, prescriptions are typically ordered by primary care providers, distributed to patients during office visits, and exchanged for fresh produce at local retailers, such as farmers’ markets, mobile markets, and grocery or food stores.

Research has suggested that exposure to pediatric FVPPs is associated with improvements in food security, [15, 19] food shopping, [20] dietary intake, [14–18] physical activity [16] and clinical markers of health in youth. [16] Qualitative research has further described motivators and barriers to engagement in pediatric FVPPs from the perspective of participating families. [21–23] Several studies have examined clinic experiences with treatment-focused FVPPs for adults with chronic health conditions, noting that FVPPs prompt conversations about nutrition and provide a tangible resource to support healthy food purchasing; [24–28] however, screening patients for eligibility and administering the program can be labor-intensive and upsetting to clinic workflow. [25, 26, 29–33] Despite growing interest in pediatric FVPPs, there is a lack of research exploring clinical staff and provider experiences with and perceptions of prevention-focused pediatric FVPPs. As the leading source of health information for patients, [34–37] primary care physicians have the opportunity to educate individuals about the importance of proper nutrition and the connection between food and health. [38, 39] Pediatric providers, who often follow children from infancy to young adulthood, are uniquely positioned to influence dietary behaviors that are likely to have long-term health, growth, and developmental impacts. [1–3, 40] As a result, understanding pediatric provider and clinic experiences with pediatric FVPPs is essential to understanding whether these programs are useful when educating pediatric patients about nutrition; feasible within busy clinical settings; and scalable to other primary care environments.

In February 2016, a large university-affiliated pediatric and resident clinic in a low-income, urban area partnered with researchers and a local farmers’ market to create Michigan’s first FVPP entirely for children. [20, 21] The program included provider-issued prescriptions for fresh produce that were distributed during all office visits. Prescriptions were redeemable for fresh fruits and vegetables at either the downtown farmers’ market or a local mobile market that offered produce box delivery throughout the city. Caregivers whose children participated in this year-round FVPP viewed it as effective in improving child dietary patterns and household food security. [21] The identical program was expanded to two pediatric clinics and one family practice clinic in subsequent years with research demonstrating positive impacts on diet, food security, and clinical markers of health in pediatric patients. [14–16] The purpose of the current study was to qualitatively explore healthcare provider and clinic staff experiences with and perceptions of this prevention-focused pediatric FVPP.

Methods

Study setting

Providers and clinic staff who participated in the current study care for pediatric patients in Flint, Michigan. Approximately 60% of children that live in Flint experience poverty, and few full-service grocery stores operate within city limits. [41] Access to essential resources and nutritious food options is limited in Flint. As a result, many children experience the double-burden of insufficient intake of nutrient-dense foods, such as fresh fruits and vegetables, while consumption of ultraprocessed and calorie-dense foods is high. [42] Local stores in Flint tend to offer lower-quality foods and fewer healthy food options than economically advantaged areas nearby. [21, 43–45].

Four clinics in Flint, each serving comparable patient populations, offer the pediatric FVPP. Clinic 1, a university-affiliated pediatric residency training clinic co-located with the Flint Farmers’ Market, introduced the first pediatric FVPP in February 2016. Clinic 1 distributes approximately 4,000 fruit and vegetable prescriptions annually. Clinic 2, one of Flint’s largest private-practice clinics, introduced the pediatric FVPP in August 2018. Clinic 2 is located approximately five miles from Flint Farmers’ Market and distributes nearly 6,000 prescriptions per year. Clinic 3 is a combined internal medicine and pediatrics residency training clinic that began offering the pediatric FVPP in May 2019. Clinic 3 distributes approximately 500 prescriptions annually. Clinic 4, the largest pediatric clinic in Flint, and surrounding Genesee County, introduced the pediatric FVPP in March 2021. Clinic 4 distributes nearly 8,000 prescriptions per year.

Pediatric Fruit and Vegetable Prescription Program (FVPP)

The pediatric FVPP was developed by researchers in concert with pediatricians and agricultural leaders in Flint. To ensure ease of implementation within busy clinical settings as well as consistent messaging to all families, the team created a streamlined process in which every child, regardless of income or health status, is provided a prescription for fresh fruits and vegetables. Fruit and vegetable prescriptions are ordered by a healthcare provider through the electronic medical record (EMR) system, printed on prescription paper, and given to patients during office visits. Prescriptions are valid for 90 days and treated as gift cards or vouchers that can only be exchanged for $15 of fresh fruits and vegetables at Flint Farmers’ Market or for delivery through Flint Fresh. The Flint Farmers’ Market is a year-round market that is open Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday with approximately 50 vendors inside and outside of the market. Flint Fresh is a mobile market and food aggregation space for local farmers that offers free delivery of participant-selected fresh produce boxes. [46].

Researchers at a partnering community-based organization (CBO) worked with pediatricians at Clinic 1 to develop the pediatric FVPP. Since 2016, this CBO has provided centralized oversight and management of the FVPP and led program expansion efforts in Flint. The CBO is responsible for training and educating clinic staff, tracking prescription distribution and redemption rates, and evaluating the program. Healthcare providers and staff at partnering clinics distribute fruit and vegetable prescriptions, provide education to patients regarding the importance of fruits and vegetables, and share regular updates and feedback with the CBO.

Approach and theoretical framework

The study design was informed by Sekhon and colleagues’ Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA), which provides a comprehensive framework to understand factors that influence the acceptability of healthcare interventions. [47] In the current study, TFA was employed to assess how those administering the pediatric FVPP perceived its appropriateness. [47] Researchers developed the focus group guide, which was grounded in the seven TFA constructs (affective attitude, intervention coherence, burden, self-efficacy, opportunity costs, ethicality, perceived effectiveness), in an effort to explore provider and staff perceptions of the acceptability of the pediatric FVPP (Table 1, Additional file 1).

Recruitment and data collection

Researchers trained in qualitative methods facilitated eight focus groups from February through April 2025. Discussions were held in a closed meeting space on site at each clinic with one moderator and one notetaker. Moderators and note takers were assigned to clinics where they had no existing professional relationship with participants to reduce potential bias. Focus groups were organized by clinic and included an average of 5 participants per group (range 2–11 participants), lasting on average 32 min (range 19–45 min). Approximately 57 healthcare providers and clinic staff across four partnering clinics were invited to participate via email and paper flyers. The number of focus groups at each clinic was determined by clinic size, with a goal of 5–10 participants per discussion, but modified as needed based on study participation and attendance. At larger clinics, groups were separated by role (providers vs. clinic staff) to encourage candid discussions. Participants were not provided with monetary compensation for their participation, but lunch was offered during focus group sessions. Prior to each discussion, all participants provided written informed consent and completed a brief demographic questionnaire. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Michigan State University Institutional Review Board – Study 00011461.

Data analysis

Focus groups were video- and audio-recorded. Video recording was used to clearly identify speakers, and audio recordings were transcribed verbatim for textual data analysis. Using a multistep coding process guided by Braun and Clarke’s Thematic Analysis, [48] researchers examined data to uncover and analyze patterns across transcripts, formulating illustrative themes. First, four researchers performed inductive coding by hand of the complete dataset, independently highlighting, labeling, and interpreting data for thematic purposes. Next, they met to discuss the themes, eliminate less reflective themes, and collapse similar themes. Finally, three researchers selected explanatory quotations to support the final themes and subthemes.

Results

A total of 40 healthcare providers and staff representing four clinics and all roles interacting with the program participated in focus group discussions (Table 2). The majority were female (77.5%) and White (55%). Focus group participants included 20 (50%) FVPP prescribers (physicians, pediatricians, physician assistants, pediatric nurse practitioners, and resident physicians), 9 (22.5%) medical assistants, 8 (20%) other clinic personnel (front desk staff, clinic/office managers, insurance specialists, clinic clerical assistants, and client services representatives), 2 (5%) registered nurses, and 1 (2.5%) registered dietitian. Average length of time implementing the pediatric FVPP among focus group participants was 3.5 years (range 0.3–9.1 years).

If they come for another visit, and we have that old prescription, we give it.” -Participant 028, FVPP Prescriber (Physician)

“Limited availability of the Farmers’ Market, Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday. If they are working or their kids are in school and they don’t have transportation, it might be hard to get there on those limited days.” -Participant 011, Registered Dietitian

“I don’t know how convenient it is for our patients [to redeem their prescriptions]. They ride the bus. They’ve got four kids that they’ve got a haul on the bus and come downtown.” -Participant 022, Medical Assistant

“Eating more vegetables and eating healthier, for people, they need to cook at home…. We need to focus on how to use the vegetables, how to cook them, and how they have to use this prescription to help them.” -Participant 004, FVPP Prescriber (Physician)

“Allowing [patients] to use the vouchers at other locations other than just the farmers’ market. In talking to the patients that I see, a lot of them do shop at Kroger and Meijer and Sam’s. So, one or all of those places.” -Participant 011, Registered Dietitian

“Walmart would be good. A lot of our families do shop at Walmart.” -Participant 015, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatrician)

“[Patients would] probably use [the prescriptions] more if they just had to go to the store and use it.” -Participant 023, Other Clinic Personnel (Clinic Clerical Assistant)

“Those [prescription] sheets can be lost very easily. You can drop it, and you leave it somewhere. You forget about it. So, at least if it’s on the phone, they can just take their phone to the market and use the coupon.” -Participant 036, FVPP Prescriber (Resident Physician)

“Make [the prescription program] electronic. If [patients] could go into the patient portal, then they could access it that way or something. I think that would be beneficial for us because then they would have or want to use their patient portal. And then they wouldn’t be losing it because it’s accessible from their phone.” -Participant 014, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatric Nurse Practitioner)

“We have [some] patients who are really on top of it with their phones, and they say they would love it if they don’t have another piece of paper to take home.” -Participant 028, FVPP Prescriber (Physician)

“I don’t think [the delivery option is] well-educated. That would be something where it could be improved. I think it’d be improving the discussion of the delivery process.” -Participant 025, FVPP Prescriber (Physician)

“Delivery option, not everyone knows. We always say that this is a coupon for the farmers’ market, so they assume they have to go there.” -Participant 028, FVPP Prescriber (Physician)

“With a lot of our patients, grandma may have [the kids], and times have changed. So, a lot of times, they don’t really know how to place [orders] online.” -Participant 008, Other Clinic Personnel (Insurance Specialist)

Reliance on Community-Based Organization (CBO)

Healthcare providers and clinic staff at each of the clinics appreciated the program support and management provided by the CBO; however, most lacked a feeling of ownership for the FVPP. Some indicated that they expected the CBO to develop plans for their clinics and educate patients whenever changes to the program arose; while others relied on the CBO for guidance with program-related issues (Table 3, subtheme 1.1).

“It’s the program that’s in charge of this, we just distribute. Patients get it for coming to our clinic.” -Participant 012, FVPP Prescriber (Physician).

The CBO, which secures funding for the FVPP, is responsible for program evaluation. Many providers and staff felt there was a lack of communication between the CBO and clinics regarding program implications and evaluation results (Table 3, subtheme 1.2). There was a clear desire across clinics to receive regular updates from the CBO regarding patient engagement in the FVPP as well as health outcomes related to program participation.

“We prescribe it. I don’t know whether the family uses it or not. Getting some feedback once in a while like, ‘You sent this many [prescriptions], and this many were redeemed,’ would be rewarding and nice and positive reinforcement.” -Participant 025, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatrician).

Operationalizing the FVPP

There was overwhelming support for the program alongside a general feeling that the pediatric FVPP was simple to execute within busy primary care offices. Most shared that the program was seamless, and implementation was easy and straightforward (Table 3, subtheme 2.1). Some further indicated that prescribing fruits and vegetables had become standard within clinic workflow.

“It’s smooth and seamless. The medical assistants know their role. The patients know their role, and it just kind of flows smooth.” -Participant 015, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatrician).

“It’s just part of our regular workflow. It feels like we’re not even thinking about it when we prescribe [fruit and vegetables].” -Participant 034, FVPP Prescriber (Resident Physician).

Many noted that clinic staff and providers learned how to implement the FVPP as a team and created their own systems to effectively facilitate the program (Table 3, subtheme 2.2). This included specific procedures and designated roles to ensure that all patients received prescriptions and understood the program. Providers from residency training clinics spoke specifically about standardized training regarding the pediatric FVPP.

“Since I work with the residents, the residents put in the [prescription] order. They might do the orders before the visit, or they might do the orders after the visit. I make sure when the visit is over, and I look at the chart, that the voucher has been prescribed. If not, the front desk staff will come back and let me know that it wasn’t ordered, and then I will prescribe it myself to ensure that the family gets it.” -Participant 025, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatrician).

Since each of the participating clinics has offered the FVPP to patients for many years, focus group participants shared that patients and families had become accustomed to the program. Many noted that patients and families often engaged in courageous conversations to remind providers and staff that they should receive a fruit and vegetable prescription during pediatric office visits (Table 3, subtheme 2.3). Most felt that parents and children had come to expect a prescription at every office visit and would ask for a prescription if one was not given.

“If they are an established patient, they already know [about the prescription program]. If we forgot to give them [the prescription], they say, ‘Where’s my prescription?’.” -Participant 001, Other Clinic Personnel (Office Manager).

Clinic benefits

Clinic staff and providers described benefits of the FVPP at each of their respective clinics. Most felt that the FVPP acted as an incentive for patients to come to their appointments and reduced visit no-show rates (Table 3, subtheme 3.1). Others felt that the program had an additional benefit of decreasing utilization of urgent and emergency room services. Some further noted that the pediatric FVPP acted as an incentive for new patients and providers to choose their clinic.

“I think [patients are] more likely to attend [appointments] because it’s an incentive for them to come to an appointment. So, I think it is something that our patients look forward to and getting them in the door is sometimes difficult.” -Participant 014, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatric Nurse Practitioner).

“Patients are less likely to go to the urgent care or the ED and more likely to come to their medical home.” -Participant 027, FVPP Prescriber (Physician).

Although the FVPP does not contain a standardized nutrition education component, clinic staff and providers indicated that the program supported nutrition education and served as a reminder to discuss healthy eating with children and families (Table 3, subtheme 3.2).

“It’s creating that awareness that you can eat healthy. With so many grocery stores closing in the area and Flint being almost a food desert, this [program] has been a welcome change.” -Participant 027, FVPP Prescriber (Physician).

A pediatrician’s job is not just taking care of sick patients, but educating patients with information about their lifestyle, eating habits, nutrition… When we give the fruit and vegetable prescription to the patients, it’s definitely a tremendous help to our education. -Participant 004, FVPP Prescriber (Physician).

In addition to providing opportunities to discuss nutrition and health with pediatric patients and their families, many mentioned that the program served as a tangible resource to address food and nutrition insecurity. Some also mentioned that the prescriptions were especially important for struggling patients and families who do not disclose food insecurity (Table 3, subtheme 3.3).

“One mom was so grateful because she forgot that we even had the program here, and she came in for an appointment. She needed food, and that [prescription] made her day. Knowing that she got $15 to go pick up some fruit and veggies for her kiddo.” -Participant 006, Medical Assistant.

Many talked about how the program facilitated bonding with young patients who might be nervous before their appointments. Others recalled conversations between patients and their parents after receiving the prescriptions as they planned to take a trip to the local farmers market together to redeem their fruit and vegetable prescription (Table 3, subtheme 3.4).

“When [families] leave [the clinic], they’ll be like, ‘We’ll go down [to the farmers’ market, ] and you can pick out whatever fruit you want.’ And the kids get excited about it.” -Participant 023, Other Clinic Personnel (Clinic Clerical Assistant).

“Some patients are like, ‘Alright, after this, sweetie… After you get your shots, we’re going to go to the farmers’ market and get some fruits.’ So, I think it creates a good bonding experience for the parent and kids.” -Participant 012, FVPP Prescriber (Physician).

Program challenges

Despite positive feedback regarding the pediatric FVPP, clinic staff and providers noted several programmatic challenges and concerns. The only requirement to receive a fruit and vegetable prescription at participating clinics is attending an office visit with the patient’s healthcare provider. Some discussed how they suspect families request appointments for the expressed purpose of receiving fruit and vegetable prescriptions. This was viewed as a concern because the appointments were considered unnecessary (Table 3, subtheme 4.1).

“We notice that some of our larger families, toward the end of the month, will utilize sick call appointments to come in and get fruit and vegetable prescriptions. So, they can go downstairs and get more fruit and vegetables to last them until the beginning of the next month.” -Participant 019, Medical Assistant.

On the other hand, some clinic staff and providers felt the program was underutilized by patients and families. The current FVPP instructs providers to order prescriptions for all patients during office visits. There is no patient requirement to enroll in the program. Some clinic staff and providers mentioned that parents sometimes leave the clinic without taking their child’s prescription or tell front desk staff that they do not need the prescription. Several also mentioned lost, forgotten, or expired prescriptions as enduring reasons for underutilization (Table 3, subtheme 4.2).

“I feel like we have people that don’t necessarily utilize [the prescriptions]. But there’s never been negative [feedback], and I think the people that do utilize it have a lot of positive feedback.” -013, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatric Nurse Practitioner).

“Maybe before the expiration date, a notification might be sent to the parent on their phone, just to make them aware that it might expire. Because I’m a mom, I just recently became a mom. I got a prescription twice and I lost it both times, and I keep forgetting about it. So, I think this would be helpful in increasing their rate of consumption.” -Participant 038, FVPP Prescriber (Resident Physician).

Some shared specific challenges with printing prescriptions after ordering. Many discussed problems with the prescription printer or running out of prescription paper; while others shared how printing delays sometimes caused patients to leave clinics before receiving a prescription. Several further shared how they modified internal clinic operations to document and address printing challenges (Table 3, subtheme 4.3).

“My biggest challenge is when the printer doesn’t print. It just sets a domino effect, and then you may forget like, ‘Oh, we were waiting for the printer, now [the patient is] gone.’ So, you feel like, ‘Oh, they got cheated out of their prescription.’.” -Participant 010, Medical Assistant.

Many noted perceived patient and family barriers to program engagement that they felt hindered their utilization of the FVPP. Although culinary education and familiarity with food preparation was mentioned, most focused on challenges related to farmers’ market hours or limited transportation (Table 3, subtheme 4.4).

“The fact that the Farmers’ Market is only open Tuesdays and Thursdays. So, when [patients] come [to the clinic on] Monday, Wednesday, or Friday, they’d have to make a separate trip to come back and get [fruits and vegetables], which isn’t always feasible or easy.” -Participant 025, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatrician).

“[Patients] don’t have transportation.” -Participant 013, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatric Nurse Practitioner).

Program suggestions

Focus group participants shared suggestions to improve the program. It is important to note that all participants expressed strong support for the FVPP and felt it was easy to implement within their respective clinics. As a result, suggestions for program improvements focused largely on improving fruit and vegetable prescription redemption rates. Most focus group participants expressed a strong desire to expand prescription redemption sites to include full-service grocery stores (Table 3, subtheme 5.1). Some discussed the lower cost of fresh produce at grocery stores compared to farmers’ markets; while others felt that it would be easier on patients and families if they could use their prescriptions at locations where they regularly shop for groceries.

“If there was an option of using the prescriptions in markets, like in Meijer [grocery stores], Walmart. Because they’re reasonable, their prices are actually cheap. It’s much, much better than the farmer’s market. For example, with $10 at the farmer’s market, they will only get some tomatoes. But there [grocery stores] they can get a lot of stuff, a lot of vegetables.” -Participant 040, FVPP Prescriber (Resident Physician).

“It would help if [patients] could [redeem fruit and vegetable prescriptions] more through a retail grocery store or something. In addition to the farmers’ market.” -Participant 009, Other Clinic Personnel (Client Services Representative).

The current FVPP provides paper prescriptions for $15 that families must provide to vendors as payment for fresh fruits and vegetables. Many providers and staff shared that engagement in the pediatric FVPP would likely improve if the prescriptions were offered on a digital platform instead (Table 3, subtheme 5.2).

“A suggestion to make things easier instead of [the prescription] being printed, maybe it can be sent to their phones or something just so you can have it digitally even if you forget.” -Participant 032, FVPP Prescriber (Resident Physician).

“An app [for the prescriptions] would be good if [patients] could just store and scan for it or something. And then they could take it right to the vendor.” -Participant 015, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatrician).

Some providers and staff felt that redemption rates may increase if patients and families were more aware of the produce box delivery option. Many indicated that they made a special effort to explain the delivery option to their patients because of persistent challenges with transportation. However, some indicated that education regarding the delivery option could be improved. Most felt that patients were largely unfamiliar with Flint Fresh, the local food hub that accepts fruit and vegetable prescriptions and offers free delivery service. (Table 3, subtheme 5.3).

“Even though our medical assistants often go over with [patients] about [how] they can call and have [a produce box] delivered, I don’t know if all of them understand that you don’t always have to go to the farmers’ market. It can be delivered to your home.” -Participant 015, FVPP Prescriber (Pediatrician).

Discussion

To our knowledge, the current study is the first to explore provider and clinic staff perceptions of a prevention-focused pediatric FVPP. This program model provides prescriptions for fresh fruits and vegetables to every patient at every office visit, regardless of income or health status. Central to study findings were consistently positive views of the FVPP, with mention of its ease of implementation and usefulness in facilitating nutrition education. This is in contrast with previous research regarding treatment-focused fruit and vegetable prescription programs tailored for adult patients. Fruit and vegetable prescription programs for adults are generally used as a treatment strategy for patients with diet-related conditions and/or for those who screen positively for food insecurity. [25, 30, 49] Although clinic staff tend to be enthusiastic about potential benefits of fruit and vegetable prescription programs for adults, [24–27, 31, 32] screening patients to determine program eligibility was viewed as burdensome, time-consuming, and disruptive to clinic workflow. [25, 26, 29–33] The current FVPP, which was developed in close collaboration with pediatricians at a large clinic in Flint, Michigan, was designed specifically for ease of implementation within busy clinical settings. The program does not require screening of patients, nor does it involve a standardized nutrition education component upon prescription distribution. Providers and staff seemed to appreciate the seamless integration of the current pediatric FVPP into regular clinic workflow as well as its inherent support of nutrition education and healthy eating discussions with patients. As state and national agencies seek to set standards to guide the development, implementation, and expansion of fruit and vegetable prescription programs, there is a growing need to carefully assess their feasibility within various clinical settings. It is imperative to consider how healthcare providers and their staff integrate fruit and vegetable prescription programs into their regular clinic workflow and whether this integration is perceived as burdensome or as beneficial to patient care.

Focus group participants also discussed the important role the pediatric FVPP played in addressing food and nutrition security among their patients. In addition to providing general nutrition guidance, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that physicians screen all households with children for food insecurity. [50] However, some providers do not screen for food insecurity, and those who do are often unable to address the underlying problem, citing a lack of resources to support families who screen positive. [51, 52] Previous research has explored caregiver perceptions of the current FVPP and indicated that caregivers similarly perceived the program to be helpful in improving both child dietary patterns and household food security. [21] Existing research has confirmed that produce prescription programs designed for youth are an effective tool to address food insecurity among children and their families. [15, 19] In the current study, many focus group participants perceived that the provision of prescriptions for fresh produce to all children during pediatric office visits combatted food and nutrition insecurity and was particularly beneficial to families who declined to disclose financial struggles.

Some participants noted that families may unnecessarily increase healthcare utilization simply to receive prescriptions. Unlike most produce prescription programs, the current FVPP provides $15 prescriptions to all children, regardless of income or health status, at every office visit. This model may unintentionally cause some families to rely on the FVPP when food dollars in the home are scarce. The current FVPP is intentionally designed to send a ubiquitous message to all pediatric patients regarding the importance of healthy eating while providing a tangible resource that illustrates physicians’ commitment to assisting youth and families in their efforts to improve dietary habits. However, it is not intended to act as a food assistance program, and additional resources are necessary for families experiencing food insecurity.

While clinic providers and staff tailored program implementation practices to fit their unique clinic needs, most described a strong reliance on the partnering CBO for logistics beyond clinic capacity as well as for evaluation efforts. Previous research on treatment-focused fruit and vegetable prescription programs has suggested that limited staff capacity at these clinics is often a deterrent to enrolling new patients, particularly when eligibility screening is required. [26, 33, 53] Similar to the current pediatric FVPP, many clinics serving adult populations have engaged not only physicians in program implementation, but also clinic staff, including front desk staff, clinic managers, medical assistants, nurses, dietitians, social workers, patient care managers, pharmacists, and medical students. [24–26, 30, 53] Other clinics have relied on one or more program managers to facilitate FVPPs to minimize the administrative burden placed on clinic staff. [24] Unfortunately, most programs cannot afford to hire on-site program managers, especially when funding is limited and difficult to sustain. [24, 33, 53] The design of the pediatric FVPP explored in the current study places the burden of initial clinic staff training, program evaluation, and funding on the partnering CBO, while program implementation and patient education fall directly on the healthcare providers and their staff. Participants in the current study shared unanimous and positive feedback regarding this program design with a consistent desire across clinics to receive more information regarding program engagement and impacts. This response indicates the critical importance of collaboration between clinics and the CBO to successfully facilitate the current FVPP.

Clinic staff and providers credited the FVPP with improving no-show rates and reducing the use of urgent and emergency room care. Because patient no-show rates are an enduring issue affecting resource utilization and posing risks to the quality of healthcare, [54] the perceived impact of the pediatric FVPP on reducing the number of no-show appointments is noteworthy, particularly among pediatric patients. Given the importance of preventive care and screening among pediatric patients as well critical assessment of growth and development that occurs during well-child visits, [55] the perceived value of the pediatric FVPP in increasing attendance at these appointments is meaningful. Moreover, many focus group participants indicated that their patients tended to use urgent care and emergency room services less frequently since the FVPP was introduced, suggesting that the FVPP may have impacts on health care costs associated with reduced emergency room utilization.

In spite of sharing their overall satisfaction with the FVPP, providers and staff were forthcoming about program-related challenges and offered suggestions for improvement. A recurring issue involved struggles with printing the paper prescriptions and a desire to address this problem by moving the FVPP to a digital platform. This finding is consistent with caregiver feedback that indicated a strong desire for digital prescriptions that would allow families to keep prescriptions for multiple children organized in one place. [22] Other challenges included both under- and over-utilization of the program as well as a desire to partner with grocery stores to improve redemption rates. These issues have similarly been raised by caregivers of children who received fruit and vegetable prescriptions in Flint [22] with efforts currently underway to partner with grocery stores to actively address underutilization of the FVPP. Structural barriers, including limited transportation access and farmers’ market hours have been reported in previous studies examining nutrition incentive programs. [56–58] The CBO and clinics continue to work together to address these structural barriers to program engagement through community partnerships and program expansion to include grocery store vendors.

Limitations of the current study should be noted. The study was small and limited to one geographic area. As such, results may not be generalizable to a broader population. This research focuses on one unique fruit and vegetable prescription program model offering universal prescriptions; therefore, findings may not be generalizable to all produce prescription program models. There may have been selection bias as feedback from those who chose not to participate in focus groups may have differed from those who did participate. Although participants were unfamiliar with moderators and notetakers, they may not have been completely candid regarding their experiences with the program as focus groups were organized by the CBO overseeing the program. However, participants were forthright with challenges in implementation and offered suggestions to improve program reach and engagement among patients and families.

Conclusions

As evidence supporting fruit and vegetable prescription programs grows, qualitative research is needed to understand the feasibility and effectiveness of various program models. While previous research has identified challenges and successes of administering treatment-focused programs for adults, little is known about clinic experiences with prevention-focused programs, particularly those designed specifically for pediatric patients. The current study demonstrates that a partnership between a CBO and primary care clinics to offer a pediatric FVPP to all patients, regardless of health status or income, is easy to implement and perceived as having notable impacts on patients and families. Moreover, the program was viewed as encouraging families to attend their appointments while reducing emergency room use. Future research should explore the scalability of this FVPP beyond Michigan.
