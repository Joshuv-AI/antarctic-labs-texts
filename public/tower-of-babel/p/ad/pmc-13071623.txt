Management of liver metastases secondary to thymic epithelial tumors: a narrative review of current management and future directions
Authors: Alex J Ma, Erica S Alexander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveHepatic metastases secondary to thymic epithelial tumors (TETs), encompassing thymomas and thymic carcinomas (TCs), are a rare but clinically meaningful phenomenon and are associated with worse overall survival (OS). Management of liver metastases is largely limited to systemic therapy and chemotherapeutic regimens; although, there are smaller-scale studies in the literature reporting use of more novel treatment regimens and liver-directed therapies. The purpose of this article is to explore management options for TET hepatic metastases, including novel therapeutic options.MethodsA review of the available electronic literature was performed through October 10, 2025, using PubMed and Google Scholar. A single author identified the studies, and both authors reviewed the selection to determine which studies to include.Key Content and FindingsThe literature involving systemic therapy options was much more developed, as this is the first-line treatment option for TET hepatic metastases. Smaller series supporting the use of surgical resection, radiation therapy, and interventional radiology therapies suggest benefit in liver-directed resection and/or therapies.ConclusionsData evaluating therapeutic options for managing liver metastases secondary to TETs is limited. Given the prognostic implications of these metastatic lesions, a broader understanding of available therapies is essential for determining optimal management.Keywords: Hepatic metastases, thymoma, thymic carcinoma (TC)

Introduction

Thymic epithelial tumors (TETs) comprise a rare group of neoplasms originating from the epithelial cells in the thymus and include thymomas, thymic carcinomas (TCs), and thymic neuroendocrine tumors (NETTs) (1,2). Thymomas, the most common primary malignancy in the anterior mediastinum, account for 75–80% of TETs, while TCs and NETTs are much rarer at 15–20% and 2–5%, respectively (1,3). In the United States, the overall incidence is approximately 1.3–3.2 cases per million per year (4,5). Although all TETs possess malignant potential, their clinical course ranges from indolent to highly aggressive, making prognosis difficult to predict (1,6-8). The aggressive tendencies of NETTs and TCs make them much more likely to have metastasized to distal sites by the time of diagnosis, compared to thymomas (3,9).

Extrathoracic spread of TETs is observed in less than 10% of cases (10,11). The most frequent sites for extrathoracic metastasis secondary to thymoma are liver, bone, lymph nodes, and soft tissue (11-14). Due to the higher likelihood of metastasis, patients with NETTs and TCs often have a worse prognosis. The 9th edition tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) model classifies patients with metastatic TETs into two grade categories, IVa and IVb, based on the extent of regional lymph node involvement and distant metastasis (15). Although the survival impact of thymic metastases to the liver has not been well established, patients with IVa and IVb have worse long-term survival rates compared to earlier stages (16). In a study by Kondo et al., 5-year overall survival (OS) rates varied based on histological type and disease stage: 70.6% for Masaoka-Koga stage IVa thymoma, 52.8% IVb thymoma, and 37.6% for stage IV TC (16). Similarly, Cheng et al. reported a median OS of 15.9 months in patients with metastases to the liver versus 26.6 months in those without, consistent with prior findings that liver metastases are a significant negative prognosticator of OS (17-20).

Given the rarity of thymic metastases to the liver, no randomized clinical trials evaluating treatment have been conducted. This review aims to summarize the existing literature regarding the management of hepatic metastases secondary to thymomas and TCs, including standard treatment regimens and novel approaches. NETTs were not included in the treatment-focused sections of this review, as their clinical course, histologic characteristics, and therapeutic approaches diverge significantly from those of thymomas and TCs. While many patients with liver metastases secondary to TETs will have other sites of disease, this review focuses on methods for managing liver progressive, liver-dominant, or oligometastatic liver tumors. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://med.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/med-2025-1-50/rc).

Methods

A search in the PubMed and Google Scholar databases was performed for all studies published up to October 10, 2025 (Table 1). Search terms included: “Thymic epithelial neoplasm”, “Thymic epithelial tumor”, “Thymoma”, “Thymic carcinoma”, in combination with, “Metastasis”, “Liver”, “Hepatic”, “Extrathoracic”. All types of publications were included for consideration, including original full-length articles, meta-analyses, review articles, and case reports.

Search strategy summary

Utilizing these search terms, the initial search yielded 146 items; the titles and the abstracts were screened for relevance. All articles published in a non-English language without available translations were excluded.

Of note, thymomas and TCs were grouped in all but the systemic management section, due to the limited number of publications supporting the use of liver-directed therapies, radiation therapy, and surgical management. Given the absence of large-scale data, case reports and small case series were included to capture all available evidence.

Discussion

Systemic therapy options

For thymomas and TCs with evidence of extrathoracic metastases, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network® (NCCN®) recommends systemic therapy as the first-line treatment (21). Of note, these recommendations are inclusive of patients with liver metastases and/or the presence of other extrathoracic disease. In the current literature, systemic therapy in the setting of advanced and/or metastatic disease has been described in the following settings:

❖ Neoadjuvant therapy to improve tumor burden in patients with potentially resectable, advanced, metastatic, or recurrent disease, with plans of subsequent surgery ± radiotherapy (22,23);

❖ Neoadjuvant therapy to improve tumor burden in patients with potentially resectable, advanced, metastatic, or recurrent disease, with plans of subsequent surgery ± radiotherapy (22,23);

❖ Primary or exclusive therapy with no plan for subsequent surgery or radiotherapy for unresectable advanced, metastatic or recurrent disease. Patients may undergo several consecutive lines of therapy in the setting of recurrence or disease progression (23);

❖ Primary or exclusive therapy with no plan for subsequent surgery or radiotherapy for unresectable advanced, metastatic or recurrent disease. Patients may undergo several consecutive lines of therapy in the setting of recurrence or disease progression (23);

❖ Adjuvant therapy for incompletely resected disease (21,22);

❖ Adjuvant therapy for incompletely resected disease (21,22);

❖ Palliative-intent to improve symptoms and quality of life and potentially prolong survival (23,24).

❖ Palliative-intent to improve symptoms and quality of life and potentially prolong survival (23,24).

Clinical evidence supporting adjuvant chemotherapy remains limited and heterogeneous, and interpretation is complicated by the frequent use of postoperative radiotherapy (22). In a recent systematic review, adjuvant chemotherapy resulted in favorable OS for patients with TC but there was no statistically significant benefit in patients with thymoma. The review also reported no significant difference in OS among patients with thymomas and TCs who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy compared with those who did not (22). However, the authors note that this survival data may be biased, as patients selected to undergo neoadjuvant or postoperative chemotherapy often present with worse prognostic features. Future studies, ideally prospective and larger in scale, are needed to clarify the role of systemic therapy in both the neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings.

First and second line therapies for metastatic thymoma and TC, as defined by the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology (NCCN Guidelines®), are detailed in Figure 1 (21). Cisplatin/doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide (CAP) is the preferred chemotherapy for patients with advanced thymoma. In a clinical trial encompassing 29 patients with advanced or recurrent thymoma and 1 patient with TC, Loehrer et al. reported an overall response rate (ORR) of 50% (3 complete responses and 12 partial responses) and a median OS of 37.7 months (25). In a prospective study including the Réseau tumeurs THYMIques et Cancer (RYTHMIC) cohort database in France, the efficacy of different systemic modalities for treatments of TETs was analyzed. In the group treated exclusively with systemic therapy, the CAP regimen was associated with an improved objective response rate (44% in the CAP group versus 17% in other systemic therapy regimens). Additionally, on multivariate analysis, CAP was a significant predictor of prolonged time to progression versus other regimens (23).

Compared to thymomas, TCs are less chemo-sensitive (26). Carboplatin plus paclitaxel (with or without ramucirumab) is the preferred regimen for TCs. In a phase II multicenter study from Lemma et al. that enrolled 23 patients with unresectable TCs treated with carboplatin plus paclitaxel, there were 5 partial responses. They reported a median survival time of 20 months and a progression-free survival (PFS) of 5 months (27). A separate phase II clinical trial by Hirai et al. included 39 patients with advanced TC also treated with carboplatin and paclitaxel. There were 1 complete and 13 partial responses; the median PFS was 7.5 months and the reported ORR was 36% (28).

Recent phase II trials conducted by Proto et al. and Tsao et al. have evaluated the efficacy of administering ramucirumab, a human monoclonal antibody that inhibits vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR-2), along with carboplatin and paclitaxel (29,30). These studies suggest that this triplet regimen may offer comparable or superior activity when compared to carboplatin plus paclitaxel alone. In the RELEVENT single-arm trial, Proto and colleagues assessed ramucirumab/carboplatin/paclitaxel (RCP) in 35 previously untreated patients, reporting an ORR of 80%, a median PFS of 18.1 months, and a manageable safety profile (29). The S1701 randomized trial by Tsao et al. included 8 patients treated with RCP, demonstrating an ORR of 88% versus 40% in the carboplatin-paclitaxel arm, with a median PFS of 8 months versus 7 months for the carboplatin-paclitaxel arm (30). Several factors that may account for the discrepancy in PFS between the two studies include the enrollment of strictly treatment-naïve patients in the RELEVENT trial versus the heterogeneous S1701 patient population, differences in median age (60.8 versus 68.2 years), and longer median follow-up (31.6 versus 16.7 months). As noted by the authors, the lower PFS observed in S1701 relative to the RELEVENT trial may also reflect the limited sample sizes in both studies, highlighting the challenges inherent to conducting large clinical studies in such a rare malignancy. In both studies, the disease control rate, defined as the percentage of patients who reported stable disease, complete response, or partial response, was 100%. Although survival data for RCP remain immature, these findings highlight its promising activity as a first-line option for TC. It is important to note that both studies excluded patients with untreated brain metastases or with significant contraindications to antiangiogenics, such as recent thromboembolic events, limiting the generalizability of the findings (21,29,30). While these results are encouraging, future studies should aim to evaluate this triplet regimen, or related combinations, in larger, multicenter trials to better establish its therapeutic potential.

Cisplatin-containing and anthracycline-based regimens, such as doxorubicin/cisplatin/vincristine/cyclophosphamide (ADOC) and CAP, have shown improved outcomes compared to carboplatin-based therapies in treating TETs (31-33). In a pooled analysis conducted by Okuma et al., which included 206 patients with advanced TC from 10 studies, cisplatin-based chemotherapy achieved a significantly improved ORR of 53.6% versus 32.8% for carboplatin-based chemotherapy (31). While this study demonstrated superior response rates from cisplatin-based therapies, it did not note the safety profile for each regimen. Cisplatin-containing and anthracycline-based regimens have been associated with increased risk of hematological adverse events and cardiotoxicity (32). As a result, their use is generally contraindicated in patients with cardiac comorbidities (33). Formal comparisons between these regimens for thymic malignancies are rare. Due to these factors, the current treatment strategy prefers carboplatin for TC, due to its more favorable toxicity profile and demonstrated beneficial efficacy (21,32).

For patients who cannot tolerate or are not responding to first-line therapies, several second-line regimens are recommended by the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology (NCCN Guidelines®) for thymoma and TC (Figure 1). Everolimus, gemcitabine ± capecitabine, octreotide ± prednisone, and pemetrexed are the preferred therapies for thymoma, with ORRs ranging from 9–41% (21,34-37). Gemcitabine ± capecitabine, lenvatinib, pembrolizumab, and sunitinib are the preferred therapies for TCs, with ORRs ranging from 9–38% (21,36,38-41). For thymoma patients with positive octreotide scans or dotatate PET/CT scans, octreotide ± prednisone are available options (21,35). For patients who cannot tolerate more toxic combination regimens due to factors such as advanced age, comorbidities, or organ dysfunction, single-agent etoposide and single-agent ifosfamide may offer some clinical benefit (21,42,43).

Current evidence supports immunotherapy as a third-line option for treatment of advanced TETs, given the modest response rates and substantial toxicity risk observed in clinical trials. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), in particular, have demonstrated antitumor activity but with a notable risk of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). In a phase II trial of 40 patients with advanced, refractory, or recurrent TC treated with pembrolizumab, the ORR was 22.5% and the PFS was 4.2 months. Importantly, 15% of patients developed one or more new severe irAEs, such as increased alanine and aspartate aminotransferase, increased creatine phosphokinase, hyperglycemia, and myocarditis (39,44). In another study by Cho et al. investigating pembrolizumab in thymoma and TC, 71.4% of thymoma patients and 15.4% of TC patients reported irAEs of grade 3 or higher (38). Despite observing some clinical activity in both groups, the authors urged caution when using ICIs in patients with TETs due to the high incidence of autoimmunity. This high immunosensitivity seen in TETs, particularly in thymoma, is thought to reflect the unique biology of thymic tumors (45). Since TETs disrupt the immune tolerance mechanisms in the thymus, an organ that trains T cells to prevent autoimmunity, abnormal autoreactive cells can escape into circulation (45). This phenomenon is more prominent in thymoma, as it retains more thymus-like immune function, and helps explain the higher rates of observed autoimmunity and immune-related toxicity compared with TC (46,47).

Additional phase II investigations reported promising efficacy and manageable safety profiles with other ICIs and combination approaches, including avelumab monotherapy, avelumab plus axitinib, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab, and notably carboplatin and paclitaxel combined with atezolizumab—the latter, which achieved an ORR of 56% (48-51). In contrast, investigations into nivolumab monotherapy showed modest clinical activity, with no significant benefit observed even when combined with ipilimumab (52,53). Due to the substantial incidence of high-grade irAEs, especially among patients with thymomas, careful surveillance of patients’ symptoms and side effects is advised after administering immunotherapy (38,54).

Data supporting the use of ICIs in patients with hepatic metastases remain limited and recommendations for use remain mixed (55,56). A multicenter, retrospective study by Hao et al. included 136 patients with advanced TC; 42 of these patients had liver metastases and were treated with programmed death receptor 1 (PD-1) inhibitors. Patients with hepatic involvement exhibited significantly poorer outcomes than those without liver metastases, with an ORR of 11.9% versus 37.2%, a median PFS of 3.0 versus 8.0 months, and a disease control rate of 47.6% versus 80.9%. Median OS was also significantly shorter at 16.1 months compared with 29.1 months in patients without hepatic metastases (55). Notably, patients with hepatic metastases fare worse than those without distant spread, across the literature. A case report published by Wong-Chong et al. described a 45-year-old, heavily pre-treated patient with metastatic TC and hepatic metastasis. While the patient’s tumors did not demonstrate programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression (PD-L1 =0%), the patient still achieved a complete metabolic response. The authors proposed that PD-L1 expression can be heterogeneous across different sites of the biopsy, which could result in false negatives and that biopsy material can be insufficient. Additionally, prior exposure to platinum-based chemotherapy can cause an immunomodulatory effect that can lead to upregulation of PD-L1 in the tumor cells (56). Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to investigate the molecular mechanisms and genetic alterations that may contribute to response to immunotherapy in patients with TC and liver metastases.

Surgical resection

For advanced tumors, surgery, if feasible, provides the opportunity for patients to improve their quality of life and potentially prolong their survival (57). Given that a complete or R0 resection is recognized as one of the most important prognosticators for OS, surgery is recommended for all patients considered operable (58-60). Surgery has been established as a key treatment for patients with stage IVa thymic malignancies, with excellent survival outcomes after resection (61-64). Although the role of surgery in stage IVb patients has not been explicitly established, resection remains a feasible and potentially beneficial option for eligible patients with oligometastatic TETs involving the liver. There are no formal guidelines specifically discussing surgical eligibility for patients with hepatic metastasis from the thymus. Surgical resection of metastatic liver lesions in TETs can be feasible based on the body of case studies available in the literature; however, there are no large-scale studies (either retrospective or prospective) to describe prognosticators of good surgical outcomes or to help establish patient selection and best practices. Many of the aforementioned articles also describe adjuvant chemoradiation in the setting of surgery, which, based on the earlier section, may confer added benefit. Across the reviewed cases, several similarities emerged regarding the management and outcomes of liver metastases secondary to TETs. First, the reported patients presented with solitary or few (≤2) hepatic lesions, often localized to one or two segments, underscoring the importance of careful patient selection in determining surgical candidacy. Second, long-term survival and disease-free intervals were achievable, with patients achieving remission durations ranging from 6 months to 15 years following hepatic resection. These findings suggest that when technically feasible, aggressive local treatment can provide meaningful disease control in appropriately selected patients. Third, several reports describe a delayed onset of hepatic metastasis, with some cases emerging years or even decades after resection of the primary thymic tumor. This observation highlights the need for continued, long-term imaging extending well beyond the initial post-treatment period. Sustained monitoring is crucial, as early detection of late-onset metastases may preserve the opportunity for curative resection and thereby contribute to prolonged survival.

Radiation therapy

Thymic tumors are known to be radiosensitive, and radiation therapy has been described in the definitive, neoadjuvant, and adjuvant settings (77-83). Although no studies have specifically evaluated radiation therapy for liver metastasis secondary to TETs, several reports on advanced, recurrent, or metastatic thymic malignancies support the use of radiation therapy as a safe and potentially effective modality. In the absence of TET-specific clinical trials, available evidence supporting the use of radiation therapy for hepatic metastases is largely derived from studies of heterogeneous cohorts of patients with liver metastases from other primary cancers. Considering these limitations, the data suggest that radiation therapy represents a rational approach for isolated hepatic metastases from TETs.

A recent single-institution study by Seyfried et al. retrospectively evaluated 76 patients with 101 liver metastases from multiple different primaries, treated with stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT). The authors identified the prescribed dose (PD) as a significant predictor of local tumor control. Based on a time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, a PD greater than 77.44 Gy was associated with a 1-year freedom from local progression (FFLP) rate of 96.8%, compared with 67.2% for doses ≤77.44 Gy. The median OS was 33 months and only 3.9% of patients experienced grade ≥3 adverse events (84). Complementing these results, in a prospective single-institution study by Hao and colleagues, 32 patients with thymoma or TC (56.3% stage IVa or stage IVb), totaling 39 lesions, were treated with SBRT using a γ-ray system. The median dose administered to the gross tumor volume was 56 Gy, achieving an ORR of 96.9% and a median local control rate of 81.25%. The median tumor shrinkage was 62.2% and the median PFS was 28 months, with no reported grade ≥3 toxicities (85). These studies suggest that SBRT is a safe modality that provides durable local tumor control, supporting its role as a potentially effective local therapy for liver metastases.

In addition, Harada et al. conducted a study using SBRT in 10 patients with advanced TC. Five patients received SBRT treatment for the primary mediastinal tumor, while the remaining five were treated for recurrent or metastatic lesions. All patients reached at least partial remission (PR) within 2 months, and two patients with stage II and III disease achieved complete remission. Notably, one patient with stage IVb TC and multiple metastases involving the liver and bone underwent 10 SBRT sessions, followed by radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and carboplatin-paclitaxel chemotherapy, and achieved long-term survival of 11 years (86). The authors reported minimal radiation-related toxicity across all 10 patients, which they attributed to the lower radiation dose per fraction of SBRT compared with conventional radiotherapy used in prior studies (79,86).

Together, these studies describe the efficacy and safety of radiation therapy for local control in advanced or metastatic thymic malignancies. While the evidence for SBRT in hepatic metastases from TETs remains limited and is largely extrapolated, the available findings discussed above support its use in oligometastatic or unresectable disease and suggest its potential adaptability for hepatic metastases from TETs. While prior literature has discussed World Health Organization histologic subtype as a prognostic factor regarding one’s risk of developing metastases, no sources have evaluated whether it informs clinician selection or use of radiation therapy to treat this patient population (87).

Liver-directed and locoregional therapies

Liver-directed and locoregional therapies encompass the spectrum of interventional techniques aimed at achieving local tumor control. These modalities include thermal ablation, specifically RFA, microwave ablation (MWA), or cryoablation, as well as yttrium-90 (Y90) transarterial radioembolization (TARE). Liver-directed therapies target hepatic lesions through vascular or percutaneous routes. Although experience with these therapies for metastatic TETs is extremely limited, emerging case-based evidence supports the potential efficacy and safety for TARE in achieving local disease control. As such, current evidence exploring liver-directed or locoregional therapies in this setting remains preliminary.

In a case series published by Yuan et al., three patients with hepatic metastases secondary to TETs (2 thymomas, 1 TC) were treated with TARE. All patients demonstrated partial responses by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), with sustained local control ranging from 5 to 31 months post-treatment. The procedure was generally well tolerated, with two patients experiencing mild post-embolization syndrome and one developing an asymptomatic gastrohepatic fistula (88). Ferreira et al. described a patient with multifocal liver metastases from c-kit positive TC who was treated with TARE. The patient remained free of fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-avid hepatic disease 16 months post-treatment, with no complications (89). Collectively, these reports provide the first published evidence that Y90 TARE can achieve durable intrahepatic disease control in patients with primary thymic malignancies. The responses observed in both studies describe the potential of TARE as a safe and effective therapy.

Research regarding thermal ablation for hepatic metastases from TETs is even more sparse. A search of the literature revealed no large-scale studies and only limited case-based evidence evaluating the use of thermal ablation for hepatic metastases of TETs. An abstract published by Chen et al. presented a small case series of patients with TETs treated with ablation therapy. Four patients (1 B2 thymoma, 3 TCs) were documented undergoing a total of 11 ablations (6 MWA, 3 RFA, and 2 cryoablations), for 9 metastases to the liver and 2 to the chest wall. The median lesion size was 1.5 cm, and no major adverse events were reported. During follow-up, local recurrence occurred in 2 patients at 10 and 11.5 months post-treatment. The median recurrence-free survival was 7 months, and three patients demonstrated prolonged survival, remaining alive at 23.5–38 months after treatment (90). The report notably demonstrated procedural success and an absence of complications. In addition, in a case report by Hoshino et al., a patient with metastatic B2 thymoma was treated with RFA as part of a multimodal therapeutic approach. First, the patient received 4 cycles of cisplatin-etoposide chemotherapy, resulting in regression of both mediastinal and hepatic disease. A single residual hepatic lesion was subsequently treated with RFA, and an extended thymectomy was performed to remove the remaining mediastinal mass, leading to complete disease remission. At the time of reporting, the patient remained alive and recurrence-free 7 months after surgery. The authors emphasized the importance of long-term surveillance given the potential for delayed recurrence (91). Overall, the current case-based data indicate that thermal ablation may be performed safely and can achieve local control in select patients with hepatic metastases from TETs.

Future directions

Available literature on the management of liver metastases secondary to TETs remains limited due to the rarity of the disease. Additionally, there is no consensus in the literature for managing liver metastases in this population, resulting in variability in clinical practice. Most published data consist of small studies and case reports, which are prone to selection bias and may overestimate the efficacy of certain treatments. Larger, prospective or multi-institutional studies are needed to better define outcomes and evaluate the efficacy of treatment approaches. Future studies can also focus on identifying predictors of response to liver-directed therapy, such as histological subtype, biomarkers, or extent of hepatic disease, as these clinical factors remain poorly understood. Additionally, more uniform reporting across studies can help clarify which patients are optimal candidates for liver-directed treatments.

The role of liver-directed therapy for patients with thymomas versus TCs is not well understood and should be further evaluated. While these two classifications differ biologically and prognostically, the current literature does not sufficiently indicate whether the usage of surgical, radiation, or other liver-directed therapies should differ by histology. Formal recommendations will be instrumental in informing clinicians on how best to select and treat patients. Conclusions

Liver metastases from TETs are a rare phenomenon, with the guidelines supporting systemic therapy; however, the literature demonstrates that other liver-directed therapy options are utilized in clinical practice. For patients with liver dominant, liver-progressive, or oligometastatic disease in the liver, preliminary studies suggest that treatment may allow for improved patient outcomes. Therapeutic management of these metastases should be guided by multidisciplinary consensus, encompassing systemic therapy, surgical resection, radiation therapy, and interventional radiology treatments when applicable. The number of therapeutic options available, many of which have shown good efficacy in small scale studies and case reports, really highlights the need to further evaluate these options and determine optimal patient and tumor selection to achieve best outcomes. Given that delayed onset of hepatic metastases from TETs has been well documented in case reports, long-term surveillance after initial treatment is essential. This article also enforces the need for larger scale reviews and prospective multicenter trials to best aid outcomes for liver metastases secondary to TETs.
