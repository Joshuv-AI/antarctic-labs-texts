Lack of real-world evidence on active surveillance in Chinese patients with prostate cancer: insights from a 10-year cohort and a physician survey
Authors: Peng Xian, Pan Li, Gangjun Yuan, Junyong Dai, Fang Yuan, Yanping Song, Dawei Zhu, Teng Zhang, Haike Lei, Jun Li, Nan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundActive surveillance (AS) is an established management strategy for low-risk prostate cancer (PCa). However, its real-world implementation in China remains limited. This study aimed to present the outcomes of patients with early-stage PCa undergoing AS and to identify the main obstacles encountered in the promotion of AS.MethodsWe conducted a two-part study consisting of a prospective observational cohort of patients who elected for AS at a tertiary cancer center (2012–2017) and a nationwide cross-sectional survey of urologists assessing their knowledge, attitudes, and practice patterns regarding AS. Clinical outcomes, adherence to follow-up, and treatment conversion were evaluated in the cohort. Survey responses were analyzed across hospital level and physician seniority.ResultsAmong the 452 patients with localized PCa evaluated during the study period, 20 met the eligibility criteria and selected AS. After a median follow-up period of 118 months, 13 patients transitioned to definitive treatment, most commonly because of suspected disease progression. Three deaths occurred, one of which was a PCa-specific death. Adherence to scheduled PSA monitoring was high, whereas repeat biopsy compliance was low. The survey included 343 valid physician responses. Familiarity with AS eligibility and follow-up protocols was significantly higher among physicians in academic hospitals and those with senior titles. However, < 10% reported having structured AS follow-up programs, and most perceived patient anxiety and preference for surgery as major barriers to AS implementation.ConclusionsAS may be a feasible management option for carefully selected Chinese patients. Efforts to expand AS use in China should prioritize patient education, structured follow-up systems, national registry development, and supportive health policy measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02193-3.Keywords: Active surveillance, Prostate cancer, Physician survey, Practice patterns, Real-world outcomes, China

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most prevalent cancer among men. Its highest occurrence is observed in North America, Northern Europe, Western Europe, and Oceania but is relatively low in most Asian nations [1, 2]. Over the past two decades, the detection rate of early, low-risk, localized PCa in China has markedly increased owing to an aging population, increased prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing, and enhanced diagnostic capabilities [3, 4]. Simultaneously, with the major increase in PCa detection rates, care for early localized PCa in China is transitioning from underdiagnosis and undertreatment to overtreatment [5]. Conversely, mainstream international guidelines for early localized PCa therapy advocate active surveillance (AS) rather than aggressive treatment modalities as the optimal management strategy, especially for very low- and low-risk individuals [6, 7].

AS focuses on delayed intervention and dynamic assessment. Its main components include the following: when the patient’s tumor biology is clearly low risk and is expected to progress slowly, radical treatment is not immediately initiated. Instead, disease progression is closely monitored using regular PSA levels, digital rectal examination (DRE), imaging evaluation, and repeat biopsies. Once evidence of tumor progression or grade improvement is detected, radical treatment can be promptly initiated to ensure long-term tumor control while avoiding overtreatment [8]. Real-world data from developed countries in Europe and America show that many patients with early-stage localized PCa select AS as their first-line management approach. AS is comparable to immediate radical surgery or radiotherapy (RT) in terms of cancer-specific and overall survival, while significantly reducing treatment-related adverse events and quality-of-life impairment [9, 10]. This strategy has become the international consensus and standard practice for managing low-risk PCa. However, AS has been increasingly reported in East Asian countries [11], its adoption remains heterogeneous, with relatively lower utilization in certain regions, including China. Although several Chinese patients with PCa meet the criteria for AS, the actual rate of treatment is only 2–5% [12]. In recent years, the Chinese urology community has increasingly recognized the importance of AS in the management of very low- and low-risk localized PCa; however, comprehensive, prospective, and long-term cohort data on AS from mainland China remain scarce. Moreover, substantial discrepancies exist in the comprehension, attitudes, and practices of AS among physicians in mainland China [13]. The absence of systematic local evidence and research on physician behavior represents a major obstacle to the widespread implementation of AS.

This study aimed to present the long-term follow-up outcomes of patients with early-stage PCa undergoing AS in China using a prospective observational cohort and evaluate the feasibility and to explore the feasibility and real-world implementation of AS. A statewide survey of physicians was conducted to assess Chinese doctors’ comprehension, attitudes, and present practices regarding AS and identify the primary obstacles to its adoption and prospective avenues for enhancement. This study sought to address the enduring deficiency of clinical and behavioral evidence within this domain.

Materials and methods

This study consisted of two complementary components: a prospective observational cohort to evaluate real-world clinical outcomes, and a nationwide survey to assess physician perceptions and practice patterns, thereby providing contextual insight into the implementation of active surveillance in China.

All patients were fully informed about the AS protocol, associated risks and benefits, and international consensus regarding AS a standard of care for early-stage PCa. They were also counseled about alternative treatment options. Final treatment decisions were made based on the patients’ informed preferences after shared decision-making discussions with the treating physician.

Active Surveillance (AS) protocol

The surveillance schedule included PSA testing and outpatient review every 3 months, DRE, and annual multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) annually. A repeat prostate biopsy was recommended 1 year after AS initiation and subsequently whenever disease progression was clinically suspected. Repeat biopsies are mainly performed via the perineal route under local lidocaine infiltration anesthesia, and in a few patients via the rectal route under topical tetracaine anesthesia (depending on the patient's condition). For patients diagnosed by biopsy, all underwent pre-biopsy mpMRI, followed cognitive MRI–ultrasound fusion based on mpMRI findings, combined with systematic biopsy.

For patients with incidental PCa diagnosed after TURP, postoperative PSA reassessment and mpMRI were performed after recovery from TURP before final AS eligibility was confirmed. Immediate repeat biopsy after TURP was not routinely required unless residual clinically significant disease was suspected. A confirmatory biopsy was recommended during surveillance, generally around 1 year after AS initiation or earlier if PSA kinetics, mpMRI, or clinical assessment suggested progression.

Ethical considerations

This study was prospectively designed using a predefined protocol before patient enrollment. At the time of initiation (2012), registration and formal institutional review approval were not mandatory for noninterventional observational studies in our region. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all patients provided informed consent for data collection and follow-up. Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout this study.

Physician questionnaire survey

The online questionnaire was designed and distributed using Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/). The survey link or quick response code was circulated among the members of the Chinese Anti-Cancer Association (Urology Division) and other urologists across China. The questionnaire contained 28 items, including 15 single-choice, 10 scaled-response, 2 open-ended, and 1 multiple-choice questions. The content covered the respondents’ demographic background, clinical experience, familiarity with AS, and opinions on its feasibility and implementation barriers in China. Appendix I provides a copy of the full questionnaire. The survey was conducted between June 28 and September 28, 2021. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. All respondents provided informed consent before completing the questionnaire.

Although the survey was completed 4 years before the present analysis, it remains valuable in reflecting physicians’ understanding and practice patterns during the early phase of AS adoption in China. This delay in publication was primarily because of the need to accumulate longer follow-up data from our institutional AS cohort to provide complementary, real-world outcomes.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software version 22.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize categorical variables as counts and percentages and continuous variables as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. Groups were compared using the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, depending on data distribution. Graphical representations of the data, in the form of bar charts, were created using the “ggplot2” package (Wickham, 2016) in R (R Core Team, 2021).

Results

Patient cohort

Baseline characteristics of the AS cohort

Between March 2012 and July 2017, 452 patients were diagnosed with localized PCa at our institution. Among them, 211 met the predefined eligibility criteria for AS, as described in the Methods section. All eligible patients were informed that AS is an acceptable initial management strategy. Ultimately, 20 patients decided to undergo AS as their primary management and were included in the cohort. The mean age at diagnosis was 68.5 ± 5.1 years. The mean baseline PSA level was 7.70 ± 3.61 ng/mL. In 18 of 20 (90.0%) patients, PSA levels at diagnosis were < 10 ng/mL, whereas 2 of the 20 (10.0%) patients had PSA levels between 10 and 20 ng/mL. With the exception of two patients classified as grade group 2, all others (18/20, 90.0%) were classified as grade group 1 at diagnosis. The initial diagnosis was obtained by systematic prostate biopsy in of 7/20 patients (35.0%) and incidentally after transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) in 13/20 patients (65.0%). Adherence to the AS protocol

Most patients complied with scheduled PSA testing and outpatient review every 3 months; 18/20 patients (90.0%) attended regular PSA-based follow-up. Annual mpMRI and DRE were completed as planned in 17/20 patients (85.0%), whereas three patients did not undergo consistent annual imaging or examination. Adherence to repeat biopsies was notably lower. Only 6/20 patients (30.0%) underwent a scheduled confirmatory repeat prostate biopsy during AS. The results indicated that repeat biopsy was the most common cause of noncompliance, which was likely attributed to multiple factors such as patients’ concerns over invasiveness, as well as logistical and clinical considerations.

Follow-up duration and management changes

Except for one patient lost to follow-up at 18 months, the median follow-up duration for the evaluable patients (n = 19) was 118 months (IQR, 103–131; range, 28–141). Thirteen of the 20 patients (65.0%) discontinued AS and transitioned to definitive or systemic treatment during follow-up. Six patients discontinued AS within the first 2 years: three underwent radical prostatectomy (RP), two received androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), and one received RT. Seven more patients discontinued AS between 2 and 5 years: three underwent RP, three received ADT, and one received RT. Five patients (25.0%) remained on AS for > 5 years without conversion to active treatment. A total of six patients underwent RP. Among them, three patients had their Gleason score upgraded from 3 + 3 to 3 + 4, one patient was upgraded from 3 + 3 to 4 + 5, and one patient showed no change in Gleason score. The remaining patient received ADT preoperatively due to a markedly enlarged prostate, and no Gleason score was available for the final surgical specimen.

In most cases, discontinuation of AS was triggered by suspected disease progression rather than anxiety alone. Specifically, five patients switched because of a rapid PSA increase, one patient because of upgrading on repeat biopsy, and one patient based on MRI or other imaging findings. Two patients developed bone metastases during the follow-up. In contrast, four patients elected to leave AS and requested definitive treatment in the absence of objective progression.

Oncological outcomes

Three deaths occurred in the cohort during the follow-up. One patient remained on AS without evidence of progression but died from cardiovascular and metabolic complications (heart disease and diabetes) at 122 months. The second patient died of a cardiovascular event at 28 months while still on AS and without signs of PCa progression. Only one death was attributed to PCa, attributable to irregular monitoring. The patient presented at 26 months with a marked PSA increase and bone metastases, progressed to castration-resistant PCa after 1 year of ADT, and died at 52 months despite subsequent new hormonal agent therapy. Physician survey

Respondent and institutional characteristics and clinical practice volume

The survey was conducted between July and September 2021 using an online questionnaire platform as described above. A total of 401 urological oncologists completed the online questionnaire. After excluding 36 incomplete responses and 22 questionnaires completed in < 3 min, 343 (85.54%) valid responses were included in the final analysis; most of them were from university-affiliated hospitals (37.3%, n = 128), followed by other tertiary hospitals (30.6%, n = 105) and secondary or community hospitals (32.1%, n = 110) (Figure S1) (Supplementary Materials). The participants were predominantly male (95.9%, n = 329), reflecting the sex distribution of the urology workforce in China. Regarding professional rank, 17.2% (n = 59) were chief physicians; 34.6% (n = 119), associate chief physicians; 26.5% (n = 91), attending physicians; and 21.6% (n = 74), residents or junior doctors. In terms of clinical experience, nearly one-quarter (24.4%, n = 84) had practiced for ≥ 20 years; 31.1% (n = 107), for 10–20 years; 21.0% (n = 72), for 5–10 years; and 23.3% (n = 80), for < 5 years. The participants were recruited from 26 provinces across China, with over half (51.6%, n = 177) located in the western inland regions, indicating a broad but regionally unbalanced representation (Table 2).

Knowledge and awareness of AS

Physicians’ familiarity with AS criteria and management protocols substantially varied across hospital levels and professional ranks (Table 3). Among respondents from academic hospitals, 84.4% (108/128) reported being “very familiar” with low-risk PCa, compared with 68.6% (72/105) in tertiary hospitals and 52.7% (58/110) in secondary or community hospitals (p < 0.001). Comparable gradients were observed for familiarity with AS eligibility criteria (“very familiar”: 79.7% in academic, 61.0% in tertiary, and 48.2% in other hospitals; p = 0.002) and AS implementation protocols (“very familiar”: 73.4%, 54.3%, and 40.9%, respectively; p = 0.008). This indicates that physicians from academic centers demonstrate both broader awareness and greater procedural confidence regarding AS.

Clinical seniority was also associated with greater familiarity. Chief physicians exhibited the strongest knowledge, with 89.8% (53/59) reporting a confident understanding of AS criteria, followed by associate chief physicians (76.7%, 99/129), and attending physicians (56.0%, 51/91; p = 0.003). Physicians with ≥ 20 years of clinical experience demonstrated higher familiarity with AS protocols compared with those practicing for 10–20 years or < 10 years (82.1% vs. 65.4% vs. 43.9%, p = 0.001), indicating a clear positive correlation between clinical experience and AS knowledge (Table 3). Geographical differences were also observed. Physicians from western inland regions reported lower familiarity with AS definitions (58.2% “very familiar”) compared with those in coastal regions (72.9%, p = 0.04), possibly reflecting regional disparities in guideline dissemination and adoption (Table 3). Overall, physicians from higher-level institutions with greater clinical experience demonstrated a superior understanding of AS principles, perhaps influencing their subsequent clinical application behaviors.

Clinical practice and AS implementation

When discussing treatment options with patients eligible for AS, only 5.8% of the physicians reported that they “actively recommend and thoroughly explain” AS, whereas 29.4% “explained in detail” and 45.8% “mentioned it briefly.” Nearly one in five physicians (19.0%) admitted that they “rarely or never introduce” AS during consultations (Table 3). This tendency substantially varied across hospital levels. Physicians from affiliated hospitals showed the highest engagement, with 7.8% actively recommending and 31.2% explaining AS in detail, compared with 2.3% and 31.0% in secondary hospitals, respectively. Doctors in smaller or community institutions were least likely to introduce AS, with 43.5% of physicians rarely or never mentioning it. Awareness of peer practice was also limited: only 5.0% of the respondents had heard of colleagues conducting large-scale AS programs, whereas 56.3% were aware of “only isolated individual cases,” and 38.8% reported “no known AS practice.” Additionally, only 9.3% of physicians reported having “dedicated staff or clinics” for AS follow-up management. This proportion sharply varied by hospital type: 14.8% in university-affiliated hospitals and 10.5% in tertiary hospitals, compared with 2.3% in secondary hospitals and none in other institutions. Thus, structured follow-up systems are largely confined to high-volume academic centers, whereas most hospitals lack a formal AS infrastructure (Table 3).

These findings indicate that scalable and systematic implementation remains rare in China. Although doctors from tertiary and academic hospitals were more likely to discuss AS, < 10% of the patients ultimately selected surveillance. The lack of institutional support and poor patient compliance, especially with repeat biopsies, are major barriers to the wider adoption of AS in real-world clinical practice in China.

Physicians’ perspectives and perceived barriers to AS

Most physicians identified patient-related factors as the primary reason AS is rarely implemented in China (Table 4). The top three barriers were limited patient understanding of AS (82.3%), patient preference for surgery (77.5%), and a lack of physician recommendation (64.4%). Other commonly cited obstacles included psychological stress during surveillance (53.1%), a medical–legal environment discouraging physicians from recommending AS (48.9%), and difficulty in managing patients seeking care from multiple institutions (46.1%). Only 21.0% mentioned workload constraints, and 4.4% selected other reasons. The ranking of barriers was consistent across hospital levels and physician seniority. These results indicate that patient perception and physician communication barriers remain dominant obstacles to the adoption of AS in China. Overall, the physicians expressed cautious attitudes toward AS implementation (Table 4). Although 61.8% agreed or strongly agreed that AS was appropriate for low-risk PCa, only 29.1% believed that it was feasible under current domestic conditions. Most physicians acknowledged multiple patient-level barriers: 83.4% felt that patients preferred surgery, 88.6% believed that patients could not fully understand AS and it was difficult to persuade them to undergo repeat biopsy, and 72.9% considered AS likely to increase patient anxiety.

Regarding clinical feasibility, 58.4% agreed that initial staging may be underestimated, 64.2% thought that explaining disease progression to patients undergoing AS was difficult, and 70.1% considered patients undergoing AS difficult to manage. Consequently, only 27.4% strongly agreed that patients with low-risk PCa in China were currently suitable for AS, and 45.7% agreed to a limited extent. The overall pattern was consistent across different hospital levels and experience groups, although physicians from academic centers were slightly more supportive of AS implementation.

In summary, both patient- and physician-related factors contributed to the limited adoption of AS in China. Despite the theoretical endorsement of AS for low-risk PCa, practical confidence remains low owing to concerns regarding patient understanding, adherence, and medicolegal responsibility.

Discussion

This study provides integrated evidence addressing two major knowledge gaps in China’s management of low-risk PCa: (1) the paucity of long-term, real-world data on the safety and feasibility of AS in Chinese patients and (2) limited understanding of how urologists perceive and implement AS in clinical practice. By combining a 10-year prospective observational cohort with a large-scale nationwide physician survey, we offer a comprehensive overview of both the clinical outcomes and behavioral determinants influencing AS adoption in China. This prospective cohort study demonstrated that AS may be feasible applied to carefully selected low- and intermediate-risk patients. However, despite its favorable safety profile, a physician survey revealed that AS remains markedly underutilized in real-world settings.

Less than 10% of eligible patients herein selected surveillance, and most physicians acknowledged substantial barriers related to patient acceptance, follow-up logistics, and institutional limitations. The low uptake of AS in our cohort likely reflects a combination of patient- and physician-related factors. In clinical practice, many patients tend to equate cancer diagnosis with the need for immediate intervention and may have difficulty accepting a “no immediate treatment” strategy. In addition, physician recommendation plays a critical role in decision-making, and inconsistent or cautious attitudes toward AS among clinicians may further limit its adoption. Importantly, the physician survey findings provide essential context for interpreting the cohort results, particularly in explaining the low uptake of AS and suboptimal adherence observed in our patient population. These findings are consistent with our survey results, in which patient anxiety, preference for definitive treatment, and limited physician recommendation were identified as major barriers. Although Western trials have firmly established AS as the standard of care for low-risk PCa [14, 15], its adoption in Asia appears heterogeneous rather than uniformly low. Earlier regional studies from Japan, Korea, and Singapore reported uptake rates of 8–20% [16, 17], whereas a recent nationwide study from Japan demonstrated a substantially higher rate of approximately 41% [18]. By contrast, AS utilization in Europe and North America generally exceeds 40–60% [19, 20]. Our findings align with this trend and confirm that China faces similar challenges: a strong cultural preference for immediate intervention, limited patient education, and insufficient institutional infrastructure. This prospective cohort from our institution provides one of the earliest long-term observations of AS for PCa in China. After excluding one patient lost to follow-up at 18 months, the median follow-up duration was 118 months. During this period, only one PCa-related death occurred, with all other disease progressions detected at an early stage and effectively managed with curative treatment. Cancer-specific survival exceeded 95% at 10 years, consistent with large international AS cohort findings [14, 20, 21]. These findings confirm that AS may be feasible and durable management option for appropriately selected Chinese patients with low- or favorable-intermediate-risk disease.

However, several unique characteristics were observed in this cohort. A relatively high proportion of patients were initially diagnosed incidentally after TURP. Adherence to regular PSA monitoring and clinic visits was generally satisfactory; however, only 30% of patients underwent the recommended repeat biopsy, underscoring a major gap between protocol design and practical implementation. The low compliance with repeat biopsy may be partly explained by patient-specific barriers. Many patients accepted AS to avoid or delay invasive treatment and were therefore reluctant to undergo another invasive diagnostic procedure in the absence of clear progression. Fear of biopsy-related pain, bleeding, infection, and urinary complications, together with a preference for PSA- and mpMRI-based monitoring, may also have contributed to the low rebiopsy rate. This limited adherence parallels our physician survey findings, in which most urologists reported substantial difficulty in convincing patients to undergo repeat biopsies. Therefore, this cohort may represent a real-world spectrum between strict protocol-based AS and less structured surveillance strategies. These findings highlight that although the oncologic safety of AS in Chinese patients appears to be comparable to that reported internationally, patient acceptance and compliance remain major determinants of feasibility in real-world practice.

Our physician survey revealed substantial heterogeneity in the awareness and implementation of AS among Chinese urologists. Although most respondents acknowledged AS as an evidence-based management option for low-risk PCa, only a minority reported routine recommendations for eligible patients. This discrepancy between knowledge and clinical practice reflects the persistent gap between guideline awareness and real-world adoption. For instance, Japanese and Korean studies have shown that familiarity with AS protocols is markedly higher among urologists practicing in academic or tertiary centers than among those in regional or community hospitals [22, 23]. These results suggest that academic exposure, multidisciplinary collaboration, and access to updated clinical guidelines are the critical determinants of AS implementation. In our survey, physicians from university-affiliated hospitals and those with senior professional titles were significantly more familiar with AS eligibility and follow-up criteria. This pattern likely reflects the uneven distribution of continuing medical education resources and specialized PCa management training across China. The Asian Prostate Cancer Study and related multicenter reports have shown that, although awareness of AS is increasing, its institutional implementation remains limited in many Asian countries [24]. These results indicate that clinical inertia and institutional limitations, not merely a lack of knowledge, play a decisive role in the underutilization of AS. The observed geographic disparities in AS awareness may be explained by differences in healthcare resource distribution and access to continuing medical education. In addition, regional variation in patient attitudes toward cancer management may further influence physicians’ perspectives and clinical decision-making.

Importantly, this prospective cohort study substantiates these findings. Despite structured counseling and standardized follow-up recommendations, and although the use of mpMRI and MRI-targeted biopsy has increased substantially in recent years—especially in tertiary and academic centers—patient adherence to repeat biopsy and long-term monitoring remains poor in China [25]. This is partly due to uneven regional accessibility and implementation of relevant examinations, with only 30% of patients undergoing the recommended re-biopsy within the first year of surveillance. This pattern parallels the perception of most surveyed urologists who identified “difficulty persuading patients to undergo repeat biopsy” as a key barrier to AS implementation. By integrating both physician- and patient-level data, our study highlights that the limited adoption of AS in China arises from multifactorial system-level constraints. Addressing these barriers will require educational interventions and structural reforms to support sustainable AS programs across different hospital tiers. Beyond physicians’ knowledge, cultural attitudes, patient expectations, and systemic constraints play pivotal roles in shaping the limited adoption of AS in Asia. In China, cancer is perceived as a disease that must be “resected” rather than “observed,” and psychological discomfort toward the notion of “living with cancer” remains profound. Such attitudes have been well-described in studies from Korea, where both patients and families often equate AS with therapeutic neglect and prefer immediate definitive treatment [26, 27]. This phenomenon is mirrored in our national survey, in which nearly four-fifth of Chinese physicians identified patient anxiety and preference for surgery as major obstacles to the recommendation of AS. Taken together, these findings suggest that patient-centered counseling and culturally sensitive communication are critical prerequisites for expanding AS uptake in Asian settings.

From a systemic perspective, infrastructure supporting AS in Asia remains underdeveloped. This is unlike Western countries, where AS is integrated into national frameworks with dedicated nurse coordinators, multidisciplinary tumor boards, and centralized registries [28, 29]. Most Chinese hospitals lack structured follow-up systems, electronic monitoring platforms, or defined reimbursements for serial MRI and repeat biopsies. These systemic gaps substantially limit the scalability and quality assurance of AS programs. Moreover, medicolegal concerns were a recurrent theme in our survey. Many urologists expressed apprehension that disease progression during surveillance might result in litigation or reputational damage. This issue was previously recognized in another study in which physicians cited fear of patient complaints and unclear malpractice protection as barriers to conservative management [13]. Hence, the successful implementation of AS in China and similar contexts will require not only clinical validation but also systemic adaptation. First, enhancing both patient and clinician understanding of AS is foundational: surveys have identified that in various settings, misconceptions about “non-treatment” and unfamiliarity with observation protocols significantly deter AS adoption [30, 31]. In Western settings, culturally tailored decision aids reduce anxiety and increase patient uptake of AS [32]. Developing analogous tools in Chinese aligned with local cultural norms can shift the default expectation from immediate intervention to informed observation. Successful international models integrate urologists, radiologists/pathologists, nurse or coordinator support, and standardized follow-up pathways, which together enhance compliance and quality control of AS [33]. Therefore, the establishment of structured, multidisciplinary AS programs is critical. Although full-scale China-wide registries remain nascent, establishing such platforms would provide an evidence base to guide policy and clinical guidelines. Finally, policy and reimbursement support are essential, and modern AS protocols often involve repeat MRI, serial biopsies, and biomarker testing. Without coverage or reimbursement systems in place, uptake and adherence may remain limited [34]. Moreover, clarifying medicolegal protections for clinicians who follow guideline-based AS pathways may reduce physician reluctance toward “nonintervention.” In summary, coordinated efforts across patient education, structured multidisciplinary management, registry development, and policy/insurance reform can help China advance from sporadic individual AS practices toward standardized, evidence-based pathways, thus aligning PCa management with global standards while adapting to national clinical realities and cultural expectations.

This study has some limitations. First, the prospective cohort consisted of a relatively small sample from a single tertiary center, and most patients were selected based on shared decision-making rather than population-based enrollment. Moreover, adherence to follow-up recommendations, particularly repeat biopsy, was variable, which may have introduced surveillance bias in estimating the risk of disease progression. Second, the physician survey was cross-sectional and was conducted 4 years earlier. Practice patterns in China have evolved over the past several years. However, recent national registry and guideline data indicate that AS uptake remains low and that the structural and cultural barriers identified in our survey persist, suggesting that the results continue to reflect enduring system-level challenges. Third, the survey relied on self-reported knowledge, attitudes, and clinical practice, which may have been influenced by social desirability or recall bias. Some responses, particularly regarding patient acceptance and institutional barriers, reflect physician perception rather than directly measured behavioral or systems data. These limitations substantially restrict the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding oncological outcomes. Finally, although this study integrated both clinicians’ perspectives and real-world patient outcomes, it did not include patient-reported experience measures or qualitative interviews. Additionally, when the cohort study was initiated, local regulations did not require approval from the ethics committee for noninterventional observational management studies. All patients received full clinical counseling and provided informed consent; however, the absence of formal institutional review board documentation may have limited the perceived rigor of the study under contemporary standards.

Despite these limitations, the combined cohort and survey approach provides complementary and clinically relevant insights into why AS diffusion in China has lagged behind international adoption. Our findings suggest that improving AS adoption in China requires a multifaceted approach, including patient-centered education, enhanced physician training, structured follow-up programs, and the development of national registries and supportive policy frameworks.

Conclusions

AS may be a feasible management option for carefully selected Chinese patients; however, given the limited sample size, these results should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger cohorts. Our findings highlight that improving AS implementation in China requires more than clinical evidence. Strengthening patient education, establishing structured surveillance programs, expanding national registry efforts, and adjusting health policy support are essential steps toward enabling a broader, standardized application of AS for low-risk PCa.
