Clonal hematopoiesis in the pathogenesis of non-hematologic diseases with therapeutic potential
Authors: Lu Lei, Jie Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clonal hematopoiesis (CH) — clonal expansion of hematopoietic stem or progenitor cells due to somatic mutation — is associated with increased pathogenic risk and inferior clinical outcome of multiple diseases other than hematological diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases, solid tumors, kidney diseases, chronic liver diseases, and neurodegenerative diseases, as evidenced by many cohort studies. Of note, advanced knowledge about the underlying mechanisms of how CH specifically affects these diseases, including clonal expansion and abnormal hematopoiesis, immune dysregulation, chromosome aberration, genomic instability and epigenetic regulation, which identifies novel therapeutic targets and provides important rationale for treatment strategies. Here, we focused on the pathogenic role of CH in non-hematologic diseases and comprehensively reviewed recent researches on the pathogenic mechanisms of CH to provide a systematic and global view of CH-related pathogenesis.

Background

Hematopoietic stem cells reside in the bone marrow, possessing the capacity for self-renewal and the potential for multi-directional differentiation. Somatic mutations in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) trigger clonal expansion and conferring selective growth advantage, which are prevalent in the human body and accumulate progressively with age [1]. Clonal hematopoiesis (CH) is the clonal proliferation of blood cells caused by somatic mutations in HSPCs of the bone marrow [2]. Growing evidence suggest that CH is associated with increased all-cause mortality in not only hematological but also non-hematological diseases [3, 4]. However, their underlying mechanisms remain largely elusive. Potential risk factors for the occurrence and development of clonal hematopoiesis need to be further clarified [4]. Here, we reviewed recent studies about the potential determinants of CH and pathogenic role of CH in non-hematological diseases.

Definition and classification of CH

Defined by the clonal proliferation of blood cells, CH is generally categorized into two types according to the etiological factors [4]: CH with known drivers (mutations in genes such as DNMT3A, TET2, etc.) and CH with unknown drivers. As for CH with known drivers, those with variant allele frequencies (VAFs) of less than 2% are named as micro-CH, while those with VAFs exceeding 2% can be further classified into myeloid and lymphoid CH. Myeloid CH includes clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), clonal cytopenia of uncertain significance (CCUS) and myeloid CH driven by mosaic chromosomal alterations (M-mCA). Lymphoid CH mainly consists of two subtypes, lymphoid CHIP (L-CHIP) and lymphoid CH driven by mosaic chromosomal alterations(L-mCA) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Concept and classification of CH

Concept and classification of CH

As the predominant type of myeloid CH receiving remarkable research attention, CHIP used to be defined as the presence of somatic mutations in driver genes of leukemia without being diagnosed with hematological diseases or unexplained persistent cytopenia according to the World Health Organization (WHO) and International Consensus Classification (ICC) guidelines [5]. The most common mutated genes in CHIP patients include DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, PPM1D, JAK2, SF3B1, SRSF2, TP53, etc. [6]. And more than 90% of CHIP mutations occur in the above eight genes [6]. Correspondently, L-CHIP is a newly defined type of CHIP with mutations in driver genes of lymphoid malignancies [4]. It’s worth noting that the concepts of CH and CHIP are usually confused. The relationship between CH and multiple diseases are often investigated from the perspective of CHIP, which is considered as a precancerous condition.

Risk factors for CH

The risk factors for CH mainly include senescence, cytotoxic therapy, lifestyle, microorganisms, and inflammation (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Risk factors for clonal hematopoiesis

Risk factors for clonal hematopoiesis

Senescence is one of the most important risk factors for CH. Aging of hematopoietic stem cells and gradual accumulation of somatic mutations underlies to CH development [7]. The mutation of CH genes is extremely rare in people below 40 years old, but the incidence of CH increases dramatically beyond the age of 40 years, exceeding 62% in people over 80 [8]. Another study compares the incidence of CH in a group of long-lived elderly people aged 90–110 years with a group of normal elderly people aged 60–89 years, which finds that not only the incidence of CH is significantly higher in the long-lived group, but the number of mutated genes per individual in this group was also significantly higher than that in the normal group [7]. Besides, the VAFs of CH genes such as DNMT3A and TET2 also increase with age by 3.95% and 9.98% per year, respectively [9].

Cytotoxic therapy is another common cause for CH. The risk ratio of CH in patients who have received cytotoxic agents, radiation therapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy is 1.3 times higher than that of patients who have not received any anti-cancer treatments according to the analysis of detailed clinical data of 10,138 cancer patients in Memorial Sloan Kettering [10]. In patients with advanced thyroid cancer, the mutation frequency of CH genes is 37%, which increased with the dose of radioactive iodine (RAI). And each 10 mCi increase in RAI dose increased the probability of CH occurrence by approximately 2% [11]. Similarly, the mutation frequency of CH genes in pediatric cancer survivors is significant higher, almost twice as many as that in healthy controls [12]. Of note, Mutations in TP53 and its regulatory factor PPM1D are frequently observed in CH of patients treated with chemotherapy (particularly platinum-based agents) and are major risk factors for therapy-related myeloid neoplasms (t-MNs). Of note, mutations in TP53 and its regulatory factor PPM1D are significantly associated with previous exposure to both chemotherapy and radiotherapy, particularly platinum agents and etoposide, contributing to therapy-related myeloid neoplasms and myelodysplastic syndromes [13, 14].

The process of CH involves both genetic alterations and environmental factors [15]. One study has reported expansion of hematopoietic stem cells in Dnmt3a-/- mice in the midst of a chronic Mycobacterium infection, demonstrating that chronic infection can promote the progression of CH [15]. In addition, a higher prevalence of CH has been observed in people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (28.2%) than that in HIV-negative populations (16.8%), and is associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes such as myelodysplastic syndromes [16]. These findings suggest that infections with microorganisms, such as bacteria and viruses, are also risk factors for the development of CH.

Poor lifestyle habits, mainly as smoking, obesity and sleep deprivation, are also risk factors for CH [17]. A genome-wide analysis in 200,453 European individuals to investigate the susceptibility to CH, which revealed that smokers are 1.2 to 1.5 [13] times more likely to carry a CH mutation compared to those who never smoked [18]. Of note the occurrence of ASXL1 mutations is strongly associated with smoking [3]. Besides, the occurrence of CH was positively correlated with waist-to-hip ratio according to the analysis of data derived from the UK Biobank [19]. It is also worth noting that the pro-inflammatory state induced by obesity can promote the progression of CH to hematological malignancies [17].

Intriguingly, the relationship between inflammation and CH is bidirectional. The chronic inflammatory environment can selectively promote the clonal expansion of cells carrying CH mutations by activating inflammatory signaling pathways such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs). On the other hand, the presence of CH also contributes to inflammation status and promotes the development of inflammatory diseases, including cardiovascular diseases (such as atherosclerosis), systemic inflammatory diseases (such as inflammatory bowel disease), and rheumatic diseases (such as gout) [20].

CHIP-related non-hematological diseases

The relationship between CHIP and hematological malignancies has been identified a decade ago [21]. Since then, growing number of studies have demonstrated that the occurrence of CHIP is associated with the risk of subsequent development of myeloid neoplasms (MNs) including acute myeloid leukemia (AML), myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), etc. [22]. The overall annual progression rate of CH to malignant bone marrow tumors is 0.5%-1% [21]. Cardiovascular diseases

CHIP is identified as a risk factor for atherosclerosis, heart failure, and pulmonary hypertension based on cohort studies. In a cohort study of 6,181 Chinese CHIP carriers and minor clone carriers (variant allele score 0.5%-2%), patients with CHIP mutations have a 43% increased risk ratio of developing coronary heart disease compared to those without CHIP mutations [23]. In another cohort study involving 3,871 Americans without hematological malignancies at baseline, researchers performed CH risk scoring on patients identified with CHIP. The results showed that individuals with high-risk CHIP had increased all-cause, cardiovascular, and hematological malignancy-related mortality rates. Meanwhile, low-risk CHIP individuals and those without CHIP showed no significant difference in survival rates. These results suggest that, in clinical practice, CHIP patients should undergo risk stratification to identify those at high risk who require enhanced monitoring or intervention [24]. Besides, CHIP also serves as an independent risk factor predicting poor clinical outcome in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [25]. A multicenter study involving 499 patients with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension between October 2006 and December 2021 has found that CHIP mutations are independently associated with inferior prognosis [26]. Specifically, CHIP gene TET2 mutation is a risk factor for the development of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction — the risk ratio of disease increasing 2.4 times [27], and associated with poor prognosis [28]. TET2 deletion also increases the risk of atrial fibrillation, with a hazard ratio of 1.113 [29]. Another CHIP gene JAK2 mutation is associated with increased risk of venous thromboembolism, statistical analysis shows an odds ratio of 6.58 and hazard ratio of 4.2 [30]. Another cohort study of patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) revealed an increased risk of adverse outcomes associated with the presence of TET2, SF3B1, SRSF2, or U2AF1 mutations. [25].

A series of observational studies have identified an association between CHIP and cardiovascular disease. Although the risk stratification of CHIP patients appears meaningful, there are currently no effective intervention strategies. Besides, the sensitivity for detecting CHIP with VAF less than 10% is limited due to the limitations of sequencing technology’s detection depth, which might lead to errors [23].

Solid tumors

CHIP gene mutations are frequently detected in patients with solid tumors. A circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-based sequencing study enrolling more than 10,000 Chinese pan-cancer patients have found that CHIP gene mutations are detected in 9.1% of patients under the age of 40 and 23.1% of patients over the age of 80, which are significantly higher than that in healthy people [31, 32]. In a prospective study based on the UK Biobank database, the investigators have observed a higher frequency of baseline CHIP mutations in patients with lung cancer than that of healthy controls, irrespective of the age groups [33]. Further results from multivariate adjustments confirmed that baseline CHIP mutations are a risk factor for the development of subsequent lung cancer in patients. Compared to individuals without CHIP mutations, those with CHIP mutations had a 36% higher risk of developing lung cancer [33]. In early-stage non-small cell lung cancer, CHIP mutations, predominantly affecting DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1, were observed in 34% of patients and associated with increased infiltration of myeloid cells, such as monocytes and neutrophils [34].

In the mouse models of cancer, researchers have found reduced T-cell depletion and increased immune infiltration in the presence of Asxl1 mutations, which promote tumor development [31]. Considering that immune cells may originate from CHIP lineages, tumor-infiltrating clonal hematopoiesis (TI-CH) has been proposed and defined by presence of at least one region within the tumor with a VAF of 2% or higher for the CHIP mutation [34, 35]. Stratification of patients according to blood-only CHIP and TI-CH revealed that those with TI-CH had shorter progression-free and overall survival compared to patients with blood-only CHIP or without CHIP mutations [34]. It can thus be seen that TI-CH is a stronger predictor of poor prognosis in cancer patients.

CHIP is associated not only with the development of solid tumors and patient prognosis, but also with an increased risk of subsequent myeloid tumors after radiotherapy and chemotherapy. The introduction of the TI-CH suggests comprehensive analysis of malignant cells and their immune microenvironment to better understand the oncogenic role of CHIP. Future research should investigate the pathogenic mechanisms of CHIP, explore the feasibility and efficacy of targeted therapies, and establish method of CHIP detection and classification for prognostic prediction.

Autoimmune diseases

An analysis of 456,692 samples from the UK Biobank revealed that participants with CHIP were at a higher risk for developing autoimmune diseases, including Crohn’s disease, rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, and vasculitis, than participants without CHIP in the control group [36]. Different CHIP genotypes were strongly associated with the risk of specific autoimmune diseases. For instance, patients with ASXL1 mutations had a 2.92 times greater risk of developing Crohn’s disease. Meanwhile, CHIP patients with TET2 and JAK2 mutations were most strongly associated with psoriasis [36]. CHIP is associated with elevated levels of inflammatory markers, which are thought to be a potential mechanism linking CHIP to autoimmune diseases. More recently, a Mendelian randomization study demonstrated the causal relationship between CHIP and rheumatoid arthritis [37].

Kidney diseases

Inspired by the association of CHIP with cardiovascular disease and solid tumor, recent studies have further evaluated the role of CHIP in kidney diseases. According to two cohorts of Canadian patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), patients with CHIP have a greater tendency for lower hemoglobin, increased use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, and elevated parathyroid hormone levels than that of patients without CHIP, resulting in accelerating the progression of CKD [38]. In another cohort study of acute kidney injury (AKI), CHIP is the causal factor for AKI as determined by Mendelian randomization analyses [39].

Liver diseases

Results from a Mendelian randomization study of chronic liver disease show that CHIP causes the progression of chronic liver disease, approximately tripling the risk ratio [40, 41]. In addition, Tet2 knockout was found to promote the progression of steatohepatitis in mouse models [40]. In addition to promoting exacerbation of chronic liver disease itself, CHIP has been shown to facilitate the progression of metabolically abnormal steatotic liver disease to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [42, 43]. After adjustment for factors such as gender, CHIP remains a strong risk factor with incidence of HCC with an odds ratio of 1.81 [42].

Neurodegenerative diseases

A UK Biobank-based study has found a statistically significant increase in the risk of developing neurodegenerative disease, particularly vascular neurodegenerative disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis in patients with CHIP [44]. In particular, Mendelian randomization [45] and cohort data [46, 47] analyses show that CHIP is negatively associated with the risk of Alzheimer’s disease, providing new insights into the relationship between CHIP and age-related diseases. The paradoxical results suggest that the association between CHIP and the aforementioned diseases may be confounded by other factors, warranting further investigation.

Pathogenic mechanism of CH

The pathogenic mechanisms of CHIP-related diseases are multifaceted. The two highly mutated genes TET2 and DNMT3A can lead to abnormal immune cell differentiation and enhanced inflammatory responses, increasing the risk of inflammatory diseases, such as atherosclerosis and fatty liver inflammation. Besides, these mutations can also promote tumor progression by facilitating T-cell infiltration and altering the tumor microenvironment. Mutations in JAK2 also promote the development of atherosclerosis via affecting the physiological functions of red blood cells. Mutations in splicing factors, such as SF3B1 and SRSF2, primarily cause tumorigenesis by disrupting genomic stability and inducing DNA damage. The following sections will detailly discuss the pathogenic mechanisms of CHIP.

Clonal expansion and abnormal hematopoiesis

CH mutations are associated with abnormal hematopoiesis in different lineages of blood cells. Clonal expansion of myeloid hematopoiesis leads to abnormal development and function of neutrophils, monocytes, etc. [48]. The main manifestations include an increased production of inflammatory mediators by monocytes and macrophages, as well as defects in the antimicrobial functions of neutrophils, such as degranulation and phagocytosis [48, 49]. The effects of CH on lymphoid hematopoiesis are mainly manifested by aberrant differentiation and function of T and B cells. Knockdown of the Tet2 or Dnmt3a genes in mouse models resulted in altered differentiation of CD8 + T cells with a tendency to central memory phenotype, as well as cytokine production by T cells [49]. Besides, deletion of the TET2 leads to abnormal differentiation of plasma cells, suppressing the production of specific antibodies and leading to immune deficiency [49]. CH mutations also impair the stem cell factor-dependent clonal expansion and differentiation of erythroid progenitor cells, which retards the maturation of erythrocytes and contribute to myelodysplastic syndromes [50]. The process of atherosclerosis includes lipid deposition, inflammatory response, endothelial damage, smooth muscle cell proliferation, plaque development, plaque rupture and thrombosis, and vascular remodeling and stenosis [51]. In transgenic murine model, Jak2V617F mutation in the erythroid lineage leads to increased erythrocyte phagocytosis, enhanced cellular iron apoptosis and necrotic core formation, thereby promoting atherosclerosis development [52]. At the same time, the levels of key enzymes related to glutathione metabolism, including glutamate cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC) and glutathione s-transferase theta 1 (GSTT1), are reduced in JAK2V617F-mutated erythrocytes, which leads to a decrease in the ratio of GSH (glutathione) to GSSG (oxidized glutathione) and causes a decrease in erythrocyte antioxidant defense capacity [52] (Fig. 4A 4).Fig. 4Pathogenic mechanism of CH. Immune dysregulation

Considering that CH is critically involved in hematopoiesis, many researchers explored the role of CH in immune system to clarify its pathogenic mechanism. A multicohort blood analysis identifies proinflammatory features in CD14 + monocytes derived from patients carrying DNMT3A and TET2 mutations. Besides, the expression of the immune-associated gene GT3 is suppressed in T cells derived from patients with CHIP, suggesting compromised T cell function [53]. In atherosclerosis-prone low-density lipoprotein receptor-deficient (Ldlr-/-) mice, Tet2 deficiency enhances macrophage-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β secretion, which promotes the development of atherosclerosis [54] (Fig. 4B 4). Tet2-/- steatohepatitis mice presented with more severe hepatic lobular inflammation, lymphoid aggregation and hepatocyte ballooning due to the enhanced expression of NLRP3 in macrophages as well as secretion of pro-inflammatory Il-6, Cxcl1, Ccl22 and Ccl17 [40]. Ccl17 and Ccl22 promote Treg recruitment upon receptor binding (Fig. 4B4). Besides, enhanced NLRP3 expression is also observed in atrium of Tet2-knockout atherosclerotic mice, contributing to increased rate of atrial fibrillation induction, a shortened atrial effective response period and right atrial action potential duration [29]. Knockdown of NLRP3 gene or treatment with NLRP3 inhibitors counteract the process of pathogenesis [29]. JAK2V617F mutation is another atherosclerosis-associated CH gene. Clonal expansion of JAK2V617F-mutated macrophages exacerbates atherosclerosis through activating JAK-STAT signaling and glycolytic metabolism, enhancing DNA replication stress and Absent in Melanoma 2 (AIM2) inflammasome [55]. Increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation in JAK2V617F-mutated erythrocytes are also contributing factors to atherogenesis [52] (Fig. 4A4). Elevated levels of various inflammatory markers, including white blood cells, platelets, neutrophils, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), have been observed in patients with autoimmune diseases associated with CHIP. However, due to delayed diagnoses of autoimmune diseases and the bidirectional relationship between inflammation and these diseases, it is unclear whether CHIP directly causes elevated inflammatory levels and drives autoimmune diseases [36].

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex system consisting of tumor cells, immune cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells and extracellular matrix, which strongly influence malignant behavior such as proliferation, invasion and metastasis, as well as therapeutic response and long-term survival [56]. CHIP is closely associated with the formation of TME. Targeted sequencing analysis of tumor-isolated leukocytes derived from patients with untreated breast cancer identified infiltration of CD45 + hematopoietic cells carrying mutations in BCOR, TET2 and DNMT3A [57] (Fig. 4C4), suggesting that CHIP-mutated immune cells could interact with cancer cells to influence tumor progression and therapeutic response [58]. ASXL1 interferes with T cell development and function, creating a microenvironment that promoted tumor progression. Conditional knock-in of Asxl1 mutation in murine model leads to reduction of T cell infiltration in TME and upregulation of programmed death receptor-1 (PD-1) in CD 8 + T cells [59] (Fig. 4C4). Furthermore, patients with TET2 mutations were most likely to develop TI-CH. In TI-CH patients, CHIP mutations were frequently detected in the bone marrow, particularly in CD206 + macrophages and other mononuclear phagocytes, such as macrophages, monocytes, and dendritic cells, as well as natural killer cells. TI-CH enhances the migration of monocytes toward lung tumor cells, creating a tumor microenvironment rich in myeloid cells in the lung tumor model of Tet2-mutant mice, and also promotes the growth of tumor organoids [34].

Chromosome aberration

Chromosomal aberration represents different alterations in the structure or number of chromosomes, including deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations, ring chromosome formation, as well as increased or decreased numbers of single chromosome or a whole set of chromosomes [60, 61]. These changes can occur spontaneously or be caused by external factors, such as radiation and medication. According to a bidirectional Mendelian randomization study in atherosclerosis, CHIP-induced longer genetically interpolated leukocyte telomere length increases the risk of atherosclerosis, underlying pathogenic mechanism involving chromosome aberration [62]. Loss of Y chromosome (LOY) in peripheral blood leukocytes is a common somatic mutation type in male. Mendelian randomization analysis revealed a correlation between LOY and the occurrence of gonadal and myeloid CH [63]. As a mechanism of action, LOY promotes cancer development through influencing genomic instability, rendering cell susceptible to DNA damage and mutation during the process of division and proliferation, thereby increasing the likelihood of cancer development [1] (Fig. 4D4). Besides, CHIP-positive patients with solid tumors are more likely to carry rare pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) germline mutations, such as ATM, and APC, which increases the susceptibility to secondary leukemia [64]. Short telomere syndrome is a premature aging disease associated with CHIP, which promotes genomic instability and subsequent tumorigenesis [65].

Genomic instability

Genomic stability, closely related to the development of diseases including tumors, is maintained by various molecular mechanisms, such as DNA repair, post-transcriptional modifications, and cell cycle regulation. SRSF2 is an essential splicing factor gene that plays a vital role in the efficient transcription of DNA replication and repair genes. SRSF2 mutations result in widespread DNA damage, causing cell cycle arrest and promoting the development of squamous cell carcinoma [66]. Additionally, mutations in splicing factors, such as SF3B1, SRSF2, and U2AF1, alter the transcriptome and result in a synergistic effect across multiple genes [67]. This can lead to consequences such as DNA damage, activation of the replication stress response, and impaired proliferation of hematopoietic progenitor cells, promoting tumor transformation and progression, as well as increased drug resistance [67].

Epigenetic regulation

As the most frequently mutated CHIP genes, DNMT3A and TET2 are also important epigenetic regulators involving in DNA methylation and demethylation. TET2 oxidizes 5-methylcytosine (5-mC) to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5-hmC) and regulates the expression of genes involving in immune cell proliferation, differentiation and inflammation. TET2-deficient T cells exhibit a differentiation tendency to memory precursors and central memory T cells through epigenetically enhancing the expression of transcription factor TCF1 and EOMES, respectively [48] (Fig. 4D4). Similarly, in DNMT3A-deficient T cells, increased expression of EOMES enhanced differentiation of memory precursor and central memory T cells [48].

CHIP also accelerates epigenetic clock [68], explaining their association with age-related diseases. In CKD patients with CHIP, the average epigenetic age acceleration, defined as the inferred epigenetic age greater than the actual age, is increased by 2 to 3 years [69], which is associated with the higher prevalence of CKD in populations with CHIP. As a potential mechanism of action, CHIP increases macrophage-secreted inflammatory cytokine, which epigenetically down-regulates expression level of Klotho in both macrophages and renal cells, contributing to kidney damage [70].

Therapeutic targeting CH

Increased knowledge about the pathogenic role of CH in multiple diseases paves the way of developing novel therapeutic strategies, including CH gene inhibitors, as well as drugs targeting immune alterations, genomic integrity and epigenetic modification.

CH gene inhibitors, such as IDH1 inhibitor (ivosidenib), IDH2 inhibitor (enasidenib), and JAK2 inhibitors (ruxolitinib, fedratinib) have been approved for administration in hematological diseases by Food and Drug Administration [71]. These drugs suppress the development of CHIP by inhibiting the action of the corresponding gene products and blocking the relevant signaling pathways. However, currently, these drugs are mainly used in hematological diseases [72], whose therapeutic potential in CHIP-related non-hematological diseases remain to be clarified.

Impaired mitochondrial DNA integrity and activation of the cGAS-type 1 interferon pathway in macrophages derived from individuals carrying DNMT3A or TET2 mutations provide therapeutic rationale for cGAS inhibitor in treating DNMT3A or TET2-mutated patients [73]. However, another study have showed that inhibition of DNTM3A could reduce the production of type I interferon in mouse peritoneal macrophages [74]. Although this discrepancy may be due to differences in the research subjects, in-depth research is needed to confirm these findings before these drugs approved for clinical administration.

Increased Csf1r expression has been observed in Tet2-deficient atherosclerotic mice and human TET2-deficient macrophages. And CSF1R inhibitors successfully rescue the progression of atherosclerosis in Tet2-deficient mice [75]. As is well-established that IL-6 induced CSF1R expression and apoptosis resistance in Tet2-deficient macrophages [75], blockade of IL-6 signaling with IL-6 monoclonal antibody such as siltuximab and olokizumab [76] show therapeutic potential.

The impact of CHIP on atherosclerosis is associated with exacerbated vascular inflammation, underlying therapeutic potential of anti-inflammatory drug colchicine [77]. In Tet2-mutant mice, administration of colchicine prevented the accelerated development of atherosclerosis. Moreover, the link between TET2 mutations and subsequent myocardial infarction is weakened in patients who had taken colchicine, according to the Mass General Brigham Biobank and the UK Biobank [77].

Besides, treatment with vitamin C reverses the abnormal clonal proliferation and restores the function of hematopoietic stem cells in Tet2-deficient mice [78, 79]. Signaling molecules such as TGF-β1 [79], whose expression is elevated in the bone marrow microenvironment with age, are also promising therapeutic targets.

In addition to targeting inflammatory pathways, recent studies have begun exploring other methods of inhibiting clonal hematopoietic progression. Since clonal expansion of blood cells depends on mitochondrial activation, researchers used mitoquinol to selectively induce mitochondrial apoptosis in Dnmt3a-mutated HSPCs in mice, causing some of the mutated cells to die and restoring normal metabolic levels in the remaining cells [80].

Despite numerous pre-clinical studies, the clinical data of therapeutic targeting CH remains limited. A randomized clinical trial conducted on patients with a history of myocardial infarction and elevated levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein found that administering the anti-IL-1β antibody canakinumab reduced the likelihood of subsequent major adverse cardiovascular events in TET2 CHIP patients at a ratio of 62% [81]. Further proteomics research has revealed that, compared to patients without CHIP mutations, the presence of CHIP mutations is associated with proteomics characteristics of inflammation and infection defense responses, as well as high-risk cardiovascular disease markers, and that canakinumab can reverse this disease process [82]. These results provide stronger support for the use of targeted anti-inflammatory therapy in treating CHIP-related diseases. In addition, clinical trials are also underway to study the NLRP3 inhibitor DFV890 for the treatment of CHIP, as well as monoclonal antibody-targeted therapies such as enasidenib and ivosidenib for the treatment of CCUS.

Although targeted therapy for CHIP shows promises, several issues remain to be addressed. First, the pathogenic mechanisms of CHIP are not fully understood and are sometimes contradictory. Further research is needed to determine clonal diversity and identify suitable, effective therapeutic targets and timing. Second, it is unclear whether inhibiting the growth of one mutant clone would accelerate the development of another. The efficacy of CHIP treatment must be balanced against potential risks to enable its widespread clinical application. Additionally, although targeted therapy that directly addresses mutated genes in CHIP may be the most direct and effective approach, many CHIP mutations are loss-of-function mutations, and many CHIP genes are epigenetic regulatory factors. Both complicate the design of targeted treatment strategies. Off-target effects also increase the uncertainty of targeted therapy. Thus, future development of targeted therapy for CH depends on more in-depth mechanistic research and broader clinical trials.

Conclusions

Acknowledged as the determinant in hematopoietic process, CH is involved in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases. In this review, we focused on the pathogenic role of CH in non-hematopoietic diseases with increasing number of cohort studies supporting the strong link or causal relationship between CH and non-hematopoietic diseases. The underlying mechanism including abnormal hematopoiesis, immune dysregulation, chromosome aberration, gene instability and epigenetic modification.

Single-cell genomics has the potential to create more precise, high-resolution cell atlases that can help identify early-stage CH. For instance, single-cell atlases of circulating CD34⁺ hematopoietic stem cells identified that increased red blood cell distribution width is an independent risk factor for CH [83]. The longitudinal sampling strategy can dynamically track CH throughout the progression of the disease. For example, a study with consecutive blood samples from patients of neuroendocrine tumors found that 64% of patients with CH at baseline developed persistent cytopenia and showed dynamic progression upon treatment of peptide receptor radionuclide therapy. In the future, CH screening in combination with longitudinal monitoring could better predict patient risk and inform mitigation strategies [84]. Considering the multiple factors affecting CH and the complicated roles of CH in different context of diseases, it will be of great interest to investigate CH at single-cell resolution and track the evolution of CH in the process of disease progression.
