Nursing students’ attitudes toward technology-enhanced psychomotor skill learning: scale development and validation using a mixed-methods approach
Authors: Tuğçe Kabak Solak, Afife Yurttaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe integration of information technologies into nursing education has increased significantly, particularly in the teaching of psychomotor skills. Although these technologies provide important learning opportunities, students’ attitudes toward their use play a critical role in the effectiveness of technology-enhanced education. However, there is a lack of valid and reliable instruments specifically designed to assess nursing students’ attitudes toward the use of information technologies in psychomotor skill learning.AimThis study aimed to examine nursing students’ attitudes toward the use of information technologies in psychomotor skill learning and to develop and validate a measurement tool for this purpose.MethodsAn exploratory sequential mixed-methods design was employed. In the qualitative phase, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 20 nursing students, and data were analysed using content analysis. Based on qualitative findings and a literature review, an initial item pool was generated. In the quantitative phase, data were collected from 891 nursing students. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were conducted to examine construct validity, and reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients.ResultsQualitative findings identified four subthemes regarding students’ attitudes toward the use of information technologies in psychomotor skill learning. Factor analyses supported a 33-item scale with four dimensions. The CFA results indicated an acceptable level of model fit (CFI = 0.907, TLI = 0.900, RMSEA = 0.080, SRMR = 0.048). The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.94, indicating high internal consistency.ConclusionThe developed scale is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing nursing students’ attitudes toward the use of information technologies in psychomotor skill learning. The findings suggest that information technologies are perceived not only as instructional tools but also as multidimensional learning environments that support learning processes, clinical practice, and professional development. This scale may contribute to the evaluation and improvement of technology-enhanced nursing education practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04927-y.

Introduction

The rapid development of digital technologies has fundamentally transformed healthcare systems and, consequently, the educational strategies used to prepare future nurses. In contemporary nursing education, the development of psychomotor skills—critical for ensuring patient safety and the quality of care—is increasingly shifting from traditional demonstration-based teaching toward technology-enhanced learning environments [1, 2]. These environments integrate tools such as simulation, virtual reality, mobile applications, and video-assisted instruction, thereby supporting more flexible, repeatable, and student-centred learning processes [3–7].

However, psychomotor skill learning is not merely a technical process; rather, it is a multidimensional construct that involves the integration of cognitive, affective, and behavioural components [8–10]. Psychomotor skill learning has long been recognized as a core component of nursing education and has been conceptually explained through educational taxonomies proposed by Bloom, Simpson, and Dave [11]. These frameworks describe psychomotor learning as a progressive process involving the development of coordinated motor behaviours through observation, guided practice, repetition, and experiential learning [12]. In nursing education, psychomotor competence requires not only technical performance but also the integration of cognitive understanding, affective engagement, and behavioural adaptation during clinical practice [8]. Although technology-enhanced approaches have been shown to have positive effects on performance, self-confidence, and motivation [13–15], these effects are not consistently observed across all contexts. This suggests that the success of technology-based learning depends not only on the technologies themselves, but also on how students perceive, interpret, and experience these tools. In this context, the use of information technologies in psychomotor skill learning presents a paradox. On the one hand, technology increases accessibility and interaction; on the other hand, it may lead to negative experiences such as cognitive overload, technostress, and cyber sickness [16–19]. Such negative experiences may influence students’ perceptions of learning and consequently weaken their acceptance of technology-based teaching methods [20, 21]. These contradictory findings suggest that the effectiveness of technology-enhanced learning cannot be fully explained without considering students’ attitudes toward technology.

Individuals’ attitudes toward technology are commonly explained within the framework of theoretical models such as the Technology Acceptance Model [22]. According to this model, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use determine individuals’ behavioural intentions and actual technology use. However, in nursing education, this model has predominantly been applied in the context of general technology use and e-learning, while psychomotor skill learning—a practice-based and embodied domain—has received limited attention [20]. This indicates a significant conceptual and methodological gap in the literature. Existing measurement tools primarily assess general attitudes toward technology and do not adequately capture the contextual and multidimensional nature of psychomotor skill learning. Therefore, current instruments are insufficient to fully understand how students engage with technology-supported skill acquisition processes. Addressing this gap is essential for both research and educational practice. Accordingly, this study aimed to examine nursing students’ attitudes toward the use of information technologies in psychomotor skill learning and to develop a theoretically grounded measurement instrument using a mixed-methods approach, followed by an assessment of its validity and reliability.

Methods

This study aimed to develop and validate a scale measuring nursing students’ attitudes toward the use of information technologies in psychomotor skill learning. An exploratory sequential mixed-methods design, as described by Creswell and Plano Clark (2020), was adopted, whereby qualitative findings informed item generation, followed by quantitative evaluation of the scale’s psychometric properties [23]. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) framework.

Participants

This scale development study was conducted in four phases: (1) literature review (2), individual interviews (3), pilot testing, and (4) scale development and psychometric evaluation. Different samples were used at each phase. Data were collected between January and July 2025 from first-, second-, third-, and fourth-year nursing students enrolled in the nursing departments of two public universities in Türkiye. First-year students were excluded from the qualitative phase because students at both universities had not yet formally commenced psychomotor skill training during the qualitative data collection period. However, the qualitative and quantitative phases of the study were conducted at different time points. By the time the quantitative data were collected, first-year students had already been exposed to technology-supported psychomotor skill learning activities within the nursing curriculum. They were therefore included in the quantitative sample.

In the qualitative phase, participants were selected using purposive sampling from both universities to obtain information-rich cases relevant to the study purpose [24]. All participants had prior experience using information technologies in psychomotor skill learning. Variation in gender, academic year, and academic achievement was considered during participant selection to capture a range of perspectives regarding technology-supported psychomotor learning. Interviews continued until data saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new codes, categories, or themes emerged from the data [25]. Data saturation was reached after 20 interviews, and no additional participants were recruited because subsequent interviews did not generate new information relevant to the study aims.

In the quantitative phase, convenience sampling was employed to recruit a sufficient number of nursing students who met the study inclusion criteria, as scale development studies require large samples to ensure stable factor solutions and reliable psychometric evaluation [26]. This approach facilitated the recruitment of an adequate sample for both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. However, because convenience sampling is a non-probability sampling method, sampling bias may have occurred, and the generalizability of the findings may therefore be limited to nursing students with characteristics similar to those of the study sample [27]. Data were collected from 461 students for the Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA). The factor structure identified through EFA was subsequently tested using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) with a separate sample of 459 nursing students recruited from the same universities at a different time point. Thus, the EFA and CFA analyses were conducted using independent samples, and no participant was included in both datasets. A total of 920 students participated in the quantitative phase. Of these, 29 questionnaires (18 from the EFA sample and 11 from the CFA sample) were excluded due to incomplete responses. The final dataset consisted of 891 participants.

Instrument

Literature review

A comprehensive literature review was performed to establish the theoretical foundation of the scale and to identify the key dimensions of attitudes toward the use of information technologies in psychomotor skill learning. Relevant national and international studies focusing on nursing education, psychomotor skill instruction, information technologies, and attitudes were examined. The findings from the literature informed the development and content of the initial item pool.

Individual interviews

Within the qualitative phase of the exploratory sequential mixed-methods design, individual semi-structured face-to-face interviews were conducted between January and February 2025 with second-, third-, and fourth-year nursing students from both universities who had experience using information technologies in psychomotor skill learning. Participants were selected using purposive sampling. First-year students were excluded because they had not yet commenced psychomotor skills training during the study period. Data were collected using a semi-structured interview form. To ensure content relevance and conceptual clarity, expert opinions were obtained from 13 faculty members with expertise in psychomotor skills, qualitative research, and scale development, including two faculty members from the Department of Measurement and Evaluation, two faculty members from the Department of Nursing Education, and nine faculty members from the Department of Fundamentals of Nursing, as well as two language experts. Based on the experts’ feedback, the wording, scope, and clarity of the interview questions were reviewed and revised accordingly. Subsequently, a pilot study was conducted with four nursing students to assess the comprehensibility and applicability of the interview form, and final modifications were made based on the findings. All interviews were conducted by the first researcher, who had completed a certified training program in qualitative research methods prior to data collection. Interviews were conducted in quiet settings to minimize distractions and were audio-recorded after obtaining written informed consent from the participants. The interviews lasted approximately 30–45 min. In addition to audio recordings, brief field notes were maintained during and immediately after the interviews to document contextual observations and support data interpretation. To enhance reflexivity and minimize potential power imbalances, the interviewer did not have a teaching or evaluative role with any of the participants. Interviews were scheduled outside class hours at times convenient for the participants, and participation was entirely voluntary. Throughout the interview process, the researcher remained aware of personal assumptions, used open-ended questions, and took care to avoid influencing participants’ responses. All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved, resulting in a total of 20 participants. To ensure confidentiality, participants were assigned identification codes (K1–K20).

Item generation and content validity

In the initial phase of scale development, an item pool was generated based on the findings of the qualitative analysis and a comprehensive literature review. The subdimensions of the scale were determined according to the themes identified in the qualitative phase. By integrating codes derived from participants’ statements with evidence from the literature, an initial item pool of 59 items was developed. Content validity was assessed using the Lawshe technique. The draft scale was evaluated by 13 content experts, including two faculty members from the Department of Measurement and Evaluation and 11 faculty members from the Department of Fundamentals of Nursing. In addition, one language expert reviewed the items for linguistic clarity and consistency. Based on expert evaluations, the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated for each item following Lawshe’s (1975) method. Items with CVR values below 0.54 were removed, resulting in the deletion of 11 items, while five items were revised to improve clarity, relevance, and comprehensibility [28]. The Content Validity Index (CVI) for the remaining items was calculated in accordance with the recommendations of Polit and Beck (2006) and was found to be 0.88 [29]. Consequently, the draft scale was reduced to 48 items prior to pilot testing. The scale was developed as a 5-point Likert-type instrument ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), as this format is appropriate for attitude measurement, easy to administer, and helps reduce extreme response bias [30]. To enhance the integration between the qualitative and quantitative phases of the study, qualitative themes and representative codes obtained from the interviews were systematically mapped onto the scale items during the item generation process. The resulting items were subsequently evaluated through exploratory factor analysis, and their alignment with the final factor structure was examined.

Pilot study

A pilot study was conducted with 60 nursing students from all academic year levels. Participants in the pilot study met the inclusion criteria for the main study but were excluded from the final sample. The purpose of the pilot study was to evaluate the clarity of the items, the length of the scale, the time required to complete it, and the instrument’s comprehensibility. Participants also provided feedback regarding the scale. Based on the pilot results, no modifications were made to the items of the scale.

Psychometric evaluation

Construct validity

To determine construct validity, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) were conducted. EFA was performed first on the initial dataset, followed by CFA using an independent sample to verify the factor structure. Prior to EFA, item–total correlation coefficients were examined, and items with correlation coefficients below 30 were removed. The suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity. EFA was conducted using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The factor structure was determined based on eigenvalues greater than 1 and examination of the scree plot. Both orthogonal (Varimax) and oblique rotation analyses were examined during the exploratory factor analysis process. Since the oblique rotation yielded a factor structure similar to the Varimax solution, the Varimax rotation was retained because it provided a clearer and more interpretable factor structure. Items with factor loadings below 0.40 were considered for removal. In addition, items exhibiting cross-loadings, defined as a difference of less than 0.10 between factor loadings on two factors, were sequentially removed to obtain a clear and interpretable factor structure [31]. The factor structure obtained from EFA was subsequently tested using CFA. Model fit was evaluated using χ²/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR, and standardized factor loadings were examined to assess construct validity [26].

Reliability

The reliability of the scale was assessed using multiple methods. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients. Split-half reliability analysis was also conducted to examine the consistency of the scale items. In addition, measurement invariance across gender groups was evaluated using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA). Configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance models were tested sequentially [32]. Invariance was assessed by examining changes in model fit indices between nested models, with ΔCFI values below 0.01 and ΔRMSEA values below 0.015 considered indicative of invariance [33].

Data analysis

The qualitative data were analyzed using content analysis following the approach proposed by Elo and Kyngäs (2008), which is suitable for systematically describing textual data and identifying patterns within qualitative material [34]. Interview transcripts were reviewed repeatedly to achieve familiarity with the data, and initial codes were generated independently by the researchers based on participants’ statements. The coding process and thematic structure were subsequently compared and evaluated collaboratively. Any differences in interpretation were discussed until consensus was achieved. Throughout the analysis process, detailed documentation was maintained to support the credibility, consistency, and transparency of the qualitative findings. Quantitative data analyses were performed using SPSS programme and AMOS software packages. Prior to conducting factor analyses, descriptive statistics were calculated, and the suitability of the dataset for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity. Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted to identify the factor structure of the scale, and the resulting structure was subsequently tested through Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) using an independent sample. Model fit was evaluated based on fit indices, including χ²/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. The reliability of the scale was examined using Cronbach’s alpha, McDonald’s omega, and split-half reliability coefficients. In addition, measurement invariance analyses were conducted to assess the applicability of the scale across different groups. Statistical significance levels were set at p < 0.01 and p < 0.05 [27].

Results

A total of 20 nursing students who had experience using information technologies in psychomotor skill learning participated in the qualitative phase of the study (Supplementary Table 1). The participants’ ages ranged between 20 and 24 years; 11 were female, 9 were third-year students, and their grade point averages ranged from 2.54 to 3.90. All participants reported that they wished information technologies to be used in courses, had utilized information technologies in psychomotor skill learning, and felt more competent in their courses as a result of using these technologies (Supplementary Table 1).

The distribution of participants according to their descriptive characteristics is presented in Table 1. A total of 443 nursing students were included in the EFA and reliability phases. Among this group, 51.2% were aged 21 years or older, 73.8% were female, and 56.2% were first-year students. In addition, 75.8% of the students had a grade point average between 2.50 and 3.50. The majority of participants (96.6%) reported that they preferred the use of information technologies in nursing education, and 85.3% indicated that they used these technologies in psychomotor skill learning. Furthermore, 64.6% of the participants stated that courses incorporating information technologies contributed to their academic development (Table 1). During the CFA phase of the quantitative analysis, data were collected from 448 nursing students. Of these participants, 51.6% were aged 21 years or older, 71.7% were female, and 52.9% were first-year students. In addition, 73.0% of the students had a grade point average between 2.50 and 3.50. The majority of participants (96.0%) reported that they preferred the use of information technologies in nursing education, and 81.3% indicated that they used information technologies in psychomotor skill learning. Furthermore, 67.4% of the participants stated that courses incorporating information technologies contributed to their academic development (Table 1).

In the interviews conducted to examine nursing students’ attitudes toward the use of information technologies in psychomotor skill learning, “contributions of information technologies to psychomotor skill learning” was identified as the main theme. In addition, four subthemes- contribution to the learning process, contribution to courses and academic achievement, use and effectiveness in clinical practice settings, and contribution to professional development and professionalism- and a total of 28 codes were generated (Supplementary Table 2).

Within the subtheme of contribution to the learning process, six codes were identified: increasing learning speed and retention, providing prior knowledge, learning with correct techniques, enhancing readiness, facilitating easy access to information, and eliminating time and space constraints. Within the subtheme of contribution to courses and academic achievement, seven codes were identified: increasing focus, improving academic performance, enhancing motivation, stimulating curiosity and interest, reinforcing theoretical knowledge, engaging with courses more positively, and learning in an enjoyable manner (Supplementary Table 2).

Within the subtheme of use and effectiveness in clinical practice settings, eleven codes were identified: enhancing self-confidence, supporting time management, increasing interaction with peers, increasing interaction with the healthcare team, supporting education for healthy individuals and patients, facilitating invasive procedures, supporting clinical decision-making, increasing interaction with patients, enabling effective case and care management, increasing interaction with instructors, and supporting stress management (Supplementary Table 2).

Within the subtheme of contribution to professional development and professionalism, four codes were identified: supporting professional values, enhancing professional development and professional awareness, increasing awareness of professional ethics, and supporting critical thinking (Supplementary Table 2).

Prior to conducting the exploratory factor analysis, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity were examined to assess sample adequacy and the suitability of the dataset for factor analysis. The KMO value for the remaining 44 items was 0.956, indicating excellent sample adequacy, and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was statistically significant (χ² = 16,490.861, df = 946, p = 0.001). Based on these findings, factor analysis was subsequently performed. Varimax orthogonal rotation was employed for the exploratory factor analysis. At this stage, 11 items were identified as cross-loading items. Accordingly items i9, i42, i8, i7, i10, i5, i34, i43, i38, i44, and i40 were sequentially removed from the analysis. Following the removal of these items, the explained variance ratios were re-examined, and an increase in the total explained variance was observed. For the remaining 33 items, the KMO value was 0.953, indicating excellent sample adequacy, and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was statistically significant (χ² = 12,279.261, df = 528, p = 0.001). These results demonstrated that the dataset was suitable for factor analysis and that the sample size was adequate (Table 2).

EFA results of the scale

The number of dimensions of the scale was determined using eigenvalues and the scree plot. The presence of four dimensions with eigenvalues greater than 1, as indicated by the scree plot, demonstrated that the scale comprised four dimensions (Fig. 1). The total variance explained by the scale was 65.738% (Table 3).

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted using data obtained from an independent sample (n = 448). Three acceptable modification indices (i1–i2, i4–i5, and i6–i7) were identified. The model fit indices were as follows: CMIN/DF = 4.068, χ² p = 0.001, CFI = 0.907, RMSEA = 0.080, RMR = 0.038, SRMR = 0.0483, NFI = 0.881, TLI = 0.900, IFI = 0.908, PGFI = 0.682, and PNFI = 0.811, indicating that the proposed model demonstrated an acceptable level of fit and supported the factor structure of the scale [35, 36]. The qualitative findings informed the development of the initial item pool, which was subsequently evaluated through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. The four-factor structure identified in the quantitative phase reflected the main subthemes derived from the qualitative findings. The relationships among qualitative themes, representative codes, and the final scale dimensions are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

To determine the reliability of the scale, Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients were examined. The Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega values for the “Impact on the Learning Process and Academic Achievement (F1)” subscale were both 0.94. For the “Use and Effectiveness in Clinical Practice Settings (F2)” subscale, the Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega values were also 0.94. The “Collaboration in Clinical Practice (F3)” subscale demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.90 and a McDonald’s omega value of 0.91. Similarly, the “Impact on Professional Development (F4)” subscale yielded a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.90 and a McDonald’s omega value of 0.91 (Table 4). For the “Attitudes Toward the Use of Information Technologies in Psychomotor Skill Learning Scale (IT-PSLS)” administered to nursing students, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.94 and the McDonald’s omega coefficient was 0.94. These values indicate that the scale demonstrates high reliability. Split-half reliability analysis was conducted with 17 items in the first half and 16 items in the second half, as assigned by the software. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.93 for the first half and 0.94 for the second half. The Spearman–Brown correlation coefficient was 0.898, and the Guttman split-half coefficient was also 0.898 (Table 4).

As a result of the measurement invariance analysis across gender groups, the configural model was examined first, and the model fit indices (CFI=0.880, RMSEA=0.067, SRMR=0.0576) were found to be at acceptable levels. This finding indicates that the factor structure was similar across both groups. In the metric model, changes in the fit indices (ΔCFI=0.000, ΔRMSEA=-0.001) and the chi-square difference test [Δχ²[29] = 37.205, p=0.141] were not statistically significant. These results demonstrate that factor loadings were equal across gender groups, confirming the establishment of metric invariance. In the scalar model, the changes in fit indices (ΔCFI=-0.001, ΔRMSEA=0.000) were minimal, and the chi-square difference test [Δχ²[33] = 46.424, p=0.061] was not statistically significant. These findings indicate that scalar invariance was achieved, allowing for meaningful comparisons of latent means across groups. In the final step, for the strict model, the ΔCFI value (-0.009) was at the threshold, while the chi-square difference test [Δχ²[36] = 181.254, p=0.001] was statistically significant. Therefore, strict invariance was not fully supported. Overall, the results demonstrate that configural, metric, and scalar invariance were established, whereas strict invariance was not achieved (Table 5).

Discussion

In contemporary nursing education, integrating information technologies into psychomotor skill teaching requires not only technological availability but also an understanding of how students perceive and engage with these environments. Systematic assessment of student attitudes may therefore represent an important component of effective technology integration, offering a structured basis for curriculum design and the improvement of clinical skill education. The integration of the qualitative subthemes related to learning processes and academic achievement into a unified quantitative dimension suggests an important role of information technologies in shaping students’ engagement with psychomotor skill learning. Students’ accounts of pre-class preparation through videos, virtual simulations, and other digital resources suggest a movement toward more learner-centred and self-directed educational structures. This interpretation is consistent with the contemporary nursing education literature emphasizing active, student-centred technology use in clinical skills training [5, 10]. Evidence indicating that simulation-based and digitally supported learning environments enhance knowledge acquisition and retention further supports this interpretation [37, 38]. The perceived flexibility afforded by digital tools—particularly the reduction of time and space constraints—may help facilitate repeated practice and preparation, which are critical components of effective psychomotor skill development. From an educational perspective, these findings suggest that evaluating student attitudes toward technology-enhanced learning may assist educators in designing structured, preparatory, and self-directed components within psychomotor curricula.

The subtheme identified in students’ qualitative accounts regarding the contribution of information technologies to increased self-confidence, effective time management, clinical decision-making, and case/care management was also reflected in the relatively high mean score of use and effectiveness in clinical practice. These findings indicate that technology-based tools may play an important role not only during the learning phase of psychomotor skills but also in the transfer of acquired skills to clinical practice. Studies reporting the positive effects of simulation, virtual reality, and video-supported education on clinical competence, stress management, and patient safety further support this interpretation [7, 39, 40]. In a randomized controlled study conducted by Lin et al. (2024), virtual reality–based education was found to enhance self-confidence and improve the accuracy of clinical decision-making, as well as to positively affect stress management. Similarly, Yavuz et al. (2025) reported that nursing students’ self-evaluation through video review of their own clinical performances significantly improved skill acquisition and increased self-confidence.

Students’ qualitative statements indicating that technology-enhanced learning environments facilitate communication, increase peer support, and enhance interaction with instructors may suggest that students perceive technology-enhanced tools as supportive of social and interactive learning processes. These findings are consistent with studies demonstrating that scenario-based high-fidelity simulation supports teamwork and problem-solving skills [41, 42]. In addition, artificial intelligence–supported virtual reality systems have been reported to improve nursing students’ interprofessional communication skills [43]. In this context, information technologies may not directly determine collaboration in clinical practice but may facilitate and support collaborative learning processes shaped by clinical environment dynamics, team interaction, and interpersonal relationships.

Students’ statements showing improvements in ethical awareness, professional values, and critical thinking skills highlight a broader role of technology-based learning environments in nursing education. Previous studies similarly suggest that technology-enhanced learning environments may be effective in strengthening ethical decision-making, patient safety awareness, and professional responsibility among nursing students [44–46]. A study by Tobias et al. (2025) reported that education based on artificial intelligence and virtual reality may be more effective than traditional methods in strengthening ethical reasoning. Similarly, Gabr et al. (2025) indicated that virtual reality–based education may significantly enhance students’ critical thinking and professional decision-making skills [47]. In this context, the use of information technologies in psychomotor skill learning may create a dynamic space of interaction among critical thinking, ethical sensitivity, and professional awareness, thereby offering a reflective, value-oriented, and learner-centred learning environment that supports professional development.

The coherence between qualitative themes and the quantitative factor structure strengthens the conceptual integrity of the scale and supports its theoretical grounding [26]. This finding is consistent with previous research emphasizing that technology-enhanced learning environments support multiple and interconnected dimensions of learning, including engagement, clinical competence, collaboration, and professional development [37, 39, 41, 45]. The confirmation of measurement invariance further suggests that the instrument may be applicable across diverse student groups. Taken together, the findings suggest that students’ attitudes toward information technologies in psychomotor skill learning may encompass multiple interrelated dimensions, including learning engagement, clinical application, collaboration, and professional development. By offering a structured means of assessing these attitudinal dimensions, the scale may support educators in evaluating and refining technology integration strategies within psychomotor curricula. Beyond its educational implications, the scale may also contribute to the preparation of a nursing workforce that is better equipped to engage with technology-enhanced healthcare environments by supporting the effective integration of digital technologies into psychomotor skills education. Future research may explore the use of this instrument across different institutional and cultural contexts and examine changes in students’ attitudes toward technology-enhanced psychomotor learning over time.

Limitations

This research was conducted with nursing students from two public universities in Türkiye; therefore, the sample was limited to a specific cultural and institutional context, which may limit the generalisability of the results. In addition, data were collected through self-report measures, which may introduce potential biases such as social desirability and response tendencies. The cross-sectional design of the quantitative phase does not allow for examination of changes in students’ attitudes toward information technologies over time. Although the structural validity of the scale was supported, full measurement invariance across groups was not fully supported, suggesting that minor differences in measurement properties may exist. Nevertheless, scalar invariance was supported, indicating that comparisons across gender at the latent mean level may be meaningful; however, comparisons based on observed scores should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study provides a context-specific measurement tool that enables the assessment of nursing students’ attitudes toward the use of technology-enhanced psychomotor skill learning. The findings indicate that students perceive technology-supported psychomotor education as a multidimensional experience encompassing learning engagement, support for clinical practice, collaboration, and professional development. Beyond its psychometric properties, the IT-PSLS holds practical value for nursing education. The scale may be used to assess students’ attitudinal tendencies prior to technology integration, evaluate the perceived effectiveness of digital components within psychomotor courses, and identify areas for improvement in simulation-based or technology-supported clinical skills instruction. Identifying attitudinal strengths and potential barriers may assist educators in adapting teaching strategies to enhance student engagement and support the transfer of psychomotor competencies into safe and effective clinical practice.
