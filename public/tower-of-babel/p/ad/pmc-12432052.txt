Temporal variability in dissolved organic matter composition export in streams
Authors: Most Shirina Begum, Meredith Kadjeski, Christina Fasching, Marguerite A Xenopoulos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExport of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) from freshwater systems has been the focus of many studies owing to its pivotal role in regulating global carbon fluxes and ecosystem function. Both the flux and composition of dissolved organic matter (DOM) are critical for understanding its ecological impact, as similar compositions can have vastly different consequences depending on the magnitude of input and hydrological context. However, very little data exists on the composition of DOM export fluxes to downstream ecosystems. Here we investigate the interaction of water temperature and discharge on DOC and DOM composition export fluxes in two streams draining contrasting watersheds (agriculture versus forested) in southern Ontario, Canada across seasons. Using Generalized Additive Models, we observed that both stream discharge and water temperature significantly affected DOM composition, and the proportion of terrestrial humic-like DOM exhibited strong positive relationship with discharge. Although DOC loads were comparable between the two streams, the export loads and fluxes of DOM composition (in terms of fluorescent loads and fluxes) differed significantly. These patterns of DOM composition fluxes in both streams remained consistent across seasons, suggesting that watershed characteristics and nutrient availability primarily govern DOM dynamics and export, while seasonal drivers such as discharge and temperature further modulate these patterns. Export loads and fluxes of DOM components were higher in spring and winter months compared to summer and autumn in both streams, while fluxes also increased at medium (Q10-Q90) and high flow (> Q10) at a variable extent in the contrasting streams. Introduction

In aquatic ecosystems, the predominant form of organic carbon is dissolved organic matter (DOM), which consists of a complex mixture of compounds influenced by their sources. Due to the linkage of streams with their surrounding terrestrial environment, interactions within watersheds influence the amount (dissolved organic carbon; DOC) and composition of DOM in surface waters (Battin et al. 2008; Fasching et al. 2019; Fuß et al. 2017; Tank et al. 2018; Williams et al. 2010; Wilson & Xenopoulos 2009). Agricultural catchments can contribute higher DOM loads than pristine catchments, not due to greater allochthonous (terrestrial) input in the traditional sense, but because of increased hydrological connectivity, soil disturbance, nutrient inputs from fertilizers, and DOM inputs from crop residues and soil microbial activities (Wilson & Xenopoulos 2009; Fuß et al. 2017). These sources tend to produce more labile, protein-like DOM that is readily mobilized during runoff events, especially in spring and summer (Fasching et al. 2016, 2019). In contrast, forested catchments typically export more humic, aromatic DOM derived from leaf litter and soil organic matter (Wilson and Xenopoulos 2009; Fasching et al. 2016). Thus, watershed land use plays a crucial role in shaping the export loads and fluxes of DOC concentration and DOM composition.

The DOC concentration and DOM composition can widely vary around the year due to seasonal processes, such as spring snowmelt and autumn leaf fall, and variables influenced by seasonality like discharge (Fasching et al. 2016; Inamdar et al. 2011; Singh et al. 2014) and temperature (Zhou et al. 2018; Shang et al. 2018; Wen et al. 2020). Seasonal variability in DOM in surface waters across the globe is regulated by processes that alter the supply, transport, and consumption of carbon and other nutrients throughout the year. Thus, seasonal changes significantly affect watershed biogeochemistry, shaping stream metabolic processes and affecting downstream export of DOM (Brooks et al. 2007; Coble et al. 2022; Fasching et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2019; Shang et al. 2018; Wilson et al. 2013). Recent studies have shown that spatiotemporal patterns provide valuable insights into DOM quantity and composition in inland water systems (Begum et al. 2019, 2022). While land use plays an important role shaping the composition of DOM in streams, hydroclimatic factors such as discharge and temperature significantly influence the export flux of DOM to downstream river (Bhattacharya & Osburn 2020, 2021; Ji et al. 2023; Roebuck Jr et al., 2019). Warmer temperatures and elevated nutrients can enhance microbial processing of DOM leading to compositional shifts toward more labile, autochthonous-like DOM (Wilson et al. 2013; Hotchkiss et al. 2015). With the changing climate, variation in discharge patterns (e.g., increased frequency of extreme weather events) and temperature (e.g., warming waters) can impact DOM processing in stream and downstream export. Thus, understanding these relationships is crucial for predicting how aquatic ecosystems will respond to ongoing environmental changes and for improving both regional and global carbon budgets.

It is well recognized that aqueous exports of organic matter and nutrients are important to downstream ecosystems (Wilson et al. 2013). Large loads of nutrients and organic matter are transported annually from terrestrial to aquatic ecosystems (Drake et al. 2018; Raymond et al. 2016). Anthropogenic alterations within catchments can affect exports of DOM and nutrients into aquatic ecosystems, with potentially significant consequences for regional carbon dynamics (Chen et al. 2021; Huotari et al. 2013; Pisani et al. 2020; Spencer et al. 2013). Most carbon export studies have emphasized the quantity or DOC concentration, while studies on DOM composition remains comparatively limited (Fasching et al. 2016; Chifflard et al. 2024). However, the composition of DOM export fluxes can affect internal processing of freshwater DOM, impacting carbon sequestration and outgassing, nutrient cycles, and ecosystem-level respiration and production (Xenopoulos et al. 2021). While DOM composition offers insight into the nature of organic inputs, it does not, on its own, reflect the magnitude of a change or disturbance. For example, a modest increase in labile carbon due to intensified agricultural activity or seasonal low flow may resemble, in composition, a much larger influx from sources such as urban wastewater or major land-use change. Yet, the ecological consequences can differ greatly: larger inputs of labile DOM may significantly alter downstream microbial carbon processing and ecosystem function. Similar to DOC concentration, a higher flux of labile DOM — for instance, delivered by a larger stream — is likely to exert a greater influence on the receiving water body than a smaller flux from a tributary, even if their compositions are similar. Therefore, both DOM composition and flux have the potential to serve as integrated tracers and contribute meaningfully to water quality monitoring. Exploring the DOM composition exports can provide valuable insights into human-induced alterations to carbon transport in aquatic ecosystems. Urban and agricultural catchments may export greater labile and microbial-like DOM, ultimately affecting the biogeochemistry of downstream ecosystems (Williams et al. 2010).

In this study, we investigate how seasonal shifts in hydrology influence nutrient levels and the export of DOM composition in two catchments with contrasting land use. The two streams, with comparable DOC, differ in several key characteristics, including the intensity of agricultural land use, riparian vegetation cover, erosion of streambank, and nutrient dynamics. This framework allows us to test our hypotheses that (1) nutrients, water temperature, and discharge influence DOM composition export fluxes and (2) seasonal changes in DOM composition fluxes are more pronounced in the agriculture stream than the forest stream.

Methods

Site description

We investigated two streams located in Simcoe County, Ontario, Canada, over the course of eight months in 2018. These streams were selected for comparison since they showed similar DOC concentration over long term monitoring. One stream, Coldwater Creek, is bordered by coniferous and deciduous forest and riparian vegetation, leading to high shade cover (hereafter termed ‘forest stream’) (44.58751, − 79.6655), and the other is Penville Creek, surrounded by grassland with little riparian vegetation (hereafter termed ‘agriculture stream’) (44.13008, − 79.7274). Both streams have sandy sediments, though the agriculture stream has steep banks due to high levels of erosion. In the catchment of the agriculture and forest streams, total cropland percentages were 47.8 and 1.5% and rural land use was 38.1 and 2.2%, while wetland percentages were 0.3 and 23.1% and forested land was 8.3 and 58.2%, respectively (Williams et al. 2016). The watershed area differs substantially between the two streams, with a larger area drained into the agriculture stream (59 km2) compared to the forest stream (23 km2) ("Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources and Forestry," 2019). The annual average air temperature was 7 °C at both sites ("Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources and Forestry," 2019). Monthly mean air temperatures range from − 9 °C (agriculture) and − 8 °C (forest) in January to 20–21 °C in July and August ("Environment and Climate Change Canada," 2018). Annual precipitation during winter 2017 through autumn 2018 was 973 mm at the agriculture site and 824 mm at the forest site ("Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources and Forestry," 2019). During 2018, monthly total precipitation was lowest at both sites in March, June, and September (≤ 37 mm), while monthly total precipitation was highest in April and October at the agriculture site (≥ 148 mm) and April and August at the forest site (≥ 137 mm) ("Environment and Climate Change Canada," 2018).

Sample collection and in situ measurements

We sampled the forest and agriculture streams during daylight hours 26 times in 2018. The sampling seasons were defined into late winter (February), spring (March – May), summer (June – August), and autumn (September – November) in these temperate streams. Sampling mostly took place either weekly (April to August) or every two weeks (September to November), and both streams were sampled on the same day for all 26 sampling events for consistent discharge conditions. Each weekly or biweekly sampling was conducted at a consistent time of the day and each stream was sampled at the same location. Discharge (m3 s−1) was measured using a SonTek FlowTracker handheld Acoustic Doppler Velocimeter. We also measured water temperature (°C), specific conductivity (µS m−1), salinity (ppt), and dissolved oxygen (DO [in %]) using YSI Pro30 and ProODO probes.

We collected water samples from just below the surface in acid washed, sample rinsed plastic bottles, and they were stored in dark at 4 °C until filtering for dissolved nutrients in the laboratory. Unfiltered water samples were stored in 50 mL falcon tubes for total phosphorus (TP) analysis. Water samples for dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) were filtered on site through 0.2 µm Isopore polycarbonate membrane filters and stored at 4 °C in the dark in acid washed and ashed (450 °C for 4 h) airtight 40 mL amber vials until analysis.

Analytical methods

Whole water was filtered through pre-ashed glass microfiber filters (Whatman GF/F; 0.7 µm) followed by Isopore polycarbonate membrane filters (0.2 µm) for DOC, absorbance and fluorescence of DOM, total dissolved phosphorus (TDP), and total dissolved nitrogen (TDN) analysis. Samples were stored in acid washed and ashed (450 °C for 4 h) amber glass vials in the dark at 4 °C except for TDN samples, which were stored at − 20 °C (TDN samples) until analysis. DOC and DIC concentrations (mg C L−1) were measured in a Total Organic Carbon analyzer (Shimadzu TOC-VWP, Columbia, Maryland, USA) following acidification and combustion of filtered samples. Phosphorus concentrations were measured as TP (unfiltered water; µg P L−1), particulate phosphorus (PP), and TDP (0.2 µm filtered water; µg P L−1) after persulfate digestion with the molybdate blue colorimetric assay (APHA, 1992) and a spectrophotometer (Cary-50, Varian, Palo Alto, California, USA). A second derivative spectroscopy method (Crumpton et al. 1992) was used to measure TDN (mg N L−1) concentrations in filtered water samples. Total suspended solids (TSS) were measured on filters after drying and weighing in a microbalance (Mettler-Toledo, Markham, Ontario, Canada). Chlorophyll a (Chl a) was measured using a fluorometer (Cary-Eclipse, Varian) following a cold/dark ethanol 24 h extraction (Marker et al. 1980). An elemental analyzer (Vario EL III, Elementar Mt. Laurel, New Jersey, USA) was used to measure particulate organic carbon (POC) and particulate organic nitrogen (PON).

DOM composition measurements and analysis

DOM UV–visible absorbance and fluorescence characteristics were determined for both streams as described in Williams et al. (2010). Light absorbance was measured from 230 to 800 nm using a Varian Cary 50 Bio UV–Visible spectrophotometer. Fluorescence excitation emission matrices (EEMs) were measured using a Varian Cary Eclipse fluorescence spectrophotometer. Scans were recorded from 230 to 500 nm excitation at 5 nm increments and 270 to 600 nm emission at 2 nm increments and at a scanning interval of 0.25 s. Inner filter effects were corrected following McKnight et al. (2001) and Mobed et al. (1996) and EEMS were normalized to Raman units (RU) using the integrated area of the Milli-Q scatter peak at 350 nm excitation following Williams et al. (2010). From these data, specific UV absorbance at 254 nm (SUVA254), spectral slope ratio (SR) (Helms et al. 2008), β:α ratio (Parlanti et al. 2000; Wilson & Xenopoulos 2009), fluorescence index (FI) (McKnight et al. 2001), and a humification index (HIX) (Ohno 2002) were calculated.

Distinct fluorescent components were extracted from EEMs using parallel factor (PARAFAC) analysis (Stedmon & Bro 2008) with the DOMFluor toolbox in MATLAB R2007b. All the EEMs from this study were fit to an existing PARAFAC model suitable for the study area (Williams & Xenopoulos 2023; Williams et al. 2016), which is also available on OpenFluor for comparison with other published models. Seven components were extracted from the model, and the components were characterized as: C1, ubiquitous humic-like; C2, terrestrial humic-like; C3, terrestrial humic-like; C4, soil fulvic-like; C5, microbial humic-like; C6, anthropogenic, microbial humic-like; and C7, protein-like component (Table S1). The Fmax values are presented in Raman units whereas relative distribution of Fmax is presented as the percentage of Fmax (% Fmax) for each component at each sampling event (i.e., [FmaxC# / ΣFmaxC1-7] × 100%).

Export calculations

We extrapolated average daily flow rate and solute export fluxes from the weekly or biweekly measurements in the stream according to Raymond and Saiers (2010). The yearly and seasonal exports of carbon (DOC and POC), nitrogen (TDN and PON), phosphorus (TDP and PP), and DOM fluorescent components were calculated with the LOADEst model (Runkel et al., 2004). Time-series streamflow data, measured in field or obtained from the nearest Water Survey of Canada metering station, and solute concentrations were used to calibrate a regression model that enabled us to estimate nutrient loads for a weekly sample resolution. Solute loads (g yr−1) were calculated using either measured or interpolated concentrations, and sensitivity analysis were conducted to confirm no substantial bias from the LOADEst model using interpolated values. Constituent fluxes (g m−2 yr−1) were then calculated by normalizing the load to the watershed area upstream of the sampling sites, (i.e., agriculture stream 59.087 km2 and forest stream 23.798 km2). Loads and Fluxes of each PARAFAC component were derived following Fasching et al. (2016). Loads of the fluorescent components in RU m3 yr−1 was derived by multiplying each component with discharge, which was then divided by the watershed area to derive fluxes in RU m yr−1. Furthermore, we used a254 (m−1), discharge, and watershed area to derive loads (m2 yr−1) and fluxes (yr−1) of aromatic DOM.

Statistical analyses

Significant (p < 0.05) differences between the streams in water quality (DO, specific conductivity, salinity, water temperature, and pH), discharge, nutrient concentrations, and DOM composition were determined utilizing the Wilcoxon signed-rank test as implemented in base R (R Core Team 2018). Based on instantaneous flow measurements collected throughout the sampling period (January 1 to December 31, 2018), low flow (Q90) and high flow (Q10) thresholds were determined as the discharge levels met or surpassed 90% and 10% of the time, respectively (Figure S1) (Strauch et al. 2015). The remaining flows were considered medium flow (Q90-Q10). In order to explore the effect of water temperature, discharge, DO, and nutrients on the coupling of DOM composition in both streams, we conducted a canonical correlation analysis (CCA). This analysis calculates a series of canonical functions that best summarize the relationship between two sets of linear combinations of variables. To assess the significance of each canonical correlation, we employed permutation testing using the ‘CCP’ package in R (Menzel 2012). No data manipulations were performed, and collinearity assumptions were met for CCA. A simple CCA was conducted with the global dataset to ensure stability with a smaller sample size (Painsky et al. 2020). Finally, to further explore the effect of most important environmental drivers (i.e., discharge and water temperature) on DOM composition, we used generalized additive models (GAMs). GAMs extend generalized linear models by allowing for flexible, nonparametric relationships between predictors and the response variable. Unlike traditional models, GAMs do not require a predefined functional form, enabling the detection and estimation of nonlinear effects through the use of smoothing functions. Model fitting was carried out using the ‘mgcv’ package in R (Wood, 2011), and all statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 3.5.1).

Results

Water quality, nutrients, and discharge

We found significantly higher DO (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, N = 26, p < 0.001), specific conductivity (p < 0.001), salinity (p < 0.001), and water temperature (p < 0.001) in the agriculture stream compared to the forest stream (Table 1). Concentrations of TSS (p < 0.001), Chl a (p < 0.001), DIC (p < 0.001), and solutes (POC: p < 0.001; PON: p < 0.001; TDN: p < 0.001; TP: p < 0.01; TDP: p < 0.001; PP: n = 25, p < 0.001) were also significantly higher in the agriculture stream than the forest stream (Table 1). Although the average discharge did not differ significantly between the agriculture and forest streams, the flow regimes were different throughout the sampling period (Table 1). The agricultural stream exhibited greater flow variability (0.0173 to 1.4959 m3 s−1, CV = 121), with more pronounced fluctuations in discharge compared to the relatively stable flow observed in the forest stream (0.0954 to 0.544 m3 s−1, CV = 44). Further, low flow (i.e., Q90) and high flow events (i.e., Q10) in the agriculture stream were more prevalent than in the forest stream causing the variation in discharge to be greater (Table 1; Figure S1).

Throughout the sampling period, DOC concentration ranged from 2.36 to 13.69 mg C L−1 in the agriculture stream and from 2.50 to 11.05 mg C L−1 in the forest stream. Average DOC did not show statistically significant difference between the two streams (Table 1); however, DOM composition varied between the streams as expected based on previous studies in these catchments including a network of 43 streams (Williams et al. 2010; Wilson & Xenopoulos 2009). Within this network of streams, forest streams had more allochthonous-like DOM, while the DOM in the agriculture streams had a greater microbial signature (Williams et al. 2010). Consequently, the representative forest and agriculture streams explored at a higher temporal resolution reinstated the higher level of microbial and anthropogenic DOM (i.e., C5, C6, C7) in agriculture stream than the forest stream.

Linking DOM composition to discharge and water temperature

CCA was utilized to explore the association between DOM composition and environmental parameters (discharge, water temperature, DO, and nutrients) in the agriculture and forest streams (Fig. 1). During periods with high nutrient concentrations (TSS, Chl a, TDN, TDP, TP) in spring and summer, DOM composition was more autochthonous-like based on positive correlations with β:α and components C6 and C7 (Fig. 1). Discharge and temperature were inversely related (r = -0.52, p < 0.01) and thus interacted oppositely with DOC and DOM composition (Table S2). Increasing DOC was associated with high water temperature and low discharge in CCA analysis, indicating high autochthonous production of DOM (Fig. 1). These findings indicate that the contributions from autochthonous sources – as reflected by elevated SR and C3 values and low apparent humic fluorescence and aromaticity – were relatively high during periods of warmer water temperatures and lower discharge (Fig. 1). During high-flow conditions, this trend shifted, with greater inputs of humic-like substances and elevated SUVA₅₂₄ values observed (Fig. 1). Higher levels of discharge flushed more terrestrial allochthonous-like DOM into the streams, while lower discharge and warmer water temperatures resulted in an increase in autochthonous-like DOM composition.

To further investigate how the DOM composition of our streams was changing under different seasonal conditions, we applied GAMs to analyze the data. The results of the GAM analyses include spline curves and 95% confidence intervals depicting the relationship between the response variables (i.e., DOM composition) and two predictor variables (i.e., discharge and temperature) (Fig. 2). The humic-like DOM composition (SUVA254 and C1-C3) was positively related to discharge, while microbial-like composition (SR, β:α, C5, and C7) was inversely related to discharge (Fig. 2). The models also showed that discharge increases the relative contributions of C1-C3 relatively faster (higher slope) in the forest stream than agriculture stream, exhibiting a stronger influence on DOM composition in the forest versus agriculture stream (Fig. 2). C3 showed positive relationship with discharge in both streams unlike the negative relationship in universal CCA analysis. Opposing relationships in global CCA vs stream specific GAMs particularly for C3 and C5 can be explained by large confidence intervals and highly variable slopes in GAM plots (Figs. 1 and 2). The models further revealed that water temperature also has a strong effect on both streams, with a smaller contribution of terrestrial humic-like material at higher water temperatures (Fig. 2). While elevated water temperatures increased β:α in the forest stream, they had the opposite effect in the agriculture stream (Fig. 2). Finally, while temperature increased C6 at a similar rate in both streams, it decreased C7 at a faster rate in the agriculture stream (Fig. 2). Overall, we see a shift in DOM composition as water temperatures increase and discharge decreases (i.e., late summer), transitioning from a more allochthonous-like signature to one that is more microbial-like and recent origin.

Carbon export loads and fluxes

While the yearly DOC load (g yr−1) was nearly identical in both streams, we saw a higher flux (g m−2 yr−1) of DOC in the forest stream than the agriculture stream (Fig. 3; Table S3). We also found that the yearly POC load and flux were both higher in the agriculture stream than forest stream (Fig. 3; Table S3). The agriculture and forest streams exported different proportions of carbon (as DOC and POC) at different times of the year (Table S4). The agriculture stream exported a larger proportion of DOC and POC in the winter and spring than the forest stream, while the forest stream exported a larger proportion of DOC in the summer and autumn than the agriculture stream (Table S4). Further, DOC and POC loads exhibited higher seasonal variation in the agriculture stream compared to the forest stream. Despite the differences between the streams, exports of DOC and POC displayed maximum values (up to 80%) in winter and spring and decreased in summer and autumn in both streams (Table S4). Similarly, yearly loads of TDN, PON, TDP, and PP were all greater in the agriculture stream compared to the forest stream (Table S3). Fluxes of TDN, PON, and PP were also higher in the agriculture stream, while the TDP flux was higher in the forest stream (Fig. 3; Table S3). Like the carbon loads, the majority (up to 86%) of the nutrients in both streams were exported in winter and spring (Table S4).

Yearly loads (RU m3 yr−1) of all seven fluorescent components were higher in the agriculture stream (Table S3). On the other hand, area-normalized yearly export fluxes (RU m yr−1) of all components were higher in the forest stream (Fig. 4; Table S3). Loads of the terrestrial humic-like components C1-C3 and anthropogenic-like component C6 were higher than the other components in both streams (Table S3). As with exports of nutrients, most of the fluorescent components were exported in the winter and spring months regardless of stream type (agriculture: 76.1–79.2% of each component; forest: 65.3–71.2% of each component) (Fig. 4a; Table S4). As expected, export loads and fluxes of all seven components increased with higher discharge levels (Fig. 4b). Export fluxes were higher at medium (Q10-Q90) and high flow (> Q10) for all DOM components compared to low flow (< Q90) in both streams. Interestingly, export flux of the DOM components during medium flow exceeded the flux during high flow in the forest stream unlike similar flux in the agriculture stream at medium and high−1 flow (Fig. 4b).

Discussion

While yearly DOC loads did not differ between the agriculture and the forest stream, major differences in the composition of DOM fluxes were determined with variables influenced by seasonality, leading to elevated export rates of nutrients and more labile DOM in the agriculture versus forest stream (Figs. 3 and 4; Table S3). Our study is the first to explore and report the export of DOM composition (in terms of fluxes of fluorescent components) in streams of contrasting land cover across seasons. While comparing the streams, we found higher relative export loads of humic- and microbial-like fluorescence in the forest and agriculture streams, respectively (Table S3). These fluxes varied on a seasonal basis with higher exports in spring and winter compared to summer and autumn. Shifts in the composition of DOM fluxes can influence downstream ecosystems, as both the quality and timing of these fluxes play a critical role in regulating stream metabolism and shaping biogeochemical processes (Battin et al. 2008; Xenopoulos et al. 2021). Seasonal pulses of labile DOM, particularly during storm events, can stimulate microbial respiration and primary production, altering the balance between autotrophic and heterotrophic processes in streams and rivers (Battin et al. 2008; Raymond et al. 2016). This alteration can lead to enhanced carbon outgassing, shifts in nutrient cycling, and modifications of aquatic food webs. Bioavailability of DOM—shaped by both its source and seasonal dynamics—can determine how efficiently carbon is processed or retained within aquatic ecosystems (Begum et al. 2019; Hotchkiss et al. 2015). As climate change intensifies hydrologic variability, understanding the interplay between DOM composition, flux, and ecosystem metabolism becomes increasingly critical for predicting carbon cycling outcomes and managing water quality.

Linking DOM composition to discharge and water temperature

Even with higher variability in discharge levels and DOC in the agriculture stream, both streams exhibited clear temporal shift in DOC and DOM composition in both streams, revealing synchronous behavior despite two very contrasting catchment inland covers and landscape properties. Seasonal dynamics of discharge and temperature, including spring snowmelt and summer drought, can further shape DOC concentration and DOM composition (Brooks et al. 2007; Catalán et al. 2013; Fasching et al. 2016; Lee & Lajtha 2016; Raymond & Saiers 2010). Hydrology has a major influence on sources of nutrients and organic matter (Brooks et al. 2007; Duan et al. 2013; Raymond & Saiers 2010; Raymond et al. 2016). While many previous studies have found that DOC increased with precipitation and discharge (Fasching et al. 2016; Inamdar et al. 2011; Raymond & Saiers 2010; Raymond et al. 2016; Smith & Kaushal 2015) and decreased with increasing temperature (Zhou et al. 2018), we observed increased DOC with lower discharge levels and warmer water temperatures. This positive linkage between DOC and higher temperature was also reported in a study on a temperate river network (Hosen et al. 2021). Lower DOC was associated with decreased production and increase in respiration driving this relationship in another study (Wen et al. 2020). Increase in DOC during low discharge could also be explained by seasonal evaporation (Kienel et al. 2013) or increased autochthonous production during extended periods of low flow in the summer months (Kothawala et al. 2014; Toming et al. 2013). Nonetheless, water temperature is expected to increase DOC concentration and export in pristine and forested streams (Raymond & Saiers 2010; Shang et al. 2018), but we did not observe a positive relationship between DOC and terrestrial allochthonous-like DOM in our study stream, likely associated with the less pristine nature of our study catchments (Williams et al. 2016).

Our results show that the DOM composition transitioned from more allochthonous-like in spring to more autochthonous-like and freshly-produced in late summer in both catchments consistent with Wilson et al. (2013). Though terrestrial humic-like fluorescence was most prominent in all DOM samples, microbial- and protein-like fluorescence became more pronounced throughout the summer and autumn owing to enhanced primary production with higher temperature and lower discharge compared to other seasons. As the streams returned to baseflow after spring snowmelt recession and organic matter production and degradation increased across the spring and summer months, terrestrial inputs of organic matter decreased and became less important relative to in-stream production of DOM (Lee & Lajtha 2016; Singh et al. 2014; Wilson et al. 2013). This is evident by elevated microbial-like fluorescence and increases in SR and β:α values, plus a decrease in humic-like fluorescence, apparent aromaticity, and humification. Alternatively, it is also possible we are seeing a relative increase in protein- and microbial-like fluorescence as the terrestrial inputs from the surrounding catchment decrease after the spring snowmelt.

The shifts in DOM composition and exports were driven by seasonal and temporal variability, but the influence of these drivers was modulated by hydrology. The proportion of DOC contributed from terrestrial sources (vs. in-stream) increased with streamflow, altering the DOM composition. The increases in SUVA254 and humic-like fluorescence (and subsequent decreases in SR, β:α, and microbial protein-like fluorescence) we saw during high-flow events represents a flush of terrestrial solutes similar to that observed in previous studies (Fasching et al. 2016; Lee & Lajtha 2016; Liu et al. 2019; Raymond & Saiers 2010). This further suggests that terrestrial sources of carbon become more important with increasing flow levels, whereas in-stream organic matter may be more important at lower streamflow (Raymond & Saiers 2010; Smith & Kaushal 2015). At low flow, values of SR and β:α increased while SUVA254 decreased, which suggests a shift in DOM sources from allochthonous to autochthonous. This pattern was further supported by elevated contributions of microbial- and protein-like fluorescence. Baseflow during the summer months enhanced the microbial-like composition of DOM by inducing biological processes related to temperature (Fasching et al. 2016; Kaplan & Bott 1982; Raymond & Saiers 2010; Singh et al. 2014). Low discharge and increased water temperature led to increased in-stream production, resulting in freshly produced organic matter (Fasching et al. 2016; Kaplan & Bott 1982; Singh et al. 2014). As a result, water DOM composition varied among the seasons as the relative contribution of allochthonous and autochthonous inputs changed.

Carbon export loads and fluxes

We observed nearly identical yearly DOC loads similar to DOC concentration in both streams. However, there were clear differences in the composition of carbon transported by the two streams (Tables S3 and S4). There was also higher variation in the exports in the agriculture stream in agreement with previous studies (Graeber et al. 2012). In addition, while taking into account watershed area, the annual flux of DOC and fluorescent components was significantly higher in the forest stream (Fig. 4). Because of the variation in discharge, the estimated seasonal loads and fluxes fluctuated widely, and the majority of DOC export occurred in winter and spring – at the beginning of the hydrological year – in both streams. Similarly, export loads and fluxes of DOM components were higher in winter and spring, whereas export of allochthonous DOM exceeded autochthonous DOM in both streams across the seasons. These results may indicate that organic matter and nutrients transported during the spring snowmelt (in addition to large precipitation events) may be a crucial source of carbon, phosphorus, and nitrogen to the stream ecosystem regardless of the contrasting catchments cover (Brooks et al. 2007; Duan et al. 2013; Raymond & Saiers 2010; Raymond et al. 2016).

The estimated yearly area-based flux of DOC from the forest stream in this study was comparable to that reported previously in two Central European headwater streams (Graeber et al. 2012). However, the yearly flux of DOC from our agriculture stream was much lower than what was found previously in European agriculture catchments (Graeber et al. 2012). Further, our DOC yields were low compared to those reported for other rivers worldwide, which may be explained by the geographic location of the study, stream order, and specific catchment land use (Table S5) (Bhattacharya et al., 2021; Graeber et al. 2012; Giling et al., 2014; Leech et al. 2016; Fasching et al. 2016). For example, in rivers in the United States examined by Spencer et al. (2013), only two had similar DOC yields to that of our study. However, this study focused on downstream river sites near the mouths of major United States rivers (Spencer et al. 2013). Similarly, the DOC flux in our streams was below the range of fluxes measured in the Baltimore-Washington D.C. metropolitan area (Smith & Kaushal 2015) and small, forested watersheds in the northeastern United States (Raymond & Saiers 2010). Finally, in a study examining pristine headwaters of an Alpine stream, Fasching et al. (2016) found a much higher DOC export flux and DOM component export fluxes compared to our results (Tables S3 and S5). Yields of the humic-like DOM in the Alpine headwater stream was much higher than the agriculture and forest streams in this study. Yields of microbial (C5 and C6) and protein-like (C6) components were lower than the humic-like components in the agriculture and forest streams in this study as well as the protein-like components in the Alpine streams (Fasching et al. 2016). Similarly, we found that the export loads and fluxes of a254 in both agriculture and forest streams were much lower than the aromatic DOM values reported in the U.S. rivers and Alpine streams, consistent with their respective DOC exports (Table S5) (Fasching et al. 2016; Leech et al. 2016; Spencer et al. 2013).

DOC and DOM composition fluxes in both streams exhibited larger export of humic-like components across the seasons compared to their microbial-humic and protein-like counterparts highlighting the synchronous behavior of DOM characteristics in catchments with variable land use (Fig. 4) and consistent with our previous DOM biodegradability work in the same streams (Kadjeski et al. 2020). DOM composition export fluxes were higher in winter and spring than summer and autumn for all the components; however, the variation in fluxes between agriculture and forest streams were more distinct in humic like components owing to the higher exports of humic-like components in the region. DOM export fluxes were strongly influenced by discharge, with flux patterns closely tracking changes in flow, and this led to consistently elevated humic substance exports throughout the year (Fasching et al. 2016). Extreme flood events (i.e., > Q10) affected export fluxes of DOM composition in forest and agriculture stream at a variable extent. While the largest fraction of humic-like components (C1-C3) in forest stream were exported at medium flow, the agriculture stream exported similar level of humic-like DOM during medium and high flow. On the other hand, agriculture stream exported a slightly larger fraction of microbial humic-like at high flow than medium flow whereas forest stream exported largest fraction of all DOM components at medium flow (Fig. 4b). Also, at extremely low flow (Q < Q90), exports of DOM components in the agriculture stream were minimal (< 0.6%) compared to the forest stream (2–4%) suggesting synergistic effects of hydrology and land use on the DOM export particularly at extreme conditions. While many previous studies reported larger export of DOC and POC at higher discharge (Brooks et al. 2007; Fasching et al. 2016, 2019; Inamdar et al. 2011), our findings highlights the importance of spring snowmelt in exporting allochthonous terrestrial DOM particularly the humic-like fractions of DOM.

Conclusions

Our study provides the first report of DOM composition export fluxes (in terms of fluorescent components) across land covers and seasons. To date, limited focus has been given to quantifying and reporting the composition of these fluxes although they are essential for the biogeochemistry of downstream ecosystems influencing the release and storage of carbon, shaping nutrient dynamics, and driving ecosystem metabolism. Our study identifies catchment characteristics and seasonal processes that have a large impact on the amount and composition of nutrient and DOM export fluxes to downstream ecosystems. The results highlight that although contrasting catchments may export similar loads of DOC, the fluxes of DOC and DOM composition can differ substantially, corresponding to seasonal variations in stream hydrology and climate. Shifts in natural hydrologic patterns are expected with climate change, leading to more frequent and intense high-flow events and likely increasing terrestrial export fluxes. This will have exacerbated consequences for agricultural areas exporting high loads of nutrients and labile DOM. Alterations in the lability and chemical structure of DOM can significantly impact watershed biogeochemistry with key consequences for in-stream processes and the transport of bioavailable DOM to downstream ecosystems.
