Dynamic anticounterfeiting and tunable lasing with screen-printed cholesteric liquid crystal elastomers
Authors: Guangyin Qu, Lulu Guo, Xiaojuan Zhang, Siqi Li, Yan Kuai, Weiwei Fu, Tianyu Yang, Jialin Wen, Benli Yu, Zhijia Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis research presents a scalable screen-printing strategy for fabricating dynamic structural-color graphics using cholesteric liquid crystal elastomers (CLCEs). The printed patterns exhibit circularly polarized reflection, mechanochromism, and multistimulus-responsive behavior, while enabling UV-tunable random lasing. Demonstrating broad substrate compatibility, this approach offers a versatile platform for advanced anticounterfeiting technologies and flexible photonic devices.

Colors in nature embellish this beautiful world and play a vital role. Colors can be divided into two categories according to the coloring mechanism, pigment-based coloration and structural coloration (1, 2). Pigment-based coloration depends on the absorption of incident light by organic or inorganic pigment molecules, and the mechanism of color development originates from the jumping behavior of electrons between specific energy levels in the molecules. When light waves are applied to pigment molecules, some wavelengths of light are selectively absorbed, and the unabsorbed components of the spectrum are reflected or transmitted to form visible colors. However, these colors are susceptible to photo-oxidation and molecular degradation, and there is a risk of fading. The production process is often accompanied by environmental pollution problems. Structural color rendering is achieved through the physical interaction of the microstructure of a material with light through interference, diffraction, or scattering, with the color originating from the precise modulation of photonic crystals (PC) or periodic nanostructures. These materials form a photonic bandgap (PBG) through a periodic distribution of dielectric constants, and light at specific wavelengths is selectively reflected by Bragg diffraction, resulting in highly saturated structural colors (3, 4). Compared with pigmented colors, structural colors have excellent resistance to photobleaching and long-term color stability, which is determined by the nonchemically bonded PC structure (5, 6). In biology, structural colors are widely found in insects (e.g., butterfly wing scales), birds (e.g., peacock feathers), marine organisms, and plant epidermis, and their color rendering functions are often associated with survival behaviors such as courtship and camouflage. Notably, some organisms (e.g., chameleons) can dynamically change epidermal nanostructures through mechanical stress or physiological regulation to achieve environmentally responsive color change, a phenomenon that reveals the unique potential of structural colors in dynamic optical regulation (7–9).

Currently, the patterning strategies of PCs mainly include photolithography, digital inkjet printing, and screen printing (10–12). Among them, photomask technology (13) is limited by the high cost and low-throughput characteristics of mask template preparation, which makes it difficult to meet the demand for industrial-scale production. Although digital inkjet printing technology has the advantages of high-resolution and noncontact processing, its practical application still faces significant challenges (14, 15). The rheological properties of nanoparticle inks are prone to nozzle clogging, and lattice defects caused by solvent volatilization during the printing process can reduce the precision of PBG regulation. In contrast, screen printing, as a mature graphic transfer process, has demonstrated excellent process compatibility and large-scale production feasibility in traditional textile printing (16, 17). The technical principle is based on squeegee-driven precursor ink patterning through holes in the screen to achieve selective deposition of the ink layer on the substrate surface. With its simplicity of equipment, low operating costs, and high adaptability to complex patterns, this technology is considered to have significant industrial prospects in the development of PC functionalization. If the high-precision patterned preparation of PC structural colors is achieved by optimizing the ink formulation and printing parameters (e.g., mesh density and squeegee pressure), it will provide an efficient solution for the industrial application of PC structural colors (18).

Cholesteric liquid crystals (CLCs), as a class of smart soft photonic crystalline materials with periodic helical superstructures (19), derive their unique optical properties from the self-assembled layered helical arrangement of molecules (20). These materials not only selectively modulate the circularly polarized light output in the visible band by Bragg Reflection but also respond to a variety of external stimuli (e.g., mechanical stress, temperature, electric field, light field, magnetic field, and changes in the chemical environment) to achieve reversible modulation of the dynamic PBG and structural color (21–23). This feature shows significant potential for applications in adaptive optics, dynamic camouflage systems, and new flexible display technologies (24). In the CLC’s system, molecules form a Layered Helical Superstructure by self-assembly, and there is a fixed Twist Angle between adjacent molecular layers, which constitutes a periodically aligned helical axis. Pitch is defined as the axial distance corresponding to 2π radians of rotation of the molecular pointing vector along the helical axis, and it is the core parameter for determining the Bragg reflection wavelength (λ = n⋅p) (where n is the average refractive index and p is the pitch). Based on PC theory, CLCs reflect only circularly polarized light in the same direction as the helical chirality and transmit reverse-polarized light, a property that provides a physical basis for the design of circularly polarized optics devices (25–27).

With the development of material functionalization technology, CLCs can be prepared by microfluidics (28, 29), in situ polymerization (30), and microencapsulation (31) into a variety of forms, including microscale LC cells, LC microspheres, LC polymer composite films, LC elastomers, and flexible fibers. However, for practical application scenarios, how to achieve high-throughput printing and complex pattern customization of CLC-based functional inks is still a key scientific challenge that restricts their industrialization (32, 33). Specifically, several critical challenges must be addressed to advance this technology: 1) the development of CLC precursor inks that exhibit both robust stability and a tunable chiral pitch; 2) the refinement of printing methodologies to preserve the orientational integrity of the helical superstructure throughout manufacturing; and 3) the co-optimization of dynamic optical performance and mechanical endurance. Resolving these impediments is fundamental to bridging the gap between fundamental research and the full-fledged engineering application of CLC-based photonic devices.

Here, we introduce a manufacturable screen-printing route to CLC elastomers (CLCEs) that converts process-imposed shear into deterministic mesoscale helix alignment for soft PCs. This study delivers three primary advances. First, we demonstrate shear-mediated preorientation and defect annihilation during solvent-evaporation–driven self-assembly, which suppresses evaporation-induced cracking and topological disorder and yields coatings with markedly improved PBG homogeneity over the printed area. Second, we establish scalable, full-color pitch programming in printed elastomers by tuning the chiral dopant loading (and thus the effective helical twisting power), enabling multi-ink patterning from blue to red with high saturation, high reflectance, and broad substrate compatibility. Third, we implement a printed CLCE–spiropyran (CLCE–SP) architecture that couple polarization-selective structural color with multistimulus optical switching (mechanical deformation, light, and heat) and enables ultraviolet (UV)-programmable random lasing with continuously tunable emission across 645 to 685 nm via photoisomerization-controlled gain and effective refractive index. Collectively, these advances address key limitations of patterning cholesteric photonic materials—namely scalability, defect control, and functional integration—and position screen-printed CLCEs as a versatile platform for high-security anticounterfeiting and flexible photonic devices.

Results

Shear-Enabled Defect Suppression and Bandgap Homogeneity.

A central challenge in patterning cholesteric elastomers is maintaining long-range helical order while preventing the stress-driven cracking and spatial bandgap nonuniformity commonly produced during solvent evaporation. In our screen-printing process, the squeegee-induced shear field prealigns the liquid-crystal mesogens prior to full solvent removal; subsequent evaporation then proceeds from a more uniformly oriented state, reducing Marangoni-driven disorder and stress concentration. As a consequence, the printed CLCE coatings exhibit continuous, crack-free morphology with substantially lower defect density and improved spatial homogeneity compared with drop-coated counterparts, which show multiscale topological defects and macroscopic crack networks. This shear-assisted pathway establishes a robust foundation for high-brightness, polarization-selective structural color with high coating integrity across large-area printed patterns.

The CLC inks are prepared by mixing (1,4-bis(4-(3-acryloyloxypropyloxy) benzoyloxy)-2-methylbenzene (RM257), ((3R,3aS,6aS)-hexahydrofuro[3,2-b] furan-3,6-diyl bis(4-(4-((4- (acryloyloxy) butoxy) carbonyloxy) benzoyloxy)benzoate) (LC756), 2,2-(ethylenedioxy) diethanethiol (EDDET), Triethylamine (TEA), Pentaerythritol Tetra(3-mercaptopropionate) (PETMP), and 2-Hydroxy-4′-(2-hydroxyethoxy)-2- methylpropiophenone (I-2959) in organic solvent (Fig. 1A). RM257 serves as a reactive mesocrystal and functions as the liquid crystalline host within the CLCE ink system. LC756 is employed as a chiral dopant to induce a chiral architecture, leveraging its high helical twisting power (HTP, βM ≈ 7 μm−1) (34, 35). EDDET acts as a chain extender in the formation of a main-chain liquid crystal elastomer (LCE), while PETMP serves as a crosslinking agent. TEA catalyzes the thiol–acrylate Michael addition reaction, and I-2959 is utilized as a photoinitiator for the subsequent photopolymerization of CLCE coatings. At room temperature, the resulting CLCE ink is a colorless transparent liquid (Fig. 1B).

The typical “red” CLCE ink is formulated by mixing the liquid crystal monomer RM257 (2.264 g), the chiral dopant LC756 (0.104 g), the chain extender EDDET (0.549 g), the crosslinker PETMP (0.120 g), the catalyst TEA (0.038 g), the photoinitiator I-2959 (0.016 g), and the solvent tetrahydrofuran (1.608 g). Subsequently, the prepared CLCE inks are then drop-coated onto the substrate surface, and after drop-coating, the reactive liquid crystal radicals (mesogenic monomer) and chiral dopants spontaneously organized to form the cholesteric phase structure during solvent evaporation-induced self-assembly, while the thiol-acrylate Michael addition reaction started. The coated films are subsequently placed in a fume hood for 2 h to allow complete evaporation of residual solvent. Finally, UV irradiation is applied to trigger photo-crosslinking (Fig. 1C). Initially, the freshly drop-coated film appears colorless. As solvent evaporation proceeds, the coating gradually develops a red color with increasing saturation. A schematic illustration of the solvent evaporation mechanism is provided in Fig. 1D.

During the initial stage of solvent evaporation, the ink coating appears colorless, indicating a disordered arrangement of the LC molecules. Solvent evaporation induces a localized surface tension gradient, which triggers Marangoni flow (35, 36) (indicated by the blue arrow in Fig. 1D). This flow arises from surface tension differences within the liquid phase, generating convective motion from regions of lower surface tension toward those of higher tension. The resulting Marangoni flow exerts shear traction on the LC molecules, promoting their directional alignment along the flow trajectory. Along with the continuous volatilization of the solvent, the ink viscosity increases significantly, leading to the fixation of the flow trajectories of the LC molecules. During this process, the molecules self-assemble into a cholesteric helical structure directed by the chiral dopant. Since the helical axis is parallel to the coating plane (especially in the top/bottom interface region), the structure produces Bragg reflection for specific wavelengths of light, resulting in a macroscopic red color. When the system viscosity reaches a critical value, Marangoni flow ceases, though a small amount of solvent remains. The solvent evaporation-induced self-assembly process continues until the residual solvent has completely evaporated, ultimately completing the curing of the LC phase (36).

Representative photos of the CLCE film prepared by drop-coating are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1. The surface morphology of CLCE coatings exhibiting structural colors after solvent evaporation is characterized by optical microscopy (Fig. 1E). Results revealed that CLCE coatings prepared via the drop-coating method exhibited significant spatial inhomogeneity and multiscale topological defects. Owing to uneven internal stress distribution, such films readily develop macroscopic crack networks during the late stages of solvent evaporation, which act to release shrinkage stresses generated during curing (37). The drop-coating process is competitively governed by multiple nonequilibrium kinetic factors—evaporation rate, capillary convection, and Marangoni flow—resulting in highly nonuniform morphology, high defect density, and poor reproducibility in the resulting CLCE films. Consequently, this method is generally unsuitable for applications that require stringent optical performance (38). In contrast, screen-printing technology (Fig. 1F) introduces a shear rheological field that promotes preorientation and alignment of the LC molecules. When combined with controlled solvent evaporation, this approach enables the fabrication of highly uniform, structurally ordered CLCE coatings with excellent integrity (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The superior surface morphology achieved through screen printing is essential for producing high-brightness, highly uniform structural colors, demonstrating potential for practical applications in fields such as optical anticounterfeiting and flexible photonic devices. Reflectance measurements further show that the screen-printed CLCE coating reaches a reflectance of 39% within 50 s (Fig. 1G). Following UV polymerization, the coating maintains high reflectance and uniform structural color.

Screen-printing technology has become a key method for the microstructural design of CLCE optical inks, enabling precise patterning of cholesteric-phase PCs and tailored modulation of their optical properties. This technique employs a precision screen mesh to achieve controlled deposition of high-viscosity CLCE inks, with the resulting film thickness being synergistically determined by the mesh aperture as well as squeegee parameters such as angle and pressure (39). The film-forming quality in screen printing is intrinsically linked to the rheological properties of the ink, with viscosity being a particularly critical parameter. Ink viscosity serves as a key process variable that governs both pattern resolution and printing consistency. When the ink viscosity is excessively low, its heightened fluidity and propensity to spread induce a series of defects. Primarily, this condition readily leads to bleeding or blurring, where the ink flows uncontrollably from the image areas into the nonimage areas of the screen under the squeegee pressure, resulting in blurred edges and general print contamination. Furthermore, in the replication of fine halftone dots, it causes abnormal dot gain and geometric distortion, which consequently compromises tonal reproduction accuracy and leads to a significant loss of detail (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A, Viscosity below 2,000 to 4,000 mPa s). Conversely, excessively high ink viscosity, characterized by poor leveling and strong cohesive forces, triggers a different set of quality issues. The most prevalent failure is screen clogging, a phenomenon where the highly viscous ink cannot be effectively transferred through the screen mesh via the shear action of the squeegee. This results in a markedly reduced ink transfer rate, directly manifesting as printing defects such as insufficient ink deposition, pinholes, and broken lines, thereby preventing the formation of a continuous and uniform ink film (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C, Viscosity higher than 10,000 to 15,000 mPa s). The precise regulation of ink viscosity is a defining factor in screen-printing process window, directly enabling the formation of defect-free, highly uniform functional films and fundamentally eliminating surface defects arising from a mismatch in rheological properties (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B, Viscosity 4,000 to 12,000 mPa s).

Notably, the central wavelength of the selectively reflected structural color from the CLCE ink is primarily determined by the concentration of the chiral dopant LC756. This study achieves patterned printing of multiple structural colors by precisely controlling the mass fraction of LC756 in the ink. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, when the mass fraction of LC756 relative to the total mesogen content, WLC756/(WRM257+WLC756), is set to 5.3 wt%, 4.9 wt%, 4.7 wt%, 4.5 wt%, and 4.3 wt%, the resulting CLCE coatings exhibit blue, green, yellow, orange, and red structural colors, respectively. Fig. 2B presents the corresponding surface morphology of the screen-printed CLCE coatings. In contrast to the drop-coated films (Fig. 1E), the screen-printed patterns display highly uniform surface topography, long-range mesoscopic order, significantly reduced defect density, and an absence of macroscopic cracks. This technique effectively suppresses the coffee-ring effect and mitigates internal stress concentration by combining shear-induced preorientation of the LC molecules with controlled solvent evaporation kinetics. As a result, it enables the construction of high-quality PC coatings, which form the basis for highly saturated and uniform structural colors, while demonstrating excellent patterning capability and practical applicability. The reflectance spectra of the printed patterns reveal central peak wavelengths of 490, 534, 577, 619, and 683 nm (Fig. 2C). The chromatic characteristics of these colors are further represented in the CIE 1931 x-y chromaticity diagram (Fig. 2D).

Additionally, screen-printing technology further enables high-precision patterned deposition of CLCE inks onto a variety of two-dimensional planar substrates (Fig. 2E)—including metals, glass, nitrile rubber, silicon wafers, PMMA, and PC sheets—as well as porous or fibrous materials such as paper (Movie S1). By tailoring the ink’s rheological properties, the process ensures strong interfacial adhesion to nonporous substrates while minimizing capillary-driven overpenetration into porous media. Thereby, it enables the successful fabrication of CLCE functional coatings with vivid structural color and highly consistent mesoscopic ordering on substrates with divergent physicochemical properties. This capability highlights the technology’s broad application prospects in fields such as flexible photonic devices and smart packaging. Furthermore, the printed patterns demonstrate exceptional long-term stability. After storage under room temperature conditions for a period of up to 10 mo, the patterns not only retained their vivid structural color without significant fading or chromatic shift but also maintained excellent dimensional integrity, with no observable shrinkage, expansion, or deformation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). These results confirm that the fabricated patterns possess remarkable resistance to environmental aging and are capable of preserving their original optical properties and geometric morphology over extended periods, underscoring their reliability and durability for practical applications.

Angle-dependent and circularly polarized patterns for anticounterfeiting.

Screen-printed multicolor structural patterns represent a significant advancement, extending the application of structural colors from static decoration to dynamic functional systems (40). The key advantage of this approach stems from its physical coloration mechanism, which arises from the periodic mesoscale structure of PCs rather than from chemical pigments or dyes. This structural origin imparts dynamic optical responses, high anticounterfeiting security, and intelligent sensitivity to external stimuli (41, 42). As a result, these materials show considerable potential for high-security anticounterfeiting, smart packaging, and next-generation flexible photonic devices.

The dynamic evolution of evaporation-induced self-assembly is monitored in a screen-printed CLCE ink layer patterned as a “rose” (Fig. 3A). As depicted in Fig. 3B, the freshly printed wet film initially exhibits no structural color due to the disordered state of the liquid crystal molecules in the presence of excess solvent. With progressive solvent evaporation, the molecules undergo shear-induced alignment and gradually organize into a helical superstructure, leading to the emergence of a red structural color that intensifies in saturation over time as the photonic lattice becomes more ordered. By systematically varying the concentration of the chiral dopant LC756 in the ink, rose patterns that selectively reflect red, orange, yellow, green, cyan, and blue structural colors are successfully produced (Fig. 3C). A dual-color rose image is further constructed by spatially patterning the ink formulations—using red or green for the petals and green or blue for the leaves—showcasing the method’s capability for complex multicolor design (Fig. 3D). Notably, the printed photonic patterns exhibit not only selective reflection at specific wavelengths but also distinct right-handed circular polarization characteristics, a hallmark of the chiral nature of the assembled mesophase. Under a right-circularly polarized (RCP) filter, or under naked eye observation, the pattern displays vivid rainbow colors resulting from the constructive interference of RCP light (Fig. 3E). In contrast, when viewed under a left-circularly polarized (LCP) filter, the structural color nearly vanishes, confirming that the reflected light is predominantly RCP. This optical behavior originates from the right-handed helical configuration of the cholesteric mesophase, which is directly induced by the molecular chirality of the dopant LC756 incorporated into the CLCE ink system.

We demonstrate an anticounterfeiting strategy employing screen-printed CLCE photonic inks to fabricate a two-dimensional barcode (QR code) with red structural color (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The CLCE material possesses a periodic helical superstructure formed by molecular self-assembly, which gives rise to a stimulus-tunable PBG. Under natural light, the printed QR code displays a defined structural color. When the viewing angle is varied, however, the Bragg-reflected wavelength shifts according to the angular dependence of the PBG, leading to a pronounced color change. This viewing-angle-dependent optical behavior is inherently challenging to replicate using conventional printing methods, thereby substantially improving the authentication reliability and distinctiveness of the anticounterfeiting label. The approach shows broad applicability in high-end product authentication, secure documentation, and encrypted information storage.

Mechanochromic Response and Strain Visualization.

Mechanically induced color-changing CLCE patterns can be developed by directly printing CLCE ink onto an elastomer substrate, such as Nitrile Butadiene Rubber and PDMS film. Under direct stretching (e.g., uniaxial), intrinsically stretchable systems employing common mechanochromic materials often experience heterogeneous strain distributions. This inhomogeneity, charactrized by more pronounced strain near the clamping points than in the central regions, leads to inconsistent color shifts across the device (Fig. 4A). To minimize the impact of uneven stress distribution between the edges and center of the sample during stretching, this experiment printed the CLCE ink pattern onto the central region of the film to investigate the uniform effect of tensile stress on the optical behavior of the pattern.

(C) Photograph of the printed “至誠至堅博學篤行” pattern and its color changes during stretching. (Scale bar: 1 cm.) (D) Reflectance spectra of printed patterns under different strains. (E) Color trajectory of the printed pattern in response to increasing strain levels displayed in the CIE 1931 color space.

When conducting a macroscopic study of the printed CLCE pattern, the optical dynamics induced by stretching reveal only a color change. The CLCE in this work functions not only as a direct strain sensor through visual cues but also as a medium for dynamic reflective patterns. This color variation is governed by the de Vries condition (43), which correlates the perceived color to changes in the material’s chiral pitch.Δλ=n×Δp,

where Δλ is the difference in the wavelength of maximum reflectance of initial and stretched states of the CLCEs, n is the average refractive index of CLCE components, and Δp is the difference in the chiral pitch of initial and stretched states of the CLCEs (Fig. 4B).

Under uniaxial tensile stress, the CLCE coating modulates its reflected color by altering its internal pitch. To validate this property, the phrase “至誠至堅博學篤行” is printed onto a black nitrile rubber substrate. Tensile tests revealed that as strain increased, the pattern color underwent a continuous blue shift—transitioning from red through green to blue (Fig. 4C). This color change synchronized with mechanical deformation without observable delay, and the entire process proved fully reversible. To quantitatively investigate the mechanical color-changing behavior of the CLCE coating, we acquired its reflectance spectra under natural light. Results indicate that the reflection peak continuously shifts from 638 nm (0%) to 480 nm (140%) as elongation increases (Fig. 4D). The macroscopic color change of the CLCEs in Fig. 4C is quantitatively validated by confirming the track of the reflective color changes of the uniaxially stretched CLCE in the International Commission on Illumination 1931 (CIE 1931) color space (Fig. 4E). The x and y color coordinates of the CIE color space are calculated using the reflection spectrum. It functions as a uniaxial strain function and demonstrates fully reversible characteristics. These results confirm that CLCEs can be used in mechano-optical sensors to visualize the applied strain.

Printed CLCE–SP: Multistimulus Switching and UV-Programmable Random Lasing.

Photochromic materials have gained considerable interest in high-end anticounterfeiting applications owing to their reversible light-responsive behavior and straightforward visual verification (44, 45). These systems exhibit distinct, reversible color changes when exposed to specific wavelengths of light, enabling active authentication mechanisms that combine intuitive readability with enhanced security. Among them, SP—a classical photochromic molecule—is widely employed in the development of next-generation anticounterfeiting inks and smart optical labels due to its rapid photoresponse, pronounced spectral shifts, high color contrast, and excellent fatigue resistance (46). Upon UV excitation, SP undergoes a structural transition from a closed SP form to an open merocyanine (MC) configuration, resulting in a visible color change from colorless to magenta (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). This process is reversible under visible light or thermal relaxation, allowing the system to revert to its initial state (47). Such reversible photochemical characteristics offer an efficient and user-friendly approach to anticounterfeiting verification.

The molecular structure of SP features two primary ring systems—a six-membered pyran ring and a five-membered spiro ring—linked by a covalent bond to form a closed, spiro-conjugated architecture. In the absence of UV irradiation, SP generally exists in a colorless or pale-yellow closed form. Upon ultraviolet photoexcitation, the molecule undergoes heterolytic cleavage of the C–O bond, initiating ring-opening of the spirocyclic system. This photochemical transformation converts the spiro carbon from sp3 to sp2 hybridization, yielding the open-ring MC isomer (48). The MC form adopts a planar, zwitterionic geometry with an extended π-conjugated system, which narrows the energy gap between the highest occupied molecular orbital and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (49). Consequently, a bathochromic shift occurs in the absorption spectrum, producing a strong visible absorption band that macroscopically manifests as a vivid purple-to-magenta color. After removal of the UV source, the MC form can revert spontaneously to the original SP state through visible-light-induced cyclization, allowing rapid, controllable color switching and resetting of the molecular state (47).

Beyond its photochromic properties, we note that the open-chain isomer of spiropyran—MC—exhibits a typical conjugated molecular structure (Fig. 5A). Similar to common organic luminescent groups, it should demonstrate excellent intrinsic fluorescence emission characteristics (50). The fluorescence emission of SP originates from the electronic transition behavior of its conjugated structure in the MC form. Upon absorbing photon energy, the ground-state MC undergoes photoexcitation and transitions to an excited state. This excited state is primarily deactivated through radiative relaxation pathways, resulting in fluorescence emission (51).

As shown in Fig. 5B, we characterized the fluorescence spectral changes of the SP ring-opening isomer MC under different intensities of UV irradiation. The figure demonstrates that as the UV irradiation intensity increases, the fluorescence intensity of MC significantly enhances, accompanied by a pronounced red shift in the fluorescence emission peak position. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the increased photoisomerization conversion rate from SP to MC within the system as UV intensity rises. This conversion leads to an increase in the system’s effective refractive index, consequently causing the red shift in the fluorescence emission peak (52).

Subsequently, a composite stimulus-responsive ink comprising CLCE and SP (CLCE+SP) is printed onto the surface of a curved ceramic vase substrate (Fig. 5C, I) to systematically evaluate its potential for applications in anticounterfeiting and smart displays (Movie S2). In its initial state, the functional coating is colorless and transparent (Fig. 5C, II and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Under UV excitation, the SP molecules undergo photoisomerization from the closed-ring form (SP) to the open-ring MC form. Due to the fluorescent emission properties of the MC form, the coating exhibits bright red fluorescence (Fig. 5C, III and SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). After removal of UV light, the material displays a purple hue (Fig. 5C, IV) under ambient light due to incomplete recovery from the MC to the SP state. Further thermal stimulation at 80 °C (Pour hot water into the ceramic vase) induces a visual color transition to pink (Fig. 5C, V). Heating to 80 °C accelerates thermal interconversion within the SP/MC system and shifts the isomer/conformer population toward less strongly absorbing states (including thermally promoted MC→SP ring closure in the polymer environment), thereby reducing the net visible absorption and producing a bleached pink color. We note that because the coating is printed on a rigid ceramic substrate, macroscopic thermal deformation of the CLCE network is constrained; therefore, the observed thermochromism is dominated by SP/MC absorption changes rather than a large thermally induced shift of the cholesteric Bragg band. This multistimulus responsiveness (light/heat), coupled with reversible color/fluorescence changes, demonstrates promising applications in advanced anticounterfeiting and dynamic optical coatings.

In the interdisciplinary field of smart photonics and soft matter materials, the development of stimulus-responsive tunable lasers represents a significant and enduring research direction (53). Among various material systems, CLCEs have emerged as an ideal platform for constructing dynamic photonic devices, owing to their periodic helical self-assembled structure and remarkable responsiveness to external stimuli such as light, heat, and mechanical force (54). Furthermore, by incorporating photochromic molecules like SP into the CLCE matrix, the resulting composite system enables precise modulation of the PBG and optical properties through photoisomerization (22).

To characterize the lasing properties of the flexible CLCE+SP device, ultraviolet irradiation is applied to the patterned surface on a ceramic vase to investigate the dynamic changes in its emission spectrum. The experimental setup for laser characterization is illustrated in Fig. 5D. A Q-switched Nd: YAG laser operating at a wavelength of 532 nm, with a pulse width of 6 ns and a repetition rate of 10 Hz, is used as the pump source. The pump energy intensity is controlled via a Glan-laser prism pair. The pump beam is split into two paths by a beam splitter, one is monitored in real time using a power meter, and the other is focused onto the sample for excitation. The axial emission from the CLCE+SP sample is coupled into an Ocean Optics QE65PRO spectrometer (resolution: 0.4 nm, s) for spectral acquisition, while a second beam is directed to a CCD camera to observe the laser spot profile.

Fig. 5E shows the variation of the emission spectra from the sample under UV irradiation at different power levels. As the UV power increases below 4 mW, only fluorescence emission is detected. When the UV power reaches 4 mW, random lasing emerges with a central wavelength of 645 nm. A further increase in UV power leads to a continuous red shift in the lasing emission wavelength. At 10 mW, the peak emission wavelength shifts to 685 nm. This behavior is attributed to the progressive photoisomerization of SP to MC within the CLCE+SP system under increasing UV intensity. The photoisomerization increases the material’s refractive index, resulting in a red shift of the lasing wavelength. The threshold for the sample is 65 µJ/cm2 when the UV light intensity is 8 mW (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). At UV power levels below 4 mW, the optical gain remains insufficient to reach the lasing threshold, and only spontaneous fluorescence is observed. By modulating the UV power, effective tuning of the lasing wavelength is demonstrated. These results confirm the potential of the CLCE+SP composite for applications in wavelength-tunable lasers.

The emergence and tuning of random lasing in the printed CLCE–SP coating can be understood as a gain–feedback interplay regulated by SP photoisomerization. Upon UV exposure, SP converts to the ring-opened MC form, which serves as the optical gain medium through its fluorescence emission. At the same time, the CLCE matrix provides distributed optical feedback via multiple scattering associated with mesoscale refractive-index inhomogeneities and microstructural variations inherent to a crosslinked, printed soft photonic network. In this context, lasing is “random” because feedback is not dominated by a single resonator; rather, it arises from recurrent scattering paths that effectively increase the photon dwell time until the net gain exceeds loss. As UV intensity increases, the MC population rises, which i) increases the available optical gain and ii) modifies the effective refractive index of the composite. This refractive-index increase shifts the spectral conditions for emission and feedback to longer wavelengths, consistent with the observed continuous red-shift of the lasing peak from 645 to 685 nm as UV power is raised. The threshold behavior follows naturally: Below ~4 mW UV, the MC fraction (and thus gain) remains insufficient to overcome scattering and absorption losses, so only spontaneous fluorescence is detected; once the MC concentration crosses a critical level, the system reaches the random-lasing threshold and exhibits spectrally narrowed emission that can be continuously tuned by adjusting UV irradiation.

Discussion

In summary, this work successfully demonstrates a high-fidelity screen-printing strategy for fabricating multicolor, stimulus-responsive structural color patterns based on CLCEs. This technique effectively overcomes the limitations of conventional methods, such as lattice defects and poor scalability, by enabling precise control over the mesoscopic helical superstructure and ensuring excellent spatial homogeneity of the PBG. The resulting CLCE coatings exhibit vibrant, highly saturated structural colors, distinct viewing-angle-dependent color switching, and selective circularly polarized reflection. The versatility of this approach is further highlighted by the incorporation of photochromic SP molecules, which impart dynamic, multistimulus responsiveness to the printed patterns. The resultant CLCE+SP composite system exhibits reversible color and fluorescence changes under light and thermal stimuli. More significantly, we demonstrate the realization of a wavelength-tunable random lasing based on a screen-printed CLCE+SP coating, where the lasing emission wavelength (645 to 685 nm) can be dynamically and continuously modulated by controlling UV excitation intensity.

The performance of our screen-printed CLCE platform can be positioned within the current landscape of dynamic photonic materials for anticounterfeiting applications (55–57). However, traditional printing techniques struggle to achieve controlled patterning and functional integration of nanostructures due to issues such as strong shear forces, rapid drying, and susceptibility to the coffee ring effect, thereby limiting their application in large-area, high-throughput fabrication (14). In contrast, our screen-printing method utilizes an industrially established process to fabricate large-area, defect-free patterns while maintaining precise control over the cholesteric helical pitch (SI Appendix, Table S2). Unlike stimuli-responsive cholesteric systems that typically emphasize a single mode of response—for instance, mechanochromic CLCE coatings (58) or phototunable CLCs (59)—our platform integrates multiple coexisting dynamic features within a single printed layer, including angle-dependent structural color, circular polarization selectivity, mechanochromism, and photochromism. Moreover, although lasing in soft matter has been previously demonstrated (60) and photochromic molecules have been employed to modulate lasing emissions (50), the implementation of a UV-tunable random laser in a scalable printed coating—where the same photochromic moiety functions simultaneously as a dynamic color switch and a tunable gain medium—has seldom been reported. This multifaceted functionality, together with excellent substrate compatibility and manufacturing readiness, distinguishes our work as a substantial step forward toward practical, high-security, and multifunctional photonic devices.

This study establishes screen-printed CLCEs as a robust and scalable platform for advanced photonic applications. The combination of facile fabrication, excellent substrate compatibility, and sophisticated optical functionalities paves the way for their practical implementation in high-security anticounterfeiting, smart packaging, flexible displays, and next-generation tunable photonic devices. Future work will focus on enhancing the environmental stability of the coatings and exploring the integration of other functional dopants to expand the repertoire of stimuli-responsive behaviors.

Materials and Methods

Material Preparation.

The diacrylate reactive mesogen (RM257) and chiral dopant (LC756) were obtained from Daken Chemica. The chain extender (EDDET) and the catalyst (TEA) were purchased from Aladdin. The mercaptan crosslinker (PETMP), the photoinitiator (I-2959), and all solvents are obtained from Macklin.

Synthesis of Printable CLCE Inks.

The printable CLCE ink was formulated with chemical compositions detailed in SI Appendix, Table S1. The primary components included the diacrylate mesogen RM257, the chiral dopant LC756, the chain extender EDDET, and the photoinitiator I-2959. A representative synthesis of the red CLCE ink was conducted as follows: RM257 (2.264 g), LC756 (0.104 g), EDDET (0.549 g), and PETMP (0.120 g) were dissolved in tetrahydrofuran (1.608 g). Subsequently, TEA (0.038 g) was added as a catalyst to initiate the Michael addition reaction. Finally, I-2959 (0.016 g) was incorporated to complete the ink preparation, rendering it ready for use.

Characterizations of the CLCE Coatings.

The structural color coatings were imaged by EOS R50 Canon digital cameras. POM images were taken with Leica DM4 P for microscopic observation. The reflection spectra were recorded using an Ocean Optics QE65PRO spectrometer. The simulation was performed using COMSOL software.
