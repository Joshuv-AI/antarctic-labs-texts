Representation in Medicare’s Value-Based Payment Models
Authors: Aidan Crowley, Qian (Erin) Huang, Erkuan Wang, Marcella Alsan, Austin S. Kilaru, Joshua M. Liao, Amol Navathe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study characterizes beneficiaries who received care through Medicare’s largest voluntary payment models from 2013 to 2022.

Introduction

Over the past decade, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) have tested over 50 value-based payment models. These models attempt to improve care for Medicare beneficiaries by holding clinicians and health care organizations accountable for patients’ cost or quality outcomes.

Most CMS models have been voluntary. Nonrandom participation can lead to systematic selection based on anticipated financial gains. Therefore, clinicians and organizations who opt in and the beneficiaries they treat may not reflect the overall Medicare population, a dynamic noted for several models, including those that emphasize health equity.1,2,3,4,5 Selective participation may impair generalizability of model results, particularly if the goal is to scale models to all beneficiaries.

It is therefore important to examine whether patients who received care through CMS models have differed from the broader Medicare population. However, no study has done so at the beneficiary level with integrated participation data across multiple major models. Our objective was to describe the characteristics of beneficiaries who received care through any of Medicare’s largest voluntary models from 2013 to 2022.

Methods

This cross-sectional study followed the STROBE guidelines and was deemed exempt from review and informed consent by the University of Pennsylvania IRB because of the use of deidentified data. Analysis was conducted from November 2025 to April 2026.

In a repeated cross-sectional analysis, we used a random 20% sample of Medicare fee-for-service beneficiaries from 2013 to 2022. Within each year, we defined 2 groups: beneficiaries attributed vs not attributed to any value-based payment model. We included CMS’s 8 largest voluntary episode-based and population-based models spanning multiple clinical conditions and model types (eMethods in Supplement 1). We determined participation status using Center for Medicare and Medicaid Innovation Model Data Sharing Model Participation Data,6 Accountable Care Organization beneficiary files, Bundled Payments for Care Improvement enrollment files, and Medicare claims (to identify bundled payment episodes). For all beneficiaries in each year, we described race, ethnicity, dual eligibility, and residence in rural or disadvantaged areas using data from the Master Beneficiary Summary File (2013-2022), 2020 Neighborhood Atlas (zip code in top 2 deciles of Area Deprivation Index), and 2020 Rural-Urban Commuting Area codes (small town or rural). We calculated proportions of beneficiaries with each characteristic across all model years at the beneficiary-year level. Given our large sample size, we used standardized mean differences (SMDs) to compare characteristics between beneficiaries receiving care in each (or any) model and those not receiving care under any of the 8 models. Since the large size of the Shared Savings Program (SSP) could drive results, we also compared beneficiaries in any model except SSP with those in no model. We considered an SMD greater than 0.1 to be a meaningful difference.7 Analysis was conducted with SAS Enterprise version 8.5 (SAS Institute).

Results

Our sample included 17 047 884 Medicare beneficiaries and 115 328 866 beneficiary-year observations (mean [SD] age, 71.1 [11.5] years; 53.9% female and 46.1% male). Beneficiary-years attributed to any model, compared with no model, were more likely among White beneficiaries (83.3% vs 72.2%; SMD, 0.27) and less likely among Black (7.4% vs 11.2%; SMD, −0.13), Hispanic (4.3% vs 10.0%; SMD, −0.22), or dual-eligible (15.7% vs 20.9%; SMD, −0.14) beneficiaries or those residing in disadvantaged areas (13.2% vs 17.2%; SMD, −0.12). When excluding SSP, these differences became more pronounced, and a difference in rural residence emerged (4.9% vs 11.3%; SMD, −0.13) (Table 1). In comparisons between each model and no model, similar patterns emerged with respect to race, ethnicity, dual eligibility, and area-level disadvantage. Except for SSP, each model tended to have a smaller proportion of rural beneficiary-years compared with those not attributed to any model (SSP SMD, −0.02; all other models SMD, ≤0.1) (Table 2).

Discussion

Beneficiaries who received care through voluntary CMS payment models tended to be White, not dual eligible, and residents of less disadvantaged areas. These results advance prior findings from individual models, establishing that beneficiaries who received innovations in care delivery through voluntary value-based payment models have differed from those who did not. These differences may be due to selective participation in payment models by clinicians and health care organizations with greater projected financial gains, which may be correlated with beneficiary characteristics.8,9,10

In addition to posing a threat to generalizability of model results, our findings of systematic differences between participants and nonparticipants also challenge the internal validity of such experiments. Sociodemographic differences between beneficiaries treated by participants vs nonparticipants may confound attempts at accurate causal comparisons, which policymakers should anticipate in the design of future models.

Limitations of this study include its focus on a subset of CMS models, which means that the no-program group may include a small number of beneficiaries attributed to smaller or disease-specific value-based models. Nevertheless, our findings may help improve the generalizability and rigor of demonstration projects as CMS increases its emphasis on mandatory models and designs future models to include more safety-net and rural clinicians and organizations. Future efforts are necessary to determine whether these models are effective at improving quality and cost-efficiency across all populations.
