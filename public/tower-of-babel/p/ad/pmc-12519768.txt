Accelerating birth equity using collaborative systems mapping
Authors: Sharita R. Thomas, Hiba Fatima, Jessica Simon, Vijaya Hogan, Kristen Hassmiller Lich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecognizing the complexity of cross-sector collaboration, holistic and innovative approaches are required to achieve birth equity. This project applied systems thinking and the Remove, Repair, Remediate, Restructure, and Provide (R4P) framework to understand and address systemic barriers to birth equity. This paper describes the systems thinking activities used to identify system actors and critical actions they can take to advance birth equity.MethodsWe applied systems thinking tools through the R4P lens in a series of collaborative and iterative activities. We conducted an environmental scan and synthesis of reports with recommendations to improve birth equity. In addition, we engaged a diverse group of birth equity actors, including those from federal agencies, state and local health departments, community-based organizations, funders, academic institutions, health systems and national nonprofits, through systems mapping workshops to co-create a series of birth equity tools.ResultsOur collaborative efforts produced an innovative Birth Equity Ecosystem Map, Birth Equity Iceberg, and interactive Birth Equity Action Map. The Birth Equity Ecosystem Map is a comprehensive synthesis of recommendations defining an equitable ecosystem, while the Birth Equity Iceberg summarizes insights on systemic barriers to achieving birth equity most critical to address in the next 5 years. The Birth Equity Action Map is an interactive and strategic tool, synthesized from diverse system actors’ experiences and perspectives, that describes needed actions and responsible actors for each systemic barrier identified. We probed for and organized actions by the five dimensions of the R4P framework to encourage comprehensive action to achieve equity. The Birth Equity Action Map and connected resources developed through this project help actors identify actions they can work on to overcome identified systemic barriers to birth equity in their community and key partners for each.ConclusionsThe tools we developed demonstrate how systems thinking and R4P can offer a more holistic and effective approach to tackling issues of birth equity and lead to more actionable insights, thereby contributing significantly to the public health goal of achieving equity. These tools can be used by birth equity advocates to assess and leverage their strengths, resources and unique context to identify priorities and collectively advance birth equity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-025-01373-1.

Background

Birth outcomes for birthing people and infants in the United States from 2000 to 2020 for People of Colour (POC) have been unacceptable [1–3]. Despite having advanced medical knowledge and technology, the United States has significantly worse maternal and infant outcomes compared with other high-income nations, suggesting that systemic inequities drive poor outcomes [4]. For example, compared with white birthing persons, POC are more vulnerable to adverse maternal outcomes [5]. Black birthing persons face higher risks for maternal mortality, being more than twice as likely to die during any perinatal period than white birthing persons [6, 7]. American Indian birthing persons face the highest risk of non-pregnancy-related maternal mortality, meaning deaths from causes such as substance misuse or homicide, than any other racial or ethnic groups [8]. For Hispanic birthing persons, mortality from maternal causes, likely exacerbated by socioeconomic inequities, increased by 44% from 2019 to 2020 [9]. Asian birthing persons faced the highest rates of admission to the intensive care unit, uterine rupture and unplanned hysterectomy in a study of low-risk pregnancies [10]. Among infant outcomes, sudden infant death syndrome rates increased inexplicably for Black infants [11]. For infants born preterm, Black infants are more likely to die than are white infants [12]. Even among low-risk infants born at term, Black infants face a higher risk for infant mortality [10].

If birth equity then refers to the assurance that all individuals have a just opportunity to experience optimal maternal and infant health outcomes, it is clear that past and current strategies to address these disparities are not working. We posit that a more systemic understanding of the entire birthing ideal state, or so-called birth ecosystem, is critical for identifying appropriate leverage points and coordinated action opportunities to achieve optimal outcomes, and is thus part and parcel of birth equity. Interventions need to make more efficient use of existing resources and partners to identify and address systems barriers to change.

Much of the existing research to understand disparate outcomes among racial and ethnic groups focuses on healthcare access and quality [13–15]. However, birth equity research acknowledges a need to expand the lens beyond individual behaviour and specific clinical interventions to include the influence of systems, specifically illuminating how aspects of systems structures and behaviours perpetuate structural racism, creating and maintaining health inequities [16–20]. Many systems influence infant and maternal health outcomes, including clinical healthcare, geography and physical environment, and the workforce and economy [21]. Notably, place of residence influences infant and maternal health outcomes through factors such as proximity to healthcare resources, residential racial segregation, investment in communities and healthcare practitioner scope of practice laws [22–25]. Economic development, reproductive rights and income inequality also interact to drive poor infant and maternal health outcomes [26–28]. Systems thinking theory suggests that identifying and mapping these and other elements of complex systems can reveal relationships and points within the system where small efforts can produce significant changes [29]. Identifying these so-called leverage points is important as they help to motivate and engage system actors and inform and sequence actions [30].

Individual champions or lone organizations have traditionally carried the weight of advancing birth equity in their community. To achieve equity, the social, economic and environmental obstacles to health must also be removed. As there are strong interdependencies between factors that impact health, the work of any one organization or sector intersects in fundamental and complex ways with other organizations and sectors. Until all organizations in a community develop a shared understanding of the systemic barriers to birth equity and work collaboratively to overcome them, equitable outcomes will not unfold or be sustained in populations. Establishing a shared vision for birth equity can identify and align actions, engaging multiple actors across sectors in coordinated investment to systemic change [31]. In addition, birth equity funders, both private and federal, have expressed a need for this overarching understanding of the interactions among current partners, investments, long-term outcomes, social determinants of health, unaddressed needs, anti-racist strategies, and ongoing community, local, state, and national initiatives. Given these complexities, this project aimed to develop systems mapping tools to accelerate the work of communities, coalitions and funders to help them strategically assess their efforts in the context of the broader system, identify collaboration opportunities, and commit to specific actions and investments to strengthen the birth equity ecosystem. In this paper, we introduce our guiding frameworks, including systems thinking and the Remove, Repair, Remediate, Restructure, and Provide (R4P) framework. We then describe our methods and the three culminating products: the Birth Equity Ecosystem Map, Birth Equity Iceberg and Birth Equity Action Map and discuss implications for organizations, coalitions and funders committed to advancing birth equity.

Methods

Aims

This project aimed to integrate existing birth equity frameworks, recommendations, practice-based priorities and evidence-based approaches to create a shared vision that can inform investment and action to address the broader systemic barriers to improved and equitable outcomes. We explored how systems thinking and the R4P framework could be utilized to holistically understand and advance birth equity. This first required documenting the characteristics of a well-functioning birth equity ecosystem. An ecosystem map supports a collective and constructive approach to addressing public health challenges. Next, we aimed to identify important systemic barriers to birth equity using the Systems Thinking Iceberg Model, which we summarized in our Birth Equity Iceberg. Lastly, we created a Birth Equity Action Map to visualize the urgent actions and leverage points needed to address the identified barriers to change.

Ethics approval

This study was reviewed by the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Institutional Review Board no. 233210 and determined not to be human subjects research.

Participants and recruitment

Our recruitment strategy targeted a wide range of birth equity partners, including individuals from federal, state, and local health agencies; community-based organizations; funding programs; academic institutions; nonprofits; advocacy organizations; and health systems. Initial outreach was conducted via email to a broad range of organizations that represented these perspectives as well as diverse focal areas (preconception health, birthing experience, postpartum health, maternal health, infant health, child health, and/or young adult health) and approaches to advancing birth equity (clinical care, health behaviours, social and economic supports, policy and systems change, and/or physical environment). Invitees were asked to indicate if they or someone from their organization was interested in participating in the systems mapping workshops and to share the invitation with any partners they felt would be valuable to include. All invitees were offered a small gift for participation in both workshops, and those from community-based organizations and family/youth leaders were offered a $250 stipend for their participation. This snowball recruitment strategy allowed us to convene a comprehensive and diverse range of perspectives, experiences and approaches to collectively explore birth equity challenges and opportunities and co-design systems mapping tools to advance birth equity (see Supplementary Material for a workshop participant summary).

Frameworks, setting and design

Our process employed a methodological approach rooted in the Systems Thinking Iceberg Model and the R4P framework [32–34]. The R4P framework guided our analysis and intervention strategies by delineating five domains critical to addressing major sources of health equity: Remove (identifying and eliminating racism, sexism, and classism in institutional structures and practices), Repair (addressing historical injustices and their lasting impacts on health equity), Remediate (mitigating current exposures affecting health equity), Restructure (revising institutional structures to prevent future inequities), and Provide (implementing actions and policies designed to address the specific needs of disparity populations while considering systemic disadvantages).

Simultaneously, the Systems Thinking Iceberg Model informed our understanding of the root causes of complex health and social challenges. This model distinguishes between visible events and patterns and the underlying systemic structures and mental models that drive them. By applying this model, we aimed to identify the systemic behaviours, structures and paradigms that perpetuate birth disparities to discover more impactful actions to accelerate birth equity.

We incorporated a series of iterative activities, including a systematic literature review, a two-part national systems mapping workshop, review of recommended recent sources that have called for specific actions to advance birth equity and feedback sessions with key partners to co-create systems mapping tools to advance birth equity (Fig. 1). We asked participants to use R4P as a frame to expand their thinking to ensure they identified as many factors as possible specifically related to inequities and not just determinants of overall outcomes. It is common practice to revert to identifying and addressing only factors related to overall outcomes when there may be unique factors that contribute to inequities in birth outcomes. The virtual participatory research design was chosen to facilitate wide collaborator engagement, ensuring a comprehensive and inclusive exploration of birth equity. Literature review

A comprehensive literature review formed the foundation of the Birth Equity Ecosystem Map. This involved an exhaustive search of existing reports, peer-reviewed articles and community-generated documents that offer insights into the systems’ needs, flaws and recommendations for enhancing maternal and infant health equity. The literature review process was iterative, including broadening our search to include various channels such as Google search strings and email outreach to experts, applying qualitative analysis to identify salient themes.

From the literature search, we also identified a minimum of 10 robust examples from each category: peer-reviewed literature, organizational literature and place-based reports, for a total of 39 sources. We meticulously chose these examples to embody the diversity and depth of each category. We then used an inductive, grounded approach to identify salient codes or themes present within the chosen literature. This process of qualitative analysis allowed us to develop an integrative codebook which served as our central tool for further analysis.

We substantiated each code or theme with direct quotes from the identified sources, corroborating our identified themes and adding depth to our understanding of them. Building upon this foundation, we initially sorted strategy and recommendation codes into themes on the basis of a framework developed in maternal child health work of Wake County, NC, United States [35]. As we progressed, we created new themes to capture strategy and recommendation codes that were not addressed elsewhere. We then expanded the iterative coding process to include additional documents, continually extracting and integrating significant quotes. This approach allowed us to develop an integrative framework that encapsulates the diversity and depth of the existing knowledge on maternal and infant health equity.

Systems mapping workshops to advance birth equity

Critical to our study was the engagement of actors across the birth equity spectrum. Through systems mapping workshops, we facilitated discussions among actors to share insights, experiences and strategies for improving birth equity. This collaborative process enabled us to identify key systems actors and activities impacting birth equity, applying systems thinking tools through the R4P lens to analyse and address these dynamics. The engagement of actors not only informed the development of our birth equity tools but also fostered a sense of collective ownership and commitment to advancing maternal and infant health equity.

Our team of researchers and support facilitators brought diverse credentials and experience in systems thinking, health equity and maternal and child health. The researchers’ professional backgrounds, gender and experience played a crucial role in the project’s approach, with a focus on creating as balanced and inclusive a research environment as possible. Specifically, all the authors identify as cisgendered women, and three of these authors identify as people of colour. As such, our methodological orientation centred around Systems Thinking and the R4P frameworks, guiding the selection and engagement of participants from various sectors to ensure representative voice and to develop a comprehensive birth equity action map. Data collection was primarily conducted through systems mapping workshops, facilitating actor participation from diverse locations and enhancing the inclusivity of the collected data. This approach, informed by our theoretical frameworks and collaborative methodologies, aimed to provide a multidimensional understanding of maternal and infant health disparities and identify actionable solutions to address these challenges.

In the two-part national Systems Mapping Workshops to Advance Birth Equity, the facilitation team led participants through a series of systems thinking activities to achieve the following objectives: (1) create a shared understanding of the birth equity system that centres racial equity, (2) identify factors that facilitate birth equity and racial equity at a community, state and federal level and (3) develop action ideas for where systems actors can have the most impact with their dollars. Both workshops were 4 hours in length, held on Zoom and utilized Google Slides and Miro to facilitate participant interaction and engagement. The workshops were held approximately 2 months apart.

Prior to workshop 1, participants were asked to complete an open-ended survey utilizing the 5R’s framework to capture perspectives on the key results, roles, relationships, rules and resources within the birth equity system [36]. The 5Rs framework is a systems thinking tool to highlight and assess the results, roles, relationships, rules and resources of a system [37]. In workshop 1, participants developed collaboration agreements, shared their hopes and fears related to advancing birth equity and/or the systems mapping process, reviewed and provided feedback on the synthesized 5 R’s survey themes (Additional File 1) and the draft Birth Equity Ecosystem Map, and used the Iceberg Model for Systems Thinking to identify underlying system structures and mental models maintaining birth inequity [33, 34, 38]. In workshop 2, participants developed a Brag Wall of their birth equity successes, shared Gives (resources, expertise or guidance they can share with others) and Gets (resources, expertise or guidance they are in need of) related to their work to advance birth equity, and the facilitation team shared the updated Birth Equity Ecosystem Map and a first draft of the Birth Equity Iceberg for feedback and discussion [39]. The remainder of the workshop was spent in breakout rooms with similar organizations grouped together to brainstorm action ideas to advance birth equity by system actor and R4P domain.

Following the second workshop, the interactive Birth Equity Action Map was developed on the basis of participant insights and key participant-recommended sources. The map was shared with participants via email with an invitation to sign-up for small group or individual feedback sessions to share reflections and suggestions on the Birth Equity Action Map, Birth Equity Ecosystem Map and/or the Birth Equity Iceberg. The facilitation team held 11 feedback sessions with a total of 22 participants.

Birth Equity Ecosystem Map components

The definition of birth equity, as put forward by Joia Adele Crear-Perry, MD, Founder and President of the National Birth Equity Collaborative, is “the assurance of the conditions of optimal births for all people with a willingness to address racial and social inequities in a sustained effort”[15].

Research and practice clearly support the need to promote health and wellbeing across the life course to improve outcomes in maternal and infant health. We conceptualized birth equity across the life course to include infant, child and youth health; preconception, prenatal, postpartum and maternal health; the birthing experience; partner health; and overall family and community wellness [37–39].

The innermost circle represents interrelated health stages and outcomes that contribute to achieving birth equity to centre our understanding of the birth equity system on these key stages/outcomes throughout the life course. To achieve these health outcomes, four key system structures were identified and are defined in Table 1: policies, clinical care, physical environment, and social and economic supports, which are visualized in the middle ring. Underpinning these and other important domains are cross-cutting system characteristics and mental models needed to achieve birth equity. These relate to the structural underpinnings of the ideal system and the critical assumptions, beliefs and values that would create and maintain an equitable system, and they are visualized in the outer ring. Each of the diamonds represent a strategy/recommendation code that emerged from the literature review.

The second tool, the Birth Equity Iceberg (Fig. 2), captures insights on the barriers that perpetuate inequitable outcomes and limit efforts to advance birth equity. This tool synthesizes over 200 notes brainstormed in the systems mapping workshops into observed events, primary and contributing trends, system structures, and mental models that are barriers to systemic change. The Birth Equity Iceberg helps to define and understand challenges from a systems perspective, which is the first step towards collectively strategising to shift structures and transform mental models.

Lastly, the Birth Equity Action Map (Fig. 2) was developed using the interactive mapping platform Kumu to describe 49 urgent actions needed by specific system actors in the next 5 years to improve birth and racial equity. It also highlights where funders can strategically leverage their resources to accelerate birth equity. The actions were grouped into eight overarching action themes described in Table 2. Discussion

The systems mapping tools we co-created with diverse cross-sector partners serve as inspiration for individuals, organizations, coalitions and funders to strategise on their role in advancing birth equity and identify the most urgent and influential actions in their context that they can impact. Our projects’ integration of the Systems Thinking Iceberg Model and the R4P framework provided a comprehensive methodology for dissecting the complex interplay of factors affecting birth equity. Through this approach, we were able to map out the Birth Equity Ecosystem, identify underlying systemic structures and mental models that act as barriers to change through the Birth Equity Iceberg, and develop 49 action ideas for specific birth equity partners to advance in the next 5 years in our Birth Equity Action Map.

Our findings align with previous research underscoring the efficacy of collaborative systems thinking approaches in addressing complex health challenges [40, 41]. While traditional interventions in maternal and child health have often emphasized clinical care and individual-level behavioural modifications, our study reinforces the importance of broader systemic perspectives [32]. This study underscores the necessity of addressing systemic issues at their root, emphasizing that interventions must extend beyond immediate solutions to consider the historical, structural and cultural dimensions of health inequities, and those that expand beyond the health sector. The Birth Equity Ecosystem Map, Birth Equity Iceberg model and Birth Equity Action Map collectively serve as strategic tools for actors across sectors to identify leverage points and enact meaningful, systemic change. These tools underscore the importance of a multi-layered approach to health equity, one that is responsive to the nuances of systemic oppression and actively seeks to dismantle barriers to health equity.

Comparatively, our project’s methodological rigour and comprehensive actor engagement set it apart from other systems thinking approaches in public health, which have often struggled with moving from system understanding to actionable strategies. Unlike studies where the transition from system understanding to action development lacked detailed guidance, our participatory design facilitated a deep dive into the systems affecting birth equity. A notable limitation of our study is that we did not directly engage birthing people themselves and instead relied on literature that supported that in their own research. Further, we guided partners through the process of identifying systemic barriers and developing targeted actions informed by the R4P framework. This ensured that our action strategies were grounded in a nuanced understanding of the system’s complexity and contributed to a growing body of evidence supporting the effectiveness of systems thinking in public health research and practice.

Moreover, the intentional allocation of time and resources to engage a diverse group of actors in the co-creation of systems mapping tools allowed for the emergence of innovative, equity-focused action ideas. Our project demonstrates the need for action and investment to prioritize cross-sector, collaborative initiatives that strategically address systemic barriers to birth equity.

Conclusions

This project underscores the critical needs for targeted collaborative approaches to achieve birth equity. By leveraging the R4P framework and systems thinking, we have visualized the ideal factors and conditions needed in the Birth Equity Ecosystem Map, elucidated systemic barriers to change in the Birth Equity Iceberg, and provided actionable insights for system actors in the Birth Equity Action Map. These tools are designed to guide organizations, coalitions and funders in identifying leverage points and enact meaningful systemic changes for birth equity.

Our findings reiterate the necessity of addressing the root causes of health inequities, emphasizing that interventions must extend beyond immediate solutions to consider historical, structural and cultural dimensions. The application of systems thinking to advance birth equity provides a robust methodology for dissecting these complex issues and developing targeted, impactful actions.

The Birth Equity Action Map, in particular, serves as a strategic tool, highlighting critical actions needed by specific system actors. This map not only identifies where system actors can use their leverage to accelerate birth equity but also fosters a shared understanding of the systemic barriers to birth equity and promotes collaborative efforts across sectors to overcome them.

In light of these insights, we call on researchers and other partners in the field to utilize and improve upon the Birth Equity Action Map as a guiding framework for actions and investments over the next 5 years. We are particularly interested in observing how researchers and practitioners will utilize, adapt or refine these tools in practical settings. This commitment will be pivotal in transforming our current understanding and practices into tangible improvements in birth outcomes.
