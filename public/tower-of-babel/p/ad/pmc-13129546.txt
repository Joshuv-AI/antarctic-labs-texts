Discovering proteo-transcriptomic networks via biologically informed heterogeneous graph learning
Authors: Jingxian Duan, Yaou Liu, Dongling Pei, Zijian Zhou, Yuanshen Zhao, Jingran Deng, Haofei Ma, Hong Zhao, Zeyu Ma, Zilong Wang, Shifu Chen, Hairong Zheng, Dong Liang, Zhenyu Zhang, Zhi-Cheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancers are shaped by genetic interactions across multiple omics layers. Despite substantial progress made at the genomic and transcriptomic levels, the detailed interplay among mRNA, proteins, and protein modifications as well as the corresponding actionable targets remained poorly explored. Here, we developed bioGraph, a biologically informed graph learning method designed to systematically identify proteo-transcriptomic networks from transcriptomic, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic data. By incorporating genetic interaction priors into a unique three-layered heterogeneous graph, bioGraph revealed functional intra-omic, inter-omic, and cross-omic regulatory networks with prognostic relevance, including previously overlooked interactions modulating cancer hallmarks. We introduced a multi-omic gene set variation analysis score to quantify the network activity. We applied bioGraph to pan-cancer datasets and identified trans-omic regulatory hub genes undetectable by conventional methods. MAP4 emerged as a marker associated with tumor growth and malignant behaviors, validated via external datasets and tumor cell line assays, demonstrating its therapeutic potential. Our findings establish bioGraph as a new tool for identifying proteo-transcriptomic networks and gene targets with rapidly expanding underutilized multi-omic resources.

Introduction

The molecular landscape of cancer is shaped by complex interactions across genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic levels [1–4]. Proteo-transcriptomics enables investigation of how genomic alterations drive cancer phenotypes [5–7]. In this process, mRNA, proteins, and protein modifications are key intermediates connecting genes to functional outcomes [8–12]. While mRNA abundance correlates with DNA copy number, only 20%–30% of mRNA variations propagate at the protein level [13, 14]. This discrepancy highlights the substantial regulatory influence of translational and post-translational processes in shaping protein abundance and function. However, systematic characterization of information flow across mRNA, proteins, and protein modifications and its potential for improving prognostication and therapeutic strategies remain poorly understood.

Recent advances in mass-spectrometry-based proteomics have enabled the characterization of the proteogenomic landscape of cancers [6, 8, 10, 15], yet only a few of them have correlated proteo-transcriptomic profiles with clinical outcomes, and very few have investigated proteo-transcriptomic networks. Most existing studies focused on the differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis [2, 4]. However, it is the interplay among genes across omics, rather than merely the quantity of genes, that provides greater insight into their collective role in driving biological processes [16, 17]. While deep learning models have emerged for multi-omics integration [18–20], methods such as convolutional neural networks and transformers often encode omic features into low-dimensional embeddings while the biological structures within the data are lost.

Multi-omics graph models have demonstrated success in learning relation-aware structures [21–23]. It is inherently suited for representing genetic networks, where nodes represent biological entities such as genes while edges between nodes represent genetic interactions. Recently, several graph learning-based multi-omic models have been developed to identify cancer genes [24, 25]. However, existing methods face two limitations: (i) simplification of multi-omic interactions by combining multi-omic data as a node feature vector, which neglects inter-omic links and (ii) biological prior knowledge is often only weakly incorporated, reducing the ability to learn robust, biologically plausible interactions from the small, high-dimensional cohorts typical of proteo-transcriptomic studies.

A related line of work, biologically informed neural networks (BINNs), incorporate biological prior knowledge by constraining model connectivity using curated biological hierarchies or masked edges. Representative BINNs include P-NET, which constrains connections from molecular features to genes and from genes to pathway hierarchies via masked edges [26]; Cox-PASNet, which couples a gene to pathway network with a Cox layer for survival prediction from gene expression and clinical covariates and explicitly notes multi-omics integration as a remaining need [27]; and GenNet-based visible networks that “merge each omic at the gene level” by combining methylation and expression within per-gene representations [28]. These designs support pathway-level attribution and reduce the effective hypothesis space. However, most BINNs achieve multi-omics fusion via per-gene aggregation or hierarchical mapping, rather than explicitly representing cross-omic regulatory relations between distinct molecular entities such as RNA, protein, or phosphoprotein. As a result, they are not optimized to recover interaction-level proteo-transcriptomic networks that reflect translation and post-translational regulation [29].

To address these gaps, we developed a biologically informed graph learning method, named bioGraph, to systematically discover vast and varied proteo-transcriptomic networks using transcriptome, proteome, and phosphoproteome data from four datasets, including a colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) dataset, a glioblastoma (GBM) dataset, a pediatric low-grade glioma (pLGG) dataset, and a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) dataset. The key methodological challenge involved modeling heterogeneous interactions across omics while maintaining biological relevance. To tackle this challenge, we developed two innovative strategies. First, we created a three-layered heterogeneous graph to flexibly model RNA, protein, and phosphoprotein entities and the interactions among them. Second, we employed a biologically informed learning approach incorporating prior knowledge of molecular interactions such as curated pathways and protein–protein interactions (PPIs) to enhance both accuracy and interpretability.

bioGraph has four strengths by providing: (i) proteo-transcriptomic network discovery. We systematically identified functional intra-omic, inter-omic, and cross-omic regulatory networks with prognostic relevance, including previously overlooked interactions modulating cancer hallmarks; (ii) cross-omic network activity quantification. We presented a multi-omic gene set variation analysis (MGSVA) score that quantified the activities of the proteo-transcriptomic networks, extending traditional GSVA to the multi-omic level. (iii) pan-cancer gene target discovery. We identified pan-cancer trans-omics regulatory hub genes such as MAP4 (Microtubule-Associated Protein 4) undetectable in conventional methods, exposing therapeutic vulnerabilities in dysregulated translation or post-translational modification; and (iv) joint computation-experiment validations. We established the bioGraph-identified new findings through pan-cancer cross-validation, external dataset replication, and tumor cell line experiments. Overall, our study may advance precision oncology by providing a new tool to leverage rapidly expanding underutilized proteo-transcriptomic data resources and uncovering clinical outcome-associated trans-omic regulators with therapeutic opportunities. An interactive platform showcasing the bioGraph-learned networks for all patients of the four cancer types is accessible at https://biograph.hapyun.com. The source codes used for building and validating bioGraph can be found at https://codeocean.com/capsule/2357965/tree.

Materials and methods

Datasets and data preprocessing

We built bioGraph models for four cancer types to assess generalizability (Supplementary Table S1): 79 COAD [1], 74 GBM [2], 73 pLGG [3] (astrocytoma, WHO grade I/II), and 92 PDAC [4] patients were acquired from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) database. Curated mRNA expression (RSEM and FPKM), protein quantification, and phosphoprotein quantification of datasets above were downloaded from cBioPortal [30]. For external validation, glioma tissues (n = 119) were obtained from local patients who underwent primary curative resection from 2019 to 2021 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (FAHZZU) and received no prior anti-tumor treatments. Transcriptomic sequencing and proteomic sequencing were performed on the glioma tissues as described in our previous publications [31]. The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04216537), approved by the FAHZZU Ethics Committee (2019-KY-176), and all participants provided written informed consent.

CPTAC data were used as provided without additional log transformation. Features (genes/proteins/phosphosites) with >20% missing values were removed; remaining missing values were set to NaN. Feature identifiers were deduplicated, and z-score standardization was applied per cohort and omic layer to harmonize measurement scales across modalities prior to graph construction.

For COAD and pLGG, disease-free survival (DFS) was the primary endpoint; DFS was defined as the time from diagnosis or treatment start to first recurrence, progression, or cancer-related death. Samples without DFS records or with DFS exceeding overall survival (OS) were excluded. For GBM and PDAC, OS (time from diagnosis to death or last follow-up) was used. Patients whose death was not cancer-related were excluded to ensure analytical focus on tumor progression.

bioGraph overall design

bioGraph is a three-layered heterogeneous graph designed to comprehensively model proteo-transcriptomic interactions across three omics layers: mRNA, protein, and phosphoprotein (Fig. 1A). The heterogeneous graph structure is employed to capture the complexity of the system, where it includes three types of nodes (mRNA, protein, and phosphoprotein) and five types of edges: three intra-omic interactions within each of the transcriptomic, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic layers and two inter-omic connections between transcriptomic and proteomic layers and between proteomic and phosphoproteomic layers. Within each omics layer, nodes and edges model gene interactions at the intra-omic level. Between layers, nodes representing the same gene are connected by inter-omic edges, enabling the effective modeling of information flow across omics layers (Fig. 1B).

To create a biologically informed structure, we incorporated biological priors into bioGraph. First, nodes were defined according to curated pathways. Only genes within the pathways from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database were kept as nodes at each layer [32]. Then, intra-omic edges were generated based on two criteria: biological pathways from KEGG gene maps and direct interactions from the Search Tool for the Retrieval of INteracting Genes (STRING)-db (version 11.0b) database. In the transcriptomic layer, we mapped genes to their pathways and identified all possible gene pairs within each pathway to create intra-omic edges, capturing RNA interactions in specific biological processes. In the proteomic or phosphoproteomic layers, intra-omic edges were created based on PPI documented in the STRING database [33], where only interactions with an affinity score of 700 or above were retained. Third, inter-omic edges were created between RNA–protein and protein–phosphoprotein node pairs of the same gene. To mimic translation and post-translational modification processes, each transcriptomic node was linked to its corresponding proteomic node, while each proteomic node was linked to several phosphoproteomic nodes. The construction of nodes and edges was crucial for representing the proteo-transcriptomic interactions. The concise three-layer design allows information sharing across omics types while reducing noise by simplifying network structure and increasing the training efficiency in small sample sizes through multi-omic integration.

From the obtained full bioGraph retaining all genes and connections across three omics, we extracted three types of function-specific subgraphs with clear biological meanings: (i) Intra-omic subgraphs revealing RNA or protein interactions within each omic layer, which may be overlooked by existing DEG enrichment analysis. (ii) Inter-omic subgraph elucidating connections between transcriptome and proteome, as well as proteome and phosphoproteome, which quantified information passing between omics. (iii) Cross-omic subgraphs representing the three-layered gene network associated with specific biological function. Moreover, by leveraging the edge weight of this network, we proposed a scoring system to quantify the activity of specific biological functions identified by the cross-omic subgraph. We validated the findings from bioGraph through tumor cell transfection experiments. These results are visualized on an interactive online platform (Fig. 1C).

The graph structure allows information to flow between related biological entities, effectively creating virtual connections and reducing network complexity, allowing for effective data-driven edge discovery in small sample sizes. The size of the resulting graph can be found in Supplementary Table S2.

Although translation and post-modification are biologically directional processes, bioGraph does not aim to infer causal molecular flow from observational cohort data. We therefore treat inter-omic relations as bidirectional during message passing, enabling information exchange across linked entities. This choice is motivated by known regulatory feedback: protein activity can modulate transcriptional programs, and phosphorylation can regulate protein activity, interactions, and stability, thereby influencing both protein abundance and downstream gene expression. Relation semantics are preserved through typed edges (translation and post-modification) and relation-specific parameters.

Performance is evaluated using C-index. We perform patient-level five-fold cross-validation for GBM, pLGG, and PDAC. Using the learned risk scores, patients are stratified into high- and low-risk groups, and Kaplan–Meier analysis is used to assess survival separation.

To assess the stability of learned graph structure, we quantify the agreement of learned edge representations across folds by computing the Pearson correlation between edge-weight vectors derived from each trained fold model. This evaluates whether the model learns consistent edge-modulation patterns under resampling, supporting the robustness of the inferred cross-omic network signals.

Performance comparison with other methods

bioGraph was benchmarked against MOGONET [25], EMOGI [24], IGNN [36], MMGL [37], SALMON [38], and MRGCN [39] using identical datasets, data splits, and stratified cross-validation. Only models capable of ingesting proteo-transcriptomic data with minimal architectural modification were included. For the prognosis task, mean cross-validation C-indices were compared.

For the EMOGI model that was specifically designed for cancer gene prediction, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was computed across five PPI networks, including ConsensusPathDB (CPDB) [40], iRefIndex [41], Multinet [42], PCNet [43], and STRING_db [33], as previously reported by Schulte-Sasse et al. [24]. To enable AUC evaluation, bioGraph’s continuous node features were binarized using a threshold determined by Youden’s J statistic [44] on training data only, then applied to validation data. Probability scores of MAP4, SORBS1, SPTAN1, SRRM1, and TMPO were generated by EMOGI trained on each cancer type. C-indices and AUCs were presented as mean ± SEM.

Ablation studies

Four ablations were performed to isolate the contribution of each design component:

(i) removal of biological priors, replacing the KEGG/STRING edge scaffold with a fully connected baseline at matched parameter budget; (ii) removal of edge-aware weighting; (iii) reduced multi-omic depth, using two-layer variants (transcriptome-proteome and proteome-phosphoproteome) and single-omic graphs (transcriptome-, proteome-, and phosphoproteome-only); and (iv) standard homogeneous GCNs including GraphSAGE [45] and GAT [46] on single-omic graphs. All ablations used the same training pipeline, hyperparameter selection strategy, and cross-validation scheme.

Intra-omic network extraction and visualization

While the original bioGraph provides a comprehensive overview of intra-omic interactions across all genes and their interconnections within the three omics layers, its large size can limit practical use. To address this, we developed a process to extract smaller, function-specific subgraphs from the original bioGraph. These subgraphs were extracted using specific criteria to ensure they are not only smaller but also biologically relevant. For intra-omic subgraphs, we applied a threshold-based approach to select the most significant interactions. Specifically, we filtered the bioGraph by edge weights, retaining only interactions above a predefined threshold (edge weight ranking top 10%) that reflect strong biological relevance. This thresholding is used only for visualization; all training and inference steps in bioGraph use the full graph without edge thresholding. The selected subgraphs were further refined by focusing on groups of biological pathways of interest, ensuring that they capture the most biologically meaningful interactions. This selective extraction process enables more manageable yet biologically rich subgraphs for downstream analysis.

To compare with current DEG enrichment analysis, biological pathways of interest were selected by DEG enrichment analysis. Within each dataset, the cross-validation process delineated an optimal fold that divided patients into training and validation cohorts. Subsequently, these cohorts were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups. Within the training set, differential expression analysis was executed across transcriptomic, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic layers using edgeR to identify molecules with significant expression differences (P < .05) between the risk groups [47]. This step was also performed in the validation cohort, with the criterion that only entities exhibiting differential expression in both training and validation sets (P < .05) were further considered.

Following the consolidation of differentially expressed entities, enrichment analysis via clusterProfiler was employed to elucidate pivotal biological pathways implicated in the risk stratification [48]. To map the gene–gene interactions within these pathways, edge weights and node features were extracted for all implicated genes. For visualization purposes, edges within the top percentile ranks of weight significance were selected for each pathway, specifically, 1% for transcriptomic, 5% for proteomic, and 10% for phosphoproteomic networks, as validated across both training and validation datasets. As the results, smaller subgraphs containing biological pathways of interest were extracted from bioGraph. The resultant subgraphs were graphically represented using Cytoscape [49], offering insights into the molecular interplay underpinning the observed phenotypic distinctions between high-risk and low-risk groups. Descriptive statistics were visualized by the funky-heatmap R package [50].

Inter-omic network extraction and visualization

Inter-omic subgraphs of interested genes were extracted, which formed tree-like structures. Gene of interest was selected by edge weight. For each gene across different cancer types, we conducted statistical comparisons of RP and PP edge weights across all patients. Genes were deemed significant at the inter-omic level based on the criterion of having statistically different RP and PP weights (P < .05) in both the training and validation datasets. The resultant tree-like networks were defined as inter-omic subgraphs. All edge-weight statistics and node-embedding analyses are conducted within each cancer cohort; we do not perform direct quantitative comparisons of edge weights or embeddings across cohorts.

Next, we sought to associate the extracted inter-omic subgraphs with specific biological functions. We calculated the ratio of inter-omic significant genes within each biological pathway, selecting the top 10 pathways for visualization. This analysis provided insights into the pathways most affected by translation and post-translational modifications in the context of cancer.

Furthermore, we investigated how these inter-omic significant genes may affect patient prognosis by assessing the correlation between the genes and patient survival. Only genes exhibiting a significant correlation (Pearson’s correlation analysis, P < .05) in both training and validation datasets were further considered. By intersecting these findings across four cancer types, we identified a core set of five genes that consistently demonstrated significance, highlighting their potential as universal biomarkers or therapeutic targets across various cancers.

Cell culture and transfection for in vitro experiments

To assess the role of bioGraph-identified gene in tumor growth, we conducted lentivirus transduction on T98G and U251 cell lines. T98G and U251 GBM cells (Cell Resource Center, Shanghai) were maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS (Cat#FSP500, ExCell Bio) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C, 5% CO2, with regular mycoplasma testing. Lentiviruses encoding MAP4 shRNA were produced in HEK293T cells co-transfected with pLKO.1 TRC-cloning plasmids, psPAX2, and pMD2.G; supernatants were collected at 48 h and used fresh or stored at −80°C. GBM cells at ∼20% confluence were infected (1:1 supernatant:medium, 5 ng/ml polybrene) and selected with puromycin (1 ug/ml) for one week.

Cell migration, proliferation, and apoptosis assays

To study the role of target gene, we conducted cell migration, proliferation, and apoptosis assays on MAP4-silenced cells. First, cell migration and invasion were assessed by wound-healing assay: T98G and U251 cells (5 × 105/well, six-well plates) were scratched at confluence, washed, cultured in 0.5% FBS medium, and imaged at 0, 24, and 48 h. Trans-well migration: 5 × 104 cells/well in serum-free upper chamber medium; lower chamber contained 30% FBS medium; and after 24 h, non-migratory cells were removed, and migratory cells were stained with Giemsa and counted.

Second, cell proliferation and colony formation assays were performed: 1 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates with 10 μl CCK-8 (Cat#BS350B, biosharp) added daily for 4 days; absorbance measured at 450 nm. Colony formation: 1 × 104 cells/well in six-well plates for two weeks, fixed with 4% formaldehyde, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet; colonies ≥50 cells counted at 4× magnification.

Third, we conducted an apoptosis assay to test the viability of the MAP4-silenced cells. Apoptotic cells were quantified using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Cat#C1062L, Beyotime). After staining, cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer. FlowJo Version 7.6.1 was used to calculate the percentage of viable, early apoptotic, and late apoptotic cells.

Calculation of multi-omic gene set variation analysis score

To quantify pathway activity at the interaction level rather than the expression level, we extended GSVA [51] and computed a multi-omic GSVA (MGSVA) score based on learned edge weights. Conventional GSVA summarizes coordinated variation of gene sets from a single expression matrix and does not incorporate cross-omic interaction structure. In response to the challenge, we developed MGSVA that employed a Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS)-like random walk statistic on edge weights within each pathway.

Identification of key biological pathways by MGSVA

MGSVA provides pathway-level activity scores from a multi-omic interaction perspective and was used to prioritize pathways associated with prognosis within each cohort. For each pathway, we computed patient-level MGSVA scores separately for each edge type. To identify pathways with consistent prognostic separation, we compared MGSVA scores between high- and low-risk groups using the Mann–Whitney U test for each edge type and then aggregated evidence across edge types by min–max normalizing the resulting U-statistics and summarizing them into an overall pathway score. Overall pathway scores were computed in both the training and validation folds; only pathways showing consistent signals across training and validation were retained for downstream interpretation. We visualized the resulting pathway-by-edge-type profiles using heatmaps to highlight cross-omic consistency and relative importance.

To further assess prognostic relevance, we evaluated the association between MGSVA scores and patient outcomes using Pearson correlation within each cohort. Pathways with significant correlations (P < .05) in both training and validation folds were considered prognostically associated. Distributions of pathway MGSVA scores in high- versus low-risk groups were displayed using split violin plots to illustrate effect direction and variability.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.0) and Python (version 3.8). The data were presented as mean ± standard error if not stated otherwise. The normality of each data set was assessed; datasets that passed the normality test were compared with the Wilcoxon test, Mann–Whitney test, or t-test depending on the result of the variation test. Datasets that did not display normal distribution and datasets with significant variation were compared by the Wilcoxon test or Friedman test. P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant, as indicated by asterisks (*). For in vitro experiments, data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using the Student’s t-test (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA (for multiple groups). All edge-weight statistics, node-embedding analyses, ablations, and model-performance comparisons are conducted within each cancer cohort; we do not perform direct quantitative comparisons of edge weights or embeddings across cohorts.

Results

bioGraph achieved better performance in survival prediction

We first assessed whether bioGraph improves prognosis prediction relative to single-omic graph models. For each of the four cancer datasets, we trained bioGraph and three corresponding single-omic graph convolutional network models using cross-validation and evaluated performance by the C-index, which measures the accuracy of survival-time ranking (Fig. 2).

Across all four datasets, bioGraph achieved the highest mean cross-validated C-index. In COAD, bioGraph reached 0.91 ± 0.08, exceeding transcriptomic (0.89 ± 0.07), proteomic (0.89 ± 0.05), and phosphoproteomic (0.49 ± 0.15) models (Fig. 2A). In GBM, bioGraph achieved 0.71 ± 0.04, compared with 0.56 ± 0.08 for the transcriptomic model, 0.66 ± 0.06 for the proteomic model, and 0.69 ± 0.04 for the phosphoproteomic model (Fig. 2B). Similar trends were observed in pLGG, where bioGraph achieved 0.73 ± 0.03 versus 0.58 ± 0.03, 0.61 ± 0.06, and 0.63 ± 0.07 for the three single-omic models, respectively (Fig. 2C), and in PDAC, where bioGraph achieved 0.74 ± 0.02 compared with 0.59 ± 0.04, 0.59 ± 0.03, and 0.53 ± 0.03 (Fig. 2D). These results show that integrating transcriptomic, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic information improves survival prediction compared with single-omic graph learning.

We next examined whether the risk scores generated by bioGraph could stratify patients into clinically distinct groups. Using dataset-specific optimal cutoffs, patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups for COAD, GBM, pLGG, and PDAC. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed significant survival differences between the two groups in both training and validation cohorts across all four cancer types (log-rank test, P < .05; Fig. 2), indicating that bioGraph-derived risk scores support robust prognostic stratification.

To further benchmark performance, we compared bioGraph with five representative multi-omic or multimodal graph-based models, including MOGONET [25], IGNN [36], MMGL [37], SALMON [38], and MRGCN [39]. The selected models are originally designed for different tasks. Specifically, MOGONET allows patient classification; IGNN and SALMON enable cancer prognosis prediction; MMGL enables disease prediction; and MRGCN allows consensus representation learning from multi-omic data.

To compare their performance in prognosis prediction, all models were evaluated using consistent cross-validation protocols across four cancer datasets. In the prognosis prediction task, bioGraph consistently outperformed MOGONET, IGNN, MMGL, SALMON, and MRGCN in terms of cross-validated mean C-indices (Table 1).

One potential explanation for the limited performance of state-of-the-art models is their tendency to either analyze each omics layer or modal type in isolation or integrate them at a superficial level, thereby failing to adequately capture the complex cross-omic networks essential for accurate prognosis. Moreover, MOGONET and MMGL were primarily designed for classification or subtype identification, focusing on maximizing label prediction accuracy. MRGCN was designed for unsupervised downstream tasks. These approaches often result in the loss of information about the underlying direct and indirect interactions between genes, proteins, and their modifications. However, they are highly effective for the specific applications they are designed for.

To test whether bioGraph’s biologically guided design adds value beyond model capacity, we conducted a structured ablation study. We first removed biological priors by replacing both the pathway-constrained node set and edge scaffold with a non-guided baseline in which all retained genes within each omics layer were fully connected, while matching bioGraph to a comparable parameter budget. In parallel, we omitted the edge-aware weighting attention-like module while retaining the biologically informed graph design. As shown in Table 2, both ablation studies reduced model performance in all four datasets. The results indicate that pathway-informed sparsity and edge-aware weighting modules provided meaningful inductive bias that improves generalization.

Furthermore, we reduced cross-omic context by evaluating two-layer variants (transcriptome–proteome; proteome–phosphoproteome) and single-omic graphs (transcriptome-only, proteome-only, and phosphoproteome-only). Across four independent datasets, two-layer graphs generally outperformed single-omic graphs, supporting the benefit of cross-omic coupling (Table 2). Among two-layer models, transcriptome–proteome outperformed proteome–phosphoproteome in three of four datasets, and phosphoproteome-only performed worst in two datasets. This suggests that phosphoproteomic signals are less stable in isolation and contribute most effectively when anchored to upstream molecular context. In single-omic graphs, we also compared our method with other standard GCNs, including GraphSAGE [45] and GAT [46]. The heterogeneous bioGraph outperformed all homogeneous graph models, demonstrating the advantage of its multi-node-type architecture. Overall, these ablations support bioGraph’s pathway-guided heterogeneous architecture as a primary driver of performance, rather than a proxy for increased connectivity or parameter count.

Moreover, the bioGraph-generated risk score correlated with various key clinical features, including tumor histology (Supplementary Fig. S1), BRAF status (Supplementary Fig. S2), and tumor necrosis (Supplementary Fig. S3), demonstrating that the bioGraph risk score is not merely prognostic relevance but also of clinical significance (Supplementary Fig. S1–S3).

Intra-omic subgraph identified distinct patterns of gene–gene interactions in four cancer types

We next examined whether bioGraph could resolve intra-omic gene–gene interaction structure within each molecular layer. To do this, we extracted intra-omic subgraphs from the trained model and focused on pathway-relevant interactions associated with risk-stratified molecular differences. For comparison with conventional analysis, we first identified DEGs between the high-risk and low-risk groups and performed pathway enrichment analysis. We then mapped the enriched pathways onto the intra-omic subgraphs and retained directly connected biological interactions. These subgraphs were further filtered by edge weight to highlight core interactions and hub genes.

Given that five-fold cross-validation produced five sets of edge weights, we first assessed the reproducibility of edge-weight distributions across validation splits. Across datasets, the edge weights were generally concordant between splits, with statistically significant correlations in most pairwise comparisons, although the strength of agreement varied by dataset and was weaker for several categories in GBM (Pearson correlation, P < .05; Supplementary Fig. S4). These results support the stability of bioGraph-derived interaction patterns despite the limited sample size.

We then compared the resulting subgraphs with traditional DEG-based enrichment analysis. DEG enrichment primarily linked sets of DEGs to enriched pathways (Fig. 3A-C, left panel), whereas bioGraph additionally resolved the interaction structure among genes within those pathways through weighted edges (Fig. 3A–C right panel, Supplementary Figs S5–S8). The inferred intra-omic architectures differed substantially across datasets. In some cases, the networks were organized around multiple hub genes (Fig. 3A right panel, Supplementary Fig. S8A), whereas others showed more dispersed gene clusters (Fig. 3B right panel and Supplementary Fig. S8B) or compact subnetworks centered on a single dominant hub gene (Fig. 3B right panel, Supplementary Fig. S6A). Similar diversity was observed across different omics layers and across COAD, GBM, pLGG, and PDAC (Supplementary Figs S5–S8), indicating that intra-omic network organization is both cancer type- and context-dependent.

We next asked whether bioGraph recapitulates signals identified by conventional DEG analysis while also revealing additional candidates. As shown in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs S5–S8, many DEGs identified by standard methods were also located within highly weighted regions of the bioGraph subgraphs (red-outlined nodes). We further quantified the overlap between DEGs and the top-ranked bioGraph nodes. In COAD, GBM, pLGG, and PDAC, transcriptomic DEGs accounted for 27%, 43%, 6%, and 21% of the top 50% significant nodes, respectively (Fig. 3D). The corresponding overlap was lower in the proteomic and phosphoproteomic layers, likely reflecting the smaller number of DEGs detected in these omics relative to the total number of measured nodes. Together, these findings indicate that bioGraph captures many genes highlighted by differential analysis while also prioritizing additional hub genes and interaction structures that are not readily resolved by DEG-based approaches alone.

Inter-omic subgraphs identified significant trans-omic interconnections shared across cancers

We next analyzed inter-omic subgraphs to characterize information flow between molecular layers. Unlike approaches that model omics modalities largely in parallel, bioGraph explicitly connects transcriptomic, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic layers through inter-omic edges, enabling the identification of RNA–protein (RP) and protein–phosphoprotein (PP) relationships (Fig. 4A). These links provide a framework for examining how translational and post-translational processes may contribute to protein function and, ultimately, to tumor prognosis. In the resulting inter-omic subgraphs, each protein was typically associated with a single RP connection, whereas multiple PP connections could be observed depending on the number of measured phosphorylation sites.

To compare the relative contributions of these two inter-omic link types, we examined RP and PP edge weights for the same gene across cancer types. In adult GBM, COAD, and PDAC, most genes showed stronger RP than PP edge weights, accounting for 91.49%, 93.37%, and 94.77% of genes, respectively (Fig. 4A). In contrast, pLGG displayed a distinct inter-omic pattern: only 19.51% of RP links exceeded PP links in edge weight, whereas 33.99% of PP links were stronger than RP links. These results indicate marked differences in trans-omic organization across cancers, with RP-linked regulation predominating in GBM, COAD, and PDAC, but not in pLGG.

We then asked whether genes showing large RP–PP edge-weight differences were concentrated in specific biological pathways. Across the four cancer types, these genes were consistently enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion, oncogenic signaling, immune processes, cancer-associated signaling, and protein production-associated cellular functions (Fig. 4B). Comparison of the top 30 pathways ranked by gene ratio further identified recurrent pathways across cancers, including ERBB signaling, Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis, focal adhesion, tight junction, spliceosome, long-term potentiation, and multiple cancer-related pathways (Supplementary Table S5). These results suggest that inter-omic regulatory differences are concentrated in core functional programs relevant to tumor biology.

To validate the impact of inter-omic interconnections on prognosis, we examined the correlation between the RP and PP edge weights with patient outcomes. By categorizing genes according to biological pathways, we found that genes significantly correlated with prognosis in both training and validation datasets were predominantly involved in oncogenic signaling, cell adhesion, metabolism, immunity, and protein production (Fig. 4C). As indicated by color coding in the plots, for most genes, RP links, rather than PP links, demonstrated significant associations with patient survival. All edge-weight statistics and node-embedding analyses are conducted within each cancer cohort. Next, we analyzed genes that exhibited significantly different inter-omic regulations. Among these genes, MAP4, SORBS1, SPTAN1, SRRM1, and TMPO were consistently identified across all four cancer types (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. S9). Each of these genes had multiple phosphorylation sites. Using bioGraph, we can identify the key phosphorylation site that has the most significant impact on tumor prognosis, as indicated by the PP edge weights. These genes were associated with various biological functions, such as cytoskeleton construction, RNA splicing, and insulin metabolism. This analysis not only underscored the significance of inter-omic linkages in predicting prognosis but also offered potential candidates of gene targets.

Considering that previous comparative studies only focused on models for prognosis prediction, we further compared bioGraph and a benchmark model EMOGI who excels at cancer gene identification and novel target discovery [24]. EMOGI utilizes a graph convolutional network framework that incorporates both multidimensional node features and the topological properties of PPI networks, enabling it to recognize cancer genes from non-cancer genes. We designed two reciprocal studies: (i) we tested whether bioGraph could effectively identify known cancer genes on the same gene labels that EMOGI was trained on; and (ii) we examined whether EMOGI could in turn identify the key targets discovered by bioGraph.

Although EMOGI performed very well on its intended task across five networks it was trained on, bioGraph still achieved a higher AUC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) for cancer gene identification in one of these networks even though bioGraph was not specifically designed for this purpose. (Supplementary Table S6). In the context of novel target discovery, EMOGI was tested on COAD, GBM, LGG, and PDAC and yielded cancer-related possibility scores for each gene. EMOGI predicts that MAP4, SORBS1, and SPTAN1 are cancer-related in three datasets; SRRM1 and TMPO are cancer-related in two datasets (Supplementary Table S7).

MAP4 identified by bioGraph was associated with tumor growth and malignant behaviors

Given that the genes prioritized by inter-omic edge weights were derived from prognosis-associated networks, we next examined whether they were also supported by independent datasets and functional assays. Owing to the limited availability of fresh tissues and experimental materials, downstream validation was focused primarily on glioma.

Analysis of external datasets showed that higher MAP4 mRNA and protein expression was consistently associated with poorer prognosis across GBM and LGG cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S10A). We also observed a positive association between TMPO expression and prognosis, as well as distinct patterns for SORBS1 in GBM and pLGG. However, several of these associations did not reach statistical significance in the smaller validation cohorts, likely reflecting limited sample size.

Notably, MAP4 was not prioritized by conventional differential expression analysis and was not significantly associated with prognosis in the smaller discovery datasets, highlighting a potential advantage of bioGraph in identifying candidate genes through multi-omic interaction structure rather than expression change alone. To further assess this observation, we analyzed a larger independent GBM cohort collected at our local hospital. In this cohort, both MAP4 mRNA and protein expression were significantly associated with shorter overall survival (mRNA, P < .015; protein, P < .01; Supplementary Fig. S10B and C). Similarly, SPTAN1 mRNA expression was negatively associated with survival (P < .001), and TMPO protein expression also showed a significant negative association with survival (P < .05).

Given the compelling evidence for MAP4, we next examined its role in tumor growth by silencing MAP4 in T98G and U251 GBM cell lines via lentivirus infection. Transfection efficiency was evaluated using fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. S11A), qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. S11B), and immunoblot (Supplementary Fig. S11C), all of which confirmed a substantial decrease in MAP4 expression in both cell lines. Wound healing, CCK-8 assays, colony formation, and trans-well assays were then carried out to assess the effect of MAP4 on GBM malignancy. We found that the self-renewal ability, migration, and proliferation of GBM cells were remarkably reduced under MAP4 depletion (Fig. 5A–D). Furthermore, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis revealed that the apoptosis rate of MAP4-silenced GBM cells (sh-MAP4#1 and sh-MAP4#2) was significantly higher than that of control cells (sh-NC) in both T98G and U251 cells (Fig. 5E). These results indicated MAP4 as a tumor-promoting factor in glioma. The findings suggest that bioGraph can effectively pinpoint genes that drive tumor growth and malignant behaviors through abnormal trans-omic information passing, even when the difference is not statistically significant when analyzed by commonly used methods with small sample sizes.

Cross-omic subgraphs characterized biological functions from a multi-omic view

We next examined cross-omic subgraphs to characterize pathway structure across transcriptomic, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic layers. Pathways were used as the basis for subgraph extraction, and for each cancer type we visualized representative pathways identified in the previous analyses (Supplementary Fig. S12). For clarity, only the top-ranked edges, defined as those within the top 50% in both training and validation datasets, together with their connected nodes, were displayed.

The resulting cross-omic subgraphs showed that the dominant interaction type differed across pathways and cancer types. In GBM, transcriptomic interactions were most prominent within the ERBB signaling cross-omic subgraph, whereas in COAD, proteomic interactions were more strongly represented in the spliceosome cross-omic subgraph. In pLGG, phosphoproteomic interactions were particularly enriched in the long-term potentiation cross-omic subgraph. Inter-omic links also showed pathway-specific patterns: RP links were prevalent in the adherens junction and ERBB signaling subgraphs, whereas PP links were especially prominent in the long-term potentiation subgraph. These findings indicate that different pathways are characterized by distinct combinations of intra-omic and inter-omic interactions, reflecting pathway-specific modes of molecular regulation across cancers.

MGSVA scoring system quantified the activity of biological functions in subgraphs

To quantify the role of intra-, inter-, and cross-omic pathways identified via bioGraph from a proteo-transcriptomic view, we proposed a scoring method named MGSVA, akin to the well-established GSVA algorithm. The MGSVA score quantifies the activity of all types of pathways, using all types of edge weights as input data for each patient (Supplementary Tables S8–S11). A positive MGSVA value indicates that the edge weights within a specific gene set are higher than those outside the set, indicating an up-regulation of pathway activity. Conversely, a negative value suggests that edge weights within the gene set are lower, indicating down-regulated pathway activity. For a comprehensive analysis, we calculated the MGSVA score for each type of edge. Pathways with significantly higher MGSVA scores were shown in Fig. 6A. Both the training and validation datasets were used for MGSVA calculation. For each pathway, the type of edge with high MGSVA score may vary. This variability was consistently observed across all examined cancer types.

Considering the clinical implications, we associated MGSVA scores with patient prognosis. Figure 6B highlighted pathways significantly correlated with prognosis across all cancer types in both training and validation datasets. They primarily involved metabolism and oncogenic signaling pathways. Notably, for the same pathway, the correlation with patient prognosis can vary depending on the type of interaction (edge). This indicated that the impact of a pathway on prognosis may differ based on whether the focus was on transcriptomic, proteomic, or phosphoproteomic interactions. Such a finding implied that therapeutic strategies designed to target different omics layers within a single pathway should be tailored accordingly. This nuance underscores the study’s contribution to precision medicine by identifying distinct pharmacological interventions for the same pathway based on its cross-omic interactions.

Furthermore, the distribution of MGSVA scores in high-risk and low-risk groups exhibited substantial difference (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S13), emphasizing the critical role of previously unexplored proteo-transcriptomic interactions in influencing tumor progression and highlighting the potential for targeted therapeutic interventions.

Online interactive platform

An interactive platform, accessible at https://biograph.hapyun.com (interactive demo), showcases subgraphs learned by bioGraph for each patient. The platform uses DEG-enriched pathways as examples. Users can explore intra-omic, inter-omic, and cross-omic subgraphs of these pathways, comparing them directly to the DEG enrichment results. The platform allows for the selection and display of subgraphs specific to transcriptomics, proteomics, and phosphoproteomics for individual patients. Additionally, cross-omic subgraphs are color-coded to distinguish each pathway clearly. The platform effectively visualizes the interactions between genes and pathways, as well as the interconnections among omics within each gene.

Discussion

We developed bioGraph, a biologically informed graph learning framework for identifying prognostically relevant proteo-transcriptomic networks. By representing mRNAs, proteins, and phosphoproteins as distinct node types within a three-layer heterogeneous graph, bioGraph captures both intra-omic and inter-omic interactions and enables network analysis across transcriptomic, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic levels. Across four cancer types, bioGraph improved survival prediction relative to single-omic graph models and several existing multi-omic methods while also revealing molecular interactions and candidate genes that were not readily identified by conventional differential expression-based approaches.

Proteo-transcriptomic analysis provides an opportunity to directly relate RNA abundance to protein expression and post-translational regulation, addressing an important limitation of RNA-centered analyses that infer protein function indirectly [13, 15, 52]. While recent studies have made much progress in characterizing the proteogenomic landscape of various cancer types [8, 10, 15, 53], only a few have correlated proteo-transcriptomic profiles with clinical outcomes, and very few methods have been proposed to identify proteo-transcriptomic networks [54, 55]. A central challenge in this setting is how to model the large number and diversity of interactions spanning transcription, translation, and post-translational modification. bioGraph was designed to address this problem by explicitly modeling molecular layers and edge types rather than treating omics modalities as independent inputs or combining them only at the feature or prediction level.

This explicit cross-omic design distinguishes bioGraph from existing graph-based multi-omic approaches. Several graph learning models have been developed for multi-omic analysis, where they aggregate multi-omic data into a single node with multi-dimensional vectors as node features or combine predictions in the label space for late fusion [24, 25]. While this approach facilitates individual gene expression analysis, it provides limited resolution of trans-omic molecular dependencies. To address this limitation, bioGraph represents mRNA, protein, and phosphoprotein measurements as separate but connected entities, allowing translation-related and phosphorylation-related relationships to be modeled directly. This structure made it possible to extract intra-omic, inter-omic, and cross-omic subgraphs linked to specific pathways and clinical outcomes. Notably, many genes and interactions prioritized by bioGraph were not highlighted by standard DEG analysis, consistent with the idea that network relevance and prognostic importance are not always accompanied by large marginal expression differences.

Several findings support the utility of this framework. First, bioGraph showed consistently strong prognostic performance across COAD, GBM, pLGG, and PDAC, including under relatively limited sample sizes. Second, the model identified intra-omic interaction structures extending beyond DEG enrichment results, thereby providing pathway-level context and hub-gene prioritization rather than only lists of altered genes. Note that sample size is considered as a key factor that comprises the statistical power of DEG analysis [56]. To address the challenge of small sample size in bioGraph, biological prior knowledge integration was extensively leveraged, providing external constraints that reduce reliance on purely data-driven edge discovery. Third, analysis of inter-omic subgraphs revealed marked differences in the relative importance of RNA–protein and protein–phosphoprotein links across cancer types. This observation unveils the needs for highly specific interventions tailored to distinct cross-omic interactions, aligning with prior research findings [57, 58]. Together, these findings indicate that prognosis-associated molecular regulation is shaped not only by changes within individual omics layers but also by interactions spanning translation and post-translational modification.

COAD, GBM, pLGG, and PDAC exhibit distinct biological characteristics [1–4, 9, 59]. Despite these differences, common genomic alterations for pan-cancers have also been identified [60, 61, 62]. In line with these discrepancies, our research has uncovered pathways differentially regulated at the inter-omic level, with consistent findings across these four cancer types. Interestingly, pLGG presents a markedly different inter-omic interaction landscape compared to the adult tumors of COAD, GBM, and PDAC. This divergence suggests the potential for unique inter-omic regulations in pLGG, warranting further investigation. The observed consistencies and divergences across cancer types underscore the intricate nature of cross-omic gene networks and reveal opportunities for future research to decode the complex interplay across proteo-transcriptomic layers. In this context, bioGraph emerges as a pivotal resource, offering a comprehensive repository of cross-omic interactions and functionally annotated gene targets with prognostic implications or therapeutic potential. By systematically mapping multi-omic dependencies from translational regulation to post-translational modifications, bioGraph not only illuminates previously obscured molecular mechanisms but also prioritizes actionable targets (e.g. kinase-substrate pairs, phosphorylation-driven signaling hubs) that could inform precision therapeutic strategies.

By analyzing cancer datasets, bioGraph identified MAP4 as a key oncogene, which was not identified by DEG analysis. MAP4, one of the microtubule-associated protein family, promotes microtubule assembly and has been shown to counteract destabilization of interphase microtubule catastrophe promotion [63]. Alterations in expression of isoforms of the ubiquitously expressed MAP4 have been reported to modulate cancer cell sensitivity to microtubule-interacting drugs [64, 65] and associated with differentiation, TNM stage, and poor prognosis [66, 67]. So far, there has been few research on the association between MAP4 and GBM. External validation and in vitro experiments have demonstrated that MAP4 promotes GBM cell growth and metastasis. This initial evidence underscores the capability of bioGraph to identify key oncogenes through abnormal inter-omic regulation, even when differences are not detected by quantitative analysis at individual omic levels.

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and limited cohort size partially constrain the generalizability of our findings, despite efforts to enhance statistical robustness through cross-validation and incorporation of multi-center datasets. Second, while bioGraph reveals prognostically relevant cross-omic networks, their utility in guiding personalized therapeutic strategies remains unvalidated. More investigations are needed to determine whether this cross-omic network can effectively guide targeted therapies. Third, our experiments were conducted on cohorts in which each patient had matched measurements across all omics modalities analyzed. In practice, multi-omics studies often exhibit partially observed profiles, where one or more modalities are missing for a subset of patients. Extending bioGraph to robustly handle such incomplete multi-view inputs will be an important direction for future work. Fourth, the mechanistic roles of candidate interactions identified in this study—particularly those involving post-translational modifications—await experimental validation. Prospective studies and functional assays are critical to systematically validate these preliminary insights.
