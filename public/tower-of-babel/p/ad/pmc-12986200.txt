Microencapsulated Hydroxyapatite–TEGDMA Systems for Self-Healing in Dental Applications
Authors: Maria Amalia Taut, Marioara Moldovan, Codruta Sarosi, Gertrud Alexandra Paltinean, Ioan Petean, Miuta Rafila Filip, Laura Silaghi-Dumitrescu, Nicoleta Ilie, Ioan Ardelean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of microcapsules for the controlled release of active substances offers an innovative strategy in restorative dentistry, expanding the possibilities beyond traditional methods. In this study, microcapsules loaded with triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) and different concentrations of hydroxyapatite (HAP)—0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%, referred to as M0, M5, M10, M15, and M20—were synthesized through in situ polymerization within an oil-in-water emulsion. Their morphology, size, and nanostructure were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM). Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) confirmed the presence of characteristic chemical bonds, whereas high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) quantified residual TEGDMA monomer. Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) further confirmed the presence and the distribution of the liquid healing agent inside the microcapsules. The analyses indicated that microcapsules incorporating 20% hydroxyapatite exhibited superior structural organization and improved shell integrity, highlighting their potential in the remineralization processes. Overall, these results support the potential of HAP–TEGDMA microcapsules for incorporation into dental composites to facilitate microcrack repair and promote dental tissue regeneration.

1. Introduction

Resin-based composites are widely used restorative materials due to their aesthetic qualities, direct clinical applicability, and acceptable mechanical performance [1,2]. Nevertheless, their clinical service life remains limited to approximately 5–10 years, primarily because of secondary caries, mechanical fatigue, and structural degradation [3,4]. Intraoral stressors—including masticatory loading, thermal fluctuations, and biofilm accumulation—promote microcrack initiation and propagation, facilitating bacterial infiltration and recurrent caries formation [5,6,7,8,9,10].

Although self-healing strategies have been proposed to mitigate crack-related failure, current self-healing dental composites face significant unresolved limitations. These include insufficient mechanical reinforcement, reduced healing efficiency under cyclic loading, limited long-term stability in the oral environment, and inadequate integration between healing agents and the resin matrix. Moreover, many systems fail to simultaneously address structural durability and biological protection against demineralization and bacterial colonization.

Therefore, the development of multifunctional self-healing restorative materials that combine mechanical resilience, sustained structural integrity, and therapeutic potential remains an unmet need in contemporary dental materials research [8,11]. One promising strategy involves the creation of self-healing composites capable of autonomously repairing microcracks without external intervention [12]. Microcapsule-based self-healing systems are particularly noteworthy: when microcracks form, the embedded microcapsules rupture and release a healing agent that flows into the damaged region through capillary action, restoring structural continuity in a process analogous to biological self-repair [8,13]. Despite their effectiveness, microcapsule-based systems face limitations related to the mechanical fragility of capsule shells, which may deform or rupture prematurely during synthesis or handling. To enhance long-term stability, recent studies have focused on improving capsule design by employing multilayer architectures [14], thicker or more protective coatings [15], or reinforced shell compositions incorporating SiO2 nanoparticles or silk fibroin [16,17,18]. These strategies have demonstrated improved shell robustness and, in some cases, introduced additional functional benefits. Building upon these advancements, the incorporation of bioactive inorganic fillers such as hydroxyapatite (HAP; Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2) offers a compelling opportunity to integrate mechanical reinforcement with biological functionality. As the principal mineral component of human teeth and bone, hydroxyapatite is inherently biocompatible and bioactive [19,20,21]. Owing to its chemical similarity to natural apatite, HAP has been extensively used in biomedical and dental applications, including bone grafts, dental cements, and oral care formulations [22,23]. When incorporated into restorative materials, hydroxyapatite can not only enhance mechanical performance but also promote remineralization and support tissue regeneration [24,25,26]. Dentistry is a rapidly evolving field driven by continuous research advances. In this context, TEGDMA-based microcapsules incorporating or functionalized with nano-enzymes represent an innovative strategy for dental applications, offering controlled monomer release, antibacterial activity, reduced risk of secondary caries, and improved longevity of restorations [27,28].

In this study, we propose a novel strategy for engineering self-healing dental composites by incorporating hydroxyapatite during the fabrication of microcapsules containing triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) as the liquid healing core. Unlike earlier approaches, which primarily incorporated HAP as a passive filler within the composite matrix or employed conventional polymeric shells without inorganic reinforcement, our study embeds hydroxyapatite directly into the microcapsule membrane. This results in a hybrid inorganic–organic shell that simultaneously enhances mechanical strength and provides bioactive functionality at the capsule interface—a key conceptual advance over prior systems.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis further confirmed minimal residual monomer release, demonstrating the enhanced robustness and integrity of the HAP-reinforced microcapsules [29].

Based on these considerations, we hypothesize that incorporating hydroxyapatite into PUF microcapsule shells will enhance mechanical strength, reduce premature rupture, and maintain the efficacy of the TEGDMA-based self-healing mechanism in dental composites.

2. Results

2.1. X-Ray Characterization

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the synthesized hydroxyapatite, shown in Figure 1, confirms the formation of a highly crystalline phase.

The pattern exhibits clearly defined diffraction peaks characteristic of hydroxyapatite, with the most intense reflection observed at 32.46°, corresponding to the (211) crystallographic plane. Using the Scherrer equation, the average crystallite size was estimated to be 27.44 nm, indicating nanoscale crystallinity consistent with previous reports. Such nanoscale crystallinity is advantageous for microcapsule incorporation, as it facilitates uniform dispersion within the polymer membrane and enhances both mechanical reinforcement and bioactive interaction with tooth structures.

2.2. Morphological Characterization (SEM, AFM)

In Figure 2, SEM analysis revealed that microcapsule morphology was strongly influenced by the hydroxyapatite (HAP) content. The control sample containing only the healing liquid (M0) consisted of fine microcapsules prone to agglomeration, exhibiting an average diameter of 1.5 ± 0.571 µm. The incorporating 5% HAP (M5) induced noticeable morphological changes, including localized ruptures and surface deformations, with a reduced average capsule diameter of 1.1 ± 0.326 µm observed at higher magnification.

At 10% HAP (M10), the microcapsule structure became distinctly porous, with clearly visible HAP deposits embedded within the membrane. The microcapsules were generally spherical exhibiting an average diameter of 0.95 ± 0.04 µm. Further increasing the HAP content to 15% (M15) resulted in greater surface heterogeneity, characterized by interconnected pores ranging from 8 to 14 µm and polymeric wave-like structures surrounding predominantly spherical capsules 1.6 ± 0.327 µm. During in situ polymerization, TEGDMA molecules crosslink to form a solid polymer network, and the observed pores are considered intrinsic features of this network. At the highest HAP concentration (20%, M20), the system exhibited the most uniform and homogeneous capsule distribution, with an average diameter of 1.2 ± 0.326 µm.

AFM analysis (Figure 3) confirmed that HAP nanoparticles improved membrane toughness and enhanced microcapsule individualization.

This observation is consistent with previous studies reporting that nano-additives can reinforce the mechanical robustness of capsule shells, enhancing properties such as stiffness and resistance to deformation relative to neat polymeric shells. The improvement is generally attributed to enhanced load transfer and more uniform stress distribution within the shell layer due to the presence of nanoparticles [30].

Surface roughness was low (Ra ≈ 53.6 nm; Rq ≈ 66.7 nm), and single-capsule imaging showed spherical morphology with a diameter of approximately 900 nm, consistent with SEM. HAP nanoparticles (40–60 nm) were homogeneously embedded within the capsule membrane, yielding a membrane roughness of Ra ≈ 21.2 nm and Rq ≈ 24.9 nm.

2.3. Chemical Structure (FTIR)

FTIR spectra for all samples (M0–M20) exhibited the same characteristic absorption bands, confirming similar overall chemical composition.

Increasing HAP content produced corresponding increases in the intensity of PO43− bands. Detected functional groups included PO43−, OH−, CO32−, and HPO42− (consistent with non-stoichiometric HAP), as well as ester and aromatic vibrations from the polymer matrix (TEGDMA, DHEPT).

The FTIR spectra of obtained microcapsules are presented in Figure 4. Their chemical bonds interact with the infrared beam, inducing specific vibrations, such as stretching and bending, that generate specific absorption bands.

All samples M0, M5, M10, M15, M20 have evidenced the same absorption bands proving their similar chemical structure corresponding to composition of samples. But the intensity of absorption bands specific to PO43− group for samples was in accordance with the amount of HAP contained.

Adsorbed water band is present between 3340 and 3309 cm−1 correspond to stretching vibration of O-H group [31]. Also evidenced was the specific bands at 2946–2950 cm−1 that were assignment to asymmetric stretching vibrations of -CH3 group [32,33,34]. The absorption bands from the ranges 560 and 600 cm−1 and 1000–1100 cm−1 were assigned to the PO43− groups [35]. Bands with the highest intensity occurring within the wavelengths of 1120–1043 cm−1 are associated with asymmetric stretching vibrations of P-O group [36].

The specific bands of the 1714/1716 cm−1 and 1542 cm−1 are assigned to the ester C=O and aromatic C=C stretching vibrations from TEGDMA compound [37]. In the region of 1500–700 cm−1 one can observe the vibrations due to stretching of groups of methoxy O-CH3, ether C-O-C, and C=C stretching of aromatic ring, in DHEPT compound or in TEGDMA containing compounds [38,39]. The vibrations of 811/813 cm−1 correspond to the out-of-plane bending vibration of C-H groups from studied compounds [34].

2.4. Monomer Release (HPLC)

HPLC analysis showed that the amount of TEGDMA released per gram of microcapsules decreased slightly with increasing HAP content (Table 1), ranging from 0.941 mg/g (M0) to 0.805 mg/g (M20). This trend suggests improved membrane integrity and reduced susceptibility to cracking during fabrication when HAP is incorporated.

2.5. Low-Field NMR Investigations

Low-field NMR measurements were employed to assess the presence of a liquid phase within the sample and to quantify the liquid-to-solid ratio [40]. The liquid component was distinguished based on relaxation time distributions derived from Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) experiments [41], coupled with numerical inverse Laplace transformation analysis [42].

CPMG measurements (Figure 5) revealed a major relaxation peak corresponding to liquid confined within the microcapsules. The position of the T2 relaxation maximum (T2max) exhibited variations with HAP concentration. However, these changes were not systematic and did not follow a clear trend. Consequently, no direct correlation between T2max and HAP content can be established based on the present data (Figure 6a). Similarly, the peak area—proportional to the liquid content—displayed only minor, non-systematic variations across the investigated samples (Figure 6b).

These results were corroborated by FID measurements. Analysis of the liquid-only contribution relative to the total signal revealed trends comparable to those obtained from the CPMG data (Figure 7). Taken together, these observations indicate that HAP incorporation does not significantly affect the amount of liquid encapsulated within the microcapsules.

3. Discussion

The findings collectively indicate that hydroxyapatite (HAP) actively modifies shell formation during interfacial polymerization rather than serving solely as a passive inorganic filler. Mechanistically, HAP nanoparticles likely function as heterogeneous nucleation sites at the oil–water interface, locally influencing polymerization kinetics and network development. At low concentrations (≤5%), the particles are sparsely distributed and primarily provide localized reinforcement without significantly altering membrane growth dynamics. In this regime, polymer chain propagation and crosslinking dominate shell architecture, resulting in limited morphological modification.

As HAP content increases, nanoparticle–polymer interactions become more significant. The hydroxyl-rich and high-surface-energy character of HAP promotes interfacial adsorption and secondary interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding or electrostatic attraction) with reactive oligomers. These interactions restrict polymer chain mobility near particle surfaces and modify local crosslink density, leading to progressive membrane stiffening. The reduced coalescence and improved capsule individualization observed at higher HAP contents can therefore be attributed to enhanced inorganic–organic interfacial integration. In this context, HAP functions as a nano-reinforcing scaffold that constrains post-formation deformation and redistributes polymerization-induced stresses during shell maturation.

At intermediate concentrations (10–15%), increasing inorganic loading begins to affect matrix continuity. Excessive particle incorporation may locally hinder complete polymer chain interdiffusion, generating microvoids or interparticle gaps that manifest as increased porosity. While moderate porosity can facilitate capsule dispersion and imaging contrast, it may introduce structural discontinuities that act as stress concentration sites under cyclic occlusal loading. Over extended service periods, particularly under thermo-mechanical fatigue in simulated oral conditions, such discontinuities could promote crack initiation or propagation within the composite matrix. This analysis underscores the importance of optimizing inorganic content to balance reinforcement with structural cohesion.

The formulation containing 20% HAP appears to reach an optimal hybrid configuration. At this composition, nanoparticle dispersion is sufficiently uniform to reinforce the membrane effectively, yet polymer matrix continuity remains largely preserved. This suggests the establishment of a percolation-like inorganic–organic network in which stress transfer is homogenized and defect formation minimized. Consequently, this formulation provides a favorable balance between membrane toughness, structural uniformity, and dimensional control—characteristics particularly relevant for dental composite applications.

Spectroscopic analysis confirms that HAP incorporation does not alter the fundamental polymer chemistry of the shell, indicating that mechanical enhancement arises primarily from physical integration and interfacial effects rather than changes in covalent structure. The detection of carbonate and hydrogen phosphate groups suggests a non-stoichiometric apatite composition analogous to biological mineral phases, which may enhance interfacial compatibility with enamel during clinical application.

Reduced monomer release further supports improved shell integrity. Mechanistically, well-dispersed nanoparticles likely mitigate localized shrinkage stresses generated during polymerization, decreasing the probability of microcrack formation. Thus, HAP contributes not only to mechanical reinforcement of the mature shell but also to stabilization of the shell formation pathway itself.

NMR relaxometry and FID analyses demonstrate that although increasing HAP content influences capsule size—potentially through modified interfacial tension and droplet stabilization—it does not significantly compromise encapsulation efficiency. This indicates that nanoparticle incorporation predominantly affects membrane structure without substantially reducing core volume, thereby preserving the functional payload required for healing.

The proposed self-healing and remineralization pathway should be considered a working hypothesis derived from structural and compositional correlations. We propose that mechanical damage induces fracture along polymer–particle interfaces within the hybrid membrane, triggering capsule rupture and release of the healing agent. The released liquid may subsequently interact with exposed HAP from the capsule shell and with enamel apatite, facilitating localized mineral deposition and crack sealing. However, direct mechanistic validation remains necessary. Future investigations should include in situ fracture experiments combined with high-resolution imaging to observe rupture behavior, spectroscopic mapping (e.g., micro-Raman or FTIR imaging) to verify localized remineralization, mechanical testing before and after healing cycles, and long-term aging studies under simulated oral conditions.

Overall, HAP modifies shell formation through interfacial adsorption, nucleation-assisted polymer growth, nanoparticle–polymer interactions, and stress redistribution during polymerization. An optimized inorganic loading—identified here at 20%—produces a structurally coherent hybrid membrane that enhances mechanical robustness while maintaining encapsulation efficiency, supporting its suitability for self-healing dental composite systems.

4. Materials and Methods

All chemicals used in the formulation of microcapsules such as urea (CH4N2O 99%), ammonium chloride (NH4Cl 99%), resorcinol (C6H6O2 99%), 37% aqueous formaldehyde solution, Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA degree of hydrolysis 87–89%), healing agent consisting in a mixture of N,N-dihydroxyethyl-p-toluidine (DHEPT 90%) and triethylene glycol di-methacrylate (TEGDMA 95%), hydrochloric acid (HCl 35–38%) were purchased from Sig-ma Aldrich (Steinhem, Germany). Hydroxyapatite was synthesized in the Polymer Composites Laboratory at the Raluca Ripan Institute for Research in Chemistry, Cluj-Napoca, Romania.

4.1. Hydroxyapatite (HAP) Synthesis

Hydroxyapatite powder was synthesized using a precipitation method. Calcium carbonate was first calcined at 1000–1100 °C to obtain CaO, which was then milled, sieved, and hydrated to form Ca(OH)2. After filtration, washing, and drying, the resulting Ca(OH)2 (0.5 M) was used as the calcium precursor. In a thermostatic glass reactor, the Ca(OH)2 suspension was stirred for 1 h before adding orthophosphoric acid (0.3 M) dropwise, maintaining a Ca/P ratio of 1.67. The reaction was carried out at 60 °C for 4 h, with the pH kept at 9.5 using 25% ammonia solution. The resulting fine white precipitate was matured for 24 h, vacuum-filtered, washed, and dried at 100 °C for 12 h.

4.2. Development of the Self-Healing System PUF–TEGDMA

The synthesis procedure was adapted from Taut et al. [10], with minor modifications. The aqueous phase was prepared by combining 13 mL of a 2.5% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) solution with 50 mL of distilled water in a Berzelius beaker and stirring at 400 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, urea (1.25 g), ammonium chloride (0.125 g), and resorcinol (0.125 g) were added and stirred until complete dissolution. The pH of the solution was then adjusted to 3.5 using 35–38% hydrochloric acid (HCl). The organic phase, consisting of 30 mL of triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) containing 1% N,N-dihydroxyethyl-p-toluidine (DHEPT) as the healing agent, was added dropwise to the aqueous phase under continuous stirring to form a stable oil-in-water emulsion. Stirring was maintained at 500 rpm for 10 min while gradually increasing the temperature. A dispersion consisting of 3.78 g of 37% formaldehyde and hydroxyapatite was prepared and subsequently introduced into the system. This procedure was carried out for each hydroxyapatite concentration (5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%). The HAP is expressed as a mass-to-mass ratio (w/w). Following the addition of the dispersion, the beaker was covered with aluminum foil to minimize evaporation during the reaction process. The reaction mixture was maintained at 60 °C under stirring at 800 rpm for 4 h, allowing ammonium chloride to catalyze the polycondensation of urea and formaldehyde, thereby forming poly(urea-formaldehyde) (PUF) microcapsules [4,8]. All syntheses were made in triplicate. The resulting emulsion was cooled to room temperature, washed several times with distilled water, filtered under vacuum, and air-dried.

4.3. XRD Analysis

XRD investigations were performed on Bruker D8 Advance (Bruker Company, Karlsruhe, Germany) using Cu kα radiation (1.540562 Å). The experimental XRD pattern (red line) was analyzed with Match version 1.0 software equipped with Powder Diffraction Files database PDF 2.0 (Crystal Impact Company, Bonn, Germany). The results show that all peaks belong to the pure hydroxyapatite PDF 09-04.32.

4.4. SEM and AFM

Microscopic analyses were performed using an Inspect™ scanning electron microscope (SEM) (FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA) operated in low-vacuum mode at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. For both SEM and AFM investigations, a thin film of dry, fine microcapsules was deposited onto double-sided adhesive tape to ensure proper immobilization during analysis. Surface topography was examined using an atomic force microscope (AFM), model JSPM-4210 (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), operating in tapping mode. Scanning was carried out with NSC15 cantilevers (resonant frequency: 325 kHz; force constant: 40 N/m) supplied by MikroMasch (Tallinn, Estonia). The acquired AFM images were processed using the JEOL WIN SPM 2.0 software package (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), measuring the surface roughness parameters Ra and Rq according to the literature data [43,44].

4.5. FTIR Spectroscopy

Chemical bonding was analyzed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) using an FTIR-610 spectrometer (Jasco Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an at-tenuated total reflectance (ATR) unit with a horizontal ZnSe crystal (Jasco PRO400S). The samples were placed directly on the ZnSe crystal and the spectra were recorded in the 4000–500 cm−1 range at a resolution of 4 cm−1, averaging 100 scans per sample, all measured at room temperature.

4.6. High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)

The released TEGDMA monomer was monitored by high-performance liquid chromatography by using a Jasco HPLC chromatograph (Jasco Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The separation was used a HPLC Tracer Excel column (120 ODSB, 5 µm, 25 × 0.46 cm) (Technokroma, Barcelona, Spain) at 22 °C column temperature, a flow rate of 1 mL/min and UV detection at 204 nm. Elution was done by gradient using acetonitrile (A, HPLC-grade) as mobile phase and water according to the method: 0–15 min, linear gradient 50–80% A; 15–25 min, linear gradient 80–500% A.

Sample preparation: A quantity of 10 mg sample with 10 mL of acetonitrile (extraction solvent) was well stirred and then the solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm PTFE syringe filter (Chromafil Xtra PTFE, 25 mm, 0.45 μm; Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) and injected into HPLC equipment. All the determinations were performed in triplicate and the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

4.7. Low-Field Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR)

Two complementary low-field NMR methodologies were employed to assess both the presence of a liquid phase within the samples and the corresponding liquid-to-solid proportions [40]. Identification of the liquid fraction relied on relaxation time distributions obtained via the Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence [41], further processed by numerical inverse Laplace transformation [42]. The liquid-to-solid ratio was quantified by analyzing the free induction decay (FID) signal acquired immediately after a hard 90° radiofrequency pulse, following the procedure reported in Ref. [40]. In the CPMG sequence, a total of 2000 echoes were collected with echo time intervals of 0.2 ms. The FID signal was obtained following a 2.5 μs hard RF pulse, with acquisition from 5.4 to 405 μs. For each experiment, 32 scans were averaged with a relaxation delay of 5 s. All measurements were conducted at a controlled temperature of 35 °C using a low-field NMR spectrometer (MINISPEC MQ20, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany) operating at a proton resonance frequency of 20 MHz.

5. Conclusions

Microcapsules containing triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA) and varying concentrations of hydroxyapatite (HAP) (0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%), designated as M0, M5, M10, M15, and M20, were synthesized via an in situ polymerization method within an oil-in-water emulsion.

SEM analysis reveals that the morphology of the microcapsules is influenced by the hydroxyapatite content, with their dimensions ranging from 0.2 to 1.2 µm. AFM confirms that the polymer embeds fine microcapsules similarly to SEM observations, with microcapsules around 900 nm and HAP nanoparticles between 40 and 60 nm. NMR experiments demonstrate that the presence of hydroxyapatite does not significantly affect the liquid component of the sample, but it reduces the size of the capsules.

All samples (M0–M20) displayed identical absorption bands for –CH2/–CH3 groups, C=O, and aromatic C=C stretching, while the PO43− bands reflected the respective HAP content. HPLC results indicate that the amount of TEGDMA monomer released from the samples varies between 0.941 and 0.805 mg of monomer per 1 g of microcapsules suggesting that hydroxyapatite contributes to the quality of the microcapsules by forming stronger shells with a reduced tendency to crack.

The integration of hydroxyapatite into TEGDMA-containing microcapsules offers a promising strategy for developing multifunctional dental restorative systems that combine mechanical reinforcement with bioactive potential.

However, clinical translation requires further investigation. Key challenges include assessing long-term stability, validating the self-healing and remineralization mechanism in situ, and evaluating compatibility with experimental composite matrices in terms of handling, esthetics, and polymerization behavior. Addressing these challenges will be essential to realize the practical implementation of HAP-reinforced microcapsules in restorative dentistry.
