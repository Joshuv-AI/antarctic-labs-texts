Discovery of Small-Molecule Antagonists of PHF1 and 19 Demonstrates the Ligandability of PRC2 Accessory Proteins
Authors: Isabelle E. Amick, Rebecca L. Johnson, Jiuyang Liu, Cameron R. Bussey-Sutton, Ikeer Y. Mancera-Ortiz, Jacqueline L. Norris-Drouin, Shelton R. Boyd, Stephanie H. Cholensky, Cathy J. Spangler, Robert K. McGinty, Brian D. Strahl, Tatiana G. Kutateladze, Stephen V. Frye, Lindsey I. James
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PHF1 and PHF19 are key accessory components of polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) which is responsible for trimethylation at histone H3 lysine 27, ultimately leading to gene repression. PHF1 and 19 are known for their methyl-lysine reader Tudor domains that recognize methylated lysine 36 on H3, and they have also been implicated as drivers of a range of cancers, including multiple myeloma. Here, we describe the first small-molecule antagonist of the Tudor domains of PHF1 and 19. We screened an internal library of ∼ 1 K compounds against the Tudor domains using a TR-FRET assay. Upon validation of a hit compound, we performed structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies to better understand the binding mode and improve the affinity of the compound for the PHF1 and 19 Tudor domains. One such compound, UNC7242, which binds PHF1 with a K d of 1.13 ± 0.04 μM and PHF19 with a K d of 0.64 ± 0.14 μM, emerged as a lead compound. UNC7242 clearly demonstrates the ligandability of these disease-relevant proteins and serves as a starting point for the future development of potent and selective PHF1 and 19 chemical probes.

Introduction

Tight control of gene expression is required for the specification and maintenance of cellular identity and healthy cellular function. This process is regulated through a complex network of transcription factors and chromatin-associating proteins. One key player in the epigenetic regulation of gene expression is polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), a multisubunit complex that controls gene silencing through the deposition of mono-, di-, and trimethylation on lysine 27 on histone H3 (H3K27me1/2/3).

While the core components of PRC2 including EZH2, EED, and SUZ12 have been extensively studied, − much less is known about the importance of the interchangeable accessory subunits that are capable of promoting or directing catalytic activity of PRC2. Among these accessory proteins are the polycomb-like (PCL) proteins 1, 2, and 3, also referred to as PHF1, MTF2, and PHF19, respectively. The PCL proteins all contain a Tudor domain, two plant homeodomain (PHD) fingers, a PCL extended domain, and a carboxy-terminal domain tail (Figure a). The Tudor domains of both PHF1 and PHF19 bind the H3K36me3 mark, while other domains engage PRC2 and are responsible for PRC2 recruitment and catalytic activation to propagate epigenetic silencing. The PCL proteins participate in PRC2 in a mutually exclusive fashion, suggesting possible functional redundancy. Their redundancy is thought to be a result of tissue-specific differences in PCL protein expression levels.

Interestingly, PHF1 and PHF19 have also been implicated in promoting tumorigenesis in a variety of cancers. Aberrant fusions of PHF1 with histone acetyltransferase-containing proteins JAZF1, EPC1, MEAF6, BRD8, and EP400 have been characterized as drivers of endometrial stromal sarcoma (ESS) and ossifying fibromyxoid tumors. − ESS can be particularly difficult to diagnose due to variable histologic appearance, long latency to recurrence, and frequent metastases. More recently, an influx of papers have demonstrated a significant correlation between PHF19 overexpression and poor prognosis for multiple myeloma (MM) patients. − MM is a plasma cell malignancy diagnosed in 30,000–40,000 people in the US annually. Despite advances in treatment, about 15–20% of patients present with an aggressive disease phenotype resulting in poor prognosis regardless of initial treatment; therefore, new treatment strategies are of high interest. PHF19 is attractive as a potential MM drug target due to its consistently high expression across a genetically diverse set of MM subtypes. , Importantly, it was recently demonstrated that the Tudor domain of PHF19 is essential for its oncogenic function in MM. Specifically, knockdown of PHF19 impairs tumorigenicity in cells and in vivo, and this phenotype can be rescued by wildtype PHF19 but not constructs harboring Tudor domain deletions or point mutations within its aromatic cage. The inability of aromatic cage point mutants to rescue the PHF19 knockdown phenotype suggests that the methyl-lysine reading ability of the Tudor domain is important for tumorigenesis and provides a strong rationale for the development of small molecules capable of binding the aromatic cage of the Tudor domain. Overall, antagonists for the PHF1 and 19 Tudor domains could be valuable tools to further explore the role of these proteins in cancer and help validate these targets for future drug discovery efforts.

Our lab has previously reported ligands for the Kme reader family of proteins including Tudor domain containing proteins. − Herein, we validate the ligandability of the PHF1 and 19 Tudor domains through the discovery, optimization, and characterization of UNC7242, a first-in-class small-molecule antagonist of PHF1 and PHF19. Following optimization of a high-throughput time-resolved fluorescence resonance energy transfer (TR-FRET) assay described previously, we discovered a low micromolar binder of both PHF1 and 19, which provided a starting point for medicinal chemistry and structure–activity relationship (SAR) studies. Using a combination of rational design and computer-aided design, we developed UNC7242, which binds the PHF1 and PHF19 Tudor domains with dissociation constants (K ds) of 1.13 ± 0.04 and 0.64 ± 0.14 μM, respectively. These ligands have the potential to enable future development of potent PHF1 and 19 antagonists that will be invaluable for both improved understanding and manipulation of these proteins in healthy and malignant cells.

Results and Discussion

Library Screening and Hit Selection and Validation

We have comprehensively demonstrated the robustness and utility of a TR-FRET assay platform to screen for small-molecule antagonists against methyl-lysine reader proteins. Briefly, this assay makes use of a biotinylated bait ligand whose displacement from a His-tagged Kme reader domain by small-molecule antagonists can be measured through a loss of the FRET signal (Figure b). Development of a sufficiently potent peptidomimetic bait ligand for PHF1 and PHF19 to enable assay development was described previously (UNC6641; Figure c). UNC6641 binds PHF1 and 19 in the histone binding pocket of the Tudor domain (Supp. Figure 1a), mimicking the native H3K36me3 substrate; therefore, small-molecule hits capable of displacing UNC6641 are also expected to bind in the Kme binding pocket. Utilizing a biotinylated version of UNC6641 (Figure c), TR-FRET assays for the PHF1 and PHF19 Tudor domains were previously established and optimized (Supp. Table 1). Under these conditions, UNC6641 gave IC50 values of 0.97 ± 0.01 μM for PHF1 Tudor and 2.58 ± 0.05 μM for PHF19 Tudor which are in good agreement with the reported K d values of 0.96 ± 0.03 and 2.1 ± 0.2 μM, respectively (Supp. Figure 1b).

We next utilized our TR-FRET assays to screen an in-house library of 960 small molecules against the PHF1 and PHF19 Tudor domains. This library is enriched with compounds containing basic amines that have the potential to mimic methylated lysine substrates and effectively bind Kme reader proteins. The general workflow utilized to identify hit compounds is shown in Figure a. First, we screened each compound at a single concentration of 100 μM in duplicate. Hits were then ranked by percent inhibition normalized to the UNC6641 (100 μM) positive control. From this, we selected 32 compounds for evaluation in a dose–response format. This included 24 screening hits with greater than 60% inhibition for PHF1 and/or PHF19 and 7 compounds from our inventory that are structurally similar to the hit compounds but were not evaluated in the primary screen. Two compounds, UNC00000209 (1; Figure b) and UNC10110457, produced IC50 values less than 10 μM for both PHF1 and 19 (Supp. Figure 2). Binding of both compounds was assessed in an orthogonal assay, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), which flagged UNC10110457 as a false positive as no significant binding was observed. In contrast, ITC confirmed UNC00000209 (1) as a validated hit compound, revealing K d values of 2.1 ± 0.1 and 1.6 ± 0.1 μM for the PHF1 and PHF19 Tudor domains, respectively (Figure c). Due to the high sequence homology of the two domains and the conserved aromatic cage residues, it was not surprising that UNC00000209 bound both domains equipotently.

UNC00000209 (1) SARs

To understand the key requirements for UNC00000209 (1) binding to PHF1 and 19 and to attempt to develop ligands with improved potency, we thoroughly and rationally investigated each portion of the dimethoxyquinazoline methyl-homopiperazine scaffold. First, we explored the C2 position as currently all published quinazoline-based inhibitors of epigenetic targets include modifications at this position, typically in the form of large heterocycles. In contrast, UNC00000209 (1) contains a chlorine at this position. Interestingly, the only modification tolerated at this position was a halogen swap from Cl to Br (2), resulting in a less than 2-fold loss in potency (Table ). Both PHF1 and PHF19 Tudor domains did not tolerate larger groups at this position including a methyl group and various amines (3–6), and strikingly, replacement of the chlorine with a hydrogen completely abolished binding (7).

We hypothesized that the methyl-homopiperazine ring of UNC00000209 (1) could be binding in the methyl-lysine pocket and potentially forming key interactions with the aromatic cage; therefore, we next replaced the methyl-homopiperazine ring with a variety of rings of similar size and composition (Table ). Overall, these modifications did not result in improved potency for PHF1 or 19. Interestingly, some modifications such as the introduction of a slightly smaller methyl-homopiperazine as in compound 8 were more well tolerated by PHF1 (3-fold loss in potency) than by PHF19 (12-fold loss in potency), suggesting that the PHF1 binding pocket may have more inherent flexibility to bind various chemical scaffolds and that this may be a route to achieve PHF1 selective ligands. Additionally, we observed that cyclizing the terminal amine (9) as well as making it exocyclic to the ring (10) resulted in a 2- to 5-fold loss in affinity. We also discovered that the terminal basic amine is required for binding as introduction of an azepane substituent (11) resulted in a loss of binding for both domains. Last, introduction of 1-methylpiperidin-4-amine (13) or the methylated analogue (12) also did not improve binding for either PHF1 or 19. Overall, this suggests that the methyl-homopiperazine ring in UNC00000209 (1) is more optimally positioned to make key interactions required for binding to the Kme reader pockets of PHF1 and 19.

Based on these results, we next substituted the methyl group on the homopiperazine ring with other functional groups to see if additional favorable interactions could be introduced to improve binding. Table shows the trends observed with these modifications, demonstrating an overall preference for slightly larger cyclic hydrophobic substituents. Removal of the methyl group to yield a secondary rather than tertiary amine (14) was well tolerated and roughly equipotent. Other small hydrophobic substituents were also generally well tolerated (15, 17, 19) with the exception of the isopropyl analogue (16) which resulted in a modest 3- to 5-fold drop in potency. Only small decreases in potency (less than 3-fold) were observed upon introduction of larger, flexible alkyl substituents, as seen with compounds 25 and 30. Encouragingly, replacement of the methyl substituent with methylcyclopropyl (UNC7242, 20), methylcyclobutyl (21), or methylcyclopentyl (22) increased the potency 2- to 3-fold for the PHF1 Tudor domain and 10- to 14-fold for the PHF19 Tudor domain. However, increasing the ring size further to a cyclohexyl group (27) decreased the potency to greater than 20 μM for both PHF1 and 19, demonstrating a clear size restriction in the binding pocket. Interestingly, swapping the cyclohexyl group for a benzyl group (28) resulted in low micromolar binding for PHF1 (3.2 μM), yet a 3-fold loss in affinity for PHF19 relative to compound 1 (21 μM). While it is challenging to know precisely why the benzyl group is more well tolerated by PHF1, considering the conserved nature of the two Tudor domains, it is possible that the aromatic ring is more well positioned to engage in potential favorable interactions with PHF1. Finally, the addition of heteroatoms to substituents off the homopiperazine ring (18, 23, 24, 26, 29, 31) resulted in consistent decreases in potency, confirming the preference for a hydrophobic group at this position of the molecule.

Last, we explored modifications to the dimethoxy quinazoline core itself (Table ). Removal of the aromaticity of the quinazoline B-ring (32) as well as both the methoxy groups at the C6 and C7 positions on the quinazoline scaffold (33) essentially abolished binding to both proteins (IC50 s > 80 μM). Interestingly, individual removal of each methoxy group revealed that the C7 methoxy is dispensable in this context (34), while the C6 methoxy is more important for binding (35); however, neither single methoxy elimination was as detrimental to the overall potency as removal of both methoxy groups (33). We also introduced heterocycles that have the potential to engage in hydrogen bonding interactions with the surrounding residues, including imidazole (36) and furan (37). These modifications reduced binding affinities by at least 7-fold in each case. We therefore decided to maintain the quinazoline scaffold, and given the C7 methoxy group is not critical, we explored various replacements of the C6 methoxy in the context of the optimized methylcyclopropyl homopiperazine unsubstituted at the C7 position (38, Table ). Compound 38 binds PHF1 and 19 with affinities of 1.1 and 1.50 μM, respectively, which is comparable to the dimethoxy analogue, compound 20. Both smaller methyl and fluorine substituents (39, 41) and larger ethoxy, nitro, and ester groups (40, 42, 43) were generally well tolerated. Interestingly, the ester substituent (43) resulted in a mild 3-fold boost in potency for both proteins relative to compound 38. Overall, no significant changes in potency were observed upon modification at the C6 position, suggesting that this substituent is unlikely to be making highly specific contacts with the protein. Finally, we explored a few additional modifications to the C2 position in the context of the methylcyclopropyl homopiperazine. Both replacement of the chlorine with trifluoromethyl (44) and hydroxyl (45) groups significantly reduced binding to PHF1 and 19, again underscoring the requirement of a halogen at this position (Supp. Figure 3).

Overall, the potency trends observed for binding to PHF1 and PHF19 were similar with a few exceptions. PHF19 exhibited a somewhat greater intolerance to modifications to the homopiperazine (e.g., 8, 12, and 13). In contrast, compound 23 which contains a methylpyrrolidine substituent on the homopiperazine completely lost affinity for PHF1, yet was equipotent to UNC00000209 (1) for PHF19. These findings highlight potential subtle differences within the binding pockets of the two Tudor domains that could be harnessed in the future for the development of PHF1 or PHF19 selective antagonists.

Due to the modest potency gains observed with compounds 20–22, we initially selected these compounds for orthogonal validation by ITC (Figure ). Unfortunately, compound 22 had poor solubility in the ITC buffer and was therefore not pursued further. However, compounds 20–21 demonstrated potent binding with dissociation constants closely mirroring their TR-FRET IC50 values. Compound 43, which similarly demonstrated submicromolar affinities by TR-FRET, was also evaluated by ITC, revealing dissociation constants of about 2 μM for PHF1 and 19 (Supp. Figure 4). With a K d of 1.13 ± 0.04 μM for PHF1 and 0.64 ± 0.14 μM for PHF19, compound 20 (UNC7242) was selected for further analysis. To ensure that UNC7242 (20) is not a pan-active Kme reader ligand, we profiled it against a small panel of Kme reader domains including the Tudor domains of MTF2, SETDB1, and UHRF1 and the chromodomains of CBX7 and MPP8 by either TR-FRET or ITC. Encouragingly, no binding was observed up to 100 μM in each case (Supp. Figure 5).

Binding Mode of UNC7242 (20)

To better understand the binding mode of UNC7242 (20) in the Kme reader pocket of the Tudor domains, we utilized NMR. We generated uniformly 15N-labeled PHF1 Tudor domain (aa 14–87) and collected its 1H,15N heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) spectra while titrating UNC7242 (20) into the protein. Large chemical shift perturbations (CSPs) in the intermediate exchange regime on the NMR time scale were induced in the Tudor domain by UNC7242 (20), indicating direct and tight binding (Supp. Figure 6). CSPs for backbone amides of the PHF1 Tudor domain (at a molar protein to UNC7242 ratio of 1:4) were plotted as a histogram (Figure a). The most perturbed residues of the Tudor domain were mapped onto the surface of the PHF1 Tudor domain (Figure b). These include A39, W41, Y47, L48, G49, and F65 which comprise the aromatic cage and adjacent residues, further confirming that UNC7242 (20) engages the aromatic cage as expected. Subsequent mutagenesis studies further supported our NMR results and that UNC7242 binds the PHF1 Tudor domain aromatic cage, as UNC7242 bound 11-fold less potently to a W41A Tudor domain mutant and binding was completely abolished when Y47 or F65 was mutated to an alanine (Supp. Figure 7).

The dashed line indicates an averaged chemical shift change plus 1 standard deviation. (b) Surface representation of the PHF1 Tudor domain. Residues that exhibit significant changes in (a) are colored orange. (c, d) Maestro docking of UNC7242 (20) into the PHF1 Tudor domain (PDB: 4HCZ) using the CSP footprint as a constraint guide. Using the binding interface predicted by the CSPs as a guide, we performed docking studies with UNC7242 (20) and the PHF1 Tudor domain (PDB ID: 4HCZ) using Maestro. To enforce the CSP footprint during sampling, we created two positional constraints on the docking grid: one was centered on the aromatic cage (W41, Y47, and F65) that could be satisfied only by aromatic atoms of the ligand, and the second was centered on the adjacent hydrophobic patch (L45, L46, and L48) that could only be satisfied by hydrophobic interactions. These acted as soft guides, encoding the topology of the NMR footprint while still allowing for conformational freedom with flexible sampling. The predicted binding poses were manually inspected, and the best match to the CSP map was selected for further analysis (Figure c,d). Gratifyingly, this binding pose is generally supported by our previous SAR findings. First, the quinazoline core appears to be involved in π–π (Figure c,d; dashed blue lines) and cation-π (Figure c,d; dashed green lines) interactions with aromatic cage residues W41, Y47, and F65 as supported by the elevated CSPs for these residues (Figure a). Unlike our peptidomimetic ligand UNC6641 which has an alkylated basic amine pointing into the aromatic cage to mimic key interactions of the native histone substrate (Supp. Figure 1a), the quinazoline core of UNC7242 appears to be binding with the aromatic cage residues in this case, which is not unprecedented in the context of known Kme reader ligands. Next, the cyclopropyl group extends out of the aromatic cage and into a hydrophobic patch made up of L38, L45, L46, and L48 (Figure c,d), likely explaining the increase in potency observed upon introduction of the cyclopropyl group, as well as the large CSP denoted for L48 (Figure a). Finally, while halogen bonds are not currently predicted by Glide docking software, the positioning of the Cl group toward the inner boundary of the binding pocket (Figure c,d) demonstrates the potential for halogen bonding with a nearby carbonyl backbone as well as the limited space for larger functional groups. While this model largely supports our hypothesis and experimental data around the binding mode of UNC7242, further structural biology data will clearly be needed to confirm the precise binding pose and explain some of the more subtle SAR.

UNC7242 (20) Is Unable to Displace PHF1 and 19 from Nucleosomes

As all prior studies were conducted with the isolated recombinant Tudor domains of PHF1 and 19 and methylated peptide substrates, we were next interested in understanding if (1) UNC7242 (20) is capable of binding full-length PHF1 and 19 and (2) UNC7242 (20) can displace PHF1 and 19 from H3K36me3-modified nucleosomes. Like many Kme reader proteins, PHF1 and 19 are multidomain proteins that contain four domains in addition to the Tudor domain which are involved in DNA and PRC2 binding, including a neighboring PHD domain (PHD1) (Figure a). X-ray crystallography studies have shown that the PHD1 domain of PHF1 and 19 stacks directly above the Tudor domain, creating a narrow interface for the H3K36me3 peptide to bind (Supp. Figure 8). The aromatic cage is also located near the interface of the two domains, suggesting that there could be differences in how our compounds access the binding pocket in the context of the full-length proteins. Therefore, we developed TR-FRET assays with longer constructs of each protein to assess the binding of UNC7242 (20) in the context of adjacent domains. We found that introduction of the PHD1 and PHD2 domains in PHF1 reduces the potency of UNC7242 (20) 6-fold (IC50 = 6.3 ± 0.3 μM), while introduction of the PHD1 domain to PHF19 reduces the potency 3.5-fold (IC50 = 2.1 ± 0.1 μM), suggesting that the presence of adjacent domains can influence ligand binding to some extent, potentially by making the binding site somewhat less accessible. Consequently, more potent Tudor domain binders may be required to enable sufficient binding to endogenous full-length proteins.

We were next interested in evaluating the ability of our compounds to bind these longer protein constructs and, in turn, displace them from methylated recombinant nucleosomes. Using biotinylated H3K36me3 recombinant nucleosomes conjugated to T1 streptavidin dynabeads, we first demonstrated the ability to pull-down purified PHF1 (Tudor + PHD1 + PHD2) and PHF19 (Tudor + PHD1). Upon addition of 100 or 200 μM UNC7242 (20), no significant changes were observed suggesting that UNC7242 (20) treatment alone is not able to significantly displace PHF1 and 19 from these methylated nucleosomes (Figure A). We also treated Jurkat N6 cell lysates with 100 μM UNC7242 (20), compound 22 which contains a cyclopentyl group in place of the cyclopropyl, or the structurally similar inactive analogue, compound 7, and upon isolation of chromatin, we evaluated the amount of full-length, endogenous PHF19 bound to chromatin in each case (Figure B). We observed only a slight decrease in the amount of endogenous PHF19 bound to chromatin in the presence of compound 22. Although a slight increase in the amount of PHF19 in the nonchromatin bound fraction was observed for all three compounds relative to DMSO, there was no significant difference in the amount of unbound PHF19 in the presence of our active compounds relative to the inactive control compound (7), again suggesting that the compounds are unable to significantly displace full-length PHF19 from chromatin. The inability of UNC7242 (20) to fully displace PHF1 and 19 from H3K36me3 nucleosomes or chromatin could be because antagonism of the Tudor domain alone is insufficient to elicit such an effect. This is aligned with the fact that PHF1 and PHF19 have been shown to interact with DNA on the surface of their Tudor domains, and other domains have known DNA binding activities, which could in turn anchor the proteins on chromatin despite Tudor domain antagonism. Alternatively, a more potent ligand may be required to promote a robust effect in these assays.

Conclusions

PHF1 and PHF19 are Kme reader domains that play an important role in normal and malignant biology; however, they have yet to be targeted with traditional small molecules, to the best of our knowledge. In this work, we implemented high-throughput TR-FRET assays for these proteins to discover first-in-class small-molecule antagonists, leading to the development and characterization of UNC7242 (20), which binds the Tudor domains of PHF1 and PHF19 with K d values of 1.13 ± 0.04 and 0.64 ± 0.14 μM, respectively.

UNC7242 (20) is a quinazoline-based small-molecule antagonist that binds the PHF1 and 19 Tudor domains in the aromatic cage of the Kme binding pocket. Through HSQC NMR, protein mutagenesis, and glide docking, we predict that the quinazoline core forms π–π and cation-π interactions with aromatic cage residues W41, Y47, and F65. Additionally, the characteristic methylcyclopropyl group extending off the homopiperazine ring improves binding, likely due to hydrophobic interactions with L48 and the surrounding hydrophobic patch. Additional structural biology information to confirm the precise binding mode of UNC7242 and related ligands will likely enable continued optimization of this scaffold to enhance binding to PHF1 and/or PHF19. While UNC7242 (20) retained moderate potency against longer constructs of PHF1 and 19 containing the neighboring PHD domain(s), the compound was unable to significantly displace these proteins from H3K36me3 nucleosomes or isolated chromatin, possibly due to significant binding of the PHF1 and 19 Tudors and adjacent domains to nucleosomal DNA. While compound mediated displacement of the PHF proteins from chromatin is one way to demonstrate target engagement in this context, other orthogonal assays such as NanoBRET assays could also be used in the future to assess cellular target engagement. It is also unclear if PHF1 or 19 displacement from chromatin is necessary for a PHF1 or 19 antagonist to elicit an on-target phenotype in other biological assays, such as cellular proliferation assays.

Continued emphasis on the development of a potent and cellularly active chemical probe for PHF1 and 19 is of great interest to both the epigenetics and clinical community. The role of the PCL proteins in PRC2 has only begun to be understood, and the current literature contains contradictory conclusions, suggesting that functional inhibition of PHF1 and 19 through chemical modulation could provide a clearer understanding of the role of these proteins, as well as the individual Tudor domains, in gene regulation. Additionally, the need for PHF1 and 19 antagonists in the clinic is consistently mounting as new papers emphasize the role of PHF1 in ESS and PHF19 in MM. Through the discovery of UNC7242 (20) and related analogues, we have effectively demonstrated the ligandability of these proteins and provided useful starting points for chemical probe discovery and, subsequently, drug development.

Synthetic Methods

General Procedures

Normal phase column chromatography was performed with a Teledyne ISCO CombiFlashRf 200 using RediSepRf SILICA columns with the UV detector set to 254 and 280 nm. Reverse phase column chromatography was performed with a Teledyne ISCO CombiFlashRf 200 using C18 RediSepRf Gold columns with the UV detector set to 220 and 254 nm. Preparative HPLC was performed using an Agilent Prep 1200 series with the UV detector set to 220 and 254 nm. Samples were injected onto a Phenomenex Luna 250 mm × 30 mm, 5 μm, C18 column at 25 °C. Mobile phases of A (H2O + 0.1% TFA) and B (CH3CN + 1% H2O) were used with a flow rate of 40 mL/min. A general gradient of 0–25 min increasing from 10 to 100% B followed by a 100% B flush for another 5 min was used. Small variations in this purification method were made as needed to achieve ideal separation for each compound. Analytical LC-MS data for all compounds were acquired using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II Series system with the UV detector set to 220 and 254 nm. Samples were injected (5 μL) onto an Agilent Eclipse Plus 4.6 mm × 3 mm × 50 mm, 1.8 mm, C18 column at 25 °C. Mobile phases A (H2O + 0.1% acetic acid) and B (C H3CN + 0.1% acetic acid) were used with a linear gradient from 10 to 100% B in 5.0 min followed by a flush at 100% B for another 2 min at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Mass spectra (MS) data were acquired in the positive ion mode using an Agilent 6110 single quadrupole mass spectrometer with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source using an AB Sciex 5800. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a Varian Mercury spectrometer at 400 MHz for proton (1H) and carbon (13C) NMR; chemical shifts are reported in ppm (δ) relative to residual protons in deuterated solvent peaks, and coupling constants are reported in Hz. All compounds reported had > 95% purity as determined by LC-MS.

2-Chloro-6,7-dimethoxy-4-(4-methyl-1,4-diazepan-1-yl)­quinazoline (1)

2,4-Dichloro-6,7-dimethoxyquinazoline (500 mg, 1 equiv, 1.93 mmol), 1-methyl-1,4-diazepane (600 μL, 2.5 equiv, 4.82 mmol), and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (1.0 mL, 3 equiv, 5.79 mmol) were dissolved in DMF (5 mL). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 h. The reaction was then quenched with saturated aq. NaHCO3 (10 mL) and extracted with ethyl acetate (3 × 10 mL). Protein Expression and Purification

Protein Expression Constructs

All constructs of PHF1 (Tudor: residues 25–87 of NP_077084, TP: residues 27–360 of NP_077084) and PHF19 (Tudor: residues 49–115 of NP_001273769, TP: residues 1–207 of NP_001273769) were expressed with N-terminal His-tags in pET28 expression vectors.

Protein Expression and Purification

All expression constructs were transformed into Rosetta2 BL21­(DE3)­pLysS competent cells (Novagen, EMD Chemicals, San Diego, CA). Protein expression was induced by growing the cells at 37 °C with shaking until the OD600 reached ∼0.6–0.8 at which time the temperature was lowered to 18 °C, and the expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM IPTG and continuing shaking overnight. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the pellets were stored at −80 °C.

His-tagged proteins were purified by resuspending thawed cell pellets in 30 mL of lysis buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate pH 7.2, 50 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, 1X EDTA free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN)) per liter of culture. Cells were lysed on ice by sonication with a Branson Digital 450 Sonifier (Branson Ultrasonics, Danbury, CT) at 40% amplitude for 12 cycles, with each cycle consisting of a 20 s pulse followed by a 40 s rest. The cell lysate was clarified by centrifugation and loaded onto a HisTrap FF column (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ) that had been pre-equilibrated with 10 column volumes of binding buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.2, 500 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole) using an AKTA FPLC (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ). The column was washed with 15 column volumes of binding buffer, and protein was eluted in a linear gradient to 100% elution buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate pH 7.2, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole) over 20 column volumes. Peak fractions containing the desired protein were pooled and concentrated to 2 mL in Amicon Ultra-15 concentrators with a 3000 molecular weight cutoff (Merck Millipore, Carrigtwohill Co. Cork IRL). Concentrated protein was loaded onto a HiLoad 26/60 Superdex 75 prep grade column (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ) that had been pre-equilibrated with 1.2 column volumes of sizing buffer (25 mM Tris pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, 5% glycerol) using an ATKA Purifier (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ). Protein was eluted isocratically in the sizing buffer over 1.3 column volumes at a flow rate of 2 mL/min, collecting 3 mL fractions. Peak fractions were analyzed for purity by SDS-PAGE, and those containing pure protein were pooled and concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 concentrators of 3000 molecular weight cutoff (Merck Millipore, Carrigtwohill Co. Cork IRL).

15N-Labeled Protein Expression and Purification

The PHF1 Tudor domain constructs comprising residues 14–87 and 28–87 were expressed and purified as described previously. Briefly, the proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) RIL cells grown in LB media or M9 minimal media supplemented with 15NH4Cl. Expression was induced by 0.2 mM IPTG with shaking at 16 °C for 20 h. Cells were harvested and lysed by sonication. The GST-tagged PHF1 Tudor proteins were purified on glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (GE Healthcare) in 20 mM Tris (pH 7.5) buffer supplemented with 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT, and 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride. The GST tag was removed overnight at 4 °C with PreScission protease. Proteins were further purified by size exclusion chromatography.

General TR-FRET Assay Protocol

The general TR-FRET protocol for Kme readers was followed as described previously. Assays were run using white, low-volume, flat-bottom, nonbinding 384-well microplates (Greiner, 784904) containing a total assay volume of 10 μL per well. The assay buffer was composed of 20 mM Tris (pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20, and 2 mM DTT). LANCE Europium (Eu)-W1024 streptavidin conjugate (2 nM) and LANCE Ultra ULight-anti6x-His antibody (10 nM) were used as donor and acceptor fluorophores associated with the tracer ligand and protein, respectively. Final assay concentrations of 60 nM 6× histidine tagged PHF1 Tudor protein (residues 28–87, N-terminal tag) and 90 nM biotin-UNC6641 tracer ligand were used for final compound testing. For PHF19, final assay concentrations of 5–20 nM 6× histidine tagged PHF19 Tudor protein (residues 49–115, N-terminal tag) and 40–50 nM biotin-UNC6641 tracer ligand were used. Assay performance was evaluated using the Z’ factor calculation at varying DMSO concentrations up to 3%. Low signals were obtained using 100 μM UNC6641 to obtain complete inhibition, and high signals were obtained without the compound (DMSO only). The Z’ factors for PHF1 and PHF19 were consistent at each DMSO concentration revealing a DMSO tolerance of up to 3% for both proteins (PHF1 Z’ at 1% = 0.81, at 3% = 0.75; PHF19 Z’ at 1% = 0.90, at 3% = 0.90).

Compounds were tested in a high-throughput manner using 384-well assay-ready plates in standard plate format: columns 1 and 2 were used for low-signal controls (100% inhibition with UNC6641), columns 23 and 24 were used for high-signal controls (DMSO only), and columns 3–22 were used for 10-point, 3-fold serial dilutions of each compound. First, controls were added to a mother plate where columns 1 and 2 were filled with 10 mM stock of UNC6641 in DMSO and columns 23 and 24 were filled with DMSO. Test compounds were dispensed across the mother plate at 100× concentration in columns 3–22 using a TECAN Freedom EVO liquid handling workstation. Using a TTP Labtech mosquito HTS liquid handling instrument, assay-ready plates were prepared by stamping 100 nL of control compound into columns 1 and 2, 10 nL of compounds from the mother plate into columns 3–22, and 25 nL of DMSO into columns 23 and 24. The protein, biotinylated tracer ligand, and TR-FRET reagents were added together and gently mixed by pipetting and rocking. Ten μL was then added to each well of an assay-ready plate using a Multidrop Combi instrument (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). Percent inhibition was calculated on a scale of 0% (i.e., activity with the DMSO vehicle only) to 100% (100 μM UNC6641) from the full column controls on each plate.

ITC Experiments

All ITC measurements were recorded at 25 °C with an Auto-iTC200 isothermal titration calorimeter (MicroCal Inc., USA). All protein and compound stock samples were stored in ITC buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol) and then diluted to achieve the desired concentrations. Typically, 100 μM protein and 1 mM compound were used; variations in these concentrations always maintained a 10:1 compound:protein ratio for all ITC experiments. The concentration of the protein stock solution was established using the Edelhoch method, whereas compound stock solutions were prepared based on mass. Experimental protocol included a single 0.2 mL of compound injection into a 200 mL cell filled with protein, followed by 26 subsequent 1.5 mL injections of the compound. Injections were performed with a spacing of 180 s and a reference power of 8 cal/s. The initial data point was routinely deleted. The titration data were analyzed using Origin 7 software (MicroCal Inc., USA) by the nonlinear least-squares method, fitting the heats of binding as a function of the compound-to-protein ratio to a one-site binding model. At these concentrations, a reasonable curve could be fit up to a K d ∼ 100 μM. Subsequently, curves too weak to be fit were reported as >100 μM.

HSQC NMR Spectroscopy

NMR experiments were carried out at 298 K on a Varian INOVA 600 spectrometer as described. The NMR sample contained 0.2 mM uniformly 15N-labeled PHF1 Tudor domain (residue 14–87) in 20 mM Tris (pH 6.8) buffer supplemented with 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT, and 10% D2O. Binding was characterized by monitoring chemical shift changes in the proteins induced by UNC7242. Spectra were processed using NMRPipe.

Molecular Docking

Docking was performed in Maestro v12.6.149 (Schrödinger Suite 2020–4, Schrödinger, LLC). The PHF1 Tudor receptor was prepared from PDB entry 4HCZ, chain A. The cocrystallized H3K36me3 peptide and crystallographic waters were removed, while the bulk solvent was retained. Bond orders were assigned, and hydrogens were added. Alternate locations were set to the highest occupancy. Protonation states were assigned with Epik at pH 7.4 ± 1.0. The Protein Preparation Wizard and Prime were used to optimize the hydrogen-bond network and to apply a restrained minimization with the OPLS3e force field. UNC7242 was imported as an SDF, desalted, and prepared with LigPrep. Ionization and tautomeric states were enumerated by Epik at pH 7.4 ± 1.0. Stereochemistry specified in the input was retained, and undefined centers were sampled with a cap of 32 total states per ligand.

The Glide receptor grid was centered on the aromatic cage by using the centroid of W41, Y47, and F65. To reflect the NMR CSP footprint during sampling, two grid-level positional constraints were defined. An aromatic cage sphere with a 2.2 Å radius was restricted to satisfaction by aromatic ligand atoms using the SMARTS pattern [a]. A hydrophobic-patch sphere adjacent to L45, L46, and L48 with a 2.2 Å radius was restricted to satisfaction by sp3 carbons using the SMARTS patterns [#6 × 4] and [#6 × 4;r3]. Glide SP docking was performed with flexible ligand sampling. Ring conformations and nitrogen inversion were enabled. Epik state penalties were included in the score. van der Waals scaling was set to 0.80 with a qcut of 0.15. Postdocking minimization was applied. Top poses were redocked in Glide XP with the halogen-bond donor term enabled. Poses were retained if both positional constraints were satisfied and at least one ligand heavy atom lay within 5.0 Å of five or more CSP-highlighted residues A39, W41, Y47, L48, G49, and F65. The top-ranked pose consistent with SARs was used for figure generation and subsequent analyses.

Nucleosome Pulldowns

Twenty μg biotinylated H3K36me3 nucleosomes (EpiCypher, 16–0320) were immobilized on 30 μL Dynabeads MyOne streptavidin T1 magnetic beads (Invitrogen) precleared with binding buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 0.1% NP-40 (IGEPAL), 1 mM DTT). One μM PHF1 (1 μM) or PHF19 was then bound to the immobilized nucleosome for 1 h rotating at 4 °C. Concurrently, 100–200 μM UNC7242 was added. After 1 h, the samples were washed 3× with the binding buffer and eluted with 1× Laemmli sample buffer (Sigma) at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were run on a precast Any kD Mini-PROTEAN TGX protein gel (Bio-Rad).

Chromatin Fractionation

Jurkat N6 cells were lysed in modified CSK buffer (10 mM pipes, NaOH pH 7.0, 340 mM sucrose, 100 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 1× complete protease inhibitor (Roche), 1× PhosStop phosphatase inhibitor (Roche), 10% Triton X-100) for 10 min on ice. The lysate was pelleted, and the recovered supernatants were discarded. The remaining pellet was washed with CSK buffer (-Triton) and then resuspended in CSK buffer (+Triton). The lysates were treated with 100 μM of compound and rotated for 2 h at 4 °C. The lysate was pelleted, and the recovered supernatants were saved as kicked-off fraction. Chromatin fraction lysate was sonicated with SONIFIER cell disruptor 350 (Branson) for 5× 1s pulses at 20% duty cycle and output control of 4. Proteins were separated by 10% bis-tris acrylamide gel and transferred onto immuno-blot PVDF membranes via semidry transfer using a Hoefer Semi-Phor transfer unit. Primary antibodies were diluted to appropriate concentration in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) with 0.5% Tween 20 (TBST) with 5% milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were washed 3× in TBST and then incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (GE Healthcare Biosciences) at 1:10,000 (Rabbit) or 1:5000 (mouse) dilution in TBST/milk for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were washed 3× with TBST and then developed using Amersham ECL Prime (GE Life Sciences). Blots were imaged using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP imager and analyzed using Image Lab software.
