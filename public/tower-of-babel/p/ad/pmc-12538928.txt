Professional education and clinical application of balance control and reactive balance training: a cross-sectional national survey of Polish healthcare professionals
Authors: Anna Ogonowska-Slodownik, Júlia O Faria, Joanna Tokarska, Avril Mansfield
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a gap in educational resources and knowledge translation for reactive balance training (RBT). This study aimed to examine current educational practices and training experiences related to balance control and RBT among Polish healthcare professionals.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was distributed among members of a Polish regulated healthcare profession. Participants were eligible if they were licensed healthcare professionals involved in treating clients with balance or mobility impairments. The questionnaire included 55 items across six sections, covering demographics, clinical practice, treatment approaches, and knowledge, attitudes, barriers, facilitators, and clinical use of RBT. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and chi-square tests for group comparisons.ResultsOf the 286 valid respondents, only 26.5% reported using RBT in clinical practice, while 54.2% either had not heard of it or did not use it. Non-users commonly reported first learning about RBT through the survey itself (44.0%). Conceptual confusion with other approaches such as Bobath concept and Proprioceptive Neuromuscular Facilitation was evident. Users demonstrated higher confidence, greater workplace support, and fewer safety concerns. All groups expressed strong interest in further training, particularly through hands-on workshops and instructional videos.ConclusionRBT remains underused in Poland, partly due to limited formal education and conceptual misunderstandings. Addressing these gaps through structured, hands-on training and institutional support may enhance implementation. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational strategies and broader knowledge translation efforts in underrepresented regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08030-9.Keywords: Rehabilitation, Knowledge, Professional practice, Postural balance, Health-care surveys

Introduction

Falls can lead to physical disabilities and a loss of independence. Even falls that do not result in injuries are associated with negative consequences, such as anxiety and fear-driven home confinement [1]. Moreover, the economic burden associated with falls is increasing worldwide [2].

A fall occurs after a loss of balance, or balance perturbation, which is the inability to maintain control of the relationship between the base of support and the centre of mass. Effective rapid balance reactions are crucial for preventing falls following a balance perturbation [3, 4]. Therefore, specific training focused on reactive balance control is warranted for those at increased fall risk, such as people with stroke [5, 6] and Parkinson’s disease [7]. Currently, reactive balance training (RBT) is the only intervention specifically designed to improve reactive balance control is; this is a balance training approach that uses repeated, balance perturbations to trigger rapid reactions for regaining postural stability in a safe and controlled environment [8]. RBT facilitates sensorimotor adaptations in stability recovery responses through trial-and-error practice. This differs from conventional balance exercises, which primarily target anticipatory balance through upright and proactive control [9]. RBT involves repeatedly exposing participants to unexpected balance perturbations delivered by instrumented treadmills, movable platforms, or sudden pulls using safety harnesses, requiring compensatory strategies such as protective stepping or rapid trunk adjustments [8]. Given that age-related impairments in postural control are key determinants of fall risk, incorporating such reactive balance recovery tasks into fall prevention interventions is essential [10]. Both theoretical and empirical evidence support that this trial-and-error practice promotes central nervous system adaptations and improves reactive postural control [11].

Systematic reviews on RBT [8, 12, 13] have shown that RBT reduces fall rates and improves reactive balance control. RBT has been shown to be nearly twice as effective as traditional balance training and functional training for preventing falls, regardless of whether they result in an unintentional loss of balance [8, 14, 15]. However, despite its proven efficacy, its application in clinical practice remains limited. Surveys conducted in Canada and Israel indicate that while more than 75% of physiotherapists report using the method, many struggle to fully understand it, often mistaking it for volitional step training or rehabilitation focused on specific joints rather than whole-body balance [16, 17].

In Poland, current clinical practice for treating patients with balance disorders largely relies on conventional strategies—most notably the Bobath concept and proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation. In addition to traditional fall-prevention physiotherapy such as lower-limb strengthening and progressive balance exercises (e.g., reducing base of support or visual input) [18, 19] in line with the European guideline [20, 21].

Although research and clinical guidelines are increasingly robust, the majority of RBT-related resources, literature, and training materials are published in English. This highlights a critical gap in educational resources and knowledge translation for RBT. This language barrier may limit access for healthcare professionals in non-English-speaking regions. In Poland, for instance, there is limited context-specific guidance on RBT, and little is known about how Polish healthcare professionals approach balance training or incorporate RBT concepts into their practice and continuing education. The aim of our study was to examine current educational practices and training experiences related to balance control and reactive balance training among Polish healthcare professionals.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional survey based on an self-developed, study-specific existing questionnaire used in two previous studies [16, 17]. The study was conducted and reported following the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines, specifically the STROBE checklist for cross-sectional studies, to ensure transparent and complete reporting [22]. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jozef Pilsudski University of Physical Education in Warsaw (protocol code 01–18/2024, date of approval 09 April 2024).

Questionnaire

Survey data were collected using an online platform (Forms, MS Teams). The survey collected demographic and professional characteristics of participants, including age, sex, professional discipline, years of clinical experience, primary care setting, and geographic location within Poland. This information allowed to describe the sample and explore potential variations in professional education and clinical application of reactive balance training across different subgroups of healthcare professionals. The questionnaire included 55 items combined into four parts: (1) demographic information; (2) approaches for treating balance/mobility; (3) knowledge of RBT, including facilitators and barriers to using RBT; and (4) frequency of use of RBT. The questionnaire primarily featured closed-ended questions with barriers and facilitators. Closed-ended items used Likert-type scales to capture participants’ perceptions, frequency of clinical application, and confidence in performing RBT on a 5-point scale (e.g., 1 = strongly disagree/never, 5 = strongly agree/always). Open-ended questions were also included to allow participants to report additional barriers, facilitators, or other relevant information. Permission to use and adapt the questionnaire was not required, as the original author of the questionnaire is a co-author of this study. As a self-developed, descriptive questionnaire, formal reliability testing was not conducted. However, content and face validity were ensured through expert review and pilot testing with a small group of healthcare professionals [17]. The questionnaire was translated into Polish and piloted with five individuals to assess clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness. It took approximately 10–20 min to complete, and remained open for three months (May-August 2024). The questionnaire was completed anonymously, and participants were instructed not to include any identifying information and complete the survey only once.

Participants

A non-probability convenience sampling approach was used, with purposive targeting of Polish healthcare professionals. A formal sample size calculation was not conducted, as this study was a descriptive cross-sectional survey. Following recommendations for cross-sectional studies [23], we aimed to invite as many eligible participants as possible to obtain a representative overview of professional education and clinical application of reactive balance training among Polish healthcare professionals. Eligibility for participation was determined through screening questions at the start of the survey. Participants were required to confirm that they were members of a Polish regulated healthcare profession and provided treatment for balance and mobility issues in any care setting. Only respondents who met these criteria were able to proceed with the questionnaire. No additional exclusion criteria were applied. Participants were invited through electronic mailings and/or social media posts from Polish Physiotherapy Association, Polish Society of Physiotherapy and Facebook group Fizjopozytywni. At the discretion of each association or group, either a single e-mail/post or multiple reminders were sent.

Before accessing the questionnaire, participants were presented with a consent statement outlining the study details, investigators’ contact information, a privacy statement, and a note on voluntary participation. To proceed, participants had to confirm their consent by clicking an agreement button.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were exploratory and intended to provide an initial picture of potential differences between groups. Given the novelty of this topic in the Polish context, the analyses were conducted to generate hypotheses and highlight trends that may inform future confirmatory research, rather than to test predefined hypotheses. Demographic and professional characteristics of participants were summarized using appropriate descriptive statistic and variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Descriptive statistics were calculated for each questionnaire item. We calculated the 95% confidence interval around the prevalence of RBT use. The sample was separated into three groups: (1) Non-users group (those who responded either “I had not heard of RBT until now” or “I had previously heard about RBT but I currently use other strategies to treat my clients’ balance impairments”); (2) Open-to-use group (those who responded “I know about RBT and am open to using it in my practice but I experience barriers to implementing RBT into practice”); and (3) Users group (those who responded either “I have conducted RBT with more than one client to treat balance impairment” or “I use RBT regularly in my practice”). We compared the frequency of ‘agree’, ‘not sure’, and ‘disagree’ responses to questions relating to barriers and facilitators between the three groups using the nonparametric chi-square test, with Fisher’s exact test applied for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Responses to open-ended questions were analyzed using directed content analysis [24]. Quantitative and related qualitative findings were presented together. An alpha of 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

Participants

A total of 432 participants provided consent; however, 130 were ineligible as they were neither healthcare professionals nor involved in treating clients with balance or mobility issues. Additionally, 11 participants were excluded for failing to complete the question regarding RBT use in practice, and 5 were removed due to contradictory responses to the RBT-related questions. Consequently, 286 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Participants flow in the study

Participant characteristics are detailed in Table 1. A total of 76 out of 286 participants (26.5%) reported using RBT. More than half of the respondents reported that they either have not heard about it or they hear but are using other strategies to treat clients (54.2%). The majority were physiotherapists (98.6%) and female (77.3%). Most respondents were between 31 and 40 years of age (45.8%) and held a master’s degree (84.3%). Participants had diverse professional experience, with nearly one-third (29.7%) reporting ≤ 5 years of practice. They primarily worked in outpatient rehabilitation (46.9%) or private practice (26.2%), and most treated clients with balance problems for up to two workdays per week. Areas of practice were predominantly neurological (32.5%) and orthopedic (24.1%), with half of respondents working mainly with adults (50.3%).

A large majority of RBT Users disagreed with the statement that RBT is ineffective (94%) and reported that their clients noticed improvements in balance (78%). When analyzing professionals’ feelings toward RBT, a significant difference was observed among the three groups (p < 0.001), with the majority of respondents expressing positive feelings, such as optimism and confidence. There was a significant difference between user and non-user groups in their confidence to apply RBT with clients (p < 0.001), and fewer Users preferred alternative approaches compared to Non-users (11.6% vs. 23.9%, p = 0.01). All groups expressed interest in using RBT more (p = 0.04).

Facilitators and barriers to implementing RBT

Responses to set questions about facilitators to RBT are presented in Fig. 2. Users were significantly more likely than both Non-users and those Open-to-use to report that they can easily complete RBT at their place of practice (93%, 51%, 46% respectively). They were also more likely to have colleagues with experience using RBT (57%, 21%, 25% respectively). Managerial support was reported significantly more often by Users than by Non-users (28% vs. 13%). Access to necessary resources was reported significantly more often by Users than by Non-users or Open-to-use (51% vs. 22%). Users were also significantly more likely than Non-users to feel they have sufficient training in RBT (25% vs. 8%). Knowledge about the required equipment was significantly higher among Users compared to Non-users (38% vs. 19%).

Facilitators to implement RBT in practice

Responses to set questions about barriers to RBT are presented in Fig. 3. Users were significantly more likely to disagree that clients’ length of stay is too short to include RBT in treatment plans compared to Non-users (77% vs. 43%). Users did not perceive delivering effective manual perturbations as more fatiguing compared to Non-users (57% vs. 37%). Uncertainty about when to use RBT was significantly higher among Non-users and those Open-to-use compared to both Users (52%, 51%, 25% respectively). Client apprehension about RBT was reported significantly more often by Users and Open-to-use than Non-users (36%, 43%, 15% respectively). Users were significantly more likely than Non-users and those Open-to-use to disagree about RBT as unsafe for their clients (94%, 50%, 65% respectively). Users were significantly more likely than Non-users and those Open-to-use to disagree that RBT takes too much time to set up (78%, 28, 46% respectively).

Barriers to implement RBT in practice

In the open-ended questions 24 respondents reported facilitators and 50 reported barriers to using RBT. Facilitators in open ended questions were only reported by Users and Open-to-use groups. Users reported formal education and training, such as knowledge gained from university studies and Proprioceptive Neuromuscular Facilitation (PNF) courses, as facilitators. However, several respondents indicated that they first learned about RBT in continuing education courses, most commonly through approaches such as the Bobath concept or PNF, although these techniques do not typically include reactive balance training.

They mentioned the role of specialized equipment and the availability of an additional therapist for support. Ensuring patient safety could be achieved through modifications, such as adjusting equipment or using external supports like chairs and treatment tables. Various tools, including PNF techniques, stochastic platforms, and balance trainers, were mentioned to enhance training. Suspension systems, such as harnesses, were also noted as effective for reducing patient fear and increasing safety, with the help of an assisting therapist. Early-career education, workplace resources, and colleague support were seen as facilitators. Specific tools like sensory cushions, mats, balls, and balance equipment (e.g., BOSU, balance boards) were mentioned. A safe training environment and access to appropriate equipment were mentioned most often as facilitators.

As for barriers among non-users, the most frequently cited barrier was a lack of knowledge about RBT, followed by concerns related to workplace acceptance, limited access to necessary equipment, financial constraints, and cognitive impairments among patients. Some also mentioned challenges related to home-based physiotherapy and a lack of a second therapist to assist in conducting RBT. Users reported barriers, including patient-related factors such as advanced age, multiple comorbidities, cognitive impairments, and patient fear. Equipment limitations and lack of knowledge were also noted, as well as the challenge of implementing RBT in telerehabilitation settings. The Open-to-use group reported leadership and organizational factors as barriers, including lack of managerial support, insufficient safety measures, and limited financial resources. Patient fear and cognitive impairments were also mentioned, alongside a lack of training opportunities and uncertainty about implementing RBT effectively.

Most participants expressed interest in learning more about RBT, particularly regarding its theoretical background, with response rates exceeding 94% across all groups (p = 0.21). Similarly, interest in the principles of training for RBT was consistently high, with over 97% of each group responding affirmatively (p = 0.56). A significant difference was observed between groups in their interest in identifying clients’ reactive balance control impairments (p = 0.04), primarily driven by greater interest among the Open-to-use group. Regarding preferred learning methods, the most frequently selected formats were hands-on workshops and watching instructional videos. Other commonly preferred methods included reviewing case studies, having access to an expert for questions, and reading scientific literature, with no significant differences between groups.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the prevalence of RBT use in clinical practice among healthcare professionals who treat clients with balance, fall, and mobility impairments in Poland. Because this is the first survey examining healthcare professionals’ perspectives on RBT in Poland, our analyses were exploratory.

In the respondent group, the majority were physiotherapists (98.6%) and 54.2% of reported either being unfamiliar with or not using this approach. This finding contrasts other studies, in which ~ 75% of healthcare professionals in Canada [17] and and Israel [16] reported using RBT. People being unfamiliar with or not using RBT in Poland likely reflects several systemic and professional barriers. RBT is a relatively recent and specialized approach [25], and it may not yet be widely incorporated into standard physiotherapy education or clinical guidelines in Poland. In addition, limited access to appropriate equipment, training opportunities, and institutional support may hinder its adoption. Clinicians may also perceive RBT as requiring high supervision, patient compliance, or resources that are not readily available in many practice settings [17].

Both studies which used the same survey noted possible inconsistencies in participants’ responses. In the study by Margalit et al. [16], respondents appeared to refer to incidental balance losses during clinical activities as reactive balance training, rather than to targeted, structured training. Similarly, Mansfield et al. [17] identified discrepancies in the Canadian cohort: in the open-ended responses, some professionals suggested that they did not explicitly challenge reactive balance, but instead applied isolated joint perturbations—a practice that may be misleading, as the term “perturbation training” has also been used to describe such interventions [26]. Moreover, some professionals reported having learned RBT through programs focused on volitional step training, which is improbable given the distinct differences between the two types of tasks [27].

Our results also revealed potential misconceptions among clinicians in Poland. Many participants reported first learning about RBT through continuing education courses such as Bobath or PNF—approaches that do not typically involve reactive balance training [28, 29]. Another notable finding was that 44% of Non-users reported hearing about RBT for the first time through the survey itself, reinforcing the interpretation that unfamiliarity with the concept remains widespread. The low prevalence of formal education in RBT among healthcare professionals in Poland may partly explain the underutilization of this approach. Only 4.5% of Non-users reported having received RBT training during their entry-level education, compared to 14.5% of Open-to-use participants and 11.8% of Users. These results reflect similar trends observed in Israel and Canada, where implementation often depends on post-graduate exposure or continuing education rather than structured training within academic programs.

When examining the most frequently reported facilitators—particularly those related to the practice environment—we found that Non-users and Open-to-use participants reported not having access to resources for implementing RBT and/or being unsure about what equipment is necessary to administer it. Implementing RBT does not require high-cost technology, large movable platforms, or specialized treadmills [30]. Several studies have demonstrated improvements in reactive balance control using internal perturbations or manual perturbations, which require only a safety harness and standard training equipment [5, 31, 32]. Moreover, low-cost technologies can also be used, such as modified commercial treadmills commonly found in gyms. By adapting these treadmills with speed controllers, it is possible to generate perturbations effectively [33].

Among the reported facilitators, the Non-user group—especially when compared to Users—was more likely to report insufficient training in RBT. This finding is consistent with their responses regarding attitudes toward RBT: confidence in applying the method was significantly higher among Users compared to the other two groups. Regarding barriers, both Non-users and Open-to-use participants expressed greater concern about the safety of RBT and uncertainty about when to incorporate it into clinical sessions. It is well established that repeated practice and mastery experiences are essential components in the development of confidence and self-efficacy [25]. Respondents also reported concerns about the need for an assistant during training and the limited time available to implement RBT within short treatment windows. However, previous studies have shown that only a few sessions may be sufficient to improve reactive postural control, facilitating implementation even for patients who attend rehabilitation for a limited period [34]. Furthermore, when a safety harness is available, the presence of more than one professional is not necessarily required [35, 36]. These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in Canada and Israel, where perceived competence and contextual support were identified as key determinants of RBT adoption [16, 17].

Interestingly, some barriers were shared across all groups, such as fatigue associated with manual delivery and uncertainty regarding clinical indications. These findings suggest that, beyond knowledge, practical and logistical issues must also be addressed to facilitate broader implementation. Interventions that provide institutional support and hands-on training may therefore have a dual benefit: enhancing clinicians’ confidence while simultaneously mitigating workplace-related barriers. Despite these challenges, interest in further RBT training remained consistently high. All groups reported a strong desire to use RBT more (over 60%) and nearly 90% among current Users. Additionally, more than 94% of respondents in all groups expressed a desire to learn more about the theoretical foundations and clinical application of RBT, a finding consistent with the results reported in both the Canadian and Israeli studies [16, 17]. Practical learning formats—such as hands-on workshops and instructional videos—were among the most preferred, reinforcing the need for accessible and applied continuing education strategies.

Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of improving access to RBT education and clarifying its clinical definition. Promoting structured training during professional education and addressing misconceptions—particularly the confusion of RBT with other neuromotor training approaches such as volitional step training, PNF, or Bobath—may be critical steps toward expanding its use. Clarifying the definition and scope of RBT could help reduce conceptual misunderstandings and promote its appropriate integration into clinical practice. Given that Users reported fewer barriers and greater confidence, supporting professionals in transitioning from conceptual understanding to practical application may have a direct impact on implementation.

These results have important clinical implications. The strong interest in additional training—especially through interactive formats—suggests that clinicians are open to adopting RBT if equipped with adequate tools and institutional support. Future research should examine how structured educational interventions influence clinicians’ confidence and adoption rates, as well as whether institutional leadership plays a mediating role in overcoming workplace-related challenges. In addition, qualitative investigations may offer deeper insight into the cultural and systemic factors that influence RBT adoption in underrepresented regions such as Central and Eastern Europe.

This study has several key strengths. It is the first survey examining healthcare professionals’ perspectives on RBT in Poland, providing novel insights into current practice, knowledge, and barriers to implementation. The study identified both practical and conceptual barriers, highlighting areas for targeted education and institutional support. The study’s exploratory approach provide a foundation for future research, including interventions to enhance RBT adoption and training.

This study has several limitations. The reliance on self-reported data introduces potential biases, such as recall bias and the tendency to present socially desirable responses. No technical measures were in place to prevent multiple submissions. Additionally, the sampling method—recruiting through professional networks and social media—may have excluded some healthcare professionals, reducing the generalizability of the findings. Lastly, as the survey focused exclusively on Polish healthcare professionals, the findings may not be directly applicable to other countries or healthcare systems.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the study revealed that although RBT is an evidence-based approach for improving balance and reducing falls, it remains underused in clinical practice in Poland, with only 26.5% of respondents reporting its implementation. This underutilization appears to stem from limited formal education on RBT, conceptual confusion with other methods and concerns about safety and resource availability. These findings highlight the need for structured educational programs, clear definitions, and workplace support to enhance the adoption of RBT in clinical practice, ultimately helping to close the gap between evidence and practice and support the use of RBT.
