Current status and prospects in diagnosis of major opportunistic fungal infections among HIV-positive population in south provinces of China and neighboring countries that are suffering high prevalence of AIDS
Authors: Guanmei Lu, Jing Dan, Xuelian Yang, Lei Tan, Junjun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights:•Limitations of conventional fungal diagnostics (culture/microscopy) are summarized, while novel molecular tools enable rapid pathogen identification.•Emerging platforms including ddPCR, SERS and nanopore sequencing are assessed for use in resource-limited endemic regions.•AI and machine learning improve early fungal diagnosis and facilitate individualized patient treatment strategies.•Regional cooperation and capacity construction help upgrade diagnostic infrastructure against cross-border fungal threats.

Introduction

As of 2022, the global population living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) stands at approximately 39 million (Joint United Nations Program on HIV/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), 2023). Given the consequent impairment of the host immune system, patients are rendered highly susceptible to co-morbidities, particularly infectious diseases. Mortality in this group is predominantly driven by challenges in accurate diagnosis and the inadequate management of these secondary conditions. Among people living with HIV (PLWH), fungal infection was strongly correlated with a high mortality rate and was a significant component of secondary infection. Parallel to the expanding HIV population, these opportunistic pathogens in persistence have been established as primary causes of AIDS-related mortality.

Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region (Guangxi) and its neighboring regions in China (Guangdong Province, Yunnan Province, Sichuan Province, Chongqing Municipality, and Guizhou Province), along with adjacent Southeast Asian countries (Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, Indonesia, Malaysia, and the Philippines), face severe challenges from opportunistic fungal infections in PLWH. A recent retrospective cohort study by H. Liang et al. [1] estimated the incidence of fungal infection in Kachin State, Myanmar, to be 7.1 per 100 person-years, declining from 19.1 (2008–2011) to 5.2 (2017–2020), but still far higher than the global average of 2.53 per 100 person-years [2]. Cambodia experienced a severe peak in 2002, with approximately 170,000 infections and 80,000 to 90,000 annual deaths [3]. Initiatives such as the China-ASEAN Free Trade Area and the “Belt and Road Initiative” strategy have significantly facilitated cross-border trade and mobility. While fostering economic integration, this connectivity has inadvertently intensified the transmission risk of infectious diseases. Economic drivers compel substantial migration between southern China and Southeast Asia; this mobility not only increases exposure to endemic environmental fungi but also disrupts the continuity of healthcare, leading to delayed diagnoses and the cross-regional spread of lethal AIDS-associated opportunistic infections. Among the most prevalent comorbidities are Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP), candidiasis, and talaromycosis. Etiologically, these conditions are caused by distinct fungal species: Pneumocystis jirovecii (P. jirovecii), Candida species (with Candida albicans being the most frequent), and Talaromyces marneffei (T. marneffei), respectively. It is noteworthy that P. jirovecii, Cryptococcus neoformans (C. neoformans), C. albicans, and T. marneffei were all included in the World Health Organization (WHO) Fungal Priority Pathogens List released in October 2022. Epidemiologically, infections caused by P. jirovecii and C. neoformans are prevalent in China, whereas Histoplasma capsulatum (H. capsulatum) is the predominant pathogen in Africa and South America. Candidozyma auris, although included in the WHO, is currently not endemic in our region of focus and is therefore not discussed in detail in this review. Accordingly, we'd like to primarily discuss T. marneffei, C. albicans, P. jirovecii, and C. neoformans, as these are the focus of this review, due to their importance in epidemiology and geographical distribution.

Mitigating HIV-related mortality necessitates robust clinical diagnostics to support timely and targeted therapeutic interventions. Regrettably, current diagnostic methods for HIV-related opportunistic fungal infections remain suboptimal and often fail to meet clinical requirements. Consequently, advancing diagnostic tools and strategies is imperative for detecting diseases at an early stage with enhanced accuracy and efficiency, thereby optimizing clinical outcomes (Fig. 1 and Table 1). Situation and future of diagnosis for T. marneffei co-infection

T. marneffei, originally isolated in 1956 (formerly Penicillium marneffei), is predominantly endemic in Southeast Asia and southern China [2]. In Asian cohorts of AIDS patients, the pooled prevalence is 3.6%, with notably elevated rates in Vietnam (6.4%), Thailand (3.9%), and Malaysia (2.9%). In Guangxi, the coinfection rate in HIV patients has reached an alarming 16.1%. Correspondingly, this coinfection accounts for 25.0% of AIDS-related mortality—nearly doubling the rate observed in HIV mono-infection (13.8%) [1]. A comparable disease burden is evident in neighboring Guangdong province, which reports an incidence of 18.8% and a mortality rate ranging from 14.0% to 25.1% [3]. Nationally, cases are clustered in Guangxi (43.0%) and Guangdong (41.0%), with sporadic occurrences in Hunan, Yunnan, and Sichuan. Historically confined to southern China, Southeast Asia, and northeastern India, the geographical footprint of T. marneffei has now expanded to 34 countries. Predictive modeling suggests potential spread to northern China, eastern India, the Korean Peninsula, Japan, eastern Australia, the central United States, and West Africa [4]. Given these trends, T. marneffei has been listed as one of the most common AIDS-related opportunistic infections in southern China, amid growing HIV risks.

Transmission of this fungus occurs via the inhalation of airborne conidia by susceptible individuals. Upon infection, T. marneffei targets macrophages in vivo, thereby compromising the host's innate immunity. Distinct clinical presentations in PLWH versus HIV-seronegative individuals underscore the need for heightened physician awareness to ensure timely diagnosis and optimized treatment outcomes. In the absence of timely antifungal therapy, mortality rates among T. marneffei-coinfected individuals can exceed 80.0% [5]. Prompt therapeutic intervention can significantly reduce this mortality to approximately 24.0%; however, patient survival is contingent upon rapid and accurate diagnosis. Regrettably, current diagnostic methods often fail to meet clinical demands, resulting in a persistent mortality rate of approximately 50.0% [6]. Detections of etiology: Microscopic examination and fungal culture

Currently, etiological detection is the gold standard for diagnosing Talaromyces marneffei, as it confirms the presence of the disease and identifies the specific causative agent. In clinical practice, etiology is routinely assessed through microscopic examination and fungal culture, utilizing blood, lymph nodes, and bone marrow as common diagnostic specimens.

With smear slides under the microscope, histopathologic examination will reveal yeast cells with cross-walls, both intra- and extracellular, in infected specimens. Although blood is the most commonly used medium in clinical tests for pathogenicity, the dissemination of T. marneffei in the bloodstream is only observed in the late stages of the infection, resulting in an underdiagnosis rate as high as 50.0% [7]. Since then, the microscopic strategy has been typically applied with other techniques, such as the absolute “gold standard” of fungal culture.

As a biphasic fungus, T. marneffei produces diffuse red colonies on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar at 25 °C–30 °C, and develops unpigmented yeast-phase hyphae at 37 °C [8]. With bone marrow and lymph node biopsies, the sensitivity achieved in culture was close to 100.0% in skin (90.0%), blood (76.0%), and sputum (34.0%). Cultures can also be performed on feces, cerebrospinal fluid, and joint fluid, although urine is generally avoided due to contamination risks [9]. Unfortunately, the favorable period required in culture reached as long as 3–14 days, leading to remarkable delays in treatments and therefore a significant increase in T. marneffei-related mortality [10], particularly among patients without rash. Thus, combining multiple samples and alternative methods is frequently necessary and conducted to improve detection efficacy [11].

Molecular biology: The polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based detections employed for T. marneffei

Due to their high sensitivity and specificity, PCR-based methods have been developed to diagnose T. marneffei co-infections. To establish detection targets, proteomic studies have identified differentially expressed T. marneffei proteins across their life cycle [12], [13] . However, Mp1p is the only maker approved for clinically detecting Talaromyces marneffei (TM) [14]. Basic local alignment search tool (BLAST) analyses confirm that the Mp1p gene is unique to T. marneffei and absent in hosts or common AIDS-related pathogens. From this advantage, PCR protocols to detect the deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) fragments of Mp1p genes were developed, and a high specificity of approximately 99.0% was achieved [15], primarily through nested PCR.

Both nested and real-time PCR demonstrate high analytical sensitivity, with real-time PCR outperforming both blood culture and nested PCR [16]. Still, the sensitivity remains only 50.0%–70.0%, primarily due to difficulties in releasing DNA [11]. The release of TM cells into blood or other accessible specimens occurs stage-dependently and can be influenced by antifungal treatment and other factors. In the next step, the integrity of the TM cell wall commonly hinders DNA extraction, even when using harsh lysis agents.

Immunological diagnosis: Promises in current application and future development

The G-test and GM-test, which detect 1,3-β-D-glucan (BDG) and serum galactomannan (GM), are essential biomarkers for confirming fungal presence and assessing disease severity. Either test has shown a certain level of diagnostic value for invasive fungal infections and is welcomed in diagnosis due to its simplicity and convenience of operation. However, neither test is suitable for independent diagnosis of T. marneffei. The G-test targets BDG, a pan-fungal component, while the GM-test detects GM, a cell wall antigen mainly found in Aspergillus and Penicillium species. Consequently, both are prone to cross-reactivity with fungi such as Aspergillus, Candida, and Cryptococcus, resulting in limited specificity. Besides, the sensitivity is also limited in both methods, as 36.4%–63.2% for G-test with a threshold close to 100 pg/mL [17], whereas 55.6%–81.4% with a 0.5 μg/L threshold for GM-test [18]. Additionally, the G-test relies on a coagulation cascade triggered by the interaction between BDG and factor G, a substance derived from the blood of the Chinese horseshoe crab (Tachypleus tridentatus). As this species is now listed as a Class II protected animal in China, the widespread use of the G-test is significantly restricted.

Recently, the monoclonal antibody mAb-4D1 (targeting TM CYA) was developed to detect a yeast-phase-specific cytoplasmic antigen in T. marneffei, identified as a glycosylated cell wall protein that also binds with Galanthus nivalis agglutinin (GNA), a mannose-binding lectin. Distinct from the BDG and GM, this component can be found only in the TM of the yeast stage or conidia-yeast transition. Correspondingly, no detectable immunoreactivity of mAb-4D1 was observed against the antigens in common fungi, such as H. capsulatum, C. albicans, C. neoformans, and Aspergillus fumigatus (A. fumigatus), from which a promising specificity was ensured. In small immunochromatography studies, this antibody demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity of 87.9% and 98.1%, respectively [19]. A similar diagnostic outcome was also observed when used in a double-antibody sandwich ELISA, with 89.2% sensitivity and 98.7% specificity [20].

Additionally, an enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using Mp1p was later developed for detecting antigens and antibodies, and it has been shown to elicit a protective immune response in mice [21]. Mp1p is a cell wall mannoprotein in T. marneffei. First cloned by L. Cao et al. [22]. In 1998, it was highly abundant and immunogenic. The detection of anti-MP antibodies in infected hosts supports the diagnostic potential of Mp1p for talaromycosis. This antigen also contributes to virulence and the regulation of inflammation. Like its DNA counterpart, the uniqueness of Mp1p enables high specificity in immunoassays based on antigen-antibody interaction. In clinical studies and applications, ELISA targeting the Mp1p antigen in plasma has achieved sensitivity ranging from 75.0% to 86.0%, while specificity is above 98.0%. The simultaneous application of plasma and urine was shown to help improve the sensitivity of diagnosis targeting the Mp1p antigen [23]. Compared to the 3–14 days typically required for fungal culture, ELISA-based detection of Mp1p antigen can be completed within just 6 hours. Although the detection of anti-Mp1p host IgG in serum showed limited sensitivity (30.0%), its high specificity (98.5%) still supports its auxiliary diagnostic value [24]. Our team member D. Jing has successfully predicted target molecules that function similarly to Mp1p [25].

Additionally, CD4+ T-cell count is a useful co-diagnostic marker for HIV-TM co-infection, with counts below 50 cells/μL indicating elevated risk of talaromycosis. The significantly shorter turnaround time highlights a significant advantage of immunological diagnostics for T. marneffei [26]. To achieve a comprehensive judgment, the patient's clinical manifestations and epidemiological history should be considered in the diagnostic process. Not only that, but the development of more effective tools is also noteworthy, for instance, the discovery of new targets, the creation of new probes, innovation in detection methodologies, strategies, and equipment.

New technologies in developing: Hopes and beyond hopes

With the rapid advancement in biotechnologies and medical sciences, a panel of new techniques is currently being developed, bringing new hope to diagnosing T. marneffei infection from different angles.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) enables comprehensive pathogen detection by analyzing DNA/ribonucleic acid (RNA) sequences from clinical specimens on a global scale. This technology is valued for its high throughput, rapid turnaround, and unbiased accuracy, demonstrating a sensitivity of 98.3% and a specificity of 98.6% [27]. It is also effective in identifying co-infections. Although mNGS shows strong diagnostic capability for AIDS complicated with T. marneffei infection, its widespread adoption is limited by the reliance on specialized sequencing equipment, complex data analysis, high costs, and operational complexity. Consequently, mNGS services are commonly provided by specialized institutions rather than routine hospitals [28]. Furthermore, as talaromycosis is predominantly endemic in underdeveloped regions, the accessibility of mNGS remains limited, and it is currently used mainly as an auxiliary diagnostic tool.

Benefit from the in-depth insights into the symptoms, as alternative microscopic tests are being developed. For early pathogenesis, specimens containing lesion tissue can be analyzed using staining methods such as Riesling, Giemsa, periodic-acid Schiff (PAS), and Gomori methenamine silver (GMS), and then examined under a microscope [11]. Morphologically, pathogenic T. marneffei appears as yeast-like microorganisms. Some contain a distinct central septum in a salami-shaped form and are localized intra- or extracellularly [15]. Dermoscopy is another option for distinguishing talaromycosis from other conditions based on vascular morphology. An umbilical notch-like rash is the most typical clinical manifestation of HIV patients with co-infection with T. marneffei [29], which is well-known and recognized by dermatologists. This test is quick, easy, and convenient at the bedside, particularly suitable for AIDS patients in advanced stages with limited mobility who need a provisional diagnosis. Despite such convenience, a disadvantage is still apparent in microscopic tests, mainly due to the dependence on personnel's professions and experiences.

To surmount the limitations in etiological diagnosis, the former understanding and experience of clinics shall be integrated on a wide range and applied beyond reliance on individuals, especially for diagnosing talaromycosis in its early stages. Emerging artificial intelligence (AI) technologies are playing an increasingly important role. J.G. Hu et al. [30] trained a diagnostic model for T. marneffei. They successfully identified four independent predictors of AIDS in combination with T. marneffei, demonstrating good agreement between the predictions and actual observations. Alternatively, based on publicly available online images of skin lesions, Wudi Wei and his team [31] proposed five candidate models. They demonstrated the robustness of two of them in diagnosing cryptococcosis and talaromycosis. Also, based on the clinical records deposited in Guangxi, W.D. Wei's team generated the machine learning (ML) model XGBoost. It achieved a splendid prediction about the hospitalization outcome of HIV patients co-infected with TM. These diagnostic models are simple, easy to understand, and have relatively good diagnostic efficacy. However, there is still room for improvement due to the small size of the training dataset, the limitations of the inclusion indexes, which are not sufficiently refined, and the lack of accuracy in the statistical methods used in the study.

Summary

Infection with T. marneffei is a significant public health challenge; however, current diagnostic tools remain largely insufficient. Regarding fungal culture, the “gold standard,” prolonged turnaround times often compromise clinical outcomes. Emerging AI-driven diagnostic models offer the potential to reduce labor intensity and reliance on specialized equipment and personnel. Concurrently, advancements in molecular biology and immunology promise enhanced diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, feasibility, and, most notably, speed. However, optimizing diagnostic accuracy—especially in complex cases—requires broadening the diagnostic repertoire, primarily by expanding the pool of molecular targets and developing novel probes and methods for existing targets. Establishing these targets will also facilitate rapid risk counseling regarding fungal exposure for immunocompromised travelers. Consequently, post-travel health assessments should include screening for fungal pathogens in symptomatic individuals.

Invasive candidiasis (IC): Current situation and challenges

Globally, an estimated 9.5 million PLWH are affected by candidiasis, with up to 95.0% of AIDS patients developing Candida infections at some point during the course of their disease. IC represents a significant public health burden, contributing to approximately 700,000 of the 1.5 million annual deaths attributable to fungal infections [32], [33]. Among PLWH, the average crude mortality rate from Candida infections is around 40.0% [34]. C. albicans accounts for 64.0% to 67.0% of cases in the Asia-Pacific region and Europe, and 49.0-52.0% in North America and Latin America [35].

The epidemiology of invasive candidiasis is highly dynamic. Variations in species distribution and antifungal resistance across geographic regions and hospital settings, illustrated by studies comparing large and small cities in China or different intensive care unit (ICU) cohorts, highlight the critical role of local epidemiology in guiding empirical therapy and infection control strategies. The Asia-Pacific region accounts for approximately 50.0% of invasive candidemia cases worldwide, with Thailand exhibiting a higher prevalence compared to other Asian countries [36]. A study of 123 Candida bloodstream infection cases in a teaching hospital in Indonesia from January 2011 to June 2022 found that non-albicans species predominated, including Candida parapsilosis (C. parapsilosis) (37.4%), Candida tropicalis (C. tropicalis) (17.1%), Candida glabrata (C. glabrata) (13.0%), Candida guilliermondii (C. guilliermondii) (3.2%), and others (4.8%). C. albicans accounted for only 24.4%. In Singapore, 11 cases of Candida ear infection and two deaths were reported in public hospitals between 2012 and 2019. A retrospective study conducted in a tertiary teaching hospital in Southwest China (2014—2018) reported C. albicans (45.3%) as the most common species, followed by C. glabrata (30.0%) and C. tropicalis (15.2%) [37]. Similarly, a retrospective analysis from Xiamen, China (2006-2024), found C. albicans (42.3%) to be the dominant species in nosocomial candidemia, followed by C. parapsilosis (20.1%), C. tropicalis (18.4%), and C. glabrata (13.3%) [38]. In Guangxi, China, the prevalence of HIV/AIDS co-infection with Candida is the second highest, second only to co-infection with T. marneffei, at 47.5% [39]. Among hospitalized PLWH in Sichuan, Candida infection to be the second most common opportunistic infection (18.3%) after bacterial pneumonia (25.8%) [40]. In another study on the distribution of strains in intensive care units in China, C. albicans accounted for 40.1% of the total, and the rate of Candida in ICUs was documented at 3.2 cases per 1,000 ICU admissions [41]. Traditionally, high-morbidity fungal infections are increasingly associated with new groups of underlying diseases, such as various non-pathogenic fungi of the genus Candida, which have become pathogenic in recent years [42]. Some of these have even caused outbreaks, contributing to a global rise in Candida-related morbidity and mortality [43]. In one word, Guangxi, and even the whole of southern China, is facing an increasing disease burden of HIV co-infection with Candida [44].

The lack of epidemiological and drug resistance data on Candida infections hinders accurate diagnosis and impairs treatment quality, making these urgent priorities. Unclear epidemiology and transmission mechanism of fungal infections are key challenges in controlling both highly prevalent and emerging fungal diseases; non-specific clinical features, complex diagnosis, and limited choice of antifungal drugs, particularly the rise of severe drug resistance in IC infections, pose significant problems that require urgent solutions in clinical medicine [45], [46] (Fig. 2B, 2E, and 2I).

Traditional etiologic detections and their limitations: Microscopy and fungal culture

One of the essential difficulties in diagnosing IC is the lack of early and effective laboratory indicators. Direct fungal smear microscopy involves preparing a smear from collected secretions or observing cultured colonies, allowing budding spores and pseudohyphae to be visualized under the microscope. This method is rapid, requiring only about one minute, efficient, and inexpensive. However, its sensitivity is low, with a positive rate of approximately 21.6%, and a negative result does not exclude infection. Additionally, direct smear microscopy does not permit species identification.

Alternatively, chromogenic culture is widely used to confirm Candida infections, as different species produce colonies with distinct colors and morphologies. Colony counts can provide a semi-quantitative assessment of infection severity, thereby guiding treatment selection [47]. Chromogenic culture also allows differentiation of strains and can help predict antifungal resistance. Serial cultures enable monitoring of treatment response and adjustment of therapy. Nevertheless, this conventional approach typically requires 2–3 days, prolonging the diagnostic timeline. Furthermore, culture-based methods can only detect viable Candida cells, failing to identify non-viable or dormant strains [48]. They also often necessitate specialized techniques, equipment, and laboratory conditions for successful culture and color development. Consequently, despite its advantages, the utility of chromogenic culture in IC diagnosis remains limited.

Blood culture and tissue biopsy are considered the “gold standard” of laboratory diagnosis [49]. Blood culture serves as a primary diagnostic tool because it enables direct identification of the organism and in vitro assessment of antifungal susceptibility. However, its clinical utility is limited by the time-consuming nature of the procedure and suboptimal sensitivity. Cultures typically require 3-5 days, with a positive rate of approximately 50.0% [50]. Moreover, positive results are often obtained only in the late stages of infection, and deep-seated candidiasis without candidemia may go undetected [51]. Tissue biopsy allows for the detection of fungal cells or pseudohyphae through histochemical or cytochemical methods [50]. However, because specimen collection is invasive, requiring procedures such as needle aspiration or incisional biopsy, its feasibility is limited in critically ill patients. The sensitivity of tissue biopsy also depends heavily on the appropriate selection of tissue sites and the homogeneous distribution of Candida within these tissues, a phenomenon often referred to as sampling effects [52].

Overall, traditional etiologic diagnostic methods have hindered timely and accurate clinical diagnosis of Candida infections. Consequently, the development of novel diagnostic strategies that are faster, more efficient, and more reliable is urgently needed.

Immunological diagnosis: Antigen and antibody detection

To improve the laboratory diagnosis of IC, better diagnostic markers shall be identified to establish more reliable, effective, and rapid detection methods. Serologic methods have become a prominent focus in this field because of their advantages, including sensitivity, simplicity, and ease of implementation. Since antigenic substances and metabolites are widely released into the bloodstream from Candida after invasion and infection, direct signs of disease were discovered and characterized stepwise [53]. The core processes of Candida infection are thought to be similar to those of other pathogenic fungi. Upon infection, cell wall components, adhesion molecules, virulence factors, growth- or metabolism-related enzymes are among the first to interact with the host. Potential biomarkers derived from these components have subsequently been investigated and applied in immunological diagnostics, monitoring of treatment response, and assessment of clinical regression.

To date, two commercialized kits targeting Candida cell wall polysaccharides have become clinically available; however, their sensitivity and specificity remain suboptimal. In 2005, BDG was incorporated into the microbiological criteria for the clinical and proposed diagnosis of invasive fungal infections by organizations in Europe and the United States, leading to the development of the BDG assay. Serum BDG detection has demonstrated moderate sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing invasive fungal infections, including candidiasis, and is particularly valuable for early diagnosis in neutropenic patients with acute leukemia. Monitoring BDG antigenemia in these patients can facilitate prompt diagnosis. BDG is not exclusive to Candida infections; it can also be elevated in bacterial infections, febrile conditions, and following certain surgical procedures, resulting in frequent false-positive results. Consequently, BDG is typically interpreted alongside coagulation indicators and clinical features. A recent evaluation of a chemiluminescent BDG immunoassay in India reported acceptable performance for invasive candidiasis, with a sensitivity of 60.5% and specificity of 81.8% [54]. Mannan, another major component of the Candida cell wall, is released into the bloodstream during infection and serves as a key antigen. Y.T. Jiang first established an ELISA method for detecting serum Candida mannan to diagnose IC, and achieved a sensitivity of 66.1% and specificity of 96.2% [55], [56]. Across various Candida species, the sensitivity of mannan detection ranges from 58.0% to 83.0%, with the highest rates observed in C. albicans infections [57]. Notably, positive mannan results can precede blood culture positivity or imaging findings in liver and spleen candidiasis, making the assay a powerful tool for early diagnosis [58]. In clinical practice, high-risk patients are typically tested for mannan two to three times per week; however, the assay’s impact on treatment decisions and prognostic outcomes requires further investigation.

In summary, immunological diagnostics for invasive candidiasis remain under development, and the overall performance of current assays is still insufficient to fully meet clinical needs. The identification of additional biomarkers and the development of novel detection methods are essential to improve both sensitivity and specificity.

Molecular and proteomic advancements: Mass spectrometry

As an emerging diagnostic approach, mass spectrometry offers high sensitivity, high specificity, and rapid turnaround times, making it particularly suited for detecting low-abundance metabolites or proteins in samples from suspected Candida infections. This technology shows significant potential for clinical diagnosis and may provide a breakthrough for the early and rapid detection of infectious diseases [59]. In 2023, a research team at the Stomatology School of Peking University validated the accuracy of matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) for strain identification in denture-associated oral candidiasis, achieving over 98.0% accuracy. They also established a rapid pre-processing protocol for saliva samples using a 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid wash, reducing detection time to 1.5 hours [60]. Moreover, MALDI-TOF MS enables the identification of strain species and subtypes based on the composition of detected Candida proteins [61], dramatically shortening diagnostic waiting times and facilitating timely therapeutic interventions [59]. Mass spectrometry also provides more comprehensive information, which is valuable for understanding pathogen prevalence and variation. In 2022, researchers of Shanghai Jiao Tong University developed an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) method that achieved the first accurate quantitative detection of the C. albicans quorum-sensing signaling molecule farnesol in biofilms, with a sensitivity of 0.1 ng/mL, revealing its dose-dependent relationship with virulence gene expression [62].

However, mass spectrometry still has notable disadvantages. It requires specialized and expensive equipment, with mass spectrometers costing approximately $2.8 million. Furthermore, the technique generates large amounts of data, whose storage, analysis, and interpretation demand substantial resources and expertise. After all, highly trained experts and high costs are both necessary to employ spectrometry for diagnostic service of IC and other infectious diseases, which significantly limit its actual application in medical agencies.

Dilemma and emerging hopes

Current conventional etiologic and immunologic diagnosis of IC still lacks rapid, accurate, informative, and cost-effective methods. In the pursuit of novel strategies, several promising techniques have emerged. Here we present examples of these emerging diagnostic technologies, which can be broadly categorized into next-generation sequencing‑based methods, mass spectrometry, alternative platforms such as T2MR, and data‑driven approaches.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) is one of the foremost approaches. In addition to directly detecting IC infection, NGS enables broad-spectrum fungal species identification from clinical samples. In 2017, R. Alonso et al. [63] demonstrated the use of NGS to analyze fungal communities in brain tissue samples, providing robust evidence of multi-species fungal colonization, including Candida species. Building on this, in 2024, T.F. Li et al. [64] optimized mNGS for pathogen detection, significantly enhancing the sensitivity and timeliness of diagnosing invasive fungal diseases, including candidiasis. Subsequent multicenter studies have further validated its clinical utility. Similarly, Nanopore sequencing technology has been employed in the diagnosis of IC and demonstrated a high specificity of 97.2% [65]. In early trials, nonspecific amplification of human DNA occurred when the 18S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene was used as a pan-fungal target. This issue was subsequently resolved by switching to the ITS region, which not only eliminated cross-reactivity but also enabled species-level identification of fungi, including Candida.

As described in Section 3.3, mass spectrometry is another advanced technology capable of rapidly and accurately identifying fungal species from both culture and non-culture clinical samples, including those suspected of IC infection [66]. In parallel, several host-derived biomarkers such as procalcitonin, serum amyloid A, and specific interleukins have been highlighted as potential tools to improve diagnostic accuracy. Alternatively, T2 Magnetic Resonance (T2MR) technology has also been investigated for IC diagnosis. Using the T2Candida Panel, this method enables rapid detection and identification of the five most common Candida species directly from whole blood within approximately 3-5 hours, without the need for prior culture [67].

Beyond directly detecting IC infections, leveraging information from continuously accumulated clinical records can be particularly valuable for identifying deep-seated infections and avoiding delays in initiating treatment. In a 2023 study, machine learning-driven whole-genome single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis using XGBoost enabled rapid and accurate identification of invasive candidiasis pathogens, including C. albicans, C. glabrata, and C. tropicalis, within 15 minutes, achieving 98.7% accuracy and reducing detection time by approximately four days compared to traditional culture methods [68].

Summary and prospects

In summary, current strategies for the diagnosis of invasive IC, whether in clinical practice or under development, generally exhibit notable limitations in terms of speed, sensitivity, accuracy, and cost[69]. Addressing these shortcomings requires concerted efforts across multiple fronts, primarily including three approaches: (1) Identification and evaluation of novel biomarkers of IC infection, encompassing molecules derived from both the pathogen and the host; (2) Development of advanced detection methods for clinical samples that combine high sensitivity and accuracy, while minimizing the need for culture or complex sample processing; (3) Innovation in data mining and information utilization strategies, particularly through the integration bioinformatics and AI tools.

These newly developed next-generation diagnostic technologies enable rapid and accurate identification of pathogenic species, providing valuable information on antifungal susceptibility. Such capabilities support the earlier initiation of targeted antifungal therapy, improve clinical outcomes, and facilitate antifungal stewardship [70], [71]. Concurrently, enhanced global and regional surveillance is essential to accurately monitor incidence and species distribution, thereby informing clinical guidelines and public health interventions.

Moreover, these technological advancements depend on comprehensive investigations of IC infections. Current research on Candida remains largely focused on a limited number of species, which is insufficient for a thorough understanding of its biological mechanisms or for the broad discovery of potential disease biomarkers. In both basic and clinical settings, extensive studies of invasive Candida infections are therefore required to advance diagnostic strategies.

Situation and clinical impact of HIV-associated cryptococcosis and pneumocystis: Epidemiological trends and clinical ramifications

Classified by WHO as a high-priority fungal pathogen, Cryptococcus species cause cryptococcosis, which primarily affects the lungs and central nervous system (CNS). Cryptococcal infections account for 19.0% of AIDS-related mortality and significantly impair the quality of life of coinfected patients. A 2015 analysis of 43 patients revealed that 88.4% presented with CNS infections, while 65.1% exhibited concurrent CNS and pulmonary disease. Despite systematic antifungal therapy, the overall case fatality rate remained at 27.9% [72]. The research team from the Vietnam Centre for Tropical Diseases (VCTD) and Bach Mai Hospital reported that, although Vietnam has achieved 70.0% antiretroviral therapy (ART) coverage, the in-hospital mortality rate for cryptococcal meningitis (CM) patients remains elevated at 35.0% [73]. A 17-year retrospective analysis from Thailand found that 65.0% of HIV-negative individuals with pulmonary cryptococcosis had underlying comorbidities, with an overall morbidity and mortality rate of 27.0%. A 2024 cohort study from Guangxi's Hezhou People's Hospital of 22 patients with HIV-associated cryptococcal bloodstream infection reported a mortality rate of 32.0%. However, 55.0% of patients were lost to follow-up after discontinuing treatment, suggesting that the true mortality burden may be underestimated [74]. Survivors of co-infection often suffer from cerebral herniation with permanent sequelae affecting up to 69.2% of patients [75]. Since 2020, highly pathogenic Cryptococcus strains have been emerging in southern China. A 2021 study in Guangxi identified clinical strains of C. deuterogattii (VGIIa) and Cryptococcus gattii (C. gattii) sensu stricto (VGIV) from the C. gattii species complex, underscoring the need for national surveillance of HIV-cryptococcal co-infections, particularly in regions such as Guangxi [76] (Fig. 2C, 2G, and 2K).

Concurrently, approximately 75.0% of HIV patients acquire P. jirovecii infection at least once in their lifetime. A global study published in the Journal of Fungi in 2017 estimated an annual incidence of 500,000 P. jirovecii cases worldwide, with a mortality rate reaching 80.0% in the absence of timely treatment [77]. Conservatively estimated, the incidence of PJP in AIDS patients within two years is 22.0%, accounting for 70.0% of HIV-associated infections [78]. According to the multicenter case-control MY-Pneumo study in Peninsular Malaysia, immunocompromised children with pneumonia were more likely to develop severe disease [79]. Interstitial pneumonia is the common clinical outcome of PJP secondary to AIDS. In mild cases, hypoxemia may be created, while results of severe cases commonly include pulmonary fibrosis, respiratory failure, and even death. This disease progresses rapidly. Critically ill patients typically die within one month [80], and remarkably high morbidity and mortality rates have been reported. In patients without antiretroviral treatment, the mortality rate ranges from 20.0% to 40.0%, while exceeding 40.0% in those without any treatment (Fig. 2F and 2J).

Currently, the commonly used examinations are based on pathogenesis and imaging. The pathogenetic examinations include direct microscopy, fungal culture, antigen detection, and molecular biology tests. However, a low fungal load in immunocompromised hosts has been reported as a significant obstacle in diagnosis; therefore, developing and choosing appropriate approaches is critical to achieve timeliness and accuracy.

Etiologic detection: Traditional approaches and challenges

The etiologic diagnosis of both cryptococcosis and PJP in AIDS patients has traditionally relied upon pathological examination and radiographic imaging.

Cryptococcal culture is considered the gold standard for determining infection, with primary clinical specimens including cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), respiratory samples [such as sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)], and blood. India ink staining of CSF is a rapid and straightforward method with a sensitivity of 80.0%, making it suitable for initial screening, though it cannot distinguish between species or genotypes [81]. For pulmonary cryptococcosis, sputum is the preferred initial specimen for screening and diagnosis. When sputum results are negative despite high clinical suspicion, BALF is employed as a supplementary diagnostic method, though its use requires careful consideration of the potential risks. Culture typically takes 3-4 days at 37 °C in untreated patients, but may extend beyond 3 weeks in treated individuals, making it challenging to meet the needs of rapid diagnosis clinics [82]. Concurrently, detection rates are highly dependent on pathogen load. CSF culture yields a positivity rate of 70.0%–90.0%, whereas blood culture positivity declines further in disseminated infections or immunocompromised patients (20.0%–50.0%), thus necessitating adjunct antigen testing to compensate for the insufficient sensitivity [83].

Due to poor development of culture techniques, diagnosing Pneumocystis is mainly based on direct examination under microscopy. Commonly tested samples include induced sputum or BALF, which are processed as cell smears or tissue sections and stained with methods such as Giemsa, GMS, or fluorescent whitener [84]. This strategy is adopted as the “gold standard” test for diagnosing Pneumocystis. BALF demonstrates a sensitivity of 80.0%–95.0% for detecting Pneumocystis, markedly higher than sputum analysis (<30.0%) [85]. BALF is an invasive procedure with certain risks; however, its diagnostic yield substantially outweighs the potential risks in critically ill or immunocompromised patients [86]. The validity of staining and microscopic observation can be compromised by improper sampling, suboptimal staining techniques, and operator inexperience, particularly when the pathogen load is low or the morphology is atypical following PJP prophylaxis or treatment. For instance, labeling C. albicans as P. jirovecii is a common mistake. In other words, the overall sensitivity is too low to be considered a suitable routine laboratory test.

Histopathologic examination offers higher sensitivity than microscopy for both fungi and the ability to distinguish between Cryptococcus and Pneumocystis species and genotypes in diseased tissue. However, its invasiveness and reliance on staff experience limit its clinical applicability. Furthermore, preliminary diagnosis in clinical settings typically integrates patient symptoms, blood gas analysis, and chest imaging. BALF examination is the primary method to confirm the clinical diagnosis of P. jirovecii pneumonia. Chest computed tomography (CT) offers a simple, speedy, and noninvasive alternative that aids in timely diagnosis.

Immunological diagnosis: Applicability and limitations

Traditional latex agglutination (LA) testing for detecting Cryptococcus capsular polysaccharide antigen has a sensitivity of 89.0%–95.0% [87]. However, its operation is cumbersome. Since 2010, the colloidal gold immunochromatographic assay (CrAg-LFA) has gradually become the mainstream technique. It targets glucuronoxylomannan (GXM)—a polysaccharide secreted in the Cryptococcus capsule that can be released into body fluids during early infection [88]. CrAg LFA is particularly valuable for screening high-risk populations, such as PLHIV with advanced disease, and has been incorporated into a rapid diagnostic assay [89]. As the most widely used method in clinical practice, it demonstrates 95.0% sensitivity and specificity for cryptococcosis [81]. However, false-negative results may occur at very high or low antigen concentrations, while false positives can arise in rheumatoid factor-positive patients or those treated with hydroxyethyl starch. Additionally, this assay cannot differentiate Cryptococcus species or identify genotypes.

Serum BDG detection has emerged as a valuable noninvasive adjunct marker for Pneumocystis. BDG is a cell wall component of P. jirovecii and other fungi, released into the bloodstream during infection. A study in China found that plasma BDG concentration was significantly higher in HIV-associated PJP patients compared to those with other pulmonary infections, with sensitivity (85.2%) and specificity (86.8%) at an optimal cut-off value [90]. The immunofluorescent assay (IFA), a primary method for detecting P. jirovecii, has a sensitivity and specificity range of 48.0%–100.0% and 81.0%–100.0%. These assays utilize monoclonal antibodies targeting antigens on the cell walls of both ascus (cyst) and trophic forms of P. jirovecii. A positive result is indicated by bright apple-green fluorescent structures showing characteristic fungal morphologies [91].

Molecular diagnosis: Advances in multiple approaches

The high sensitivity and specificity of PCR tools provide excellent accuracy, particularly in the early diagnosis of Cryptococcus and Pneumocystis infections associated with HIV. It supports a variety of non-invasively collected samples, such as sputum, BALF, oropharyngeal rinses, and blood. Compared to conventional methods, PCR offers distinct advantages, particularly for organisms like Pneumocystis that are challenging to culture.

Multiplex PCR demonstrated 100.0% sensitivity and specificity in detecting Cryptococcal infection. Real-time-PCR (RT-PCR) exhibited a sensitivity of 96.4% and a specificity of 100.0% [92], while the sensitivity of nested PCR reached above 89.0%. These methods also showed high accuracy in strain identification. For Pneumocystis, qPCR demonstrated an overall sensitivity of 84.8% and specificity of 96.1%. Sensitivity was higher in HIV-positive patients (91.0%) than in HIV-negative patients (81.0%). Performance varied by sample type: sensitivity was 94.0% for BALF and 81.0% for sputum, with specificity ranging from 80.0% to 90.0% for both. Oral washes achieved 100.0% sensitivity and 78.0% specificity. Additionally, these tests can be conducted rapidly, with reports generated within 6 hours. In summary, PCR-based strategies demonstrated power and robustness, qualifying them as a routine diagnostic tool for Cryptococcal and Pneumocystis infections.

Integrating PCR for specific pathogens or pan-fungal targets, followed by Sanger sequencing or targeted massive parallel sequencing (MPS) on tissue samples, is helpful for pathogen identification and monitoring treatment responses in AIDS patients with co-infections of Cryptococcus and Pneumocystis [93]. However, high-quality equipment and excellent technical support are required unavoidably, from which high costs are always derived, hindering applications in a broader range, especially in economically underdeveloped areas.

mNGS demonstrates exemplary performance in diagnosing cryptococcal and Pneumocystis infections. With this technology, a diagnostic report of cryptococcal infection in HIV-negative CSF yielded a sensitivity of 93.5%, a specificity of 96.0%, a positive predictive value of 87.8%, and a negative predictive value of 98.0% [94]. Another study showed a sensitivity of 44.3% and a specificity of 99.8% in plasma samples. Generally, compared with CSF, BALF shows a relatively higher sensitivity, making it more suitable for mNGS analysis. Similar results were also achieved with mNGS in the diagnosis of PJP. The sensitivity and specificity of lower respiratory tract specimens were comparable to those of PCR and outperformed GMS staining [84]. The combination of mNGS and serum BDG further enhances the diagnosis of suspected PJP [95].

mNGS generates extensive data, enabling the simultaneous detection and identification of co-pathogens and complex strain infections common in AIDS-associated pneumonia [95]. The mNGS is still limited by some factors. Besides the high cost of equipment and operation, contamination by exogenous microorganisms and the low efficiency of DNA/RNA extraction from thick-walled fungi are two major causes that lead to the inefficiency of mNGS. Standardization and workflow optimization are also needed for better performance and wider routine use [96], [97].

New markers and emerging technologies in diagnosis

Two new biomarkers, cell-free DNA (cfDNA) and S-adenosylmethionine (AdoMet), have been developed for diagnosing HIV-associated Pneumocystis. Noninvasive PCR targeting cfDNA reports approximately 100.0% sensitivity in confirmed cases and improved specificity compared to β-D-glucan among immunocompromised patients [98]. These findings offer a new diagnostic option for individuals who are eligible for bronchoscopy [98]. Another potential biomarker undergoing exploration is AdoMet, a metabolic intermediate that Pneumocystis scavenges from the host. Low abundance of plasma AdoMet was demonstrated as an indicator sensitive and specific to acute PJP infection in HIV-positive individuals, with levels increasing upon successful treatment. While promising, this marker requires further clinical validation.

A retrospective study reported that a combined serum BDG assay achieved 100.0% sensitivity in serum samples from confirmed PJP cases [99]. Pulmonary imaging exhibits considerable variety and lacks specificity for other infectious pathogens, while rapid molecular diagnostic technologies are under development [97]. The Recombinase polymerase amplification-lateral flow strip (RPA-LFS) system targets the capsule-associated gene CAP64 and provides results within 20 minutes. With its specificity and sensitivity as high as 100.0%, this tool is potentially suitable for rapid, on-site clinical diagnosis [97]. Beyond detection, molecular typing methods, such as multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), are used to assess the genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships of Cryptococcus species complex isolates, which are essential for epidemiological surveillance and understanding the spread of virulent strains [99], [100]. To facilitate further progress in the diagnosis of HIV-associated pneumonia, accumulated clinical materials and records will be utilized for technical improvements. For instance, sputum staining and monoclonal antibody immunofluorescence will benefit from higher sensitivity and specificity [101].

In the diagnosis of Cryptococcus, targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) has brought out a key advantage in accurately distinguishing minor differences in complex infections. For instance, from a single BALF sample, nucleic acid sequences of either A. fumigatus or C. neoformans can be detected simultaneously and unambiguously via tNGS [102], highlighting the potential of tNGS to resolve complex co-infections.

Summary and future directions

Invasive fungal infections are frequent complications predominantly caused by pathogens such as H. capsulatum [103], Cryptococcus [104] and P. jirovecii [105]. Relatively, Cryptococcus and Pneumocystis infections are prevalent in China [100], while histoplasmosis is distinctly endemic to the Americas (e.g., the U.S. and Brazil) [106].

Direct microscopy remains the gold standard for diagnosing PJP. Regarding cryptococcosis, fungal culture is recognized as the definitive and confirmatory diagnostic tool, serving as the gold standard. mNGS, despite its high sensitivity and specificity, is limited by high costs and susceptibility to interference; therefore, it is primarily reserved for use in auxiliary diagnosis in complex cases. Radiological imaging, while lacking specificity, is instrumental in localizing lesions and monitoring disease progression. Ultimately, diagnosing AIDS-associated pneumonia necessitates a multimodal approach that leverages the distinct advantages of various technologies. This comprehensive strategy enhances diagnostic accuracy and timeliness, facilitating individualized treatment plans and thereby effectively reducing morbidity and mortality.

Discussion and conclusions

China stands as a pioneer in engaging with global health governance initiatives. Building on the consensus established at the first China-ASEAN Forum on Disease Control in 2016, substantial progress has been made in regional health collaboration. Health security has become a central pillar of China-ASEAN cooperation, providing significant impetus for the “Health Silk Road” initiative. China has cumulatively invested 10 million dollars to strengthen regional health capacity, training over 930 specialized technicians from ASEAN countries. Collaborative projects span critical infectious disease control, including malaria, dengue fever, and HIV/AIDS. For instance, with Chinese support, Cambodia has attained the National AIDS Authority “89-98-98” targets (89.0% diagnosed, 98.0% treated, 98.0% virally suppressed). Furthermore, cross-border collaboration between China’s Guangxi and Vietnam's northern border provinces has synchronized epidemic surveillance, joint interventions, and capacity-building programs. These efforts have yielded a follow-up rate exceeding 85.0% and a treatment coverage of 84.5% in the border communities.

Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, a representative province in southern China, serves as an essential coastal area bordering the Gulf of Tonkin/South China Sea and a vital land gateway between China and Southeast Asia. Owing to its humid subtropical monsoon climate, the region’s environmental conditions favor the growth and reproduction of fungi—particularly the pathogenic species discussed herein. Given the expanding population of PLWH, the diagnosis and treatment of fungal infections have emerged as significant public health challenges. Similar difficulties are increasingly observed in neighboring regions with comparable geographic and climatic profiles, including other provinces in southern China and adjacent Southeast Asian countries (Fig. 3A and 3B).Fig. 3Spectrum of major fungal infections in Southeast Asian neighboring countries and southern China. In addition to these environmental factors, a series of issues jointly exacerbate the challenges, including unclear epidemiology and transmission mechanisms, poorly characterized clinical presentations, and limited options for antifungal medication. The corresponding solution lies in the logic of sustainable development. To address this issue, research efforts should be intensified in epidemiology, pathogenic mechanisms, and translational studies targeting fungal infections. To benefit clinical outcomes and patients’ quality of life, novel diagnostic techniques are becoming the key players in overcoming the technical bottlenecks currently associated with HIV-related fungal infections.

To date, the gold standard for diagnosis remains fungal culture or microscopic examination. However, these methodologies face significant limitations in practice. These gold-standard tests are highly dependent on well-equipped laboratories and professional personnel, which is particularly problematic in resource-limited settings. In Southeast Asia, more than 40.0% of HIV-associated disseminated fungal infections have been missed in estimation [107] due to several factors, such as the lack of independent microbiology laboratories, limited external quality assessment (EQA) for hospitals, insufficient training of laboratory personnel, and sub-optimal operational procedures. These factors collectively undermine the practical utility of the gold standard in high-burden areas. On the other hand, the limitations regarding turnaround time and diagnostic sensitivity of gold standard tests continue to draw attention in this area. Therefore, rapid and accurate new diagnosis, particularly with low dependence on external resources, is urgently needed to facilitate timely and targeted therapy.

Molecular techniques, such as PCR, have significantly improved diagnostic efficiency due to their high sensitivity and are now widely implemented in microbial detection. However, the effectiveness of PCR can be limited by the efficiency of DNA extraction, primarily due to difficulties in lysing the fungal cell wall, thereby preventing the release of DNA. T2MR can provide rapid, culture-independent detection of pathogens directly from whole blood within hours, representing a significant advancement in speed and efficiency. Currently, T2MR is relatively well-established for Candida detection only. Moreover, NGS remains limited in resource-constrained settings due to its dependence on specialized instruments and advanced technical expertise. Regional resource disparities fundamentally hinder the adoption of advanced diagnostics in high-burden regions. Although the foundational capabilities of genomics analysis have already existed in some countries of Southeast Asia, the availability of such equipment remains only two-thirds that of developed countries. In medical imaging, disparities are even starker—for example, a fiftyfold gap exists between Vietnam and Japan in the penetration of specialized devices. Furthermore, public health investment as a share of gross domestic product (GDP) in this region lags far behind that of major developed economies, highlighting a profound structural underinvestment [108]. In such resource-limited settings, highly feasible and simplified strategies are urgently required for fungal diagnosis.

Immunodiagnosis also provides a key advantage with significantly reduced turnaround time and simplified operation. Future advancements will be driven by the discovery of novel biomarkers from diverse sources, such as pathogen components, host immunoglobulins, or other immune complexes, and various host factors. On the other hand, emerging technologies for target detection also enhance the capability of diagnosis. For example, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) offers significant advantages for practical applications, including the portability of handheld devices, rapid detection within minutes, and, particularly, a low cost of operation, which is based on the label-free detection of intrinsic Raman fingerprints of target molecules in pathogens [109]. Meanwhile, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) achieves specificity of up to 100.0% for pathogenic nucleic acids at copy numbers as low as 3.2 copies/mL, which is significantly more sensitive than qPCR [110]. Furthermore, multiplex diagnostic panels will be developed to offer an integrative diagnosis for complex infections originating from multiple pathogens in suspects.

To meet the clinical demands in resource-limited settings, AI and ML have been utilized to interpret extensive, or even global, clinical and laboratory data. Nowadays, a panel of national and regional assistance programs has been launched to provide support in finance and policy. Besides the model built by W.D. Wei et al. [39] for T. marneffei, another model based on the mNGS data of BALF has also demonstrated high accuracy, reaching 0.955 of ACC in distinguishing fungal infection from various lung infections or cancers [111]. Zhujiang Hospital has developed an AI-assisted diagnostic system that, for the first time, combined dynamic vital signs with static imaging in a multimodal approach. Since it only needs limited processing power in local devices, this model is expected to be adopted by primary care hospitals, ultimately setting a new standard for AI-based diagnosis of infectious diseases. The utilization of these integrated approaches facilitates early screening, precise pathogen typing, and effective therapy monitoring. Hence, patient morbidity and risk of epidemic transmission are then expected to be suppressed from a technical perspective. It’s more important that the installation and utilization of diagnostic models can be deployed independently of their site of origin. Dissemination of expert-level diagnostic capabilities will then be enabled to grassroots healthcare institutions, from which the shortage of professional expertise can be compensated, and the goal of cost reduction and efficiency improvement becomes practicable.

Therefore, the ultimate goal for future development is to enhance both the speed and precision of diagnosis, enabling early proactive containment of fungal infections to prevent life-threatening complications. We hope the development and application of these diagnostic methods and biomarkers will achieve “early diagnosis and treatment”, thereby reducing mortality among HIV patients with fungal co-infections. More importantly, China's policies and practices in managing fungal diseases can be disseminated to the global community. The overarching objective is to enhance the prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and control of fungal diseases, benefiting the population in China and worldwide.
