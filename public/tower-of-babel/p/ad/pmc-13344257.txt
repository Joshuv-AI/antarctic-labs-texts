ER-mitochondria tether ML1 coordinates peripheral fission and wholesale mitophagy for plant adaptation to carbon starvation
Authors: Benhui Shi, Baolei Li, Songyang Wang, Xu Zhang, Yu Tang, Jun Jiang, Liting Zhai, Yang Shao, Jiaqi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plants frequently encounter carbon starvation from extended darkness or canopy shading or in nonphotosynthetic tissues, requiring rapid mitochondrial remodeling to match reduced metabolic flux. Here, we reveal a coordinated program integrating peripheral fission–mediated damage segregation and wholesale mitophagy. Carbon starvation triggers a shift from symmetric midzone fission to asymmetric peripheral fission, generating small depolarized fragments alongside larger polarized mitochondria. Unexpectedly, damage-independent wholesale mitophagy targets medium-sized mitochondria for both burden reduction and resource mobilization while excluding small peripheral fission products. We identify mitochondria-ER (endoplasmic reticulum) linker 1 (ML1), a carbon starvation–inducible ER-mitochondria tether, as the central coordinator. At ER-mitochondria contact sites, ML1 promotes peripheral fission and recruits ATG18a (autophagy-related protein 18a) for wholesale mitophagy. Loss of ML1 impairs this coordinated remodeling, resulting in elongated mitochondria, compromised function, and hypersensitivity to carbon starvation. These findings reveal how plants achieve rapid metabolic adaptation through coordinated mitochondrial remodeling.

INTRODUCTION

As sessile organisms, plants must endure fluctuating nutrient availability in situ, rendering them particularly vulnerable to periods of carbon limitation. Carbon starvation occurs when photosynthetic carbon fixation cannot meet metabolic demands, arising from extended darkness or canopy shading or in nonphotosynthetic sink tissues such as roots, flowers, and developing seeds that depend on translocated sugars (1–3). These episodes can persist for hours to days, imposing sustained metabolic pressure that threatens cellular energy homeostasis and survival. Survival requires plants to minimize energy consumption while preserving core functions through selective down-regulation of energy-intensive processes, macromolecular recycling, and dynamic organellar remodeling. How plants achieve this metabolic reprogramming at the organellar level remains incompletely understood.

Mitochondria occupy a central position in carbon metabolism, serving as the primary site where respiratory substrates are oxidized to generate adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) (4). With adequate carbon supply, mitochondria process glycolytic products through the tricarboxylic acid cycle and electron transport chain, sustaining cellular energy demands (5, 6). However, carbon starvation creates a metabolic paradox. As substrate availability declines, maintaining a large mitochondrial population becomes energetically counterproductive. Mitochondria themselves consume ATP for protein import, quality control, and membrane potential maintenance (7, 8), while ongoing respiration continues to deplete the dwindling pool of respiratory substrates. This creates an unsustainable scenario where the organelles responsible for energy production become net energy consumers, potentially accelerating cellular energy depletion. To survive extended carbon limitation, cells must therefore dynamically adjust their mitochondrial populations to balance metabolic supply and demand. A previous study has established that carbon starvation triggers mitophagy, the selective autophagic degradation of mitochondria, which can reduce mitochondrial mass and alleviate metabolic burden (9), raising a critical question of how cells rapidly eliminate large numbers of mitochondria. Classical mitophagy models, derived largely from damage-induced scenarios, propose that depolarized or dysfunctional mitochondria are selectively targeted for degradation (10, 11). However, mitochondrial damage induced by substrate depletion is typically a gradual process, while carbon starvation requires rapid and substantial reduction of mitochondrial mass to match declining metabolic flux (9, 12). This paradox suggests that carbon starvation–induced mitophagy may operate through mechanisms distinct from damage-based quality control, but how cells select mitochondria for degradation under metabolic stress remains unknown.

Emerging evidence indicates that endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-mitochondria contact sites play crucial roles in coordinating mitophagy. In yeast, ER-mitochondria tethering is essential for mitophagy formation (13), and in mammals, multiple tethers including PTPIP51 (protein tyrosine phosphatase–interacting protein–51), MFN2 (mitofusin-2), SYNJ2BP (synaptojanin 2 binding protein), and BAP31 (B cell receptor–associated protein 31) participate in mitophagy regulation (14–16). A recent study has revealed that ER-mitochondria contact sites play an important role in plant mitophagy. The TraB family proteins localize to mitochondrial outer membranes and interact with the ER protein VAP27 (VAMP–associated protein 27-1) to form contact sites while simultaneously recruiting autophagy-related protein 8 (ATG8) to facilitate autophagic degradation of depolarized mitochondria induced by chemical uncouplers, representing a damage-based quality control mechanism (11). However, whether ER-mitochondria contact sites participate in mitophagy induced by physiological stresses such as carbon starvation remains unexplored. Unlike acute chemical damage, metabolic stresses may require coordinated organellar adaptation beyond simple damage clearance, but the underlying mechanisms remain unknown. Beyond maintaining organelle number, mitochondrial fission contributes to quality control through asymmetric division and selective segregation. In both yeast and mammals, fission generates daughter mitochondria (DMs) with heterogeneous properties, including differences in membrane potential (17–21). Damaged mitochondria with low membrane potential are excluded from subsequent fusion and targeted for autophagic degradation, enabling the separation of dysfunctional organelles from the healthy pool. A recent study in mammalian cells identified two functionally distinct fission modes (22). Midzone fission generates similar-sized daughters for proliferation and requires ER-mediated preconstriction, whereas peripheral fission produces small mitochondria (SMs) enriched with damaged material for mitophagy and is regulated by lysosomal contact independently of ER (22). In plants, mitochondrial fission is mediated by conserved proteins such as DRP3A (dynamin-related protein 3A)/DRP3B and plant-specific factors like ELM1 (23–25). However, plant mitochondria are substantially smaller than their mammalian counterparts in both size and morphology (24, 26). Unlike the tubular-reticulate mitochondrial networks commonly observed in animal and yeast cells, plant mitochondria predominantly exist as small punctate structures (0.2 to 1.5 μm) (27), posing technical challenges for live-cell imaging and quantitative analysis of fission/fusion dynamics. Consequently, while microscopic observations have revealed correlations between ER-mitochondria interactions and mitochondrial constriction (26, 27), whether plant mitochondria undergo functionally distinct fission modes similar to those in mammals, how ER-mitochondria contact sites regulate different fission modes, and whether these modes contribute to quality control during metabolic stress such as carbon starvation all remain unknown.

In this study, we identified mitochondria-ER linker 1 (ML1), a carbon starvation–inducible ER-mitochondria tether protein that coordinates peripheral fission–mediated damage segregation and wholesale mitophagy of the bulk mitochondrial population. This coordinated mechanism does not rely on damage-recognition signals but instead enables rapid mitochondrial removal to balance metabolic flux, representing an adaptive strategy distinct from conventional damage-induced mitophagy.

RESULTS

Carbon starvation shifts mitochondrial fission from midzone to peripheral mode

To characterize mitochondrial dynamics, we first defined two distinct fission patterns on the basis of the relative sizes of DMs. Midzone fission occurs when a mitochondrion divides at approximately the middle position, producing two DMs of similar size. In contrast, peripheral fission involves an SM (≤25% of mother mitochondrion size) budding off from a larger mitochondrion (LM) at a peripheral location (Fig. 1A). Using time-lapse confocal microscopy of mitochondria in Arabidopsis root cells, we directly visualized these two fission modes. Midzone fission events showed mitochondria dividing at central constriction sites, generating two DMs with comparable dimensions (Fig. 1B and movie S1). Peripheral fission events displayed asymmetric division at terminal positions, producing one large mitochondrion and one SM (Fig. 1C and movie S2). Quantitative analysis of DM lengths revealed notable differences between the two fission modes (fig. S1A). In midzone fission, both DMs exhibited similar lengths of ~1.0 μm, with no significant size disparity between them. In sharp contrast, peripheral fission generated highly asymmetric DMs, with the SM measuring ~0.4 to 0.5 μm in length and the LM reaching ~1.5 μm, representing a nearly threefold size difference (fig. S1A). Quantification using a length asymmetry index further confirmed this distinction, with midzone fission showing near-symmetry (asymmetry index ~0.1), while peripheral fission displayed strong asymmetry (asymmetry index ~0.5) (Fig. 1D). Beyond morphological differences, we examined whether DMs from these two fission modes have different physiological properties using staining with tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE), a cationic dye that accumulates in polarized mitochondria (10). In midzone fission, both DMs maintained comparable membrane potential after division (Fig. 1, E and G). In contrast, peripheral fission generated asymmetric membrane potential distribution, with the LM retaining strong TMRE signal, while the SM exhibited markedly reduced intensity, indicating depolarization (Fig. 1, F and G). These findings demonstrate that midzone fission generates symmetric daughters with comparable membrane potential, while peripheral fission produces asymmetric daughters with the smaller one being depolarized.

Given that the two fission modes generate DMs with distinct properties, we examined how fission dynamics change during carbon starvation and whether these changes correlate with mitophagy activity (9). To address this, we performed time-lapse imaging of Arabidopsis roots coexpressing Mito-GFP (green fluorescent protein) and mCherry-ATG8e during carbon starvation (Figs. 1H and 2, A and B). Initially (0 hours), midzone fission predominated at ~75% of total fission events, while peripheral fission was rarely observed. During carbon starvation, this pattern reversed markedly, with peripheral fission rising to ~70% by 6 hours and then continuing to increase to ~90% by 24 hours. Accompanying these fission dynamics, the mitophagy level (percentage of ATG8-labeled autophagosomes containing mitochondrion) rose sharply, peaking at ~40% between 6 and 12 hours of starvation before declining to near baseline levels by 24 hours (Fig. 1H). Notably, neither fission mode exhibited significant changes in fission duration across the time course (fig. S1B), indicating that carbon starvation modulates fission frequency and mode selection rather than the kinetics of individual fission events.

These dynamic shifts in fission patterns were accompanied by changes in mitochondrial size distribution. The average mitochondrial length decreased significantly from ~1.0 μm at 0 hours to ~0.80 μm by 24 hours of carbon starvation (fig. S1C). Length distribution analysis revealed that this size reduction reflected an accumulation of shorter mitochondria (0 to 1 μm) over time (fig. S1D). Notably, the proportion of very small mitochondria (0 to 0.6 μm, likely representing peripheral fission products) increased from ~18% at 0 hours to ~30% by 6 to 24 hours of carbon starvation (fig. S1E), consistent with sustained peripheral fission activity.

Carbon starvation activates damage-independent wholesale mitophagy

Given the concurrent increases in peripheral fission and mitophagy during carbon starvation, we asked whether the small, depolarized mitochondria generated by peripheral fission were the primary targets for autophagosomes and found an unexpected selectivity. Medium-sized mitochondria were progressively surrounded by ATG8e-labeled structures, forming typical ring-like phagophores that eventually enclosed the entire mitochondrion (Fig. 2A and movie S3). In contrast, small peripheral DMs generated from peripheral fission events (Fig. 2B, marked by white arrows) were not targeted by ATG8e-labeled autophagosomes and remained free of ATG8e association throughout the imaging period despite the presence of active autophagy machinery in the same cells (Fig. 2B and movie S4). Statistical analysis revealed that ATG8e predominantly colocalized with non–peripheral fission–derived mitochondria throughout the 24-hour time course, with only occasional recruitment to small peripheral DMs indicative of piecemeal mitophagy (23), which represented a minority of mitophagy cases under carbon starvation (Fig. 2C, fig. S2A, and movie S5). Length measurements revealed that mitochondria undergoing mitophagy averaged ~1.0 μm (fig. S2B), with nearly 80% falling within the 0.6- to 1.5-μm range, while both shorter mitochondria (0 to 0.6 μm, representing peripheral fission products) and longer mitochondria (1.5 to 3 μm) were largely excluded from autophagic degradation (fig. S2C). Quantitative TMRE staining revealed that ATG8e-positive mitochondria maintained membrane potential comparable to both nontargeted mitochondria under carbon starvation and mitochondria under normal conditions (Fig. 2, D and E). This selective targeting pattern demonstrates that carbon starvation does not trigger the clearance of small depolarized mitochondria as a damage control mechanism but instead activates a “wholesale” mitophagy program that removes mitochondria independently of their functional state, achieving rapid metabolic downsizing as a metabolic adaptation strategy.

Given that small, depolarized peripheral mitochondria are excluded from ATG8e-mediated autophagy, we asked whether they could enter the vacuole through alternative pathways. Quantitative analysis of mitochondria in the 0- to 0.6-μm size range confirmed that only 15% (41 of 277) associated with ATG8e during carbon starvation (fig. S2D), indicating that the vast majority of small peripheral fission products are excluded from ATG8e-mediated autophagy, with the minor fraction of ATG8e-positive events likely representing occasional piecemeal mitophagy (fig. S2A).

In wild-type (WT) plants treated with concanamycin A (ConcA) to inhibit vacuolar degradation (28), carbon starvation induced robust accumulation of mitochondria within vacuoles (marked by FM4-64–labeled tonoplast), while very few were detected under normal conditions (Fig. 2F). In autophagy-deficient atg5-1 mutants, although mitochondria were rarely observed in vacuoles under normal conditions, a small number could still be detected after carbon starvation (Fig. 2F), raising the possibility of an autophagy-independent delivery route, potentially mediated by microautophagy. Quantification showed that vacuolar mitochondria increased markedly from ~1.5 to ~6 per 100 μm2 in WT plants after carbon starvation, while atg5-1 mutants showed only a modest increase (Fig. 2G). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further confirmed the presence of SMs within vacuoles of atg5-1 mutants under carbon starvation (fig. S2E), providing direct ultrastructural evidence that mitochondria can enter the vacuole independently of ATG5-dependent macroautophagy.

Comprehensive quantitative analysis of mitochondrial populations revealed a notable reciprocal pattern between cytoplasmic and vacuolar compartments in atg5-1 mutants. While cytoplasmic and vacuolar mitochondrial lengths were indistinguishable between WT and atg5-1 at the baseline, carbon starvation led to divergent outcomes in the two genotypes. In the cytoplasm, atg5-1 mutants accumulated significantly larger mitochondria than WT (Fig. 2I), consistent with impaired wholesale mitophagy leading to retention of medium-to-large mitochondria. Conversely, mitochondria within atg5-1 vacuoles were significantly smaller and displayed lower TMRE intensity compared to those in WT vacuoles across all time points examined (Fig. 2J and fig. S2, F to H), with a size and membrane potential profile consistent with peripheral fission products. Together, these results reveal adaptive wholesale mitophagy that removes mitochondria independently of damage signals, while small peripheral fission products appear to access a minor autophagy-independent clearance pathway, although the precise mechanism and its quantitative contribution remain to be fully elucidated.

ML1 functions as a carbon starvation–inducible ER-mitochondria tether protein

Previous studies have implicated the ER in regulating mitochondrial fission and mitophagy through physical contact sites (11, 13, 29). We therefore examined whether ER-mitochondria contacts change during carbon starvation. In WT seedlings, mitochondria-ER colocalization was minimal under normal growth conditions. However, after carbon starvation, this colocalization increased markedly (Fig. 3, A and B). To identify candidate proteins involved in the formation of ER-mitochondria contact sites under carbon starvation, we searched for Arabidopsis proteins containing domains characteristic of ER-mitochondria tethers, specifically transmembrane (TM) domains and lipid-binding domains for interorganellar bridging. This analysis identified ML1 (AT1G73200), which was previously predicted as a potential membrane contact site tether based on domain architecture (30), but remained functionally uncharacterized. ML1 contains a TM domain, a pleckstrin homology (PH) domain, and a synaptotagmin-like mitochondrial-lipid-binding protein (SMP) domain (Fig. 3C), consistent with membrane tethering functions. To test whether ML1 localizes at ER-mitochondria contact sites, we coexpressed ML1-mScarlet with mitochondrial and ER markers in Nicotiana benthamiana epidermal cells. ML1 formed discrete puncta colocalizing with both Mito-GFP and mCherry-HDEL (Fig. 3D). Quantitative line scan analysis across the ER-mitochondria interface revealed a sharp peak of ML1 signal precisely at the contact site, while the ER signal remained relatively flat along the same line (Fig. 3E). To quantify this enrichment across multiple contact sites, we measured the relative fluorescence intensity at the contact site versus adjacent noncontacting regions of the same mitochondrion. The relative enrichment of ML1 was significantly higher than that of ER (Fig. 3F), demonstrating that ML1 is specifically concentrated at ER-mitochondria contact sites rather than reflecting a local accumulation of ER signal. Three-dimensional reconstruction of confocal Z-stacks in stable transgenic seedlings of the ml1 knockout mutant (fig. S3, A to C) expressing Mito-mScarlet, ML1-mBaojin (under native promoter), and mTagBFP2-HDEL confirmed that ML1 specifically localized at ER-mitochondria interfaces (Fig. 3G).

Next, we tested whether ML1 is required for the carbon starvation–induced increase in ER-mitochondria contacts by examining ER-mitochondria colocalization. Notably, the carbon starvation–induced increase in ER-mitochondria contacts observed in WT plants was completely abolished in ml1 mutants (Fig. 3, A and B). To directly test whether ML1 functions as an ER-mitochondria tether, we examined the effect of ML1 overexpression on ER-mitochondria contacts by transiently coexpressing Mito-GFP and mCherry-HDEL in N. benthamiana epidermal cells with or without ML1. Compared to control cells expressing only mitochondrial and ER markers, ML1 overexpression significantly increased ER-mitochondria contacts (Fig. 3, H and I). To investigate whether ML1 recruitment to mitochondria is regulated by carbon starvation, we monitored ML1-mitochondria colocalization in stable transgenic ml1 seedlings expressing Mito-mScarlet and ML1-mBaojin driven by the native ML1 promoter. Under normal growth conditions, ML1-mitochondria colocalization was relatively low. After carbon starvation, this colocalization increased markedly (Fig. 3, J and K). Furthermore, Western blot analysis showed that ML1 protein levels were modestly up-regulated (~1.2-fold) following carbon starvation treatment (fig. S3D).

To independently validate the formation of bona fide ER-mitochondria contact sites and the role of ML1 in this process at a nanoscale resolution, we performed three complementary experiments. First, TEM of WT and ml1 root cells revealed that carbon starvation reduced the average ER-mitochondria membrane distance in WT plants, whereas this reduction was abolished in ml1 mutants (fig. S3, E and F). Second, using a dimerization-dependent GFP (ddGFP) reporter system in which the two GFP fragments were targeted to the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) and ER membrane, respectively, we observed that the ddGFP signal, which specifically reports ER-mitochondria physical contacts, was markedly induced upon ML1 coexpression in N. benthamiana epidermal cells, with the newly formed ddGFP puncta colocalizing with ML1 (Fig. 3, L to N). Third, Förster resonance energy transfer-fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FRET-FLIM) analysis using yellow fluorescent protein (YFP)-TOM20-1 and Sec62-mScarlet1 as a donor and acceptor pair showed that ML1 coexpression significantly increased the FRET-positive ratio above the baseline, providing direct nanoscale evidence that ML1 promotes ER-mitochondria proximity (fig. S3, G to I). Together, these three independent nanoscale-validated approaches converge on the conclusion that ML1 functions as a bona fide ER-mitochondria tether that promotes the formation of physical contact sites between the two organelles.

ML1 mediates mitochondrial remodeling through peripheral fission under carbon starvation

We next asked whether ML1 participates in the shift toward peripheral fission by examining the spatial relationship between ML1 and mitochondria during fission events using time-lapse confocal microscopy. We quantitatively analyzed ML1 localization at mitochondrial fission sites and classified fission events into four categories according to fission mode (midzone or peripheral) and the presence or absence of ML1 (Fig. 4A). Under mock control, midzone fission was the predominant mode, occurring at similar frequencies with or without ML1 contact. Peripheral fission events were rare under these conditions (Fig. 4B). However, upon carbon starvation, the distribution shifted markedly. The frequency of peripheral fission increased substantially, with the majority of these events associated with ML1 contact (Fig. 4B). Among all observed fission events, peripheral fission with ML1 contact increased from 12% (9 of 78 events) under mock conditions to 30% (23 of 77 events) under carbon starvation (Fig. 4C). This 2.5-fold enrichment indicates that ML1 is specifically recruited to mediate peripheral fission in response to metabolic stress.

Next, we examined the spatial relationship between ER and mitochondria during fission events in both WT and ml1 mutant plants under carbon starvation (Fig. 4D). In WT plants, ER was frequently observed at peripheral fission sites (Fig. 4E). Notably, in ml1 mutants, ER-associated peripheral fission was significantly reduced (Fig. 4, E and F). Quantitative analysis revealed that among all fission events, ER-associated peripheral fission decreased from 49% in WT (115 of 234 events) to 38% in ml1 mutants (73 of 193 events) (Fig. 4F), while ER-associated midzone fission remained comparable between genotypes (Fig. 4F). These results demonstrate that ML1 is required for efficient ER recruitment to peripheral fission sites, suggesting a direct link between ER-mitochondria tethering and the peripheral fission process.

To directly visualize the role of ML1 in peripheral fission dynamics, we performed time-lapse imaging of mitochondria and ER during peripheral fission events under carbon starvation. In WT plants, peripheral fission proceeded rapidly, with the small DM cleanly separating from the mother mitochondrion (Fig. 4G and movie S6). In contrast, ml1 mutants exhibited markedly delayed peripheral fission (Fig. 4H and movie S7). Quantitative analysis confirmed that peripheral fission time was significantly prolonged in ml1 mutants compared to WT plants (Fig. 4I). These results demonstrate that ML1 is not only recruited to peripheral fission sites but also actively facilitates the fission process itself.

The reduced peripheral fission efficiency in ml1 mutants may result in altered mitochondrial morphology under carbon starvation. To test this, we examined mitochondrial length in WT and ml1 mutant plants. Under mock conditions, both genotypes showed similar mitochondrial lengths (Fig. 4, J and K). However, under carbon starvation, WT plants showed reduced mitochondrial length, whereas ml1 mutants failed to reduce mitochondrial length and maintained significantly longer mitochondria (Fig. 4, J and K). This phenotype was confirmed by TEM analysis (Fig. 4, L and M). In WT plants, carbon starvation led to a reduction in average mitochondrial length. In contrast, ml1 mutants failed to reduce mitochondrial length under carbon starvation (Fig. 4M). These results demonstrate that ML1-mediated peripheral fission is essential for the reduction in mitochondrial length under carbon starvation, and loss of ML1 impairs mitochondrial morphological remodeling during metabolic stress.

ML1 recruits ATG18a to promote wholesale mitophagy

To identify proteins interacting with ML1 under carbon starvation, we performed proximity labeling using ml1 mutant plants expressing ML1-TurboID driven by the native ML1 promoter (fig. S4A). Mass spectrometry analysis identified autophagy-related protein 18a (ATG18a), a core autophagy protein required for autophagosome formation (28, 31), as significantly enriched (Fig. 5A and table S1). We validated the ML1-ATG18a interaction by coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and luciferase complementation imaging assays (fig. S4, B and C). Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays showed that ML1 and ATG18a generated strong interaction signals, forming distinct puncta at ER-mitochondria contact sites, while control combinations showed no signals (Fig. 5, B and C, arrows). These results demonstrate that ML1 physically interacts with ATG18a at ER-mitochondria contact sites.

We next examined whether ML1 is required for ATG18a recruitment to mitochondria during wholesale mitophagy under carbon starvation. In WT plants, mCherry-ATG18a formed ring-like structures surrounding Mito-GFP, characteristic of autophagosome formation (Fig. 5D). However, in ml1 mutants, ATG18a-mitochondria association was markedly reduced (Fig. 5D). Quantification revealed that the percentage of mitochondria associated with ATG18a decreased significantly from ~50% in WT to ~20% in ml1 mutants (Fig. 5E), indicating that ML1 is essential for efficient ATG18a recruitment to mitochondria. Time-lapse imaging showed that in WT plants, mCherry-ATG18a progressively assembled around mitochondria, forming complete ring-like structures that eventually enclosed the entire organelle, characteristic of wholesale mitophagy of medium-sized mitochondria (Fig. 5F and movie S8). In contrast, in ml1 mutants, although mCherry-ATG18a formed ring-like structures, these rings frequently formed away from mitochondria rather than surrounding them (Fig. 5G and movie S9). These results demonstrate that ML1 serves as a critical platform for recruiting ATG18a to mitochondria.

To further investigate whether mitophagy is compromised in ml1 mutants, we monitored mitochondrial delivery to vacuoles by confocal Z-stack imaging combined with FM4-64 staining of the tonoplast, followed by three-dimensional reconstruction. In WT plants, carbon starvation led to robust accumulation of mitochondria within the vacuolar lumen when vacuolar degradation was inhibited by ConcA treatment, with intravacuolar mitochondria clearly distinguishable from cytoplasmic mitochondria and distributed throughout the vacuolar interior (Fig. 5H). In contrast, ml1 mutants showed substantially fewer intravacuolar mitochondria under the same conditions (Fig. 5H). Quantification confirmed that mitochondrial accumulation in vacuoles was markedly reduced in ml1 mutants compared to WT (Fig. 5J). To further assess mitophagy efficiency, we monitored the degradation of mitochondrial proteins during carbon starvation. In WT plants, the levels of mitochondrial outer membrane protein adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) and inner membrane protein CYC1 (cytochrome c1, heme protein, mitochondrial) progressively decreased during carbon starvation, indicating active mitophagy (Fig. 5I). In contrast, ml1 mutants showed impaired degradation of both mitochondrial proteins, with sustained protein levels throughout the time course (Fig. 5I). Together, these results demonstrate that ML1 is required for efficient wholesale mitophagy by recruiting ATG18a to ER-mitochondria contact sites.

ML1 is required for mitochondrial health under carbon starvation

We next examined the physiological consequences of impaired peripheral fission and mitophagy in ml1 mutants by checking mitochondrial membrane potential under carbon starvation. Staining with TMRE showed that after carbon starvation treatment, WT plants exhibited moderate reduction in membrane potential, whereas ml1 mutants showed significantly reduced TMRE intensity, indicating more severe membrane depolarization (Fig. 6, A and B). These results demonstrate that ML1 is essential for maintaining mitochondrial health during carbon starvation.

To investigate the physiological importance of ML1, we subjected WT, atg5 (an autophagy-deficient mutant), ml1, and complemented lines (pML1::ML1-mBaojin in the ml1 background, expressing ML1 fused with mBaojin at the C terminus under the control of the native ML1 promoter) to carbon starvation and examined their phenotypes. Under mock conditions, all genotypes showed normal growth and green coloration (Fig. 6C). However, under carbon starvation, both atg5 and ml1 mutants displayed severe chlorosis, while WT and complemented lines remained relatively healthy (Fig. 6C). Quantification of chlorophyll content showed that WT plants maintained high levels under carbon starvation, whereas atg5 and ml1 mutants exhibited markedly reduced levels (Fig. 6D). The complemented lines showed intermediate levels, confirming that the phenotype resulted from the loss of ML1 function. Similarly, ml1 mutants also exhibited significantly reduced root elongation under carbon starvation compared to WT and complemented lines (fig. S5, A and B). To test whether the ML1 function is specific to carbon starvation, we examined plant responses under nitrogen starvation. Unlike carbon starvation, ml1 mutants showed no enhanced sensitivity to nitrogen deficiency, displaying chlorosis, growth inhibition, and survival rates comparable to WT (fig. S5, C to F). These results demonstrate that ML1 is specifically required for plant response to carbon starvation.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we uncovered a coordinated mitochondrial quality control program in which plants adapt to carbon starvation through peripheral fission–mediated damage segregation and wholesale mitophagy of medium-sized mitochondria. Under normal conditions, plant mitochondria primarily undergo midzone fission to maintain population homeostasis through symmetric division. Upon carbon starvation, ML1 is recruited to ER-mitochondria contact sites where it promotes peripheral fission to segregate damaged regions and recruits ATG18a to facilitate selective mitophagy, together reducing both the mitochondrial size and number to minimize metabolic burden (Fig. 6E). These findings reveal that ER-mitochondria contact sites integrate mitochondrial fission and mitophagy, representing an adaptive quality control strategy distinct from conventional damage-induced mitophagy.

The wholesale mitophagy program characterized here operates through a damage-independent mechanism that targets mitochondria regardless of their functional state. This damage-independent strategy provides a critical advantage during early carbon starvation by enabling rapid mitochondrial population reduction. Rather than screening individual organelles for damage signals, cells remove mitochondria in bulk to achieve immediate metabolic downsizing. Each eliminated mitochondrion reduces ATP consumption for maintenance, protein import, and respiration, thereby conserving limited carbon reserves regardless of its functional state. Meanwhile, wholesale mitophagy may serve a dual metabolic function. Beyond this energy-saving effect, degradation of mitochondrial components generates respiratory substrates that can sustain limited energy production. Mitochondrial proteins are catabolized to amino acids that can feed into the tricarboxylic acid cycle or gluconeogenesis, while lipids are broken down through β-oxidation to produce acetyl-coenzyme A (32, 33). This dual function, simultaneously minimizing energy consumption and mobilizing internal resources, positions wholesale mitophagy as an integrated metabolic adaptation rather than simple organelle disposal (34).

In contrast, small depolarized fragments generated by peripheral fission are largely excluded from this wholesale program. Despite being damaged, these fragments impose minimal metabolic burden because of their small size and compromised activity. Their clearance can thus be deferred without immediate metabolic penalty. Our observation of residual mitochondrial delivery in atg5 mutants suggests alternative, potential microautophagy-mediated clearance of these fragments that may operate on different timescales (35, 36). Under transient carbon limitation, such as overnight periods, these fragments would be addressed through recovery-associated mitochondrial biogenesis once carbon supply resumes. The wholesale mitophagy program characterized here appears to represent an early adaptive response (peaking at 6 to 12 hours) that rapidly reduces mitochondrial burden. As carbon starvation extends beyond the initial phase, damaged mitochondria progressively accumulate in cells. When this accumulation reaches a certain threshold, it may trigger programmed cell death and the transition to senescence. Studies monitoring mitophagy over extended dark periods (6 to 12 days) have observed that mitophagy levels increase as senescence progresses (9). This late-phase mitophagy may be mediated by damage-recognition receptors such as FMT and TraB (10, 11), which target depolarized or dysfunctional mitochondria, representing a mechanistically distinct process from the early ML1-mediated wholesale program that selectively removes functional organelles.

A central finding of our study is that ML1 coordinates two seemingly distinct processes, peripheral fission and wholesale mitophagy, at ER-mitochondria contact sites. This dual functionality raises important questions about how a single tether protein can orchestrate multiple mitochondrial remodeling events. The interaction between ML1 and ATG18a reveals how ER-mitochondria contact sites coordinate mitophagy. ATG18a, a phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate–binding protein essential for autophagosome formation, is recruited to mitochondria, specifically at ML1-positive ER contact sites. This spatial organization concentrates both the lipid membrane source (ER) and autophagy machinery (ATG18a) in close proximity to cargo mitochondria, potentially accelerating autophagosome nucleation. However, the damage-independent nature of this wholesale program raises fundamental questions about cargo selection. Given that both targeted and nontargeted mitochondria display similar membrane potential, the selection criteria remain unclear. We speculate that ER-mitochondria contact stability, mitochondrial size compatibility with autophagosome formation, metabolic cues at contact sites, calcium signaling, or specific protein modifications at ER-mitochondria contact sites may serve as recognition signals, allowing cells to preferentially remove metabolically competent mitochondria for rapid adaptation to energy limitation.

Our observations reveal that most mitochondrial fission events, both midzone and peripheral, occur at sites marked by ER contact. Peripheral fission efficiency is severely compromised in ml1 mutants, while midzone fission remains largely unaffected, demonstrating that ML1-mediated ER tethering is specifically required for peripheral but not midzone division. The asymmetric division at mitochondrial tips presents unique challenges compared to midzone fission because the termini are dynamic and mobile, making stable contact difficult to establish, and the extreme asymmetry requires highly localized membrane remodeling. ML1-stabilized ER contacts may help address these challenges by providing a physical scaffold that anchors the mitochondrial tip long enough for the fission machinery to complete scission, locally enriching membrane-remodeling lipids (such as phosphatidic acid or diacylglycerol) that facilitate the extreme membrane curvature required for asymmetric division.

While ML1 shares structural features with ER-mitochondria tethers in yeast [MMM1 (maintenance of mitochondrial morphology protein 1)] and mammals [PDZD8 (PDZ domain containing 8)] (29, 37), its dual role in coordinating fission and mitophagy appears distinct. A recent study demonstrated that PDZD8 mediates ER-mitochondria contact site formation through phase separation via its intrinsically disordered region (38). This raises the possibility that carbon starvation–induced metabolic changes may lower the threshold for phase separation at contact sites, enabling a modest increase in ML1 protein levels to drive a disproportionately large expansion of ER-mitochondria contacts. Such a threshold-based amplification mechanism would allow cells to rapidly scale up contact site formation in response to metabolic stress without requiring large changes in gene expression. As sessile organisms, plants must endure prolonged nutrient limitation in situ, requiring sophisticated mitochondrial remodeling strategies. During extended carbon starvation, maintaining a large mitochondrial pool would be counterproductive, as their ongoing respiration and maintenance would deplete limited energy reserves. The coordinated reduction in mitochondrial size and number allows plants to downscale their metabolic machinery while preserving essential mitochondrial function. This adaptive strategy may extend to other stress conditions where mitochondrial dynamics change, such as senescence and pathogen infection, suggesting that ML1-mediated contact sites serve as a general platform for metabolic adaptation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

All Arabidopsis thaliana lines used in this study were in the Columbia-0 (Col-0) ecotype background. The atg5-1 mutant (SALK_020601) and ml1 mutant (SAIL-533-G09) were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center. Stable transgenic lines expressing fluorescent markers and fusion proteins were generated through Agrobacterium-mediated floral dip transformation. For visualizing subcellular compartments, mitochondrial markers (Mito-GFP and Mito-mScarlet), the autophagosome marker (mCherry-ATG8e), ER markers (mCherry-HDEL and mTagBFP2-HDEL), and the ATG18a marker (mCherry-ATG18a) were expressed under the control of the 35S promoter. For ML1 localization and functional studies, pML1::ML1-mBaojin and pML1::ML1-TurboID were generated expressing ML1 fused with mBaojin fluorescent tag or TurboID driven by the native ML1 promoter in the ml1 mutant background. Primers used for cloning are listed in table S2.

Arabidopsis seeds were surface sterilized with 75% (v/v) ethanol for 5 min, followed by 10% (v/v) bleach containing 0.01% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 10 min, and then washed five times with sterile distilled water. Sterilized seeds were stratified at 4°C in the dark for 3 days to synchronize germination. For normal growth conditions, seeds were sown on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) medium (Coolaber, cat. no. PM1010-50L) containing 1% (w/v) sucrose and 0.8% (w/v) agar (pH 5.7). Plates were placed vertically in growth chambers at 22°C under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod. Seedlings were grown for 5 days before being subjected to stress treatments. N. benthamiana plants were grown in soil under controlled conditions at 24°C with a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod for 4 to 5 weeks before being used for transient expression experiments.

Stress treatment

For carbon starvation treatment, 5-day-old seedlings grown on 1/2 MS medium with 1% sucrose were transferred to 1/2 MS liquid medium without sucrose and incubated in complete darkness for the indicated time periods (0, 6, 12, 18, or 24 hours). For nitrogen starvation treatment, seedlings were transferred to nitrogen-free 1/2 MS liquid medium (Coolaber, cat. no. PM1060-N-250g) and grown under normal light conditions for the indicated time periods. For ConcA (Glpbio, GC17519) treatment to inhibit vacuolar degradation, seedlings were treated with 0.5 μM ConcA for 6 hours during carbon starvation. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates, and representative results are shown.

Molecular cloning

For generating fluorescent protein fusions, the coding sequences of target genes were amplified from Arabidopsis Col-0 cDNA using gene-specific primers and cloned into appropriate vectors using NovoRec plus One step PCR Cloning Kit 2.0 (Novoprotein). For ML1 (AT1G73200) constructs, the native promoter region (1721 base pairs upstream of the start codon) was amplified from genomic DNA and cloned upstream of the ML1 coding sequence, which was then fused with fluorescent tags including mBaojin, mScarlet, or TurboID at the C terminus. Several fluorescent marker constructs used in this study were obtained from previous publications, including Mito-GFP (39), mCherry-HDEL (28), mCherry-ATG8e (28), and mCherry-ATG18a (28, 40). For Mito-mScarlet, the mitochondrial targeting sequence was amplified from the Mito-GFP construct and fused to the N terminus of mScarlet. For mTagBFP2-HDEL, mTagBFP2 was amplified from an mTagBFP2-ATG8a construct (41), and the signal peptide and HDEL retention signal were amplified from the mCherry-HDEL construct and used to flank mTagBFP2 at the N terminus and C terminus, respectively. For the ddGFP reporter system, ddGFPA was fused to the N terminus of TOM20-1 (ddGFPA-TOM20-1), and ddGFPB was fused to the N terminus of Sec62 (ddGFPB-Sec62). The control constructs included free ddGFPA (lacking the TOM20-1 anchor) and free ddGFPB (lacking the Sec62 anchor). All ddGFP constructs were driven by the 35S promoter. For FRET-FLIM analysis, YFP was fused to the N terminus of TOM20-1 (YFP-TOM20-1) as the donor, and mScarlet1 was fused to the N terminus of Sec62 (mScarlet1-Sec62) as the acceptor. Free mScarlet1 (lacking the Sec62 anchor) was used as a negative control. All constructs were driven by the 35S promoter.

Live-cell imaging

Live-cell imaging was performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 900 or Leica Stellaris 5) equipped with appropriate laser lines and detection settings for multichannel fluorescence imaging. Excitation was performed at 488 nm (GFP), 561 nm (mScarlet and mCherry), or 405 nm (mTagBFP2), and emission was collected at appropriate wavelength ranges. For multichannel imaging, sequential scanning was used to minimize spectral bleed-through between channels. Images were acquired using a 63× oil immersion objective. Z-Stack images were collected with 0.3- to 0.5-μm intervals to capture the three-dimensional organization of subcellular structures. For multichannel imaging in N. benthamiana epidermal cells coexpressing Mito-GFP, mCherry-HDEL, and ML1-mScarlet, a 488-nm excitation laser with emission filters of 494 to 546 nm was used for GFP, a 561-nm excitation laser with emission filters of 570 to 590 nm and TauGating (3 to 8 ns) for mScarlet, and a 561-nm excitation laser with emission filters of 595 to 640 nm and TauGating (0.3 to 1.4 ns) for mCherry, with the TauGating technique exploiting differences in fluorescence lifetime to effectively separate mScarlet and mCherry signals despite their similar spectral properties.

FRET-FLIM imaging was performed on a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope equipped with a FALCON FLIM module. YFP (donor) was excited at 488 nm, and emission was collected at 500 to 550 nm. mScarlet1 (acceptor) was excited at 561 nm. Fluorescence lifetime data were acquired and analyzed using LAS X software, and FRET efficiency maps were generated on the basis of the donor lifetime changes.

For time-lapse imaging of mitochondrial fission and mitophagy events, images were acquired every 0.484 s, with each time-lapse video consisting of 500 consecutive frames to capture the complete fission process. For fission mode quantification (Fig. 1H), 8 to 10 independent seedlings were imaged per time point (0, 6, 12, and 24 hours of carbon starvation), with one time-lapse video acquired per seedling. Mitochondrial fission events were manually identified from time-lapse movies and classified into two categories on the basis of the relative sizes and positions of DMs. Midzone fission was defined as division events occurring at approximately the middle of the mitochondrion, generating two DMs of similar size. Peripheral fission was defined as asymmetric division events occurring at terminal positions, producing one SM (≤25% of the mother mitochondrion length) and one large mitochondrion. The fission site position was determined by measuring the distance from the fission site to the nearest mitochondrial terminus relative to the total mitochondrial length. Fission duration was measured as the time from initial constriction to complete separation of DMs. The length asymmetry index was calculated as (L1 − L2)/(L1 + L2), where L1 and L2 represent the lengths of the two DMs, with values approaching 0 indicating symmetric division and values approaching 1 indicating highly asymmetric division.

To monitor mitophagy, seedlings coexpressing mitochondrial markers (Mito-GFP) and autophagosome markers (mCherry-ATG8e or mCherry-ATG18a) were subjected to carbon starvation treatment and imaged at various time points. For mitophagy quantification in Fig. 1H, ~25 independent seedlings were analyzed per time point (0, 6, 12, and 24 hours of carbon starvation). Mitophagy events were identified by the formation of ring-like or cup-shaped ATG8e or ATG18a structures surrounding mitochondria. Mitophagy levels were quantified as the percentage of ATG8e-positive autophagosomes containing mitochondria relative to the total number of ATG8e-positive structures. For each mitophagy event, the size of the targeted mitochondrion was measured. For mitochondrial membrane potential assessment, seedlings were incubated in a 1× TMRE working solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, C2001S) for 10 min, and then roots were briefly washed with 1/2 MS liquid medium to remove excess dye. TMRE was excited at 550 nm with emission collected at 565 to 620 nm. For mitochondrial superoxide detection, seedlings were incubated in 1/2 MS liquid medium containing 5 μM MitoSOX Red (Invitrogen, M36009) for 20 min, washed three times with fresh medium, then immediately imaged with excitation at 405 nm and emission at 610 nm. For vacuolar membrane labeling, seedlings were incubated in 1/2 MS liquid medium containing 0.08% (v/v) FM4-64 (Coolaber, CD4673) for 3 hours to allow dye internalization via endocytosis and tonoplast labeling. After brief washing, FM4-64 was excited at 543 nm with emission collected at 580 to 650 nm. All experiments were independently repeated three times with similar results, and representative data are shown.

Image processing and analysis

All images were processed and analyzed using ImageJ/Fiji software. For quantitative analysis, the mitochondrial length was measured manually using the segmented line tool in ImageJ. Fluorescence intensity was measured by drawing regions of interest around individual mitochondria or organelles and calculating the mean gray value after background subtraction. For colocalization quantification of ER-mitochondria contacts, merged confocal images were converted to RGB (red, green, and blue) format using the “Stack to RGB” function in ImageJ. Colocalization analysis was performed using the “Color Threshold” function, where the hue parameter was adjusted to specifically select yellow pixels representing colocalized regions of green (mitochondria) and red (ER) signals, with saturation set to the full range and brightness adjusted to ensure that the selected regions of interest accurately represented colocalized areas. The colocalized area was then measured using the “Measure” function. The percentage of colocalization was calculated as the colocalized area divided by the total area of the respective organelle. Similar threshold-based approaches have been used in a previous study of organelle contact sites (42). Line scan analysis was performed using ImageJ. A straight line was drawn perpendicularly across the ER-mitochondria interface in the merged confocal image. The fluorescence intensities of ML1 and ER along the line were extracted using the “Plot Profile” function and normalized to the starting intensity to obtain relative gray values. To quantify the relative enrichment of ER and ML1 at mitochondria-ER contact sites, fluorescence intensity was measured using Fiji. Regions of interest were defined at mitochondria-ER contact sites (“Contact”) and at adjacent noncontacting regions of the same mitochondrion (“No Contact”). The relative gray value was calculated as the ratio of the mean fluorescence intensity at the contact site to the mean intensity of the noncontacting region for each signal (ML1 or ER) independently. ddGFP puncta were manually counted from confocal images. ddGFP punctum density was calculated as the number of puncta per unit area (μm2) for each cell. For quantification of FRET signals at ER-mitochondria contact sites, images were analyzed using LAS X 4.7.0 software. For each individual cell, the number of individual mitochondria was determined on the basis of the YFP-TOM20-1 fluorescence signal. High-intensity FRET puncta were defined as those with a FRET efficiency of ≥50%, as calibrated by the pseudocolor scale bar. The ratio of high-intensity FRET spots (≥50%) to the total number of mitochondria per cell was calculated as the FRET-positive ratio. Three-dimensional rendering and visualization of organellar structures from Z-stack images were performed using Imaris software (Bitplane). Mitochondria (Mito-GFP) were identified using the “Spots” module, while vacuoles (FM4-64) were reconstructed using the “Surfaces” module. To distinguish the subcellular localization of mitochondria, spots with “shortest distance to surfaces (FM4-64) > 0” were identified as being sequestered within the vacuolar lumen. These mitochondria inside the vacuole were then manually categorized and color coded as purple using the “Edit Labels” function. Conversely, mitochondria located outside the vacuole (distance values ≤0) were labeled in green. For selected images requiring enhanced resolution (Figs. 1, B and C; 3F; 4A; and 5, D, F, and G), sparse deconvolution was performed to improve the image quality and clarity of subcellular structures (43).

TurboID-based proximity labeling and mass spectrometry analysis

Seven-day-old Col-0 and pML1-ML1-TurboID in the ml1-3 mutant background were transferred to liquid 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 50 μM biotin and incubated for 5 hours. After treatment, seedlings were washed three times with ice-cold double-distilled water to remove surface biotin and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total proteins were extracted in extraction buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 1 mM EDTA, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail]. To minimize nonspecific binding, free biotin was removed from the protein lysates using Sephadex G25 desalting columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 89893). The desalted protein solution was immediately supplemented with tris-HCl (pH 7.5), NaCl, PMSF, and protease inhibitor cocktail to restore the concentrations to those of the original Protein Extraction Buffer. Streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 65001) were preequilibrated by washing twice with Protein Extraction Buffer. The desalted protein samples were then incubated with the beads at 4°C for 12 hours with gentle rotation. Following incubation, the magnetic beads were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline. The samples were digested with trypsin overnight at 37°C with gentle shaking. The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 ZipTips according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For proximity labeling analysis, label-free quantification data were processed using the DEP package in R. Candidate proximal proteins were identified with enrichment cutoffs of fold change > 2 and P < 0.01.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For Co-IP experiments, N. benthamiana leaves expressing ML1-myc and YFP-ATG18a were harvested and ground in liquid nitrogen. Approximately 1 to 2 g of tissue was resuspended in 3 to 5 ml of Co-IP buffer containing 50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 0.5% (v/v) NP-40, 1 mM PMSF, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysate was incubated on ice for 30 min with occasional mixing and then cleared by centrifugation at 12,000g for 15 min at 4°C. A small aliquot of the supernatant was saved as input control. The remaining supernatant was incubated with anti-myc magnetic beads (AlpaLifeBio, cat. no. KTSM1336) or anti-GFP magnetic beads (AlpaLifeBio, cat. no. KTSM1334) for 2 to 4 hours at 4°C with gentle rotation. After incubation, beads were washed five times with Co-IP buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2× SDS loading buffer for 5 min. Input samples and immunoprecipitates were analyzed by Western blotting using anti-myc and anti-YFP antibodies to detect ML1-myc and YFP-ATG18a, respectively.

Western blotting

For Western blot analysis, equal amounts of total protein were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis on 10% polyacrylamide gels prepared using the One-Step PAGE Gel Rapid Preparation Kit (SparkJade, EC1023-B) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore) using a wet transfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat milk in tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature with gentle shaking. After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in TBST containing 5% bovine serum albumin (Solarbio) or silk milk overnight at 4°C. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-ATPase (1:10,000, generated as previously described in B. Tan’s lab) (44), anti-CYC1 (1:10,000, Phytoab, PHY0566A), anti-GFP (1:5000, Abclonal, AE012), anti-myc (1:5000, TransGen, HT101), anti-mCherry (1:5000, Proteintech, 26765-1-AP), and anti-Actin (1:5000, Abclonal, AC009). After primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed three times with TBST for 15 min each and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies goat anti-mouse (1:10,000, ELK Biotechnology, SA002) or goat anti-rabbit (1:10,000, AS09 602, Agrisera) for 1 hour at room temperature. Following three additional washes with TBST, protein bands were visualized using the M5 Hiper ECL Western HRP Substrate (Mei5Bio, MF074-05) and detected with a chemiluminescence imaging system (ChampChemi).

Luciferase complementation imaging

For luciferase complementation imaging assays, ML1 and ATG18a coding sequences were cloned into split-luciferase vectors JW771 and JW772 to generate nLUC-ML1 and ATG18a-cLUC fusion constructs. These constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 and co-infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. As negative controls, nLUC-ML1 or ATG18a-cLUC was co-infiltrated with the empty complementary vector. After 2 to 3 days of incubation, to visualize luciferase activity, leaves were incubated with 1 mM dual-luciferin–free acid (GoldBio, LUCK-100) dissolved in 0.01% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 5 min in the dark. Luminescence images were captured using a charge-coupled device camera (Tanon-5200).

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC)

For BiFC assays, ML1 and ATG18a coding sequences were cloned into pB5GW BiFC vectors to generate ML1-nYFP and cYFP-ATG18a fusion constructs. As negative controls, ML1-nYFP or cYFP-ATG18a was co-infiltrated with the empty complementary vector. After 2 days of incubation, infiltrated leaf areas were examined by confocal microscopy. YFP fluorescence resulting from the complementation of N-terminal YFP (nYFP) and C-terminal YFP (cYFP) fragments was excited at 514 nm with emission collected at 525 to 575 nm. The subcellular localization of the BiFC signals was determined by coexpression with ER markers (mCherry-HDEL). For quantification, the number of BiFC puncta per cell was manually counted from multiple cells.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For ultrastructural analysis of mitochondria, 5-day-old Arabidopsis seedlings were subjected to carbon starvation treatment by transferring to 1/2 MS medium without sucrose and incubating in complete darkness for 48 hours. Leaf tissues were excised and immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) overnight at 4°C. After fixation, samples were washed three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 15 min each, followed by postfixation with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1 to 2 hours. Samples were then washed again three times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer for 15 min each. Dehydration was carried out through a graded ethanol series (30, 50, 70, 80, 90, and 95%) for 15 min at each concentration, followed by treatment with 100% ethanol for 20 min and final dehydration with pure acetone for 20 min. For infiltration, samples were sequentially treated with embedding resin mixtures at ratios of 1:1 for 1 hour and 3:1 for 3 hours, followed by pure embedding resin overnight. Samples were then embedded in fresh resin and polymerized at 70°C overnight. Ultrathin sections (70 to 90 nm) were cut using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate for 5 min each, dried, and examined with a JEOL JEM-1200EX transmission electron microscope operating at 80 kV. Mitochondrial length was measured using ImageJ software from TEM images. To enhance resolution and clarity, TEM images were processed using the EMCellFiner algorithm (45). For quantification of ER-mitochondria distances from TEM images, mitochondria with adjacent ER membranes within visible range were identified, and the minimum distance between the mitochondrial outer membrane and the closest ER membrane was measured for each mitochondrion using ImageJ. Mitochondria without any adjacent ER in the visible field were excluded from the quantification.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates, and data are presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM), as indicated in figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed Student’s t tests were performed (Figs. 1D; 2G; 3, F, G, and I; 4I; and 5E and fig. S3C). For comparisons among multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by post hoc tests was used. Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons in Figs. 1G, 2 (C, E, and I), 3B, 4 (K and M), 5 (C and J), and 6D and figs. S1 (A and C), S2G, and S5 (B, D, and F). The Waller-Duncan test was used for multiple comparisons in Figs. 2J and 6B and fig. S3 (F and I). For nonparametric multiple comparisons, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction was used (Fig. 3N). Statistical significance was defined as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ns (not significant) indicates P ≥ 0.05. The exact sample sizes (n) and P values are provided in the respective figure legends.
