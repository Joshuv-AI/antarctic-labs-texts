Type III Sugaya Classification after Rotator Cuff Repair: Preoperative Risk Factors and Progression to Retear
Authors: Soo-Young Jeong, Ki Won Young, Tae Kang Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroudThe Sugaya classification is a widely used classification system to assess rotator cuff tendon integrity after repair. However, Sugaya type III has received less attention in the literature compared to types I/II or IV/V. Existing data on preoperative risk factors and long-term outcomes are limited, and for Sugaya type III, there remains inconsistency regarding whether it should be classified as a healed or retorn tendon. Repair integrity was examined by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) according to the Sugaya classification at 6 months postoperatively and ultrasound (US) imaging at the final follow-up. Based on MRIs at 6 months postoperatively, there were 76 (61%) Sugaya type I/II, 33 (27%) type III, and 15 (12%) type IV/V. The Sugaya classification based on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is widely used to analyze the integrity of the rotator cuff tendon after surgery. This system classifies repaired rotator cuff tendons into 5 categories based on tendon signal intensity, thickness, and remaining defects, rather than simply as retorn and healed.1) It is accepted as a reliable and reproducible method by several studies evaluating inter- and intraobserver reliability tests.2) The postoperative rotator cuff integrity based on this classification was found to be correlated with clinical outcomes after rotator cuff repair (RCR), such as pain, functional scores, and abduction muscle strength.2,3)

Among the 5 categories of the Sugaya classification, type III has received less attention in the literature compared to types I/II or IV/V. In general, types I and II are considered healed, while types IV and V are considered retorn. However, there has been a disagreement as to whether Sugaya type III should be considered a retorn or healed tendon.3,4,5,6,7) This discrepancy might be attributed to the fact that there are few existing data in the literature on preoperative risk factors, clinical features, and the long-term structural outcomes of Sugaya type III. As for the type IV and V tendons (complete retear), multiple risk factors have been studied, such as age, diabetes, osteoporosis, preoperative tear size, and muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration of the rotator cuff.8,9,10,11,12) Similarly, there would be preoperative risk factors affecting the development of Sugaya type III after repair, compared with types I and II. Furthermore, given its definition as a thin supraspinatus tendon with intact continuity, this less optimal tendon status than types I and II might be at risk of developing a later complete retear with deteriorating clinical outcomes, as shown in types IV and V.13,14)

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate preoperative risk factors and longitudinal outcomes of Sugaya type III after RCR by serial imaging studies. We hypothesized that Sugaya type III would be associated with preoperative tear severity and carry a higher risk of progressing to a complete retear compared to Sugaya type I/II.

METHODS

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nowon Eulji Medical Center (IRB No. 2021-04-021). As it used fully anonymized data without identifiable information, the requirement for informed consent for publication was waived in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Patient Selection

This retrospective study reviewed medical records and radiological images of 124 patients who underwent arthroscopic RCRs performed by a single surgeon (TKL) between April 2016 and February 2020 in Nowon Eulji Medical Center. The inclusion criteria of this study were (1) patients who underwent symptomatic supraspinatus tendon tears with or without infraspinatus and subscapularis tendons; (2) availability of MRI at 6 months postoperatively; and (3) ultrasound (US) and clinical assessment at the final follow-up. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) revision RCR; (2) isolated partial-thickness tears; (3) cases with incomplete radiologic follow-up; and (4) patients lost to follow-up before 12 months. During the study period, total 321 patients underwent arthroscopic RCRs for symptomatic rotator cuff tears involving the supraspinatus tendon. Of these, 203 patients underwent postoperative MRI at 6 months, and 124 patients who additionally completed US and clinical evaluation at the final follow-up constituted the study participants (Fig. 1). There were 42 male and 82 female patients with a mean age of 63 years (range, 26–82 years). The mean duration of follow-up MRI evaluations was 6.2 months (range, 4.2–12.3 months). The mean duration of final US evaluations and the clinical follow-up period was 25.2 months (range, 12–73 months).

The flowchart of patient selection. MRI: magnetic resonance imaging, US: ultrasound.

Surgical Procedures

All surgeries were performed with the patient in the lateral decubitus position under general anesthesia by a single surgeon (TKL) using the standard technique of arthroscopic RCR using suture anchors. The repair technique was either the single-row technique or the suture-bridge technique, depending on tear size, tear configuration, and tendon quality. When torn tendons were often retracted to the glenoid in large to massive tears and were not easily approximated up to the articular margin of the greater tuberosity, anterior and/or posterior release and mobilization of retracted tendons according to the standard technique (anterior interval slide-in-continuity and/or posterior interval slide) were performed and then repaired to the tuberosity using suture anchors. If torn tendons were not reduced to the articular margin of the greater tuberosity footprint at the completion of all releases both anteriorly and posteriorly, they were defined as irreparable tears. For irreparable tears, partial repair, the latissimus dorsi tendon transfers, or superior capsule reconstruction were performed, and they were excluded. The postoperative rehabilitation protocol was identical in all patients. An abduction brace was applied for 4 weeks postoperatively with elbow and finger exercises. A stretching exercise of the shoulder joint was started after the removal of the brace, and strengthening exercises were initiated at 3 months postoperatively.

Clinical and Radiological Evaluations

Based on medical chart reviews, demographic data were collected, including age, sex, trauma history, and comorbidities. Trauma history was defined as a preoperative event that could independently cause significant injury to the shoulder joint, such as a slip down, fall down, sports injury, or a bicycle or motor vehicle accident. The clinical outcomes at the preoperative, 6-month postoperative, and final follow-up visit were assessed with the visual analog scale for pain, the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, the University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) score, the short-form 36-item health survey (SF-36), and range of motion.

The radiographic assessment included preoperative standard radiographs and MRIs of the shoulder joint. Based on preoperative MRIs, tears were measured in mediolateral and anteroposterior dimension and then classified as partial-thickness tears, small (< 1 cm), medium (1–3 cm), large (3–5 cm), and massive (> 5 cm) full-thickness tears. Muscle atrophy of the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis was evaluated using the scale proposed by Warner et al.15) and fatty infiltration was evaluated using the Goutallier fatty infiltration staging system adapted by Fuchs et al.16) At 6 months postoperatively, MRI examinations were performed in all patients. Based on these images, the structural integrity of the repaired supraspinatus tendon was classified according to the Sugaya classification.1) At the final follow-up visits, the US examinations were performed by the treating surgeon (TKL). Based on the US images, the final structural outcomes of repairs were rated as a retorn or healed tendon. All radiographic images were reviewed by an experienced musculoskeletal radiologist (YSC; more than 10 years of experience) without knowledge of the present study.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.0 (IBM Corp.). The differences between preoperative and postoperative clinical outcomes were compared using the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Preoperative demographic data, clinical scores, and US results were compared between the Sugaya type I/II and type III groups. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test, or the Fisher exact test when minimal expected counts were present. For continuous variables, normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests; Student t-test was applied when normality was satisfied, and the Mann-Whitney U-test was used otherwise. The same statistical procedures were employed for comparisons between the healed and retorn tendon groups. Finally, multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to determine the independent preoperative variables affecting the development of Sugaya type III, compared with type I/II. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Structural Results

The patients’ demographic data, preoperative MRI findings, and clinical scores are summarized in Table 1. On the 6-month postoperative MRIs, there were 11 patients with Sugaya type I (9%), 65 with type II (52%), 33 with type III (27%), 11 with type IV (9%), and 4 with type V (3%). On the final US images at a mean of 24 months postoperatively, there were 104 (84%) healed and 20 (16%) retorn tendons. When correlated with MRI results at 6 months, all 15 patients with types IV and V remained retorn on the final US images. On the other hand, 5 patients with type I, II, or III had a newly developed retear in the final US evaluation, including 1 of 76 Sugaya I/II types (1.3%) and 4 of 33 Sugaya type III patients (12.1%) (Fig. 2). Comparison between Sugaya Type I/II and III Groups

Comparing the preoperative data of demographics, MRI findings, and clinical scores of the Sugaya type I/II and III groups, the univariate analysis revealed several variables that were correlated with the development of Sugaya type III after RCR surgery, such as age, mediolateral tear size, anteroposterior tear size, and muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration of the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and subscapularis (Table 1). However, our multivariate analysis showed that fatty infiltration of the infraspinatus was the only independent preoperative risk factor for the development of Sugaya type III (OR, 6.667; 95% CI, 1.745–25.010; p = 0.005) (Table 2). When comparing the postoperative clinical outcomes of Sugaya types I/II and III, all clinical scores were not significantly different at 6 months postoperatively and at the final follow-up visit.

DISCUSSION

Sugaya type III after RCR indicates an insufficient tendon thickness despite continuity with the greater tuberosity footprint. It must be a less optimal tendon structure, compared to Sugaya type I or II and may have a clinical impact on the long-term outcome of RCR. This study demonstrated that Sugaya type III at 6 months after surgery was associated with preoperative inferior muscle quality of the infraspinatus and was more likely to progress to a complete rupture during the follow-up, compared to the type I and II group. We recommend monitoring Sugaya type III patients by follow-up imaging studies and alerting patients to the risk of late retear.

The Sugaya classification has been widely used in the literature. However, many aspects of Sugaya type III remain undetermined, such as the pathogenesis of its development and its influence on clinical and structural outcomes in the long term. It is unknown whether it is a thin tendon from the beginning at the time of repair or it has developed from a better tendon quality. Although the pathogenesis of Sugaya type III is beyond the scope of this study, we found that more advanced preoperative fatty infiltration of the infraspinatus was associated with the development of postoperative Sugaya type III. Severe muscle atrophy and Goutallier stage 3 or 4 fatty infiltration of the supraspinatus are well-known factors for the irreparability of rotator cuff tears. Similarly, poor muscle quality of the infraspinatus may make it more difficult for the surgeon to achieve a tension-free repair sufficiently covering the greater tuberosity footprint. This difficulty may lead to the development of a less optimal tendon thickness at the time of the operation or failure of maintaining a robust tendon during the healing process.

Tendons of Sugaya type III after repair were found to be at risk for a later retear in this study. There are few studies that focus on Sugaya type III or have discussed this issue in depth, but similar results can be found in the literature. Kim et al.17) reported that among 24 patients who had shown type III repair on 3-month postoperative MRI, 1 patient exhibited Sugaya type IV on 1-year postoperative MRI and 3 patients showed a full-thickness retear on their 1-year postoperative US. Stahnke et al.18) also found the deterioration of tendon integrity from 12 to 24 months among 13 patients, including 3 patients from type III to IV and 2 patients from type III to V. Therefore, it can be stated that repaired tendons of Sugaya type III in the early postoperative period should be continuously monitored as they appear to be at risk of later progressing to a complete retear over time. The cause of this structural deterioration of the Sugaya type III tendon status is not known, but there can be several possible explanations. First, a thin tendon with intact continuity to the greater tuberosity on postoperative 6-month MRIs may be scar tissue rather than healthy tendon tissue. The absolute distinction between the tendon signal intensity of scar tissue and that of normal tendon tissue by MRI is known to be challenging.19) Thin scar tissue bridging a defect with continuity on MRI may have been interpreted as Sugaya type III in our study. Less optimal tissue in Sugaya type III patients is not likely to provide substantial functional benefits in terms of load transfer and may lead to a retear over time. Second, the thin supraspinatus tendon in Sugaya type III patients might be weak, given that the tensile strength of newly remodeled collagen tissue is reduced.20,21) They might experience typical tear propagation to the infraspinatus tear posteriorly due to increased strain in the tendon fibers adjacent to the tear.22,23) This type of tear progression has been proposed as a possible mechanism by which untreated partial-thickness tears of the supraspinatus tendon progress to full-thickness tears.23)

However, the different results in serial changes of RCR integrity were also observed. Koh et al.7) reported serial postoperative MRI evaluations after RCR in 31 patients and showed that 5 Sugaya type III repairs on 6-months postoperative MRI remained the same on 19-months images. Gulotta et al.24) evaluated the repair integrity up to 5 years by serial US evaluations and showed that the healing rates of all patients even improved from 64.3% at 1 year to 75.4% at 2 years and 81.2% at 5 years. Malavolta et al.3) also showed that the postoperative appearance of the supraspinatus tendon can be improved from type I to type II and even the decrease in the number of retears was observed in type III repairs. Therefore, the progression of Sugaya type III in the early postoperative period should remain the subject of further research.

Based on our study results on the possible structural deterioration of Sugaya type III, it is important to analyze the postoperative tendon morphology with the use of the Sugaya classification rather than simply the presence of a retear. Malavolta et al.3) emphasized that the presence and intensity of residual tendinopathy (Sugaya type II) are essential to elucidate the clinical and structural prognosis after RCR. Yoshida et al.2) demonstrated that the Sugaya classification negatively correlated with shoulder abduction strength after RCR. More recently, Muniandy et al.25) reported MRI results 10 years after RCR and showed that patients with Sugaya type III exhibited lesser muscle degeneration in the supraspinatus and infraspinatus and performed better in functional assessment compared with Sugaya type IV and V patients. They concluded that Sugaya type III should not be considered as a retear.

This study has several limitations. First, we used different imaging methods at 3 evaluation time points (MRI at postoperative 6 months and US images at the final evaluation). If we had used MRI at both time points, we could have evaluated direct changes in each category of the Sugaya classification, as well as the state of muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration. However, this is our routine protocol for evaluation after RCR given the cost-effectiveness of the examination; the use of MRI at 6 months and US images for the later follow-up at 1 year and annually thereafter. The major advantage of US examination over MRI is that it can be repeated at a low cost, and thus the use of US for serial evaluation of the rotator cuff has been frequently reported.24,26,27) The accuracy of US images can be examiner-dependent and interpretation of RCR integrity is known to be challenging. In this study, to overcome this issue, a single examiner (the treating surgeon) with more than 10 years of experience in shoulder surgery and ultrasonography performed all US evaluations, and an independent single musculoskeletal radiologist interpreted the results. However, involvement of the treating surgeon in the US examinations can introduce potential detection bias. The lack of intra- and interobserver reliability tests for MRI and US is another limitation. The imaging follow-up was heterogeneous, with MRI uniformly performed at 6 months, whereas US assessments were conducted at variable intervals ranging from 12 to 72 months. As the risk of retear is cumulative over time, outcomes obtained at different timepoints are not directly comparable. This variability may have influenced the reported retear rates and should be considered when interpreting our findings. Third, this study had a small number of patients with Sugaya type III (33 patients), of whom only 4 developed a later retear. Due to the small number, it was not possible to statistically analyze the risk factors for developing a later retear. Similarly, although the incidence of late development of a retear was significantly higher in Sugaya type III patients than in Sugaya type I/II, our post hoc analysis revealed low power for this outcome (only 65.1%). This low statistical power may be due to the small number of patients in the Sugaya type III group in our study (33 out of a total of 124 patients). Similarly, because the number of Type III events was limited relative to the number of predictors, our multivariable logistic regression model is at risk of overfitting; therefore, these results should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings and provide more robust estimates. Finally, the retrospective nature of the study and the short follow-up duration should be acknowledged as limitations.

In conclusion, postoperative Sugaya type III repair status is associated with preoperative inferior muscle quality of the infraspinatus and can progress to a retear with a higher risk than Sugaya type I/II.
