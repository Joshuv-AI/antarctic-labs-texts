Giant sacrococcygeal chordoma presenting with isolated urinary symptoms: A case report
Authors: Jimmy Cojab Sacal, Ariela A Souroujon Torun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Chordomas are rare low-grade malignant tumors arising from remnants of the primitive notochord. They most commonly affect the sacrum and typically present with lower back pain and radiculopathy. Genitourinary or gastrointestinal symptoms usually occur later in the disease course and are commonly associated with neurologic deficits.Case description: We report the case of an 83-year-old Latin American male who presented with a 1-year history of intermittent dysuria, urinary frequency, nocturia, tenesmus, and dyschezia, without back pain or neurologic symptoms. Initial urologic and infectious evaluations were unrevealing. Subsequent abdominal and pelvic imaging revealed a giant presacral mass originating from S4, measuring 13.5 cm, consistent with sacral chordoma. The patient underwent partial surgical resection; however, due to the highly vascular nature of the tumor and intraoperative complications, he developed multiorgan failure and died shortly after surgery.Conclusion: Sacral chordomas may present atypically with isolated urinary or gastrointestinal symptoms in the absence of neurologic complaints. In patients with persistent pelvic or lower urinary tract symptoms and a negative urologic workup, early cross-sectional imaging should be considered. Awareness of these rare presentations may facilitate earlier diagnosis and improve outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Chordomas are rare malignant bone tumors that account for approximately 1–4% of all primary malignant bone neoplasms.[1,6,11] They arise from embryonic remnants of the notochord and most frequently involve the sacrococcygeal region (approximately 50%), followed by the skull base (35%) and mobile spine (15%). These tumors are characterized by slow but locally aggressive growth, a low tendency to metastasize, and relative radioresistance.

Clinical presentation is often insidious, leading to delayed diagnosis. Lower back pain is the most common presenting symptom in sacral chordomas, followed by radiculopathy due to sacral nerve root compression.[2,8] Bladder, bowel, and autonomic dysfunction usually appear later in the disease course. Because sacrococcygeal masses may represent malignant pathology, early tissue diagnosis and appropriate imaging are essential.

CASE PRESENTATION

An 83-year-old Latin American man with a medical history of hypertension, mitral valve replacement, osteoporosis, recurrent urinary tract infections, and prior prostatectomy presented with a 1-year history of intermittent dysuria, urinary frequency, nocturia, tenesmus, and dyschezia. He denied weight loss, lower back pain, radicular symptoms, or prior neurologic complaints.

He was initially evaluated by internal medicine and urology. Urodynamic studies demonstrated chronic urinary retention (>50% postvoid residual). Infectious studies were negative, and no genitourinary structural abnormalities were identified. Conservative management, including lifestyle modifications and laxatives, failed to improve symptoms.

The patient was subsequently referred to colorectal surgery. Sigmoidoscopy revealed an extrinsic obstructive mass effect on the sigmoid colon. Physical examination showed no lymphadenopathy, no lumbar spine tenderness, preserved motor function in both lower extremities, and no sensory deficits or saddle anesthesia.

The tumor involved the lower sacral segments (S3–S5), with compression of adjacent sacral nerve roots. A posterior sacral approach was selected based on tumor location and extent. The procedure consisted of intralesional debulking rather than en bloc resection due to tumor size, vascularity, and proximity to major vascular structures. Preservation of nerve roots was attempted, although complete delineation was limited intraoperatively. The presacral component was accessed posteriorly; however, complete resection was not feasible.

Laboratory studies revealed elevated lactate dehydrogenase (493 U/L), macrocytic anemia (hemoglobin 11.4 g/dL, mean corpuscular volume 103 fL), and acute kidney injury (creatinine 1.77 mg/dL, blood urea nitrogen 34.7 mg/dL). Urinalysis and remaining serum chemistries were unremarkable.

Abdominopelvic computed tomography demonstrated a dense, partially calcified mass infiltrating the sacrum. Contrast-enhanced pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a large expansive presacral tumor originating at S4, measuring up to 13.5 cm in maximal diameter, with mixed solid components and mass effect on adjacent pelvic structures, including the rectum and bladder [Figures 1 and 2]. These findings were highly suggestive of sacral chordoma.

The patient underwent surgical exploration with the intent for resection. A posterior sacral approach was selected based on tumor location and extent. Intraoperatively, a highly vascularized tumor was identified. Partial resection was achieved; however, complete excision was not possible due to proximity to major vascular structures, including the abdominal aorta. Significant intraoperative hemorrhage required multiple blood transfusions. Preoperative embolization was not performed due to the urgency of surgical intervention and the patient’s clinical status.

Histopathologic examination demonstrated a malignant mesenchymal neoplasm with abundant myxoid matrix and focal sarcomatous areas, consistent with sacral chordoma. These findings are characteristic of conventional chordoma,[3] which typically demonstrates physaliphorous cells within a myxoid stroma.[4]

Postoperatively, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit, where he developed hemodynamic instability requiring vasopressor support. Despite aggressive management, he progressed to multiorgan failure and died several hours after surgery.

DISCUSSION

Chordomas are rare sarcomas with an annual incidence of <0.1/100,000 individuals.[6,10] They predominantly affect males and typically present in the fifth to sixth decade of life. Diagnosis is frequently delayed due to the slow-growing nature of the tumor and nonspecific early symptoms. Most of the patients are diagnosed at an advanced local stage, often with large tumors that pose significant surgical challenges.[7]

The most common presenting symptom of sacral chordoma is lower back pain, followed by radiculopathy.[8] Bladder and bowel dysfunction usually accompany neurologic involvement. In contrast, our patient presented exclusively with urinary and gastrointestinal complaints, without back pain or neurologic deficits, representing an atypical and misleading clinical picture.

Imaging plays a central role in diagnosis. Chordomas typically appear as large lytic lesions with soft tissue components and may contain calcifications in up to 60% of cases. MRI is the preferred modality for assessing tumor extent and involvement of adjacent structures.[9] In this case, the tumor measured approximately 13.5 × 9.9 × 9.6 cm [Figure 3], corresponding to an estimated volume of 667 cc.

Complete surgical resection with wide margins remains the cornerstone of treatment and offers the best chance for local control. However, tumor size, location, proximity to critical structures, and risk of neurologic injury often limit resectability. High-dose radiation therapy may be used as adjuvant treatment due to the relative radioresistance of chordomas. Preoperative biopsy is commonly recommended to confirm diagnosis and guide management; however, in this case, the diagnosis was strongly suggested by imaging findings and the clinical scenario, leading directly to surgical intervention. Preoperative embolization has been described as a strategy to reduce intraoperative bleeding, although it was not performed in this case.[5]

This case has several limitations, including the lack of immunohistochemical analysis, the absence of formal frailty assessment, and the lack of histopathological imaging, which may limit the pathological and surgical interpretation. The case highlights how the absence of back pain or neurological deficits may delay suspicion of a spinal tumor, particularly when initial evaluations focus on urologic or gastrointestinal causes. This reinforces the importance of considering early cross-sectional imaging in patients with persistent unexplained pelvic or urinary symptoms.

Atypical presentations of sacral chordoma, including urinary-dominant symptoms without back pain, have been reported in the literature, although they remain uncommon. These presentations are associated with delayed diagnosis due to the absence of classic spinal symptoms. Previous reports suggest that such cases may initially be misattributed to urologic or gastrointestinal conditions, highlighting the importance of early imaging when standard evaluations are inconclusive.

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention was based on progressive symptoms and significant mass effect on pelvic organs. Although a biopsy is generally recommended to confirm a diagnosis, in this case, imaging findings were highly suggestive of chordoma, and surgical management was prioritized. Preoperative embolization, while useful in reducing intraoperative bleeding in highly vascular tumors, was not performed due to the urgency of the intervention and the patient’s clinical condition.

CONCLUSION

Sacral chordomas can present atypically with isolated urinary or gastrointestinal complaints, delaying diagnosis and treatment. Clinicians should maintain a broad differential diagnosis in patients with persistent pelvic or lower urinary tract symptoms and negative initial evaluations. Early imaging may facilitate timely recognition of rare but serious conditions such as chordoma and improve prognostic outcomes.
