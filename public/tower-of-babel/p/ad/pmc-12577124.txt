Isolation and characterisation of AFP+/DLK1+ double-positive hepatic stem/progenitor-like cells from fibrotic partial hepatectomy model
Authors: Shuangliang Sun, Tegshjargal Badamjav, Namur Narid, Urjims Xu, Dunfu Suo, Batbold Batsaikhan, Bayar Hexig
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe liver is an organ with a strong regenerative capacity. Currently, partial hepatectomy (PHx) remains the most effective treatment option within various therapeutic strategies for liver diseases. When the liver undergoes acute injury, such as PHx, the remaining hepatocytes become hypertrophic, re-enter the cell cycle, initiate proliferation, and restore liver function. However, identifying the role of adult hepatic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) in this repair process remains challenging.MethodsIn this study, liver fibrosis was induced in mice by subcutaneous injection of carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) into the back for 8 weeks. Subsequently, 2/3 PHx was used to establish a fibrotic PHx model. Samples were collected at 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 4 d, and 7 d after PHx. The expression of HSPC-associated markers, including Afp, Dlk1, Ki67, and Lgr5, was analysed using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and immunofluorescence.ResultsGene expression analysis revealed that the higher concentration of AFP⁺/DLK1⁺ double-positive cells was in the 50–70% Percoll layer, isolated via density gradient differential centrifugation. Pre-injection of CCl4 promoted liver regeneration after 2/3 PHx, with a peak regenerative phase observed at 24–48 h. Experimental results demonstrated that AFP+/DLK1+ double-positive cells exhibit stem cell-like properties and are involved in liver regeneration. Interestingly, adult and embryonic HSPCs were found in distinct Percoll layers and required different in vitro culture conditions.ConclusionsAFP and DLK1 serve as potential markers for selecting HSPC-like populations from the fibrotic PHx model. Our findings indicate that the fibrotic liver exhibits the most vigorous regenerative activity at 48 h after PHx, making it the optimal period for the isolation and extraction of HSPC-like cells which refers only to marker expression and proliferation, the relevance of the findings presented herein remains to be further verified.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04728-1.

Background

The liver serves as the body’s “metabolic hub,” playing a pivotal role in various vital physiological processes, including energy storage and metabolism, amino acid utilisation, secretion, and detoxification. However, in terms of cell differentiation, it is not an active organ, and a large proportion of liver cells are usually in a static state; only 0.001% to 0.01% of liver cells are undergoing mitosis [1]. Simultaneously, the liver is exposed to a multitude of factors that contribute to the occurrence of various liver diseases, encompassing hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), viral hepatitis (including multiple strains), alcoholic hepatitis, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), cirrhosis, and other acute/chronic liver diseases. HCC is the sixth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [2]. Currently, among various treatment strategies, partial hepatectomy (PHx) remains the most effective treatment [3]. Like Prometheus in ancient Greek mythology, the liver has a rapid and powerful ability to regenerate. As early as 1931, Higgins and Anderson first demonstrated that livers could grow to their original size after 2/3 hepatectomy, and the same phenomenon was found in different rodents [4]. This phenomenon primarily relies on the liver’s reparative mechanism, encompassing two fundamental components: regeneration and wound healing [5]. Of these, regeneration depends on the proliferation and differentiation of hepatic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) to generate new liver cells, including hepatocytes, bile duct epithelial cells, endothelial cells, Kupffer cells, and hepatic stellate cells [6]. The liver in mammals is capable of regenerating 70% of its lost weight and functionality within a few weeks [7]. Following an acute injury, such as hepatectomy, a complex and coordinated regenerative response ensues, involving the orchestration of multiple cytokines and growth factors, augmented portal blood flow, as well as dynamic interactions between parenchymal and non-parenchymal cells within the liver [8]. In recent years, although some progress has been made in elucidating which factors, pathways, and cell types are involved in liver regeneration, the exact mechanisms and interactions between the various cells in the liver remain largely unknown [9].

Currently, PHx for 70% of mice without underlying liver disease has reached a mature stage. However, it fails to replicate the clinical condition following extensive liver resection in patients with pre-existing liver diseases. Notably, most patients with liver cancer present with complications such as liver cirrhosis or fibrosis [10]. In cirrhosis, the liver parenchyma is replaced by fibrous tissue and regenerative nodules, resulting in disorganisation of the fundamental framework of liver lobules, and the mortality rate during PHx is exceedingly high [11]. Even when successful resection occurs, regenerated hepatocytes can only form a pseudolobule that lacks normal functionality, thus rendering regeneration futile. Therefore, this study used liver fibrosis as the starting point for the next phase of research [12]. Liver fibrosis is a pathological process resulting from an imbalance in the synthesis, degradation, and deposition of extracellular matrix during persistent liver injuries and subsequent tissue repair reactions. Prolonged exposure to injury factors increases the likelihood of developing cirrhosis or even liver cancer [13]. Furthermore, extensive liver resection complicated by underlying lesions can lead to inadequate regeneration of residual liver tissue, ultimately causing acute liver failure [14]. The investigation of the impact of fibrotic lesions on liver regeneration following PHx holds immense significance for both liver-related research and clinical treatment.

HSPCs are a type of cell with stem cell characteristics related to liver development and regeneration. They are capable of self-renewal, proliferation, and bidirectional differentiation into hepatocytes and bile duct epithelial cells, and have a potential application prospect in liver repair and regeneration [15]. Studies on HSPCs date back to 1956, when Farber et al. conducted a study on the mechanism of rat hepatocyte carcinogenesis and first discovered a group of HSPC-like cells called Hepatic Oval Cells (HOC), which exhibit the following morphological characteristics: small size (approximately 1/5-1/2 of normal hepatocytes), oval or ellipsoid shape, and possess large karyoplasm [16]. In 1987, Evarts et al. successfully induced HOC, further confirming their role as precursor cells for liver regeneration [17]. Subsequent studies by Fausto [18], Sell [19], and others have demonstrated that these cells are activated in animal models of liver injury and possess dual differentiation potential into both hepatocytes and bile duct epithelial cells. The majority of data regarding this cell population comes from animal models that utilise toxin-induced inhibition of native liver cells, combined with triggers to stimulate liver regeneration.

However, the isolation, characterisation, and in vitro culture of HSPCs still pose challenges [20]. In addition, the contribution of HSPCs to liver regeneration following PHx of fibrotic liver remains to be elucidated. Previously, putative HSPCs have been isolated and expanded in vitro using FACS with cell surface markers [21]. However, the use of fluorescent dyes in FACS may impede the clinical application of HSPCs [22]. In this study, we used nontoxic Percoll to isolate AFP+/DLK1+ double-positive cell populations from the regenerating liver of the fibrotic PHx model via differential cell density [23, 24]. Through characterisation, we demonstrated that this cell population exhibited stem cell-like properties and was involved in liver regeneration.

Methods

Study design

The study design consisted of three sequential stages: (1) establishment of the fibrotic model mice, (2) implementation of 2/3 PHx, and (3) isolation of AFP+/DLK1+ double-positive HSPCs through density gradient differential centrifugation. A schematic overview of the study design is presented in Fig. 1. This study has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Establishment of the fibrotic model mice

Eight-to ten-week-old male ICR mice (body weight 28–38 g) were obtained from the State Key Laboratory of Reproductive Regulation and Breeding of Grassland Livestock, College of Life Science, Inner Mongolia University (Inner Mongolia, China) and housed under 12/12-h light/dark cycles with free access to standard pelleted chow and water. As wild-type mice from an outbred stock, no genotyping was performed. Prior to the start of the defined experimental procedures (CCl4 injections), no previous procedures had been performed on any of the animals included in this study. The Animal Care and Use Committees approved the protocols of these animal experiments. Liver fibrosis was induced by subcutaneous injection of carbon tetrachloride (CCl4; Macklin, Shanghai) in olive oil (1:4, v/v; AGRIC) into the dorsal region at a dose of 0.02 mL/g body weight, twice per week for 8 weeks. The control group received an equal volume of olive oil. The normal group consisted of age-matched mice that did not undergo any treatment. Mice were randomly assigned to groups by drawing lots. To minimize bias and order effects, the sequence of handling and injecting mice during each CCl4 administration round was randomized. Animals that died before the scheduled endpoint were excluded from all analyses, as their premature death precluded the collection of complete data sets.

2/3 hepatectomy

After the establishment of the fibrotic model mice, we performed 2/3 PHx for each mouse, as described in a previous study [25]. The normal group consisted of healthy adult mice that underwent PHx concurrently with the model group. Anaesthesia was induced with 3% pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 0.01 mL/g body weight. After disinfecting the skin with 75% ethanol, a midline abdominal incision was made to expose the xiphoid process. A 3-0 silk thread was tied around the base of the left lateral lobe near the liver hilum, with the knot placed as close to the base as possible. The lobe was resected just above the ligature using curved microsurgical scissors. A second knot was placed between the stump and the median lobe, and the median lobe was pulled down and resected above the ligature. The peritoneum and skin were closed with 3-0 sutures (Fig. 3a). Samples were collected at 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 4 d, and 7 d after PHx. Euthanasia was humanely performed via cervical dislocation without chemical agents, in compliance with AVMA guidelines and institutional animal welfare policies, to ensure rapid and irreversible unconsciousness in small rodents.

Density gradient differential centrifugation

Digestion

Anaesthesia was induced with 3% pentobarbital sodium (0.01 mL/g body weight). After disinfection with 75% ethanol, a midline incision was made to expose the hepatic portal vein and inferior vena cava. A syringe was inserted into the hepatic portal vein (inlet) and another into the inferior vena cava (outlet). D-Hank’s solution (~ 50 mL, prewarmed to body temperature) was perfused to remove blood and impurities. Subsequently, 0.1% collagenase Type IV digestive enzyme (approximately 50 mL) was perfused through the portal vein until distinct turtle-back cracks appeared on the liver surface. The liver was then excised and transferred to a sterile petri dish. The under-digested liver tissue was thoroughly minced, mixed with fresh 0.1% collagenase Type IV digestive enzyme, and digested with agitation in a 37 °C water bath for 7 min. After digestion, an equal volume of culture medium was added to terminate the digestion process. Finally, the resulting suspension was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer to obtain a mixture of whole liver cells.

Centrifugation

The cell suspension was centrifuged at 50×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and subjected to a second centrifugation at 200×g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet the liver non-parenchymal cells. The resulting pellet was resuspended in the culture medium for further purification.

Purification

A gradient buffer containing 70%, 50%, and 30% Percoll was layered sequentially (bottom to top) in a centrifuge tube. The diluted cell suspension was gently added on top (1:1 volume ratio with gradients). Tubes were centrifuged at 600×g for 10 min, at 4 °C (acceleration: 9; deceleration: 1). Cells from each gradient interface were collected into new tubes and washed 2–3 times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Histological staining (HE, Sirius Red, and Masson Trichrome)

Liver tissues were transferred to 4% paraformaldehyde and then dehydrated with a gradient of ethanol and xylene, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 3 μm paraffin sections. The liver sections were stained with HE (Solarbio, Beijing), Sirius Red (Solarbio, Beijing), and Masson Trichrome (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for histopathological and fibrogenesis detection. Representative images were obtained using the Olympus IX71 microscope with a DP80 digital camera (Nikon, Japan).

Serum biochemical assays

Blood biochemical parameters, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (Jiancheng, Nanjing), were assayed to evaluate mouse liver damage. After anaesthetising (3% pentobarbital sodium, for a dose of 0.01 mL/g body weight), the whole blood was taken from the mouse heart, and the serum was isolated after 3000 rpm centrifugation for 15 min at 20 °C and stored at − 80 °C for further experimental analysis. Serum samples were collected after the establishment of the liver fibrosis model mice (20% CCl4 twice/week for 8 weeks), and 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 4 d, and 7 d after 2/3 PHx. Biochemical analyses of ALT and AST were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot

The total protein was extracted from the liver tissues using RIPA buffer (Solarbio, Beijing).

The total protein was separated on an Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis(SDS-PAGE) gel. The proteins were then electro-transferred onto PVDF membranes, which were blocked using 5% non-fat milk dissolved in Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20 ༈TBST༉ for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies including collagen Ⅲ (1:500; Wanleibio, Shenyang, WL03186), collagen Ⅰ (1:1000; Proteintech-cn, Wuhan, 14695-1-AP), α-SMA (1:1000; Proteintech-cn, Wuhan, 55135-1-AP), and β-actin (1:500; Wanleibio, Shenyang, WL01372). After washing with TBST, the bands were incubated with species-matched secondary antibodies (1:2000; Bioss, Beijing) at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were visualised with sensitive enhanced HRP substrate (Proteintech-cn, Wuhan). The protein bands were visualised using a Bio-Rad ChemiDocTM MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). All images were analysed using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues and cells using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, Japan), and reverse transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan). cDNA was subjected to qRT-PCR analysis using LightCycler® 480 II Real-Time PCR System (Roche Applied Science, Mannheim, Germany) with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Japan) and specific primers. The primer sequences are listed in Additional file 1. The results were normalised against β-actin expression, and mRNA enrichments were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Immunofluorescence

Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated with a gradient of ethanol and xylene, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 3 μm paraffin sections. Sections were boiled in sodium citrate buffer for antigen retrieval for 40–45 min. For the immunofluorescence staining of HSPCs, cells were plated in 35 mm glass dishes coated with Poly-L-lysine (Phygene, Fujian) and washed with PBS. Then, HSPCs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich, USA) for 10 min. After PBS washes, samples were blocked with 2% BSA for 1 h. Both the pretreated sections and cells were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies against Alpha Fetoprotein (AFP) (1:1000; Proteintech-cn, Wuhan, 14550-1-AP) and Delta-Like Non-Canonical Notch Ligand 1 (DLK1) (1:1000; MBL, Beijing, D187-3) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark (1:400, Abcam, USA). Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured using the A1R laser confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was employed to assess the dual-labelled abundance of AFP+/DLK1+ in different Percoll layers. Cells were harvested and washed twice in PBS, and then incubated with viability Dye for 10 min. After washing with PBS, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma Aldrich, USA) for 10 min. Cells were then blocked with 2.5% BSA for 1 h. The cells were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies against AFP (1:200; Proteintech-cn, Wuhan, 14550-1-AP) and DLK1 (1:200; MBL, Beijing, D187-3) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark (1:1000, Abcam, USA). The stained cells were immediately analysed using the CytoFlex S (Beckman Coulter, USA), collecting 10,000 events from the primarily gated population of interest. The collected data were further analysed using the CytExpert software.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD. Differences between two groups were compared using Student’s t-test, and those between more than three groups were compared using twoway ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism® software (version 8.0.2) and Image J/FIJI software. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the technical challenges of longitudinal sampling in fibrotic PHx models, biological replicates were prioritised for key timepoints.

Results

Injection of CCl4 causes liver fibrosis

We used a method of subcutaneous injection of 20% CCl4 (0.02 mL/g) into the dorsal region twice a week for 8 weeks to establish the fibrotic model mice [26] (Fig. 2a). The control group was injected with an equal amount of olive oil, while age-matched naive mice served as the untreated normal group. After 8 weeks, the weight of mice in the control group showed a steady increase, while the model group increased more slowly and slightly, indicating that CCl4 was well absorbed and had an effect on the mice (Fig. 2b). From the perspective of mouse characteristics, the mice in the control group were in good health, with a regular diet, quick movement, formed stools, and shiny coats. In the model group, the activity of the mice was slightly reduced, the stool was unformed, the fur was messy and dull, the back skin was ulcerated, and the stress response was more severe. Comparing the appearance of the liver, that of the model group was dark and yellow, and to the touch, it was hard and grainy. The difference was significant compared to the control group and the normal group (Fig. 2c). ALT and AST, commonly misnamed “liver function tests,” are actually “liver damage tests”, as they are released by damaged cells [27]. Serum biochemical indexes showed that the model group was higher than the control group and normal group, and the liver injury was apparent. (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2d). The results of HE staining and Sirius Red staining showed that the hepatocytes in the control group and normal group were arranged neatly, the hepatic cords were radial, and there was no inflammatory cell infiltration, showing no statistically significant intergroup differences. In contrast, in the model group, the degeneration of hepatocytes was obvious, the accumulation of fat vacuoles, the disorder of cell arrangement and substantial inflammatory cell infiltration. Collagen deposition was mainly located in the perisinusoidal region and between sinusoids, with red fibrous tissue hyperplasia forming fibrous septa (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2e). Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated progressive fibrogenesis in model group mice, characterised by distorted lobular architecture and bridging collagen deposition extending from portal triads to central veins. This contrasted sharply with the intact collagen distribution observed in both the control group and the normal groups (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2e). The formation of mature fibrous septa at portal-central interfaces confirmed successful establishment of liver fibrosis. Based on the comprehensive histopathological analysis and confirmation by a pathologist, the fibrotic model mice were determined to be at stage S2 (Scheuer scoring system). Western blot results showed that the protein expression level of Collagen III, Collagen I, and α-SMA in the model group increased significantly at the end of CCl4 injection (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2f). The original uncropped images of the Western blots are shown in Supplementary Fig 1.

These results demonstrate that the method of subcutaneous injection of 20% CCl4 twice a week for 8 weeks successfully establishes a fibrotic model in mice, with a stable and efficient success rate. This model can be used in subsequent experiments.

Pre-injection of CCl4 promotes liver regeneration after 2/3 PHx

One week after establishing the fibrotic model mice, we performed 2/3 PHx on the fibrotic model mice, and the samples were collected at 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 4 d, and 7 d post-PHx. The normal group consisted of healthy mice that underwent PHx concurrently with the model group [28] (Fig. 3a). The liver /body weight ratio (%) showed that compared with the normal PHx mice, the fibrotic PHx mice exhibited an apparently higher ratio at 48 h after PHx. However, this difference did not achieve statistical significance (p >0.05) (Fig. 3b). We measured serum ALT and AST levels at the indicated time points after PHx. Both groups exhibited similar patterns post-PHx: liver injury was severe initially but decreased gradually and reached normal levels by 48 h (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3c). Interestingly, the ALT, AST levels in fibrotic mice were lower than in control mice from 12 h to 48 h after PHx, whereas the opposite trend was observed at 4 d. We concluded that compared with hepatocytes in normal mice, fibrosis results in a decreased proportion of functional hepatocytes, a reduction in the total amount of enzymes that can be released [29], and chronic liver injury may activate antioxidant or anti-apoptotic pathways, which could partially alleviate acute injury. However, the opposite result occurred at 4 d. This might be attributed to the fact that the fibrotic regenerated liver contains a higher proportion of stem cell-like cells, which exhibit less mature cellular functions compared to hepatocytes.

To assess liver regeneration state, we examined key parameters for regenerating livers by qRT-PCR: the mRNA expression levels of stemness and proliferation-related genes Afp; Dlk1; Lgr5; Ki67; HNF4α (Transcriptional regulator which controls the expression of hepatic genes during the transition of endodermal cells to hepatic progenitor cells), liver function and hepatocyte related genes Alb; Krt-8; Krt-18, and bile duct related genes Krt-7, Krt-19 (Fig. 3d). The qRT-PCR results showed that the expression of cell stemness and proliferation-related genes exhibited an initial upward trend followed by a subsequent decline, although the expression trend of Afp did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05). However, Dlk1 (p < 0.0001); ki67 (p < 0.0001); Lgr5 (p < 0.001), and HNF4α (p < 0.01) were highly expressed at 48 h, and exhibited a higher level in the fibrotic PHx group compared to the normal PHx group. The liver function-related gene Alb increased significantly at 48 h in the normal group (p < 0.0001), whereas the fibrotic PHx group exhibited significantly lower Alb expression compared to the normal PHx group (p < 0.0001). The fibrotic PHx group is significantly lower than the control group at the indicated time points. We conclude that the normal liver is capable of maintaining basic liver function following PHx. In contrast, the fibrotic liver fails to do so, thereby initiating liver regeneration to meet the body’s demands. The hepatocyte-related genes Krt-8 did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05), but Krt-18 (p < 0.01) was highly expressed at 48 h. The bile duct-related genes Krt-7 and Krt-19 also increased at 48 h, and their expression level exhibited a similar pattern between the two groups (p < 0.01). All qRT-PCR analyses revealed that the stemness and proliferation-related genes were generally upregulated in the fibrotic PHx group at 48 h. Next, we further showed the liver regeneration state by immunofluorescence analysis in the fibrotic PHx group (Fig. 3e). Starting at 12 h post-PHx, AFP and DLK1 expressions were detected, peaking at 48 h and declining from 4 d onward (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 3f).

Combined with these results, we preliminarily determined that pre-injection of CCl4 promotes liver regeneration after 2/3 PHx, with a rapid regeneration phase occurring between 24 h and 48 h. Notably, AFP+/DLK1+ double-positive cells exhibit stem cell-like properties involved in liver regeneration.

Isolation of AFP+/DLK1+ double-positive cells from regenerating liver by density gradient differential centrifugation

To minimise cellular damage during isolation, we prepared three Percoll density gradient solutions (30%, 50%, and 70%) for cell separation and extraction. Multiple centrifugation purifications were performed at different speeds based on the varying floating densities of liver cells, ultimately resulting in the isolation of AFP+/DLK1+ double-positive cells from regenerating liver at 24 h and 48 h in the fibrotic PHx model (Fig. 4a). Flow cytometry was employed to assess the dual-labelled abundance of AFP+/DLK1+ in different Percoll layers (Fig. 4b). The results revealed that the 30% layer primarily contained cell debris. In contrast, the 50– 70% Percoll layer at 48 h exhibited the highest proportion of AFP⁺/DLK1⁺ double-positive cells (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4c), suggesting that the target cell population resides predominantly in this layer at that time point. qRT-PCR analysis of Afp, Dlk1, Lgr5, and Ki67 revealed that the expression levels were significantly higher in cells from the 50%–70% Percoll layer, compared to other layers (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4d). Notably, Afp remained highly expressed at 24 h, whereas Dlk1 exhibited a high expression level at 48 h. Next, the AFP+/DLK1+ double-positive expression was further confirmed through cell immunofluorescence analyses (Fig. 4e). PolyL-lysine was utilised to adhere 50%–70% Percoll layer cells to the petri dish, followed by immunofluorescence, revealing a higher proportion of AFP+/DLK1+ cells in the 48 h group compared to the 24 h group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4f). These results demonstrate that density gradient differential centrifugation can effectively isolate AFP+/DLK1+ double-positive cells, with the 50%-70% Percoll layer at 48 h serving as an optimal source. These cells retain stem cell-like properties post-isolation.

Discussion

HSPCs are multi-origin stem cells with strong self-renewal capacity and the potential for bidirectional differentiation into bile duct epithelial cells and hepatocytes. These characteristics make them highly promising for applications in liver wound healing and regeneration. At present, HSPCs combined with cell transplantation have become a novel strategy to replace organ transplantation, and can reduce the occurrence of immune rejection. Therefore, it is of great significance to further elucidate how liver regeneration is initiated after PHx of the fibrotic liver. Here, we have investigated the marker expression of HSPCs produced in the regenerating liver and provided insight into their functional potential in liver regeneration.

In this study, considering the significance of HSPCs in liver disease treatment, to mitigate cell damage caused by separation and extraction processes, target cells were sorted using density gradient differential centrifugation based on their distinct floating densities. Percoll is the most frequently used medium for density gradient centrifugation, comprising colloidal silica particles coated with polyethylpyrrolidone or silane [30]. Since its coated surface does not adhere to cells, Percoll enables efficient and rapid cell separation under non-toxic conditions by creating isoosmotic density gradients in the range of 1.0 to 1.3 g/mL, without the requirement for costly equipment or specialised technology [31].

In this study, we initially identified the cell markers, AFP and DLK1, to permit selection of the HSPC-like cell population from the regenerating liver. AFP is a glycoprotein derived from the cells of the embryonic endoderm. During embryonic development, AFP is first produced in the fetal liver and yolk sac [32], and its level remains consistently low throughout the adult life cycle [33]. However, in the event of liver injury, the AFP gene is specifically activated within hepatocytes, resulting in the robust expression of AFP and its active involvement in the regulation of diverse biological processes [34, 35]. Previous studies have also found that AFP promotes cell proliferation by promoting the transition of cells from the G1 phase to the S phase [36]. DLK1 is a transmembrane protein that belongs to the NOTCH non-canonical ligand family [37]. Recent studies have revealed that DLK1 plays a crucial role in cellular differentiation by regulating the maintenance of stem cell pools during both fetal and adult stages [38]. DLK1 is expressed in many tissues during embryonic development, but in adults, expression is low and is mainly restricted to multiple immature stem/progenitor cells (notably hepatoblasts) [39, 40]. DLK1 has been shown to be a marker of hepatoblasts, the transient amplifying progenitors of hepatic stem cells [20]. Thus, these facts collectively substantiate the validity of AFP and DLK1 as potential cellular markers of HSPCs in regenerating liver, despite their lack of exact positive correlation within these cell populations. Previous studies have demonstrated that 2/3 PHx could initiate HSPC-dependent liver regeneration [41]. Our study advanced this knowledge by conducting 2/3 PHx on a liver fibrosis model to enhance HSPC-like activity and simulate the status of basic liver diseases, such as liver fibrosis, in most patients. The expression levels at 24 h and 48 h after PHx were significantly higher compared to other periods, as evidenced by the upregulation of stemness and proliferation-related genes Afp, Dlk1, Ki67, Lgr5, and HNF4α. Moreover, the fibrotic model group exhibited significantly elevated expression levels compared to the normal group. This result is consistent with previous research suggesting that liver regeneration after 2/3PHx alone is compensatory hyperplasia, rather than proper HSPC-dependent liver regeneration [42]. Among these genes, HNF4α is commonly regarded as a liver function marker; however, we observed a similar expression pattern to Dlk1 because, as a transcriptional regulator, HNF4α controls the expression of hepatic genes during the transition of endodermal cells to hepatic progenitor cells (PubMed:30597922). Another liver function gene, Alb, was significantly lower in the fibrotic model group than in the normal group. We conclude that the normal liver is capable of maintaining basic liver function following PHx. In contrast, the fibrotic liver fails to do so, thereby initiating liver regeneration to meet the body’s demands. When we further isolated the HSPC-like cell population and analysed AFP+/DLK1+ double-positive expression in 50%–70% Percoll layer cells by flow cytometry, we found that the level of DLK1 expression was lower than that shown by tissue immunofluorescence. Unlike in isolated HSPC-like cells, where only membrane-bound DLK1 can be captured, tissues exhibit simultaneous expression of both membrane-bound and secreted DLK1 (including in the extracellular matrix) [43]. After attempting to continue culturing the isolated cells in vitro, we observed that, unlike embryonic HSPCs, adult HSPC-like cells exhibited challenges in observing cell growth and colony formation on the substrate surface, as well as difficulties in maintaining long-term culture. We conclude that HSPC-like cell populations derived from individuals with underlying fibrotic diseases exhibit inherent fragility, posing challenges in replicating the in vivo microenvironment. To address these limitations, our next endeavour will be to overcome current limitations in the long-term culture of HSPC-like cells isolated from the fibrotic PHx model (e.g., hypoxia-optimised media, co-culture with niche stromal cells). Further, the HSPClike cells cultured in vitro were transplanted in vivo to verify the function of AFP+/DLK1+ HSPC-like cells in liver regeneration and provide evidence that they have the potential for bidirectional differentiation of hepatocytes and bile duct epithelial cells.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified AFP and DLK1 as markers that permit the selection of the HSPC-like cell population from the fibrotic PHx model. While our results demonstrated that the 48 h post-PHx in fibrotic liver was the most vigorous stage of liver regeneration, making it the optimal period for the isolation and extraction of HSPC-like cells, the relevance of the findings presented herein remains to be further verified. Further studies are required to investigate optimal strategies for maintaining the in vitro expansion, culture, and proliferation of HSPC-like cells, as well as their efficient differentiation into both bile duct epithelial cells and hepatocytes.
