Inheritance patterns of male asexuality in hybrid males of a water frog Pelophylax esculentus
Authors: Marie Doležálková-Kaštánková, Dmitrij Dedukh, Veronika Labajová, Eleonora Pustovalova, Lukáš Choleva
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGametogenesis produces gametes as a piece of genetic information transmitted to the offspring. While during sexual reproduction, progeny inherits a mix of genetic material from both parents, asexually reproducing organisms transfer a copy of maternal or paternal DNA to the progeny clonally. Parthenogenetic, gynogenetic and hybridogenetic animals have developed various mechanisms of gametogenesis, however, their inheritance is not fully understood. Here, we focused on the inheritance of asexual gametogenesis in hybrid Pelophylax esculentus (RL), emerging after crosses of P. lessonae (LL) and P. ridibundus (RR). To understand the mechanisms of gametogenesis in hybrids, we performed three-generation experiments of sexual P. ridibundus females and hybrids from all-male hybrid populations. Using fluorescent in situ hybridization, micronuclei analysis, flow cytometry and genotyping, we found that most adult hybrid males simultaneously produced two types of clonal sperm. Also, most male tadpole progeny in two successive backcrossed generations simultaneously eliminated L and R parental genomes, while some progeny produced only one type of sperm. We hypothesize that the reproductive variability of males producing two kinds of sperm is an adaptive mechanism to reproduce in mixed populations with P. ridibundus and may explain the extensive distribution of the all-male lineage across the European River Basin.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73043-1.Keywords: Crossing experiments, Spermatogenesis, Male hybridogenesis, Monospermic, Amphispermy, Micronuclei, FISH.Subject terms: Cytogenetics, Evolutionary biology, Genetic hybridization, Evolutionary genetics

Introduction

Gametogenesis is a fundamental process that ensures the formation of gametes and the transmission of genetic information to subsequent generations. While sexual organisms utilize meiosis with crossing-over to form recombinant gametes, asexual animals have developed alternative reproduction modes1–5. Modifications of classical gametogenesis can lead to unreduced ova, which are genetically identical to the mother. Without fertilization or karyogamy, these ova can give rise to parthenogenetic or gynogenetic offspring6–8. However, not all asexual animals transmit their entire genomes. During hybridogenesis, one parental genome is passed on while the other one is eliminated9–12. This process, known as hemiclonal reproduction, has been observed in the Bacillus stick insects13, topminnow Poeciliopsis monacha-lucida14, Iberian minnow Squalius alburnoides15, Hypseleotris Australian carp gudgeons16,17, Hexagrammos greenling18 and Pelophylax water frogs19–22. Despite these observations, the inheritance of these reproductive modes through generations of hybrids remains largely unknown.

The reproductive system in Pelophylax water frogs involves two hybridizing sexual species, P. lessonae (genotype LL) and P. ridibundus (genotype RR) and a hybrid P. esculentus (genotype RL), which reproduces hybridogenetically23–25. Male and female diploid hybrids produce haploid clonal gametes containing an unrecombined genome of only one parent, i.e. a complete set of either lessonae or ridibundus chromosomes26–28.

To maintain RL genotype in subsequent generations, hybrids must mate with a parental species whose genome has been eliminated25,29,30. In the L-E system, P. esculentus produces clonal haploid ridibundus gametes and mates with sympatric P. lessonae14,31,32. In contrast to the well-studied L-E system, the reverse R-E system involving sympatric occurrence of P. esculentus and P. ridibundus has received less attention. Populations exhibiting this system have been documented in Eastern Ukraine33–37 and Central Europe38–43. Eastern Ukrainian populations of P. esculentus include diploid and triploid individuals of both sexes33,36,37,44, while in Central Europe, populations only comprise diploid hybrid males coexisting with P. ridibundus. A common feature of the R-E system is the presence of hybrid males exhibiting two types of gametogenesis. The first type represents classic hybridogenesis with pre-meiotic elimination of the R genome and production of clonal L gametes (these males are referred to as monospermic males)15,36,43,45. The second type of hybrid males can simultaneously produce both L and R clonal gametes34–37,41,43,45,46. This phenomenon has been termed hybrid amphispermy35, and males exhibiting this trait are referred to as amphispermic males.

Genome elimination in P. esculentus typically occurs through the formation of micronuclei. This process takes place during early tadpole development and does not continue in adult animals31,36,47–50. The mechanisms underlying hybrid amphispermy, where both parental genomes persist in male gametogenesis, are still poorly understood. Vinogradov et al.35 proposed a mechanism in which different gonial cells selectively eliminate either the paternal or maternal genome, resulting in the production of two types of clonal gametes. Subsequently, Doležálková et al.41 observed numerous bivalents composed of chromosomes from both parental species in Central European amphispermic males and suggested that pre-meiotic elimination may be absent in these individuals, allowing both parental genomes to segregate into R/L spermatids during meiosis. More recently, Pustovalova et al.45 demonstrated the presence of premeiotic genome elimination and endoreplication in distinct germ cell populations of amphispermic diploid hybrid males from Eastern Ukraine. These findings suggest that hybrid individuals may exhibit varying gametogenic pathways, leading to the production of different types of gametes.

This study unravels the inheritance of gamete production in the exclusively male lineage of hybrid water frogs. We conducted a series of crossing experiments involving backcrossing hybrid males with sexual P. ridibundus, yielding B1 and subsequently B2 generations. Employing a range of methodological techniques, we investigated: (i) the type of parental genomic content in meiotic chromosomes, spermatids, and sperm of progeny, (ii) the genetic composition of the resulting progeny, (iii) the type of eliminated chromosomes in tadpole micronuclei associated with parental taxa.

Methods

Study species and sampling

The parental (P) generation consisted of three adult P. esculentus males from R-E populations along the Upper Oder River drainage, one P. esculentus female from L-E population, eight P. ridibundus females from two R-E populations, and three P. ridibundus females from pure R-R populations collected in the Elbe River drainage (Table S1). We analyzed 53 laboratory-raised juveniles within the B1 generation and 76 laboratory-raised juveniles within the B2 generation. Males were distinguished from females based on external and internal morphological characters51,52. Initial taxon identification was based on morphology, followed by genetic verification using microsatellite, mitochondrial and nuclear markers, as well as FISH karyotyping. After dissection, tissue samples were obtained from muscle or interdigital membranes and fixed in ethanol. Gonads were used for fertilization and fixed for meiotic chromosome preparations. All experimental procedures were conducted with the approval and under the supervision of the Ethical Committee of the School of Biology, Kharkiv National University, Kharkiv, Ukraine, following committee protocol No. 1/23 of March 15, 2023. Permission No. 358/2011 required for collecting frogs from wild populations was obtained from the Agency for Nature Conservation and Landscape Protection of the Czech Republic.

Design of crossing experiments

From 2018 to 2021, a series of 11 crossing experiments were conducted to investigate gamete production by hybrid males. The detailed crossing scheme can be found in Fig. 1; Table 1. In the initial round of crosses (parental P generation), three P. esculentus males from the R-E population were backcrossed with two P. ridibundus females. The resulting progeny, families 12-AB, 13-AB, and 14-AB, formed the B1 generation. Subsequently, in the second round of crosses, seven males from the B1 generation were backcrossed with another six P. ridibundus females and one P. esculentus female. This resulted in the formation of families 4-CD, 10-CD, 2-EF, 9-EF, 10-EF, 22-EF, 2-GH, and 16-GH, comprising the B2 generation.

Crosses were performed using the artificial fertilization procedure according to Berger et al.53 with some modifications provided in Doležálková-Kaštánková et al.54. Adult males were anaesthetized with a 2 mg/l MS-222 solution and dissected. A portion of the testis was used for fertilization and remaining portion of the testis was processed for preparation of meiotic metaphases. Fully metamorphosed progeny were divided into two groups: some were reared in outdoor cages for further observation, while the rest were anaesthetized with a 2 mg/l MS-222 solution and sex was determined by dissection. Tails and bodies of juveniles were fixed in ethanol for taxon identification, while gonads were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 1× PBS for subsequent microanatomy and analysis of the eliminated genome.

Taxa assignment using mitochondrial and nuclear markers

Taxonomic assignment of adult and juvenile frogs was accomplished using a combination of mitochondrial (ND2 gene) and nuclear markers (microsatellite loci, Albumin intron1, RAG1). DNA extraction was performed from muscle tissue or tails using a commercial Tissue DNA Isolation Kit (Geneaid Biotech, Taipei, Taiwan). Microsatellite markers were employed to identify species genotypes following the protocols outlined by Christiansen and Reyer55. Genotypes of individuals were determined by species-specificity of amplified alleles in 13 polymorphic microsatellite markers according to Doležálková-Kaštánková et al.15. Thirteen polymorphic microsatellite loci were selected: Multiplex 1 (Res14, RICA18, RICA1b5, RICA2a34, Ga1a19, RICA5, Rrid013A) and Multiplex 2 (Re1Caga10, Res22, RICA1b6, Re2Caga3, Rrid082A, Rrid169A). Species genotypes were determined based on the presence or absence of species-specific alleles amplified by these microsatellite markers. Fragment-length analysis was performed using an ABI 3730 Avant capillary sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California, USA) in conjunction with an internal size standard (GeneScan-500 LIZ, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Allele identification and scoring were carried out employing GeneMapper v. 3.7 software (Applied Biosystems, Zug, Switzerland). For B2 juveniles, an additional marker, RAG156 was utilized to assess their genotypes. PCR amplification was performed using primers Mart-FL1 and AMP-R1 rana, and the resulting products were digested with restriction enzyme DraII. Digested fragments were separated on a 1.5% agarose gel. Mitochondrial ND2 and nuclear Albumin intron1 markers were employed to genotype only P generation males and females that participated in the crosses. PCR conditions for these two markers followed the protocols57,58. ND2 sequence specificity was determined based on the presence or absence of polymorphic sites known in P. lessonae and P. ridibundus individuals (GenBank under Accession Nos. AM749727 and AM749715). The newly generated sequences were deposited in GenBank under Accession Nos. PP335547 – PP335550 and PP335554 – PP335558 for P. ridibundus and PP335541 – PP335553 and PP335558 for P. esculentus (Table S1). PCR products of Albumin intron1 are known to exhibit length polymorphism that varies between taxa59,60.

Preparation of chromosomal suspensions

To obtain high-quality meiotic chromosomes from the gonadal tissues of adult P, B1, and B2 males, we followed a modified protocol based on the method described by Zaleśna et al.28 and elaborated in Doležálková et al.41. The dissected gonads were hypotonized in 0.075 M KCl for 10–15 min, crushed through a fine strainer to get homogenized suspension. The suspension was fixed in a 3:1 methanol / acetic acid solution in four rounds (10, 20, 30, 60 min). Subsequently, aliquots of the fixed chromosomal suspension were dropped onto microscope slides, followed by staining with a 5% Giemsa solution (pH 6.8) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) to enhance chromosome visibility. The stained slides were meticulously examined using a Carl Zeiss AxioImager.Z2 (MetaSystems) microscope equipped with Metafer4 software to identify and select slides with representative meiotic figures. Finally, the selected slides were destained in the methanol-acetic acid fixative to remove excess Giemsa stain, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, and 96%, 3 min each), and stored at -20 °C for further FISH analysis. This protocol ensured the preparation of high-quality meiotic chromosomes from gonadal tissues of adult P, B1, and B2 males, suitable for subsequent fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) studies.

Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) and image processing

Fluorescent in situ hybridization was made on spermatid nuclei as well as on chromosomal mitotic and meiotic metaphases. Genomic DNAs of P. lessonae and P. ridibundus were used for probe preparation; probe to species-specific pericentromeric tandem repeat RrS1 detect P. ridibundus but not P. lessonae chromosomes59. RrS1 probe labelled with biotin-16-dUTP (Roche) was performed by PCR according to previously published protocol using following primers31: Forward: 5’-AAGCCGATTTTAGACAAGATTGC-3’; Reverse: 5’-GGCCTTTGGTTACCAAATGC-3’. We also used PlesSat01-48 probe labelled with digoxigenin-16-dUTP (Roche) which is specific only to the pericentromeric region of two pairs of P. lessonae chromosomes (nucleolus organizer regions (NOR)-bearing and acrocentric chromosomes)60. We used the following primers for PCR labelling of PlesSat01-48 probe probe: Forward: 5’-TTTGGCTTCCAAGGGCCGGG-3; Reverse: 5’-TGACCAAAAACGACACTCCC-3. Hybridization mixture included 50% formamide, 10% dextran sulfate, 2× Saline Sodium Citrate (ЅЅС), 5 ng/µl of labeled probe and 10–50-fold excess of salmon sperm DNA. We used either only RrS1 probe or a mixture of RrS1 and PlesSat01-48 probes. Common denaturation of probe and target DNA on slides was performed at 75 ºC for five minutes. After 12–24 h of incubation at room temperature (RT) slides were washed in 0.2× SSC (saline-sodium citrate buffer) at 60 ºC. Biotin was detected by streptavidin conjugated with fluorochrome Alexa488 (Invitrogen, San Diego, Calif., USA); digoxigenin was detected by anti-digoxigenin antibodies conjugated with rhodamine (Invitrogen, San Diego, CA, USA). Afterwards, slides were placed in a series of ethanol (50, 70 and 96%), dried and counterstained with Vectashield/DAPI (1.5 mg/ml) (Vector, Burlingame, Calif., USA). Chromosomes with hybridization signals were inspected using a Provis AX70 (Olympus) fluorescence microscope equipped with standard fluorescence filter sets. Selected images for each fluorescent dye were captured separately with a black and white CCD camera (DP30BW Olympus) using Olympus Acquisition Software. The digital images were then pseudocoloured (blue for DAPI, red for rhodamine, green for Alexa488) and imposed using MicroImage software (Olympus, version 4.0). Further figure adjustment was performed in Photoshop CC2023 24.1.1 software.

Whole-mount FISH and analysis

Whole-mount FISH was performed on gonads dissected from tadpoles according to Dedukh et al.48. Gonadal tissues were incubated in 0.5% solution of Triton X100 in 1× PBS for 4–5 h, washed in 1× PBS and impregnated in 50% formamide, 10% dextran sulfate, and 2× SSC for 3–4 h at 37 °C. Hybridization was performed either only with probe to RrS1 (20 ng/µl) or with the usage of the mixture of probes to RrS1 and PlesSat01-48 repeats (10 ng/µl of each probe). Hybridization mixture contained 50% formamide, 2× SSC and 10% dextran sulfate and salmon sperm DNA (with 10–50-fold excess compared to the probe concentration). After denaturation of tissues at 72 °C for 15 min and their incubation for at least 24 h at RT, tissues were washed three times in 0.2× SSC at 50 °C for 15 min each. Afterwards tissues were transferred in 1% blocking solution (Roche) prepared in 4× SSC for 1 h at RT. Biotin and digoxigenin were detected by streptavidin conjugated with Alexa 488 (Invitrogen, San Diego, CA, USA) and anti-digoxigenin antibodies conjugated with rhodamine (Invitrogen, San Diego, CA, USA), correspondingly. After incubation with streptavidin and rhodamine overnight at RT, tissues were washed in 4× SSC for 15 min tissues and stained with DAPI (1 µg/µl) (Sigma) prepared in 1× PBS at RT overnight. Prior examination, tissues were placed in a drop of Vectashield antifade solution and analyzed using Leica TCS SP5 confocal laser scanning microscope based on the inverted microscope Leica DMI 6000 CS (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Diode, argon and helium-neon lasers were used to excite the fluorescent dyes DAPI, fluorochromes Alexa488 and rhodamine, respectively. LAS AF software (Leica Microsystems, Germany) was used for image capture and processing. Based on the FISH signals, spermatids with two L signals and weak R signals (based on the exposition value) were evaluated as haploid L spermatids; spermatids with no L and strong R signals were assessed as haploid R spermatids; and spermatids with one and three signals of L probe together with strong or weak signals of R probe were assessed as aneuploid. Metaphases with a combination of R and L univalents and metaphase plates exhibiting abnormal pairing of R and L chromosomes in bivalents were classified as unusual. Metaphase plates with incomplete chromosome numbers (less than 13 in haploid stages or less than 26 in diploid stages) were identified as aneuploid. Based on previous studies of various water frog populations31,48, we conservatively estimate a rate of 15% for “mistakes” in micronuclei with incorrectly eliminated genomes. In these populations, cells with incorrectly eliminated chromosomes did not produce fertile gametes and were not represented in the progeny. In our case, we use a threshold of monospermic males as of those with observed up to 15% of R : L spermatids.

Flow-cytometry of the sperm

To analyze sperm DNA content using flow cytometry, a few drops of sperm suspension fixed in 70% ethanol were mixed with 1 ml of nuclear lysis buffer (LB01) to release the nuclei. This mixture was incubated for 10 min to ensure efficient nuclear extraction. The suspension containing the released nuclei was then filtered through a 0.22-µm filter to remove any debris or cellular contaminants. To stain the DNA, 2 µl of RNase A (ribonuclease A) was added to the filtered suspension to degrade RNA, which could interfere with the DNA binding dye. Subsequently, 50 µl of propidium iodide (PI) was incorporated to allow for fluorescence-based DNA quantification (PI)61. The stained suspension was incubated for 10 min on ice to allow the PI to bind to the DNA. Finally, the stained sperm suspension was analyzed on a flow cytometer (CytoFlex, Beckman Coulter). The sorter’s software was employed to measure the relative fluorescence intensity of the histogram peak, reflecting the DNA content of the sperm cells. The coefficient of variation (CV) value, which indicates the spread of the fluorescence intensity distribution, was also assessed. Histograms with 5,000 events were captured for each sample to provide representative data for the sperm population.

Results

In an effort to unravel the inheritance of asexuality in P. esculentus males from Central-European R-E populations, we have conducted three-generation experimental crosses, followed by the genotyping of the progeny and a detailed analysis of gametogenesis in adult males and their progenitors.

Taxon determination and genetic diversity

To accurately determine the taxonomic identities of adult and juvenile individuals, we employed a combination of microsatellite allele profiling and mitochondrial and nuclear gene sequencing. Microsatellite analysis revealed the presence of R and L alleles, representing eight P. ridibundus genotypes and 73 P. esculentus genotypes, as detailed in Table S2. Species specificity of amplified alleles is meticulously described in Table S3. Leveraging GenAlex software, we identified two distinct allelic profiles: The R-profile characterized a diverse assortment of amplified R alleles, and this profile was observed in individuals possessing the ridibundus genome, including adult P. ridibundus females and P. esculentus males/females (Figure S1); The L-profile was discovered in individuals harbouring the lessonae genome, specifically P. esculentus males and one P. esculentus female. In stark contrast to the variable R alleles, all hybrid males (P fathers, B1 progeny, B2 progeny) exhibited identical L alleles across all microsatellite loci, constituting a single multilocus genotype (L-profile; Figure S1). This finding strongly indicates the clonal inheritance of the lessonae genome in hybrid males. The presence of either one or two alleles per locus (Table S2, data available from the Dryad Digital Repository) firmly established the diploid nature of all analyzed individuals. Moreover, no allele-dosage effects suggestive of polyploidy were observed. In 60 juveniles analyzed, electrophoresis of digested RAG1-PCR products yielded fragments of varying lengths. The most prevalent fragments, 936 bp, 565 bp, and 371 bp, perfectly aligned with the species-specific profiles of hybrid P. esculentus genotype.

Mitochondrial sequence variation of the ND2 gene revealed two types of mitochondrial sequences in females used for crosses and males from the P generation: P. ridibundus mtDNA in six RR females and three hybrid males, and P. lessonae mtDNA in two RR and one hybrid females (Table S1). Length polymorphism of the nuclear marker Albumin intron1 was visualized on an agarose gel and revealed the length of 1050 bp corresponding to ridibundus alleles, 850 bp corresponding to kurtmuelleri (K) alleles, and 450 bp corresponding to lessonae alleles. Of the nine females analyzed, two had two RR alleles, five had a combination of R and K alleles, one had two KK alleles, and a single P. esculentus female had both R and L alleles (Table 1).

Characterization of progeny within experimental crosses

This study employed a three-generation cross experiment involving three P-generation males (M1, M2, M3). The first round of crosses paired the P-generation males with two P. ridibundus females (F1, F2) possessing unique mtDNA types and varying levels of P. kurtmuelleri introgression in their nuclear DNA (Fig. 1; Table 1). This resulted in three B1 families (12-AB, 13-AB, 14-AB) with 349 juveniles that attained stage 25 according to Gosner62. Seven B1 juveniles were selected and reared to maturity. Mature B1 males were backcrossed with six P. ridibundus and one P. esculentus females (F3 - F9) to produce eight B2 families with 591 juveniles (Tables 1 and S4). Sex determination and genotyping of the B1 and B2 juveniles revealed that all genotyped progeny were hybrids with male sex only. Thirteen analyzed families from the B1 and B2 generations exhibited significant mortality during embryogenesis and subsequent tadpole development. Embryonic mortality in early developmental stages ranged between 44.9% and 99.1% (Table S4).

In situ hybridization on meiotic cells and spermatids

Using FISH with species-specific probes to chromosomes of P. ridibundus (RrS1) and P. lessonae (PlesSat01-48), we identified genome composition of 645 meiotic chromosomal plates and 778 spermatids of 12 hybrid males from P, B1 and B2 generations (Tables 1, 2 and 3). We detected five males that produced both types of gametes L and R simultaneously, i.e. were amphispermic. In three amphispermic B2 males (22-EF1, 10-EF1, 10-EF2), we found a similar proportion of L and R spermatids (even in the left and right gonad separately, see Table S5), while two of the analyzed B1 males had a prevalence of L or R spermatids (75% of L spermatids in 13-AB1, 57.8% of R spermatids in 14-AB9). In addition, in four of these amphispermic males (22-EF1, 10-EF1, 10-EF2, 14-AB9), we found quite a high ratio of aneuploid spermatids (25.3–54.5%) with one or three L signals (Table 2; Fig. 2i). Other seven hybrid males (14-AB4, 12-AB2, 12-AB3, 14-AB3, 14-AB1, M1, 2-EF1) were designated as monospermic because the vast majority of spermatids contained the L genome. When we excluded aneuploid spermatids that were quite frequent (13.3–60.9%), the ratio of produced L spermatids ranged from 85 to 100%. One of the hybrids, specifically 2-EF1, produced 100% of spermatids with the L genome exclusively.

Our analysis of spermatocytes I and II revealed phases characterized by 13 bivalents, a typical pattern for normal pairing, and phases featuring 13/26 univalents of L or R chromosomes. Stages with 13 univalents corresponded either to the premeiotic haploid stage following chromosome elimination or the haploid stage after chromosome segregation in the second meiotic division. Stages with 26 chromosomes (L univalents) were indicative of either the diploid stage after premeiotic elimination of R and duplication of L or the diploid prophase in the second meiotic division. We also observed unusual metaphases with a combination of R and L univalents and metaphase plates exhibiting abnormal pairing of R and L chromosomes in bivalents (Fig. 2c, f), as well as aneuploid metaphase plates.

Investigation of meiotic plates in amphispermic males (13-AB1, 14-AB9, 22-EF1, 10-EF1, 10-EF2) revealed a lack of meiotic plates with 13 bivalents, instead presenting rare configurations. Notably, we observed plates with unpaired 26 R + L univalents (up to 25.0%) and plates with paired R + L bivalents + univalents (up to 40.6%). Furthermore, meiotic plates with 13/26 univalents of L or R chromosomes were detected (75.0% with L genome in 14-AB9, 39.4% with L genome and 3.0% with R genome in 13-AB1) (Table 2; Fig. 2d, f), suggesting premeiotic elimination of one parental genome. Aneuploid metaphases were frequently observed in amphispermic males, with a maximum rate of 63.4% detected in the left gonad of male 10-EF2. In contrast, the proportion of aneuploid metaphases in monospermic males was significantly lower (up to 17.9%), except for male 2-EF1, which produced 40.0% of aneuploid plates. Additionally, monospermic males exhibited higher frequencies of 13/26 univalents with a dominance of L chromosomes (10.5–80.0%). Five of these males (12-AB2, 12-AB3, 14-AB3, 14-AB1, 2-EF1) also produced meiotic plates with 13 bivalents (up to 3.7% of R bivalents, up to 32.5% of L bivalents), indicative of genome elimination and duplication. The proportion of unusual meiotic phases (up to 19.3% of plates with R + L univalents and up to 46.4% of plates with R + L bivalents + univalents) in these monospermic males was comparable to the values observed in amphispermic males (Table 2).

Micronuclei analysis

To investigate the ability of hybrids to undergo genome elimination, we analyzed the gonadal microanatomy of 22 tadpoles from seven crosses (4-CD, 10-CD, 9-EF, 2-EF, 22-EF, 16-GH, 2-GH) (Table 3). All analyzed hybrids exhibited a uniform distribution of germ cells throughout the gonad (Fig. 3). Within the cytoplasm of germ cells in all tadpoles, we observed micronuclei, which are indicative of premeiotic genome elimination. By employing whole-mount FISH with species-specific probes, we identified the parental species’ genomes in micronuclei and gonocytes. Micronuclei containing RrS1 signal were designated as those bearing R chromosomes, while micronuclei lacking RrS1 signal or exhibiting PlesSat01-48 signal were designated as bearing L chromosomes. In a vast majority of micronuclei showing signals from both L and R probes, we observed only a single signal per micronucleus, suggesting that each micronucleus typically contains only one chromosome. In five tadpoles from two crosses (2-EF, 16-GH), we detected R chromosomes in 87.5–93% of all micronuclei, suggesting preferential elimination of the P. ridibundus genome. As these tadpoles selectively eliminated R chromosomes in the majority of micronuclei, we classified them as monospermic. In another 15 tadpoles from six crosses (4-CD, 10-CD, 9-EF, 22-EF, 2-GH, 16-GH), we observed elimination of R chromosomes in 40–80% and elimination of L chromosomes in 21–60% of all micronuclei. As these tadpoles eliminated both genomes in micronuclei, we designated them as amphispermic. In five crosses (4-CD, 10-CD, 9-EF, 22-EF, 2-GH), all tadpoles were amphispermic, while in one cross (2-EF), all analyzed tadpoles were monospermic. Notably, in one cross (16-GH), one tadpole was amphispermic, while the other was monospermic. In interphase nuclei, we detected a reduced number of R or L chromosomes in different germ cells. This observation suggests that R and L genomes were eliminated within distinct populations of germ cells.

Flow-cytometry of sperm

Using fixed cell suspensions from three males (12-AB3, 12-AB5 and M3) we measured DNA content in the sperm. As P. ridibundus genome contains 8–16% more DNA than the P. lessonae genome63, we were able to distinguish L and R sperm in the suspension from testes of studied males. B1 males from the family 12-AB (12-AB3 and 12-AB5) showed signals of L sperm while P-male M3 had doubled signals of L and R sperm indicating amphispermy (see Fig. 4). To verify the doubled signal of the amphispermic male M3, we compared its profile with an earlier confirmed amphispermic male that produced two types of sperm leading to RR and RL progeny (male M11 in a publication46).

Discussion

Combining crossing experiments and cytogenetic analysis of the meiosis, we have revealed visually irregular inheritance patterns of reproduction across three consecutive generations of P. esculentus. Despite the challenges they face due to their hybrid origin and potential infertility in a heterogamic sex64, studied male water frogs exhibited the remarkable ability to produce fertile gametes. The all-male lineage of P. esculentus stands as an extraordinary evolutionary example in the animal kingdom, defying the deeply ingrained machinery of eukaryotic meiosis to establish a thriving male lineage. Despite the absence of conspecific females, this lineage perpetuates through diverse modes of male hybridogenesis, showcasing its remarkable resilience and adaptability.

Gamete production of hybrid males

Combining sex determination through gonadal analysis with microsatellite profiling genotypes, we unequivocally demonstrated that all B1 and B2 progenies were solely composed of hybrid P. esculentus genotypes and exclusively of male sex. The allelic profiles of these hybrids exhibited no evidence of recombination between L and R genomes, strongly supporting the clonal inheritance of one of the parental genomes. Furthermore, the absence of P. ridibundus genotypes in the progeny suggests that all crossed males produced exclusively fertile clonal lessonae sperm through gametogenesis involving premeiotic elimination.

Nevertheless, the results from in situ hybridization showed the ability of hybrid fathers to produce different types of clonal spermatids (Fig. 2g-j). By the identification of P. ridibudnus and P. lessonae genomes in meiosis and in spermatids, we clearly identified two types of gametogenesis in diploid hybrid males. Firstly, we observed the formation of haploid spermatids with L genome suggesting premeiotic elimination of R genome typical for monospermic males. Secondly, we detected simultaneous formation of L and R spermatids that was confirmed by flow cytometry of mature sperms. Such hybrid amphispermy was observed in hybrid males from Czech, Ukrainian and German R-E systems35–37,43,45,46,65.

Cytogenetic and molecular methods applied to individuals of different ontogenetic stages allowed us to track amphispermic inheritance through three generations. Earlier, amphispermic males were detected using various approaches, including enzymatic activity of allozyme alleles65, morphometric characters66, flow cytometry of sperm suspensions35,37, meiotic analysis41,45 and genotyping of progeny from experimental crosses16,36. Amphispermic males were also found in E systems16,66, but they are quite rare in such systems. Only in R-E systems, they play a significant role, transmitting the L genome throughout their gametes37,45,46.

Intriguingly, the simultaneous production of both R and L eggs in females is an exceptionally rare occurrence. Diploid hybrid females frequently produce a combination of haploid R eggs and diploid RL eggs. Diploid eggs are essential for the formation of triploid hybrids in various L-E, R-E, and E systems14,36,44,55,67. Nonetheless, a few instances of hybrid females producing both R and L oocytes have been documented. For example, Dedukh et al.16 identified one hybrid female in the E-E system in Western Poland, and Biriuk et al.37 detected such a female using enzyme electrophoresis of the LDH1 allozyme marker. Atypical expression of L-specific alleles in R oocytes could also stem from DNA introgression. Several studies have documented interspecies introgression of lessonae genes into P. ridibundus genomes43,68–72. Therefore, it is plausible to assume that not all previously published results were accurate and that the ability to inherit two types of clonal gametes is a trait exclusive to hybrid males, typically from R-E systems.

Gametogenesis of B2 generation

In B2 generation tadpoles, the analysis of micronuclei formation revealed that genome elimination occurs via premeiotic elimination and likely chromosomal lagging during anaphase. Micronuclei with R and L chromosomes suggest premeiotic elimination of different genomes (Fig. 3a, b). Mature B2 males exhibit both R and L genomes in meiotic phases, mainly as 26 univalents or a mixture of bivalents and univalents (Fig. 2a-f). Occasional spermatocytes with 13 bivalents of P. ridibundus and P. lessonae chromosomes were also observed (Fig. 2g-j). The persistent occurrence of both genomes in spermatocytes with 26 univalents and a mixture of bivalents and univalents indicated a deviation from standard hybridogenesis and might signify an absence of premeiotic elimination in amphispermic males. Nevertheless, the existence of micronuclei in B2 juveniles contradicts this hypothesis. Hence, amphispermic males likely possess multiple germ cell types, including those with premeiotic elimination of R/L genomes and those without elimination with RL combinations (Fig. 2a-f). The process of spermatogenesis in amphispermic males is not fully understood and the question which germ cells results in fully fertile sperm remains. Despite high mortality rates and increased aneuploidy54, see also Table S4), amphispermic males can produce fertile sperm and reproduce successfully. The lack of observed introgression between R and L genomes in our progeny suggests that spermatocytes with RL bivalents (Fig. 2c, f) and univalents may have difficulties completing spermatogenesis and turning into fertile sperm.

Inheritance of amphispermy

Through a series of FISH experiments on testes of tadpoles and adult frogs, as well as flow cytometry of sperm, we have uncovered the inheritance patterns of asexual gametogenesis in hybrid males across generations. Our findings reveal that amphispermic males can produce monospermic hybrid sons that exclusively transmit lessonae gametes. Additionally, we observed that monospermic males can also produce amphispermic hybrid sons capable of forming both R and L gametes simultaneously (Fig. 1). These results indicate that gametogenic alterations are not inherited from diploid hybrid males to their hybrid offspring. Considering that all hybrid males belong to a single hemiclonal lineage and share the same clonal lessonae genome, the only P. ridibundus genome variation stems from the P. ridibundus mothers in each generation. Therefore, we hypothesize that the mother’s genome may influence the type of reproduction mode in the progeny. It is worth noting that hybrid progeny inherits new mtDNA from their mothers, which could potentially affect the type of gametogenesis as well. However, our tracking of mtDNAs in the progenies did not reveal any direct impact on the type of reproduction in juveniles.

According to prevailing assumptions, the P. ridibundus genome triggers elimination in hybridogenetic water frogs from L-E populations48,73,74. In these populations, each hybrid individual eliminates the L genome, a mode of gametogenesis inherited from generation to generation. However, in R-E systems, the ridibundus genome must be eliminated to maintain hybrid genotypes in the population. In our monophyletic hybrid male lineage15, the interaction between the mother’s R genome and the clonal L genome can result in either the elimination of the R genome or the mutual elimination of both the L and R genomes. Since two siblings from the same parents exhibited different types of gametogenesis (one was monospermic, the other amphispermic, see also Fig. 1; Table 3), we can infer that elimination is not influenced by the mother’s entire R genome but rather by specific R-specific alleles transmitted to the gametes. The type of mitochondrial DNA and the rate of P. kurtmuelleri introgression in nuclear DNA did not significantly impact the type of asexual gametogenesis.

Mortality, aneuploidy and selection against RR progeny

We observed generally high mortality during embryogenesis (44.9–99.1% dying embryos) in arisen families. High mortality rates have been observed in several studies investigating hybrid mortality in experimental crosses24,31,36,43,46,70,75. This mortality can be attributed to a range of factors, from unfavorable environmental conditions to genetically determined mechanisms3,76. The high mortality observed in our crosses is likely linked to intergenomic incompatibilities between the two parental genomes, lessonae and ridibundus, which have previously been shown to affect individual viability54.

Examination of hybrid gonads revealed a high frequency of aneuploid meiotic phases and aneuploid spermatids (up to 60%), affecting a majority of males. Aneuploidy during gonocyte development typically leads to aneuploid meiotic figures, resulting in abnormal sperm formation (Fig. 2g, j) and a strong correlation with reduced embryonic survival. Additionally, high levels of aneuploidy during gonocyte multiplication were frequently observed during early spermatogenesis and spermatogenesis in adult males from E systems, contributing to decreased fertility50. In diploid and triploid hybrid males, this is linked to alterations in genome elimination and endoreplication pathways50. In addition to an increased number of aneuploid phases, we also observed unusual pairing of R and L chromosomes in the late prophase of amphispermic males. These findings align with previous observations of so-called pseudobivalents41 identified in adult hybrid P. esculentus males originating from the same region as the hybrid fathers used in this study. It is established that meiosis in hybrids can be negatively influenced by the genetic variability of the two parental genomes. For instance, a karyological analysis by Biriuk et al.77 confirmed that P. esculentus exhibits significantly lower proportions of standard bivalents compared to sexual species (spermatocytes I with a normal karyotype of 13 bivalents). A similar case was presented by Ragghianti et al.65, who demonstrated atypical pairing patterns on meiotic chromosomes of hybrid males.

Contrary to our findings from the meiotic examination of amphispermic males from R-E systems in Eastern Ukraine, spermatocytes with RL genomes were either scarce or absent. During gametogenesis of diploid hybrid males from Eastern Ukraine, a high number of spermatocytes with 13 bivalents of P. ridibundus and P. lessonae were observed45. This observation implies that premeiotic genome elimination of R and L genomes in different germ cell populations is more precise and widespread in amphispermic males from Eastern Ukraine. We deduce that the genetic basis underlying hybrid amphispermy may differ in diploid hybrid males from various R-E systems. Prior findings support this notion that amphispermic hybrids in the R-E system in Central Europe share a common origin15.

In our crosses, we observed only the hybrid genotype (RL) in the offsprings of both B1 and B2 generations. Genetic profiling of the progeny suggests that all hybrid males that were crossed transmitted only the lessonae genome to their gametes. It has been shown that hybrid males from Oder River populations can clonally transmit both parental genomes to their offspring46. Therefore, it is possible that RR genotypes may have appeared in the offspring even though they have not reached later developmental stages. It is challenging to determine whether the high mortality in RR offspring is due to genetic incompatibilities between the two R genomes or some post-zygotic selection. Studies examining the reduced viability of RR offspring36,37,74,77–80 have always relied on results from homotypic crosses (P. esculentus × P. esculentus). The combination of two clonally inherited genomes is typically associated with the accumulation of mutations. Guex et al.81 hypothesized that these deleterious mutations could be responsible for abnormal larval development and high mortality in such sexual progenitors. However, the ontogenetic co-operation between two R genomes in backcrossed RR progeny has never been investigated.

Conclusion

Examination of hybridogenetic water frog hybrid males through artificial crosses revealed a non-heritable pattern of male hybridogenetic gametogenesis. Diploid hybrid males, representing a monophyletic hybrid lineage15, possess at least two distinct types of clonal gamete production, namely monospermy and amphispermy. However, our integrative approach demonstrated that neither of these strategies is directly inherited by subsequent generations. We hypothesize that the decision of the new male generation regarding the mode of gametogenesis activation may be influenced by specific alleles in the R genome, inherited from the P. ridibundus mother for a single generation, rather than by the L genome clonally inherited across generations within the lineage. Considering the high mortality and presumed selection against RR progeny, we hypothesize that amphispermic males dominate this lineage. Despite the diverse mechanisms of clonal gamete production, both modes are expected to function effectively in the R-E system across Central Europe.
