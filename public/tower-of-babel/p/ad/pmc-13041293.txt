Social media use and associated food-related habits among Kenyan teenagers: a cross-sectional study of digital influence on public health nutrition
Authors: Moses Amram Kutwah, Dorcus Mbithe David-Kigaru, Joseph Kobia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial media use is increasingly influencing lifestyle choices and, notably, dietary choices and food-related practices of teenagers. This study aimed to determine the extent and nature of social media use and its associated food-related habits among teenagers in Kenya.MethodsThe study employed a cross-sectional study design incorporating both quantitative and qualitative techniques. The study was conducted in the metropolitan area of Mulolongo, Machakos County, Kenya. Using proportionate and simple random techniques, the study sampled 357 teenagers, 13–19 years old. The study utilized structured questionnaires administered in face-to-face interviews, alongside focus group discussions for data collection. Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis, while quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive, bivariate, and multivariate logistic regression analysis.ResultsFindings revealed that (72.5%) of teenagers use social media, averaging 2.65 ± 0.13 hours on weekdays and 3.08 ± 0.14 hours on weekends on social media, with WhatsApp, Instagram, and TikTok being the most frequently used platforms. Usage patterns showed a statistically significant gender difference, p < 0.05. Social media was rarely used to seek health or nutritional information (0.8%). While on social media (34.5%) of teenagers watch food-related videos, (32.8%) snack while browsing and (54.3%), reported exposure to food advertisements, with fast food ads being the most common. Notably, access to WhatsApp (Crude O.R. = 4.41, 95% CI: 1.57–12.39, p < 0.001) and Instagram (Crude O.R. = 4.22, 95% CI: 1.18–15.08, p = 0.02) was associated with higher odds of being underweight, while exposure to food advertisements was associated with both underweight (Crude O.R. = 0.48, 95% CI: 1.22–2.05, p = 0.04) and overweight risks (Adjusted O.R. = 4.4, 95% CI: 1.90–21.73, p = 0.04). Qualitative insights substantiated quantitative findings, asserting that participants’ food choices were influenced by aspirational consumption patterns linked to online trends, habitual snacking during digital engagement, and intensified exposure to food-related advertisements and promotions, particularly for fast foods and snacks, across social media platforms.ConclusionSocial media use among teenagers is linked to both underweight and overweight status. The pervasive influence of food advertising, specifically energy-dense, nutrient-deficient food, shapes unhealthy practices, while limited exposure to credible health content signals a missed opportunity. Public health action should focus on regulating online food advertisements and leveraging social media for nutrition education, and strengthening teenagers’ digital literacy to advance healthy eating behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01276-6.

Introduction

Social media has emerged as a dominant force shaping teenage behaviors, including food-related habits. Social media sites like YouTube, TikTok, and Instagram expose teenagers to frequent reels of food content covering cooking lessons and diet trends to fast food advertisements and promotions. This digital influence overlaps with formative years of growth and development, peer influence and attachment in food choices, making this age group vulnerable to triggers prone to influence their dietary behaviors [1].

Globally, children and adolescents are the most active social media users. By 2025, over 90% of teenagers in developed countries report daily use of at least one social media site, with average screen time surpassing three hours per day [2]. In Africa, the digital space is quickly growing. Youth aged 13–20 form the majority of new internet users, with mobile internet driving this engagement. Despite infrastructural gaps, countries like Nigeria, South Africa and even Kenya are experiencing a sharp increase in adolescent connectivity [3].

In the East African region, Kenya is leading with high digital engagement among youth. According to Digital Data Reports, Kenya had over 10 million active social media users, with teenagers constituting a substantial proportion [4]. Social media sites like WhatsApp, Facebook and TikTok lead in usage, with rising engagement in food-related content. As of early 2025, Kenya had 15.1 million active social media users, accounting for 26.5% of the Kenyan population, with the youth being the predominant users of social media [4].

Social media influences teenagers’ food choices and dietary behaviors through various mechanisms. These include peer-to-peer influence, celebrities and algorithmic exposure, which reinforce food preferences through targeted content and emotional attachment where food is connected to mood, identity, or social belonging. And so, visual appeal, convenience, and perceived popularity regularly outweigh nutritional considerations, thereby leading to preferences for unhealthy foods and increased consumption of high-calorie and poor diets characterized by nutrient deficiency [5]. Long hours on social media among children and adolescents, greater than 2 hours daily, have been associated with unhealthy eating habits, sedentary lifestyles, and an increased risk of being overweight and obese [6, 7]. These outcomes stem from behavioral shifts that reduce time allocated for physical activity, there is increased exposure to unhealthy food advertisements and peer-fixated norms that reinforce poor food choices. Consequently, reduced time for family interactions undermines emotional well-being and possibly moderates protective influences on eating habits.

Empirical evidences consistently reveal that social media exposure is interlinked to poor food choices and unhealthy dietary habits of teenagers. A systematic review compiling data from 41 countries involving adolescents found that excessive social media use was associated with unhealthy dietary behaviors, including skipping meals, snacking and exposure to sugar-sweetened beverages [8]. This evidence is consistent with research emerging in East Africa, which shows the influential role of digital food marketing and influencer culture in shaping perceptions of what is desirable to eat [9]. Notably, exposure to non-core (unhealthy) food messages was linked to increased intake of these foods, mediated by perceived social norms, while exposure to core (healthy) food messages was associated with higher food literacy and increased consumption of fruits and vegetables [10, 11].

Social media can support healthy teenagers’ dietary behaviors. Programs that promote fitness, peer-to-peer health challenges and cooking lessons offer promising avenues for disseminating nutrition-related content. However, the impact of such content is dependent upon parental control, digital literacy and school-based nutrition education. Parental control can moderate exposure to unhealthy food content and guide healthier decision-making. Digital literacy enhances teenagers’ ability to critically evaluate persuasive food content and peer norms online. School-based nutrition education reinforces informed eating principles and counters misinformation from online interactions. Together, these mechanisms can build resilience against unhealthy dietary influences from social media and transform it for health promotion.

Emerging evidence provides socioeconomic, gender-based and rural–urban inequalities in social media access and associated food-related habits among adolescents in Kenya and the broader East Africa region [5, 12]. Adolescents in urban, high-income settings benefit from better smartphone ownership, digital literacy and reliable internet connectivity, enhancing more social media access. Conversely, those in rural and low-income settings face connectivity challenges with inadequate digital infrastructure and technological skills gaps. These digital divides result in unequal opportunities to educational, social and economic benefits from social media use.

Furthermore, the rapid urbanization and shifting food environments in Kenya’s metropolitan region have augmented existing social inequalities. Limited access to safe and open spaces for physical activity has resulted in increased sedentary behavior as more time is spent online. This transition, coupled with the repeated exposure to online food advertisements, has contributed to unhealthy dietary behaviors and intensified the risk of obesity and non-communicable diseases. While gender differences in social media use are less pronounced in Kenya, girls are more inclined to visually focused platforms such as Instagram, driven partly by social affirmation. In contrast, boys are more inclined towards entertainment and gaming sites [13, 14].

Despite global insights, Kenya lacks disaggregated data on how social media is interlinked to teenagers’ food choices, consumption patterns and dietary behaviors. Existing literature focuses largely on social media access and general youth behavior, with partial investigation of nutrition-specific outcomes [5, 12, 15]. This gap hampers the formulation of evidence-based policies and targeted interventions towards adolescent nutrition, particularly in urbanized settings where digital influence wields significant power. Therefore, the rationale for this research arose from the urgent need to examine; (a) the digital gadgets teenagers use to access the internet, their internet connectivity and the types and patterns of social media platforms use; (b) the nature and exposure to food-related content and its influence on eating habits of the teenagers; (c) the broader implication of social media use on teenagers’ nutritional status within the metropolitan region in Kenya. With increasing rates of overweight, undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies among children and adolescents, unravelling the influence of social media sheds light on the provision of more targeted and culturally relevant interventions. Furthermore, in alignment with Kenya’s digital health and adolescent health policy frameworks, this research provides empirical support for integrated behavioral change interventions. The purpose of this study was to establish the influence of social media use and associated food-related habits among teenagers in Mlolongo, Machakos County, Kenya.

Methods

Research design and location of study

This research employed a cross-sectional analytical design. The investigation was conducted in Mulolongo Ward, located within Mavoko Sub-County, Machakos County. According to 2019 demographic data, children aged 0–14 years accounted for 32.6% of the county's total population (N = 1,421,932), while youths aged 15–34 comprised 35.8%. Mavoko Sub-County was purposively selected for being the most populous among the nine sub-counties, representing 22.6% of Machakos County's total population. This demographic population is linked to the area's role as an industrial hub, rapid infrastructure and real estate development, and its proximity to Nairobi City, which enhances access to Wi-Fi internet services. Similarly, Mulolongo Ward was selected purposively as the smallest yet most densely populated ward within Mavoko Sub-County. The ward borders Nairobi County, where most of its populace works. The ward's infrastructure supports widespread free Wi-Fi accessibility for residents [16].

Study population

The primary study population was teenagers, 13 to 19 years old, in Mulolongo Ward, Mavoko Sub-County. Parents of the teenagers formed the secondary study population. All respondents over 18 years provided written informed consent to participate in the study. For participants below 18 years, written informed consent was provided by a parent or legal guardian and the minor provided written assent before administration of the questionnaire.

Sampling procedure

Mulolongo ward is administratively divided into four Community Health Units (CHUs). To decide which unit to start with, proportionate sampling was used to subdivide the 4 CHUs based on population density, where Mulolongo A and B were clustered together as densely populated. In contrast, Sabaki and Syokimau were clustered together as less densely populated. A ratio of 3:2 was used to subdivide the two regions. This was based on the recent 2022 national elections statistics. Proportionate sampling ensured that the sample drawn accurately reflected the distribution of key subgroups within the target population. This enhanced the representativeness and generalizability of our findings. With the help of CHPs and community elders, the research team moved to the centre of the CHUs. A simple random sampling technique was applied to select households with teenagers aged 13–19 years who participated in the study. Once the enumerators reached the midpoint of the selected CHU, a simple random sampling technique was applied to select households with teenagers aged 13–19 years who participated in the study. The spin bottle method was used to identify the first household with a teenager to interview. In this method, the enumerators identified a flat surface and spun the bottle until it took the direction facing the mouth of the bottle. The first teenager in the household in that direction was selected, and assent and consent were first sought where necessary. In households with multiple eligible teenagers, the enumerators followed a structured draw-lots procedure. First, all participants meeting the study inclusion criteria, age range, and who consented were identified. Each eligible individual was then assigned a unique identification number. These numbers were written on identical slips of paper, folded to obscure their contents, and placed into a bowl. A single slip was drawn blindly, and the teenager who corresponded to the selected number was enrolled to participate in the study. The number of eligible individuals and the outcome of the draw were documented. This method upheld the principles of fairness, transparency, and equal probability of selection. Then, the team administered the questionnaire and took anthropometric measurements of the teenager. Once the first teenager was fully assessed, the enumerators repeated the spin bottle method outside of the already sampled household to get the direction of the next household. An enumerator who reached the end of the village/estate before completing the numbers required walked back to the centre of the CHU and spun the bottle again. If the enumerator double-selected a previously sampled household, that household was excluded, and the exercise was repeated until all eligible households with teenagers were sampled.

Data collection tools and data collection procedures

Structured questionnaire and digital data capture

A structured questionnaire was administered to study respondents, capturing demographic data, social media use, and associated food-related habits among the teenagers when accessing social media. (Supplementary file 1). Quantitative data collection was conducted using the KoBo Toolbox platform, which facilitated digital administration of the questionnaire. Research team members installed the KoBo Collect application on their Android devices. Geographic coordinates were systematically recorded via GPS for each surveyed household. Following data collection, information from the server was consolidated into a single dataset, cleaned, and subsequently exported to SPSS for statistical analysis.

Focus group discussion (FGD)

A focus group discussion guide was used among teenagers and their parents to obtain in-depth perspectives on social media use and associated food practices while on social media (Supplementary file 2). Participants for the FGDs were recruited randomly during the study. Consent was obtained from each participant, and adherence to homogenous composition regarding gender and age group was maintained. A total of six focus group discussions (FGDs) were held with teenagers, each comprising eight participants, and five FGDs with parents, each involving six participants. Due to the high population density in Mulolongo A and B CHUs, four FGDs with teenagers and three with parents were organized there. Additionally, one separate FGD for teenagers and another for parents was conducted in Syokimau CHU and another in Sabaki CHU. Each group was composed of between 6–8 discussants, a moderator, and a rapporteur [19]. The moderator guided the discussion while the rapporteur took notes and recorded the proceedings in an audio recorder. In the teenager group, the purpose was to collect detailed insights and perspectives on their use of mobile gadgets, access to the internet, visits to social media, and the associated food-related practices. For the parents’ group, the researchers intended to collect perspectives and beliefs from the parents on teenagers’ access to the internet and social media use, time spent on these sites at the expense of other actively involved events at home.

Anthropometry (weight and height measurements)

The nutritional status was determined using anthropometric measurements of weight and height. The Omron HN289 personal weighing scale was used to take weight measurements, while a wooden adult height board (UNICEF) was used to take height measurements. To mitigate measurement error, a rigorous and standardized protocol was established for both weight and height assessments. All data collectors received standardized training on a detailed procedure for instrument use and participant positioning. The Omron weighing scale was calibrated regularly throughout the data collection period. Participants were measured in light clothing without shoes and stood centrally on the scale for weight, adopting the Frankfort Plane position with heels, buttocks, and scapulae against the stadiometer for height to ensure consistent posture. The wooden UNICEF height board was secured to a stable, vertical surface to ensure accuracy. The headpiece was then lowered gently to compress the hair. Measurements were then recorded at the point of stabilization. First and second readings for both measurements were recorded, and the average was calculated to get accurate measurements. Furthermore, the consistency of measurements across different data collectors was empirically validated through an assessment of inter-rater reliability. A subset of teenagers was independently measured by two or more raters in a blinded fashion. The resulting paired measurements were analyzed using the Intra-class Correlation Coefficient (ICC) to quantify the level of agreement and establish the objectivity and reproducibility of the nutritional status assessments across the research team.

Recruitment and training of research assistants and handling non-response

Before data collection, all participants were clearly informed about the study’s objectives, procedures, and their rights. Participation was fully voluntary, and individuals were free to withdraw at any stage without consequence. This was emphasized during the consenting process to minimize perceived coercion. Research assistants received comprehensive training to approach participants respectfully and address concerns that might lead to refusal. They were equipped to handle refusal ethically. All instances of refusals, especially due to digital literacy or privacy concerns, were documented. This allowed for inclusivity, transparent reporting, and assessment of potential selection bias. Non-response was addressed analytically through descriptive reporting. The initial sample size was increased to account for anticipated non-response, ensuring that statistical power and representativeness were maintained despite attrition.

Pre-testing of the questionnaire, reliability, and consistency

The structured questionnaire and the focus group discussion guide underwent pre-testing to assess clarity, coherence, and sufficiency of the questions. Subsequent validation was conducted by research supervisors, the Department of Food, Nutrition, and Dietetics at Kenyatta University, and a group of subject matter experts who reviewed and refined the instruments to ensure methodological rigor and alignment with the objective of the study. To evaluate reliability, the test–retest procedure was implemented by administering the questionnaire and the guide in multiple households in the Athi River ward before formal data collection, thereby assessing its temporal consistency. During the discussions, participants were assured of confidentiality and anonymity. This encouraged honest disclosure and reduced response distortion.

Data analysis plan

Analysis of quantitative data

WHO AnthroPlus software was used to analyze anthropometric data for teenagers. The nutrition status of the teenagers was classified with reference to the WHO growth standards of BMI-for-age Z-scores [20]. The calculated nutrition indicators were exported into SPSS version 25 for further analysis. For quantitative data, the analytical approach consisted of descriptive, bivariate, and multivariate techniques using SPSS (v.25). Descriptive statistics summarized respondents’ characteristics and variable distribution, whereas Pearson’s χ2 tests identified significant associations (p < 0.05) between the selected variables at 95% confidence intervals. At the bivariate level, associations between the dependent variable (teenagers' nutritional status) and independent variables (social media use and associated food-related habits (Model I) were evaluated, yielding Crude Odds Ratios (COR) with 95% confidence intervals. Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression (Model II) was performed to adjust for potential confounding factors, generating Adjusted Odds Ratios (AOR) at 95% confidence intervals.

Variable selection for regression analysis

To investigate the influence of social media on teenagers’ dietary behaviors, the study first identified key dimensions of social media use. This involved assessing the types of digital devices and internet connectivity through which participants accessed social media platforms. Subsequently, the researchers examined the types of social media sites accessed, the underlying motivations for usage, and the degree to which these sites served as sources of nutrition-related information. Frequency of use, operationalized as average hours spent per day, was also captured as a possible predictor. In the third phase, attention was directed toward the kind of food-related content encountered during social media use, alongside behavioral correlates such as browsing habits and food-related activities undertaken while browsing. These variables were theorized as independent variables, informed by established behavioral nutrition models and empirical evidence linking social media exposure, peer influence, and the broader food environment to dietary choices [21–23]. Notably, the frequency of social media use was retained due to its documented association with shifts in food preferences, body image perceptions, and exposure to targeted food advertisements among youth populations. Finally, nutritional status of the teenagers was modeled as the dependent variable to represent a critical endpoint in public health nutrition as a proxy for overall health and well-being.

Approach to qualitative analysis

Thematic analysis was employed to systematically examine the qualitative data, enabling organized coding and identification of key themes related to social media use [24]. The first step involved transcribing the recorded interviews into verbatim text. Each transcript was then cross-checked for accuracy by listening to the audio and ensuring no information was omitted. The data was sorted into files based on demographic details from the discussions. Both inductive and deductive coding approaches were applied to identify key themes, concepts, and categories. Combining inductive and deductive approaches allowed for a balanced analysis, providing structure while also accommodating emerging themes [25]. The inductive approach allowed themes to emerge naturally from the data, which involved repeatedly reviewing the data to identify recurring concepts and ideas. Codes were then assigned to relevant segments grouped into potential themes. The deductive approach, guided by the research objective, generated codes that aligned with specific thematic areas of interest. Through the iterative approach, the 17 subthemes were saturated into 7 unifying themes to provide an all-inclusive representation of the data (Table 1). The emerging themes were adjusted to ensure they precisely represented the data and were clearly defined with descriptive labels. The final step involved writing a narrative that explained how the themes developed and their connection to the study objective. To enrich the robustness of the findings, the emerging themes were triangulated with quantitative data sets by systematically linking patterns between thematic insights and quantitative indicators. These included social media use, food-related content encountered during social media use, browsing habits, and reported food-related activities undertaken while browsing to assess convergence and divergence. Results

Characteristics of the study teenagers

The study found a nearly equal distribution of male 49.9% (n = 178) and female 50.1% (n = 179) respondents, with no significant gender difference, p > 0.05. The average age of the teenagers was 16.22 years (SD = 1.51), with most, 79.0% (n = 282) falling between 15 and 19 years old. A majority, 72.5% (n = 259), had completed secondary education, with no notable gender disparity. Access via digital gadgets, internet connectivity, site preference, and frequency of visit

Findings for this study revealed that 72.8% (n = 260) of the teenagers had access to digital gadgets, with 53.1% of males using them compared to 46.9% of females, p = 0.04. Moreover, 76.9% (n = 236) of the gadgets accessed by teenagers were smartphones, with male teenagers leading in access at 53.8%. Statistically significant gender differences were observed in the use of tablets for internet access, with 11.4% (n = 35) reporting tablet use, p = 0.01. Use of mobile data bundles and Wi-Fi at homes by 55.7% (n = 171) and 21.8% (n = 67) of the teenagers, respectively, was the predominant means of access to the internet. Findings showed that 52.1% (n = 89) of teenagers financed their internet access using money received from parents, while 44.4% (n = 76) used savings from pocket money. Food-related habits while engaging in social media

Food-related content accessed on social media

The findings indicated that 34.5% (n = 123) of teenagers reported enjoying watching food-related videos on social media, with a statistically significant difference observed between genders, p < 0.001. Within this category, 90.2% (n = 111) of teenagers reported enjoying watching videos focused on food menus and meal preparation, 32.5% (n = 40) on food cultures of different countries, and 30.9% (n = 38) liked to view videos on food content and their ingredients. Although only a small proportion of teenagers, 4.0% (n = 5), reported viewing videos related to weight loss, this behaviour exhibited a statistically significant gender difference, p < 0.001. Additionally, 54.3% (n = 194) encountered food advertisement contents while browsing through social media platforms. On average, food advertisements would appear 3 times in 7 days while teenagers were visiting social media. Fast food advertisements, including those for pizza, hamburgers, fried chicken, and sandwiches, were the most frequently 72.1% (n = 140) viewed type of food-related content on social media. Additionally, video clips featuring food recipes and menus accounted for 44.3% (n = 86) of the content viewed, while cooking-related advertisements comprised 19.0% (n = 37) and advertisements for beverages and sugary drinks accounted for 17.5% (n = 34). Statistically significant gender differences were observed across these categories, with p-values of 0.01, 0.02, and 0.01, respectively (Table 6). Food practices while surfing

Approximately 32.8% (n = 117) of respondents reportedly ate while browsing an average of at least three times a week. A total of 59.8% (n = 70) of teenagers mostly ate and browsed through social media at the same time while taking main meals (breakfast, lunch, or supper). At the same time, 30.8% (n = 36) of respondents consume crisps and cookies, while 26.5% (n = 31) eat biscuits and pastries while browsing social media. Overall, 59.4% (n = 212) of teenagers reported spending their pocket money to purchase snacks such as cookies, cakes, ice cream, and chocolate bars, a behaviour that demonstrated a statistically significant gender difference, p = 0.03. Additionally, 38.4% (137) spent their pocket money on buying soft drinks, including soda and carbonated beverages. In contrast, only 16.8% (n = 60) allocated their pocket money to purchasing fruits and blended juices. Although not a core focus of this study, 2.2% (n = 8) of teenagers reported using their pocket money to buy alcoholic beverages and related products, a pattern that also showed significant gender variation, p = 0.03 (Table 6). Level of parental concern and programming of social media for informed food choices

Thematic insights drawn from both parents and teenagers indicated that most parents were worried about how much time their teenagers spend on social media. Their concerns extended beyond screen time to the nature of online content, particularly exposure to harmful behaviours and interactions with strangers that could exert a negative influence. In response, some parents have implemented time restrictions on social media use, while others actively check what their children are doing online. Most parents also agreed that without supervision, teenagers were likely to overuse social media. Since young people’s brains are still developing, they may struggle to resist the addictive and persuasive nature of social media content. Nonetheless, focus group discussions with both parents and teenagers highlighted the potential of social media as a platform for promoting healthier food choices. Nutrition status of teenagers based on BMI for age Z-scores and age

For modeling purposes, nutritional status served as the outcome variable, with social media use, food content exposure while online, and food-related habits of teenagers included as independent variables to reflect their relevance in public health nutrition and their potential impact on overall health and well-being. Accordingly, the analysis yielded results for both underweight and overweight categories of nutritional statuses. The prevalence of underweight was 8.1% and overweight was 9.3%. When stratified by age, among respondents aged 13–14 years, the prevalence of underweight was 17.3% and overweight was 9.3%. Conversely, within the 15–19-year-old age group, the prevalence rates were 5.7% for underweight and 11.0% for overweight.

Discussion

The study revealed that a high level of social media usage among teenagers in Machakos County, Kenya. Notably, there was a statistically significant gender difference in access to digital devices, with males reporting greater access than their female counterparts. The majority of teenagers financed their internet access through parental support or savings from pocket money. The most popular social media platforms were WhatsApp, TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, and Facebook. However, only a small fraction of teenagers reported using social media to access health or nutrition-related content, with most using these platforms for entertainment. Qualitative insights from focus group discussions corroborated these findings on the rare use of social media engagement to seek health or nutrition information. This alignment strengthened the credibility of the observed behavioral patterns of teenagers. On weekdays, teenagers spent an average of 2.65 ± 0.13 hours on social media, increasing to 3.08 ± 0.14 hours on weekends, with substantial daily use of WhatsApp, Instagram, and TikTok. In focus group discussions, parents expressed concern over the amount of time teenagers spent online, noting its impact on family engagement and a reduction in opportunities for physical activity, habits that are detrimental to psychosocial development and long-term health.

Quantitative data established that teenagers frequently encountered food-related content on social media, specifically, fast food advertisements, which strongly influenced their snack purchasing behaviors. Narrative insights from focus group discussions validated these patterns, with teenagers describing a sharp appeal toward visually appealing fast-food advertisements and an interest in meal preparation videos. Teenagers reported that such content triggered their cravings and food choices, habitually preferring sugary and processed snacks over healthier selections such as fruits. This convergence between quantitative findings and discussions highlights the persuasive supremacy of digital food advertisement of unhealthy foodstuffs and its implications for immediate teenagers’ dietary habits and long-term health. Quantitative findings indicated that teenagers enjoyed watching food-related videos on social media, with an interest in menus and meal preparation content. This digital interest was further contextualized by qualitative insights from focus group discussions, where parents voiced appreciation and concern regarding their teenagers’ adoption of new cooking techniques from social media. Whereas some parents recognized the educational potential of such exposure, others raised concerns about safety, nutritional adequacy, and cultural relevance. Together, these datasets offer a balanced outlook highlighting teenagers’ growing culinary interest from social media, along with parental concern about the implications. Furthermore, teenagers reported eating while browsing social media, a behavior that may disrupt hunger and satiety cues, possibly leading to mindless eating and increased consumption of energy-dense and nutrient-poor foods such as crisps, cookies, biscuits, and pastries, as revealed in quantitative data. Narratives from focus group discussion corroborated this finding, with several participants describing that their habitual snacking was funded by pocket money, frequently to purchase snacks they had seen advertised on social media.

The study revealed a concerning link between social media use and nutritional outcomes of the teenagers, where 8.1% of teenagers were underweight, while 9.3% were overweight. Importantly, access to digital devices, use of WhatsApp and Instagram exhibited higher odds of being underweight, while exposure to food advertisements was a significant, shared risk factor for both underweight and overweight. This underscores the urgent need for comprehensive public health strategies that must address social media use as a key driver of poor nutritional outcomes. These findings offer valuable insights into teenage social media practices in Kenya and their implications for dietary behaviors. While global studies have documented the impact of social media on dietary choices and body image among youth [26, 27], few have focused on low-resource settings such as Machakos County, which presents unique patterns. The gender disparity in device access and social media use also reflects broader digital and social media divides seen in other regions [28]. Boys were found to receive greater access to digital devices and social media, particularly because they asked and received financial support from parents for data bundles, and often saved their pocket money for the same purpose, more so than girls. Moreover, girls made less frequent use of mobile data and home wi-fi to engage in social media platforms. Contrary, girls could have been less confident to borrow devices from siblings, which possibly limited their digital skills development and confidence in platform engagement. Studies show that boys tend to prioritize gaming when using digital devices while girls use social media for emotional expression, visual satisfaction, and coping with sadness and loneliness [29, 30]. These gender disparities reflect deeper social, cultural, and emotional dynamics with significant implications for teenagers’ well-being and digital equity. Targeted interventions should focus on promoting equitable access, fostering digital literacy, safe online spaces, and incorporating gender sensitive health support systems.

In contrast to findings from high-income countries, where teenagers use social media to seek nutrition and fitness-related advice [31]Kenyan teenagers primarily use social media for entertainment rather than seeking health and nutrition-related information. In Mulolongo, being a metropolitan region, teenagers are exposed to diverse digital content and lifestyles. Besides, teenagerhood is a developmental stage encompassed by heavy social connectivity, peer pressure, and consequently a preference for online-entertainment-driven activities more so than educational or health-related content [18, 32]. A possible explanation is that social media platforms' algorithms tend to prioritize entertainment-related content; hence, nutrition-oriented information is less visible unless users deliberately seek it out. Even when such content appears, teens may engage with it passively, unless it is visually compelling, emotionally relatable, and endorsed by celebrity influencers. Additionally, low media literacy among teens and difficulty in evaluating the credibility of online sources could further reduce the active quest for nutrition information.

The minimal engagement with health or nutrition content on social media was unexpected, given the potential of these platforms to disseminate health information. Analysis from focus group discussions with teenagers and parents highlighted the need to develop and air nutrition-focused programs, forums, talks, and shows with compelling health communication content to effectively reach youth through social media platforms. Although minimal, our findings revealed that a small fraction of teenagers expressed interest in watching weight loss-related videos, an outcome that exhibited a statistically significant gender difference. This implies that social media may offer a valuable avenue for weight loss interventions among children and adolescents. Beyond this, both quantitative and qualitative data underscore the usefulness of social media platforms as a source of culinary skills, including menus and diverse cuisines. These platforms facilitated peer-to-peer learning while also serving as channels for delivering useful nutrition information and ultimately fostering healthy eating habits. Additionally, exposure to food-related content on social media platforms was found to enhance awareness about healthy diets. To reinforce this potential, effective interventions should prioritize nutrition education approaches that are peer-supported and user-centered. The contents disseminated should encourage teenagers to adopt healthy habits, explore food products, learn new recipes, and exchange culinary experiences, leading to improved dietary practices and overall well-being.

The association between social media use, particularly exposure to food advertisements, and nutritional status builds upon existing literature [33]. Adolescents are increasingly engaging in social media. However, the dual interrelationship with both underweight and overweight is unique. This suggests that social media use may simultaneously amplify restrictive and excessive eating behaviors. On one hand, teens exposed to idealized body images, possibly by peers or influencers, may develop body dissatisfaction, which may precipitate disordered eating patterns contributing to underweight. On the other hand, in the same platforms, the frequent promotion of energy-dense and nutrient-poor foods through targeted advertising and utilization of algorithm-driven content personalization may increase the likelihood of overconsumption and overweight outcomes [34, 35]. The strong engagement with snack and fast-food ads while in social media in Kenya mirrors patterns observed in countries such as the United States and the United Kingdom, where exposure to such advertising propagates increased consumption of unhealthy foods [26, 36, 37]. Globally, the influence of food advertisements on social media platforms remains a concern, since younger users may not be able to distinguish the blurred line between entertainment and advertising. Furthermore, engagement with such content through liking and sharing may reinforce unhealthy consumption norms and normalize frequent intake of fast foods and sugary beverages [26, 33, 38]. These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions that address both the content teens view and the mechanisms of delivery. Policies targeting social media food marketing, along with social media literacy programs and psychosocial support, may be key in reducing the dual burden of malnutrition among children and adolescents. Future studies should investigate the varying impact of social media across different socio-cultural settings to develop equitable and context-sensitive nutrition frameworks.

Our present findings contrast with those of previous observations that associate increased screen time with obesity [39, 40]. Specifically, the notable association between WhatsApp and Instagram usage and underweight status is a unique and unexpected finding, which differs from the more commonly reported link between social media use and overweight/obesity [40]. This suggests that social media engagement could be influencing reduced food intake or dietary restrictions. A plausible explanation for this may lie in the psychosocial and behavioral ways these platforms trigger. Social media usage poses appearance-based social comparisons, which can intensify body image distress, especially during the teenage years, a phase characterized by peer-to-peer influence and social identity [41]. This dissatisfaction may contribute to disordered eating habits and poor mental health outcomes, like low self-esteem and anxiety. Nevertheless, exposure to curated content from celebrities who promote unrealistic body images may support the adoption of thinness as a mark of beauty [42]. Although not investigated in our study, such influence has been documented in Instagram and TikTok, where exposure to idealized body images worsened body dissatisfaction and low self-esteem among teenage girls [43]. Girls may also be more likely to engage in fad diets and the gratification of extreme thinness, thereby facilitating normalization of destructive behaviors. Additionally, long hours on social media may displace time for regular meals and healthy eating routines with family, further intensifying nutritional risks. Our findings, therefore, reflect distinct contextual factors, such as food insecurity or differing patterns of physical activity, underscoring the need for more localized studies to understand social media’s diverse impacts on dietary behaviors and the proper interpretation of digital health research. While this study did not directly assess these facets, future research could unravel the frequency with which teenagers consume unhealthy foods as influenced by exposure to social media. Moreover, further investigations can look into whether certain types of social media content may discourage consumption of unhealthy (junk) foods to offer a more nuanced understanding of its dual influence on dietary behaviors among teenagers.

A key strength of the study is its mixed-methods design, which triangulated quantitative data with rich qualitative insights from both teenagers and parents. This approach provides a nuanced understanding of the motivations and behaviors underlying social media use and online food-related habits. The study captures teenage exposure to online food-related content and online eating habits in a rapidly digitizing county, offering a perspective from an understudied and underrepresented population in Kenya in existing research. However, the study has several limitations. While this research employed a cross-sectional design, integrating longitudinal approaches together with experimental approaches like follow-up surveys and behavioral interventions could improve temporal depth and give room for stronger inferences on causality between social media use and nutritional outcomes. Future research should thus employ longitudinal or mixed-method designs to reveal the directionality and progression of consumption of online food-related content on adolescent eating habits over time. Self-reported data may be subject to recall or social desirability bias, particularly regarding sensitive topics such as eating practices. Subsequent research should consider incorporating digital food diaries, such as wearable app-based logging, to strengthen accuracy and reduce bias in reporting sensitive topics such as dietary behaviors.

While our study provides valuable insights into social media use and food practices among teenagers, the sample's limited geographic and demographic diversity must be acknowledged. This constraint may affect the generalizability of the findings to adolescents in other counties or rural settings, where internet access and patterns of social media engagement may differ. Future research should include multi-county or national-level sampling to unravel regional differences in internet connectivity and social media use and thus enhance understanding of the broader applicability of these findings.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that teenagers from Machakos County, Kenya, spend significant time on social media, with minimal focus on nutritional awareness. Exposure to online food advertisements strongly influences snack purchases, cooking habits, and food practices of teenagers. This is the first study in Kenya that has linked social media use to both underweight and overweight status among teenagers, highlighting the pervasive influence of food advertisement and the limited use of social media for health information. Government agencies should integrate digital health literacy into the national education curriculum, equipping teachers with the skillsets needed to critically navigate online food-related environments. This includes embedding healthy eating modules and establishing peer-led student clubs that use social media to promote positive nutrition habits. Additionally, government authorities should establish and implement regulations that curb the exposure of children to unhealthy food advertisements on social media. Parents and guardians should help teenagers identify credible sources of nutrition information, promote balanced screen time, and co-viewing of health-related content. Lastly, health professionals must collaborate to design inclusive, gender-responsive support and evidence-based nutrition content tailored to the evolving needs of children and adolescents.
