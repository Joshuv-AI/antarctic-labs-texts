Body Image, Disordered Eating, and Psychological Functioning as Prospective Predictors of Inflexible Eating
Authors: Tayarna Bolger, Mariel Messer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveMost research on dieting behaviours has examined rigid dieting, characterised by strict, rule‐bound approaches to eating that reflect an all‐or‐nothing mindset. However, this work has primarily emphasised behavioural features, with comparatively less attention to the cognitive and emotional processes underlying rigid control. Inflexible eating has recently been proposed as a specific construct capturing psychological inflexibility in the domain of eating, including rigid adherence to self‐imposed rules and distress when these are violated. While conceptually related to rigid restraint, inflexible eating extends beyond traditional behavioural conceptualisations by incorporating these psychological processes. Despite its relevance, prospective evidence on its predictors remains limited.MethodsThis study examined whether baseline body image facets (body dissatisfaction, fear of weight gain, overvaluation, and preoccupation), disordered eating behaviours (binge eating, driven exercise, and muscularity‐oriented disordered eating [MODE]), and psychological functioning (depressive and anxiety symptoms) prospectively predicted inflexible eating. A total of 1760 women (M = 34.00 years, SD = 9.40; 81.3% White) completed measures at Time 1 (T1), with 1237 providing 3‐month follow‐up data (Time 2; T2).ResultsBivariate analyses showed that all T1 body image facets, poor psychological functioning, and disordered eating behaviours except binge eating predicted higher T2 inflexible eating. In multivariate analyses, T1 fear of weight gain, MODE, and depressive symptoms uniquely predicted greater T2 inflexible eating.ConclusionsFindings identify key psychological and behavioural precursors of inflexible eating, underscoring potential targets for early intervention and prevention.Keywords: body image, dietary restraint, disordered eating, longitudinal, psychological functioning

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs) are serious psychiatric conditions characterised by disturbances in eating behaviours, body image, and weight‐related cognitions, and are associated with significant psychological impairments and severe medical complications (Fairburn et al. 2003; Stice et al. 2011). The prevalence of eating disorder symptomatology has been increasing worldwide, with global estimates rising from 3.5% to 7.8% between 2000 and 2018 (Galmiche et al. 2019). While only a minority of individuals meet full diagnostic criteria for EDs, disordered eating behaviours, which refer to specific patterns of problematic eating such as restrictive eating practices and binge eating, are highly prevalent in the community and confer significant psychological distress, functional impairment, and adverse physiological outcomes (Ortega‐Luyando et al. 2015; Santana et al. 2023). Thus, identifying mechanisms that contribute to these behaviours and increase the risk of progression to clinical EDs, is therefore critical for informing prevention and early intervention efforts.

Dietary restraint has been consistently identified as one such mechanism implicated in the development and maintenance of EDs (Duarte et al. 2017; Fairburn et al. 2003; Linardon et al. 2019). Two forms of dietary restraint have been identified: flexible and rigid. Flexible restraint involves graded approaches to eating, characterised by behaviours such as taking smaller servings of food or eating a variety of foods in limited quantities to help regulate weight (Linardon and Mitchell 2017). In contrast, rigid control reflects an all‐or‐nothing approach, characterised by highly restrictive calorie counting, avoiding high‐calorie foods, or skipping meals for weight‐loss purposes (Linardon et al. 2019). It is important to note that while calorie counting can be implemented flexibly in behavioural weight loss programmes, it is considered rigid restraint when practised in an inflexible, rule‐bound manner that is psychologically distressing and associated with heightened concern over adherence (Linardon et al. 2019; Tylka et al. 2015). Empirical evidence demonstrates that rigid control is consistently linked to heightened body image concerns, disordered eating behaviours, and poorer psychological functioning, whereas flexible control is more commonly negatively associated with these maladaptive outcomes (Appleton and McGowan 2006; Duarte et al. 2016; Linardon et al. 2019; Pollert et al. 2016).

Although dietary restraint has traditionally been conceptualised in behavioural terms, emerging research suggests that problematic eating regulation also involves key psychological processes that are not fully captured by existing restraint measures. Specifically, Duarte et al. (2017) introduced the construct of inflexible eating, defined as the tendency to rigidly adhere to self‐imposed eating rules regardless of internal cues (e.g., hunger and satiety) or external contexts (e.g., social situations), accompanied by a sense of control when rules are followed and distress when they are violated. This construct reflects psychological inflexibility in the domain of eating, capturing not only behavioural restriction but also the cognitive rigidity and emotional consequences underpinning rule‐governed eating. Importantly, while inflexible eating is conceptually related to rigid restraint, it is not synonymous with it. Rather than representing a subtype of dietary restraint, inflexible eating provides a more specific operationalisation of the psychological processes that may underlie particularly maladaptive forms of restraint. This distinction is important, as it may help explain why some individuals who engage in dietary restraint experience greater psychological distress and eating pathology than others. However, despite its theoretical and clinical relevance, prospective research examining predictors of inflexible eating remains limited.

Negative body image facets have been widely recognised as key influences on disordered eating behaviours and may similarly contribute to inflexible eating. Relevant facets include body dissatisfaction (a subjective negative experience of one's weight and shape), fear of weight gain (an intense fear of gaining weight or becoming fat), preoccupation (excessive shape and weight‐related thoughts that interfere with daily functioning), and overvaluation (overemphasis of body shape and weight) (Fairburn et al. 2003; Prnjak et al. 2022). The transdiagnostic theory of eating disorders provides a valuable framework for understanding these relationships, proposing that overvaluation is a core psychopathology that underpins and drives the behaviours and cognitions of EDs (Fairburn et al. 2003). When individuals judge their self‐worth primarily through weight and shape, everyday fluctuations in eating, weight, or appearance can evoke intense distress, which may prompt rigid, rule‐governed eating patterns aimed at restoring a sense of control. Beyond overvaluation, the theory also highlights maintaining mechanisms, such as perfectionism, low self‐esteem, and mood intolerance, which, together with body dissatisfaction, fear of weight gain, and preoccupation, can drive individuals towards rigid dieting as a maladaptive strategy to manage weight or shape‐related anxiety.

Indeed, cross‐sectional evidence also supports these theoretical links. Several studies have shown that higher levels of body dissatisfaction, fear of weight gain, preoccupation, and overvaluation have been associated with cognitive inflexibility, highly rule‐bound eating, and rigid dietary control across a variety of populations, including female university students, community female adolescents, community sample of female and male adults, and a non‐obese sample (Duarte et al. 2016; Ferreira et al. 2016; Linardon and Mitchell 2017; Stewart et al. 2002; Tylka et al. 2015). Together, these findings suggest that such behaviours may serve as maladaptive strategies to reduce appearance‐related distress. However, reliance on cross‐sectional research limits causal inference, and to date limited studies have examined these relationships prospectively.

In addition to body image facets, specific disordered eating behaviours may predict inflexible eating. One common disordered eating behaviour is binge eating, which is characterised by the consumption of an objectively large amount of food within a short period of time (e.g., typically less than 2 h), accompanied by a sense of loss of control over one's eating (Messer et al. 2024). Dietary restraint theory posits that restrictive eating can precipitate binge eating by increasing physiological and psychological vulnerability to loss‐of‐control eating episodes (Ruderman 1986). Conversely, binge eating has been linked to subsequent rigid dieting, indicating a cyclical pattern of overeating followed by compensatory restriction (Smith et al. 1999; Stewart et al. 2002; Tylka et al. 2015). This cyclical pattern is further supported by the cognitive behavioural theory for bulimia nervosa, which proposes that binge eating often leads to the implementation of strict dieting as a way of managing both the physical consequences of binge eating and the associated psychological distress, which includes guilt, shame, and the belief that stricter control may prevent future binges (Fairburn et al. 2003). While prior prospective research has examined this cyclical pattern broadly, less is known about how binge eating predicts inflexible eating specifically. Examining this relationship may clarify the temporal mechanisms linking disordered eating behaviours with the cognitive rigidity underlying inflexible eating.

Another potential predictor of inflexible eating is driven exercise behaviours. Driven exercise behaviours refer to intense, compulsive physical activity, where individuals feel compelled or the urgency to engage in exercise as a means to control their weight or shape, ultimately avoiding negative consequences of not exercising (Schaumberg et al. 2022; Stiles‐Shields et al. 2011). While exercise behaviours have been widely studied in relation to general dieting and disordered eating, research examining their link to inflexible eating is scarce. Importantly, driven exercise may uniquely contribute to inflexible eating because it combines a psychological compulsion to adhere with rigid, rule‐bound exercise patterns, reflecting both the behavioural and cognitive rigidity captured by inflexible eating. Cross‐sectional research demonstrates that frequent or compulsive exercise, especially when motivated by weight and shape control, is significantly linked with problematic eating attitudes, strict dietary practices, and broader ED psychopathology (Alcaraz‐Ibáñez et al. 2020; Martin et al. 2020; Zmijewski and Howard 2003). This pattern suggests that exercise may function as a compensatory behaviour designed to offset dietary lapses or to maintain a desired body weight or shape. Over time, such compensatory patterns could reinforce rigid food rules and heighten dietary control, thereby increasing vulnerability to inflexible eating. However, prospective evidence on how driven exercise and inflexible eating interact is lacking.

A more recent behavioural pattern relevant to inflexible eating is muscularity‐oriented disordered eating (MODE; Anderson et al. 2024). MODE refers to specific dietary behaviours that are aimed towards gaining lean muscle, which includes the overconsumption of protein‐based foods, liquefying meals to increase calorie consumption, and ensuring continual access to pre‐planned meals (Anderson et al. 2024). Although previous research has primarily focused on men, studies suggest that the pursuit of leanness and muscularity may drive the adoption of maladaptive rigid behaviours (Lavender et al. 2017; Tod and Edwards 2015). This may occur due to the distress and anxiety when individuals are unable to adhere to their rigid food rules (Murray et al. 2012). These findings indicate that MODE not only involves behavioural dietary rules, but also carries the psychological costs, including anxiety, guilt, and fear of dietary failure. Given the growing cultural emphasis on muscularity and leanness among women, understanding MODE's predictive role in inflexible eating is increasingly important.

Emerging research has also demonstrated that poor psychological functioning, particularly higher levels of depression and anxiety, may be associated with inflexible eating. Previous cross‐sectional studies have demonstrated that higher levels of restrained eating were significantly associated with elevated symptoms of anxiety and depression (Appleton and McGowan 2006; Pollert et al. 2016; Stewart et al. 2002). One explanation is that symptoms of depression and anxiety often involve a diminished sense of control and reduced motivation, prompting individuals to engage in rigid, rule‐based eating patterns in an attempt to restore a sense of order (Appleton and McGowan 2006; Pollert et al. 2016). These behaviours may provide temporary relief from emotional distress, reinforcing the use of strict food rules and ultimately contributing to the development of inflexible eating. Yet, the direction of these relationships remains uncertain, as most evidence is cross‐sectional. Clarifying whether psychological distress precedes inflexible eating could inform early intervention strategies targeting emotional regulation and control.

In summary, literature to date has consistently demonstrated robust associations between body image concerns, disordered eating behaviours, poor psychological functioning, and strict diets, with evidence suggesting such factors may increase the likelihood of engaging in inflexible eating. A significant limitation within the existing literature is the predominance of cross‐sectional research designs, which prevents conclusions about the temporal direction. Another limitation of the existing literature is that research on rigid dietary restraint has predominantly focused on its behavioural features, with less attention given to the cognitive and emotional processes that underpin rigid control, whereby these processes are now captured by conceptualisations such as inflexible eating.

Thus, to address these limitations, the aim of this study was to examine prospectively whether various body image facets, disordered eating behaviours, and poor psychological functioning predict levels of inflexible eating over 3 months. It is hypothesised that greater baseline levels of negative body image facets (body dissatisfaction, fear of weight gain, overvaluation, and preoccupation), higher baseline scores of disordered eating behaviours (binge eating, MODE, and driven exercise), and poorer baseline psychological functioning (higher depression and higher anxiety symptoms) will each be positively and independently associated with higher inflexible eating scores at a 3‐month follow‐up.

Methods

Participants and Procedure

Study participants were recruited as part of an ongoing project investigating risk and protective factors of ED symptoms (Linardon 2024). Recruitment occurred through advertisements posted on a psychoeducational platform (https://breakbingeeating.com) which provides comprehensive information on EDs, including their causes, consequences, prevalence, warning signs, symptoms, and available treatment options. According to a recent study conducted by Linardon. 2020 visitors to this platform are highly symptomatic, with 52% meeting criteria indicative of a threshold ED, and 87% reporting engagement in at least one ED behaviour in the past month.

Inclusion criteria for this study required participants to be women, aged 18‐years and older, and were able to read and understand English. While study advertisements primarily targeted women due to their higher prevalence of disordered eating behaviours and body‐image pressures (Ortega‐Luyando et al. 2015; Santana et al. 2023), non‐binary participants were not explicitly excluded. In previous studies, efforts to recruit men resulted in extremely low participation, yielding samples too small for a meaningful analysis, therefore, an a priori decision was made to focus on women in the present study. The survey was presented in a fixed order and took approximately 20 min to complete. No compensation was provided for participation. All participants provided informed consent, and ethics approval was obtained through Deakin University Human Research Ethics Committee.

A total of 1765 participants provided baseline data on study variables, including five non‐binary individuals (0.28%). Given the very small number of non‐binary participants, a decision was made to exclude these individuals from subsequent analyses. This resulted in 1760 adult women who completed a baseline survey via Qualtrics (T1) and were invited to complete the same survey at a 3‐month follow‐up (T2; n = 1237). A 3‐month time‐lag was utilised based on prior research indicating that this interval is sufficient to detect fluctuations in ED symptoms, thereby balancing sensitivity and feasibility in prospective assessment (Levinson et al. 2016; Sala and Levinson 2016). Participants were aged between 18 and 80 years (M = 34.00, SD = 9.40), and most identified as White (81.3%), with other reported racial groups, including Asian (9.0%), mixed (7.8%), African American (1.5%), American Indian/Alaskan Native (0.3%), and Pacific Islander (0.1%).

Materials

Inflexible Eating

Inflexible eating was measured using the 11‐item Inflexible Eating Questionnaire (IEQ; Duarte et al. 2017). Items assessed individual's inflexible eating patterns using a 5‐point scale, where 1 = fully disagree and 5 = fully agree (e.g., ‘having well defined eating rules makes me feel organised/in control’). Scores were summed, with higher scores reflecting more severe inflexible eating rules. The IEQ demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.95) in this study, with additional evidence of good unidimensional structure, internal consistency, and convergent validity reported in Duarte et al. (2017) and Linardon et al. (2019).

Body Image Facets

Consistent with previous research (Linardon 2024; Mitchison et al. 2017), the four body image facets were assessed using the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE‐Q; Fairburn and Beglin 1994). Two items each were used to assess body dissatisfaction (‘how dissatisfied have you been with your weight’ and ‘how dissatisfied have you been with your shape’) and overvaluation (‘has your weight influenced how you judge yourself as a person’ and ‘has your shape influenced how you judge yourself as a person’). These items were rated on a 7‐point scale where 0 = not at all and 6 = markedly, and were averaged to produce overvaluation and dissatisfaction scores. Spearman–Brown coefficients indicated very good reliability for body dissatisfaction (0.89) and overvaluation (0.93) in this study. Fear of weight gain was evaluated using a single item of ‘have you had a definite fear that you might gain weight?’. Preoccupation was also measured using a single item of ‘has thinking about shape or weight made it very difficult to concentrate on things you are interested in (e.g., working, or following a conversation)?’. Both items were rated on a 7‐point scale, ranging from 0 (no days) to 6 (every day). The EDE‐Q demonstrated good global internal consistency (α = 0.87) in the current study and further evidence of reliability and validity has been documented extensively (Fairburn and Beglin 1994; Linardon 2024; Mitchison et al. 2017).

Disordered Eating Behaviours

Binge Eating

The Eating Pathology Symptoms Inventory (EPSI; Forbush et al. 2013) 8‐item binge eating subscale was employed to assess binge eating behaviours over the past 4 weeks. Items (e.g., ‘I ate until I was uncomfortably full’) were rated from 0 (never) to 4 (very often), and summed to generate a total score, with higher scores reflecting more severe symptoms. Internal consistency for the current study was excellent (α = 0.91), consistent with the robust psychometric support documented by Forbush et al. (2013).

Driven Exercise

Driven exercise was measured using a single item from the EDE‐Q, which asked participants to report how many times in the past 28 days they have engaged in driven or compulsive exercise as a means to control their body shape, weight and amount of fat, or to burn calories.

Muscularity‐Oriented Disorder Eating

The 15‐item Muscularity‐Oriented Eating Test (MOET; Murray et al. 2019) was used to assess eating practices that occur in the pursuit of a muscular body. Items are assessed in relation to the past 28 days and are rated along a 5‐point scale, where 0 = never true and 4 = always true (e.g., ‘I have used meal replacement supplements when I felt full’). Total scores were derived from summing responses, with higher values indicating greater severity of MODE. The MOET displayed excellent internal consistency (α = 0.90) in the current study and is further supported by strong psychometric evidence from Murray et al. (2019). The MOET demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.90) in the current study and has demonstrated strong psychometric properties in samples of women (Cunningham et al. 2021; He et al. 2023).

Poor Psychological Functioning

Depression and Anxiety Symptoms

Symptoms of depression and anxiety were measured using the 4‐item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐4; Kroenke et al. 2009). This scale contains two items assessing depression (e.g., ‘how often have you been feeling down, depressed, or hopeless? and, how often have you been bothered by little interest or pleasure in doing things?’) and two items assessing anxiety (e.g., ‘how often have you been feeling nervous, anxious or on edge? and, how often have you been bothered by not being able to stop or control worrying?’). Items are rated from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) over the past 14 days. Scores are summed to produce separate depression and anxiety totals, with higher values reflecting greater severity. Spearman–Brown coefficients indicated strong internal consistency for both depression (0.88) and anxiety (0.85) in this study. Previous research also confirms the PHQ‐4's strong psychometric evidence (Kroenke et al. 2009).

Statistical Analysis

Prior to conducting the main analyses, data cleaning and screening occurred, and assumptions for the general linear model were checked. Due to a high attrition rate (29.7%), Little's MCAR test was conducted to assess whether data were missing completely at random. The test was significant, (χ 2(11) = 39.22, p < 0.001), indicating that the data were not missing completely at random. Given this, and the more symptomatic characteristics of participants who dropped out, multiple imputation was used to handle the missing data, as it provides unbiased parameter estimates, even for large amounts of missing data (Madley‐Dowd et al. 2019). Regressions were then run on 20‐imputed datasets in accordance with Rubin (1988), and results were pooled to produce estimates.

To examine associations between study variables, Pearson's correlations were conducted. For variables derived from single items (fear of weight gain, preoccupation, and driven exercise), Spearman's correlations were used. This decision was based on the ordinal response format of these items and to reduce the impact of non‐normal distributions, making a non‐parametric approach the most conservative and appropriate. Bivariate regressions were first run to examine prospective relationships between each T1 predictor and inflexible eating at T2. This step was included as a preliminary screening procedure to identify candidate predictors for inclusion in the multivariate model, thereby reducing model complexity and limiting the risk of overfitting given the number of predictors examined. To further control for the increased risk of Type I error due to multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied (Murray et al. 2012; Stice et al. 2011), adjusting the significance threshold to p < 0.0056 for the nine predictors tested. Multivariate regressions were then conducted using the significant bivariate predictors to examine their unique effects while controlling for any shared variance. All analyses were conducted on imputed data sets using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 30) and controlled for T1 inflexible eating scores.

Results

Preliminary Analyses

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power to determine the sample size required to detect small‐to‐moderate effects in a multiple regression model with nine predictors. Assuming an alpha level of 0.05 and statistical power of 0.80, the analysis indicated that a minimum of n = 1140 participants was required to detect a small effect size (f 2 = 0.02). The final sample (n = 1760) exceeded this requirement, indicating that the study was well‐powered to detect small effects.

Preliminary analyses indicated that all key assumptions of linear regression were satisfied. Given the inclusion of multiple predictors within a single model, multicollinearity was carefully assessed. Consistent with the recommend guidelines (Field 2024), no concerns were identified: all variance inflation factors (VIFs) below 5, and bivariate correlations between predictors were below 0.70, indicating that multicollinearity was unlikely to bias parameter estimates. There was no evidence of heteroscedasticity observed, and residuals were normally distributed and independent. Outliers represented less than 1% of the total sample in both bivariate and multivariate models, and their inclusion or exclusion did not impact the overall findings. As such, outliers were retained in the analyses.

Study Attrition

A 29.7% attrition rate was observed, with 1760 participants completing the survey at T1 and 1237 at T2. Independent samples t‐tests found that dropouts were slightly younger and reported slightly higher scores on inflexible eating, smaller elevations in negative body image experiences, and modestly elevated frequencies of disordered eating behaviours (see Table 1). Depression was also slightly elevated among dropouts, whereas anxiety symptoms did not differ significantly. Overall, effect sizes were small, indicating while dropouts had somewhat higher symptomatology, the differences were modest.

Sensitivity Analyses

To assess the robustness of the results, sensitivity analyses were conducted. Both bivariate and multivariate regressions were re‐run using complete case analyses (see Appendix A). Bivariate analysis results were largely consistent with the imputed data, with binge eating remaining non‐significant (p = 0.031). In the multivariate regression, a similar pattern of effect sizes was observed, however, only MODE emerged as a significant predictor of T2 inflexible eating. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted, including the five non‐binary participants, and the pattern of findings remained unchanged, indicating that primary results are robust.

Discussion

The present study prospectively examined whether baseline body image facets, disordered eating behaviours, and poor psychological functioning predicted greater levels of inflexible eating behaviours at a 3‐month follow‐up in adult women. Bivariate analyses identified several risk factors associated with greater inflexible eating, including higher baseline levels of body image disturbances (body dissatisfaction, fear of weight gain, overvaluation, and preoccupation), disordered eating behaviours (driven exercise and MODE), and poor psychological functioning (anxiety and depression symptoms). These findings align with previous cross‐sectional research linking negative body image, disordered eating, and psychological distress to highly rule‐bound eating behaviours (Duarte et al. 2016; Linardon and Mitchell 2017; Martin et al. 2020) and extend these relationships prospectively to inflexible eating.

Contrary to expectations, binge eating did not significantly predict inflexible eating. This finding diverges from theoretical models, including the cognitive behavioural theory for bulimia nervosa (Fairburn et al. 2003), and previous cross‐sectional research (Stewart et al. 2002; Tylka et al. 2015). One explanation may relate to the timing of assessment: associations evident in cross‐sectional studies may reflect short‐term fluctuations in eating behaviour rather than stable prospective effects. In particular, the relationship between binge eating and inflexible eating is likely to operate on more proximal, momentary timescales (e.g., within‐day or day‐to‐day), where episodes of binge eating and subsequent restrictive responses dynamically influence one another. It is also possible that binge eating may follow periods of inflexible eating, suggesting that inflexible eating may precede binge eating rather than being predicted by it. Additionally, while inflexible eating is rooted in perfectionism, control, and rigid cognitive rules (Duarte et al. 2017), binge eating can stem more from emotional dysregulation, impulsivity, or reward sensitivity, entailing that binge eating might not be the primary driver in maintaining inflexible eating rules (Fairburn et al. 2003; Schaumberg et al. 2016). This raises the possibility that other important predictors, such as perfectionism, chronic stress, or low self‐esteem, might also play a significant role in inflexible eating, warranting further research.

In the multivariate analysis, after controlling for shared variance among predictors, only MODE, fear of weight gain, and depressive symptoms emerged as significant positive predictors. Firstly, MODE's emergence as a positive predictor suggests that the pursuit of muscularity and leanness may foster inflexible eating patterns among women. Here, individuals may turn to inflexible eating as a tool to meet precise nutritional targets (e.g., eating 5 g of protein per kilogram of body weight), to maintain meal timing regiments, or individuals may experience anxiety about perceived muscle loss, all of which reduces dietary flexibility and variety, and intensifies adherence to strict dietary plans.

Fear of weight gain also emerged as a significant positive predictor of inflexible eating, supporting previous cross‐sectional findings linking excessive fears of weight gain to highly rule‐bound eating (Linardon and Mitchell 2017), and extends these associations to inflexible eating, confirming a prospective relationship over time. This relationship may be explained by the tendency of women with elevated weight gain fears to resort to rule‐bound eating strategies, believing such approaches provide a rapid solution for effective weight control, alleviating associated body‐related concerns (Ferreira et al. 2016; Linardon 2024). Finally, depressive symptoms emerged as a significant positive predictor of inflexible eating. One possible explanation is that individuals who report elevated depressive symptoms may turn to inflexible eating to regain a sense of control or motivation in one domain of their life, or, as a way to override feelings of apathy, hopelessness, or low self‐worth (Appleton and McGowan 2006; Pollert et al. 2016). Taken together, these results suggest that both appearance‐driven motives and affect‐regulation difficulties contribute uniquely to the maintenance or persistence of inflexible eating, underscoring the multifaceted psychological underpinnings of this behaviour.

Several unexpected findings also emerged from the multivariate model. Notably, other body image facets (overvaluation, body dissatisfaction, and preoccupation), driven exercise behaviours, and anxiety symptoms did not predict inflexible eating after accounting for shared variance. One possible explanation is that these body image facets may exert weaker effects when considered alongside stronger predictors, such as fear of weight gain. Additionally, the negative experiences of body dissatisfaction, preoccupation, or overvaluation may not be sufficiently strong enough to influence an individual's motivation to engage in an inflexible eating approach. Instead, individuals may pursue alternative coping strategies, such as increasing exercise or adopting an intuitive or flexible dieting approach, as suggested in prior research (Linardon and Mitchell 2017). Similarly, individuals with anxiety may rely on alternative coping strategies, including overeating, avoidance, or distraction, instead of restrictive practices. Lastly, the relationship between driven exercise and inflexible eating may only emerge in the presence of certain traits, such as perfectionism or body image concerns, which warrant further research of these factors.

Despite key strengths, findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Firstly, the longitudinal study design precludes conclusions regarding causality (Messer et al. 2022). Future research should seek to experimentally manipulate the significant predictors using a randomised controlled design to robustly evaluate the causal effects on inflexible eating. Additionally, mediation, moderation, and cross‐lagged analyses could further clarify how and under what conditions specific variables, traits, or behaviours influence inflexible eating, establishing temporal direction, and refining our understanding of risk pathways for more targeted interventions. Secondly, despite the large sample size, the homogeneous sample of White women recruited through an online website focused on eating behaviours limits generalisability and introduces potential selection bias, as participants were likely to have a pre‐existing interest in eating behaviours. Moreover, all variables were derived from self‐reports, which may have introduced reporting biases or overestimation of key symptoms (Linardon 2024). Future research should examine whether a similar pattern of results occurs across different genders, racial groups, or individuals with disabilities, and should also consider employing varied recruitment and data collection methods, such as the use of semi‐structured interviews, to enhance validity and generalisability. Finally, variables were only assessed at two time‐points across a span of 3 months. Although this timeframe has been considered sufficient to detect fluctuations in ED symptoms (Levinson et al. 2016; Sala and Levinson 2016), it may be inadequate for capturing changes in the emerging construct of inflexible eating. It is possible that the relationships between predictive variables and inflexible eating depend on the timing of assessment or environmental factors. Thus, future research should consider examining both shorter and longer time‐lags to better understand the temporal dynamics of these relationships and the timescales over which they occur.

These findings underscore the need for targeted interventions that address the psychological and behavioural mechanisms underlying inflexible eating. Strategies targeting inflexible eating may benefit from focusing on fear of weight gain, depressive symptoms, and MODE. Evidence from randomised controlled studies indicates that cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) can reduce fear of weight gain and subsequent disordered eating behaviours in clinical and non‐clinical samples (Alleva et al. 2015; Calugi et al. 2018). For example, reductions in fear of weight gain were accompanied by decreases in dietary restraint, highlighting the potential of CBT to prevent inflexible eating (Calugi et al. 2018). Similarly, behavioural activation may alleviate depressive symptoms, thereby reducing vulnerability to inflexible eating (Linardon 2024; Chartier and Provencher 2013). Additionally, cognitive dissonance‐based interventions have also been shown to reduce muscle dysmorphia‐related psychopathology (Brown et al. 2017; Kilpela et al. 2016), potentially mitigating future inflexible eating behaviours. In addition to these formal, evidence‐based interventions, smaller‐scale or community‐based programmes could be tailored to specific risk factors. For example, communities could implement body image‐focused programmes that address fear of weight gain, mood management initiatives for depressive symptoms, or educational programmes that target the internalisation of muscularity among individuals high in MODE. Such programmes could be delivered through community health centres, online platforms, or schools, increasing accessibility and reaching at‐risk populations.

Overall, the present study highlights key risk factors associated with the maintenance of inflexible eating, providing potential targets for early intervention and prevention strategies. Addressing these risk factors may help reduce vulnerability to persistent inflexible eating patterns and associated eating pathology, representing an important step toward reducing the public health burden of these conditions.
