Otitis in pet guinea pigs: Causes, clinical signs, treatment and outcomes
Authors: Patricia Muffat‐es‐Jacques, Mathilde Charrasse, Paul Raymond, Minh Huynh, Thomas Coutant, Charly Pignon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOtitis is a major disease impacting both pet guinea pigs and laboratory guinea pigs that are used as models in human otological studies.MethodsMedical records from two veterinary clinics were retrospectively reviewed to identify guinea pigs diagnosed with computed tomography (CT)‐confirmed otitis between 2014 and 2023. The clinical signs, treatment and bacteria isolated in these cases were noted, and predisposing factors were identified.ResultsThirty‐six guinea pigs out of the 1477 seen at the clinics during the study period had otitis, giving a prevalence of 2.4%. The majority (61%) of guinea pigs had non‐specific clinical signs, with 12 (33%) having respiratory signs and nine (25%) having dental disease. Only 14 animals (39%) had vestibular signs. Females were less likely to have otitis than males (odds ratio: 0.2). No age predilection was identified (age range 5–60 months). Animals with vestibular signs or respiratory signs had 21.8 and 9.5 times higher odds of having otitis, respectively. Treatment was divided into medical management or surgery and antibiotic therapy. No difference in survival times was observed regarding treatment received.LimitationsLimitations of the study include the small number of animals and lack of repeat CT.ConclusionVeterinarians should remember that otitis is a common disease in guinea pigs of all ages, with males being slightly more predisposed than females. Affected animals can have non‐specific clinical signs, such as respiratory or dental disease. Head tilt is the most common vestibular sign.

INTRODUCTION

Guinea pigs are popular pets, 1 accounting for 1% of the pet population in the UK. 2 Guinea pigs have also been used as a model in human otological studies, and otitis media (OM) has been reported as a major disease in laboratory guinea pigs since 1976. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Minarikova et al. reported that only 11% of guinea pigs with otitis were detected following physical examination, with torticollis being the only reported clinical sign. 7 The remaining animals were identified following imaging investigations.

Computed tomography (CT) is considered the superior imaging modality for diagnosing otitis in animals and humans, as a previous study found that 25% of dogs and cats affected by otitis could not be identified from radiographs. 8 As for humans, dogs, cats, rabbits and rats, OM in guinea pigs likely follows a subclinical course and, without investigations, the true impact of the disease is unknown. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13

Although an OM prevalence of only 0.8% 7 was recorded in pet guinea pigs, this is likely an underestimation, as an incidence of 25‒40% was recorded in laboratory guinea pigs. 14 , 15 Higher prevalences of OM have also been reported in other species, with 29‒50% of laboratory rats being affected, 11.5‒32% of rabbits being diagnosed with subclinical OM on postmortem examination and 5‒20% of dogs being affected with otitis externa (OE). 8 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Medical management for OM and otitis interna (OI) is considered ineffective in cats and dogs, and a combination of medical and surgical treatments is necessary in rabbits. 11 , 21 Although many surgical procedures have been reported for dogs, cats and rabbits, 11 only two surgical procedures, total ear canal ablation and lateral bulla osteotomy (TECA‐LBO) and myringotomy, have been described in guinea pigs, with three out of four animals undergoing TECA‐LBO dying within 2 weeks and myringotomy being reported in only three guinea pigs. 22 , 23

This high prevalence is particularly concerning as ear disease is associated with significant pain in humans, and rabbits with ear disease have been reported to have a lower quality of life. 24 However, there is currently little information available regarding pain in guinea pigs, for which only postoperative ethograms are available. 25 , 26 , 27 Moreover, it has been highlighted that practitioners feel inadequate in recognising and treating pain in guinea pigs. 27 Therefore, it is vital to better define otitis in guinea pigs to improve the diagnosis and management of the disease and thereby improve animal welfare.

The objectives of this study were to report (1) the prevalence of otitis in pet guinea pigs undergoing a head CT at two veterinary hospitals, (2) the clinical signs of affected animals, (3) the treatments performed, clinical aftermath and survival, (4) the bacteria isolated and (5) the predisposing or prognostic factors, if any.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Case selection

The medical records of guinea pigs seen at a veterinary teaching hospital between 1 January 2014 and 1 May 2023 and a private veterinary referral hospital in the same geographical region between 1 January 2017 and 1 May 2023 were reviewed. The inclusion criteria for the guinea pig cohort was that a skull CT (80‐slice multidetector, helical system, Aquilion Lightning 80, Canon Medical Systems at the teaching hospital and 16‐slice, Lightspeed Brivo CT385 Series, General Electric Medical Systems at the private veterinary referral hospital) with or without contrast had been performed. There were no exclusion criteria. Transverse and longitudinal views and multiplanar reconstructions were reviewed in a dynamic adjustable bone and soft tissue window. Head CT scans were graded ‘no otitis’ when no abnormal material was observed, ‘OE’ when the external acoustic meatus was partially or completely filled with soft tissue density material, ‘OM’ when the tympanic cavity was partially or completely filled with or without lysis, and ‘OI’ when the petrous portion of the temporal bone was filled with material and/or lysed (Figures 1, 2, 3). Two groups were then established depending on the presence or absence of otitis.

Medical record review and CT scan

Data extracted from the medical records included age, sex, bodyweight, physical examination findings, diagnostics performed (CT, radiographs, bacteriology), treatments and outcome. Regarding outcome, improvement or lack thereof was initially recorded at recheck, which occurred 1−4 weeks after CT. The owners of animals recorded as being alive at recheck were recontacted at the time of writing, which ranged from 6 months to 5 years after the CT scan, to confirm survival.

To perform the CT scans, the animals were anaesthetised using 2−3% isoflurane in oxygen via a facemask after a 5‐minute period of preoxygenation. An intravenous catheter was attempted in the cephalic vein to inject iohexol (700 mg/kg; Omnipaque 350, GE Healthcare). All animals having clinical signs consistent with lower respiratory tract disease underwent either a head and thoracic CT scan or head CT and thoracic radiographs to detect pneumonia. Images were interpreted by a European or American board‐certified diagnostic imaging specialist.

Bacterial identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing

Samples collected during surgery from animals diagnosed with OM/OI were sent for aerobic and anaerobic culture, bacterial identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST), performed as described in Monge et al. 28 AST was performed using marbofloxacin, enrofloxacin, azithromycin, benzyl‐penicillin, doxycycline, trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole (TMPS), gentamicin and tobramycin.

Statistical analysis

A binomial logistic regression was first performed to identify any predisposing factors for development of OM/OI. Animals diagnosed only with OE (n = 2) were excluded due to their low number. Sex, age, clinical signs and presence of concurrent disease on CT were analysed to determine any impact on the presence of OM/OI. All independent variables were tested in univariate models, and only variables with a p‐value of less than 0.2 were kept in the multivariate model. For each binomial logistic regression, the linearity of the continuous variable age with respect to the logit of the dependent variable was assessed using the Box‐Tidwell test. The presence of outliers was assessed by looking at standardised residuals, and values of more than three standard deviations were excluded.

Second, the outcomes of animals with OM/OI were compared with those of animals without otitis using a chi‐squared test. For the purposes of the analysis, each animal's outcome was simplified as improvement (partial or total) or no improvement (stable or worsening).

Third, focusing only on animals with OM/OI, a second binomial logistic regression was performed to ascertain the effects of sex, age, clinical signs, concurrent disease on CT and treatment on the likelihood of clinical improvement. Treatments were simplified into two options: only medical treatment and surgical treatment with or without medical treatment. All independent variables were tested in univariate models, and only variables with p‐values of 0.1 or less were kept in the multivariate model, with the same model verification as described previously.

Finally, a Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis was performed, considering the median survival time between CT diagnosis and death. Guinea pigs that were diagnosed with OM/OI were first compared with those without otitis. Then, for those with OM/OI, a complementary analysis was performed to investigate the potential impact of sex, vestibular signs, respiratory signs, dental disease (based on CT), rhinitis (based on CT) and treatment on survival. A log rank test was conducted to determine differences in survival distributions. All statistical analyses were performed on SPSS software (SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0, IBM), and p‐values of less than 0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

A total of 1477 guinea pigs were seen at the two practices during the study period: 922 at the teaching hospital and 555 at the private practice. CT scans were performed for 62 of these animals, with 36 (25 at the teaching hospital and 11 at the private practice) being diagnosed with otitis and included in the study. Twenty‐six (72%) of the guinea pigs with otitis were males and 10 (28%) were females. All males were intact and only one female was spayed. Their ages ranged from 5 to 60 months, with a median of 36 months. Their bodyweights ranged from 350 to 1245 g, with a mean of 870 g.

Physical examination

Animals with otitis (n = 36) presented with dysorexia/anorexia (n = 15, 42%), vestibular signs (n = 14, 39%), respiratory signs (n = 12, 33%), dental disease (n = 9, 25%), exophthalmia (n = 3, 8%), pruritus (n = 1, 3%) and epistaxis (n = 1, 3%). Animals presented for dental abscess, elongated teeth or dental spurs were all classified under ‘dental disease’. Animals with either abnormal nasal discharge, increased or decreased respiratory noise or increased respiratory effort were all categorised as having ‘respiratory signs’. Vestibular signs observed included head tilt (13/14, 93%), horizontal nystagmus (3/14, 21%), facial paralysis (3/14, 21%), ataxia (2/14, 14%) and head shaking (2/14, 14%). All animals presented with ataxia also had a head tilt. The duration of clinical signs ranged from 3 days to over 6 months. All animals diagnosed with otitis presented with clinical signs, with the majority presenting with one (18/36, 50%) or two (16/36, 44%), and a minority presenting up to three clinical signs (2/36, 6%).

Animals without otitis (n = 26) presented for diverse reasons and therefore had a wider range of clinical signs. The most common signs included dysorexia or anorexia (n = 12, 46%), dental disease (n = 11, 42%), presence of an abscess (n = 7, 26.9%, with six located dorsally on the mandible and one retrobulbar abscess) or cutaneous mass (n = 3, 11.5%). Other signs included respiratory signs (n = 2, 7.7%) and exophthalmia (n = 2, 7.7%). Epiphora, alopecia, palpable ovarian cyst, heart murmur and hypothermia were each reported only once. Two animals had no clinical signs upon presentation. None of these animals had observable vestibular signs upon clinical examination. The duration of these clinical signs ranged from 12 hours to 2 months.

Otitis media

Thirty‐three (92%) guinea pigs (eight females and 25 males) were diagnosed with OM, with 55% (n = 18) affected in both ears. The majority of animals (n = 27, 82%) had neither concomitant OE nor OI. However, only 21% (n = 7) had no other CT findings except OM. Indeed, the remaining 26 guinea pigs had concomitant diseases, including dental disease (n = 13, 50%) and respiratory pathology (pneumonia, n = 6, 23%; rhinitis, n = 11, 42%; pneumonia and rhinitis, n = 1, 4%). Other findings included meningoencephalitis (n = 4, 15%), cardiomegaly (n = 3, 12%), retrobulbar abscess (n = 1, 4%) and ovarian mass (n = 1, 4%) (Table 3).

Animals with dental disease were predominantly males (11/13, 85%), with eight animals having bilateral otitis. Both of the females with dental disease had unilateral otitis.

Of the seven animals diagnosed with pneumonia on CT or radiographs, three (all females) had bilateral OM, with one also having rhinitis. The other four animals (two females and two males) had unilateral OM.

Ten of the 12 guinea pigs (83%) with rhinitis were males. Of these 12 animals, eight (67%) had bilateral OM (two females and six males) and four (33%) had unilateral OM (all males) (Table 3).

Otitis interna

Unilateral OI was detected in five males, and bilateral OI was detected in one female. All but one of these six guinea pigs had concurrent OM or OE. This was a male guinea pig with unilateral OI that was also diagnosed with rhinitis. For the remaining five animals, three also had OM and OE (described previously) and two also had OM but no OE. Of these two animals, one was affected unilaterally and the second had bilateral otitis (Table 2).

Clinical management, treatment and outcomes

Of the 36 animals with otitis, seven (19%) underwent surgery and 22 (61%) had only medical management. Seven (19%) animals were excluded as one died after CT, two died postoperatively following mass excision or teeth extraction, and treatment information was not available for four animals.

Surgical management

All surgically treated animals except one had a concomitant disease (rhinitis, n = 2; pneumonia, n = 2; cardiomegaly, n = 1; meningoencephalitis, n = 1). Five animals underwent myringotomy as described by Volait‐Rosset et al. 22 All five of these guinea pigs were prescribed antibiotics for 4 weeks: azithromycin (n = 2 at 30 mg/kg every 12−24 hours; Zithromax, Pfizer), chloramphenicol (n = 2 at 50 mg/kg every 24 hours; Delpech) or TMPS (n = 1 at 30 mg/kg every 12 hours; Corylap, Laboratoires Biove). Two animals improved after the procedure, with one still alive 6 months after the procedure and the other dying of unknown causes 2 years later. The third animal remained stable and died 4 years later. The fourth animal worsened despite the procedure and was euthanised 6 weeks later. The last animal was lost to follow‐up, so the treatment outcome is unknown. Two other guinea pigs underwent a TECA‐LBO—as described by Volait and Pignon 23 —and were prescribed antibiotics for 4 weeks (TMPS or enrofloxacin at 10 mg/kg every 12 hours; Baytril, Elanco France), but both died within 1 month of the procedure. No definitive cause of death was identified for these two animals, as their owners declined postmortem examination.

Medical management

Twenty‐two animals were medically managed and were prescribed antibiotics: azithromycin (n = 7 at 30 mg/kg every 12–24 hours; Zithromax, Pfizer) doxycycline (n = 4 at 5−10 mg/kg every 12 hours; Doxyval, CEVA Sante Animale), chloramphenicol (n = 2 at 50 mg/kg every 24 hours; Delpech), enrofloxacin (n = 2 at 10 mg/kg every 12 hours; Baytril, Elanco France), TMPS (n = 2 at 30 mg/kg every 12 hours; Corylap, Laboratoires Biove) and marbofloxacin (n = 1 at 5 mg/kg every 12 hours; Marbocyl, Vetoquinol). Four animals had a bi‐antibiotic therapy composed of doxycycline and marbofloxacin (n = 1), doxycycline and azithromycin (n = 1) or azithromycin and marbofloxacin (n = 2).

Five guinea pigs that were medically managed were diagnosed with otitis only. Three of these animals improved after antibiotic treatment, with two still alive, 4 and 1 year after their CT, respectively. The third animal died 1 year later due to anorexia and diarrhoea. The other two animals deteriorated and were euthanased within 24 hours (Table 4).

Pain management

All animals diagnosed with otitis received supportive care. Twenty‐five guinea pigs received NSAIDs (meloxicam at 1 mg/kg every 12 hours for 5−14 days; Metacam, Boehringer Ingelheim Animal Health France). For two animals, this was combined with either tramadol (10 mg/kg every 12 hours; Tralieve, DECHRA Veterinary Products) or gabapentin (10 mg/kg every 12 hours; Viatris Sante), and for six animals this was combined with force‐feedings (Recovery Plus, Science Selective, Supreme PetFoods). One animal received gabapentin only, and another received betamethasone (0.08 mg/kg every 24 hours for 7 days; Sanofi‐Aventis France) only. Two animals only had force‐feedings.

Bacterial identification and AST

Six bacterial culture and sensitivity tests were performed. The samples were collected during surgery (two during TECA‐LBO and 4 during myringotomy). A monoculture of bacteria was identified for each sample and included Streptococcus pneumoniae (n = 3), Staphylococcus pseudointermedius (n = 1) and Gram‐positive aeroanaerobic bacilli (n = 1). One sample was negative. All bacteria isolated were sensitive to first‐line antibiotics (penicillin, sulphonamides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides: spectinomycin only), 29 except S. pseudointermedius, which was resistant to doxycycline and fluoroquinolones.

Statistical findings

Predisposing factors for OM/OI

Box‐Tidwell analysis revealed that age was linearly related to the logit of the dependent variable. Univariate analyses revealed six variables with a p‐value of less than 0.2: sex (p = 0.03), vestibular signs (p = 0.007), respiratory signs (p = 0.02), dental disease signs (p = 0.11), uncommon clinical signs (pruritus, exophthalmia, epistaxis) (p = 0.16) and rhinitis (p = 0.04). The logistic regression model was statistically significant (χ 2(6) = 25.60, p < 0.001). The model explained 45.2% (Nagelkerke R 2) of the variance in the presence of OM/OI and correctly classified 74.2% of cases. Only three variables were statistically significant in the multivariable analysis: sex (p = 0.04), vestibular signs (p = 0.003) and respiratory signs (p = 0.02). Females had five times lower odds of presenting with OM/OI than males (odds ratio [OR] = 0.2, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.04–0.92). Guinea pigs with vestibular and respiratory signs had, respectively, 21.8 (95% CI 2.9–163.4) and 9.5 (95% CI 1.36–66.1) times higher odds of presenting with OM/OI (Table 5).

Outcome of OM/OI

Only 45 guinea pigs had sufficient data to be included in the analysis. There was no statistically significant association between outcome and the presence of OM/OI (χ 2(1) = 0.26, p = 0.61).

Factors associated with outcome of OM/OI

Thirty‐four guinea pigs had OM/OI but eight were excluded due to lack of information. Therefore, a binomial logistic regression was performed on 26 guinea pigs with OM/OI, looking at outcome at follow‐up. The inverse square of age was linearly related to the logit of the dependent variable. Univariate analyses revealed three variables with a p‐value of 0.1 or less: age (p = 0.10), vestibular signs (p = 0.01) and uncommon clinical signs (p = 0.06). The logistic regression model was statistically significant (χ 2(3) = 10.02, p = 0.02). The model explained 43.4% (Nagelkerke R 2) of the variance in outcome and correctly classified 76.9% of cases. Only the presence of vestibular signs (p = 0.04) was statistically significant in the multivariable analysis. Animals presenting with vestibular signs had a 10.8 (95% CI 1.2–98.9) times higher odds of improvement (Table 6).

Survival of guinea pigs

Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis was performed on all (n = 62) guinea pigs with head CT to determine the influence of OM/OI on their median survival time.

A similar percentage and pattern of censored cases was present between the otitis (12/34 [35.3%]) and control groups (12/28 [42.9%]). No statistically significant difference (χ 2(1) = 0.11, p = 0.74) was observed in survival distribution for animals with OM/OI (mean survival time of 210 days) and animals without (185 days).

Survival of guinea pigs with otitis

A Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis was performed for vestibular signs, respiratory signs, dental disease, rhinitis and treatment but could not be performed for sex due to the low number of females. A similar percentage of censored cases was present for animals with (6/14 [42.9%], 5/12 [41.%], 2/8 [23.1%] and 4/13 [30.8%], respectively) and without (6/20 [30.0%], 7/22 [31.8%], 11/26 [42.9%] and 8/21 [38.1%], respectively) the tested parameter. Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis was also performed for treatment type, and a similar percentage of censored cases was present for animals that underwent surgical (2/7 [28.6%]) and medical (9/22 [40.9%]) treatment.

The mean survival time for guinea pigs with vestibular signs (547 days) was greater than that for those without vestibular signs (61 days), but this difference was not statistical significant ( χ2(1) = 3.34, p = 0.07). DISCUSSION

In guinea pigs, as well as dogs, cats, rabbits and rats, OM is difficult to detect on physical examination as animals are commonly asymptomatic. 12 , 15 , 22 , 27 , 30 , 31 , 32 Therefore, clinical signs and otoscopic examinations are often unrewarding for diagnosis of OM. 8 This was previously highlighted in a study where only 11% of guinea pigs with otitis were diagnosed during their physical examination, as they presented with torticollis. 7 Otoscopic examination has been used to observe changes in the tympanic membrane's appearance. 33 Unfortunately, this examination is not accurate, as an intact or normal‐looking tympanic membrane does not exclude middle ear disease. 12 , 34 This is partly due to the different routes of transmission of OM in animals, which include progression of OE through the tympanic membrane, secondary ascending infection via the Eustachian tube from respiratory tract or dental infections and haematogenous spread. 34 , 35 Therefore, some animals may present with non‐specific clinical signs such as lethargy, dysorexia/anorexia and weight loss. 15 , 36 In rats and rabbits, up to 30% of animals with respiratory signs had subclinical OM. 13 , 36 This was also observed in our study, with 42% (15/36) of guinea pigs with otitis presenting with dysorexia/anorexia and 33% (12/36) with respiratory disease. However, when OM progresses to OI, more obvious clinical signs can be identified. 15 , 22 , 30 Reported clinical signs of OI include head tilt, ataxia, circling, torticollis and facial nerve paralysis. 30 These vestibular signs usually occur ipsilateral to the more affected side. 12 , 31 , 37 In our study, the most common vestibular sign was head tilt (93%, 13/14). Although not previously described in guinea pigs, horizontal nystagmus was observed in three (21%) patients. Overall, 61% (22/36) of affected guinea pigs had non‐specific clinical signs.

Early diagnosis of otitis is important as OM can spread into the inner ear, resulting in OI and leading to more severe clinical signs. The infection can also affect the brain by spreading through the internal acoustic meatus along the vestibulocochlear nerve. 12 Although the underlying clinical signs of OI can be easily observed, they are not pathognomonic, and OI is difficult to assess on CT unless severe destruction of the inner ear is present. However, CT is recognised as the gold standard to evaluate middle ear structure. 12 , 22 , 31 , 37 , 38 Moreover, OI cannot be seen on radiographs or ultrasound. 39 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enables a better visualisation of the soft tissue component of the external ear, inner ear and brain, but tympanic bullae are harder to visualise and prolonged anaesthesia is necessary, which carries risk for small patients. 39 Even though OI was successfully diagnosed in a ferret undergoing an MRI; in dogs, the inner ear could not be properly visualised in up to 70% of patients with peripheral vestibular signs. 40 , 41 Therefore, in our study, only animals with a head CT to confirm or exclude otitis were included. This could therefore have introduced a selection bias. A prevalence of 2.7% (25/922) at the veterinary hospital over 9 years and 2% (11/555) at the veterinary practice over 6 years was identified. Although this is higher than the previously reported 0.8%, it is less than the 25–40% recorded prevalence in laboratory guinea pigs. 7 , 14 This may be explained by the proximity of individuals in laboratory settings and potential genetic homogeneity.

The risk factors for otitis vary depending on species. Although no age or sex predisposition for otitis has been identified in rabbits, older rats were identified as being more likely to develop otitis, with females being more likely to have bilateral otitis. 11 , 16 However, in our study, male guinea pigs were slightly more predisposed to developing otitis than females, and no age predisposition was observed. In dogs, most affected animals tend to have unilateral otitis, with most cases of OM being secondary to chronic OE. 34 However, 55% of the guinea pigs in our study had bilateral OM. Of the 33 guinea pigs diagnosed with OM, 82% (n = 27) had no OE or OI. Therefore, OM may not be secondary to chronic OE in this species. Guinea pigs with vestibular signs had 21.8 times greater odds of presenting with OM/OI, resulting in vestibular signs being significant predisposing factors in guinea pigs; unlike in rabbits, where the correlation between vestibular signs and otitis is still debated. 11 A concomitant disease was identified in the majority (79%, 26/33) of guinea pigs diagnosed with OM. The most common was respiratory disease (69%, 18/26), with affected animals having 9.5 times higher odds of presenting with OM/OI. Similarly, rats with chronic respiratory disease are prone to aural infections. 13 In rabbits, 30‒80% of animals with chronic upper respiratory disease had concomitant OM, which tended to be bilateral. 12 , 35 , 36 This finding was not identified in this study, with similar numbers of animals with respiratory disease having bilateral and unilateral OM. Although not a statistically significant predisposing factor, the second most common concomitant disease in guinea pigs with OM was dental disease (50%, 13/26).

Medical treatment of OM/OI is considered ineffective for most cats, dogs and humans due to the reduced blood flow in the pus‐filled tympanic bulla, the inability to remove pus from the recessed cavity and inappropriate antibiotic diffusion. 11 , 21 , 42 Although guinea pigs have a thinner membrane in their cochlea than humans do, which could allow better penetration of systemic drugs, a combination of medical and surgical treatment is usually preferred, as in rabbits. 11 , 28 , 42 Medical treatment usually involves antibiotics selected depending on AST results. However, up to 25% of bacterial cultures yield no results. 15 In guinea pigs with otitis, bacteria isolated include S. pneumoniae (20%), Streptococcus equi subspecies zooepidemicus (15%), Bordetella bronchoseptica (12%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (11%). 32 Pasteurella and Actinobacillus have also been reported. 15 In our study, S. pseudointermedius was also isolated. Pending AST, azithromycin is usually the treatment of choice, as 98% tissue concentration has been reported in rabbits. 11 As an alternative, TMPS, chloramphenicol, parenteral cephalexin and parenteral penicillin can be used prior to prescribing fluoroquinolones, in concordance with the 2016 European regulation. 11 , 28 Animals should be provided with analgesia, and guinea pigs with severe vestibular signs require further supportive environmental care, supplemental feeding and fluid support, as reported in rabbits. 11 Chiropractic manipulation has been advocated to help resolve neurological deficits and head tilt. 11 Although medical therapy helps to stabilise clinical signs and pain, it rarely resolves OE or OM as these are frequently chronic conditions at the time of diagnosis. 43

In humans with OM, middle ear biofilms are considered to be major causes of persistence and recurring infections, requiring surgical intervention. 9 Indeed, surgical intervention is usually the treatment of choice for OM/OI in rabbits. 43 The surgical techniques used are analogous to those used in dogs and cats, with modification due to variation in the anatomy of rabbits and guinea pigs. 43 TECA‐LBO has previously been described in guinea pigs; however, three out of four animals died within 2 weeks of the surgery. 23 It was hypothesised that the invasiveness of TECA‐LBO caused the high mortality. 22 , 23 Myringotomy has also been described in guinea pigs, leading to the resolution of clinical signs in two of the three animals that underwent the procedure. The third animal improved after surgery but still had chronic head tilt. 22 In our study, only 19% (7/36) of animals that were diagnosed with otitis underwent surgery (with or without medical treatment). Indeed, the majority (61%, 22/36) received only medical treatment. For the animals without concomitant diseases, only two out of seven improved after surgery and three out of five improved after medical therapy. Most of the guinea pigs that improved initially presented with vestibular signs (4/5). Although animals presenting with vestibular signs had statistically higher odds of improving, there was no statistically significant association between outcome and presence of otitis overall. Guinea pigs that had vestibular signs had increased survival time compared with those without vestibular signs, although this difference was not statistically significant. It is possible that a type II error could have occurred due to the small sample size. Similarly, no statistically significant difference in survival time was observed between surgically (median = 45 days, range 0–833) and medically (median = 365 days, range 17–712) treated guinea pigs despite there being a numerical difference. Vestibular signs are easier for owners to identify, and animals could, therefore, have received treatment faster or been identified with having improved clinical signs without improvement of their otitis. This could have introduced bias, especially as a repeat CT was not performed. However, as no grading system for otitis is available in guinea pigs, comparisons are difficult. Further studies are required to determine whether the treatment selected and patient outcome may be correlated with the severity of otitis.

This study underscores the significance of otitis in guinea pigs, with a prevalence of 2.4% and a higher predisposition in males. Clinical signs are often non‐specific, including respiratory and dental issues, with vestibular signs being the most common specific indicator. 2 , 12 , 44 Diagnostic imaging, particularly CT, is crucial for accurate diagnosis. Treatment outcomes did not significantly differ between medical and surgical approaches, emphasizing the need for early detection and comprehensive management to improve patient welfare.
