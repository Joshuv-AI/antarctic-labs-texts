A Family of Cyclic Lipopeptides Found in Human Isolates of Bacillus Ameliorates Acute Colitis via Direct Agonism of Toll-Like Receptor 2 in a Murine Model of Inflammatory Bowel Disease
Authors: Edward Horwell, Maria Vittoria, Huynh A Hong, Philip Bearn, Simon M Cutting
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Bacillus-derived cyclic lipopeptides (surfactin, iturin, and fengycin) form potent Heterogeneous Lipopeptide Micelle (HeLM) complexes. HeLM is a small molecule that has been shown to have immunomodulatory effects. However, how HeLM regulates inflammation is not clear, moreover its application to Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), specifically Ulcerative Colitis (UC), has not been tested before.AimsTo use a murine model of IBD and determine the effects of HeLM and related molecular mechanisms of action.MethodsColitis was induced in mice by administration of 4% Dextran Sodium Sulfate. Three preparations were tested against negative and positive controls: Purified HeLM, the wild-type strain that produces it, and an isogenic mutant that does not produce HeLM. Clinical, biochemical, and histological scoring systems were used to assess the severity of colitis. RT-qPCR and cell cultures were used to determine the levels of molecular signaling. Fecal samples were processed for metagenomic analysis.ResultsPurified HeLM, and the wild-type strain, significantly decreased the severity of colitis as determined by the disease activity index (DAI), mouse colitis histology index (MCHI), fecal calprotectin, and colonic length. This effect was not seen in the mutant. HeLM was found to be an agonist to TLR-2, seemingly activating the Toll-Like Receptor 2/IL-10 pathway, with subsequent downregulation of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6). At higher concentrations HeLM inhibited lipopolysaccharide ligands from activating TLR-4. The reduction in colitis was not due to microbiome modulation, as had previously been hypothesized.ConclusionOur results indicate that HeLM ameliorates colitis by TLR-2-induced IL-10 production and possibly via the inhibition of lipopolysaccharide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10620-024-08534-2.Keywords: Animal models, Ulcerative colitis, Inflammatory bowel disease, Bacillus, Lipopeptide, Toll-like receptors, Drug discovery, Inflammation

Introduction

Ulcerative Colitis (UC), the most common sub-type of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), is a chronic disease of the colon and rectum with a prevalence of ~ 900,000 people in the USA and 4 million worldwide [1]. Typical symptoms are diarrhea, fecal urgency, rectal bleeding, weight loss, and abdominal pain. UC is more prevalent in developed economies where environmental factors are thought to be significant to the genesis of the condition. This is demonstrated via epidemiological data showing that families migrating from regions with low incidence to developed economies, exhibit an increased incidence in their second-generation of offspring [2]. Indeed, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have shown an individual’s genetics explains 4–27% of the variance in UC [3]. The underlying pathophysiology is believed to be an abnormal immune response to the colonic microbiota, of which there are ~ 1000 species typically resident in the human colon, with evidence that specific luminal bacterial species may be involved in this process [4]..

Bacillus is an ancient genus of spore-forming bacteria that is found ubiquitously in the environment. The first documented therapeutic use of Bacillus, albeit contentiously debated in the literature, was in 1940 when scientists in North Africa isolated B. subtilis from camel feces that locals consumed to treat dysentery [5]. More recently, the secondary metabolites of Bacillus have gained interest for their clinical application in the treatment of several gastrointestinal diseases, with machine learning identifying the genus as a key component for Fecal Microbial Transplant (FMT) success [6].

Bacillus velezensis, a species of Bacillus, is a commensal of the human gastrointestinal tract (GIT) [7], with several strains having recently been shown to secrete high concentrations of a family of non-ribosomal cyclic lipopeptides that are heat and acid stable, thus able to pass the stomach and enter the bowel [8]. Above the critical micelle concentration (CMC), these lipopeptides form small-molecule micelle complexes and are referred to as Heterogeneous Lipopeptide Micelles (HeLM) [9]. HeLM has been shown to exhibit activity against various pathobionts associated with UC [7] as well as having specific immunomodulatory effects thought to be mediated via Toll-Like Receptors (TLR) [10, 11]. Accordingly, our hypothesis follows that B. velezensis (or its secondary metabolite—HeLM) may have utility in the treatment of UC. We will report on work that used a purified preparation of HeLM, from a previously characterized strain of B. velezensis, EHv5WT that was isolated from healthy human fecal samples [7].

Thus, the purpose of this study was to establish a murine model of colitis with symptoms and histology similar to that found in humans with active UC and determine the effects of oral administration of HeLM and EHv5WT while also exploring the possible mechanisms. To remove any doubt on the active molecule, we go further in creating an isogenic mutant of EHv5WT that does not produce HeLM.

Materials and Methods

Bacillus velezensis Strains

Two strains of B. velezensis are reported in this study. EHv5WT is a wild-type HeLM producing strain that has been fully characterized [7]. Previous work demonstrated the greatest potential for therapeutic effect and has been selected accordingly [7, 9, 12]. EHv5HeLM- is an isogenic mutant that carries an insertional disruption in the surfactin synthetase (srfAA) gene responsible for the non-ribosomal synthesis of the lipopeptide surfactin, the main constituent of the HeLM group, without it the CMC is not reached and micelle formation is not achieved [9].

Interventions

(i)EHv5WT—a suspension of EHv5WT in PBS. Bacteria were grown in brain heart infusion broth (BHIB) at 37 °C for 8 h and then sub-cultured in 200 mL of BHIB with 16-h incubation at 37 °C. The culture, composed of vegetative cells (~ 80%) and spores (~ 20%), was harvested (10,000 × g, 10 min) and suspended in PBS for a concentration of 5 × 109 CFU/mL.

(iii)HeLM—purification of HeLMs from EHv5WT culture has been described previously [7, 9]. Briefly, the supernatant of EHv5WT was filter sterilized (0.20-µm pore size) and then precipitated in 25% (w/v) ammonium sulfate at 4 °C overnight. Following centrifugation (3000 × g, 30 min), the infranatant was encapsulated in a cellulose ester dialysis membrane (MWCO = 3.5–5 kDa; Spectra/Por) against dialysis buffer (three volume changes), reducing the contaminant level by 1 × 106. The sample was then further purified with size exclusion–high-performance liquid chromatography (SEC–HPLC) (Kratos Spectroflow 400 HPLC with Superdex 200). The purity of the active SEC fraction has been demonstrated previously using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry [7]. Due to the impracticalities in directly assessing the concentration of HeLM in EHv5WT, we utilized its surfactant properties as a surrogate marker, using an oil displacement assay [13], and diluted until it had parity with the activity from the EHv5WT solution. The EHv5HeLM- showed no oil displacement activity.

Animals

Sixty-eight pathogen-free BALB/cN mice (female and male, aged 10 weeks, Charles River, UK) were housed in groups of up to five in HEPA-filtered individually ventilated cages (IVCs). Animals were maintained at a controlled temperature (18–21 °C) with 12-h light/dark cycles and given ad libitum access to standard mouse chow (5LF5, LabDiet) and water. To increase the efficiency of processing the animals, this experiment was done over three different time points, all under the same conditions. The experiment went through ethical consideration at our institution’s Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body and was approved for license by the UK Home Office.

Induction of Acute Colitis and Treatment

Dextran Sodium Sulfate (DSS) is a chemical widely used to induce and model UC. It results in a histological pattern that is morphologically analogous to human UC: mucosal erosions, increased epithelial permeability, submucosal inflammatory infiltration, and crypt loss [14]. A pilot study to ascertain the optimal concentration of DSS over a seven-day course to produce a moderate–severe acute colitis found 4% to be optimal, in keeping with similar published models [14]. Colitis-grade DSS (MP BioMedicals) was added to autoclaved H2O to 4% (w/v), and given ad libitum. A priori calculations for group size estimation, based on weight loss data from this pilot, determined a minimum sample size of 5 per group. A random number generator was used to assign each cage of mice to a treatment group. Each group was given a daily intragastric (I.G.) gavage (0.2 mL) via a soft-tip 20G polypropylene feeding tube of either PBS (naïve and DSS-Only groups) or treatment (Fig. 1). On day 7, water was exchanged for 4% DSS in all groups, other than the naïve group. Fresh DSS was prepared every 2–3 days. All animals were sacrificed on day 15.

Clinical Assessment of Colitis

The parameters for the disease activity index (DAI) [15]: body weight, stool consistency, and rectal bleeding (micro or macroscopic), were measured daily starting 1 day prior to the first treatment dose (day − 1). Scores from each category were added to create a total score ranging from 0 (no disease) to 12 (severe disease) and are presented as group averages. Two investigators were used to calculate the DAI to reduce potential subjective error in quantifying stool consistency. Microscopic fecal blood was detected using a guaiac-based Fecal Occult Blood test kit (Hema-Screen, Immunostics, Inc.). After culling, the colorectum and anus were excised (equivalence to a pan-proctocolectomy), the total intestinal length was photographed and measured against a ruler, and later the length of the colorectum and cecum were measured.

Histological Assessment of Colitis

The rectosigmoid colon was sectioned and fixed in 10% (v/v) neutral buffered formalin (Sigma-Aldrich) prior to histological processing with hematoxylin and eosin staining (Royal Veterinary College, UK). Each slide was blinded, assessed under the microscope, and scored according to the Mouse Colitis Histology Index (MCHI) [16]. Two investigators separately scored each blinded specimen and where scores were divergent, the mean of the two were used.

Biochemical Assessment of Colitis

Feces, collected via the clean catch method on the final day of the experiment, were stored at − 20 °C for future processing. Following the manufacturers protocol, a two-site sandwich technique enzyme-linked-immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used for the direct measurement of calprotectin (measured at 450 nm) in each fecal sample (Immunodiagnostik AG & SpectraMax Plus384). Each sample was measured in duplicate for accuracy. Data were analyzed using the Four-Parameter Algorithm to generate ng/mL quantifications of calprotectin present in each sample.

Gene Expression Analysis of the Rectosigmoid Colon

To elucidate potential pharmacodynamic effects, RNA expression was analyzed via RT-qPCR. The rectum and distal sigmoid were chosen to reflect the typically distal nature of UC in humans, a physiological observation that is also displayed in this model [17]. The rectosigmoid was sectioned to provide 100-mg tissue samples for RNA extraction. Tissue was placed in 1-mL TRIzol and homogenized for 30 s. Due to the inhibitory nature of residual DSS on reverse transcriptase, a modified RNA extraction method was used, incorporating Chomczynski’s guanidinium thiocyanate–phenol–chloroform precipitation [18] with Viennois’s lithium chloride purification [19]. Recent evidence suggests that colonic RNA degradation occurs within 10 min of cell death [20]. Accordingly, to minimize the time to TRIzol, a standard technique was developed with the aim to perform rectosigmoid excision and processing within 1.5 min from sacrifice. Residual gDNA was removed and reverse transcription was performed (QuantiTect Reverse Transcription, Qiagen) and qPCR then followed with each specimen was measured in triplicate to increase accuracy (QuantiNova Sybr Green PCR, Qiagen and Rotor-Gene 6000 Thermocycler, Corbett Research), all following the manufacturer’s protocol. Primer sequences (Supplementary Fig. 1) were chosen from published papers, validated in silico [21] and during the pilot study. The Livak 2−ΔΔCt method was used to generate a comparative gene expression fold change against the naïve control group, using the target house-keeping gene (β-actin). Results were then transformed logarithmically for statistical analysis and are shown to the base 2.

Cell Culture Analysis

HEK-Blue hTLR2 and hTLR4 cell lines (InvivoGen) were used to screen for potential agonistic/antagonistic effects of HeLM on these selected receptor proteins. This was done in duplicates to increase accuracy. HEK-Blue Null1 cells were used as the negative control. Pam2CSK4, LPS EK, and hTNF-α were used for agonistic controls in the TLR-2, TLR-4, and Null1 cell lines, respectively. For the antagonistic controls, the anti-hTLR2, LPS RS, and hTNF-α were used. Quanti-Blue was added to the cell culture supernatant and optical density was measured at 650 nm. For detailed methods, see Supplementary Fig. 2.

Metagenomic Analysis of the Colonic Microbiome

Feces were collected via the “clean catch” method from each mouse on day 0 and at the end of the experiment. DNA was extracted (Quick-DNA Fecal/Soil MicroPrep Kit, Zymo), as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. 16S rRNA was amplified in the V3–4 region with sequencing, library preparation (Ilumina’s TruSeq) and bio-informatic analysis was carried out by Novogene. Species richness and diversity within communities (α diversity) were measured by the observable OTUs and the Shannon Index of diversity. Comparative analysis of microbial community composition of the groups before and after treatment (β diversity) was determined by UniFrac distance (weighted and unweighted) and by Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, plotted on a Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) chart.

Statistical Analysis

All animals used were included in the analysis. GraphPad Prism (Version 9.4.1 for Windows, Dotmatics) was used for analysis. Statistical significance was accepted as p < 0.05. One-way ANOVA was used for group differences in colonic length, MCHI, calprotectin, and RT-qPCR results. Data were normally distributed, as assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05); homogeneity of variance was assessed by Brown–Forsythe, being rejected if p < 0.05. Dunnett’s post hoc analysis was used to compare the results to the control. For any multiple comparisons, Tukey’s post hoc analysis was employed. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. A two-way repeated measures ANOVA was used for the DAI scores to determine the effect of the different treatment groups over time. Sphericity was assumed to be present as per the Greenhouse and Geisser method. Dunnett’s multiple comparisons post hoc analysis was performed and data is graphed as the mean. The Kruskal–Wallis test was employed for group analysis of metagenomic data, with post hoc analysis with the Tukey test for any significant values.

A list of reagents used (with catalogue numbers) can be found in Supplementary Fig. 6.

Results

There was no evidence (reduction in water consumption, change in stool type, weight loss, or scoring on the Mouse Grimace Scale [22]) that daily I.G. gavage of PBS or treatment caused any toxicity or adverse events during the first seven days prior to initiating DSS administration. Water consumption (with or without DSS) for each group was monitored during throughout the experiment, via weighing of the water bottle, with no significant difference in consumption between the groups.

Severity of Colitis

Weight Loss

When compared to the naive control, the only two groups that lost significant weight were the “DSS-Only” group with a mean weight change of − 9.5% (p = < 0.0001 [95% CI 6.848 to 16.58]) and EHv5HeLM- group with a mean weight change of − 6.9% (p = 0.0007 [95% CI 3.106 to 15.12]). EHv5WT and HeLM both showed a non-significant weight loss of − 2.92% (p = 0.1074 [95% CI − 0.6731 to 10.96]) and − 0.68% (p = 0.5586 [95% CI − 2.494 to 8.307]), respectively, Fig. 2A.

Colonic Length

Colonic length has an inverse relationship to colonic inflammation and is established as a marker for the severity of inflammation in murine models of colitis [14]. Compared to the naïve control group (10.7 cm), all groups had significant shortening of the colon. However, compared to the DSS-only group (7.4 cm), EHv5WT and HeLM did significantly better with an average length of 9.3 (p = < 0.0001 [95% CI − 2.588 to − 1.112]) and 9.2 cm (p = < 0.0001 [95% CI − 2.439 to − 1.069]), respectively. There was significant shortening in the EHv5HeLM- group, 7.0 cm, which was not significant to the DSS-only group (p = 0.5137), Fig. 2B. No significant difference in the cecal length was found.

Disease Activity Index (DAI)

Symptoms of colitis, defined as a DAI score ≥ 2, were first displayed 3 days after administration of DSS, for both DSS-only and EHv5HeLM- groups. The severity of colitis in these two groups increased thereafter and became significantly divergent to the naïve control after five days of DSS (p = 0.0002 and 0.0004, respectively). In contrast, EHv5WT and HeLM remained protected from colitis, only reaching significant change from the naïve control after 7 days of DSS (p = 0.0170 and 0.0197), Fig. 2C. Importantly, when compared to the DSS-only group (the positive control), with a mean DAI score of 9.5 on day 15, EHv5WT and HeLM provided significantly successful treatment against colitis such that their scores were 3.8 (p = < 0.0001 [95% CI 3.105 to 8.161]) and 3.9 (p = < 0.0001 [95% CI 3.345 to 8.029]), respectively. This protective effect was not seen in EHv5HeLM- which had a mean score of 8.7 (p = 0.8659 [95% CI = − 2.185 to 3.919]). EHv5WT, 3.9, and HeLM, 3.85, showed no significant increase in DAI when compared to the DSS-Only control, (p = 0.1731 and 0.0643, respectively), Supplementary Fig. 4. While the male mice in all groups demonstrated a more severe pattern of colitis, this was still significantly ameliorated in the EHv5WT and HeLM groups. When repeated measures from the analysis are removed, these two groups remain statistically non-divergent from the naïve control—i.e., their DAI score was unchanged despite receiving DSS.

Histological Scoring

The MCHI was developed to standardize histological assessment of colitis in the murine model and has been validated with a high inter-rater reliability [16]. The score range is 0 (no inflammation) to 22 (severe disease). The score for the naïve group on the MCHI histological scoring system was 0. The EHv5WT group and the HeLM groups scored 2.8 and 3, respectively, this was not significantly different from the naïve group (p = 0.5212 and 0.4429), Fig. 3A. The DSS-Only group had the highest score of 15.5 (p = < 0.0001), EHv5HeLM- scored 15.2 (p = < 0.0001). While the MCHI scoring system does not use categories (e.g., mild, moderate, or severe), it is our opinion that the EHv5WT and HeLM scores would represent a mild histological grading, equivalent to a Nancy Index score of 1. Accordingly, while there is no statistical difference in these scores and the naïve group, it should be noted that there is still a degree of “mild” inflammation that is seen on histology that could have clinical significance.

Fecal Calprotectin

The heterodimer complex of S100A8/S100A9 proteins (Calprotectin) are pro-inflammatory metal sequesters found in abundance in the neutrophil cytoplasm. Upon neutrophil apoptosis, these proteins are released into the colonic lumen. Measurement of calprotectin has an established history in clinical practice as an objective measurement of colitis severity.

While both Ehv5WT and the HeLM groups demonstrated elevated calprotectin levels, compared to the naïve control (5.6 ng/mL), there was non-significant difference with mean concentrations of 16.3 (p = 0.4006, 95% CI − 28.27 to 6.850) and 15.1 ng/mL (p = 0.5178, 95% CI − 27.06 to 8.059), respectively, Fig. 3B. The DSS-only group measured 39.6 ng/mL, while EHv5HeLM- was 41.0 ng/mL (p = < 0.0001 for both).

Gene Expression Analysis

“Pro-inflammatory” Genes

All groups receiving 4% DSS had an upregulation in TNF-α; however, EHv5WT and HeLM had a significantly diminished response compared to the DSS-Only group, Fig. 4A. The expression fold change were as follows: DSS-Only = 2.72, EHv5WT = 1.64 (p = 0.0014), HeLM = 2.10 (p = 0.0445), and EHv5HeLM- = 2.31 (p = 0.3286). IL-6 gene expression was also increased across all groups. However, there was a significant difference, with a moderated expression change, in the EHv5WT = 0.53 (p = 0.0007) and HeLM = 1.13 (p = 0.0172) compared to the EHv5HeLM- = 3.06 (p = 0.1165) and the DSS-Only group = 2.24, Fig. 4B. IL-1β was increased in the DSS-Only and EHv5HeLM- groups (1.78 and 1.05, respectively). The HeLM group had a dampened level of expression change, 0.82 with the CI crossing 0 (p = 0.2408). The EHv5WT group had a marginal decrease in gene expression, − 0.08 (p = 0.0127), Fig. 4C. We found no significant difference in IL-17 or IL-23 expression across the groups (data not shown).

Anti-inflammatory and “Protective” Genes

Gene expression for IL-10 was markedly divergent between the treatment groups. The gene expression change for DSS-Only was − 0.849, similar to EHv5HeLM- = − 0.98 (p = 0.9896). The other two groups however had significantly increased expression with EHv5WT = 2.14 (p = < 0.0001) and HeLM = 2.71 (p = < 0.0001), Fig. 4D. We found a non-significant general trend of a reduced expression of the Claudin-1 gene across all groups other than the HeLM group that showed a marginal increase in expression, albeit with a broad range. The expression fold changes were as follows: DSS-Only = − 1.20, EHv5WT = − 0.75 (p = 0.7572), HeLM = 0.33 (p = 0.0127), and EHv5HeLM- = − 0.42 (p = 0.03723, Fig. 4E. There was no significant difference found for MUC-2 (data not shown).

Toll-Like Receptors (TLRs)

The DSS-Only and EHv5HeLM- groups were similar to each other, with the expression fold change for TLR-4 being increased 0.27 and 0.22 (p = 0.9933), respectively, while that of TLR-2 was decreased − 0.42 and − 1.11 (p = 0.5236), respectively. The inverse was true for EHv5WT and HeLM-treated groups with TLR-4 showing a reduced expression − 0.67 (p = 0.0011) and − 0.40 (p = 0.0090) and TLR-2 being increased 0.98 (p = 0.0360) and 1.69 (p = 0.0005), respectively, Fig. 4F, G, suggesting that the HeLM molecule may be agonistic to TLR-2.

Cell Culture Analysis

Purified HeLM was found to be toxic to the HEK-Blue cell culture lines at high concentrations (150 μg/mL). HeLM, and its cyclic lipopeptides, have strong biosurfactant properties that disrupt membranes and have long been known to be cytotoxic at higher concentrations to a variety of cell lines in vitro [23]. Accordingly, HeLM required a 1/200 dilution (0.75 μg/mL) for incubation to be successful. Results were deemed significant if the fold induction was ≥ 2. There was no agonism of the HEK-Blue Null1 cell culture (negative control), furthermore HeLM did not inhibit the agonism of TNFα (positive control). HeLM demonstrated significant agonism to the HEK-Blue hTLR2 cell line. This was seen down to a dilution of 0.5 × 10−7 (0.00075 ng/mL), Table 1. No significant agonistic effects were seen on the HEK-Blue hTLR4 cell line, Table 1. At higher concentrations (0.75–0.075 μg/mL), HeLM was found to have a significant antagonistic effect on HEK-Blue hTLR4. This appears to be due to HeLM having an inhibitory effect directly on the control agonist, LPS (derived from Escherichia coli), rather than on the hTLR4 complex, Table 1.

Discussion

UC is a chronic inflammatory disease of the colorectum, where the exact pathophysiology has yet to be fully defined. While there have been several theories over the true pathogenesis, scientific progress over the past decade supports the general hypothesis that UC is caused by mucosal barrier defects leading to an abnormal immune response to the luminal microbiome, in a genetically predisposed host [26]. While several species that have shown correlation with UC (for example, certain strains of E. coli and Clostridium difficile), no single bacterium appears to be causative. In a more general sense, it seems that an overall decrease in bacterial abundance, particularly in Firmicutes, is predictive for UC, but the causative nature of this finding remains contentious. Recent clinical trials in humans suggest that oral administration of selected bacterial species can induce remission and prevent relapse in IBD, with the effect particularly apparent in UC [27]. These trials include bacteria under the “probiotic” label as well as the more targeted bacterially derived “secondary metabolite” approaches [28]. The latter playing a vital role historically in the development of other medicines (e.g., penicillin, pravastatin, doxorubicin, and sirolimus).

Bacillus are increasingly being recognized as an integral part of the normal commensal make-up of the colonic microbiota [29]. They are acquired from the environment and proliferate within the GI tract, becoming allochthonous in the human colon [8]. Many Bacillus species produce lipopeptides (LPs), and we have discovered a strain of B. velezensis (EHv5WT), isolated from healthy human samples, that produces these in significant concentration. These LPs are from the surfactin, iturin, and fengycin families, with surfactin being the dominant component present in our strain and required to achieve the CMC [7, 9]. These three families are cyclic lipopeptides that exist in several isomeric forms with clinically relevant attributes. The amphiphilic nature of these LPs have been shown to breakdown the biofilm of several gastrointestinal pathogens [12], documented anti-bacterial, antiviral, and anti-fungal properties [7], as well as having demonstrated an anti-inflammatory effect in pancreatic cell cultures [30]. These LPs have been shown to have low toxicity across several different species [31]. Interestingly, when these specific LPs reach the CMC, micelle formation occurs—creating a Heterogeneous Lipopeptide Micelle (HeLM). HeLM is a small molecules ~ 10 nm in size. The individual LPs act synergistically when part of the HeLM, and have greater biological potency than the sum of their individual components [32]. Indeed, we have previously shown that using a solution of an individual lipopeptide has a significantly diminished levels of activity in vitro, suggesting the heterogeneous micelle is crucial [7].

The DSS (dextran sodium sulfate) model of acute colitis, first described in 1985 [33], is well established at modeling UC. Administration of 4% DSS resulted in clear pathological signs of colitis in the positive control (“DSS-Only”). Strikingly, EHv5WT proved protective against DSS-mediated colitis. It does this via the production of the biologically active secondary metabolite—HeLM. This is demonstrated in two ways: Firstly, the group that received the purified preparation of HeLM showed a near identical response both in regards to the efficacy against colitis and transcriptome effects that were seen in the EHv5WT group. Secondly, the isogenic mutant EHv5HeLM-, which does not synthesize HeLM but is otherwise identical, conferred no protection against colitis and was essentially indistinguishable from animals in the DSS-only group. Interestingly, when giving mice EHv5WT in tenfold dilutions, a dose response is noted, with a sharp rise in inflammation after the 100th dilution (5 × 106 CFU/mL), Supplementary Fig. 3. Our results demonstrated that male mice developed more severe colitis than the female groups, an effect documented in C/57 mice elsewhere [34]. Despite this increase in severity, the beneficial treatment effect of EHv5WT and HeLM on the male mice was maintained and remained significantly improved to the positive control.

The remaining question is in understanding the mechanism of action. The results from our metagenomic analysis suggests that there was no significant variation in the α or β diversity of the microbiome during the study. While we did find subtle variation when investigating the taxonomic classification down to the species level, this effect size was small and needs to be put in the context of the voluminous data that is produced by Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) methods and having a false-positive rate set at 0.05. Given the documented in vivo antimicrobial effects of HeLM, we were expecting to see a decrease in α diversity. It is possible that the use of autoclaved water and housing the mice in IVC cage limited any potential effect on the microbiome. Moreover, we did not perform metagenomic analysis at day 7, potentially missing any effect that EHv5WT or HeLM has on the microbiome when not co-administered with DSS. Despite this, recently published data using a strain of B. velezensis that produces the similar LPs also found no change in the microbiome after 7 and 14 days of administration, corroborating our findings [35]. We therefore cautiously conclude then that the amelioration of colitis demonstrated here does not result from secondary effects via a shift in the microbiome.

We have shown that mice receiving EHv5WT and HeLM demonstrate an upregulation of TLR-2 and downregulation of TLR-4 genes. Expression of TLR-2 and TLR-4 is dynamic and dependent on the luminal microbiota, with a growing body of evidence implicating them in the pathogenesis of IBD [36]. This guided our cell culture work, which demonstrated clearly that purified HeLM acts as a strong agonist to TLR-2, with activity down to 0.00075 ng/mL. TLR-2 forms heterodimers TLR-1 and TLR-6 allowing for the detection and discrimination to a range of different lipopeptides, typically from Gram-positive bacteria [36]. TLR-2 agonism has the potential for both pro- and anti-inflammatory outcomes dependent on the stimulating ligand [37]. One study has suggested that TLR-2 agonism maintains and repairs the colonic epithelial barrier in a context-dependent manner, we could not find evidence of this effect in the transcriptome of our study [38]. Our results suggest that HeLM induces an anti-inflammatory effect via TLR-2, seemingly by the dependent TLR-2/IL-10 pathway [39, 40], a mechanism that has been observed in other studies exploring the anti-inflammatory mechanisms of HeLM-based LPs [11, 41]. This is further exemplified by the results of the gene expression analysis, which showed significant upregulation of IL-10, with concomitant downregulation of TNF-α and IL-1β. We cannot be certain, however, that the mechanism of action behind the reduction in inflammatory cytokines is purely TLR-2 dependent. It is possible that these effects are independent of HeLM or perhaps there are distinct mechanisms involved that have not been detected by our experiments that could, in part, explain the reduced expression of inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, one paper has suggested the IL-1β is important in colonic epithelial recovery, raising the question of the utility of this treatment in the chronic setting [42].

The downregulation of TLR-4 should also be discussed. This receptor molecule is involved in the recognition of LPS from Gram-negative bacteria and is a key driver in IBD as well as in cytokine storm seen in septic shock [43]. We believe there are two reasons for this downregulation, firstly our cell culture results demonstrate that HeLM was antagonistic to TLR-4, but only when in combination with the stimulatory LPS. This finding strongly suggests that HeLM either binds to, or denatures, the E. coli-derived LPS molecule, thereby inhibiting the ability of LPS to act antagonistically on TLR-4, rather than HeLM acting directly on TLR-4. We are not the first to report on this interesting characteristic of the molecule, with evidence suggesting there is direct inactivation of the potent endotoxin Lipid-A, a lipid chain that forms the LPS complex [44]. A second potential explanation regards the observed increased IL-10 expression, which is known to downregulate TLR-4 as part of its anti-inflammatory repertoire [45].

There are some limitations to this study that we will address. Firstly, we employed one murine model, DSS-induced colitis. This is an invaluable model and is widely used due to its reproducibility and broad-based mechanistic nature—modeling the immune response to the microbiome in the setting of colonic epithelia damage [17]. However, future research using different models may prove insights that have been missed in this study, e.g., assessing HeLM on transgenic/knockout mice and T-cell transfer models of IBD. As such, the applicability of these findings may not translate into clinical utility. Secondly, there is the potential that the use of beta-actin as a house-keeping gene introduced a degree of inaccuracy in the gene expression analysis. It has been shown previously in C/57 mice that this gene, while it is not statistically different between colitic and normal states, has a high standard deviation in CT values which can reduce the statistical power and accuracy [46]. Nonetheless, the widespread contemporaneous use of this house-keeping gene for this animal model, as well as our prior experience with it, lead us to use beta-actin over other potential candidates (e.g., Eef2). Thirdly, the metagenomic method employed (16S rRNA) does not examine any shift in fungal or viral/phage abundance and is therefore potentially missing secondary microbiome effects. Moreover, we have not examined changes in the metabolome, which can be independent of metagenomic shifts. Further examination is clearly required, and we are actively developing a clinical trial to assess the safety and efficacy of HeLM in human subjects.

In summary, this study has identified a strain of B. velezensis that is able to successfully treat moderate-severe colitis in a murine model. It does this via the small molecule, HeLM. We have shown that HeLM acts as a strong TLR-2 agonist and apparent antagonist (via LPS inhibition) of TLR-4. Accordingly, we observed reduced expression of specific Th1-associated cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) and increased IL-10. This favorable change in cytokine gene expression diminishes the inflammatory response and increase protective phenotypes, significantly reducing the severity of colitis. Moreover, despite HeLM’s in vitro evidence of antibiotic effects, we observed no perturbation in the microbiome of the colon.
