Peptide Hydrogel Enhances Adipocyte Viability and Reduces Inflammatory Response in Fat Grafting: A Preclinical Study
Authors: Itay Wiser, Noa Harduf, Guy Tomer, Gregory Perelman, Alexandra Tsitrina, Hanna Rapaport, Guy Gersh, Inna Solodeev, Orel Govrin, Bar Kamhine, Yoav Barnea, Benjamin Meilik, Yaron Rapaport, Nadav Orr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFat grafting is gaining traction in regenerative and aesthetic medicine because of ease of adipose tissue harvesting and potential applications in autologous soft tissue reconstructions. A key challenge remains the limited viability of lipoaspirate-derived adipocytes following the procedures of harvesting, processing, and implantation, which leads to suboptimal outcomes in graft survival.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to evaluate whether a hydrogel composed of the amphiphilic and anionic β-sheet peptide Pro-Asp-(Phe-Asp)5-Pro (PFD) enhances fat graft outcomes by improving adipocyte viability, reducing inflammatory response, and facilitating tissue integration compared with conventional fat grafting.MethodsPFD-lipoaspirate mixtures were characterized in vitro, and a lead mixture was tested in porcine models used for initial safety and efficacy assessment of subcutaneous implantation at 1 and 3 months after treatment. A single animal was used for each time point, with multiple treatment sites in each animal and a minimum of 3 sites for each of the treatment and control formulations. Histological and immunohistochemical analyses were performed.ResultsPFD hydrogel exhibited superior injectability with smaller extrusion force than hyaluronic acid. The hydrogel could be readily mixed with lipoaspirate to form cohesive, uniform compositions that demonstrated a smoother and more consistent injection force pattern, compared with lipoaspirate only. Hydrogel−lipoaspirate formulations significantly enhanced cell viability compared with lipoaspirate. Within the lipoaspirate, the hydrogel degraded enzymatically. In vivo studies showed the hydrogel−lipoaspirate seamless integration with the surrounding adipose tissue with a 7-fold reduction in inflammatory zones compared with lipoaspirate-only treatment. Macrophage profiling indicated a transition toward predominantly M2 polarization between 1 and 3 months, supported by neovascularization and near-complete resorption by 3 months.ConclusionsPFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixture showed favorable outcomes in this preclinical model through improved adipocyte survival, reduced inflammatory response, and seamless tissue integration. These preliminary findings suggest potential utility in fat grafting applications, although clinical validation remains necessary.Level of Evidence: 5 (Therapeutic)

Autologous fat grafting has become one of the most rapidly growing procedures in plastic surgery, with over 200,000 procedures performed annually in the United States representing a 72% increase over the past 5 years.1 Despite widespread adoption across reconstructive and aesthetic applications, fat grafting remains challenged by fundamental limitations: unpredictable volume retention rates ranging from 25% to 80% and the frequent necessity for multiple procedures to achieve desired outcomes.2-4

A key challenge in this area is the limited viability of adipocytes following the procedures of harvesting, processing, and injecting, which leads to suboptimal outcomes in graft survival and functionality.5,6 For fat grafting to be effective as a filler and provide reliable volume retention postinjection, it is critical to maximize adipocyte survival and preserve the regenerative capacity of the fat tissue throughout the procedure.7 Contemporary studies demonstrate that 50% to 90% of transferred adipocytes undergo apoptosis within the first 48 h posttransplantation, primarily because of ischemic conditions and mechanical trauma during processing.8 This massive cellular loss necessitates overcorrection and contributes to unpredictable aesthetic outcomes, limiting broader clinical adoption of fat grafting techniques.9

In the past 2 decades, around 40 medical devices for fat harvesting, processing, and injection were cleared by the FDA.10 However, none have achieved market dominance or significantly transformed the fat grafting landscape, highlighting the continued need for innovative approaches.

Various biomaterials have been developed to provide an extracellular matrix (ECM)-like environment to support adipocyte survival and function following implantation. Hydrogels are particularly attractive in this context because of their high-water content, biocompatibility, and tunable mechanical and biochemical properties.11-13 The mechanical properties of biomaterials used as ECM-like implants are crucial for ensuring the success of the procedure. Proper mechanical compatibility helps prevent tissue damage and inflammatory responses, ultimately reducing the need for revision surgeries. It has been proposed that minimizing mechanical stress on fat cells during processing is crucial for preserving cell viability.14

Peptide-based hydrogels may mimic the topological and mechanical properties of natural ECM and, as such, may provide excellent opportunities to direct cell behavior for regenerative medicine and tissue engineering. The relatively facile, inexpensive, and automatable preparation of peptides, coupled with low batch-to-batch variability, make self-assembling peptide hydrogels increasingly attractive for a wide range of biomedical applications.15-17 Recent developments in functional biomaterials, based on designed β-sheet peptides, show particular promise.18-21

The amphiphilic and anionic β-sheet peptide Pro-Asp-(Phe-Asp)5-Pro (PFD) forms stable hydrogels through self-assembly into fibril bilayers.18,20,21 These hydrogels have demonstrated the capacity to support the growth of various cell types and exhibit bone tissue regeneration through calcium-phosphate biomineralization.22

Here, we developed PFD-hydrogel formulations that enable the formation of clinically relevant hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures aimed at enhancing outcomes of autologous fat injections. We focused on the formulations’ compositions, mechanical properties, ability to support cell viability, degradation mechanisms, and effects on autologous lipoaspirate integration and healing in a swine model.

METHODS

PFD-Hydrogel Formulation

PFD peptide exhibiting the sequence PDFDFDFDFDFDP, with >98% purity by high-performance liquid chromatography, was purchased from PolyPeptide, Strasbourg, France. The peptide was first solubilized in water, then titrated with diluted hydrochloric acid (HCl) to pH 6 to 6.5 and vigorously mixed until a transparent hydrogel was achieved. Hydrogel sterilization was performed based on ISO-17665.

PFD-Hydrogel Characterizations

Viscoelastic properties of hydrogels were measured using dynamic frequency sweep tests on an AR 2000 controlled stress rheometer (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE) operating in a cone and plate mode (4° cone angle and 20 mm diameter). The linear regime between 0.5 and 100 Pa was first determined by stress sweep measurements at a constant angular frequency of 6 rad/s. The elastic and loss modules, G′ and G″, were measured in frequency sweep mode in the 0.05 to 100 rad/s range and at a constant stress within the linear regime. Viscosity was measured over the range of 0.1 to 100 Hz.

Hydrogels were visualized by transmission electron microscope (TEM; FEI Talos F200C, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) following their disintegration in deionized water (1:100 v/v), immediate placement on carbon-coated copper grids, and staining with Uranyl-Less.

A force gauge apparatus (FG-5000A Lutron, Lutron Electronics, Coopersburg, PA) was used to measure the force required to extrude 0.5 mL hydrogel compositions at a constant rate of 1 mL/min or a Universal Tensile Testing Machine (Shimadzu Corporation, Nakagyō-ku, Kyoto, Japan) by extruding the materials from a 10 mL syringe through a 3 mm cannula at a rate of 10 mm/min. This measurement was applied to unprocessed human lipoaspirate, mixed lipoaspirate, and PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixture. Mixtures were prepared by syringe-to-syringe transfers using a standard Luer lock connector (Nordson Corporation, Westlake, OH).

Human Lipoaspirate Processing and Viability Assessment

This study was conducted under the approval number 0330-25 of the Ethics Committee of Tel Aviv Medical Center (Ichilov), Tel Aviv, Israel. The lipoaspirate was processed using a standard Coleman procedure.23 Briefly, lipoaspirate, ∼200 mL, collected from a single female donor, was allowed to settle for 20 min. Next, the lower liquid phase was drained, and the upper oil layer was removed. Fat was washed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (1:1 v/v) and centrifuged for 3 min at 300×g after which the lower liquid layer was removed, and the lipoaspirate was homogenized using syringe-to-syringe mixing.

The viability of fat cells in PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures was measured by Alamar blue assay.24,25 The mixtures were incubated for 3 days at 37°C, 5% CO2. Then, cultures were supplemented with Alamar blue reagent (0.025 mg/mL), and following a 3 h reaction, fluorescence was measured (560 nm excitation, 590 nm emission). The relative viability results were expressed as relative fluorescence units per gram of fat.

Hydrogel Degradation Studies

The degradation of PFD into peptide fragments was monitored using a ninhydrin assay, which yields a yellow color for the free amine of the proline residue and a blue color (optical density [OD] = 570 nm) for the other amino acids. Based on this distinct difference, the degradation of PFD with its only free amine at the proline termini was monitored by the appearance of blue color in the digested peptide solution (Supplemental Figure 1). Using calibration curves, the OD at 570 nm could be correlated to the concentration of free amine groups, hence to the extent of peptide degradation. Ninhydrin (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA) was dissolved in ethanol at a concentration of 2% v/v and subsequently mixed with an equal volume of acetate buffer (pH 4.5). For the assay, 100 μL of the freshly prepared ninhydrin solution was added to each sample containing the peptide substrate subjected to enzymatic degradation. The mixture was incubated under controlled conditions to allow a complete reaction between ninhydrin and liberated amines. The resulting blue color was quantified spectrophotometrically, providing a measure of the extent of peptide hydrolysis. Appropriate controls, including nondegraded peptide and reagent blanks, were included to account for background signal.

The PFD peptide was incubated with lipoaspirate to evaluate the possible degradation of the peptide by unknown enzymes present in fat tissue (see Appendix). A summary of the test compositions and the controls is provided in Supplemental Table 3. Furthermore, mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF, Kratos Analytical, Manchester, United Kingdom) analysis was used to identify degradation products following enzyme cleavage both by enzymes present in lipoaspirate and, for a reference, by proteinase-K, which is known to cleave peptide bonds next to the carboxyl-terminal of aromatic amino acids.

In Vivo Porcine Model

Animal studies were performed at a certified Contract Research Organization (Lahav, Israel), approval number NPC-La-IL-2301-162-4, according to the rules set forth by the animal welfare regulation. The study was designed to evaluate local tissue reaction and implant dynamics at 1- and 3-month periods after subcutaneous injection of PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures into the fat layer in female domestic pigs. One animal was treated for each of the 2 time points with 14 injection sites as follows: PFD hydrogel 2% w/v mixed with lipoaspirate at 16:84 v/v (hydrogel only, 3 sites) or at 33:67 v/v (lipoaspirate only, 4 sites), respectively. Autologous lipoaspirate was extracted using the modified Coleman method.23 Female animals, 80 to 100 kg at study initiation, were sedated, and implant locations were tattooed on the dorsal area, on both sides of the centerline. Next, 180 to 240 mL of tumescent solution containing epinephrine (1 mg/mL), lidocaine (1 mg/mL), and bicarbonate (0.1%) was injected into the extraction area. After 10 to 20 min incubation, fat was extracted using a 3 mm aspiration cannula, processed as described above, and mixed with PFD hydrogel using a dual-syringe device with a static mixer (Medmix, Baar, Switzerland). Formulations and controls, 2.5 mL each, were delivered into the subcutaneous fat layer at the dorsal area, 1 to 2 cm deep, using a 3 mm Mercedes cannula. Animals were sacrificed at 1 and 3 months postinjection, and specimens were collected and fixed in 10% formalin at room temperature for at least 24 h before paraffin embedding.

Histological Analysis and Immunostaining

Thin sections were cut using a microtome and mounted on glass slides for general staining (hematoxylin and eosin, H&E) and immunostaining (outsourced to Patho-Lab, Israel). The stained sections were analyzed using an optical microscope (XYZ Microscope Co., San Francisco, CA), and digital images were captured for further analysis. Antibodies to 4 types of markers were applied: Perilipin 1, a marker for adipocytes (PRLN1, LS-C140916, LBio, Bothell, WA); CD31 (M0823, DakoCytomation, Carpinteria, CA), a marker for endothelial cells; CD86, a general marker for macrophages (AA 140-175, ENCO, Novi, MI); and CD206, M2 macrophage marker (orb180464, Biorbyt, Cambridge, United Kingdom). The slides were incubated with the individual antibodies, and a signal was later processed using anti-rabbit (PRLN1, CD86, and CD206) or anti-mouse (CD31) conjugates. Computer-assisted image analysis described below was performed to quantify the relative levels of each marker.

Image Analysis

Digital histological images were analyzed using QuPath (Philadelphia, PA) software (version 0.5.1, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29203879/). Injection zones were manually delineated based on the histopathological signs of local inflammation, fibrosis, and accumulation of lipid droplets. Control zones were selected from morphologically normal adipose tissue located several millimeters away from the injection sites, devoid of visible pathological alterations.

Live adipocytes were identified based on the presence of clear membranous PRLN1 immunostaining and preserved cell membrane morphology. Regions of interests, each measuring 500 × 500 µm, were selected within the injection zone, in adjacent tissue, and in distant control areas. Adipocyte territories were segmented by thresholding the generic 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) channel to delineate cell borders. Standard morphological features, including area, circularity, and solidity, were calculated for all segmented cells using QuPath's built-in shape analysis tools.26 To exclude damaged or dead adipocytes from the final dataset, cells with circularity below 0.35 and solidity below 0.7 were removed from further analysis. For each sample (n = 3), at least 100 cells were measured, and differences in cell areas were estimated by the Wilcoxon test.

Segmentation of CD68- and CD206-positive macrophages was performed by first detecting nuclei using StarDist (Hofors, Sweden), a deep learning–based instance segmentation method leveraging star-convex polygon representations for accurate cell boundary delineation.27 Following nuclei identification, cell objects were generated by expanding nuclei regions by a 3 μm radius. CD68+ and CD206+ cells were identified by DAB intensity thresholding applied to each cell region. CD31+ capillaries were identified similarly with an area threshold >50 μm2.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons were performed using appropriate tests, including the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for cell area differences and the Mann−Whitney test for group comparisons. A minimum of n = 3 per group was analyzed, with ≥100 cells measured per condition. A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 3.6.3) with RStudio (Boston, MA; version 1.2.5033).

RESULTS

Pro-Asp-(Phe-Asp)5-Pro (PFD)-Hydrogel Formulation and Physical Properties

Uniform and visibly clear PFD hydrogels prepared at different peptide concentrations, 1% to 5% w/v, and injected through a 30 G needle maintained rigidity (Figure 1A). TEM imaging demonstrated PFD hydrogels are composed, as previously reported, of entangled fibrils that are nanometer scale in width and micron in length (Figure 1B).18

(B) Transmission electron microscope image of the PFD-hydrogel fibrils (scale bar = 50 µm). (C) The storage modulus of PFD hydrogels at different % w/v concentrations, showing exponential correlation between peptide concentration and elastic modulus. Filled circles and open circles correspond to before and after sterilization, respectively. Hydrogels with concentrations ≤2.5% w/v show <17% reduction in their G′ value poststerilization (loss moduli and viscosity values are provided in Supplemental Figure S1). (D) Extrusion force of HA (Restylane Lyft) injected through a 25 G needle (triangles) and PFD 2% w/v hydrogel (circles) injected through a 30 G needle. See Supplemental Table S1 for rheology and extrusion force measured values.

Rheological characterizations demonstrated an exponential relationship between peptide concentration and elastic modulus (Figure 1C and Supplemental Figure 2). Specifically, the 2% w/v hydrogel exhibited a G′ = 278.40 ± 8.77 Pa (at 1 Hz), comparable to commercial crosslinked hyaluronic acids (Supplemental Table 1) while maintaining significantly lower viscosity (Supplemental Table 1). Notably, PFD hydrogels showed superior injectability with ∼4.3-fold lower extrusion force (∼3 N) compared with crosslinked hyaluronic acid (∼13 N) when tested through appropriate needle gauges (Figure 1D). Sterilization by autoclave resulted in minimal changes to mechanical properties (Figures 1C and Supplemental Figure 2).

Enhanced Structural Stability of PFD Hydrogel−Lipoaspirate Mixtures

The structural stability of PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures compared with lipoaspirate alone was readily apparent upon placing a fixed volume of the compositions on a glass substrate (Figure 2A). The compositions containing PFD appeared more stable than lipoaspirate alone, and higher peptide content correlated with increased apparent rigidity of the compositions. Next, PFD hydrogels, 2% w/v, lipoaspirate mixtures prepared at several v/v ratios were placed in a 24-well plate and inspected visually over time. Lipoaspirate spread into clusters over the well immediately after being covered by cell culture medium, whereas the PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures maintained a cohesive structure over a period of 10 days (Figure 2B).

(A) Images of Pro-Asp-(Phe-Asp)5-Pro (PFD) hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures placed on cover glasses demonstrating their apparent rigidity correlating with peptide content; from left to right: PFD hydrogel 4% w/v mixed with lipoaspirate at 33:67 and at 16:84 v/v, respectively; PFD 2% w/v mixed with lipoaspirate at these v/v ratios, a lipoaspirate that underwent syringe-to-syringe mixing, and a lipoaspirate sample with no mixing. (B) Images of culture wells containing PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures on Day 0 and following 10 days in culture. Lipoaspirate alone (left images) spread on the whole of the well bottom, and PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures (right images) maintained cohesivity over the period of 10 days.

PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures and lipoaspirate only were characterized by rheological measurements (Figure 3A, B). The G′ and viscosity values at 1 Hz show a logarithmic increase in these values for higher peptide content compositions, in accordance with the apparent rigidity, from G′ = 62 Pa for lipoaspirate only to G′ = 193 Pa for PFD hydrogel (2% w/v):lipoaspirate 33:67% v/v and up to G′ = 1004 Pa for the same v/v composition prepared with 4% w/v PFD hydrogel (Figure 2B). Extrusion force measurements of lipoaspirate delivered through 3 mm cannula (Figure 3C, dotted line) showed irregular spikes in the applied force, whereas mixed lipoaspirate (dashed line) and PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixture (solid line), both generated by syringe-to-syringe mixing, showed much smoother force patterns. Notably, the PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixture was also superior to the mixed lipoaspirate alone by avoiding the late spikes.

Values represent an average of 2 measurements. Samples are denoted as follows: (i) lipoaspirate only, (ii) PFD 2% w/v lipoaspirate 16:84 v/v, (iii) PFD 2% w/v lipoaspirate 33:67 v/v, (iv) PFD 4% w/v lipoaspirate 16:84 v/v, and (v) PFD 4% w/v lipoaspirate 33:67 v/v. Improved Cell Viability In Vitro

PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate compositions were next analyzed for cell viability (Figure 4). Following 3 days of culture, the detected viability was higher in all the PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate compositions compared with lipoaspirate alone. Of note, viability correlated with the quantity of PFD in the composition, showing higher viability for 33:67% v/v (PFD hydrogel:lipoaspirate) compositions compared with those of the 16:84% v/v. There was no significant difference between the compositions prepared with the PFD 4% w/v compared with the 2% w/v PFD hydrogels (data not shown). Based on these cell viability assays, PFD hydrogel 2% w/v was selected for the in vivo studies described below.

Viability assay based on Alamar blue staining of Pro-Asp-(Phe-Asp)5-Pro hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures following 3 days of culture. *P-value <.05 vs lipoaspirate alone (n = 3, T-test).

Hydrogel Degradation In Vitro

The degradation of the PFD hydrogel following implantation is expected to involve dissolution of the peptide from the hydrogel phase into the ECM, followed by enzymatic breakdown. This mechanism was supported by an experiment in which free PFD (0.2, 0.5, and 1% w/v) and PFD hydrogels (2% and 3% w/v) were incubated in lipoaspirate, and their degradation products, exposing free amine groups (NH2), were monitored over time using the ninhydrin assay. Free PFD peptides dissolved in lipoaspirate degraded rapidly, reaching completion within 2 days, and appeared to follow classical Michaelis–Menten enzyme kinetics (Figure 5A), with higher peptide concentrations degrading more quickly. In contrast, PFD hydrogels exhibited markedly slower, concentration-dependent degradation rates when mixed with lipoaspirate (Figure 5B). The 2% w/v hydrogel degraded completely by Day 11, whereas the 3% w/v hydrogel retained 70% of the peptide on Day 13.

Kinetics of Pro-Asp-(Phe-Asp)5-Pro (PFD) degradation in lipoaspirate at the indicated concentrations, represented as concentration or % of maximum free amines (NH2) where maximum degradation was calculated based on 5 cleavage sites per peptide molecule. (A) Dissolved PFD. (B) PFD hydrogels that were mixed with lipoaspirate at a 1:3 v/v ratio.

Degradation mechanistic studies identified neprilysin as the primary enzyme responsible for PFD breakdown in adipose tissue (Supplemental Tables 2-5, Supplemental Figures 2, 3, and Appendix).28 The specific neprilysin inhibitor sacubitrilat (LBQ657) showed dose-dependent inhibition of PFD degradation in lipoaspirate, whereas the broad-spectrum protease inhibitor phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride showed no effect (Supplemental Table 3). This finding demonstrates that PFD degradation follows physiological enzymatic pathways rather than nonspecific proteolytic breakdown.

Inflammatory Responses In Vivo

Each of the following formulations, PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate compositions, PFD-hydrogel only, and autologous lipoaspirate, was injected subcutaneously into a porcine model to evaluate local tissue reactions and implant dynamics at 1 and 3 months (Supplemental Figure 4). Histology of the injection sites included H&E staining, Perilipin for adipocyte size analysis, CD68 as a general macrophage marker, and CD206 as a marker for anti-inflammatory (M2-like) macrophages involved in tissue repair and homeostasis.29

Tissue cuts stained with H&E revealed marked differences in apparent inflammation at 1 month following the treatments (Figure 6). Transplanted lipoaspirate (Figure 6A, B) appeared as highly inflamed regions, consisting of immune cells, composed of macrophages and lymphocytic clusters. The lipoaspirate also contained large lipid droplets that are remnants of adipocytes, which did not survive the harvesting and implantation procedure. In contrast, tissue slides of the PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate compositions (Figure 6C) showed smaller inflammation zones reminiscent of the implantation procedure. These regions were identified as being composed predominantly of macrophages (Figure 7), with no lymphocytes and no capsular fibrous borders. A gross analysis of the apparently inflamed zones (Figure 6D and Supplemental Figure 5) reveals a significant 7-fold reduction in nonadipose inflammatory area for PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate compositions (9.27 ± 10.7 mm2) vs lipoaspirate controls (65.4 ± 30.6 mm2), n = 4, P = .029. Furthermore, these H&E slides also highlight a seamless integration of the implanted composition with the surrounding adipose tissue. Indeed, borderless integration with adipose tissue was also evident in samples obtained from PFD-hydrogel only, injected into fat tissue (Supplemental Figure 4B), which show scattered islands of macrophages that probably engulfed the hydrogel. By 3 months, PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate compositions showed only scattered remnants, indicating near-complete resorption and replacement by native tissue.

Sections of PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate composition (Figure 7), lipoaspirate, and PFD-hydrogel-only treatments (Supplemental Figure 6) were stained for PLIN1 to identify adipocyte viability. PLIN1 staining enabled allocating the treatment zone, which appears as an infiltrate in the adipose tissue. The infiltrate is composed of connective tissue strands with intermingled viable adipocytes (Figure 7B). The infiltrate shows an island of macrophages (Figure 7), which are likewise PLIN1 positive; it has been previously reported that macrophages, which are associated with an anti-inflammatory (M2-like) phenotype, express PLIN1.30 The infiltrate, which occupies a small region of the full section, is most probably surrounded by viable adipocytes (Figure 7C) that were transplanted with the treatment. The infiltrates demonstrated seamless integration with the surrounding native adipose tissue, precluding clear demarcation between the transplanted and host tissues. Analysis of adipocyte size distribution revealed distinct patterns between treatment groups. In the lipoaspirate-injected samples, the distribution exhibited a bimodal profile, indicating the presence of 2 distinct adipocyte populations with 2 maxima in size distribution at ∼3000 and <∼100 mm2. The PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate composition also showed 2 maxima in size distribution, however, with a lower extent of the smaller adipocytes compared with the lipoaspirate only (Supplemental Figure 6 and Supplemental Table 6). The median adipocyte size in the lipoaspirate (332 µm2) and PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate composition (682 µm2) treatments were significantly smaller than the median of the respective control zones (1245 µm2, P < .001, and 1336 µm2, P < .001, respectively). In contrast, in PFD-hydrogel-only treatment, no significant difference was found in adipocyte size compared with the control region (Supplemental Figure 6B and Supplemental Table 6).

Favorable Macrophage Response and Neovascularization

Further analysis of immune cell infiltration in the PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate composition treatment focused on macrophages, identified by the pan-macrophage marker CD68 and by CD206 to assess their polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype (Figure 8). At 1-month implant sections showed abundant and closely packed CD68 macrophages that within 3 months dropped in number (from 185 to 60 cells, respectively, P < .001 measured in 250 × 250 mm2, see Supplemental Figure 7) and appear to be in a loosened structure, enabling their replacement by the surrounding adipocyte tissue. The CD206 macrophages appear sparse at 1 month, whereas at 3 months, their presence is more dominant (from 12 to 43, respectively, P < .001 measured in 250 × 250 mm2; see Figure 8 and Supplemental Figure 7). Furthermore, at 1 month, the macrophage infiltrate appeared rich in a new vascular network, evident by CD31 staining (Supplemental Figure 8) that assists in connecting the implant zone with the general vascular system. At 3 months, vasculature regresses, as could be expected, along with the healing.

DISCUSSION

The improved cell viability observed in compositions with PFD hydrogels subjected to mixing and injection, both in vitro and in a swine model, could be associated with better mechanical support for adipocytes.31 This benefit can be attributed to the hydrogel's unique structural properties, which enable uniform and cohesive mixing with lipoaspirate that facilitates smooth and low-resistance injection. The improved cell viability in PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate mixtures in vitro can also be related to the hydrogel's fibrillar structure, which mimics the ECM. In addition, the PFD hydrogel contains hydrophobic components that may enhance oxygen miscibility and enable its controlled release in response to metabolic demands, thereby supporting adipocyte viability.

Unlike lipoaspirate-only injections, which induced a severe inflammatory response and a pronounced shift in adipocyte size at the treatment site, the PFD hydrogel−lipoaspirate composition showed uniform and seamless integration into the surrounding tissue, with only a mild shift toward smaller adipocytes while preserving the overall size distribution pattern seen in normal tissue.

In vitro analyses revealed that PFD is degraded by physiological enzymatic pathways rather than by nonspecific proteolytic cleavage, supporting its potential for controlled and predictable resorption in clinical applications. Based on the in vitro dissolution and the degradation assays, it is reasonable to assume that the PFD−lipoaspirate composition in vivo will also be cleared through dissolution and enzymatic degradation. Indeed, histological sections showed small sections of macrophages, which may have phagocytosed residuals of the treatment. By 3 months, treatment remnants were largely composed of disintegrating macrophages, exhibiting a pro-healing, M2-like phenotype that was progressively replaced by adipose tissue.

Collectively, these findings suggest that PFD hydrogel provides short-term physical support, including increased resistance to shear forces encountered during implantation and ECM-like integration with host adipose tissue during the critical early posttransplantation period, followed by a mild macrophage-mediated response that supports long-term healing and graft integration.

This study has several limitations. Although the porcine model is suitable for initial evaluation, it may not fully recapitulate human fat grafting physiology. The 3-month follow-up should be extended to capture long-term clinical outcomes with attention to adipocyte retention and tissue remodeling. Further mechanistic studies should elucidate the molecular pathways underlying PFD benefits, especially its role in oxygen delivery and inflammation response. Testing across different anatomical sites with long-term follow-up could provide broader validation and allow for direct volume retention measurements.

Although these preclinical findings are encouraging, clinical evaluation and validation through controlled trials will be essential to establish the position of PFD hydrogel in fat grafting practice. The technology shows promise as an accessible, effective enhancement method, but careful clinical evaluation and optimization remain necessary before routine clinical implementation can be recommended.

CONCLUSIONS

This preclinical study suggests that PFD peptide hydrogel may represent a novel biomaterial approach to enhance fat grafting when compared with conventional autologous fat grafting. The PFD hydrogel reduced inflammatory response compared with fat grafting alone while improving the mechanical stability and cell viability of lipoaspirate. In addition, PFD hydrogel demonstrates in vitro dissolution over days accompanied by a well-defined enzymatic degradation mechanism.
