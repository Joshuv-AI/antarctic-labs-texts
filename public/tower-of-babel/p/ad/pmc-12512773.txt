Exploring the genetic causality of childhood maltreatment on multiple risk-related outcomes using Mendelian randomization: evidence from European-Ancestry GWAS
Authors: Zheng Zhang, Hao Ren, Haitang Qiu, Qinghua Luo, Qiyin Li, Yimo Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Childhood maltreatment (CM) has been implicated as a risk factor for various maladaptive behaviours in adulthood, but evidence from genetic epidemiology remains limited. This study aimed to assess the potential causal effects of genetically proxied liability to CM on multiple risk-related behaviours using Mendelian randomization (MR) in European-ancestry populations.Methods: MR analysis was conducted using publicly available GWAS summary statistics to assess the effects of CM on seven outcomes: aggressive behaviour (AB), automobile speeding propensity (ASP), alcohol drinking (AD), smoking behaviour (SB), general risk tolerance (GRT), age at first sexual intercourse (AFI), and lifetime number of sexual partners (LSP). The primary analysis used the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, with additional checks via MR-Egger and weighted median methods. CM was instrumented with SNPs identified in GWAS of self-reported maltreatment; hence the exposure reflects a genetically proxied liability to report CM and may capture gene–environment correlation rather than a direct biological determinant.Results: Genetically proxied CM liability was significantly associated with increased risk of AB (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.09–1.30, PFDR < 0.001), SB (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.05–1.28, PFDR = 0.006), GRT (β = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.12–0.29, PFDR < 0.001), and earlier AFI (β = −0.36, 95% CI: −0.49 to −0.23, PFDR < 0.001). A nominal association was found with ASP (β = 0.07, 95% CI: −0.03–0.17, PFDR < 0.001). CM showed a weak protective effect against AD (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99, PFDR = 0.013). No significant effect was observed on LSP (β = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.13–0.28, PFDR = 0.192).Conclusion: This study provides genetically informed evidence suggesting that a higher liability to CM exposure may increase the likelihood of aggression, smoking, risk-taking, and early sexual initiation. These findings underscore the long-term behavioural consequences of early adversity and highlight the importance of preventive interventions.KEYWORDS: Childhood maltreatment, Mendelian randomization, risk behaviour, aggression, smoking, risk tolerance

Introduction

Childhood maltreatment (CM), which includes physical, emotional, and sexual abuse, as well as neglect, is a major public health concern. CM has been shown to negatively affect individuals’ ability to engage in healthy social interactions, with its consequences often persisting into adulthood (McCrory et al., 2022). It is associated with a wide range of mental and physical health problems, including anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), asthma, obesity (Danese & Tan, 2014; Han et al., 2022; Xiao et al., 2021), and even accelerated biological aging (Yang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024).

The global prevalence of CM remains alarming. According to recent estimates from UNICEF, 60% of children under the age of five are frequently subjected to corporal punishment or emotional aggression by caregivers, 40% of children aged 2–4 are often neglected, and 20% of women and 14% of men report having experienced sexual abuse during childhood (Child Maltreatment, n.d.; Organization, 2020). In the UK, an analysis of 11.83 million patients from the THIN primary care database revealed that the documented prevalence of CM increased from 176.3 per 100,000 children in 1997 to 416.1 per 100,000 in 2017, with the actual rate likely to be higher due to underreporting (Chandan et al., 2020). In the United States, the National Survey of Children’s Exposure to Violence (NatSCEV) found that among 4000 children and adolescents aged 0–17, 37.3% had experienced physical assault, 15.2% had suffered maltreatment by caregivers, and 4.6% of adolescent girls reported sexual abuse within a single year (Finkelhor et al., 2015). In China, data from seven provinces indicate that among primary and secondary school students, 20% had experienced physical abuse, 30% emotional abuse, 12% sexual abuse, and 47% neglect (Wang et al., 2020). Similarly, in Australia, the Australian Child Maltreatment Study (ACMS) reported that among 8,500 randomly sampled individuals aged 16 and above, 32.0% experienced physical abuse, 28.5% sexual abuse, 30.9% emotional abuse, and 8.9% neglect during childhood (Mathews et al., 2023), contributing to an estimated 184,636 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost nationally (Grummitt et al., 2024).

Risk behaviours are defined as actions that may result in harm to oneself or others, either physically or psychologically, and often emerge during adolescence, with potential long-term effects into adulthood (Bozzini et al., 2021; Kipping et al., 2012) These behaviours include aggression behaviour (AB), alcohol drinking (AD), smoking behaviour (SB) (Bozzini et al., 2021), automobile speeding propensity (ASP) (23andMe Research Team et al., 2019), general risk tolerance (GRT) (23and Me Research Team et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020), age first had sexual intercourse (AFI), and lifetime number of sexual partners (LSP) (Thibodeau et al., 2017; Valois et al., 1999; Waldron et al., 2008). Such behaviours are linked to a wide range of adverse health and social outcomes, including anxiety, depression, sexually transmitted infections, obesity, suicidal behaviour, premature mortality, substance misuse, gambling, unemployment, contact with the criminal justice system, low educational attainment, and socioeconomic disadvantage (Bensley et al., 2000; Campbell et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2024; Kipping et al., 2015; Norman et al., 2012; Tinner et al., 2021).

Previous observational studies have identified associations between CM and various maladaptive outcomes such as impulsive violence (Carlson et al., 2015; Chang et al., 2021; McIntyre et al., 2024), alcohol misuse (Diaz et al., 2020), and sexual risk behaviour (Diaz et al., 2020; Thibodeau et al., 2017). However, these associations are often confounded by unmeasured variables or mediated by complex pathways, making it difficult to establish clear causal relationships. Furthermore, the generalizability of findings from single-region cohort studies may be limited, reducing their applicability to broader populations (Campbell et al., 2020).

While numerous observational studies have explored the associations between CM and various risk-related behaviours, these findings are often susceptible to confounding, measurement error, and reverse causality, limiting the strength of causal inference. Mendelian Randomization (MR), which uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs), provides a robust framework to overcome these limitations by mimicking the structure of randomized controlled trials (Davies et al., 2018). In the present study, we employed a two-sample MR design to examine the potential causal effects of genetic liability to report CM on multiple behavioural outcomes, including AB, AD, SB, ASP, GRT, AFI, and LSP. Notably, the genetic instruments used for CM were derived from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of self-reported experiences of maltreatment, and likely reflect complex gene–environment correlations rather than deterministic genetic causes. By clarifying these relationships under MR assumptions, this study aims to deepen understanding of the long-term behavioural consequences of early adversity and provide genetically informed evidence to guide preventive strategies and public health policies.

Methods

Study design

In this study, we integrated large-scale GWAS summary-level data for MR analysis. Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) served as IVs to assess the causal effects of CM (data from Warrier et al., 2021, incorporating cohorts such as UKB, PGC, ALSPAC, ABCD Study, and Generation R Study) on seven risk-related behavioural outcomes: AB (EAGLE consortium), ASP, SB, and GRT (SSGAC meta-analysis), AD (UKB dietary habits GWAS), and AFI and LSP (IEU OpenGWAS database, MRC-IEU UKB GWAS pipeline). SNP selection rigorously followed the three fundamental MR assumptions: (1) strong association of SNPs with CM; (2) independence from potential confounding variables; and (3) SNPs affect behavioural outcomes solely via CM, without horizontal pleiotropy (Davies et al., 2018). Genetic instrument selection

The genetic instruments for CM were derived from the largest GWAS meta-analysis to date, conducted by Warrier et al. (2021). This meta-analysis incorporated data from five cohorts – UK Biobank (n = 143,473), the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC; n = 26,290), the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC; n = 8,346), the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (n = 5,400), and the Generation R Study (n = 1,905) – comprising a total sample of 185,414 individuals of primarily European ancestry. The CM phenotype was defined as a composite measure encompassing emotional, sexual, and physical abuse, as well as emotional and physical neglect. Assessments were based on either retrospective self-reports or prospective caregiver reports, using standardized instruments across cohorts (see Table 1 for details).

To address the heterogeneity in CM measurement across studies, Warrier et al. conducted extensive genetic correlation analyses. These analyses demonstrated high genetic concordance among different operationalizations (rg = 0.47–1.00), CM subtypes (rg = 0.62–1.00), and retrospective versus prospective reports (rg = 0.72, p = .046). Furthermore, polygenic scores (PGS) derived from retrospective CM GWAS significantly predicted prospectively measured CM, reinforcing the validity of the unified phenotype used in the meta-analysis.

Genome-wide significant SNPs associated with CM (p < 5 × 10−⁸) were selected as IVs following linkage disequilibrium clumping (p² < .001, 10,000 kb window). These SNPs represent genetic liability to reported CM experiences and serve as valid proxies under the assumptions of MR. To ensure instrument strength, F-statistics were calculated for each SNP, with all selected variants exceeding the conventional threshold of F > 10 (Flatby et al., 2023). Detailed information on the selected SNPs, including rsIDs, effect alleles, beta coefficients, standard errors, minor allele frequencies (MAF), and F-statistics, is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

To satisfy the second core assumption of MR – that genetic instruments are independent of potential confounders – we screened and excluded SNPs potentially associated with outcome-related confounding variables using the LDassoc tool available at https://ldlink.nih.gov. The strength of each selected SNP was evaluated by calculating the F-statistic using the formula: F = [(N-k-1)/k] × [R2/(1-R2)], where N denotes the effective GWAS sample size, k is the number of IVs, and R² represents the proportion of variance in CM explained by the SNP. The R² value was estimated using the formula: R2 = 2 × (1–MAF)×MAF × (β/SD)2 (Wu et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2021), where MAF refers to the minor allele frequency, β is the standardized effect size of the SNP on CM, and SD is the standard deviation of the phenotype. SNPs with an F-statistic < 10 were excluded to ensure sufficient instrument strength and mitigate weak instrument bias.

Data source for risk-related behaviours

Summary-level GWAS data for seven risk-related behavioural phenotypes were used as outcomes in this study: AB, ASP, AD, SB, GRT, AFI, and LSP. These datasets were derived from large-scale GWAS conducted predominantly in populations of European ancestry, with specific sources detailed as follows:

The GWAS summary statistics for AB were obtained from the largest meta-analysis to date on childhood aggression, involving 87,485 children contributing 328,935 observations across 29 cohorts from Europe, North America, and Oceania (Ip et al., 2021). Aggressive behaviour was analysed as a quantitative trait, assessed by multiple raters (parents, teachers, self-reports) and measurement instruments. GWAS were conducted within cohorts and subsequently meta-analysed using a multivariate approach to account for phenotypic covariance and overlapping samples.

Summary statistics for ASP, SB, and GRT were obtained from a large-scale meta-analysis encompassing over one million participants (23and Me Research Team et al., 2019). Specifically, ASP was measured via self-reported speeding frequency in the UK Biobank cohort; SB was defined as an ‘ever versus never’ smoker phenotype derived from both the UK Biobank and Tobacco and Genetics (TAG) Consortium; and GRT was measured by general risk-taking preferences assessed across participants from the UK Biobank and 23andMe cohorts. The GWAS for GRT specifically included 939,908 participants, identifying 124 genome-wide significant SNPs across 99 genomic regions.

The GWAS summary data for AD were acquired from a study of dietary habits conducted in up to 449,210 individuals of European ancestry from the UK Biobank (Cole et al., 2020). The analysed phenotype was ‘total drinks of alcohol per month,’ treated as a continuous trait exhibiting significant SNP-based heritability (h² = 11.3%) with 104 independent genome-wide significant loci identified.

GWAS summary statistics for AFI and LSP were obtained from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU) OpenGWAS database(IEU OpenGWAS Project, n.d.). These datasets included up to 9,851,867 participants, generated using the UK Biobank GWAS pipeline developed by the Medical Research Council Integrative Epidemiology Unit (MRC-IEU) (Hemani, Zheng, Elsworth, Wade, Haberland, Baird, Laurin, Burgess, Bowden, Langdon, Tan, Yarmolinsky, Shihab, Timpson, Evans, Relton, Martin, Davey Smith, et al., 2018). Analyses were conducted using BOLT-LMM, a linear mixed modelling approach that effectively accounts for population stratification and sample relatedness, with genotyping array and age included as covariates (Loh et al., 2015).

To reduce potential bias from population stratification, only GWAS summary data from individuals of European ancestry were included. All contributing studies obtained ethical approval and informed consent from participants. Detailed information on the GWAS datasets, including the measurement of CM and risk-related behavioural traits, is presented in Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Our primary analysis employed the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method with a random-effects model to assess the causal associations between CM and seven risk-related behavioural outcomes (AB, ASP, AD, SB, GRT, AFI, and LSP) (Lin et al., 2021). To evaluate the robustness of these results and address potential pleiotropy, we implemented four sensitivity analyses: weighted median (Bowden et al., 2016), MR-Egger regression (Bowden et al., 2015), MR-PRESSO (Verbanck et al., 2018), and contamination mixture methods (Burgess et al., 2020). The weighted median approach provided reliable causal estimates as long as at least 50% of the SNPs were valid instruments (Bowden et al., 2016). MR-Egger regression was utilized to detect and adjust for directional pleiotropy, accounting for SNP effects on outcomes independent of the exposure (CM) (Bowden et al., 2015). MR-PRESSO was applied to identify and exclude outlier SNPs, recalculating estimates to assess their influence (Verbanck et al., 2018). The contamination mixture method offered robust causal estimates even in the presence of potentially invalid SNPs (Burgess et al., 2020).

Instrument strength for each SNP was evaluated using F-statistics, and heterogeneity across genetic instruments was assessed with Cochrane’s Q test. False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was conducted to control for multiple testing and reduce Type I error risk. Results for continuous outcomes were reported as β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% CIs were presented for binary outcomes. All statistical tests were two-tailed. Analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR (Hemani, Zheng, Elsworth, Wade, Haberland, Baird, Laurin, Burgess, Bowden, Langdon, Tan, Yarmolinsky, Shihab, Timpson, Evans, Relton, Martin, Smith, et al., 2018a, 2018b), MR-PRESSO (Verbanck et al., 2018), and Mendelian Randomization (Yavorska & Burgess, 2017) packages in R software (version 4.0.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Using the IVW method as the primary analytical approach, genetically predicted CM was significantly associated with multiple risk-related behavioural traits (Figures 2 and 3). Specifically, CM was positively associated with AB (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.09–1.30, pFDR < .001), indicating a putative causal association between higher genetic liability to CM and an increased likelihood of aggressive behaviour. A similar risk-enhancing association was observed for SB (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.05–1.28, pFDR = .006), suggesting a genetically informed link between CM and higher odds of smoking initiation. Furthermore, CM showed a robust positive association with GRT (β = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.12–0.29, pFDR < .001), providing genetically informed evidence for a relationship between early adversity and greater tolerance for risk.

In the domain of sexual behaviour, CM was significantly associated with earlier AFI (β = –0.36, 95% CI: –0.49 to –0.23, pFDR < 0.001), indicating a genetically informed tendency toward earlier sexual initiation among those with higher genetic liability to CM. A nominally positive association was observed for ASP (β = 0.07, 95% CI: –0.03 to 0.17, pFDR < .001); however, the CI included zero, and this finding should be interpreted with caution.

CM exhibited a weak but statistically significant negative association with AD (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99, pFDR = .013), which may suggest a possible protective or compensatory behavioural pattern. No statistically robust association was found for LSP (β = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.13–0.28, pFDR = .192) after FDR correction.

These IVW-derived results, fully consistent with the values presented in Figures 2 and 3, support genetically informed inferences regarding the potential role of CM in shaping various externalizing and risk-related behaviours. The findings underscore the behavioural consequences of early adversity and highlight the need for preventive strategies targeting CM.

To further examine the robustness of these IVW-based findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses using MR-Egger, Weighted Median, Simple Mode, and Weighted Mode approaches (Figure 4). These methods yielded results broadly consistent with the primary IVW analyses. The scatter plots demonstrated similar directional trends across these approaches for AB, SB, GRT, AFI, and ASP, indicating that the observed genetically informed associations were unlikely to be substantially influenced by horizontal pleiotropy. The associations observed for AD and LSP were relatively weaker and showed more variability across methods. Nevertheless, the overall consistency in direction across multiple MR approaches strengthens confidence in the robustness of these genetically informed relationships.

The MR-Egger intercepts for most outcomes were close to zero and non-significant (all p > .05), indicating no strong evidence of directional pleiotropy. However, the intercept for AB approached nominal significance (p = .058), suggesting the possibility of mild directional pleiotropy. Although not statistically significant, this finding warrants cautious interpretation. Other indicators for AB, including Cochran’s Q (Q = 21.63, p = .086) and the MR-PRESSO global test (p = .117), did not indicate substantial heterogeneity or distortion. The IVW estimate remained directionally consistent.

Cochran’s Q test indicated significant heterogeneity for ASP (Q = 19.22, p = .023) and AFI (Q = 27.89, p < .001). For ASP, the heterogeneity may reflect variation in underlying behavioural dimensions (e.g. impulsivity, rule-breaking), each potentially influenced by distinct genetic pathways. The heterogeneity in AFI may be driven by gene–environment interactions, as this outcome is shaped by sociocultural factors in addition to biological traits.

For SB (Q = 16.49, p = .036), GRT (Q = 15.00, p = .091), and LSP (Q = 9.15, p = .242), heterogeneity was either marginal or non-significant. In all cases, IVW estimates remained consistent. The MR-PRESSO global test did not detect horizontal pleiotropy for any outcome (all p > .05), and no outlier SNPs were identified; hence, distortion tests were not applicable (NA).

All IVs were strong, with F values ranging from 146.1 to 154.2, indicating low risk of weak instrument bias. These results collectively support the validity of the causal estimates while emphasizing the need for caution when interpreting outcomes with borderline pleiotropy or moderate heterogeneity. Discussion

In this two-sample MR study, we identified potential causal effects of CM on several risk-related behaviours. Specifically, CM was positively associated with AB, SB, GRT, and earlier AFI. A modest inverse association was observed for AD, while no significant causal effects were found for ASP and LSP. These findings suggest that CM may increase individuals’ vulnerability to certain externalizing behaviours, particularly those related to impulsivity, emotional regulation, and risk perception.

Our findings support previous observational studies linking CM with AB (Lewis, 1992; Li et al., 2022; South et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024), and further provide genetic evidence for a potential causal relationship. Previous research suggests that the pathway from CM to AB may be mediated by psychological traits such as authenticity, alexithymia, callousness, and the quality of interpersonal relationships (Li et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024). These factors are likely shaped by environmental influences. At the genetic level, polymorphisms in genes such as OXTR (Zhang et al., 2018), CREB1 (Y. Zhang et al., 2022), and SIRT1 (Wang et al., 2022) have been found to interact with CM in influencing AB. Although OXTR polymorphisms have been shown to independently affect anger and aggression(Butovskaya et al., 2020), the current consensus suggests that both genetic predispositions and environmental exposures jointly shape AB. Neurobiological studies in animal models further demonstrate that early environmental stress can alter hormone and neurotransmitter levels, such as testosterone, norepinephrine, dopamine, and serotonin, thereby modulating aggressive responses through hormonal and neurochemical pathways(Lewis, 1992).

CM also appears to increase the likelihood of SB. Prior studies have shown that CM elevates the risk of smoking during late adolescence – especially among girls – with a clear dose–response relationship between the severity of maltreatment and smoking frequency(Jun et al., 2008). This increased adolescent smoking may further raise the risk of continued smoking into adulthood (Kristman-Valente et al., 2013; Taha et al., 2014). Moreover, anxiety, depression, and trauma-related stress induced by CM may serve as mediators that both promote SB and reduce the likelihood of successful cessation attempts (Smith et al., 2015). Nicotine from tobacco acts via nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) to modulate neurotransmitters such as dopamine and adrenaline (Subramaniyan & Dani, 2015), producing short-term emotional relief (Angelescu & Sandu, 2023). However, chronic nicotine dependence can worsen negative emotional states and even contribute to psychiatric symptoms, thereby reinforcing SB in a feedback loop (Airagnes et al., 2025).

In contrast to smoking, our study found that CM was associated with a reduction in AD, a finding that differs from some observational studies. For example, a longitudinal cohort study of 2,594 rural Chinese children and adolescents reported that CM was linked to higher short-term drinking frequency (Niu et al., 2024). Other research has suggested that urgency (Shin et al., 2015), maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation strategies (Khosravani et al., 2019), and behavioural dysregulation play key roles in the CM–AD pathway, reflecting a generally positive association between CM and alcohol use (Shin et al., 2019). Several factors may explain the discrepancy between our findings and prior work: (1) Although smoking and drinking are both addictive behaviours, their genetic architectures differ substantially, with most SNPs associated with smoking and drinking located in distinct functional genomic regions (Caporaso et al., 2009; Clarke et al., 2017). This may account for opposing MR estimates despite behavioural similarities. (2) In UKB, AD was defined as ‘total drinks of alcohol per month,’ a continuous measure of drinking level, whereas SB was defined as ‘ever versus never,’ reflecting initiation. This difference in phenotype definition may also contribute to the divergent results. (3) The effect size for CM on reduced AD in our MR analysis was small (OR: 0.93–0.96), and although the PFDR was significant, the possibility of a type I error cannot be excluded. Thus, this ‘protective effect’ should be interpreted with caution.

We also found a strong negative association between CM and AFI, consistent with findings from observational studies (Z.-Y. Wang et al., 2019; Wen et al., 2022). CM, particularly neglect, may promote anxious attachment styles, leading individuals to seek sexual intimacy at earlier ages as a form of emotional compensation (Thibodeau et al., 2017). On a molecular level, CM may epigenetically alter expression of the glucocorticoid receptor gene NR3C1 (McGowan et al., 2009), disrupting HPA axis functioning and accelerating pubertal development or stress reactivity, thereby contributing to earlier AFI (Negriff, Blankson, et al., 2015; Negriff, Saxbe, et al., 2015).

Although LSP also reflects a component of sexual behaviour, no significant causal effect of CM was found in this domain. This may be due to age-related moderation; in adulthood, social roles such as marriage and employment may constrain LSP more than earlier developmental factors (Humphrey & Dumontheil, 2016).

Finally, GRT refers to an individual’s willingness to tolerate uncertainty and potential risk when making decisions (Karlsson Linnér et al., 2019). Higher GRT is generally associated with a greater likelihood of engaging in risk-taking behaviours. CM may increase individuals’ tolerance for uncertainty and reward-related cues by impairing emotion regulation and altering reward expectancy processing (Mueller & Peterson, 2012). However, GRT is also influenced by the type of maltreatment (Wang et al., 2019), individual resilience (Tian et al., 2021), and the contextual environment (Xu et al., 2024). Consequently, high GRT may, in certain psychologically uncertain contexts, paradoxically manifest as risk avoidance rather than risk-seeking (Singh et al., 2022). This complexity may help explain why no significant associations were observed between CM and ASP or LSP in our study, as these behaviours may trigger avoidance responses in specific situations. Overall, this MR study provides the first causal evidence linking CM to elevated GRT in adulthood. Although the behavioural manifestations of high GRT may vary depending on the context, elevated GRT is generally associated with an increased risk of AB, SB, and early AFI.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, although MR is less prone to confounding and reverse causality compared to traditional observational studies, it is still subject to potential bias due to horizontal pleiotropy, measurement error in exposure, and weak instrument bias. While sensitivity analyses (e.g. MR-Egger, MR-PRESSO) suggested minimal pleiotropic effects, we cannot completely rule out residual bias. Second, the genetic instruments for CM were derived from GWASs using both retrospective and prospective measures, which may introduce heterogeneity in phenotype definition. Third, all GWAS datasets used in this study were based on individuals of European ancestry, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Fourth, our outcome measures were limited to self-reported behavioural traits, which may not fully capture the complexity or context of risk-related behaviours. Finally, although we explored multiple risk behaviour outcomes, we did not conduct subtype-specific analyses for CM (e.g. abuse vs. neglect), which may have yielded more nuanced insights.

Conclusion

This MR study provides genetic evidence supporting a potential causal role of CM in the development of several risk-related behaviours, including AB, SB, increased GRT, and earlier AFI. These findings highlight CM as a critical early-life factor contributing to long-term behavioural vulnerabilities. By demonstrating robust associations through a genetically informed approach, our study underscores the importance of early identification and intervention in maltreated children. Future research should explore subtype-specific effects of CM and expand analyses to more diverse populations to better inform targeted prevention strategies and public health policies.
