A detailed DFT inspection on the trapping of aspirin from drinking water using B12N12 nanocage
Authors: Hamad Khan, Qaisar Ali, Mian Hussain Shah, Khaleel Muhammad, Ajmal Shah, Abdullah F AlAsmari, Fawaz Alasmari, Momin Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAspirin is used as an analgesic and antipyretic, but its release into the water bodies may also cause various diseases, such as stomach ulcers, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and tinnitus. This study investigates the elimination of aspirin using B12N12 nanocages employing DFT simulations. Three complexes, namely, BN-Com-A, BN-Com-B, and BN-Com-C were optimized utilizing DFT-D3 correction, B3LYP theory and 6-31G(d, p) basis set. The adsorption energy values revealed strong chemisorption in BN-Com-A and BN-Com-B with Eads values greater the − 11 Kcal/mol, whereas BN-Com-C showed physisorption, having Eads values less than − 11 kcal/mol. UV-visible, IR and Raman spectra were calculated to confirm the geometry of aspirin. RDG/NCI studies were performed to verify non-covalent interactions, and QTAIM analysis was performed to determine the nature of interactions. FMO and DOS analyses showed that the HOMO–LUMO gap was reduced upon complexation. EDD and PDOS analyses explained the large orbital overlap between aspirin and the nanocage. NBO analysis showed charge transfer between the nanocage and aspirin, confirming the existence of interactions between the monomers. The PCM study shows stability of the complexes in aqueous medium depicting the effectiveness of the mechanism in aqueous medium. Recovery time calculations also demonstrated that the nanocage can be recovered and reused. Therefore, B12N12 nanocages can be considered effective in the elimination of pharmaceuticals from water and can contribute to future studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53566-5.Keywords: Aspirin, Nanocage (B12N12), Pharmaceuticals, Density Functional Theory (DFT)Subject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Aspirin is one of the most commonly used non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), used for the treatment of pain, fever, inflammation, and cardiovascular diseases1–3. Since it is widely used and cannot be fully metabolized in the human body, a significant portion of aspirin remains unmetabolized and as a result, continuously enters water bodies4–7. Hospital effluents, outflows from pharmaceutical industries, and domestic wastewater are major sources of its presence in water bodies8–10. The long retention of aspirin in the water bodies and its continuous exposure to humans through drinking water cause serious issues such as gastrointestinal disorders (ulceration and bleeding), renal impairment, hepatic toxicity, hypersensitivity reactions, and disruption of platelet function, leading to an increased risk of bleeding. Aspirin also causes undesirable effects on the aquatic organisms and may also lead to ecological imbalance11–15. The high water solubility of aspirin over a wide pH range and chemical stability inhibit its natural removal, thus complicating the elimination of aspirin from polluted water. Different methods have been explored for the removal of aspirin, including biological degradation16, photolytic degradation17, membrane separation18, ion exchange19, and advanced oxidation20 processes. Although these approaches offer certain advantages, they also have drawbacks and limitations such as high operational costs, incomplete removal efficiency, low selectivity, and poor scalability for large-scale applications21–23. In comparison to these conventional techniques, adsorption-based strategies are more efficient due to their simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and higher removal efficiency for trace contaminants. Over the last few years, nanomaterials have gained significant attention owing to their unique structural and physicochemical properties. Inorganic nanocages such as B12N12 nanocage have demonstrated excellent chemical stability, high surface area, high surface reactivity, and favorable electronic characteristics, making them strong candidates for environmental remediation, especially for emerging pollutants. The literature on nanocage-based materials has already shown that they are effective in the adsorption of pharmaceutical contaminants, which makes them ideal as next-generation adsorbents24–32. This study examines the adsorption of aspirin on B12N12 nanocage, which is a seemingly straightforward study using Density Functional Theory. Frontier molecular orbital and natural bond orbital evaluation of charge transfer, density of state, quantum theory of atoms in molecules, electron density difference analysis, partial density of states analysis, and determination of dipole moment were used to investigate the adsorption mechanism. Additionally, non-covalent interaction (NCI) and reduced density gradient (RDG) analyses were used to clarify the nature and intensity of interactions present. The polarizable continuum model is used to study solvent effects, whereas regeneration ability was evaluated to assess the efficiency, stability, and reusability of the B12N12 nanocages as a potential adsorbent to remove aspirin from aqueous medium. This study aims to investigate the adsorption behavior, interaction mechanisms, and reusability of aspirin on B12N12 nanocage using theoretical simulations to evaluate its potential as an efficient adsorbent for water purification.

Results and discussions

Figure 1 shows the optimal molecular structures of aspirin and B12N12. The BN bond lengths in the tetragonal and hexagonal rings reported in the previous literature are 1.49Å and 1.43Å, respectively33. The MEP (molecular electrostatic potential) map indicates that the Boron and Nitrogen atoms of the nanocage (B12N12) and the C = O and O-H groups of the aspirin are the active sites for interaction, as shown in Fig. 2. The red regions in the MEP map represent electrophilic centers, and blue regions represent nucleophilic centers. As a result, such regions were selected for interaction analysis. Three configurations of aspirin with the B12N12 nanocage were investigated, designated as BN-Com-A, BN-Com-B, and BN-Com-C. The structures of the optimized complexes are illustrated in Fig. 3. In the BN-Com-A configuration, the oxygen and hydrogen atom of the C = O and O-H groups of the carboxylic acid that is present in the aspirin is directed to the boron and the nitrogen atoms of the B12N12 nanocage. Bond analysis showed interaction distances of 2.32Å and 1.81Å respectively, with 118.2° and 163.4° bond angles in the gas phase. An elongation of 0.02Å and 0.02Å of bonds in C = O and O-H group upon complexation indicates that there are strong interactions between the oxygen and hydrogen atoms of aspirin and the boron and nitrogen of the nanocage. Table 1 shows that the computed adsorption energy (Eads) of this complex is -13.16 kcal/mol, indicating chemisorption. One single site was investigated in the BN-Com-B configuration for interactions, in which the C = O group of aspirin was directed towards the boron atom of the B12N12 nanocage. Geometry optimization shows the existence of an intermolecular bond of 1.61Å between the oxygen atom of aspirin and the boron atom of the nanocage and a C-O-B bond angle of 138.4°. The small bond length indicates the establishment of a strong covalent interaction. There is also elongation of the C = O bond in aspirin by 0.02Å upon the complexation, which is a good indication of the sensing ability of the nanocage for the drug molecule. The calculated adsorption energy (Eads) of this complex is around − 17.74 kcal/mol as shown in Table 1, indicating that the complex was chemisorbed. The hydrogen atom of aspirin of the BN-Com-C structure has interactions with the nitrogen atoms of the nanocage. Combined with structural relaxation, it has been determined that such interactions are mainly dominated by weak van der Waals forces. The calculated distances of interactions are 1.84Å with minimal movement of 0.01Å bond length, indicating reduced sensing at these points. Also, the Eads value of this complex is -8.96 kcal/mol, which is also in agreement with physisorption as indicated in Table 1. These results demonstrate the adsorption capability of the nanocage for emerging pollutants as reported in a previous study for the elimination of paracetamol from drinking water. The study predicted chemisorption in BNP-1 and BNP-3 by depicting Eads values approximately − 27.94 and − 15.31 kcal/mol, and physisorption in BNP-2 by demonstrating Eads values about − 6.97 kcal/mol. The oxygen atom of the C = O group in paracetamol strongly interacted in BNP-1 with the B atom of the nanocage, and the O atom of the O-H group in paracetamol interacted with the boron atom of the nanocage in BNP-3. The lowest adsorption energy values in BNP-2 are due to a weak hydrogen bond between the H of O-H group in paracetamol and the N atom of the nanocage. The previous study identified B and N atoms of the nanocage as active sites for interaction with O and H atoms in the paracetamol drug. The B and N atoms of the nanocage and O and H atoms of the aspirin drug demonstrated interaction consistent with the previous study. The previous and current studies suggest that boron nitride nanocages are promising candidates for the elimination of emerging pollutants from water efficiently34.

Both experimental35 and computational analyses of the UV-Visible spectra were performed to verify the molecular identity of aspirin. The main absorption peak calculated using Density Functional Theory (DFT) showed a main absorption maximum at 262 nm. corresponding to a π → π* transition in the aromatic system. This theoretical value was found to be in good agreement with the experimental λmax at 278 nm. The strong qualitative and quantitative agreement of the two techniques provides strong evidence for the optimized structure of the aspirin molecule as depicted in Fig. 4.

UV-Visible Spectra of aspirin on the left side (Experimental) and right side (simulated).

The experimental FT-IR36 spectrum of aspirin in Fig. 5 shows those distinct absorption bands that match the key functional groups of the molecule. Strong peaks in the 1186–1308 cm− 1 range correspond to C-O stretching vibrations of the ester and phenolic oxygen moieties. Next, bands between 1456 and 1604 cm− 1 are the aromatic C-C stretching and the in-plane deformations of the benzene ring, and confirm the presence of an aromatic framework. A broad, intense band in the 1687–1751 cm− 1 region corresponds to C = O stretching of both the acetyl group and the carboxyl group, which is an important structural characteristic of aspirin. The peaks in the experimental and simulated spectra were almost in the same position, as shown in Fig. 6. The C-O stretching at 1234 cm− 1 and the 1387–1533 cm− 1 peaks are well aligned with the skeletal vibrations of the benzene ring. The C = O stretching frequencies are computed using DFT at 1811 cm− 1 and 1870 cm− 1, 1811–1870 cm− 1 which are slightly higher than the experimental values due to the harmonic approximation in DFT, which tends to overestimate vibrational frequencies. It also contains a sharp peak with a higher frequency at 3762 cm− 1 for the O-H bond in the simulated spectrum because the model does not account for effects such as hydrogen bonding and the surrounding environment. Overall, it is evident that the experimental and simulated IR spectral values are in close agreement, which indicates that the DFT-based approach effectively reproduces typical C-O, C = O, O-H, and benzene ring vibrations of aspirin. This confirms the reliability of the structural and computational models.

The experimental Raman37 spectrum in Fig. 7 provides supplementary information on the vibrational behavior of the aromatic and carbonyl modes of aspirin. The strong band at 1606 cm⁻¹ corresponds to aromatic C = C stretching of the benzene ring, which typically exhibits high Raman intensity. Medium-to-strong peaks at 749 cm− 1, 1043 cm− 1, 1190 cm− 1, and 1291 cm− 1 correspond to C–H bending and C–O stretching vibrations of the benzene ring. The C = O stretching band of the acetyl group is observed at 1752 cm− 1. Aliphatic and aromatic C–H stretching vibrations appear at 2935 and 3078 cm⁻¹, respectively. The simulated Raman spectrum in Fig. 8 displays the same features. The C-H bending and the benzene ring modes are indicated by several Raman active peaks in the region 700–1300 cm− 1. The experimental C = C stretching band at 1606 cm− 1 is close to the calculated bands at 1500–1650 cm− 1. The peak in the computed spectrum for the C = O group at 1800–1900 cm− 1 displays C = O hormonal hits at very high strength, with slight discrepancies due to DFT overestimation. The high-frequency C-H stretching vibrations of the 3000–3100 cm− 1 range are also well reproduced with peaks of 2935 cm− 1 and 3078 cm− 1, consistent with the experimental peaks. Overall, the good correspondence between the experimental and the simulated Raman spectra indicates that our computation model is effectively capturing the main benzene ring, CO, C = O, and C-H vibrations of aspirin. These results validate, in fact, the molecular structure, as well as the DFT methodology, which we adopted in this study.

The changes in the global reactivity descriptors in Table 2 indicate significant changes in the electronic properties of B12N12 upon interaction with aspirin. The ionization energy of the pristine nanocage is 7.72 eV, with the rest of the adsorption complexes having a lower value, suggesting increased reactivity of the nanocage for the adsorption of aspirin. The ionization energies of the complexes are reduced significantly as compared to B12N12, indicating an increased ease of electron removal with the occurrence of adsorption. A similar trend is observed for electron affinity (EA). The initial EA of B12N12 (0.86 eV) increases in all the complexes, indicating that the nanocage acquires a better capacity to accept electrons during the adsorption process. The most favorable configuration corresponds to the highest increase in EA, and the complex that has the greatest increase in EA. This is an improvement over the pristine nanocage, which shows the process of the enhancement of charge transfer capability through adsorption. Changes in electronegativity compared to B₁₂N₁₂ values, which are about 4.29 eV. Both complexes have a slight enhancement or decline in electronegativity that reflects adjustments in the electron-withdrawing ability of the nanocage by the formation of the adsorption complexes. Whenever there is an increase in values, it indicates that the attraction towards the electron density is improved as compared to the initial nanocage. Reactivity differences can be clearly reflected by chemical hardness between the pristine nanocage and complexes. The hardness of B12N12 is 3.43 eV, whereas all the complexes have a considerably lower hardness. This makes the complexes more chemically active and more prone to charge transfer than the undecorated nanocage. In line with this, the softness also increases, even though its initial value is 0.291, which also confirms the augmented reactivity of the adsorbed systems. The chemical potential of B₁₂N₁₂ (− 4.23 eV) remains negative amongst all the complexes, which indicates that adsorption does not affect electronic stability. Slight variations in µ, however, show variations in the force behind the exchange of electrons when compared to the pristine structure. The most significant change is observed in the global electrophilicity index. The complexes are more electrophilic than pristine B₁₂N₁₂, giving an ω value of 2.68. This increment indicates that there is an increase in the strength of the electron-accepting system of the nanocage after adsorption. The complex that shows the greatest increase in the ω nature is the most favorable amongst pristine nanocages in terms of electrophilic nature and is suitable for the adsorption of aspirin.

A detailed understanding of the type and strength of interactions between B12N12 nanocage and the aspirin molecule was obtained using QTAIM (Quantum Theory of Atoms in Molecules) analysis40. The analysis was performed at the B3LYP level of theory and 6-31G(d, p) basis set employing the Gaussian 16 software package (https://seagrid.org/). The QTAIM graphs with bond critical points (BCPS) are shown in Fig. 10. Topological parameters at the BCPs, such as kinetic energy density (Gb), Laplacian of electron density (∇²ρb), electron density (ρb), potential energy density (Vb), and total energy density (Hb) were used to characterize the interactions as covalent or non-covalent, based on electron sharing or transfer. A positive value of (Hb) usually depicts a closed shell (electrostatic) interaction at BCPs, and a negative Hb indicates shared (covalent) interactions. Also, the type of interactions can be further classified from the sign of (Hb) and (∇²ρb). When the values of (Hb) and (∇²ρb) are positive, the interactions are weak electrostatic and covalent when the values of (Hb) and (∇²ρb) are negative. When (Hb) values are negative and (∇²ρb) are positive, the interactions are of partial nature, comprising both electrostatic and covalent interactions. In addition, the bonding nature can also be inferred from the -Gb/Vb ratio. Weak electrostatic interactions are shown when the values are greater than 1, and covalent interactions exist when the values are less than 0.5. The existence of partial interactions is demonstrated when the values of -Gb/Vb are greater than 0.5 and less than 1. Table 3 provides the QTAIM results that indicate that BCP 37 and BCP 54 of BN-Com-A, BCP 55 of BN-Com-B, and BCP 28 of BN-Com-C have negative Hb values, which symbolize that there are shared (covalent) interactions between the adsorbent and aspirin molecule. The increased electron density at BCP 37, 54, and 28 of BN-Com-A, BN-Com-B, and BN-Com-C shows the existence of stronger interactions at these sites. The negative values of Hb and positive values of ∇²ρb at BCPs 37 and 54 in BN-Com-A witness the presence of partial interactions. Similarly, the same results are reproduced at BCPs 55 and 28 in BN-Com-B and BN-Com-C, showing the existence of both covalent and electrostatic interactions. The -Gb/Vb ratio provides further insight into the nature of interactions in the complexes. The values of -Gb/Vb ratio for all three complexes are less than 1 and greater than 0.5, demonstrating partial interactions, confirming these findings. All these results indicate that the B12N12 nanocage has both high and selective interaction capabilities for the aspirin molecule, which makes it an effective adsorbent for drug removal applications.

The FMO (frontier molecular orbital)41, the HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital), and the LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) before and after adsorption were examined to study the adsorption behavior of the aspirin drug on the B12N12 nanocage. Fig. 11 shows the corresponding distributions of the molecular orbital, and the obtained values are summarized in Table 1. According to Fig. 11, the HOMO density is mostly concentrated on nitrogen atoms in the pristine (B12N12) nanocage, and the LUMO density is mainly concentrated on boron atoms. When the aspirin molecule is adsorbed, significant electron density redistribution occurs on the HOMO distributions of the formed complexes. The HOMO in the BN-Com-A complex is mostly localized on the nitrogen and oxygen atoms of the C = O group of the aspirin molecule, and LUMO is localized in the proximity of the adsorption site, which indicates a large charge transfer between the nanocage and aspirin. In BN-Com-B, the HOMO is still localized on the B12N12 surface, where the LUMO is mostly localized on the aspirin molecule at the interaction site. Such an orbital disposition indicates net charge transfer between the nanocage and aspirin drug. On the other hand, in BN-Com-C, HOMO and LUMO are highly delocalized across the aspirin molecule, meaning that they are weakly interacting with each other and indicating minimal charge transfer. The HOMO and LUMO energy values associated with the isolated B12N12 nanocage were determined as -7.70 eV and − 0.84 eV, respectively, resulting in a band gap of 6.86 eV. For the aspirin molecule, the energy levels of the corresponding molecule are − 6.89 eV (HOMO) and − 1.46 eV (LUMO) with a band gap of 5.43 eV. When BN-Com-A is complexed, the HOMO of the complex is equal to -6.50 eV and the LUMO is equal to -1.42 eV, resulting in a reduced band gap of 5.62 eV. This enhances the electronic conductivity and sensitivity of the nanocage for the drug molecule. Likewise, the reduced HOMO energies (-6.53 eV and − 6.78 eV) and the increased LUMO energies (-2.27 eV and − 1.28 eV) are also seen for BN-Com-B and BN-Com-C with energy gaps of 4.25 eV and 5.49 eV, respectively. These results indicate that adsorption triggers significant electronic modulation of the nanocage. The significant reduction in the band gap of BN-Com-A and BN-Com-B, compared to BN-Com-C, can be explained by the fact that the orbital overlap and the interactions of the nanocage with the aspirin molecule have increased. FMO (Frontier molecular orbital) and DOS (density of states) analyses were used to determine the sensing properties of the B12N12 nanocage for the aspirin drug. The results summarized in Table 1 show a significant decrease in HOMO energies and an increase in LUMO energies, leading to a significant narrowing of the band gap. The highest band gap values about 5.62 eV were obtained for BN-Com-A, followed by BN-Com-B and BN-Com-C with band gap values of 4.25 eV and 5.48 eV respectively. The (DOS) density of state analysis reveals the appearance of new electronic states between the occupied and unoccupied orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) after the complexation of aspirin and the nanocage. These newly formed states indicate strong electronic coupling between the adsorbate and adsorbent, leading to a noticeable reduction in the band gap for all complexes, as shown in Fig. 12. This band gap narrowing reflects enhanced electronic interactions and improved charge transfer characteristics, which are consistent with the adsorption behavior observed in the complexes.

The EDD (Electron density difference)42 analysis was conducted to further confirm the intermolecular interactions between the aspirin drug and the nanocage. Fig. 13 shows the EDD plots of BN-Com-A, BN-Com-B, and BN-Com-C. The difference between the electron density (ED) of the complexes and the summed ED of the respective individual monomers, i.e., the drug and the nanocage, was used to generate these plots. Based on the coordinates of the optimized geometries of the complexes, the ED of the isolated species was identified. The occurrence of blue and green areas in the EDD maps of the optimized systems depicts orbital overlap and interactions between the aspirin drug and the nanocage. In addition, a large green contour indicates electron density depletion. The electron density of the O and H atoms of the aspirin molecule in BN-Com-B is lost to the Boron and Nitrogen atoms of the nanocage. Similarly, the O atom of the aspirin drug transfers electron density to the Boron atom of the nanocage in BN-Com-A. On the other hand, the green region and blue region, which are separated in BN-Com-C, suggest weak intermolecular forces between the aspirin and the nanocage (B12N12). The EDD analysis is very similar with the NBO (charge transfer) analysis.

Figure 14 presents the partial density of state (PDOS) analysis showing that the electronic states near the fermi level are mainly contributed by the s and p orbitals of boron and nitrogen atoms of the adsorbent, interacting with the s and p orbitals of oxygen and hydrogen atoms of the adsorbate, respectively. A noticeable hybridization is observed near the fermi level, particularly involving the s orbital of hydrogen and p orbital of N at the active sites in BN-Com-A and BN-Com-C systems, indicating strong electronic coupling. In the case of BN-Com-B, significant interactions are observed between the s orbital of B and the p orbital of O, which contribute to the modification of the electronic structure where the states on the left side of fermi level are associated with HOMO and those on the right side correspond to LUMO, collectively supporting the adsorption characteristics of the complexes.

Natural Bond Orbital (NBO)43 analysis was used to analyze the charge transfer properties. The geometry optimization of the B12N12 aspirin complexes was performed at the B3LYP function 6-31G(d, p) basis set, level of theory using the Gaussian 16 software package (https://seagrid.org/). The outcome showed that the negative charges were localized on the O atom of the carbonyl and hydroxyl groups in the aspirin molecule, and the boron and nitrogen atoms of the B12N12 nanocage carry positive and negative charges, respectively. The BN-Com-B and BN-Com-A configurations showed a decrease in the charge on the oxygen atom in aspirin from − 0.573 e, -0.605 e to -0.553 e and − 0.592 e, indicating charge transfer from aspirin to the nanocage about -0.020 e and − 0.013 e. In the case of BN-Com-A and BN-Com-C, the charge on the hydrogen atom of the O-H group increased from 0.509 e to 0.534 e and 0.525 e, indicating charge transfer of 0.025 e and 0.016 e from the nanocage to aspirin, respectively, as shown in Table 1. BN-Com-B had the highest level of charge transfer, compared to all the other configurations, which is attributed to its highest adsorption energy and small bond length values, indicating a robust chemisorption process. Likewise, a significant adsorption strength was also confirmed in BN-Com-A, showing a noticeable charge transfer. BN-Com-C, on the other hand, exhibited the lowest adsorption energy and the least amount of charge redistribution, indicating physisorption.

In this research, we have investigated the removal of aspirin from wastewater using B12N12 nanocage, with DFT-D3 including the BSSE method. Three complexes were optimized, namely BN-Com-A, BN-Com-B, and BN-Com-C. BN-Com-A and BN-Com-B showed chemisorption with adsorption energy values greater than − 11 kcal/mol in both DFT-D3 and DFT+BSSE methods. BN-Com-C depicted physisorption in both methods with adsorption energy values less than − 11 kcal/mol.

Using the polarizable continuum model (PCM), water was used as a solvent to ensure the adsorption behavior of the adsorbent in the solvent (Water). The adsorption energies showed an increase for BN-Com-A and BN-Com-C, whereas the adsorption energy values decreased for BN-Com-B in aqueous medium, as summarized in Table 4. For BN-Com-A, the adsorption energy increases from − 13.16 kcal/mol (gas phase) to -17.42 kcal/mol (water), indicating stronger adsorption in aqueous medium. Similarly, BN-Com-C also shows a significant increase from − 8.96 kcal/mol (gas phase) to -16.99 kcal/mol (Water), suggesting enhanced stability of the complex in water. In contrast, BN-Com-B shows a slight decrease in adsorption strength, from − 17.74 kcal/mol (gas phase) to -16.10 kcal/mol (water). These variations occur because the aqueous environment affects the stability of complexes differently. These values confirm that aspirin is strongly adsorbed onto B12N12 nanocage in the presence of a polar solvent, and the nanocage is effective in a physiological environment. Besides, the difference in dipole moment (DM) between the pure nanocage and the drug-loaded complex was calculated, and the findings are shown in Table 4. The isolated B12N12 nanocage has a zero-point dipole moment because of its highly symmetrical structure. Nonetheless, when in contact with the aspirin molecule, the dipole moments of the complexes increases significantly, which is a sign of strong redistribution of charge between the nanocage and the drug. The increase in DM values indicates an increase in charge separation and justifies the existence of strong interactions. The highest values of dipole moment were obtained for BN-Com-B, demonstrating the strongest interactions among the optimized complexes. The increased dipole moment, adsorption energy, and degree of charge transfer correlate directly, which supports the feasibility of B12N12 nanocages as an effective drug sensor or carrier in the biological environment.
