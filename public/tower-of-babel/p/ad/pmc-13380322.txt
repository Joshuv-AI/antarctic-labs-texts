Ageing in Honeybees: Telomere Length Is Driven by Climate and Resource Availability Rather Than Urbanisation or Pollution

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnderstanding how biodiversity responds to human‐caused environmental change represents a major scientific challenge. Among the biological processes underlying these responses, ageing plays a critical role by shaping individual life histories and, ultimately, demographic dynamics. Telomeres, the repetitive nucleotide sequences at the ends of linear chromosomes, are widely used biomarkers of ageing, as their length can be shaped by environmental conditions, and can predict health and lifespan. Despite this, few broad‐scale ecological studies have examined how telomere length varies in response to environmental change in wildlife. Here, we investigate whether telomeres of a globally widespread pollinator species, the honeybee Apis mellifera , are shaped by gradients of urbanisation, environmental pollution, climatic conditions, and floral resources in central Argentina. To that end, we sampled 981 individuals across 9 localities and 57 sites spanning a ~1000 km gradient of environmental variation. Urbanisation (β = 0.032, 95% CI [−0.048, 0.112]) and chemical contamination (PC1: β = 0.026, 95% CI [−0.075, 0.126]; PC2: β = 0.022, 95% CI [−0.065, 0.109]) were not associated with variation in telomere length. In contrast, climatic conditions and floral characteristics emerged as key predictors of telomere variation, with telomere length declining with increasing mean annual temperature (β = −0.085, 95% CI [−0.158, −0.012]) and increasing floral abundance (β = −0.109, 95% CI [−0.194, −0.024]). Our results indicate that climatic conditions and resource availability are stronger predictors of honeybee telomere length than urbanisation or environmental pollution. These findings suggest that telomere‐based patterns of ageing are shaped by the thermal and ecological context experienced by foragers in this key pollinator species.

Introduction

Understanding how wild populations respond to environmental change represents a key challenge in evolutionary and conservation biology (Diffenbaugh et al. 2017; Gaitán‐Espitia and Hobday 2021). This knowledge is essential for predicting population dynamics and ecosystem resilience under rapidly changing conditions (Bradshaw and Holzapfel 2009; Fuller et al. 2010; Somero 2012; Traill et al. 2010). However, despite substantial progress in describing ecological and demographic responses to environmental change, we still lack a mechanistic framework explaining how anthropogenic and climatic factors are translated into physiological changes that affect individual performance and ultimately population‐level outcomes. Processes linked to ageing are particularly relevant in this context because they can mediate the effects of environmental conditions on fitness and demographic resilience across different spatial scales.

Ageing often refers to the progressive loss of physiological functions such as reproduction or locomotion, and the decline in survival probability over an organism's lifespan (Peel et al. 2004; Young 1997), driven both by genetic and environmental factors (Melzer et al. 2020). Such environmentally‐driven changes in ageing rates can ultimately shape life‐history trade‐offs and evolutionary dynamics (Cohen et al. 2020; Gaillard and Lemaître 2020; Lemaître et al. 2015; Monaghan et al. 2008). Yet, quantifying ageing in ecological contexts can be challenging, which explains the use of molecular biomarkers that capture cumulative physiological state. In this context, telomeres are among the most widely used biomarkers of ageing (Aubert and Lansdorp 2008; López‐Otín et al. 2023). Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences located at the ends of chromosomes that protect them from degradation and fusion during cell division (Aubert and Lansdorp 2008; Blackburn 1991). These sequences progressively shorten with each cell division, and this shortening can be further accelerated by environmental stress and unhealthy lifestyle factors (Chatelain et al. 2020; Epel et al. 2004; Salmón and Burraco 2022; Zhang et al. 2013). Critically short telomeres can trigger cellular apoptosis (Blasco 2007) which can lead to tissue degeneration and organismal ageing (Bize et al. 2009; Monaghan 2014; Wang et al. 2018; Wilbourn et al. 2018). In wildlife, shorter telomeres have been associated with increased disease susceptibility, and reduced fitness (Eastwood et al. 2019; Giraudeau et al. 2019; Kong et al. 2013; Pauliny et al. 2006). The study of variation in telomere length can therefore provide valuable information about the consequences of environmental challenges for wildlife health span and ageing.

Most current evidence on the consequences of variation in telomere length in nature comes from fine‐scale comparisons among neighboring populations (reviewed in Burraco et al. 2022), whereas fewer studies have assessed telomere variation across broader spatial scales (but see e.g., Dupoué et al. 2017; Dupoué et al. 2022; Ibáñez‐Álamo et al. 2018; Kärkkäinen et al. 2022; Kirby et al. 2017; Reichard et al. 2022; Rodriguez et al. 2025; Rouan et al. 2023; Wolf et al. 2025). Moreover, most available studies on the impact of environmental conditions on telomeres have focused on single stressors, rarely accounting for multiple factors (Salmón and Burraco 2022). Telomere dynamics have been particularly understudied in ectotherms, especially in invertebrates (Burraco et al. 2020; Friesen et al. 2021; Olsson et al. 2018), despite these organisms being highly sensitive to environmental stress (Seebacher et al. 2015).

The honeybee Apis mellifera is a globally distributed eusocial insect and a key pollinator of many wild plants and crops, playing a fundamental role in ecosystem functioning and agricultural productivity (Papa et al. 2022; Requier et al. 2019). Its ecological success is partly due to its remarkable adaptability to a wide range of habitats, including urban, agricultural, and natural landscapes (Conte and Navajas 2008; Hollens‐Kuhr et al. 2025; Parker et al. 2010). However, A. mellifera populations have recently experienced significant declines, attributed to multiple interacting stressors (Goulson et al. 2015), which may be linked to altered ageing‐related processes. As a species with both managed and wild populations, the honeybee is frequently exposed to diverse anthropogenic stressors, making it a suitable model for studying the effects of global change on biological processes such as animal health and ageing, and their potential consequences for ecosystems (Münch et al. 2008).

Here, we addressed whether exposure to multiple anthropogenic and environmental stressors, including urbanisation, environmental pollution, climatic conditions and floral resource availability, is associated with increased physiological ageing in populations of this widespread pollinator in Argentina, using telomere length as biomarker. Urbanisation and environmental pollution are known to challenge organisms across many taxa by increasing oxidative stress and telomere shortening (Chatelain et al. 2020; Hollens‐Kuhr et al. 2025; Salmón and Burraco 2022). Likewise, climatic conditions, particularly temperature, can influence telomere dynamics in ectotherms by affecting activity levels, metabolic rate, oxidative balance and energy expenditure (Burraco et al. 2020; Friesen et al. 2021; Ritchie and Friesen 2022). Floral resource availability may also shape telomere dynamics, because reduced or unstable resources can increase foraging effort and energetic constraints, or competition (Bateson and Nettle 2018; Goulson et al. 2015; Margotta et al. 2018; Wolf and Schmid‐Hempel 1989). In our study, we analysed telomere length in adult foraging workers of A. mellifera sampled across 57 sites within 9 localities spanning an ~1000 km environmental gradient. In social insects, ageing trajectories can differ among castes and social roles, and honeybee workers show strong task‐ and season‐related plasticity in lifespan and ageing (Koubová et al. 2021; Münch et al. 2008). Moreover, telomere biology in honeybees is influenced by caste, with queens showing higher telomerase activity than workers or drones (Korandová and Frydrychová 2016; Koubová et al. 2021). By focusing on adult foraging workers, we minimised variation associated with caste, while targeting the worker group most directly exposed to external environmental conditions. We hypothesised that the presence of multiple stressors associated with anthropogenic environmental change accelerates ageing in honeybees, leading to shorter telomeres. Under our main hypothesis, we predicted that honeybees inhabiting more urbanised or polluted environments, experiencing harsher (e.g., warmer or drier) climatic conditions, or foraging in landscapes with reduced or unstable floral resources, would exhibit shorter telomeres. Alternatively, because honeybees can buffer environmental challenges through behavioural flexibility, high mobility, detoxification capacity and colony‐level processes such as division of labour and resource sharing (Conte and Navajas 2008; Parker et al. 2010), telomere length may show weak or no associations with the environmental gradients included in our study.

Materials and Methods

Study Sites

We carried out the study in central Argentina (Figure 1), where the climate is mostly temperate but environmental conditions vary considerably across sites. We selected nine localities (ordered in decreasing order of population size): Ciudad Autónoma de Buenos Aires (CABA), Córdoba, Mendoza, La Plata, San Juan, Bahía Blanca, Tornquist, Pedro Luro and Rivera. At each locality, we selected five to nine sampling sites spanning different degrees of urbanisation, determined by the percentage of impervious surface (see the section on landscape analysis for details).

Honeybee Sampling

Sampling sites included urban green spaces (public and private parks, gardens), nature reserves or native habitat areas, and agricultural areas within a 60‐km radius of focal cities. We collected foraging A. mellifera specimens during standardized 15‐min walk surveys (Montgomery et al. 2021), targeting individuals visiting flowering plants. These foragers were typically 3 weeks old (Seeley 1989), although the onset of foraging can vary with colony conditions and worker state (Woyciechowski and Moroń 2009). The average sample size per site (N = 57 sites) was 17 bees. Immediately after sampling, specimens were preserved in 96% ethanol until processing for molecular assays.

Landscape Analysis

Sampling sites were georeferenced using Google Earth Pro. For each site, a 500‐m buffer zone (a circular area with a 500‐m radius) was defined, centred on the point where honeybees were collected. Previous research identified this buffer size as appropriate for characterizing habitat use by most floral visitors, including honeybees (Banaszak‐Cibicka and Żmihorski 2012; Hülsmann et al. 2015; Kaluza et al. 2016; Monasterolo et al. 2022). Within each buffer zone, all impervious surfaces (buildings, roads, and other structures > 0.2 ha) were mapped as polygons and their total area (in ha) was calculated using Google Earth Pro tools and following (Graffigna et al. 2024, 2026). The total impervious surface area was estimated as the sum of these features. Finally, the percentage of impervious surface cover was calculated ([total impervious surface/500 m buffer area] × 100), hereafter referred to as the urbanisation index.

Environmental Pollutants in Pollen

We leveraged a complementary experiment conducted to quantify a broad diversity of persistent organic pollutants (POPs) in pollen collected by wild cavity‐nesting bees. In particular, pollen from multiple brood cells (746 nests) from 39 solitary bee species (see Table S1) was pooled at each site to generate a single site‐level composite sample for pollutant analysis. Wild bees and honeybees can overlap in the floral resources they use (Sgolastra et al. 2024; Steffan‐Dewenter and Tscharntke 2000; Torné‐Noguera et al. 2016) hence pollen provisions collected from wild cavity nesting bees provide an integrated summary of local floral resources beyond a snapshot of flowers visited a single moment. These data were therefore considered a site‐level proxy of contaminant availability in bee‐used floral resources, rather than a direct measure of individual A. mellifera exposure (Sgolastra et al. 2024). At each site, artificial nest traps were installed to attract cavity‐nesting solitary bee species (see Graffigna et al. 2026 for a detailed description). From completed nests, pollen provisions, i.e., the compact masses of pollen stored by female bees as food for their larvae, were collected, providing an integrated sample of floral resources used by bees at each location.

The pooled samples were then frozen, lyophilized, and ground before analysis. POP analysis was conducted following the procedure described by Poma et al. (2017). Overall, the concentrations of 41 POPs, including 21 polychlorinated biphenyls, 12 organochlorine pesticides and 8 polybrominated diphenyl ethers, were measured. The complete list of contaminants and their individual concentrations and the analytical procedure is detailed in Tables S2 and S3.

Floral Resources

To assess the importance of the availability of feeding resources as a driver of telomere length variation, different indicators for the diversity, quantity and temporal distribution of floral resources (i.e., floral abundance, floral richness, floral Shannon index and stability of floral abundance over time) were characterized. Specifically, three complementary approaches were used: standardized transects, time‐specific non‐systematic walks, and total area estimation, with protocols adapted to site characteristics (see Figures S1 and S2). In natural and agricultural areas, floral surveys were conducted along two 50 m × 2 m transects per site, while in urban and peri‐urban sites (primarily gardens) two 100 m × 5 m transects placed along adjacent sidewalks bordering the garden block were used. For private gardens, the total area was recorded to estimate site‐wide floral abundance and species richness.

Finally, to capture incidental floral diversity, 5‐min random walks at all sites were conducted, recording all flowers and inflorescences within a 5 m observational width (sampled area = distance walked × 5 m). In urban and peri‐urban sites, walks within green areas located within 500 m of the artificial nest traps (parks, reserves, among others) were prioritized. Species abundance was categorized using an 8‐tier scale (1–10, 11–50, 51–100, 101–500, 501–1000, 1001–5000, 5001–10,000 and > 10,000 individuals). Floral abundance was standardized as the mean number of flowers per m2 across all estimates per site. Floral stability was quantified as the inverse of the coefficient of variation (1/CV), with the CV calculated as the mean floral abundance per site, divided by its standard deviation to account for temporal variability across sampling visits.

Floral richness was estimated using the effective number of species from Hill numbers (Hill 1973), with q = 0 corresponding to species richness, at each site and for each floral resource survey methodology (standardized transects, timed random walks and total area estimation). We used the iNEXT package in R (Hsieh et al. 2016), which allows estimating species diversity by accounting for sample completeness using sample coverage. Sample coverage is a metric that reflects the extent to which individuals of a community are included in a sample (Chao and Jost 2012). To compare species richness among sites, the richness expected for coverage of the site with the lowest coverage was calculated for each site, making richness estimates comparable among sites (Chao and Jost 2012). Finally, the expected richness estimates resulting from each survey methodology were averaged to obtain one richness estimate per site.

Climatic Variables

To characterize the environmental conditions at each sampling site, values of bioclimatic variables were extracted from the WorldClim database (www.worldclim.org). Raster layers at 30 arc‐seconds (~1 km2 per grid cell at the equator) were used. This scale matches the normal foraging range and landscape use of honeybees (Danner et al. 2016) and captures environmental variation at ecologically relevant spatial scales in our study. The following bioclimatic variables were included in our analyses: Bio1 (annual mean temperature), Bio4 (temperature seasonality), Bio5 (highest temperature of the hottest month), Bio6 (lowest temperature of the coldest month), Bio12 (annual precipitation), Bio14 (precipitation of the driest month), and Bio15 (precipitation seasonality), all known to influence animal telomere dynamics (e.g., Criscuolo et al. 2020; Dupoué et al. 2020; Eastwood et al. 2022; Rodriguez et al. 2025; van Lieshout et al. 2022). Raster data were handled and extracted using the R packages raster (version 3.6.26; (Hijmans et al. 2015)) and terra (version 1.7.5; (Hijmans et al. 2022)).

DNA Extraction and Relative Telomere Length Quantification

Genomic DNA was isolated from the muscle tissue of honeybees using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol. Ethanol was allowed to evaporate for 20 min, then each tissue sample was incubated at 65°C for 30 min in 0.8 mL of CTAB buffer (2% [w/v] CTAB, 1.4 M NaCl, 0.2% [v/v] 2‐mercaptoethanol, 20 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 100 mM Tris pH 7.5). DNA was purified by a threefold extraction with chloroform followed by precipitation with cold 2/3 [v/v] isopropanol. As a quality check, partial sequences of the mitochondrial COI (cytochrome oxidase subunit I) marker were amplified by PCR with the primers LCO1490 and HCO2198 (Vrijenhoek 1994). The products of PCR reactions were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and via bidirectionally sequencing through Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Korea). The sequences were edited to remove the primers and consensus sequences were assembled using BIOEDIT 7.2.5 (Hall 1999). The obtained consensus sequences were compared with reference sequences from GenBank through the BLASTn algorithm (Altschul et al. 1997), and the identity of the specimens studied was confirmed. COI sequences were aligned with the ClustalW algorithm of Mega X software (Kumar et al. 2018).

The isolated genomic DNA was used to quantify relative telomere length following a standard qPCR‐based protocol commonly used across animals (Nussey et al. 2014). This protocol compares the amount of telomeric sequences –(TTAGG)n in honeybees relative to a housekeeping gene, actin in this study, which has been widely used as a control gene in this species (Lourenço et al. 2008; Scharlaken et al. 2008). Specifically, the following primer combinations were used: TEL‐1 5′‐CGGTTTGTTTGGTTTGGTTTGGTTTGGTTTGGTT‐3′ and TEL‐2 5′‐GGCTTGCCTTACCTTACCTTACCTTACCTTACCT‐2′ for telomeric sequence amplification (Grula et al. 2024), and ACT‐F 5′‐GTATGCCAACACTGTCCTTTCTG‐3′ and ACT‐R 5′‐ATGGTGGATGGTGCTAGGGC‐3′ for actin amplification. For telomere qPCRs, reactions included forward and reverse primers at 500 nM, 5 μL of SyberGreen, 3 μL of water, and 1 μL of DNA at 20 ng/μL, in a total volume of 10 μL. Telomere qPCR conditions were 95°C for 10 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 20 s, 58°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 20 s, and a dissociation melting curve. For actin qPCRs, reactions included forward and reverse primers at 500 nM, 5 μL of SyberGreen, 3 μL of water, and 1 μL of DNA at 20 ng/μL, in a total volume of 20 μL. Actin qPCR conditions were 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 58°C for 15 s, and 72°C for 30 s, and a dissociation melting curve.

Samples were run in 384‐well plates, and the efficiency of each plate was tested by including a standard curve with seven samples serially diluted in triplicate. Primer‐dimers were not detected in the samples nor in the blanks. Then, for each sample, cycle threshold (Ct) and efficiency of the telomere and control gene were used to calculate relative telomere length. Samples were run in duplicate and discarded those with CV% greater than 5% (1 out of all samples, after repeating 25 samples). Efficiencies were 106.2 ± 1.0 and 103.0 ± 2.6 for telomere and control amplification, respectively. Average intra‐sample CV% was 1.75% and 0.55% for telomere and control gene, respectively. All qPCRs were performed on a LightCycler 480 (Roche). Final sample size for relative telomere length was 981.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team 2024). Figures were plotted with the help of the package ggplot2 version 4.0.0 (Wickham 2016). All continuous predictors were inspected for their distributional properties prior to modelling. The variables exhibiting right‐skewed distributions (relative telomere length, urbanisation index after adding 0.1 to avoid infinite values, and all floral metrics) were log‐transformed to improve linearity and meet parametric assumptions. To avoid collinearity among climatic conditions, a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the seven bioclimatic variables at 30 arc‐second spatial resolution (Bio1, Bio4, Bio5, Bio6, Bio12, Bio14 and Bio15). The PCA was computed on scaled variables; the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) together captured 87.6% of the total variance (PC1 = 64.9%, PC2 = 22.7%; Figure S3). These PCs were retained for analyses, as multivariate descriptors of temperature‐ and precipitation‐related climatic gradients. Linear mixed‐effects models were fitted with relative telomere length as the response variable and urbanisation index, the first two climatic principal components, floral richness, floral stability, floral Shannon index, and floral abundance, as fixed effects. To account for sampling structure and laboratory batch effects, we included site nested within locality, and qPCR plate as random factors. Multicollinearity among predictors was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF) with the check_collinearity function from the performance package (Lüdecke et al. 2021). All VIF values were < 1.8, indicating low collinearity. For interpretation of effect sizes and comparison of predictor contributions, a standardized version of the model was fitted in which all continuous variables (predictors and response) were z‐transformed (mean = 0, SD = 1). Standardized coefficients were used for visualization (forest plots of β coefficients). We assessed model significance via Type II Wald χ 2 tests.

Since contaminant data were available for only a subset of individuals (N = 671, compared with 981 individuals in the full dataset), a separate model was fitted to assess the influence of environmental contamination on relative telomere length. Prior to analysis, contaminant variables with zero variance across samples were excluded. Site‐level mean concentrations were then calculated for the remaining contaminants, and a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. The first two principal components, which together captured 59.7% of the variance (39.5% and 20.2%, respectively), were retained and they were included as predictors in a model with the same structure as described above. Standardised versions of the model were fitted to obtain comparable effect sizes and to generate forest plots of β coefficients. The model significance was assessed using Type II Wald χ 2 tests.

Results

Relative telomere length showed substantial variation across sampled individuals (N = 981 bees, log‐transformed relative telomere length: mean ± SD = 0.521 ± 0.318; range = −0.822 to 1.543). This variation was also evident spatially, with site‐level mean relative telomere length ranging from 0.271 to 0.722 and locality‐level mean relative telomere length ranging from 0.385 to 0.578. Overall, sampling sites covered broad environmental variation, including different urbanisation levels (as percentage of impervious surface) ranging from 0% to 91.1%, mean annual temperatures from 12.7°C to 18.1°C, annual precipitation from 222 to 1107 mm, or floral abundance from 0.17 to 243.92 flowers per m2. Detailed environmental information for each sampling site is provided as a site‐level table in the accompanying dataset (see Data availability statement). Climatic PC1 (explaining 64.9% of the variance) captured a combined gradient of temperature seasonality and precipitation, whereas climatic PC2 (explaining 22.7% of the variance) represented a primarily thermal gradient, strongly associated with mean annual temperature (Figure S3). In relation to contaminants, PC1 (explaining 39.5% of the variance) was characterized by high positive loadings of multiple polychlorinated biphenyl congeners (PCBs), whereas PC2 (explaining 20.2% of the variance) reflected a gradient contrasting several organochlorine pesticides and metabolites with contributions from selected polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs) (Figure S4).

Linear mixed‐effects models showed no effect of urbanisation on bee telomere length (β = 0.032, 95% CI: [−0.048, 0.112]; Table S4; Figure 2). Likewise, none of the contaminant principal components explained variation in telomere length (PC1: β = 0.026, 95% CI: [−0.075, 0.126]; PC2: β = 0.022, 95% CI: [−0.065, 0.109]; Table S4 and Figure 2). In contrast, climatic and floral‐linked conditions influenced bee telomeres. Regarding climate, while PC1 had no detectable effect (β = 0.001, 95% CI: [−0.075, 0.076]; Table S4 and Figure 2), PC2 showed a significant negative association with relative telomere length (β = −0.085, 95% CI: [−0.158, −0.012]; Table S4; Figure 2). Because climatic PC2 mainly captured variation in mean annual temperature, this result indicates that telomere length declined with increasing mean annual temperature, with bees from warmer environments exhibiting shorter telomeres (Figure 3). Finally, in relation to floral resources, telomere length declined with increasing floral abundance (β = −0.109, 95% CI: [−0.194, −0.024]; Table S4, Figures 2 and 4), while floral richness (β = −0.017, 95% CI: [−0.121, 0.086]), floral stability (β = 0.049, 95% CI: [−0.020, 0.118]), and floral Shannon index (β = −0.035, 95% CI: [−0.142, 0.073]; Table S4 and Figure 2) were unrelated to telomere length.

Discussion

Our study shows that telomere length in honeybees varies substantially with environmental conditions across broad geographic gradients. Specifically, telomere length decreased with increasing mean annual temperature and floral abundance, whereas no association was detected with urbanisation or environmental pollution. Together, these findings suggest that climatic and biotic conditions influence physiological stress and ageing in the honeybee, a globally distributed pollinator with key ecosystem functions. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine how multiple components of global change jointly shape invertebrate telomeres.

Understanding the impact of global change on animal life histories, particularly the ageing process, is crucial for predicting how populations will respond to increasingly human‐altered environments (Cohen et al. 2020; Gaillard and Lemaître 2020; Lemaître et al. 2015; Lemaître and Gaillard 2017; Monaghan et al. 2008). In our study, neither urbanisation nor environmental pollution explained variation in honeybee telomere length, contrasting with the expectation that anthropogenic disturbances or contaminant exposure would impose chronic physiological stress. Reviews and meta‐analyses have reported broadly negative associations between anthropic disturbances—including environmental pollution—and telomere length across taxa (Chatelain et al. 2020; Miri et al. 2019; Passos et al. 2022; Salmón and Burraco 2022; Zhang et al. 2013). These syntheses, however, also highlight substantial heterogeneity, with many individual studies documenting no detectable effect or even apparently positive relationships. Such variability suggests that telomere responses to anthropogenic pressures could be context‐dependent and modulated by the interaction between specific pollutants and physiological pathways underlying telomere regulation, such as oxidative stress or telomerase (Salmón and Burraco 2022). In this context, unaffected telomere length by highly urbanised and polluted environments may indicate that Apis mellifera can buffer or compensate for these stressors under natural conditions. Their high mobility, efficient detoxification mechanisms, and colony‐level buffering through social immunity, division of labour, and resource sharing may reduce the individual physiological burden of environmental challenges (Berenbaum and Johnson 2015; Evans and Pettis 2005; López‐Uribe et al. 2025). It is also plausible that the specific indicators used here captured only part of the anthropogenic pressures experienced by honeybees. In particular, persistent organic pollutants measured in pollen provide an integrated proxy of contaminant exposure at the site level, but they may not fully reflect other relevant stressors such as heavy metals or colony‐level exposure histories. Indeed, because persistent organic pollutants were quantified in pollen provisions collected by cavity‐nesting bees rather than directly from A. mellifera bodies or colonies, they may not completely represent individual‐ or colony‐level exposure histories. Future experiments manipulating environmental conditions across urbanisation gradients and pollutant mixtures will be essential to determine the extent to which honeybee populations can cope with or adapt to human‐driven environmental change.

Beyond the lack of effects from urbanisation and environmental pollution, our results show that honeybee telomere length is primarily shaped by climatic conditions and floral resource availability. Telomeres were shorter in bees from warmer environments and also in populations inhabiting areas with higher floral abundance. A negative link between temperature and telomere length has been reported previously in other taxa such as amphibians, reptiles or fish (Burraco et al. 2020; Debes et al. 2016; Olsson et al. 2018; Zhang, Han, Burraco, Hao, et al. 2023), which seems to reflect increased metabolic activity, oxidative stress, and energy expenditure (Friesen et al. 2021; Ritchie and Friesen 2022; Zhang, Han, Burraco, Wang, et al. 2023). Regarding A. mellifera , while it could be partially considered ‘endothermic’ at the colony level, foragers are exposed to ambient temperatures, which could influence their physiology (Stabentheiner et al. 2010, 2021). Foraging workers are therefore likely to experience direct thermal constraints such as thermoregulatory costs, water loss or oxidative challenge which are all linked to telomere erosion, during flight and resource collection. In addition to temperature, telomere length decreased with increasing floral abundance. Although high floral abundance is often assumed to reflect resource‐rich environments, this association may be more complex in heterogeneous landscapes. High flower abundance can attract more foragers, increase competition among pollinators, or be associated with greater flight activity and energetic expenditure, all of which can impose physiological costs on honeybee workers (Margotta et al. 2018; Wolf and Schmid‐Hempel 1989). Such behavioural and energetic differences may, in turn, contribute to variation in telomere length (Bateson and Nettle 2018). Notably, the effect size of floral abundance was comparable to that of temperature, suggesting that biotic resource‐related conditions may be as relevant as climatic conditions in explaining spatial variation in honeybee telomere length.

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, our study is based on variation in telomere length across honeybee populations, and does not quantify telomere shortening rates through time. Individual age or growth trajectories, in addition to colony history, could not be controlled in our broad‐scale field design. Moreover, although muscle tissue provides a standardised basis for comparing individuals across sites and contexts, telomere dynamics can be tissue‐specific (Burraco et al. 2023, 2025; Burraco and Boonekamp 2026; Demanelis et al. 2020); thus, future studies comparing multiple tissues would help determine whether the patterns observed here reflect overall organismal telomere dynamics. Furthermore, although we focused on adult foraging workers to reduce variation associated with caste and broad social role, some variation in worker age among sites cannot be excluded given the plasticity of temporal polyethism in honeybees (Woyciechowski and Moroń 2009). Future longitudinal and experimental studies combining telomere dynamics with direct measures of telomere‐related mechanisms (e.g., oxidative stress, telomerase activity) and individual and colony traits would help clarify the mechanisms underlying the patterns observed here.

Conclusions

Telomeres have been presented as sentinels for understanding the impacts of global change on natural populations, particularly in ectothermic organisms that exhibit limited thermoregulatory capacity and high sensitivity to environmental variation (Lemaître et al. 2022). In honeybees, our results show that telomere length varies more strongly with climatic variability and floral resource availability than with direct measures of urbanisation or environmental pollution. While distinguishing between individual‐level physiological costs and population‐level adaptation remains challenging, our findings highlight telomere length as a sensitive biomarker linking environmental variation to ageing dynamics in a key pollinator. Together, these results suggest the central role of climate and resource availability in shaping ageing‐related processes under global change.
