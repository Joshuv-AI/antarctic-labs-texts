Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy: From Short-Term Risk Prediction to Mechanistic Insights and Preventive Strategies
Authors: Jung Bin Kim
Published: 2025

Epilepsy remains burdened by the persistent risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), which is the leading epilepsy-related contributor to potential life-years lost among young adults. The original investigation featured in this issue, which analyzed 161,773 treatment events obtained from Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database, represents the most-extensive short-term (30-day) sudden-death risk model published to date, offering timely insights for both clinicians and researchers.1

The authors identified 3,454 cases (2.1%) of sudden death within 30 days of observation and developed a logistic regression model with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.752.1 The model includes the following key independent predictors: indicators of acute disease activity, such as recent admission to an intensive care unit (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]=1.71) or general ward (aOR=2.14); comorbidities such as chronic kidney disease (aOR=1.53), diabetes mellitus (aOR=1.30), and psychotic disorder (aOR=1.24); treatment-related variables such as intravenous antiseizure medication (ASM) use within the previous year (aOR=1.57) and poor medication adherence (medication possession ratio <0.5, aOR=1.15); and contextual factors including advanced age and seasonal variation, with an increased risk during colder months (aOR=1.15).1 The study findings highlight the critical importance of seizure control, medication adherence, and early recognition of systemic instability as modifiable factors for reducing the risk of SUDEP.

The current data provide evidence that SUDEP has a multifactorial pathophysiology. Although its precise mechanisms remain unclear, converging data implicate seizure-related disruptions in respiratory, cardiovascular, and autonomic homeostasis. Neuroimaging analyses have identified decreases in the posterior thalamic volume, while an increase in amygdalohippocampal gray-matter volume was observed among high-risk individuals.2 The prevailing hypothesis posits a cascade of physiological disturbances triggered by generalized tonic–clonic seizures, culminating in respiratory and cardiac arrest. There are numerous reports of SUDEP appearing to result from a failure of the brainstem to restore autonomic stability postictally.3 This cardiorespiratory decoupling manifests as postictal hypoventilation, ictal arrhythmias, and impaired arousal mechanisms.4 Continuous-monitoring studies have delineated the following stereotypical terminal sequence: initial sympathetic activation, followed in turn by central apnea, bradycardia, and asystole, with respiratory arrest typically preceding cardiac arrest.

Emerging evidence also highlights the role of a genetic predisposition in SUDEP susceptibility. Investigations into rare dysfunction of voltage-gated channels have expanded the genotypic spectrum beyond SCN1A to include mutations in SCN3A, SCN4A, KCNA1, KCNA2, KCNB1, KCNC1, and KCNMA1.5 A Korean pediatric cohort study identified pathogenic variants in 15 patients, with 87% exhibiting missense mutations and most presenting with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. These findings suggest the clinical relevance of genotype-driven ASM strategies and reinforce the role of individualized medicine in preventing SUDEP.

Furthermore, experimental studies have implicated focal neural circuits in the pathogenesis of SUDEP. Seizure propagation to the amygdala has been shown to induce central apnea and hypoxemia, likely mediated through respiratory pathways in the brainstem.4 The identification of a respiration-inhibition zone within the human amygdala further supports this mechanism. Collectively these data support the conceptualization of SUDEP as a systems-level failure involving seizure termination, autonomic recovery, and cardiorespiratory resilience.

Several recent contributions in the Journal of Clinical Neurology have helped to elucidate the multifaceted nature of SUDEP. A 2023 epidemiological study of epilepsy-related mortality in South Korea found declining overall rates but noted an increase among the elderly, possibly highlighting gaps in age-specific prevention.6 A 2024 investigation into rare dysfunctions of sodium and potassium channels expanded the therapeutic and diagnostic implications of genetic screening.5 Concurrently, a review on hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis dysfunction investigated the interplay between chronic stress, autonomic dysregulation, and seizure susceptibility, and revealed a potential mechanistic pathway linking neuroendocrine abnormalities with the risk of SUDEP.7 Additionally, a 2025 longitudinal study of vagus nerve stimulation in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy demonstrated sustained seizure reduction and improved quality of life, with autonomic stabilization having the potential to mitigate the SUDEP risk.8

The clinical implications of the Taiwanese cohort study are manifold. First, medication adherence emerged as a critical determinant of survival, with a 50% reduction in drug availability doubling the risk of short-term mortality. Structured adherence interventions should therefore be prioritized, including patient education and digital monitoring systems. Second, risk stratification at hospital discharge is essential, with recent inpatient treatment, intravenous ASM administration, and multisystem comorbidity prompting intensified follow-up and targeted patient education. Third, integrating wearable cardiorespiratory monitoring devices capable of detecting peri-ictal hypoxia or arrhythmias may improve the predictive abilities of logistic models and facilitate timely interventions. Fourth, the independent association between psychotic disorders and SUDEP risk necessitates integrated neuropsychiatric care that addresses both adherence challenges and suicide risk.

Looking ahead, several research avenues warrant exploration. Cross-national validation of the 30-day risk model is required to establish its generalizability across diverse populations. Bridging studies are needed to elucidate the causal pathways linking surrogate clinical markers such as intravenous ASM use with mechanistic biomarkers such as postictal hypoventilation and autonomic variability. The application of artificial intelligence tools to integrate electronic health records, physiological data from wearable devices, and genomic profiles holds promise for real-time SUDEP risk monitoring. Interventional trials targeting specific mechanisms, including serotonergic modulation or noninvasive brainstem stimulation, should incorporate mechanistic endpoints to determine the efficacy in autonomic restoration.

The Taiwanese cohort study serves as a compelling reminder that SUDEP prevention is both an urgent clinical need and a long-term scientific challenge. Combining rigorous risk stratification with mechanistic insight, as well as leveraging the diverse evidence base provided in recent Journal of Clinical Neurology publications spanning from genetic channelopathies to stress-related neuroendocrine disruption and neuromodulation outcomes, the epilepsy community can move closer to realizing a future where fatal seizures can be anticipated and averted. The convergence of big-data analytics, precision genetics, and systems neuroscience offers a transformative opportunity to reframe SUDEP from an unpredictable tragedy into a preventable event anchored in evidence-based practice and individualized care.
