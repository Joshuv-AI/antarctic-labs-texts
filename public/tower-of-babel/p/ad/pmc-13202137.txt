When success becomes a barrier: tackling inertia in necessary change in clinical practice in neonatal vascular access
Authors: Matheus van Rens, Kevin Hugill, Mohammad A A Bayoumi
Published: 2026

Neonatal care has advanced rapidly in scientific knowledge, technology and precision over recent decades. Yet daily bedside practice does not always reflect this trajectory. The reasons are complex and multifactorial, but include resistance to change by practitioners and their organisations. A tendency to stick with what is familiar is firmly embedded in organisational psychology, and neonatology is not immune to these behaviours (table 1). Many of the barriers impeding successful change stem from human and system factors.14 However, these explanations alone do not fully capture the broader ecological and cultural forces that allow inertia to persist within neonatal systems.5

Resistance to new practices is rarely about evidence alone. It is about psychology, risk perception, economics and cultural identity. From our experience in neonatal vascular access, a major barrier to change is the institutionalisation of yesterday’s standards—in which legacy protocols, regulatory conservatism and professional comfort with the familiar inhibit meaningful evolution of practice. Once embedded, such standards become self-reinforcing through audit metrics, accreditation processes and peer expectations.

Legacy success, modern stagnation

The development of neonatal infection-prevention bundles to reduce central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI) rates has been an undoubted success story. Dimopoulou and colleagues6 highlight rigorous adherence to bundles as the cornerstone of CLABSI prevention. This is not misguided; these interventions work. They remain essential and should not be discarded.

However, a consequence of this success is cultural anchoring. Once a practice becomes associated with improvement, it acquires near-sacred status, and a compliance mindset is established. While this preserves safety, it also makes change harder. Innovations struggle to enter routine practice even when supported by evidence (box 1). The result is an unspoken paradox: neonatal care has become very good at improving yesterday’s solutions but slow at adopting tomorrows. In this context, deviation from established practice may be perceived as introducing risk rather than addressing emerging or unrecognised harms.

When safety success creates inertia

Neonatal care has achieved major safety gains through standardisation, bundles and compliance-driven quality improvement. However, sustained success can unintentionally generate inertia. Practices linked to positive outcomes may acquire protected status (validation freeze), while deviation is perceived as risk (safety conservatism). Familiar protocols offer cognitive comfort, reinforcing established hierarchies and slowing the adoption of new evidence. When learning systems prioritise adherence over adaptation, innovation is delayed. Recognising these translational frictions is essential to enable progress without undermining patient safety.

Missed opportunities: when innovation is not adopted

In neonatology, the challenge is rarely a lack of innovation. Change struggles not because data are absent, but because systems are designed for stability rather than evolution. Several recent advances in neonatal vascular access illustrate the persistent disconnect between evidence generation and clinical adoption.

Cyanoacrylate adhesive securement

Despite observational evidence suggesting that cyanoacrylate glue improved catheter stability, reduced dislodgement and minimised infection, its adoption remains inconsistent.1 2

Innovation without infrastructure: terminology and specialisation gaps

Interpreting neonatal vascular access research and designing structured decision frameworks are further complicated by inconsistent terminology and definitions.9 Although international nomenclature proposals have been published, adoption remains inconsistent, limiting comparability across studies and clinical practice.

Similarly, despite being well established in other fields, vascular access specialisation remains underdeveloped in neonatology, with limited leadership, structure and investment. The absence of dedicated expertise and standardised language further contributes to variability in practice and hinders the effective translation of evidence into care.

It is important to recognise that not all innovations should be adopted into routine care. Decisions must be guided by a balanced assessment of available evidence, clinical relevance, cost, usability and potential unintended consequences. In some cases, careful evaluation may justifiably lead to non-adoption. However, the absence of structured evaluation frameworks risks conflating lack of evidence with lack of value, thereby reinforcing inertia rather than enabling informed decision-making.

Moving from inertia to implementation: a structured approach

Improving neonatal vascular access practice requires more than recognising inertia. It requires a structured approach to translating evidence into routine care. The discipline of implementation science provides a useful framework for understanding how innovations can be effectively adopted across different healthcare settings. Adoption of new practices is influenced by interacting factors across multiple levels of the healthcare system, including patient needs and expectations, clinician knowledge and beliefs, organisational priorities and workflow integration, as well as broader regulatory, economic and industry-related constraints. These factors do not operate in isolation but interact dynamically, shaping whether innovations are evaluated, accepted or rejected in practice. Addressing only one of these elements is rarely sufficient to achieve sustained change.10

Several practical strategies may help bridge the gap between innovation and routine practice.

First, implementation considerations should be embedded early in the research pathway. Studies evaluating new technologies or practices should include not only efficacy and safety outcomes but also feasibility, usability and cost implications within real-world neonatal settings.

Second, closer integration between quality improvement and research is needed. Rather than operating in parallel, these domains should function as a continuous pathway in which innovation is developed, evaluated and implemented within clinical care. This requires embedding implementation science methodologies across the full research pipeline—from early concept development and feasibility assessment, through efficacy evaluation, to real-world implementation and sustainability. In this model, high-quality and ethically conducted research becomes part of standard care rather than an external activity.

Third, neonatal units may benefit from dedicated vascular access expertise and leadership structures. Specialised teams or designated clinical leads can support consistent practice, reduce variability and facilitate the evaluation and adoption of new approaches.

Finally, transdisciplinary collaboration is essential. Progress in neonatal vascular access will depend on learning not only from other medical specialities but also from fields such as implementation science, human factors engineering and organisational psychology, which offer critical insights into how change can be effectively achieved and sustained in complex healthcare systems.

By embedding these principles into neonatal care systems, innovation can become an expected and evaluated component of practice rather than an exceptional or disruptive event.

The compliance mindset: a barrier to future safety

Quality improvement culture in neonatology has been grounded in standardisation, compliance and reduction of variability. These principles remain essential. However, over-reliance on compliance has unintended consequences. Innovation can feel like deviation rather than progression and learning systems may stagnate once outcomes plateau.

When success is defined primarily by the absence of harm, opportunities to detect latent risk or incremental improvement may be overlooked.

The NICU of the future requires a dual mandate: to protect safety through evidence-based standardisation while simultaneously enabling structured innovation and learning. One cannot be achieved at the expense of the other. Moving from inertia to advancement requires a framework in which innovation is expected rather than exceptional, evaluated rather than assumed risky, monitored rather than feared and supported at a system level rather than left to individual champions.

Achieving this requires investment in neonatal vascular access expertise across units and clinical networks. Such structures can reduce practice ambiguity, move beyond narrow bundle compliance and support continuous improvement with professional and parental involvement.

Neonatal care stands at an inflexion point. Yesterday’s practices laid the foundation for extraordinary gains in survival and outcomes. That foundation, however, must not become a ceiling. Progress depends on moving beyond the comfort of ‘what has always worked’ towards a culture in which curiosity is normalised and innovation is expected rather than exceptional.
