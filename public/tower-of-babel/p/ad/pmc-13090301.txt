Trends in Pediatric Orthopaedic Firearm Injuries: A 28-Year National Representative Database Review
Authors: Sarah Lu, Shane Ross, Christopher J. Williams, Patrick P. Nian, Samuel A. Beber, Isabella G. Marsh, Shevaun M. Doyle, Jessica H. Heyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFirearm-related morbidity and mortality remain a major public health issue in the United States, contributing substantially to preventable pediatric deaths and nonfatal orthopaedic injuries. The aim of this paper is to investigate trends and characteristics of orthopaedic firearm injuries for fatal and nonfatal cases.MethodsThe Firearm Injury Surveillance Study (FISS), a publicly available and nationally representative database composed of 102 emergency departments (EDs), was queried retrospectively for patients 0–18 years of age who sustained orthopaedic injuries involving firearm use between 1993 and 2021. The database yielded a national estimate of 15,426 total cases from 1993 to 2021. Demographics, injury characteristics, perpetrator, intent, and ED disposition were collected and analyzed. Simple linear regressions were utilized to assess trends in the number of orthopaedic injuries and fatalities over time.ResultsOrthopaedic injuries resulting from firearm use had an overall declining trend (β = −6.15) from 1993 to 2021, with notable spikes in 2012 and 2020. The average annual incidence was 29.7 injuries per 100,000 children. Most of these injuries were sustained by males (81%) and the 15- to 18-year-old age group (80%). Over half of these injuries were self-inflicted (59.5%). Additionally, 69% of all injuries were unintentional. Fractures and strains/sprains were the most common, comprising 72% and 17% of cases, respectively. Injuries were most frequently sustained to the finger (26%), hand (11%), and foot (9%). Over 30% of injuries resulted in a fatality, and nearly 10% of cases involved a crime. Fatal cases had a decreasing trend from 1993 to 2021 (β = −4.39), with a spike in 2012. Males (62%), Caucasians (60%), and adolescents (age: 14.0–18.9 years) (100%) accounted for the greatest proportions of fatalities. In total, 88.2% of fatalities were unintentional.ConclusionsPediatric orthopaedic injuries from firearm use have seen a decline from 1993 to 2021, with a disproportionate representation by adolescent males. Despite the decline, the frequency of unintentional and self-inflicted injury persists and highlights the need for greater awareness of firearm safety and education.Key Concepts(1)Analysis for a nationally representative database reveals that there was an overall decline in pediatric orthopaedic injuries sustained by firearms from 1993 to 2021, but there were spikes in prevalence in 2012 and 2020.(2)Males 15–18 years of age accounted for the greatest proportion of firearm injuries.(3)Most firearm-related injuries were fractures involving the upper extremity and mostly to the male and adolescent population.(4)Firearm injuries were frequently self-inflicted and unintentional, underscoring the need for greater firearm safety and education.Level of EvidenceIII

Introduction

Firearm injuries have remained a significant public health crisis in the United States (US), especially in the pediatric population. In 2019, firearm injury surpassed motor vehicle accidents as the leading cause of death in ages 0–19 years in the US [1,2]. Nonfatal injuries occur 2 to 4 times more often than fatal firearm injuries, resulting in significant morbidity [3]. Specific to the pediatric population, the burden of firearm trauma on the health care system is substantial in both volume and cost [4,5]. National data indicate that over 13,000 children were hospitalized for firearm-related injuries from 2010 to 2014 [6,7], and the cost of care for these cases is estimated to exceed $330 million annually [5]. Multiple policy and public health strategies, such as minimum age requirements for use and purchase of firearms, mandatory safety courses, child-access prevention laws, and licensing regulations, have been implemented at both the federal and state levels in response [8]. This issue has prompted medical organizations including the American Medical Association and American Association of Pediatrics to advocate for firearm prevention strategies [9,10].

Firearm-related injuries frequently cause orthopaedic injuries, with studies finding nearly 50% of patients have a concomitant fracture [[11], [12], [13]]. There exists a paucity of literature describing the national temporal trends and characteristics of firearm-related injuries in the pediatric population [11,14]. To address this gap, the primary aim in this study was to quantify national trends in fatal and nonfatal pediatric orthopaedic firearm injuries over a 28-year period. The secondary aims are to characterize the anatomical, orthopaedic, and situational factors associated with these injuries.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data were collected from the Firearm Injury Surveillance Study (FISS), a publicly available database, that is a subset of the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS). Data entries from this database were consolidated from the National Center for Injury Prevention and Control (NCIPC) and the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), federal organizations that collect data to monitor incidents from firearm use and their characteristics from 102 emergency departments (EDs) across the US. Each entry consists of an initial ED visit from individuals sustaining firearm injuries and captures patient demographics, injury characteristics, parties involved, and a brief case narrative. Death was recorded, even if death occurred after a patient’s discharge, admission, or transfer. The FISS has a nationally representative database of over 155 million annual ED visits across EDs from across the US [15,16]. From this population, the NEISS is able to calculate and determine a sample of 102 representative EDs throughout the US. Each ED is assigned a stratum depending on case volume, ED acuity level, and geographical density. This estimate is scaled with a ratio adjustment factor to stabilize FISS estimates over time. Thus, the FISS calculates a corresponding weight to estimate the annual number of injuries based on a representative case. For instance, a 5-year-old male with a fracture of the leg has a weight of 15, indicating that there are an estimated 15 individuals with the same injury profile.

Case selection

The FISS database was queried for injuries caused by gunpowder-based firearms (ie, rifles, handguns, and shotguns) experienced by patients aged <19 years of age from January 1, 1993, to December 31, 2021. Injuries were defined as those resulting from direct ballistic trauma caused by firearms, as well as injuries incurred during firearm operation (eg, sprains related to recoil). Orthopaedic injuries were defined as including amputations, avulsions, crushes, dislocations, fractures, and strains/sprains. Only cases in which the primary diagnosis was an orthopaedic injury were included as the narratives only contained information solely on the primary diagnosis. All case narratives were manually reviewed to confirm that only cases with a primary orthopaedic injury were included.

Variables of interest

This study collected the number of firearm-related orthopaedic injuries and fatalities, as well as the age, sex, and race of the patient. Injury type (fracture, strain/sprain, crush, amputation, avulsion, or dislocation), injury anatomical location (ie, the shoulder, knee, foot, etc.), and initial ED action were collected. The perpetrator, intention behind the injury, and the circumstances of the injury were recorded. Children were defined as ≤13.9 years of age, and adolescents were defined as 14.0–18.9 years of age. The perpetrator refers to the individual handling the firearm and is categorized as stranger, friend, family member, spouse, or self. The intention captures how the injury occurred and is classified as intentional, unintentional, self-harm, or from law enforcement. Further analysis examined the circumstances surrounding each injury, categorizing situations as involving a crime, drug use, a physical or verbal altercation, or resulting in a fatality. The initial ED action refers to how the injury was managed during the patient’s first visit to the ED. It does not contain information about follow-up. Initial ED actions are categorized as treated in the ED, admitted for hospitalization, transferred to another hospital, transferred and hospitalized, held for observation, left against medical advice, or dead upon arrival. Fatalities included patients who sustained a primary orthopaedic injury and ultimately passed away or at a follow-up medical visit apart from the initial ED visit.

Statistical analysis

Standard descriptive statistics were utilized to characterize study variables. Linear regressions were used for trend analysis for both the number of orthopaedic injuries and fatalities over the course of the study period. Annual incidence rates were calculated per 100,000 children for the overall cohort as well as for sex and age group. Counts and percentages were utilized to report age group, sex, race, injury type, anatomical location, perpetrator, ED disposition, and the additional analysis. The mean and standard deviation are reported for patient age. Further characterization and analysis were conducted for cases resulting in a fatality. Statistical analysis was performed with Python code in Google Collab (Mountain View CA, USA).

Results

Trends in orthopaedic firearm injuries

Between 1993 and 2021, 505 cases were recorded, representing an estimated 15,426 firearm-related orthopaedic injuries nationwide. In this period, there was an overall decline in the number of injuries, with a β of −6.15 injuries per year. The year 1999 had the lowest number of injuries, while there was a rise in cases in 2012 and 2020 (Fig. 1). The average annual incidence rate for pediatric firearm injuries was 29.7 children per 100,000 children (Table 1). Demographics and circumstances

The most represented age group was adolescents (80%), and the overall mean age at time of injury was 15.5 ± 3.0 years (Table 2). Males constituted the majority of cases (81%). Orthopaedic injuries were self-inflicted, defined as injuries where the patient was the perpetrator, in 60% of cases, while 29% were inflicted by a stranger. Injuries were most commonly unintentional (69%), followed by intentional injury (28%); only 1% were secondary to intentional self-harm (1%) (Table 2). Trends and characteristics of fatalities

Of the 15,426 estimated injuries of patients who sustained a firearm-related orthopaedic injury, 4,985 died (Table 4). A trend analysis of these fatalities shows a similar decline during the study period with a spike in 2012. The rise in overall cases in 2020 was not similarly mirrored by a rise in fatalities (Fig. 2). Males and children made up for 62% and 100% of fatalities, respectively. Of all fatalities, 88% were unintentional and 12% were an assault. Discussion

This nationally representative 28-year review of pediatric firearm-related orthopaedic injuries identified more than 15,000 cases, highlighting key demographic and temporal trends. Although orthopaedic injuries declined overall from 1993 to 2021, there were notable rises in 2012 and 2020. National data show climbing rates of all pediatric firearm injuries, including nonorthopaedic injuries, beginning around 2014 with a large increase in 2019 [17]. Previous studies indicate that the surge in firearm purchases during the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to increase in injury rates observed in 2020 [17]. Our findings indicate that while pediatric firearm injuries have risen nationally, this increase is not reflected specifically in primary orthopaedic injuries, which continue to show a gradual downward trend. Our study shows that the pattern of injuries is similar to other traumatic orthopaedic injuries, with upper extremity fractures being the most common [18]. Prior studies show approximately 28% of pediatric firearm-related orthopaedic injuries lead to lasting deficits, including growth arrest, chronic pain, or deformity [12]. Although only 6% of the cohort required amputation, these injuries still pose significant long-term physical and psychosocial challenges [19].

Most injuries and fatalities occurred in adolescent males, consistent with prior literature on pediatric firearm-related fractures [20,21]. Fowler et al. [3] propose that males are disproportionally affected by firearm injuries due to inexperience handling firearms, situational factors, or weapon carrying Although white patients represented the largest share of injuries and fatalities in our cohort, prior research suggests that Black patients, immigrants, and youth from lower socioeconomic backgrounds experience higher rates of severe trauma, which may account for this difference [10]. Pediatric patients are at risk for injuring the physes, placing them at risk for long-term complications. In this cohort, nearly 90% of fatal cases were unintentional and involved children under 14 years of age, whereas adolescents were disproportionately affected by nonfatal firearm injuries. These injuries highlight the critical need for safe storage and prevention efforts in homes with accessible firearms. The current literature highlights that assault and homicide are responsible for a majority of fatal and nonfatal pediatric firearm injuries when evaluating injuries across all organ systems [2]. Some studies state that suicide is the leading cause of fatal firearm injuries in patients aged 10 to 19 years [1,22,23]. In contrast, our analysis was restricted to cases in which the primary diagnosis was orthopaedic, likely accounting for the higher proportion of unintentional and self-inflicted injuries observed in our cohort. Our findings provide a complementary perspective by highlighting the musculoskeletal burden of firearm injury and the substantial role of preventable, nonassault mechanisms within orthopaedic practice.

Generally, our study finds that unintentional injuries, self-inflicted injuries, and firearm accidents that result in fatalities account for the greatest number of primary orthopaedic firearm injuries. This study reinforces the recommendations issued by national organizations such as the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), American College of Surgeons, and American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), which advocate for safe firearm storage, education, and research-driven prevention programs [24]. Policies including child-access prevention laws, education in schools, and mandatory safety training have been associated with reductions in pediatric firearm injuries and deaths [25,26]. Despite these measures, injury rates remain high, suggesting an ongoing need for broader adoption and enforcement of safety interventions. Few primary orthopaedic firearm injuries were associated with crime, drug use, verbal arguments, or physical altercations. There is a critical need to prioritize awareness of unintentional injuries in the pediatric population as current attention remains disproportionately focused on homicides and violence-related injuries. Tailored interventions, particularly those directed toward adolescent males and households with known firearm ownership, may yield the greatest benefit. Studies show that over 4.6 million US children live in homes with unsecured, loaded firearms and that adolescents frequently know where firearms are kept even when parents believe they do not [27]. Strengthening public health messaging around secure firearm storage, including locked devices, unloaded storage, and separate ammunition access is one of the most impactful strategies to mitigate unintentional pediatric injury.

This study has several limitations inherent to the NEISS-FISS dataset. Deaths on arrival were not recorded in 2020-2021. This potentially underestimates fatalities. Injury identification was based on the primary diagnosis being orthopaedic, which likely results in a significant underestimation of orthopaedic injuries secondary to firearms. Additionally, injuries coded as primarily injuring a different system with orthopaedic injury as a secondary injury were not included in this study. Secondary diagnoses were excluded due to inconsistent reporting. Because our inclusion criteria required the primary diagnosis to be orthopaedic, associated multisystem injuries contributing to mortality may not be fully captured within the dataset. As such, we cannot determine which fatalities were directly attributable to isolated musculoskeletal injury versus concomitant injuries. As multiple types of injuries occur during a firearm injury, the narrow scope of this study may cause inaccurate national estimates and trends. Misclassification of injury type, mechanism, or anatomical region is also possible. Additionally, the FISS lacks detailed clinical information such as specific mechanisms, fracture characteristics, treatments, or long-term outcomes. This limits assessment of injury severity and recovery.

Conclusion

Pediatric orthopaedic firearm injuries have shown a slight decline over the past three decades but continue to represent a significant source of preventable morbidity and mortality. These cases and fatalities primarily involved adolescent males, with most injuries resulting from unintentional or self-inflicted firearm handling. The high frequency of upper extremity fractures and notable fatality rate underscore the need for improved firearm safety education, secure storage practices, and broader prevention efforts. Clinicians, surgeons, and policymakers play critical roles in closing these gaps and implementing targeted strategies to reduce pediatric firearm harm.
