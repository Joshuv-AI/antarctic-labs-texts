Antifungal potential of biocontrol agents against Penicillium georgiense causing onion mold in Pakistan
Authors: Muhammad Waris, Manzoor Ali Abro, Muhammad Ahsan, Atta Ullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Onion is an important vegetable crop prone to many abiotic and biotic stresses. The blue mold rot of onion caused by Penicillium georgiense (P. georgiense) is one of the most damaging diseases in Pakistan and worldwide. The current study includes the factors that are responsible for losses at a considerable level where onion is being grown, transported, and stored. This study represents the first comprehensive integration of morphological and molecular identification of fungal pathogens isolated from onions (collected from different regions), showing the symptoms of blue mold rot. Standard tissue isolation and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) techniques were used for morphological and molecular characterization of the pathogen, respectively. Microscopic examination revealed the presence of short-chains of ellipsoidal to subspheroidal conidia, similar to the characteristic morphological features of P. georgiense. Further confirmation was done from sequencing of amplified Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) regions which showed 97–99% similarity to the P. georgiense species complex. Different biocontrol agents (BCAs) were evaluated against P. georgiense isolates under in vitro conditions, where Trichoderma sp., used as biocontrol agent, expressed maximum antagonistic effect. It significantly restricted the radial colony growth of the test fungus to only 37 mm, compared to control, where the colony reached upto 90 mm. In addition, other biocontrol agents such as Paecilomyces lilacinus (P.lilacinus) and Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis) also demonstrated significant inhibitory effects on the radial colony growth of the test fungus. These BCAs inhibited the colonial growth to 59 and 67.67 mm, respectively. Based on the findings of the current study, P. georgiense is the significant causal agent of blue mold onion rot at all the studied locations, and can be efficiently controlled by using BCAs, a safe, environmental friendly and sustainable approach in agriculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10123-026-00855-8.

Introduction

Onions (Allium cepa L.) are grown in a wide range climate from temperate to subtropical regionsdue to their wider adaptability. Onion (Allium cepa L.) is a staple crop in both rural and urban settings, with their growing regions encompassing both smallholder farms and large-scale agricultural operations. Pakistan produces millions of tons of onions annually, making it one of the world’s and Asia’s top growers of onions. In addition to meeting domestic needs in the country, onion production in supports substantial export volumes in Pakistan (Khan et al. 2020). As far as total production is concerned, Sindh is the highest onion-producing region, accounting for a sizeable onion yield producing province of the country (Khan et al. 2022). In case of agricultural commodities, post-harvest losses (between harvests to consumer delivery) result in significant reduction in quantity or quality of the produce. Harvesting, handling, transportation, storage, processing, and marketing are some of the supply chain steps when these losses take place (Hasan and Uddin 2011). Post-harvest diseases, which develop after the crop has been harvested, also account for significant post harvest losses. Approximately 35–40% of fruits and vegetables, including onions, are lost worldwide as a result of various post harvest diseases (Gupta and Verma 2002). Such kind of post-harvest losses are also a significant concern for onion crop. Post-harvest losses represent a major challenge in onion production systems worldwide. These losses occur during harvesting, transportation, handling, storage, and marketing processes, significantly reducing both quantity and quality of produce. Fungal pathogens are among the major contributors to post-harvest deterioration, resulting in heavy losses in fruits and vegetables globally (Hasan et al. 2010). Onion molds, including black mold and blue mold rots, are significant post-harvest diseases that have been linked to several field and storage diseases that impact onion yield and storage capacity. Mold growth, sprouting, and rotting can cause quick decay of onions and increase post-harvest losses. The transportation of onions from farms to markets further complicates their post-harvest management and increases the risk of spoiling and mechanical damage. Since onions are especially vulnerable to changes in temperature and humidity while in transit, the above-mentioned issues are made worse (Falola et al. 2023). A saprophytic fungus called Penicillium sp. mainly affects onions after harvest, causing blue mould and soft rotting. Usually, the fungus enters through wounds during harvest, handling, or storage. It creates mycotoxins like patulin, which can harm onions’ quality and endanger customers’ health. Humid and poorly ventilated storage conditions are especially harmful for the disease. In addition to preserving marketable onion quality, controlling this disease is crucial for lowering the possibility of mycotoxin contamination (Anum et al. 2024). The fungal pathogen Penicillium sp. isolates have varying morphological characters, including the texture and color of the colony, production of pigments of yellow color, and near to that exudating and non-exudating isolates (Overy et al. 2005).

As far as Biological control techniques with the use of antagonistic organisms or natural predators are are concerned there are important methods and these are environmentally friendly. Mold-causing pathogens are antagonistically affected by specific strains of Bacillus and Trichoderma spp. demonstration of these biocontrol agents successfully lowers Penicillium sp. infections by either outcompeting the pathogen for nutrients or by generating compounds that prevent the growth of fungi (Jalali et al. 2021). Utilizing natural organisms like bacteria, fungi, or viruses to reduce or manage the population of pathogenic organisms was known as biological control. Because biological control agents (BCAs) can either directly oppose the pathogen or strengthen the host plant’s defenses through mechanisms like competition, antibiosis, and induced resistance, using BCAs to manage Penicillium sp. was a potential strategy. With an emphasis on microbial antagonists and the reasons behind their effectiveness, this research focuses on biological control methods for controlling blue mould in onions. Keeping in view the importance of onion crop in Pakistan economy, and post harvest losses due to Penicillium sp., current study was planned to identify the causal pathogen of post harvest disease (onion rot), and to find suitable biological management strategy to combat the pathogenic mold.

Mold-affected and rotted onions were collected from the surveyed areas, keeping in view the representative symptoms of blue mold rot of onion or mold rot nearly related to that, all the processes were carried out with proper careful observation of symptoms (Schuck et al. 2014).

Isolation of pathogen from diseased samples and confirmation by Koch’s postulates

Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) was used for the isolation of fungal pathogen from the collected and examined infected onion samples, and pathogen characteristics were studied. Subsequent re-isolation was done to verify the target pathogen presence and its virulence, thus Koch’s postulates were also confirmed. Afterward from 6 ± 2 days old, pathogen culture plates with developing fungus were observed, and a 5 mm was transferred aseptically by placing it onto the center of different labeled Petri plates filled with different diagnostic agar media, including Potato-Dextrose Agar, Czapek Agar Dox, and Malt Extract Agar, and incubated at 26 ± 1 °C for 6–8 days. Fungal cultures were observed regularly mycelial colony growth and data of colony growth diameter was recorded.

Virulence test

The virulence test of all 3 purified fungal isolates were performed by inoculating the healthy onion bulbs of the respective locations. The test was performed to confirm the Koch’s postulates. Fungal isolates that had already shown disease symptoms on onion bulbs in the initial isolation were used to confirm the causal association of P. georgiense with onion bulbs having rottening symptoms with varying degree of infection. After surface sterilizing asymptomatic mature healthy peeled onion bulbs with (1%) sodium hypochlorite, 10 µl droplet single spore suspensions 106 conidia spores per ml of each isolate inoculum were pipetted for pathogenicity and virulence confirmation. A hemocytometer (Neubauer- Germany) was used to measure the spore concentration. Onion bulbs which were selected for controls/ untreated, sterile distilled water was maintained and used for them with three replications. The inoculated and control sets of onion bulbs were then placed inside the labelled sterilized polythene cups and were sealed with aluminum foil. The experiment was carried out three replications of onion bulbs, and incubated at 25 ± 2 °C in humid chambers with 80 ± 10% relative humidity for 10 ± 2 days and regularly observed after a week and particular mentioned time. Disease severity and lesion development were recorded after inoculation under controlled conditions as prescribed by Schuck et al. (2014). For grading of blue mold rot of onion for virulence test/study the disease rating scale was followed as prescribed by Wani and Nisa (2011).

Morphological identification

For morphological characterization, Pathogen cultures were observed, and microscopic examinations were performed with contrast microscope (Model TCM 400 LABOMED Labo, America Inc. USA) and Labmod 10x, 40x and 100x magnification and PixelPro application suite 3.0 (Labo, America Inc. USA) of camera atlas LABOMED for all 3 purified fungal pathogenic isolates and identification of respective isolates were done through descriptions as well manuals and criteria was as prescribed by (Simmons 2007; Kiltgaard et al. 2014).

Molecular identification

Molecular identification of the same isolates was performed as prescribed by Bandh et al. (2012), and for molecular characterization of representative Penicillium species isolates, sequencing was done by using inter transcribed spacer (ITS1–4) genome region of the rDNA for identification. DNA of the test fungus was extracted and labeled separately while using mycelia from 8 days old culture, allowed to air dry, and then utilized to isolate genomic DNA using the Cetyl (hexadecyl) Trimethyl Ammonium Bromide (CTAB) method as explained by Stewart and Via (1993) with minor modification. The extracted DNA was kept for further molecular studies at -20 °C. PCR to amplify the Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS1-4) region of fungi was carried out using primers, ITS1 forward sequence labeled as ITS1-F: (5′-TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCGG-3′), and ITS4 reverse sequence labeled as ITS4-R: (5′-TCCTCCCGCTTATTG ATATGC-3′), PCR amplification was carried out in 20 µL reaction volume consisting of template DNA (30 ng/ 2µL), 1.5 µL primer (each of ITS-1 F and ITS-4R), 10 µL of DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix (is a pre-mixed solution that includes all the required ingredients in a handy packaging, such as DreamTaq DNA polymerase, optimized DreamTaq Green buffer, MgCl2, and dNTPs, to make PCR setup easier), and 5 µL of Nuclease-free distilled water. PCR was performed using the Proflex PCR system. Using a thermal cycler, the amplification was performed in 35 cycles, starting with an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, first extension at 72 °C for 1 min, and final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. After PCR amplification, the resulting products are examined using agarose gel (1% w/w) electrophoresis to verify their size and existence. The ITS1-4 region was amplified using PCR, yielding a predicted amplicon size of approximately 550–600 base pairs. To verify the amplification of specific DNA sections, the PCR products were subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis and stained with ethidium bromide (10 µg/mL). The gels were then visualized under a UV transilluminator and documented using an Invitrogen iBright FL1000 imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with iBright 1.7.0 software. To further purify the amplified PCR results Illustra GFX PCR DNA and Gel Band Purification Kit was used. Sequence analysis was performed using BioEdit (version 7.7.1), and the obtained sequences were later adjusted and also aligned from the chromatogram (Hall 1999). Now the fungal species isolated final aligned ITS sequences taken and were further verified and compared using the particular suite for standard nucleotides on the National Center for Biotechnology Information’s (NCBI) GenBank database website using Basic Local Alignment Search Tools (BLAST) and the orders of All three isolates’ ITS-based primer sequences were entered into the NCBI GenBank database, and the corresponding accession numbers were obtained. The Tamura-Nei model was used for a maximum likelihood approach to infer evolutionary history (Tamura and Nei 1993). Heuristic search was done to construct the initial tree or trees after Neighbor-Join search, and a matrix of pairwise distances was estimated after maximum composite likelihood. (Kumar et al. 2018).

In vitro evaluation of known biocontrol agents against P. georgiense

In the current study, different selective Biocontrol agents (BCAs) viz., Trichoderma sp., Paecilomyces lilacinus, Bascillus subtilus were obtained from Plant Pathology labouratory of Sindh Agriculture University, Tandojam (SAU) and evaluated in vitro for their antagonistic potential against P. georgiense by dual culture method as shown in Table 2. In this technique a mycelial plug of 5 mm size from seven days old culture plates of virulent pathogenic fungus was aseptically placed onto the one side of 90 mm petri dishes having selective medium. Similarly, a 5 mm agar plug of the selective biocontrol agents was aseptically placed onto the other side of petri dishes (exactly opposite to test pathogen). In control plates, plugs of only test fungus were placed. All the plates were incubated at 26 ± 1 °C untill the growth of the test fungi in control plates reached plate margins. After seven days of incubation, colony (mycelial radial growth) diameter of the test fungus and biocontrol agents was recorded and percentage radial growth inhibition was calculated using the formula (Yelmame et al. 2010). Statistical analysis

Data from in vitro studies were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using a Completely Randomized Design (CRD) under a factorial arrangement in SPSS software version 23. Treatment means were compared using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. Results are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Graphs and error bars were generated using Microsoft Excel.

Discussion

Mold infestations in stored onion bulbs have been observed, compromising quality and yield in the El-Minia region of Egypt. Pathogenicity study has identified virulent isolates from these sampling areas, contributing to significant losses (AboelAinin et al. 2024) However the similar findings have been observed in the current study. Moreover in another study surveys of various locations was carried out similarily which showed that bulb rot symptoms were caused by a fungal pathogen in Spanish provinces also. Allium sativum bulbs were collected, and it was found that rot prevailed in more than 56%, with post-harvest rot caused by fungi. And after pathogenicity confirmation, various pathogens were identified, including Penicillium sp. (Galvez and Palmero 2021). However, in the current study it was observed that environmental conditions influenced the disease severity at different sites such as warm temperatures and high relative humidity prevailing in onion-producing districts such as Hyderabad and Ghotki favor fungal sporulation, colonization, and bulb deterioration during storage and transportation. Similarly another findings showed that the disease highly concerned with environmental conditions and greatly influence the prevalence and virulence of post-harvest fungal pathogens. In contrast, comparatively cooler climatic conditions may reduce pathogen aggressiveness and disease development (Tripathi and Dubey 2004).

Moreover a survey was conducted at different storage sites of the province (Chiang Mai) of Thailand, three different isolates were recovered from blue mold-affected garlic bulbs, and Penicillium sp. was identified morphologically and molecularly with the use of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) primer and sequences (Suwannarach et al. 2024) Study is aligned with the findings of current work. In another experiment Morphological and molecular characterization was considered. Growth medium was used, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests were performed to get an amplified (ITS) region product, which was sequenced and processed, registered in data base of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), and used for phylogenetic analysis as an isolated specie as well as the morphological studies proved similar to the molecular findings of isolates of Penicillium spp. (Houbraken et al. 2021) Similarity Penicillium georgiense was confirmed in the current finding and confimed morpho- molecularlarly. As morphological identification alone may not accurately differentiate closely related Penicillium species because of overlapping colony characteristics and microscopic features. Therefore, molecular characterization based on ITS region sequencing provides reliable species-level identification and phylogenetic confirmation (Visagie et al. 2025).

The post-harvest disease, commonly known as blue mold, poses a considerable threat to stored onions, facilitating deterioration and mycotoxin contamination (Snini et al. 2016). Prolonged storage aggravates economic losses due to blue mold, highlighting the need for effective management strategies. Surveys of various locations have emphasized the prevalence of this issue (Galvez and Palmero 2021). A study was carried out to find bulb rot symptoms caused by fungal pathogens in Spanish provinces. Allium sativum bulbs were collected, and results revealed that rot prevailed in more than 56%, with post-harvest rot caused by fungi. And after pathogenicity confirmation, various pathogens were identified, including Penicillium sp. However, the study having somehow similarity with the current findings. Chen et al. (2024) described that fungal pathogens in the storage are being affected with blue mold diseases, and the pathogen Penicillium sp. was predominant and confirmed as disease causing microorganism of blue mold. In another finding, Codonopsis pilosula, during the post-harvest condition, is affected by blue mold, caused by P. expansum (Lv et al. 2023).

Theoretically, sequence-based identifications should minimize misidentifications. However, discrepancies persist in practice, prompting a collaborative effort to standardize Penicillium taxonomy over the past decade (Houbraken et al. 2021; Visagie et al. 2014).

In the current study, various isolates of P. georgiense were recovered from all sampling districts, and the results revealed that the cause of blue mold rot of onion was confirmed after Koch’s postulate and mopho-molecular studies showing the similarities with the findings of various research as interpreted above. Furthermore, characterizing the causal agent of a disease is a crucial preliminary step in developing effective disease management strategies (Miller et al. 2009). Following identification, management approaches utilizing antagonistic biocontrol agents can be employed to target disease-causing agents (Zivkovic et al. 2010). Application of Trichoderma sp. exhibited effective findings in order to control the postharvest fungal pathogen (Adebesin et al. 2009). It was observed in another study that biocontrol agents like bacterial strains having antagonistic ability could not only alter the mycelial development of fungal pathogens but could also inhibit their growth with a range of more than 30% and may leads up to 56% (Kohl et al. 2019). Furthermore, Zhao et al. (2021) carried out research to examine the effectiveness of a yeast on the decay of blue mold after harvest. Findings showed that yeast was found to efficiently reduce the mycelial growth and blue mold decaying pathogen, with a fungal load of 107 cells per milliliter. As per these tests, it was observed that the biocontrol agent greatly disturbed the germination of P. expansum spores and the growth of their germ tubes by lowering them. Another similar experiment was performed on pears also, and it was proved that the same biocontrol agent reduces the blue mold lesion spread on the fruit and reduces the disease incidence (Zhang et al. 2019).

In this study, an in vitro experiment evaluated the efficacy of biocontrol agents against P. georgiense, revealing varying levels of growth inhibition. Trichoderma sp. exhibited the highest mycelial growth inhibition, followed by other biocontrol agents. Their antagonistic ability to reduce pathogen growth aligns with previous findings. The use of biological control agents (BCAs), primarily fungi, is increasingly seen as a sustainable method for reducing postharvest losses (Ramudingana et al. 2025). Moreover, As for as in vivo studies are concern Trichoderma species are widely recognized for their post-harvest biocontrol potential, also due to their rapid colonization ability, secretion of antifungal metabolites. Similarly, Bacillus subtilis also produces lipopeptides including surfactin, fengycin, and iturin that suppress fungal pathogens under storage conditions also (Sharma et al. 2009). Bacillus subtilis is an antagonistic bacterium affecting fungal pathogen growth; consortium use resulted in > 50% inhibition (Win et al. 2021). Bacillus species produce lipopeptides with antifungal properties, suppressing fungal pathogens (Farzand et al. 2020). The antagonistic activity observed during dual culture assays may involve several mechanisms including antibiosis, competition for nutrients and space, mycoparasitism, and secretion of hydrolytic enzymes. Trichoderma spp. are known to produce chitinases, glucanases, and secondary metabolites that degrade fungal cell walls. Bacillus subtilis suppresses fungal growth through production of antifungal lipopeptides, whereas Paecilomyces lilacinus exhibits antagonism through enzymatic degradation and competitive colonization (Guzman-Guzman et al. 2023). Bacillus strains and resistance inducers efficiently controlled Penicillium sp. (Maalik et al. 2023). B. subtilis and Trichoderma sp. showed efficient results against the blue mold pathogen (El-mougy and Abdel-Kader, 2019).

Conclusion

Biological control offers an eco-friendly and sustainable alternative to synthetic fungicides for managing post-harvest fungal diseases. It was concluded from the findings that P. georgiense was identified as the causative agent of blue mold rot in onions from surveyed locations in Pakistan. Molecular identification via PCR amplification and ITS region sequencing confirmed 97–99.2% similarity to P. georgiense. In vitro experiments revealed that Trichoderma sp. effectively inhibited radial colony growth (37 mm) compared to the control (90 mm). Paecilomyces lilacinus (59 mm) and Bacillus subtilis (67.67 mm) also significantly reduced the growth. These biocontrol agents exhibit promise for managing P. georgiense moreover, the use of antagonistic microorganisms minimizes environmental contamination, and supports sustainable agricultural production systems however, in vivo studies are recommended.
