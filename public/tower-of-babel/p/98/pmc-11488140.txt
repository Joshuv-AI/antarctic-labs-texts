A call for improving of musculoskeletal education on physical medicine and rehabilitation studies: a systematic review with meta-analysis
Authors: Ying Jin, Liqian Ma, Jie Zhou, Bing Xiong, Asanga Fernando, Huon Snelgrove
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence of musculoskeletal diseases is increasing in clinical practice, leading to a growing demand for rehabilitation medicine. However, education in musculoskeletal education is lacking in the training of physical medicine and rehabilitation students. This study aims to evaluate the impact of musculoskeletal education on medical students’ learning of rehabilitation medicine and analyze the correlation between influencing factors and education.ObjectivesThe primary objective of this study is to evaluate the impact of musculoskeletal education on medical students’ learning of rehabilitation medicine. Furthermore, the authors aim to analyze the correlation between various influencing factors and the effectiveness of this education, with the goal of informing future educational interventions and enhancing student outcomes.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across the Pubmed, Ovid, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) databases for relevant articles published from January 2012 to September 2024. The search focused on medical students as the research subjects and musculoskeletal education as the intervention method. Both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and non-randomized controlled trials (non-RCTs) were included. The selected studies underwent a systematic review, and meta-analysis was performed using R 3.4.4 software.ResultsAfter a thorough search, 41 studies were included. The retrospective study indicated that the most common impacts were knowledge acquisition, self-confidence, and satisfaction. The subsequent meta-analysis revealed significant increases in students’ knowledge acquisition [standardized mean differences (SMD) = 2.15, 95% confidence interval [CI] (1.36, 2.94), I2 = 97%, random effect model] and self-confidence [SMD = 5.81, 95% CI (2.88, 8.75), I2 = 97%, random effect model]. Compared with the general teaching control group, the knowledge acquisition of students in the observation group improved [SMD = 0.25, 95% CI (0.00, 0.51), I2 = 83%, in the random effect model]; However, no significant difference in satisfaction occurred [SMD = 0.27, 95% CI (-0.47, 1.01), P > 0.05, I2 = 89%, in the random effects model].ConclusionMusculoskeletal education primarily impacts knowledge acquisition self-confidence, and satisfaction among physical medicine and rehabilitation students. The meta-analysis further establishes that musculoskeletal education significantly enhances knowledge acquisition and confidence in these students.Trial registrationCRD42024563899 (completed on July 17, 2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06178-4.

Introduction

Musculoskeletal education is a branch of anatomy that encompasses the study of rheumatism and skeletal muscle health and disease [1]. Conditions such as gout, low back pain, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and osteoporosis are prevalent in clinical practice [2]. In the United States, musculoskeletal disorders affect 33.3% of the adult population, compared to the combined rates of cardiovascular diseases and chronic respiratory conditions [3]. The prevalence extends globally and is notably associated with concurrent chronic diseases including cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, chronic respiratory disease, and obesity [4]. Epidemiological studies reveal that musculoskeletal disorders afflict 20–33% of the global population, impacting more than 163 million individuals worldwide [5]. Subsequently, this poses a significant global burden regarding health, social, and economic costs.

Owing to population aging, the prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders is projected to increase. Research demonstrates the benefits of rehabilitation for patients with such disorders [6], including alleviating pain, addressing structural abnormalities, and mitigating dysfunction from acute or chronic injuries or disease. This, in turn, improves patients’ overall quality of life and facilitates their integration into family and society [7]. Therefore, musculoskeletal education is integral to rehabilitation science [8]. Notably, over half of patients with severe COVID-19 required rehabilitation after discharge [9]. The impact of the epidemic has increased the incidence rate of musculoskeletal related diseases, especially in the elderly [10]. Therefore, there is an increasing necessity for qualified doctors to manage musculoskeletal diseases persists. All graduated doctors should be able to describe and recognize the pathophysiological state of the musculoskeletal system; specifically, they should have a proficient level of musculoskeletal knowledge [3].

Because students have very limited exposure to anatomy during their clinical training, medical students are poorly prepared for anatomy at the time of their residency program [11, 12]. In the emergency department, for example, musculoskeletal and sports medicine are underrepresented in U.S. medical school curricula, with only 15% of schools requiring clinical musculoskeletal internships. This results in medical students entering residency programs with a lack of knowledge in this area [13]. UK medical schools are currently failing to adequately train medical students to manage orthopaedic patients, with students reporting low ability scores for all essential trauma and orthopedics skills [14]. Furthermore, it has been observed that a significant gap persists between the education provided in musculoskeletal anatomy and the practical application of this knowledge in rehabilitation settings. This discrepancy is concerning as it can hinder the ability of healthcare professionals to deliver effective and comprehensive treatment plans for patients with musculoskeletal injuries or conditions. Research conducted by Singh et al. [15] highlights this issue, emphasizing the need for a more integrated approach to musculoskeletal education and rehabilitation teaching in order to bridge this gap and improve patient outcomes. The impact of this on the rehabilitation profession is undoubtedly negative. Therefore, it is crucial to explore the influence of musculoskeletal education on learning rehabilitation medicine and evaluate the correlation between its influencing factors and teaching.

Methods

This research conducted a systematic review with meta-analysis to synthesize the available evidence on the effectiveness of musculoskeletal education in physical medicine and rehabilitation studies. The systematic review process involved a comprehensive search of electronic databases, screening of identified studies for eligibility, and data extraction from included studies. The meta-analysis component aimed to quantitatively pool the results of eligible studies to assess the overall effect of musculoskeletal education on relevant outcomes, such as level of knowledge acquisition, gain of confidence, satisfaction with knowledge. This approach was chosen to provide a comprehensive and rigorous evaluation of the current state of research in this area and to identify gaps in knowledge that can inform future research and practice. This study was executed and its findings were presented adhering to the standards outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement [16]. Furthermore, the study protocol was formally registered in PROSPERO under the unique identifier CRD42024563899.

Data sources

Pubmed, Ovid, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) were systematically searched, and the period considered was from January 2012 to September 2024. Only articles published in peer-review journals and written in the English language were included. No restrictions for ethnicity, gender, age, and setting were observed.

The authors excluded studies that:

(1) The intervention measures have nothing to do with relevant medical education. (2) Lack of clear outcome indicators. (3) Review studies. (4) Publications in languages other than English. Two authors systematically screened the literature according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed using an electronic data collection form created specifically for this review. Study characteristics were extracted by two independent authors. Kappa value was used to evaluate the consistency of data extraction between the two groups.In case of ambiguity and disagreement after research discussions, a third researcher arbitrated. When Kappa = 1, it indicates that the data presented by the two groups are completely consistent, and the data can be used for the final analysis. The content of literature extraction included author, year, place, grouping, teaching intervention models, and results. Thereafter, RCTs were retrieved by the JADAD and non-RCTs were retrieved by the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) to account for selected criteria, such as selection, comparability, and outcome, to evaluate the quality of the experimental design [17].

Primary and secondary outcomes

Despite the acknowledged importance of musculoskeletal education in medical training, the literature reveals that only a limited number of studies have delved into its influence on medical students’ learning outcomes. Specifically, these studies have explored the extent to which musculoskeletal education on medical students’ learning, such as the degree of knowledge mastery, skill acquisition, and learning attitude of students [1]. Given this scarcity of research, the present study aims to contribute to the field by focusing on a crucial aspect of medical students’ learning: the level of knowledge acquisition pertaining to musculoskeletal anatomy.

The primary outcome of this study is the level of knowledge acquisition among medical students in the domain of musculoskeletal anatomy. This outcome will be evaluated through assessing students’ comprehension and recognition of this anatomical area, encompassing both the depth of their knowledge mastery and their proficiency in applying this knowledge in practical situations. Typically, this assessment will involve examining students’ scores on relevant tests or evaluations, providing a quantitative measure of their learning outcomes in this critical area.

The secondary outcomes: in addition to assessing the primary outcome of knowledge acquisition, this study will also examine two secondary outcomes that are equally crucial in evaluating the effectiveness of musculoskeletal education among medical students. Firstly, gain of confidence, among participants, which measures the extent to which they experience an increase in self-assurance and perceived competence in managing musculoskeletal conditions or executing rehabilitative techniques following the intervention. This outcome captures the change in their ability to apply their knowledge and skills confidently in real-world scenarios related to musculoskeletal healthcare. Secondly, satisfaction with knowledge, focusing on their overall satisfaction with the educational content provided and its relevance to their musculoskeletal healthcare needs or professional development. This outcome aims to capture their perception of the value and usefulness of the information they have learned, providing insights into how well the educational intervention met their expectations and needs.

Methodological quality assessment

For non-RCTs, the NOS was utilized as a standardized tool to assess the quality of the studies. For RCTs, the JADAD scale, a well-established and validated instrument, was adopted for evaluating the quality of the RCTs. Moreover, to strengthen the rigor of the RCT evaluation, the assessment was extended beyond the JADAD scale by incorporating the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias (RoB) tool (Supplemental File Figs. 1 and 2). The creation of the RoB figure was facilitated using RevMan V5 (Cochrane).

Bias test of publication

A funnel plot was used to evaluate the bias for both RCTs and non-RCTs. When the funnel plot was asymmetric, the publication bias was assessed visually by funnel plots, and statistically by a regression asymmetry test (Egger’s test) (Supplemental File Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the I2 inconsistency test and chi-square-based Cochran’s Q statistic test in which P < 0.05 indicates the presence of significant heterogeneity. The fixed-effect model (Mantel-Haenszel method) was used to calculate the pooled SMD when substantial heterogeneity was not observed. In cases of potential heterogeneity, the random-effects model (DerSimonianLaird method) was adopted. As the indicators to be merged have different measurement ranges (such as the hundred-mark, ten-point, and five-point systems), the continuous variables were combined using the standardized method for de-dimensioning. The final result was reported with SMD with 95% CI. All P-values were two-sided and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Subgroup analysis was performed according to the study design (RCTs and non-RCTs). R 3.4.4 software was used to report outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis

One article was eliminated sequentially, and the remaining articles (n-1) were pooled for the meta-analysis. By observing the changes in the combined results, the original meta-analysis results were evaluated to determine whether the results were significantly changed by some studies.

Result

Study selection

In total, 2052 articles were identified through a computer search, manual screening, and preliminary search. After removing articles with poor quality and high repetition, 84 articles were left for secondary screening. Of them, 8 articles with unclear comparison group and 35 articles without final outcomes were excluded. Of the remaining 41 [3, 15, 18–56] articles were selected and included in the study. Study characteristics

All included studies were of high-quality with scores according to the NOS (ranging from 6 to 9 ) and JADAD (ranging from 2 to 4 ) criteria. Table 1 summarizes the study characteristics, designs, and findings of the 41 included articles. Among them, 10 were published in 2019; 7 in 2021; 5 in 2020; 4 in 2018, 2015, and 2014; 3 in 2017; 2 in 2016; and 1 in 2022 and 2013. Eight studies were from the United States; 4 each from the UK, Ireland, Germany, and Canada; 3 from Australia; 2 each from Greece, Spain, and Sweden; and the remainder from Barbados, Belgium, China, Colombia, France, India, Japan, and Malaysia. Of the 41 articles, a total of 46 studies used different interventions. 21 studies had control and observation groups, of the included studies, 29 employed a before-and-after control design, which compared outcomes before and after an intervention in both a control group and a treatment group. 5 studies involved musculoskeletal-related courses [18, 22, 30, 41, 47], and 5 employed online simulations of human musculoskeletal [27, 32, 34, 48, 54] and practical operation of prosections [26, 29, 35, 37, 46]. Other studies used strategies such as flipped classroom [39, 49, 52], mutual evaluation of students [20, 36, 55], and podcasts [29, 37]. Finally, musculoskeletal education predominantly affected physical medicine and rehabilitation students in three key areas: knowledge acquisition, self-confidence, and satisfaction.(funding sources see in Supplemental File Table 3).

A total of 28 articles reported differences before and after learning, and 11 had more than one observation group. Thus, 50 comparison groups were used to analyze the difference in knowledge between the observation and control groups. Gain of confidence

Assessed through self-reported changes in confidence levels, pre- and post-intervention, using Likert-scale questionnaires or similar tools. This could encompass feelings of preparedness, self-efficacy, and comfort in applying the learned knowledge or skills.

Because there were few studies with the control group in the confidence analysis, it was not suitable for meta-analysis. Therefore, only the results of students’ confidence before and after receiving teaching intervention were presented in this analysis. A total of 7 articles reported a difference in students’ confidence before and after receiving relevant learning, and 4 had multiple observation groups; thus, 16 comparison groups were used to analyze the difference in knowledge between the observation and control groups. The results showed that SMD was 5.81 (95% CI = 2.88, 8.75) [I2 = 97%, in the random effect model], which indicated that the confidence of medical students increased significantly after receiving relevant teaching (Fig. 4). The funnel plot indicated that some publication bias occurred, and the funnel point distribution was not very symmetric (Supplemental File Fig. 7). Furthermore, the Egger’s regression test demonstrated evidence of publication bias (P < 0.05). The results of the sensitivity analysis showed that when each study was eliminated one by one, the results did not change greatly. The results were relatively robust and were not greatly affected (Supplemental File Fig. 8).

Measured through validated questionnaires or surveys that assess the participant’s perceived satisfaction with the educational program’s content, structure, and delivery. It can include items related to relevance, comprehensiveness, and usefulness of the information provided.

Discussion

Overall evaluation and innovation points

This study systematically summarized 41 articles published over the past decade, focusing on the influence of musculoskeletal education on the learning of rehabilitation. By exploring the importance of anatomy in medical students’ learning [8], the research was narrowed to examining the effects of musculoskeletal studies specifically on the learning outcomes of medical students majoring in rehabilitation, which is relatively innovative. Through a systematic retrospective analysis of existing studies, influencing factors were identified,, and a meta-analysis was used to quantify the impact of these factors. The degree of knowledge acquisition and self-confidence played key roles in musculoskeletal education.

In the conducted analysis, students showed obvious differences in knowledge acquisition before and after comparison. knowledge acquisition also showed differences between RCTs and non-RCTs. Indicating that the assessment of teaching quality was more likely to be different before and after students received education. Similarly, after musculoskeletal teaching intervention, medical students’ self-confidence was also improved. Only with sufficient learning self-confidence can they lay a theoretical foundation for subsequent learning. However, there was no significant difference in student satisfaction, which may have been influenced by the insufficient sample size, perhaps indicating that medical students do not have high expectations of musculoskeletal teaching.

Regarding specific educational tools and methods, the study considered online musculoskeletal simulations, prosection practices, peer evaluations, and podcasts. Online simulations provide a flexible and accessible way for students to practice and learn about musculoskeletal structures and functions, offering a realistic and interactive experience. However, their quality and realism can vary, and technical issues may hinder their use. Combining online simulations with live demonstrations or hands-on practice can yield better results. Prosections, or cadaver dissections, offer a detailed view of musculoskeletal structures but can be costly and time-consuming to prepare, and may not be accessible to all students. When used in conjunction with lectures or online resources, they can provide a comprehensive learning experience. Peer evaluations can promote collaboration and provide feedback, but may not always be accurate or reliable. They are best used as a supplementary tool. Podcasts offer a convenient way for students to learn on their own time, but the quality and accuracy of information can vary. Using podcasts in conjunction with textbooks or online simulations can provide a more comprehensive learning experience. Overall, the effectiveness of these tools and methods may depend on how they are integrated into the overall educational strategy.

Clinical/practical significance

Studies have shown that Australian interns have insufficient knowledge of musculoskeletal medicine, and medical students have a tight teaching schedule. However, limited time is often allocated to undergraduate teaching in musculoskeletal diseases [57]. This may require a review of undergraduate medical education. Less than 2% of the time currently allocated to musculoskeletal disorders [58]. Another study showed that participation in a two-week clinical elective significantly increased musculoskeletal knowledge among fourth-year medical students, but this alone did not increase clinical confidence [59]. There was data to show that medical students were not confident in their musculoskeletal knowledge, with no difference between longer and shorter courses, but confidence was significantly higher among students who had completed orthopaedic clinical rotations or other orthopaedic experience [60]. It can be imagined that the lack of knowledge acquisition and self-confidence was detrimental to both doctors and patients, and most doctors are not satisfied with their musculoskeletal education, only 29% of doctors think that they have enough musculoskeletal education after completing the basic course. 80% of doctors believe they are not educated enough about musculoskeletal [61].

Recovery from in-person health care has been further complicated by the COVID-19 pandemic [62]. Rehabilitation after COVID-19 is becoming increasingly pronounced, among which telemedicine has become a hot topic [63]. However, in the case of remote rehabilitation diagnosis and treatment of patients, this increases the need for rehabilitation doctors. Thus, training qualified rehabilitation doctors is imminent, and rehabilitation teaching is particularly important. Musculoskeletal education is the first course for learning rehabilitation therapy [8]. In 1999, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education recognized a set of competencies required of every practicing physician. Professionalism, patient care, medical knowledge, interpersonal and communication skills, systems-based care, and practice-based learning and improvement are the six core competencies [64]. The improvement of musculoskeletal teaching has improved the six core competencies of medical students to some extent, providing a foundation for clinical practice. This study illustrates the positive impact of musculoskeletal education in the rehabilitation profession. For teachers, teaching tasks can be arranged from the following three aspects: improving knowledge refinement by, providing interesting, vivid, and practical knowledge; encouraging students to improve their confidence in learning professional knowledge; and teaching students to follow their aptitude to meet the knowledge requirements of different students. Musculoskeletal education is more difficult than other disciplines. Therefore, students must improve their self-confidence and dare to explore and acquire this kind of knowledge.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of the meta-analysis are multifaceted. Firstly, it constitutes the inaugural comprehensive synthesis to meticulously evaluate and compare the effectiveness of musculoskeletal education in enhancing medical students’ learning of rehabilitation medicine, while also delving into the correlation between various influencing factors and the educational outcomes. This comprehensive approach, to the knowledge, is unprecedented. Secondly, to ensure the robustness and reliability of the findings, the authors conducted rigorous sensitivity analyses, which provided further validation to the conclusions.

It is imperative to acknowledge several limitations inherent in the study. From the perspective of literature selection, studies beyond June 2023 to the present were not included. Further, the search was limited to only English articles. In the confidence and satisfaction analysis, the funnel plot demonstrated that a specific bias in the included literature was observed, indicating that the sample size of the included studies was not enough and heterogeneity may exist. During literature retrieval, with the search term “rehabilitation,” few relevant articles were found. Therefore, the selection of teaching intervention methods and research subjects can only be done through subjective exclusion, At the research level, some teaching interventions have brief durations, non-standardized content and quality, small sample sizes, and instances of errors.

Conclusions

This study conducted a systematic retrospective study on the selected literature and concluded that the influence of musculoskeletal education on physical medicine and rehabilitation students was primarily knowledge acquisition, self-confidence, and satisfaction. Additionally, a meta-analysis of these three aspects concluded that after the intervention of musculoskeletal education, physical medicine and rehabilitation students had significantly improved knowledge acquisition and self-confidence. The inclusion criterion for the literature required that the outcome indicators be the six core competencies. However, this requirement is often overlooked in actual teaching and practice. Future research should prioritize the improvement of these competencies in medical students and provide a theoretical basis for medical teaching.
