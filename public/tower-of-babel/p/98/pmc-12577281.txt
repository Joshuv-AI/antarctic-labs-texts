Effect of exercise interventions on depression, anxiety, and self-esteem in children and adolescents: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Sichen Zhou, Yuxiong Xu, Yizhuo Yang, Hanan Song, Xiaobing Wang, Yanli Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the effects of different exercise interventions on depression, anxiety, and self-esteem in children and adolescents, and to determine the optimal exercise treatment approach.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in the PubMed, The Cochrane Library, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science databases for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on the impact of exercise interventions on depression, anxiety, and self-esteem in children and adolescents. The search period spanned from the inception of each database to April 2025. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18 and R software.ResultsA total of 27 RCTs, encompassing 3059 children and adolescents, were included. Pairwise meta-analysis showed that exercise significantly improved depression (MD = -0.29, 95% CI [-0.44, -0.14]) and self-esteem (MD = 0.23, 95% CI [0.07, 0.38]), with a positive but non-significant effect on anxiety (MD = -0.12, 95% CI [-0.32, 0.09]). Network meta-analysis indicated that aerobic training significantly outperformed the control group in reducing depression, while resistance training significantly outperformed the control group in enhancing self-esteem. However, none of the exercise interventions showed significant effects on alleviating anxiety. Cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) rankings showed that resistance training was most effective in improving depression, combined aerobic and resistance exercise was most effective in alleviating anxiety, and resistance training was most effective in enhancing self-esteem. The quality of the evidence in this network meta-analysis was rated as “low” to “moderate”.ConclusionResistance training produced greater improvements in depressive symptoms and self-esteem than other exercise modalities; moreover, combined aerobic-and-resistance training may be beneficial for alleviating anxiety symptoms in children and adolescents.Trial registrationThe review protocol was preregistered in the PROSPERO database (Registration No. CRD42025645711).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25044-6.Keywords: Exercise type, Children and adolescents, Depression, Anxiety, Self-esteem, Network Meta-Analysis

Introduction

As societal pressures, academic burdens, and family dynamics continue to intensify, psychological distress among children and adolescents is becoming increasingly prevalent [1].Depression [2] and anxiety [3] are among the most common mental health disorders affecting this population globally, and they have emerged as significant public health concerns. According to the United Nations Children’s Fund, approximately 20% of children and adolescents are affected by these mental disorders, with the prevalence continuing to rise [4].Depression is primarily characterized by persistent low mood, loss of interest, and self-neglect, while anxiety is associated with excessive worry, tension, and fear [5].Additionally, low self-esteem may lead individuals to interpret stressors in a highly negative light, thereby triggering depression and anxiety [6]. This has been identified as a potential significant predictor for both depression and anxiety. The Lancet has highlighted that depression and anxiety not only affect individuals’ physical and mental health but also significantly impair their social functioning, learning abilities, and quality of life [7, 8].

Existing studies have demonstrated that exercise significantly alleviates depression and anxiety symptoms in children and adolescents [9–12].Given its favorable safety profile, ease of implementation, and minimal adverse effects, exercise represents an attractive non-pharmacological treatment and adjunctive option. Clinical practice guidelines from the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia recommend incorporating physical activity into the management of depression, often combining resistance training with vigorous aerobic training at a frequency of at least two to three sessions per week. Nevertheless, there is no unified consensus on standardized recommendations for exercise dose or modality [13–16].Accordingly, the mental health benefits of exercise may vary by modality in children and adolescents, and the optimal intervention for specific psychological problems requires further investigation and validation. Prior evidence indicates that aerobic training can reduce depressive symptoms in middle-school students [17], alleviate anxiety [18], and enhance athletic performance [19]; resistance training improves depressive symptoms in adolescents with obesity and strengthens self-concept [7]; and mind-body exercises (e.g., Tai Chi, yoga) help relieve psychological difficulties in children with obesity [20]and increase self-confidence [21]. Moreover, participation in exercise entails social interaction; through team-based activities, adolescents can mitigate psychosocial stress during puberty, experience immediate feelings of achievement, cultivate self-efficacy and self-identity, and garner greater social support and emotional gratification, thereby improving their capacity to cope with negative emotions [22–24]and promoting the adoption of healthier lifestyles [25].

Despite growing evidence that exercise can improve depressive symptoms, anxiety, and self-related outcomes in children and adolescents, the modality with the greatest benefit remains uncertain. Accordingly, this study employs a network meta-analysis to synthesize the effects of exercise interventions on depression and anxiety in youth and, innovatively, to include self-esteem as a target outcome. We compare the differential impacts of aerobic training, resistance training, combined aerobic and resistance training, and mind-body exercise on depression, anxiety, and self-esteem in children and adolescents. Our aim is to generate rigorous and clinically actionable evidence to inform exercise prescriptions for this population.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. This research adheres fully to all recommended reporting items listed in the PRISMA checklist (additional file 1). The review protocol was preregistered in the PROSPERO database (Registration No. CRD42025645711).

Search strategy

We searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and WOS to collect relevant RCTs related to the research objectives. The search period extended from the inception of each database to December 2024. A combination of subject headings and free text terms was used for literature retrieval, with the language restricted to English. The search terms included: anxiety, depression, exercise/training/physical activity/aerobic exercise/resistance training/Tai Chi/yoga, among others. The search strategy used in PubMed was as follows: ((“Exercise“[Mesh] OR “Physical Activity“[Title/Abstract] OR “Aerobic Exercise“[Title/Abstract] OR “Resistance Training“[Title/Abstract] OR “Tai Chi“[Title/Abstract] OR “Yoga“[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Children“[Mesh] OR “Adolescents“[Mesh] OR “Teenagers“[Title/Abstract] OR “Youth“[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Depression“[Mesh] OR “Anxiety“[Mesh] OR “Self Concept“[Mesh] OR “Self Esteem“[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Randomized Controlled Trial“[Publication Type] OR “RCT“[Title/Abstract])) (Detailed search strategies for each database can be found in addition file 2).

Study selection and data extraction

Two researchers (Zhou and Xu) independently conducted literature searches and data extraction on April 1 - May 13 2025. In the case of any discrepancies, a third researcher (Yu) intervened to discuss and reach a consensus. All retrieved articles were imported into Endnote for deduplication. After removing duplicates, studies were selected based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The two researchers (Zhou and Yang) cross-checked the extracted data by importing it into an Excel spreadsheet on May 24–30, 2025. The data extracted included the following: author(s), publication year, country of study, study design type, basic characteristics of the participants (such as gender, age, diagnostic criteria, presence or absence of comorbidities), intervention details (including sample size, test scales used, type of exercise, intervention duration, exercise intensity, and frequency), outcome measures, and data types.

Quality assessment

This study strictly adhered to the Cochrane Risk of Bias assessment tool manual. The risk of bias in the included studies was evaluated across six domains: selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other sources of bias [26].

Statistical analysis

This study employed Stata18 to conduct a pairwise meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, and the I² statistic was used to quantify the degree of heterogeneity (with values of 75%, 50%, and 25% representing high, moderate, and low heterogeneity between studies, respectively [27]). Based on the results of the heterogeneity test, either a fixed-effects model or a random-effects model was chosen for the analysis. The outcome measures in this study were continuous variables, and since the assessment scales used varied across studies, the standardized mean difference (SMD) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were used as effect sizes. An SMD of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 correspond to small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively [28].

A network meta-analysis was conducted using R software. First, a network evidence plot was generated to visualize the relationships between direct and indirect comparisons of different exercise interventions. When a closed loop of interventions was formed, the node-splitting method was used to assess inconsistency, and the corresponding p-value was reported [29]. If p >0.05, and the 95% CI included 0, the consistency within the loop was considered acceptable; otherwise, inconsistency was deemed to be present. The intervention ranking was determined by comparing the surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) curve. A higher SUCRA value indicates a higher likelihood of a better ranking [30], suggesting superior efficacy of that specific exercise modality.

In this study, the quality of evidence was evaluated following the CINeMA principles [31]. This approach comprehensively considers six factors: within-study bias, between-study bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and inconsistency. These factors were all taken into account when evaluating the results of the network meta-analysis. Quality grading was applied to individual outcome measures. For each factor, limitations were classified as “none,” “moderate,” or “serious,” corresponding to a downgrade of 0, 1, or 2 levels, respectively. The final evidence quality was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low. After the evaluation, all content was cross-checked and verified by two researchers. In cases of disagreement, consultation with a third-party expert was conducted to resolve the discrepancies.

Results

Search results

A total of 4,514 articles were identified through database searches: PubMed (n = 277), Scopus (n = 1,458), Embase (n = 1,295), Cochrane Library (n = 822), and WOS (n = 662). In addition, 43 articles were obtained from relevant systematic reviews related to the study topic. After removing duplicates, 1,197 articles remained. Following initial screening, 411 articles were identified for further review. After full-text examination and excluding articles due to issues with study design (non-randomized controlled trials), interventions/controls, research objectives, or outcome measures, 194 articles were retained. Finally, after excluding studies with unavailable data or those where the control group did not include non-exercise interventions, 27 articles were included for the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies

This meta-analysis included 27 studies published between 1987 and 2022, involving a total of 3,059 participants [9, 11, 18, 32–55]. These studies were conducted across 11 countries: 10 studies in the United States, 3 in the United Kingdom, 3 in Australia, 3 in Iran, and 1 study each in China, Germany, Belgium, Mexico, Spain, Brazil, and Canada. The types of exercise interventions included AT, RT, CT, and MBE. Of the 27 studies, 17 focused on AT, 4 on RT, 5 on CT, and 4 on MBE. All 27 studies included a control group that did not receive any exercise intervention, and 2 studies involved multiple intervention groups (AT, CT/AT, RT, CT). The average intervention duration was 13.85 ± 2.17 weeks, with 41% of the studies having an intervention period of 12 weeks or more. The average frequency of intervention was 2.75 ± 0.22 times per week, with each session lasting an average of 57.28 ± 7.3 min. Detailed characteristics are provided in Table 1.

Randomization and bias risk assessment

All included studies mentioned random group assignment, with 9 studies explicitly detailing the randomization methods used [9, 34, 45, 48, 50, 51, 53–55], the majority of which employed computer-generated random numbers for group allocation. Six studies clearly stated that a single-blind design was implemented [11, 33, 43, 45, 53, 55]. The risk of bias assessment for the included RCTs revealed that most studies exhibited a low risk of bias, while a few studies presented unclear or high risks in terms of allocation concealment (bias risk assessment for each study and a summary of bias risk are shown in Fig. 2).

Pairwise meta-analysis results

Depression

A total of 21 studies [9, 11, 18, 32–43, 45, 48, 50, 51, 53, 55] were included in the analysis of depression. Compared to the control group, exercise significantly improved depressive symptoms in children and adolescents (MD = −0.29, p < 0.001, 95% CI [−0.44, −0.14], I²= 61.9%). The depression scores in the exercise group were 0.29 lower than those in the control group, with the difference being statistically significant. (Detailed results are shown in additional file 3)

Anxiety

A total of 14 studies [11, 18, 32, 33, 37, 39–43, 46, 51, 52, 55] were included in the analysis of anxiety. Compared with controls, exercise did not significantly reduce anxiety symptoms in children and adolescents (MD = −0.12, p < 0.001, 95% CI [−0.32, 0.09], I²= 73.5%). The anxiety scores in the exercise group were 0.12 lower than those in the control group. (Detailed results are shown in additional file 3)

Self-esteem

A total of 13 studies [11, 34–36, 40, 42, 44, 46, 47, 49, 50, 54] were included in the analysis of self-esteem. Compared to the control group, exercise significantly improved self-esteem in children and adolescents (MD = 0.23, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.07, 0.38], I² = 54.5%). The self-esteem scores in the exercise group were 0.23 higher than those in the control group, with the difference being statistically significant. (Detailed results are shown in additional file 3).

Network meta-analysis results

Network relationship

This study included 27 studies with a total of 3,059 participants and performed a network meta-analysis [9, 11, 18, 32–55]. For depression-related outcomes, 21 studies were included (19 on AT, 2 on RT, 2 on CT, and 2 on MBE), with a total meta-angroup, 812 in the control group). For self-esteem, 13 studies were included (6 on AT, 3 on RT, 2 on CT, and 4 on MBE), with a total of 1,626 participants (991 in the exercise group, 635 in the control group).We constructed a network evidence plot for each outcome (Fig. 3). Each node represents a different type of exercise intervention, with the size of the node indicating the sample size of that intervention. The lines between nodes represent direct comparisons between two interventions, and the width of the lines indicates the number of studies that conducted the comparison. If no direct comparison was made between two interventions, a network meta-analysis was performed to evaluate their relative effects.

Heterogeneity and consistency analysis

We assessed the heterogeneity and consistency of all interventions in the network meta-analysis. The consistency test results showed that for depression-related studies, direct and indirect data between designs were consistent (p > 0.05), with no significant inconsistency observed. Therefore, the consistency model was used for analysis. The overall heterogeneity was high (I²=58.2% [31.5%; 74.5%]), indicating some heterogeneity between studies (tau²=0.0742).

For anxiety-related studies, direct and indirect data between designs were consistent (p > 0.05), with no significant inconsistency found. The consistency model was again employed. The overall heterogeneity was large (I²=77.4% [61.7%; 86.7%]), suggesting substantial differences between studies (tau²=0.1837).

For self-esteem-related studies, direct and indirect data between designs were consistent (p > 0.05), with no significant inconsistency observed. Thus, the consistency model continued to be used. The overall heterogeneity was moderate (I²=57.6% [21.5%; 77.1%]), indicating some heterogeneity between studies (tau² = 0.0586).

Outcome analysis

Depression

A total of 21 studies assessed the impact of different types of physical exercise on improving depressive symptoms in children and adolescents [9, 11, 18, 32–43, 45, 48, 50, 51, 53, 55]. The results indicated that AT significantly outperformed the control group, while the effects of RT, CT, and MBE showed no significant differences when compared to the control group. According to the probability ranking based on SUCRA, RT (77.2%) was the most effective, followed by AT (76.5%), MBE (52.8%), CT (30.4%), and control group (13.2%) in improving depressive symptoms in children and adolescents (Table 2).

Publication bias

We assessed publication bias using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test within the network meta-analysis. Visual inspection suggested slight asymmetry; however, Egger’s tests provided no statistically significant evidence of small-study effects for any outcome (depression: p = 0.0553,p>0.05; anxiety: p = 0.0586,p>0.05; self-esteem: p = 0.2361,p>0.05). Although minor asymmetry was noted for depression and anxiety, the potential impact of bias appears limited, supporting the overall robustness of the pooled estimates.(Detailed results are shown in additional file 4).

Quality of evidence evaluation

According to the CINeMA, the evidence quality was ultimately classified into two levels: low and moderate. For improving depression, comparisons of CT vs. N, CT vs. RT, and N vs. RT were rated as moderate quality data. Comparisons of CT vs. AT, RT vs. AT, and N vs. AT were influenced by study bias, imprecision, and heterogeneity, leading to a low-quality rating. For alleviating anxiety, CT vs. RT and N vs. RT were rated as moderate quality data, while CT vs. AT, RT vs. AT, N vs. AT, and CT vs. N were affected by study bias, imprecision, and heterogeneity, resulting in a low-quality rating. For enhancing self-esteem, comparisons of N vs. AT, CT vs. RT, and N vs. RT were rated as moderate quality data. However, CT vs. AT, RT vs. AT, and CT vs. N were downgraded to low quality due to study bias, imprecision, and heterogeneity. (Detailed results can be found in additional file 5,additional file 6)

Discussion

A total of 27 studies were included in the meta-analysis, comprising 3,059 participants. We ranked the effects of each intervention on various psychological outcomes. The results indicate that physical exercise interventions significantly improved depressive symptoms in children and adolescents. RT was found to be the most effective, followed by AT, MBE, and CT. For anxiety symptoms, CT may be effective, followed by RT, AT, and MBE. Regarding self-esteem improvement, RT demonstrated the best results, followed by AT, MBE, and CT.

Exercise has been demonstrated to exert a positive effect in ameliorating mental health issues such as depression, anxiety, and self-esteem among children and adolescents [19, 56, 57]. Among youth, aerobic training, resistance training, combined aerobic-resistance training, and mind-body exercise are the modalities most frequently implemented as intervention. Aerobic training is a principal component of school physical education and has been shown to induce neurogenesis, thereby promoting learning, memory, and emotion regulation in children and adolescents and mitigating depressive symptoms [58]. Resistance training implemented as part of multicomponent exercise regimens, school physical education curricula, and after-school fitness programs [59, 60]has garnered increasing attention. Through muscle-brain crosstalk involving the release of myokines and related factors, RT modulates immune function [61], attenuates stress dysregulation associated with adverse psychological states [62], and reduces cardiovascular disease risk [63]. Combined aerobic and resistance exercise encompasses diverse, engaging formats that achieve high participation among children and adolescents. Such programs are associated with improved social functioning, reductions in body weight among youth with obesity, enhanced metabolic health [64], and attenuation of inflammatory status [65]. Mind-body exercise comprises traditional practices that integrate physical activity with focused attention to promote holistic health through coordinated movements, controlled breathing, and enhanced mental concentration. By modulating the release of neurotransmitters (e.g., endorphins and dopamine), MBE can elevate positive affect [20, 66]and, in parallel, induce favorable changes in the functional organization of the cerebral cortex, thereby contributing to improved mental health. Despite the significant impact of diverse exercise interventions on the mental health of children and adolescents, their modality-specific benefits warrant further investigation to strengthen the empirical basis for clinical implementation.

The SUCRA results of this study indicate that RT demonstrates a significantly greater advantage in improving depressive symptoms, consistent with previous findings [67, 68]. The mechanism of action of RT may be attributed to short-term, high-intensity stimulation that enhances the expression of neurotrophic factors [69], reduces inflammatory responses [70], and strengthens synaptic connections between neurons [71], ultimately improving depressive symptoms. Furthermore, RT can effectively enhance muscle performance by increasing muscle strength, bone density, and the recruitment of motor neurons, thereby improving the overall coordination between muscles and nerves [72].From a psychological perspective, RT helps children experience a tangible sense of strength gains [73], which enhances their body image affirmation and self-esteem, thereby improving depression [, 75]. Despite numerous studies investigating the optimal exercise modality for alleviating depression, there remains some controversy. For example, studies by Li [76] and Wang [77] suggest that AT has a more significant effect on depression improvement, while Yu et al. [78] found that CT yields the best results. In contrast, high-intensity interval training was deemed the most effective for treating depression in university students [79]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in the study populations and the heterogeneity of the included literature.In this study, we selected children and adolescents aged 7 to 18 years as participants. In contrast to university student populations, children and adolescents exhibit significant physiological developmental differences. Adolescents at this stage are more sensitive to hormones, possess greater neuroplasticity, and exhibit faster improvements in motor skill learning and neuromuscular coordination. Additionally, adolescents’ cognitive abilities are still developing and are in a critical period for habit formation. During this period, adolescents show more positive adherence to RT and greater psychological perception compared to university students, which leads to higher exercise completion rates and enhances the effects of RT.

For alleviating anxiety, the cumulative ranking probability indicates that CT may be advantageous, yielding greater effects than AT or RT alone. Prior studies indicate that, compared with AT alone, CT yields greater improvements in functional exercise capacity and respiratory outcomes among adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis [80]. CT has also been shown to effectively control pediatric obesity and to reduce cardiovascular risk [81]and body mass index [82]. However, evidence regarding its effects on anxiety symptoms in children and adolescents remains limited. The underlying mechanism may be attributed to the following: First, exercise-induced myokine interleukin-6 upregulates anti-inflammatory mediators interleukin-1 receptor antagonist and interleukin-10 production while concurrently suppressing TNF-α. Functioning as an acute anti-inflammatory signal, this IL-6 mediated pathway has been documented after both aerobic and resistance exercise; when combined in a CT regimen, the effects may be additive, thereby enhancing the overall efficacy of the intervention [83]. This anti-inflammatory cascade can, in turn, attenuate anxiety-related peripheral inflammation and metabolic stress and is accompanied by a broader myokine response [84]. Second, exercise can improve neural function through different signaling pathways. Aerobic and resistance modalities activate neuroplasticity through distinct signaling pathways [85], exert synergistic neurotrophic effects and potentiate immunoregulatory responses [86], while concurrently improving cardiorespiratory function and augmenting muscular strength [87]. Third, exercise can enhance the body’s tolerance to anxiety. AT and RT target partially distinct domains in the treatment of anxiety: AT preferentially mitigates general psychological distress and state anxiety, whereas RT more robustly improves disorder-specific symptoms, reduces anxiety sensitivity, and enhances distress tolerance as well as tolerance of uncertainty (i.e., lowers intolerance of uncertainty) [88]. Consequently, CT may integrate these complementary effects to yield broader and more effective anxiolytic benefits. Fourth, From the perspective of children and adolescents, the diversified mix of activities in CT offers a more game-like and engaging experience, effectively increasing exercise frequency and willingness to participate, enhancing social interaction, and fostering intrinsic motivation for autonomous participation in physical training. In the pairwise meta-analysis, we did not observe a statistically significant reduction in anxiety symptoms among children and adolescents (MD=−0.12, p<0.001, 95%CI[−0.32,0.09], I2 = 73.5%). Several factors may have attenuated observable effects. First, most trials were modest in size, limiting statistical power and increasing the risk of type II error. Second, anxiety outcomes were assessed using different instruments with varying sensitivity to change, which may dilute pooled effects. Third, incomplete reporting of allocation concealment and blinding in some studies may introduce bias, and heterogeneity in intervention delivery (modality, intensity, supervision) further complicates inference. Future trials should predefine moderator analyses (e.g., sex, age band, baseline severity, and standardized dose parameters), adopt harmonized outcome measures, and ensure adequate sample sizes to more precisely delineate whether particular modalities or doses yield clinically meaningful anxiolytic effects.

This study found that exercise significantly improves the self-esteem of children and adolescents, which is consistent with previous research [89]. Putative mechanisms may include the following: exercise elevates circulating BDNF in adolescents, thereby enhancing plasticity in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex [90], modulating the function of neural circuits involved in affective processing and strengthening positive self-appraisal. Exercise may also upregulate the endocannabinoid(eCB) system [91], which can diminish negative affect and promote feelings of pleasure. In addition, when children and adolescents experience symptoms of depression and anxiety, not only do they reduce social interactions with peers, but they also experience a decline in self-esteem and self-efficacy, thereby increasing the risks of suicide, criminal behavior, and other adverse outcomes [92]. Children with low self-esteem typically exhibit strong social avoidance behaviors and low willingness to engage in physical activity. They tend to avoid peer interactions or participation in group activities, which affects their social competence and the development of close relationships. Prolonged states of low self-esteem can have profound impacts on children’s academic performance, mental health, and overall quality of life, potentially laying the foundation for more severe psychological issues, such as depression, in the future.According to the SUCRA results, RT has the most significant effect in enhancing self-esteem. In line with prior research, depression has been shown to have a neurobiological link to self-esteem [93], and after depression treatment, positive self-cognition is also improved [94]. Therefore, our findings are highly credible, suggesting that RT has a dual effect in treating depression and enhancing self-cognition.From a psychological perspective, compared to other types of exercise, RT in children and adolescents is more structured, typically accompanied by higher levels of supervision and control. This structured intervention effectively reduces children’s and adolescents’ focus on negative emotions, while simultaneously enhancing their sense of social belonging, further improving their self-esteem and sports performance [21]. Based on our findings, in addition to strengthening the education on mental health awareness and increasing the participation in physical activities, the mediating role of social support should be fully leveraged [25]. Appropriate resistance training should be incorporated into school physical education curricula and personalised exercise prescriptions.

Common forms of MBE include yoga, Tai Chi, Qigong, and Baduanjin. These practices emphasize the coordinated interplay of physical postures, breath regulation, and focused attention to enhance physical fitness, psychological well-being, and emotional balance. In the present analysis, however, mind-body exercise operationalized as yoga and Tai Chi did not yield statistically significant improvements in mental health outcomes among children and adolescents. Synthesizing prior meta-analytic evidence, the mental-health benefits of MBE remain uncertain [95–98]. Several factors may account for this uncertainty. First, MBE is generally of low-to-moderate intensity and acts primarily through autonomic and respiratory regulation, whereas reward-system sensitivity closely linked to adolescent anxiety and depression and molecular pathways such as BDNF and eCB system are strongly intensity dependent. Moderate-to-vigorous aerobic training most consistently elicits eCB-mediated hedonic/anxiolytic responses [99], and higher-intensity or interval protocol more likely to upregulate BDNF [69]. Consequently, the effects of MBE may be comparatively limited. Second, pediatric health guidelines recommend ≥ 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous activity daily and vigorous-intensity activity on ≥ 3 days per week to enhance muscular strength [74, 75], thresholds that many school-based yoga or Tai Chi classes fail to meet in either intensity or volume. Third, the included studies reported higher attrition in MBE than in other modalities, potentially reflecting insufficient dynamic stimulation, poor alignment with participants’ interests or needs, and limited short-term positive reinforcement, all of which may reduce adherence and engagement [100]. In addition, several trials had small sample sizes and were conducted in general school populations rather than clinical cohorts, making significant improvements more difficult to detect [101]. Cultural variability in the acceptability of traditional MBE may further hinder dissemination and uptake, and MBE’s benefits may accrue over longer time frames, such that short-duration interventions underestimate its effects [102]. Therefore, future research should endeavour to expand sample sizes, focus on longer-term intervention cycles, and optimise exercise guidance programmes to enhance the scientific rigour, practicality, and adherence of mind-body exercise. This will enable a more comprehensive assessment of its potential benefits for the mental health of children and adolescents.

Limitation

However, this study has several limitations. First, restricting inclusion to English-language publications may have introduced selection bias and contributed to publication bias. Second, few studies adequately reported allocation concealment and blinding, increasing the risk of selection/performance bias and weakening internal validity. Third, the absence of individual participant data constrained analyses to study-level aggregates, precluding consistent adjustment for key confounders (e.g., intervention duration, intensity, modality, sex, age, overall dose) and likely contributing to the observed heterogeneity. Fourth, intervention cycles, intensities, and session durations lacked standardized dosing and control, and notable imbalances in sample size across studies may have biased pooled estimates and lowered the certainty of evidence; greater methodological harmonization is warranted. Fifth, control conditions varied in content and intensity, potentially introducing bias and reducing comparability. Sixth, the inclusion of older trials that predate contemporary diagnostic criteria and modern exercise prescriptions may limit comparability with current practice. Seventh, this review was limited to unimodal exercise interventions and did not evaluate multimodal programs, which may constrain ecological validity and real-world applicability.

Conclusion

Exercise appears effective for reducing depressive symptoms and enhancing self-esteem in children and adolescents, and may also confer benefits for anxiety. Effects vary by modality: resistance training shows an advantage for improving depression and self-esteem, whereas combined aerobic-resistance programs may help alleviate anxiety. However, given the small number of included studies, their overall moderate methodological quality, and indications of publication bias and between-study heterogeneity, these conclusions should be interpreted cautiously and require further verification. Future research should stratify and/or meta-regress by sex, age, and exercise dose (e.g., frequency, intensity, duration, and modality) to strengthen the evidence base and inform more effective strategies to prevent or reduce depression, anxiety, and low self-esteem in youth.
