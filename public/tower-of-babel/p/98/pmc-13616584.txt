Atypical Dilated and Nonhypertrophic Cardiac Transthyretin Amyloidosis: Concept of Overlapping Cardiomyopathies
Authors: Louis Simard, Christian-Yves Couture, Joëlle Morin, Mathieu Bernier, Kim O'Connor, Erwan Salaun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is typically described as a restrictive cardiomyopathy characterized by deposition of insoluble amyloid proteins in the myocardium, resulting in heart failure (HF) with preserved left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (EF). The usual echocardiographic pattern combines diffusely increased myocardial wall thickness (LV wall thickness ≥12 mm, right ventricular [RV] wall thickness ≥5 mm), and associated other cardiac structures thickening, normal LVEF, typical global longitudinal strain (GLS) reduction with relative apical sparing pattern, and pericardial effusion.1,2 We present a case of atypical nonhypertrophic dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) with possible ATTR-CM deteriorating preexisting stable HF.

Clinical Presentation

A 76-year-old man was referred to our HF clinic for investigation and management of worsening HF and deteriorated LVEF. The patient was treated for the past 10 years in a regional hospital for HF with mildly reduced EF (HFmrEF) related to suspected toxic DCM (Figure 1). After the diagnosis of HFmrEF, the patient appropriately ceased alcohol consumption and had since been completely abstinent. Noncardiac medical history included hypertension, dyslipidemia, benign prostatic hyperplasia, gastroesophageal reflux, and rotator cuff surgeries. The patient was clinically stable as New York Heart Association functional class I or II for many years on optimal medical therapy. One year before presentation, a dual-chamber pacemaker was implanted for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF) with slow ventricular rate. Six months later, the LVEF deteriorated from 40% to 20% with persistent AF and remained severely depressed after external cardioversion. Device interrogation showed a high RV pacing rate of 85% without AF (atrial pacing rate of 80%). Upon referral to our institution, initial clinical assessment showed a sedentary patient with a New York Heart Association III functional class. Physical examination showed blood pressure of 90/50 mm Hg with a heart rate at 75 bpm, elevated central venous pressure of 10 cm of water, pedal edema, and bibasilar lung crackles. High-sensitivity troponin I and N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide were 26 ng/L and 7 733 ng/L, respectively.

Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed dilated LV pattern without increased wall thickness (measured at 8 mm) but biventricular systolic dysfunction and small pericardial effusion (Figure 2, Videos 1 and 2). Both atria were severely dilated (left and right atria indexed volume of 71 mL/m2 and 70 mL/m2, respectively). Mitral annular velocities were severely reduced (septal e’ velocity of 1.9 cm/s and lateral e’ velocity of 4.9 cm/s) with elevated LV filling pressures (mean E/e’ ratio of 21) and mitral flow deceleration time of 145 ms. The GLS was also severely reduced to –4% without relative apical sparing (Figure 2). Cardiovascular magnetic resonance showed maximal myocardial wall thickness of 10 mm and diffuse subendocardial late gadolinium enhancement uptake with extension into the midmyocardium involving both ventricular and atrial walls suggestive of an infiltrative process (Figure 3). Serum and urine analysis did not suggest light chain amyloidosis. Both 99mTechnetium-labeled pyrophosphate (99mTc-PYP) scintigraphy and single-photon emission computed tomography showed moderate myocardial tracer uptake corresponding to a grade 2 on the Perugini scale (Figure 3), but an equivocal heart-to-contralateral lung ratio of 1,35. Given the discordant assessment by multimodal imaging regarding possible involvement by ATTR deposit, invasive coronary angiogram was performed and an LV endomyocardial biopsy was obtained. These showed no significant obstructive coronary artery disease, but there was mild to moderate myocardial amyloid deposits as demonstrated by staining with Congo red and sulfated Alcian blue (Figure 3). Liquid chromatography and mass spectrometry confirmed the presence of ATTR-related deposits without amino acid sequence abnormality in the transthyretin protein, consistent with age-related or wild-type ATTR amyloidosis. Therapies for HFrEF were adapted consequently and tafamidis was initiated. Considering the high rate of ventricular pacing, LVEF deterioration, and LV contraction dyssynchrony, cardiac resynchronization therapy was performed. This did not result in any improvement of the LV systolic function, which reduces the likelihood for pacemaker-induced cardiomyopathy. Discussion

We present an uncommon case of combined cardiomyopathies with ATTR amyloidosis complicating preexisting DCM without increased wall thickness. The ATTR-CM involvement was suspected after comprehensive multimodal imaging assessment in a patient with worsening HFrEF and finally diagnosed upon histological examination of an endomyocardial biopsy. The patient rapidly deteriorated despite therapeutic adjustments, cardiac resynchronization therapy, and specific treatment of amyloidosis. Typically, ATTR-CM is suspected in the presence of HF with preserved LVEF, LV and RV concentric wall remodeling or hypertrophy, typical relative apical sparing pattern of the GLS, and diffuse midmyocardial late gadolinium enhancement on multimodal imaging assessment. However, ATTR deposits may also be involved in atypical cases such as asymmetric hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or ischemic cardiomyopathy or may complicate other preexisting or concomitant cardiac conditions. In the context of overlapping cardiomyopathies, the interpretation of imaging becomes even more challenging by mixing the patterns of 2 different cardiac diseases (Table 1).3 Indeed, the apical sparing amyloidosis pattern of GLS may be altered by the preexisting cardiomyopathy or by ischemic infarct sequelae. Furthermore, valvular disease such as aortic stenosis may lead clinicians to falsely consider LV hypertrophy to be solely a consequence of the heart valve disease. Trachtenberg et al.5 showed that systematic genetic testing in patients with DCM reported very rare (0.5%) cases of ATTR variants, all of them without clinical features of hereditary ATTR-CM. To our knowledge, only a few cases of age-related ATTR-CM with presence of LV dilated pattern have been reported in the literature.6 However, this phenotype could be more common than currently recognized. Rushakoff et al.7 showed that 4/121 heart explants with nonrestrictive dilated phenotypes presented diffuse myocardial amyloid infiltration. It remains uncertain whether the presence of cardiac amyloidosis is a participating cause of the HF or a simple bystander comorbidity. However, the rapid deterioration with infiltrative involvement in this case suggests that amyloid deposition likely played an aggravating role.

99mTc-PYP scintigraphy has become the noninvasive reference standard test for the diagnosis of ATTR-CM but must be interpreted cautiously in overlapping cardiomyopathies. In our case, a Perugini grade 2 supported the suspicion, but the heart-to-contralateral lung ratio remained below the diagnostic cutoff of 1,5. These equivocal findings underline the need for histological evidence in complex cases of suspected overlapping cardiomyopathies. Furthermore, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy has been reported as a possible cause of false-positive 99mTc-PYP8, making the distinction between these 2 entities challenging without histologic examination and leading to potential inappropriate management. The association of early-stage ATTR-CM with low-grade and/or equivocal 99mTc-PYP results is also discussed, but the unclear binding mechanisms of the radiotracer and the change shown with specific ATTR-CM therapies require caution in interpreting the nuclear results.9 However, it is plausible that lesser amounts of amyloid fibrils may have a significant clinical impact when another cardiomyopathy is present, therefore possibly reducing the sensitivity of the 99mTc-PYP validated diagnostic criteria. At the present time, nuclear imaging methods lack the precision required for amyloid burden assessment, risk stratification, and monitoring of disease progression as well as response to therapy.9 Regarding ischemic cardiomyopathy, acute or subacute myocardial infarction may cause focal tracer uptake, while the amyloid deposition and myocardial uptake may occur only in the noninfarcted segments in the context of fibrosis from a previous remote myocardial infarction.10,11

Beyond diagnostic challenges, management of ATTR-CM associated with DCM remains uncertain. Permanent pacemaker implantation may be indicated due to conduction disorders, and resynchronization therapy may be superior to RV pacing.12 Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator for primary or secondary prevention has not shown clear survival benefits, despite frequent ventricular arrhythmias and sudden death in patients with cardiac amyloidosis.12 However, no consensus exists regarding management of overlapping cardiomyopathy, emphasizing the need for expert center evaluation.

Specific ATTR amyloidosis therapies have proven efficient to slow amyloidosis progression; however, the randomized trials studying tafamidis and vutrisiran in cardiac amyloidosis excluded patients without increased wall thickness phenotypes.13,14 Benefits in combined cardiomyopathy with normal wall thickness therefore remain unclear. Historically, the average survival after cardiac ATTR amyloidosis diagnosis was 3 years, but the prognosis has improved to 5 or 10 years depending of the time of diagnosis in the course of the disease and the introduction of disease-modifying agents.15 In overlapping cardiomyopathies, prognosis depends on the stage of each disease, which makes the assessment even more challenging. In our case, the amyloid involvement likely precipitated rapid decline in a patient with previously stable DCM, despite signs of nonsevere amyloid infiltration. Early detection of amyloid involvement in the follow-up of patients with another specific cardiomyopathy or atypical cardiomyopathy is key to prognosis (Figure 4). Regarding HFrEF therapy in ATTR-CM, only limited and retrospective data have been published and support the use of sacubitril/valsartan and sodium-glucose cotransporter inhibitors if tolerated, with discontinuation of beta-adrenergic blocking agent therapy if not tolerated.

Conclusion

This case of combined cardiomyopathies with ATTR-CM complicating preexisting cardiac injuries related to DCM highlights the need for thorough investigations to properly address patients with atypical clinical and/or imaging presentations. Novel disease-modifying therapies have proven to be effective, but timely introduction is key. Consequently, high-level suspicion is required, and absence of increased myocardial wall thickness should not preclude the clinician from suspecting early-stage ATTR-CM with possible significant impact even with a lesser amount of amyloid infiltration. Histological assessment should be performed in case of atypical HF presentation or deterioration to ensure early diagnosis and appropriate management when an infiltrative process remains suspected.
