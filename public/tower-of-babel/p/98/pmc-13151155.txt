The interplay of risk factors for future falls and fall-related injuries among community-dwelling older adults: a Bayesian network analysis
Authors: Yan Cai, Wei Zhu, Chenshu Wu, Yan Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFalls are a leading global cause of injury and mortality in older adults, necessitating understanding risk factor interplay for prevention. This study aims to investigate complex interactions among risk factors associated with future falls and fall-related injuries in older adults.MethodsA prospective cohort study used China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2015 and 2018) data from 8,316 community-dwelling older adults. Falls and fall-related injuries were self-reported. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was employed for variable selection. A Bayesian network analysis utilizing the Max-Min Hill Climbing algorithm and Bayesian estimation method was subsequently implemented to identify interactions among risk factors related to falls and fall-related injuries.ResultsFall incidence was 23.29%, and the incidence of severe injuries after falls was 10%. According to variables identified by LASSO regression, two Bayesian networks were constructed for falls (21 nodes and 48 directed edges) and fall-related injuries (20 nodes and 48 directed edges). Results revealed complex associations among the risk factors for falls and fall-related injuries, highlighting direct factors such as fall history, hip fracture history and balance, as well as the indirect effects of factors such as age, vision and cognitive function. Some specific combinations of direct risk factors contributing to a high probability of falls and fall-related injuries were also identified.ConclusionFalls and related injuries are frequent in community-dwelling older adults, associated with by multiple interconnected factors. Bayesian network analysis helps uncover the relationships between these variables and quantify the associations of major factor. Clinical practice may benefit from prioritizing multifactorial exercise programs targeting grip strength, lower limb strength, and balance. Consideration should be given to fall risk assessments and tailored interventions for older adults with prior falls, hip fracture history, or those using walking aids to effectively reduce falls and related injuries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27192-9.

Background

Falls represent a significant global public health concern, particularly among older adults, ranking high among the causes of injury, disability, and death [1]. A previous meta-analysis pointed out that the prevalence of falls in older people of the world was 26.5% [2], with 10% sustaining fall-related injuries such as fractures, head trauma, or soft tissue damage [3]. Recent data from the Global Burden of Disease database in 2021 revealed that there were 215.6 million incident cases, 43.8 million disability-adjusted life years, and 0.8 million deaths attributed to falls [4]. These statistics underscore an urgent need for intervention, particularly as aging populations expand worldwide. Evidence confirms that most falls and their consequences are predictable and preventable through targeted strategies [5, 6]. Consequently, investigating the risk factors for falls and fall-related injuries to guide the accurate risk assessments and the development of potential prevention strategies is highly important.

The risk factors for falls and fall-related injuries are multifactorial and usually complex [7]. Some shared significant risk factors include fall history [8, 9], advanced age [10, 11], low muscle strength [9, 12], reduced balance [13, 14] and cognitive degeneration [15, 16]. Several studies have also reported the distinction between risk factors for falls and those for fall-related injuries [17–19]. Furthermore, these risk factors do not function separately but combine in complex manners to impact the occurrence of falls and fall-related injuries. For example, diminished cognitive function and reduced muscle strength can contribute to balance issues and gait disturbances, thereby elevating the risk of falling [16, 20, 21]. Impaired vision significantly affects postural control, thereby causing falls [22]. Despite the acknowledgment of these interactions, conventional statistical methods often fail to capture complex network relationships. Previous studies have not sufficiently examined the interdependencies among these risk factors, nor have they considered the adverse effects of the complex relationships among risk factors on falls and fall-related injuries. It is important to comprehend these connections to devise comprehensive strategies to minimize the rates of falls and fall-related injuries.

This study introduces the Bayesian network as a potent analytical framework to decode these intricate relationships. In contrast to traditional regression models that presume linear independence, Bayesian network analysis supports non-linear interactions and feedback loops [23]. As a probabilistic graphical model, it can illustrate conditional dependencies among variables [24], allowing for the identification of both direct and indirect pathways to falls and fall-related injuries, as well as highlighting high-probability intervention measures [25]. This capability bridges important gaps in traditional methods that often simplify the complexity of multifactorial causality. Therefore, to address these knowledge gaps and methodological limitations, we utilized data from a large national community cohort and performed a Bayesian network analysis to elucidate the complex interactions of risk factors related to future falls and fall-related injuries among older adults. The results from this research can provide a scientific groundwork for designing comprehensive and targeted prevention strategies of falls and fall-related injuries.

Methods

Design and participants

The present study was a prospective cohort study utilizing data sourced from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [26]. The CHARLS is a broad, multidisciplinary survey that effectively represents individuals aged 45 years and over from 28 provinces across the nation, employing the probabilities proportional to size sampling technique. All CHARLS waves received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board at Peking University. Data from CHARLS 2015 and 2018 were used for secondary data analyses in this study. Since the most recent anthropometric data available were from 2015, we also utilized 2018 data to gather the latest insights into future fall outcomes. Questionnaires and measurements were the main methods for collecting relevant data. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (see Supplementary Table 1 for STROBE Checklist).

This study included individuals who: (1) were aged 60 years and older at the time of the 2015 survey, and (2) had fall-related outcomes data from the 2018 survey. The specific process of selecting samples is shown in Fig. 1. The study finally included 8316 older adults.

Measures

Falls and fall-related injuries

Our study examined two main outcomes, falls and fall-related injuries, on the basis of two self-reported questions in 2018: “Have you ever fallen down since your last visit?” and “How many times have you fallen down seriously enough to need medical treatment?”. In this study, falls were identified as a binary variable (yes or no), while fall-related injuries were described using three categories (no fall, fall without severe injury, and fall with severe injury). In cases where falls resulted in injuries needing medical treatment, they were labeled as severe injury.

Covariates

With reference to the literature review [27, 28] and the data available in the current database, 22 variables were identified as baseline covariates in 2015. These variables were grouped as follows: (1) socio-demographics factors, including age, gender, and living alone; (2) nutritional status factor indicated by body mass index (BMI); (3) fall history; (4) physical abilities factors, encompassing basic activities in daily living (BADL) disability, instrumental activities in daily living (IADL) disability, grip strength, lower limb strength, walking speed, balance, and use of walking aids; (5) cognition factor (cognitive impairment); (6) psychological factor (depressive symptom); (7) health conditions factors, including hypertension, diabetes, stroke, arthritis, hip fracture history, and vision; and (8) lifestyle factors (smoking, and drinking). All variables in the study were coded as categorical according to the statistical requirements. The operationalized definitions for certain variables were as follows.

Age was categorized into two groups: 60–74 years, often referred to as young-old adults [29], and 75 years and older. According to the Chinese guidelines for BMI classification [30], the normal range of BMI is defined as 18.5 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 24.0 kg/m2. A BMI of less than 18.5 kg/m² is indicative of underweight status, while a BMI ranging from 24.0 kg/m² to less than 28.0 kg/m² signifies overweight status. A BMI of 28.0 kg/m² or higher is classified as obese. The BADL (dressing, bathing, eating, getting into or out of bed, using the toilet, and continence control) and IADL tasks (cooking, cleaning, shopping, managing assets, and taking medications) were utilized to assess the disability status of participants [31]. If participants reported challenges or inability in completing one or more tasks, they were deemed to have a BADL or IADL disability. The grip strength of the dominant hand of the participants was measured using a dynamometer [32]. Low grip strength was characterized by measurements under < 28.0 kg for men and < 18.0 kg for women [33]. Lower limb strength was evaluated via the chair stand test [34], with a completion time of 12 s or more indicating low lower limb strength [33]. A walking speed below 1 m/s was deemed poor [33]. The participants were deemed to have good balance if they could successfully complete both the semi-tandem and full-tandem standing position tests without assistance [35, 36]. The use of walking aids was defined as the utilization of one or more assistive devices (walking stick, travel device, manual wheelchair, and electric wheelchair) by the participant. Two primary dimensions of cognitive functions, executive function and episodic memory, were evaluated in CHARLS. Orientation, visuoconstruction and mathematical performance were the three dimensions used to assess executive function, utilizing the Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status (TICS-10) and figure redrawing tasks. The measurement of episodic memory involved tasks requiring immediate and delayed word recall. Extensive documentation in prior studies [37, 38] outlines the methodology for evaluating cognitive functions in the CHARLS questionnaire, where scores range from 0 to 21. In this study, individuals scoring about 1.0 standard deviation below the average in the general population were classified as having cognitive impairment [39]. The presence of depressive symptom was determined using the 10-item version of Center for Epidemiological Study of Depression (CESD-10) scale, with a score of at least 10 serving as the cutoff [40]. The assessment of vision was based on the questions, “How good is your eyesight for seeing things at a distance/up close?”. The available responses were excellent, very good, good, fair, and poor. Participants who marked “poor” on any question were identified as having poor vision, and those who opted for “fair” on one question but avoided “poor” on both were classified as having fair vision, with all other selections indicating good vision. The variable assignments were detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative information was expressed as numbers (n) and percentages (%), and group comparisons were performed using the chi-square and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Levels of significance below 0.05 were regarded as significant. Following prior studies [41, 42], imputation for missing data was applied to covariates with missing values under 30%. To maintain the natural temporal order and prevent future information from influencing past data, we excluded the two outcome indicators (falls and fall-related injuries, collected in 2018) from the imputation process. Missing values in the covariates (collected in 2015) were then imputed using the missRanger package [43, 44], which employs Random Forest algorithm trained on observed data. We used the default parameters of the package (num.trees = 100) and default settings of ranger. Afterward, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with the glmnet package was used to screen risk factors for future falls and fall-related injuries. The optimal tuning parameter (λ) was determined through 10‑fold cross‑validation. LASSO addresses variable collinearity by enhancing the penalty coefficient λ and aids in selecting crucial risk factors [45]. This pre‑screening step reduces dimensionality, thereby lowering the risk of overfitting and spurious edge identification in the subsequent Bayesian network analysis. The Bayesian network analysis models the conditional dependency structure among the pre‑selected variables, thus revealing relationships that LASSO alone cannot capture. This sequential approach has been successfully applied in previous studies [23, 46].

The Bayesian network consisted of a directed acyclic graph (DAG) paired with a conditional probability table (CPT), where each node symbolizes a random variable and the arcs connecting the nodes depict relationships between the variables [47]. Building a Bayesian network involved both Bayesian structure learning and Bayesian parameter learning. Initially, the Max-Min Hill Climbing (MMHC) algorithm was employed for structure learning [48]. The Bayesian network structure for falls and fall-related injuries were developed by integrating expert knowledge with data insights. To improve network learning accuracy and decrease the likelihood of model overfitting, structure learning requires the discretization of continuous variables, which also adds practical value to data mining outcomes. In the second phase of parameter learning, a Bayesian estimation approach was used to produce a CPT for each node [49]. A 10-fold cross-validation procedure was applied in this process to test the model’s robustness and optimize the parameters, with falls and fall-related injuries serving as the predictive nodes. Simultaneously, the class imbalance issue in the training set was addressed using the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) [50], which has been proven to improve parameter estimation in a previous study [51]. The bnlearn package was utilized to perform Bayesian network analysis. The aforementioned analyses were conducted in R 4.4.3 software. The specific packages and corresponding functions used in the statistical analysis were listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Once the Bayesian networks were constructed, GeNIe 5.0 Academic software was utilized for visual display and sensitivity analysis of the networks [52]. Through sensitivity analysis, we identified nodes with high sensitivity values, which quantify the absolute magnitude of change in the target node probabilities per unit change in the node’s parameters. These values indicate that slight modifications in these nodes may lead to a large change the probability of target nodes (falls or fall-related injuries).

Results

Characteristics of the study population

The study included 8,316 participants, of which 6,890 (82.85%) were aged 60 to 74 years, and 4,291 (51.60%) were women. Throughout the 3-year follow-up, 1,937 (23.29%) older adults experienced falls, and those who suffered falls without severe injury and those who suffered falls with severe injury were 1,100 (13.23%) and 837 (10.06%), respectively. The study examined 24 characteristic variables, and 19 of them had missing data. The missing data rate spanned from 0.42% to 29.08%, and 91.67% of the variables had less than 20% missing data. The characteristics of participants were distributed similarly before and after imputing the missing data (Table 1). Comparative analyses using both the original and imputed datasets showed that, with the exception of BMI, the incidence of falls and fall-related injuries differed significantly across subgroups defined by various characteristics (all P < 0.05), and these differences were consistent before and after imputation. These comparative analyses demonstrated that the data distribution remained highly similar after imputation, supporting the robustness of the subsequent study findings. The comprehensive distribution and statistical details were available in Supplementary Tables 4 and Supplementary Table 5.

Screening results of risk factors for falls and fall-related injuries

As indicated in Supplementary Table 6, LASSO regression and 10-fold cross-validation revealed 20 variables significantly related to falls, in addition to walking speed and hypertension (λ.min = 0.001). The model for fall-related injuries retained 19 variables, with walking speed, hypertension, and smoking being the exceptions (λ.min = 0.004). Supplementary Fig. 1 presented the graphical results of the LASSO regression analyses.

Bayesian networks learning and sensitivity analyses

Two Bayesian networks were constructed based on the variables identified by LASSO regression. Figure 2 depicted the topology of these networks regarding risk factors associated with falls and fall-related injuries. The first Bayesian network, which addressed risk factors for falls (Fig. 2A), was composed of 21 nodes and 48 directed edges. It indicated that fall history, grip strength, lower limb strength, and balance were directly associated with falls. While hip fracture history, stroke, BADL disability, IADL disability and use of walking aids had overlapping risk factors with falls, a path to falls was not determined. The second Bayesian network concerning fall-related injuries included 20 nodes and 48 directed edges (Fig. 2B). It revealed that hip fracture history, BMI, grip strength, lower limb strength, balance and use of walking aids had direct associations with fall-related injuries. There were shared risk factors for BADL and IADL disabilities and fall-related injuries, but no direct or indirect link was found. Through a complex network, the remaining variables, such as cognitive impairment, depressive symptom and vision, were indirectly associated with falls or fall-related injuries.

In Table 2, the sensitivity values for different nodes in Bayesian networks were listed, which were created to assess their association with the target outcomes for older adults. The top three variables, including fall history (0.123), lower limb strength (0.021) and balance (0.016), had the greatest direct association with falls with higher sensitivity. Regarding fall-related injuries, the results indicated that hip fracture history (0.106), use of walking aids (0.093) and BMI (0.034) were the most influential direct factors, as reflected by their higher sensitivity values. In the context of Bayesian networks, it is important to note that once the distribution of all direct risk factors is determined, the probability of outcome nodes remains unaffected by changes in indirect risk factors. The top 10 conditional probability distributions of falls and fall-related injuries were detailed in Tables 3 and 4, respectively, quantifying the associations between direct risk factors and the probabilities of these incidents. For instance, the results revealed that there was a 78.57% probability of future falls in older adults having fall history, normal lower limb strength, poor balance and low grip strength. For older adults with hip fracture history, not using walking aids, an underweight BMI, normal grip strength, low lower limb strength and good balance, them had a 95.17% probability of falls without severe injuries. And older adults having hip fracture history, not using walking aids, normal BMI, low grip strength, low lower limb strength and good balance had a 98.72% probability of falls with severe injuries. The complete conditional probability distributions of the parent node combinations for falls and fall-related injuries were showed in Supplementary Tables 7 and Supplementary Table 8.

Discussion

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to explore the complex network relationship between risk factors for falls and fall-related injuries through a large national population sample. In the current study, we found that 23.29% of older adults in the Chinese community experienced falls, a rate higher than the 13.4% reported in the Netherlands [53] and 16.5% in Japan [54]. This may be associated with this study’s extended follow-up period and China’s relatively poorer socioeconomic conditions [4]. The incidence of severe injuries after falls was 10.06%, similar to what previous studies have shown [9, 55]. The findings from Bayesian networks uncovered the association paths between risk factors for falls and fall-related injuries, highlighted direct and indirect factors and their importance, and discovered combinations of risk factors that contributed to a high risk of falls and fall-related injuries.

The Bayesian networks in our study indicated that grip strength, lower limb strength and balance were direct risk factors commonly associated with falls and fall-related injuries in older adults. The decline in grip strength and lower limb strength is frequently viewed as a marker of functional deterioration in older adults [33, 56]. On the one hand, muscle weakness can diminish balance control and result in gait issues of older adults [57], which were regarded as a direct cause of falling [58]. On the other hand, a decrease in muscle strength might hinder older adults’ ability to respond to disturbances, leading to difficulties in adjusting body posture after losing balance [56, 59]. All of the above scenarios increase the risk of falls and fall-related injuries. Although there were similar mechanisms, Hirase et al. [17] noted that balance difficulties have a greater influence on fall risk than on injuries resulting from falls, which matches our discovery that the sensitivity of balance concerning fall-related injuries was lower compared to falls in Bayesian networks. Our findings also revealed that grip strength ranked higher in sensitivity for fall-related injuries, indicating its potential greater role in preventing fall-related injuries. It might be because grasping an object during a fall allows older adults to slow down or redirect the fall, which reduces the body’s direct impact with the ground and consequently lowers the injury severity.

Moreover, several distinct direct factors were identified. Specifically, we found that a history of falls was directly linked to falls and had the strongest association. Studies have shown that individuals who have fallen are more likely to develop a fear of falling [60], which can lead to increased fall risk due to restricted activities, altered gait and posture, and unusual attention distribution [61]. Our results also suggested that hip fracture history, use of walking aids, and BMI were direct risk factors for fall-related injuries, with these three factors having the strongest associations. Falls are responsible for more than 90% of hip fractures, and these fractures frequently indicate osteoporosis, which is closely linked to a heightened risk of re-fractures following a fall [62, 63]. Earlier studies found that older people using walking aids were more prone to fall-related injuries, which might be associated with the improper use and insufficient stability of certain walking aids [64, 65]. Findings from Zhang et al.’s study [66] demonstrated that obesity in older adults was associated with a reduced risk of future hip fractures, in contrast to underweight individuals who faced a higher risk. However, Madigan et al. [67] observed that individuals with obesity suffered more injuries from falls. At present, there is still controversy regarding the relationship between BMI and fall-related injuries, and additional research is warranted.

Regarding indirect factors, we observed that age was the leading indirect risk factor for both falls and fall-related injuries. Many studies have demonstrated this because the physical function of older people decreases with age [10, 53]. For falls, our study indicated that arthritis, gender (female), and diabetes had the importance of being second only to age, pointing to the need for targeted prevention strategies for these groups. This finding is in line with existing research that reports a relatively high incidence of falls in these particular subgroups of older adults [68–70]. For fall-related injuries, we found that stroke was the second leading factor. This reminds us of the importance of developing specific prevention strategies for elderly individuals with a history of stroke. Besides the general risk factors observed in the aging population, these older adults might possess particular risk factors and characteristics linked to their condition [71]. Additionally, our Bayesian networks suggested that depressive symptom was indirectly associated with the risk of falls and fall-related injuries through associations with grip strength and lower limb strength, while cognitive impairment and vision were related to the risk through associations with balance ability among older adults. Previous studies have demonstrated the connection between these variables [72–75].

Finally, despite the existence of numerous and complex risk factors, Bayesian network analysis reveals that once direct risk factors are stabilized, variations in indirect risk factors are not associated with the occurrence probability of falls and fall-related injuries. By comparing the conditional probabilities of falls or fall-related injuries under different parent node combinations, this study helps clinical workers identify high-risk factor combinations intuitively, thereby providing suggestions for achieving precise prevention or intervention. In most of these combinations, having fall history, having hip fracture history and using walking aids were often present, consistent with the fact that these factors mentioned above had a considerable correlation strength. Moreover, we had an interesting finding that among the top-ranked high-probability groups, not all older adults with poor physical abilities (primarily reflected in grip strength, lower limb strength and balance) were the most likely to fall and suffer from injuries, but rather certain older people with normal functions were at greater risk. This situation may be related to unmeasured variables such as physical activity levels, outdoor exposure, and risk-taking behaviors. For instance, when all abilities are diminished, older adults engage less in daily activities like outdoor walking and housework, which are common scenarios where falls and fall-related injuries occur [76, 77].

Implications for clinical practice and future research

Based on findings of this study, clinical practice should prioritize multifactorial interventions targeting the key direct risk factors and high-risk combinations of factors, including improving grip strength, lower limb strength and balance through tailored exercise programs, which are essential for preventing both falls and injuries caused by falls. Assessment and intervention strategies should also pay more attention to specific clinical populations, especially on preventing falls in older adults with a history of falls, and preventing fall-related injuries in older adults with hip fracture history and those using walking aids. Future research needs to further validate the counterintuitive finding that some older adults with certain normal physical ability are at high risk for falls or injuries from falls, while also illuminating the role of activity patterns in it. The dispute over the association between BMI and the severity of fall-related injuries is also worth further study and resolution.

Limitations

There were several limitations in this study. First, the absence of detailed medication types and environmental data concerning falls in the CHARLS database prevented us from investigating their effects in the Bayesian networks, despite they were typically regarded as significant external contributors to falls and fall-related injuries [15, 78–80]. Future research should incorporate these variables. Second, we constructed discrete Bayesian networks that necessitate the discretization of continuous variables based on clinical cut-offs. While this enhances clinical interpretability, it may result in some loss of statistical information and granularity compared to using continuous variables, potentially affecting the detection of dose-response relationships. Third, since the CHARLS did not record the specific occurrence times of fall-related outcomes, an analysis for the differences in risk factors of falls and fall-related injuries across varying time horizons was not performed. Finally, the findings of this study, conducted only on a Chinese population, may not be directly transferable to the older adults in other countries and regions.

Conclusions

The high incidence of falls (23.29%) and severe injuries after falling (10.06%) underscores the urgency of targeted interventions. Bayesian networks clarified the complex interactions between the risk factors for falls and fall-related injuries. In the network relationship, grip strength, lower limb strength, and balance were shared direct risk factors for both outcomes. Fall history was the dominant direct factor for falls, while hip fracture history, use of walking aids and BMI most directly associated with fall-related injuries. The probability distribution concerning the combination of direct risk factors suggested that not all older adults with poor physical ability exhibited the highest risk, and some with normal function faced elevated susceptibility. Clinical prevention for falls and fall-related injuries could be enhanced by focusing on multifactorial strategies targeting identified relatively important factors and high-risk subgroups.
