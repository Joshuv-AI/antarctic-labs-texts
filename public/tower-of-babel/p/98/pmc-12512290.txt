Prevalence and risk factors of ovine enzootic abortion caused by Chlamydia abortus in the Layyah district of Punjab, Pakistan
Authors: Muhammad Baqir, Laeeq Akbar Lodhi, Muhammad Aamir Aslam, Tariq Jamil, Muhammad Hammad Hussain, Muhammad Moazam Jalees, Huma Jamil, Muhammad Khalid Mansoor, Muhammad Saqib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chlamydia abortus causes enzootic abortion in sheep and poses a zoonotic public health threat. Since chlamydiosis leads to significant economic losses, this cross-sectional sero-epidemiological study was conducted to determine the prevalence of antibodies against Chlamydia abortus in ewe flocks with a history of abortion from the district Layyah of Pakistani Punjab. For this purpose, 184 serum samples were tested from 50 privately owned sheep flocks located across three sub districts (Tehsils) by commercially available iELISA kit. The seroprevalence (%) along with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and potential risk factors were analysed. A 25.8% (CI 15.2–39.6) flock-based and 7.9% (CI 4.4–12.6) individual-based true seroprevalence was observed. The highest seroprevalence was recorded in Tehsil Layyah (58.7% flock-level and 18.5% individual-level) followed by Karor Lal Esan (17.4% flock-level and 4.7% individual-level), whereas no flock was found positive in Tehsil Choubara. The association of potential risk factors; age group, flock size, feeding system, rearing system, species and breeds, with seropositivity was determined by chi-square testing. Only the history of abortions was found to be significantly (OR: 11.0, p < 0.001) associated with the higher seroprevalence in the sampled flocks. This study is the first to document the seroprevalence of ovine enzootic abortion from the district of Layyah, and we recommend further investigations and extension efforts to consider a control strategy in affected flocks.

Introduction

Ovine enzootic abortion (OEA) is a significant pathogenic constraint and economic problem in sheep farming countries worldwide, including the vaccinated flocks [1]. Enzootic abortion was first reported in 1950, and since then, many epidemics have been reported in different parts of the world [2]. The etiological agent, Chlamydia abortus (C. abortus), a Gram-negative, strict intracellular bacterium was formerly classified as a distinct genus Chlamydophila (Cp.) [3]. This disease results in an annual incidence of up to 5% abortion, and during epidemics in naïve populations, the abortion level can exceed 75% [4]. C.abortus causes reproductive problems in small and large ruminants, such as infertility, stillbirth, birth of weak lamb, endometritis and epididymitis [4]. Lesions develop in the endometrium, caruncles, and placenta along with other disorders, such as conjunctivitis, arthritis, pneumonia, focal hepatitis and epididymitis [5–7]. Infected ewes transmit C.abortus to others through infected birth fluids, utero-vaginal discharges, aborted fetuses and semen during the breeding season. During epidemics, ewes abort in the last 2–3 weeks of gestation and develop immunity after abortion, but the animal remains a carrier for up to 3 years because the bacteria remain in their reproductive system [8].

C. abortus has a latency period before infecting the gravid uterus, but its mechanism of infection remains unknown. Experimental subcutaneous inoculation results in placental lesions after 90 days of infection [9]. Additionally, this bacterium has significant public health importance and has been reported to produce clinical pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) and abortion in humans through the ingestion of C. abortus contaminated food and water or through inhalation of infected barn dust. It can colonize human placenta and lead to spontaneous abortion, stillbirths or preterm labor, Infections in pregnant women, as well as complications like renal failure, hepatic dysfunction, and disseminated intravascular coagulation. Respiratory illness has also been reported in individuals with occupational exposure [1]. C. abortus has been reported in India [10], China [11], Saudi Arabia [12], Egypt [13], Turkey [14], Italy [15], Switzerland [16], United Kingdom [17], Brazil [18], and Poland [19]. There are reports of abortions in Pakistani sheep, and previously in the district of Layyah, sheep with history of abortions were found seropositive for brucellosis [9]. However, recent studies reported serological evidence of C. abortus in the Sindh and Punjab provinces of Pakistan [20, 21]. To this end, this study was planned to investigate the prevalence of C. abortus antibodies and related risk factors in sheep flocks with a history of abortion in Layyah district of Punjab, Pakistan.

Materials and methods

For the study, 50 privately owned ovine flocks with a history of abortion from three subdistricts (Tehsil Layyah, Tehsil Karor Lal Esan and Tehsil Choubara) of district Layyah of Punjab, Pakistan, were conveniently selected during 2022–2023. These flocks were previously tested through Rose Bengal Plate Agglutinition (RBT) test for the brucellosis in a local lab, and were all found negative. The owners were informed about the study. As most of them were illiterate, verbal consent was obtained. The owners were asked whether they were familier with C. abortus as cause of abortions, but none replied, “yes”. The geographical coordinates (latitudes and longitudes) of the sampled Tehsils were 30.96935 and 70.94279, 31.22331 and 70.95005 and 30.90579 and 71.47743, respectively. According to Livestock Department, there were approximately 0.505 million ewes in district Layyah. Individual and flock-level data on different variables, including age, breeds, abortion history, flock size, retention of fetal membranes, species managed together, and feeding as well as rearing system used, were also obtained during the sampling. Individual samples were further categorized into 3 age groups viz., < 2 years, 2–4 years and > 4 years. All individual samples collected from these farms were segregated based on the history of abortion into two groups, i.e., sheep with a known lifetime history of abortion and those without a history of abortion.

A total of 272 ewes (98 from Layyah, 87 from Karor Lal Esan, and 87 from Choubara) were sampled. The flock size ranged from 5 to 50 heads, and average flock size recorded was approximately 13 heads (Layyah ~ 13, Karor Lal Esan ~ 10, and Choubara ~ 15). From each animal, approximately 4 mL of blood was withdrawn into Gel Clot Activator vacutainer from the jugular vein and kept in a cool box containing an ice pack before transportation to the laboratory. Eighteen samples were not suitable for testing, so 254 sera were separated by centrifugation at 3000 g for 10 min, transferred to cryovials (1.8mL) and stored at − 20 °C till serologic testing [22]. From this sampling frame, 188 samples were randomly selected (using Survey Toolbox) for laboratory analysis; including 64 blood samples from Tehsil Layyah, 60 from Tehsil Karor Lal Esan, and 60 from Tehsil Choubara. To counteract the non-specific reactions, the samples were tested by indirect enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (iELISA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines (ID ScreenChlamydophila abortus Indirect Multi-species, ID Vet, Grabels, France). The test is based on a synthetic antigen from a major outer membrane protein (Momp) specific to Chlamydia abortus.The assay was reported to have 100% sensitivity and 99.7% specificty For each sample, the seropositivity (S/P) percentage was calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm S/\mathrm P=\frac{\mathrm{Test}\;\mathrm{Sample}/\mathrm{Negative}\;\mathrm{control}\;(\mathrm{OD})\;\times\;100}{\mathrm{Positive}\;\mathrm{control}\;(\mathrm{OD})\;}$$\end{document}

Sample presenting an S/P of less than or equal to 50% were considered negative, whereas less than 60% and greater than 50% were considered doubtful, and samples with greater than or equal to 60% were considered seropositive. The data was analyzed statistically with Pearson Chi-Square Test using R software (TEAM 2010). The 95% confidence interval (CI) and odd ratio (OR), were also calculated.

At the flock-level, flock size (p = 0.623), retention of fetal membranes (p = 0.106), mortality (p = 0.636), breeds (p = 0.128), different species interactions (p = 0.427), and rearing systems (p = 0.216) were not found to be associated with higher seroprevalence in the sampled flock. Since there were no cases in the flocks managed under different grazing systems, calculation of odds ratio and risk analysis was not possible.

Discussion

Several infectious agents are known to lead to abortion in sheep, including Chlamydia abortus, Toxoplasma gondii, Coxiella brunetii, Salmonella abortusovis, Mycoplasma agalactiae, Listeria monocytogenes, and Brucella melitensis [22–24]. The district of Layyah, Punjab, has a large sheep population, which is owned by small farmers. Reports of abortions other than brucellosis among sheep from Punjab have been documented [25]. Therefore, this study was planned to understand whether these abortions could be investigated with reference to Chlamydia abortus. Previously, a seroprevalence of 3.3% was reported in sheep of Jhang district of Punjab [25], and 12.5% in Hyderabad, Tando Allahyar and Mirpurkhas districts [21], and 35.3% in Dadu district of Sindh [20].

Different diagnostic tests like complement fixation test (CFT), indirect enzyme link immunosorbent assay (iELISA), competitive enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (cELISA), immunofluorescent assay (IFAs) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), are being used for the diagnosis of ovine enzootic abortion caused by C. abortus. In this study, we used an ELISA to detect antibodies against C. abortus [25, 26]. A Slovakian study concluded that iELISA is more sensitive as compared to CFT for the screening of C. abortus antibodies [27].

The animal-level seroprevalence in our study was 8.15%, which was comparable to that reported in Saudi Arabia (7.5%) [12], the Slovak Republic (11.30%) [27], and Egypt (13.85) [13]. However, it is lower than that reported in [20] Jorden (21.8%) [8], Mexico (21.3%) [28], Brazil (21.5%) [29], Bosnia (43.3%) [30], Iran (25.6%) [31], Algeria (31%) [32], and China (18.7%) [33]. The differences in the flock structure, managemental practices, use of diagnostic methods and climatic conditions could be the potential cause of these differences. Compared with other studies conducted in Pakistan, the seroprevalence found in our study was higher than that reported in four Tehsils of Punjab (3.3%) [25] and lower than three districts of Sindh (12.5%) [21], However, flock-level seroprevalence was not reported by both studies. The flock level prevalence percentage (26%) found in our study was considerably lower than that reported from Iran (81.4%) [31], Brazil (77.7%) [29], Italy (71.4%) [15], Turkey (46.7%) [14], Bosnia and Herzegovina (84.2%) [30], and Algeria (74.0%) [32]. Individual and flock-level variations could be the reason behind these differences in seroprevalence, as it has been documented that exposure to C. abortus can vary depending upon the location, structure of herds, and individual status of an animal within a flock [34].

Location-based differences were observed in the seroprevalence of C. abortus in our study. The flock and individual-level (I) prevalence was significantly (p < 0.05) higher in Tehsil Layyah (I: 18.7%, F: 58.8%) than Tehsil Karor Lal Esan (I:5.0%, F:17.7%), whereas, no flock was found positive in Tehsil Choubara. Similar differences in prevalence among different locations have been reported from studies conducted in Jordan and Brazil [8, 29]. Differences in flock size, flock structure (open/closed), and the use of common water troughs could explain these variations [8, 29, 35]. However, our sample size and data were limited to explore these variables, which is a limitation of our study.

Although numerical differences were observed, the age-based seroprevalence of antibodies against C. abortus was not found significantly different in this study. Similar results had been reported previously from Jorden [8]. Whereas studies from Egypt [13], Iran [31] and Syria [36] indicated that older groups of ewes with abortion history are more susceptible to the disease.

Although a study from Jordan reported the association between C. abortus seroprevalence and flock size of sheep [8], we did not find any significant (p > 0.05) difference between the flocks with ≤ 10 (23.1%) and those with > 10 (29.2%) sheep. Our results are in accord with those reported in a study conducted on Algerian ewes [32], in which flock size was not associated with seroprevalence of C. abortus.

We found that ewes with an abortion history (27.8%) had significantly (p < 0.01) higher seroprevalence than those without any history of abortion (3.4%) in Tehsil Layyah and Karor Lal Esan. Furthermore, ewes with an abortion history were found 11.0 times more likely to test seropositive. C. abortus is a significant cause of abortions, and its seroprevalence often correlates with abortion history. The virulent strains of C. abortus in the flocks are related to subclinical diseases, overpopulation, and undernourishment. In conclusion, the infection produces immunity that can last for many years and is related to the spontaneous expulsion of the fetus, and infection becomes latent in ewes, leading to further miscarriages [14]. Similar findings were reported in studies conducted elsewhere [13, 24, 27, 37–39]. History of abortion was linked to the varying degree of C. abortus seroprevalence; 8.9% in ewes from Ahvaz, Iran [38]. 6.9% from Namibia [40]10.3% from Turkey [14], 31% from Algeria [32], and 43.3% from Bosnia and Herzegovina [30]. Our results along with previously published studies, demonstrate that Chlamydia abortus could be the culprit for causing a significant proportion of abortions in sheep from Tehsils Layyah and Karor Lal Esan. However, this could not be established in sheep belonging to Tehsil Choubara because no seropositive animal was found. Because multiple organisms can cause abortions in sheep, it is necessary to investigate these flocks for other abortifacient agents like Coxiella burnetii and Toxoplasma gondii [14].

In the current study, anti-Chlamydophila abortus antibodies (34.2%) were found only in ewes managed under a mixed feeding system (grazing and stall feeding). This was a common feeding practice in the study area that might have favored disease transmission among flocks in district Layyah. The shared feeding and watering utensils in flocks with mixed feeding practices likely increased ewes’ exposure to C. abortus through the oral-nasal route [38]. Compared with the rearing system, the sheep maintained under semi-intensive system were only found positive for the antibodies against Chlamydia abortus (28.3%). Contrary to our results, higher seroprevalence (32.5%) was recorded in intensively managed sheep from Brazil [29]. Overpopulation and contaminated feed may have been the factors for the higher values observed in this study.

C. abortus infection is commonly reported in many countries of the world, mainly in ewes and goats. However, limited epidemiological information has been reported from Pakistan. We investigated the prevalence of C. abortus in sheep flocks with abortion history in three Tehsils of Layyah in this study. The selective sampling of flocks with a documented history of abortion is a limitation of this study. This approach may have led to the overestimation of prevalence at both the individual and flock levels compared with a random sample of the general flocks. Furthermore, the selection method may have influenced the association between individual infection status and flock-level prevalence, potentially altering the observed association. Future research employing stratified or random sampling methods, including apparently healthy flocks, could provide more generalizable estimates and facilitate more robust modeling of disease risk and transmission dynamics. In ewes, C. abortus infection may cause various reproductive disorders. For eradication and control, investigation of the factors associated with the spread of C. abortus infection in sheep is important. The disease can be controlled by adopting certain measures at three levels; use of vaccines, administration of therapeutic drugs, and proper handling measures, which include removal of birthing materials and disinfection of the areas. In Pakistan, no commercial vaccine is available against C. abortus, and there is a an urgent need to conduct detailed epidemiological investigations to understand and control this important zoonotic pathogen.

Conclusions

From the present study, it can be concluded that ewes from district Layyah were exposed to C. abortus, a possible cause of abortion in the sampled ewes of of Layyah district. However, the precise epidemiological picture is unclear due to the limited sample size, absence of microbiological and molecular investigation in our study. Nevertheless, the zoonotic potential of C. abortus underscores the significance of these findings for public health. The involvement of other abortificants in flocks of Tehsil Choubara requires further investigation. It is deemed necessary to used molecular techniques to confirm the C. abortus in aborted animals. In this preliminary study, it was also observed that none of the farmers were aware of the disease and its zoonotic potential. We recommend that continuous farmer education, training, and awareness campaigns on preventive measures and control strategies be carried out in both the government and private sectors to reduce the rate of infection and eventually control this disease. To ascertain the actual prevalence of OEA nationwide sero-epidemiological studies are recommended.
