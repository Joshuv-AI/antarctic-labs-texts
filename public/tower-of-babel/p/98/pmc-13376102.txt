Integrated single-cell and bulk RNA-seq analysis reveals an NK-cell-related immune landscape and a prognostic signature for hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yanxia He, Zhumei Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is characterized by high heterogeneity and poor prognosis. Despite the importance of natural killer (NK) cells in innate immunity, their role in the HCC microenvironment remains unclear. This study aims to develop an NK-cell-related signature to optimize prognostic prediction and personalized therapy.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) identified candidate genes. Differential expression and univariate Cox analyses were performed on an integrated cohort comprising The Cancer Genome Atlas Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA-LIHC) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset GSE76427. To ensure data consistency, technical batch effects were eliminated using the ComBat algorithm. The integrated cohort was randomly assigned to training and testing sets (1:1). A prognostic model was constructed via Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) Cox regression (seed = 2) and validated in an independent ICGC-LIRI-JP cohort. Survival differences and model accuracy were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves. Finally, the Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm and the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) database were applied to characterize immune cell infiltration and predict immunotherapy response, respectively.ResultsFrom 790 initial candidates, 18 prognosis-related DEGs associated with NK-cell-related cellular programs were identified, which classified HCC into two distinct subtypes. A three-gene prognostic model (AP1S3, RPL23, and HM13) was established, showing high predictive accuracy in both the internal testing set and an independent external ICGC-LIRI-JP cohort. High-risk patients exhibited significantly poorer survival and specific clinical profiles. Notably, the risk score served as an independent prognostic factor, and a combined nomogram offered superior predictive power. Further characterization of the tumor microenvironment revealed that a high-risk score correlated with a remodeled stroma and a distinct potential for immunotherapy response. Additionally, high-risk patients showed increased sensitivity to targeted agents, such as sorafenib. Finally, the expression patterns of these signature genes were validated via RT-qPCR and immunohistochemistry (IHC), further supporting their role as key components of the identified prognostic landscape.ConclusionThe established risk score model, reflecting an NK-cell-related immune landscape, effectively predicts HCC prognosis and provides an integrated molecular landscape for optimizing personalized interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05198-4.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Bulk RNA-sequencing, NK-cell-related genes, Immune microenvironment

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver cancer and a major global health challenge, ranking as the sixth most common malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Due to the advanced stage at diagnosis for the majority of patients, the efficacy of curative interventions, such as surgery and ablation, remains severely restricted. Despite significant progress in targeted therapies (e.g., sorafenib, lenvatinib) and immune checkpoint inhibitors, the 5-year survival rate has stagnated at approximately 18%. This poor prognosis is largely attributed to the high heterogeneity of HCC, frequent recurrence, and the development of drug resistance [2, 3].

Extensive research has employed diverse strategies to investigate the molecular landscape of HCC. For instance, bioinformatics-based frameworks have been utilized to identify potential therapeutic targets and “hub genes” that may contribute to tumor progression [4]. Parallel mechanistic studies have further explored specific regulatory axes, such as the miR-3130-5p/FDX1 and circ_0102231/miR-873/SOX4 pathways, providing insights into how non-coding RNAs participate in gene expression modulation [5, 6]. Additionally, investigations into natural products, including proteins from Adenium obesum, have highlighted the ongoing search for novel anticancer agents in preclinical settings [7]. While the molecular mechanisms of oncogenesis have been widely investigated, the intricate cellular heterogeneity and specific interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME) remain to be further elucidated.

Within the TME, natural killer (NK) cells serve as a vital component of the innate immune system [8]. They possess the unique ability to recognize and eliminate tumor- and virus-infected cells without prior sensitization [9, 10]. Upon activation, NK cells release cytotoxic granules containing perforin and granzyme to lyse tumor cells, upregulate apoptosis-inducing ligands, and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby directly eliminating tumor cells [11]. However, in the HCC microenvironment, NK cell function is frequently impaired. The TME can modify the local chemotactic landscape to recruit less cytotoxic NK cell subsets or induce dysfunction through metabolic restriction; for instance, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase inhibition disrupts glycolysis, while adenosine accumulation restricts NK cell maturation [12, 13].Clinically, a higher abundance of hepatic NK cells correlates with improved prognosis in early-stage HCC [14]. Conversely, patients with advanced HCC exhibit a diminished proportion of NK cells and compromised effector functions in both the periphery and the liver, leading to ineffective tumor control [15]. Therefore, distinguishing the heterogeneity of NK cells and understanding their dysfunction is crucial. To address the limitations of bulk analysis, there is a compelling need to decipher these interactions at single-cell resolution. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology now enables the deconstruction of this complexity by characterizing the molecular signatures and functional states of individual cells [16].

In this study, we leverage scRNA-seq data to profile NK-cell-related prognostic signature in HCC. We aim to identify pivotal NK-cell-related genes, explore their correlations with clinical prognosis and immune status, and construct a robust prognostic model. By integrating scRNA-seq and bulk transcriptomic data, this approach aims to uncover novel mechanisms underlying NK cell dysfunction and identify potential therapeutic targets to revitalize antitumor immunity in HCC.

Materials and methods

Data collection

In this study, scRNA-seq data from two primary HCC samples (GSE166635) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), somatic mutation data, and corresponding clinical information for the Cancer Genome Atlas Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA-LIHC) cohort were retrieved from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The initial dataset comprised 374 tumor samples and 50 solid tissue normal samples. Additionally, the GSE76427 dataset, comprising 115 primary tumor samples from 115 patients with HCC, was retrieved from the GEO database. For the TCGA-LIHC cohort, gene-level copy number variation (CNV) data were obtained from the UCSC Xena database (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). The ICGC-LIRI-JP cohort was retrieved from the ICGC database (https://dcc.icgc.org/). The 231 primary HCC samples with complete clinical follow-up were selected as an independent external validation set.

ScRNA-seq data processing and marker identification

The scRNA-seq data analysis was conducted using the R software (version 4.2.2) and the Seurat package (version 4.3.0). Initial data loading applied a preliminary screening with the parameters min.cells = 3 and min.features = 200. Quality control (QC) was subsequently implemented to eliminate low-quality cells and potential doublets. The mitochondrial and erythrocyte gene ratios were calculated using the PercentageFeatureSet function. Cells were retained only if they met the following specific criteria: [1] > 500 detected genes; [2] < 20% mitochondrial content; [3] < 3% erythrocyte gene expression; and [4] 1,000 < UMI counts < 20,000 (to exclude empty droplets and doublets). Following normalization, high-variability genes were identified via the FindVariableFeatures function. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA). To mitigate batch effects and ensure an even distribution of cells across different samples, the Harmony algorithm was employed for data integration [17]. The integration process was based on the top 15 principal components (PCs), with specific execution parameters set to theta = 2 and max.iter.harmony = 20. To ensure the reproducibility of the integration results, a fixed random seed of 1234 was implemented. The dataset was subsequently clustered, resulting in the identification of 18 independent clusters. The FindAllMarkers function was utilized to identify NK-cell-related genes (|log2FC| > 0.25, p < 0.05). To ensure high-confidence annotation, we applied a dual-verification approach combining automated classification (SingleR) with manual marker validation. The identity of each cluster was confirmed by visualizing the expression of established lineage markers.

Bulk RNA-seq data differential analysis

Differential expression analysis was initially performed on the TCGA-LIHC cohort using the “limma” package (version 3.54.0). Genes meeting the screening criteria of |log2FC| > 1.0, FDR < 0.05 were identified as NK-cell-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To further explore potential biological functions and signaling pathways, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. To construct a robust cohort for prognostic validation, the TCGA-LIHC and GSE76427 datasets were integrated. The combined data were normalized using the normalizeBetweenArrays function, and technical batch effects between the platforms were corrected via the ComBat algorithm, with the efficacy of the correction validated by PCA (Supplementary Fig. 1). Univariate Cox survival analysis was performed on this batch-corrected meta-cohort to identify NK-cell-related DEGs significantly associated with prognosis (p < 0.05).

Protein interaction network and copy number variation analysis

To thoroughly examine the interactions among the NK-cell-related DEGs associated with prognosis, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was created utilizing the resources of the STRING database (https://string-db.org/). The genomic heterogeneity of HCC is influenced by a multitude of complex factors, and an evaluation of the genetic alterations can aid in the elucidation of the pathogenesis. Hence, an investigation was conducted to examine the modifications of NK-cell-related genes on chromosomes, along with the chromosomal positioning of each gene. The CNV information pertaining to prognosis-related NK-cell-related DEGs was extracted and presented in the form of bar and circle plots.

Consensus clustering

The ConsensusClusterPlus function was utilized to perform unsupervised consensus clustering on HCC samples to identify distinct molecular subtypes. The clustering process was executed with 50 repetitions (reps = 1000) using a subsampling ratio of 80% for items (pItem = 0.8) and 100% for features (pFeature = 1). We employed the K-means (km) algorithm based on “euclidean” distance, with a fixed random seed of 123 to ensure reproducibility. The maximum cluster number, k, was set to 9, and the optimal k = 2 was determined through the analysis of the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve and the delta area plot. The reliability of the clustering classification was validated through the PCA and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding(t-SNE). Subsequently, survival analysis was conducted on the various clusters to assess overall survival rates. The heatmap was employed to demonstrate the distribution of clinical features among the various clusters. The ssGSEA algorithm was employed to estimate immune cell infiltration in both clusters. The GSVA algorithm was adopted to identify the primary biological processes and active pathways of the different clusters [18]. Additionally, the GSEA algorithm was applied for comparison purposes to enhance precision.

Construction and validation of the prognostic model

The batch-corrected meta-cohort was randomly assigned into a training set and a testing set at a 1:1 ratio. To ensure objectivity, all feature selection was conducted exclusively within the training set. Using a fixed seed = 2, LASSO regression was executed using the “glmnet” R package (family = “cox”). To identify the most robust prognostic features, we implemented 10-fold cross-validation with a maximum of 1,000 iterations (maxit = 1000). The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined based on the minimum mean cross-validated error (lambda.min). Subsequently, the genes identified by LASSO were subjected to multivariable Cox regression analysis to ascertain their respective coefficients. The risk score for each patient was calculated using the following formula: RiskScore =. where represents the Cox regression coefficient and is the gene expression level. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. Model performance was validated using Kaplan-Meier curves, time-dependent ROC curves (timeROC), and risk status plots. The model’s robustness was further evaluated in an independent external validation cohort (ICGC-LIRI-JP). Using the fixed coefficients and cutoff value derived from the training set, risk scores were calculated for ICGC patients, followed by Kaplan-Meier and time-dependent ROC analyses.

Nomogram and clinical independent prognosis

Next, independent prognostic analysis was completed by integrating clinical characteristics with risk score. Then, a clinically applicable tool was also established to specifically predict the survival probability of individual patient at distinct time points. The coxph function was performed to construct a model of nomogram. The calibration curve was utilized to evaluate the precision of the model, enabling the observation of the proximity between the predicted and actual probabilities. The evaluation of net clinical benefit was conducted through the application of decision curve analysis (DCA) [19]. The increase in risk for various risk groups was demonstrated through the cumulative risk curves. The differentiation of clinical characteristics, such as age, gender, grade and stage, among different risk groups was accomplished through the utilization of the “chi-squared” test.

Immune status and therapeutic response assessment

The Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) is an algorithm that deconvolutes the expression matrix of immune cell subtypes to infer the proportion of each type of immune cells, thereby quantifying the infiltration of immune cells in samples [20]. In this study, CIBERSORT was performed to investigate the characteristics of immune cell infiltration in various risk groups, and the outcomes were presented through bar graphs and violin plots. The ESTIMATE algorithm was performed to evaluate the tumor purity and stromal cell abundance, thereby providing a comprehensive characterization of the TME [21]. The relationship between immune score, stromal score, and risk score was examined using the Spearman correlation test to determine the tumor purity. Immunotherapy response was predicted using the TIDE database (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/). Finally, drug sensitivity (IC50) for diverse anticancer agents was estimated via the “oncoPredict” package based on the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/).

Experimental validation via RT-PCR and HPA

Subsequently, we employed real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) to corroborate the findings derived from bioinformatics analysis, thereby substantiating the expression of model genes at the transcriptional level. The experimental groups comprised of human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines HepG2 and HuH-7, while human normal liver cell line WRL68 was used as the normal control. All the cells were purchased from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in DMEM to attain the requisite quantity. The Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, United States) was utilized to extract the total RNA from the aforementioned cells. Subsequently, 2 ug of the total RNA was transcribed into cDNA, with GAPDH serving as an internal control. The SYBR Green PCR kit (Takara, Japan) was employed to conduct RT-PCR. The relative mRNA expression level was determined using the 2-ΔΔCt method. The primers (Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co.,Ltd.) were as follows: RPL23- Forward: GGTCCTCTGGTGCGAAATTC, RPL23-Reverse: GAAGTCTGTTCAGCCGTCCCTTG; AP1S3- Forward : TCGACAAGGGAAATTACGGCTACAG, AP1S3-Reverse: CCTGTGACCACGGGAGAGAATAATC; HM13- Forward : GCCTGCCCTCCTATACCTGGTC, HM13- Reverse: GATTCTGTCACTGCCGCTGGATC.

Concurrently assessing the expression patterns of model genes at the transcriptional level, we conducted a review of immunohistochemical images sourced from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). The HPA database is a comprehensive resource that integrates proteomic, transcriptomic, and systems biology data, and encompasses both normal and tumor tissues for protein expression analysis. Our investigation yielded three significant model genes (AP1S3, RPL23, and HM13), and we analyzed the immunohistochemical images of human tissues using the HPA database to ascertain the expression patterns of these genes in tissues.

Results

NK-cell-related genes were identified by ScRNA-seq analysis

Figure 1 depicts the research methodology employed in this study. Quality control measures were implemented for the HCC scRNA-seq data, resulting in the acquisition of 23,767 genes and 24,832 cells after initial screening. The mitochondrial gene ratio and erythrocyte ratio were subsequently calculated, and their distribution was illustrated in Fig. 2A Following the removal of low-quality cells, a total of 19,089 cells were remained for further analysis. The relationships between sequencing depth, mitochondrial gene ratio, and the number of detected genes were examined (Fig. 2C, D). We observed a strong positive correlation between sequencing depth and gene number (R = 0.91), whereas almost no correlation was found with mitochondrial content (R = -0.02). These results indicate that the filtered cells are of high quality, as the gene detection was driven by sequencing depth rather than technical noise or cell degradation. Following data normalization, the top 2,000 highly variable genes were identified, and the 10 genes exhibiting the greatest variance were highlighted (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, PCA downscaling was executed on these highly variable genes to further compress them (Fig. 2E). The top 4 characteristic genes in the principal components (PCs) were chosen for display using dot and heatmap (Supplementary Fig. 2A-B). For subsequent cell clustering, the first 15 PCs were visualized (Fig. 2F).

Cell type identity was established through an integrated annotation workflow. Firstly, the integration of samples was conducted with the Harmony method, resulting in the acquisition of 18 cell clusters through cluster analysis with the UMAP algorithm (Fig. 2G). A heatmap was generated by selecting the top 10 marker genes of each cell cluster (Supplementary Fig. 2C). The 18 clusters were subsequently annotated with cellular annotations based on “HumanPrimaryCellAtlasData”, ultimately leading to the identification of 9 distinct cell types (Fig. 2H). Specifically, the clusters were assigned as follows: Macrophages (clusters 0, 2), Hepatocytes (clusters 1, 10), Monocytes (clusters 3, 7, 8, 9), T cells (clusters 4, 5, 6), Smooth muscle cells (cluster 11), B cells (clusters 12, 17), Epithelial cells (clusters 13, 15), Endothelial cells (cluster 14), and NK cells (cluster 16). The quantitative distribution of these populations is summarized in Table 1. The first 10 marker genes were illustrated in bubble plots for NK cells, all of which exhibit high expression levels (Fig. 3B). To ensure high-fidelity cell type identification, the results of SingleR-based automated classification were systematically cross-referenced with the expression profiles of canonical lineage markers. As demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 3, each cluster was assigned to its respective lineage based on convergent evidence from both automated and manual validation. Notably, the NK-cell cluster was unique enrichment of TYROBP (also known as DAP12) and NKG7, while strictly lacking T-cell specific markers (CD3D and CD3E) and myeloid-specific signatures (LYZ and CD68). This exclusionary expression profile (TYROBP+/CD3D-/LYZ-) provided definitive evidence for the accurate distinction of NK cells from T cells and myeloid lineages. It is noteworthy that while the expression of certain cytotoxic granule genes (NKG7) was observed, their relatively moderate levels may be attributed to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characteristic of HCC, as previously documented [22, 23].

Differential analysis of bulk RNA-seq data

We obtained a total of 790 NK cell marker genes by integrating the single-cell analysis with the bulk RNA-seq data from GSE76427. Based on 790 NK cell marker genes and conducting an analysis of bulk RNA-seq data to identify differential gene expression between normal and tumor samples, a total of 35 NK-cell-related DEGs were extracted (Fig. 3E). The up- or down-regulation of NK-cell-related DEGs in tumor tissues was shown in the volcano map (Fig. 3D). The results of the observation GO and KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the NK-cell-related DEGs were implicated in the biological process of “protein targeting to membrane (GO:0006612)”, the cytological component of “cytosolic large ribosomal subunit (GO:0022625)”, the molecular biology function of “structural constituent of ribosome (GO. 0003735)” and other enriched GO terms (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the findings were pertinent to signaling pathways such as “ribosome”, “C-type lectin receptor”, “T-cell receptor” etc. (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, a PPI network of NK-cell-related DEGs was generated via the utilization of the STRING database (Fig. 4C), elucidating the interplay between each gene. Subsequently, Univariate Cox analysis was performed to identify the 18 NK-cell-related DEGs that were prognostically significant, with all but one gene, “IL1RL1”, being classified as risk genes (Fig. 4D). Notably, “IL1RL1” was identified as a protective gene. The network diagram effectively illustrated the expression levels and interrelationships of prognosis-related NK-cell-related DEGs (Fig. 4F). Consensus clustering to identify HCC subtypes

Consensus clustering was performed based on the 18 prognostic NK-cell-related DEGs. According to the CDF and delta area (Fig. 5C, D), the clustering analysis results were most reliable when a small value of k with a decreasing slope of CDF occurred. Furthermore, by comparing the matrix heatmap at k = 3 and k = 2, the clusters with the least mutual influence were selected, indicating that dividing the HCC sample into two subtypes was the most appropriate approach (Fig. 5A, B). The clustering accuracy was evaluated through the utilization of PCA and t-SNE (Fig. 5F, G), which demonstrated a reasonable level of accuracy. Survival analysis revealed that patients in subtype A exhibited a significantly lower survival rate (p = 0.002, Fig. 5E). Furthermore, the expression of all risk genes was found to be significantly elevated in subtype A (***p < 0.001, Fig. 5H). The heatmap provides a comprehensive visualization of the prognostic NK-cell-related DEGs across subtypes A and B, alongside their correlations with key clinicopathological features (Fig. 5J). The ssGSEA algorithm was employed to determine immune cell infiltration in subtypes A and B, revealing statistically significant differences in infiltration levels for 10 out of the 23 immune cells, including “activated CD4 T cells” and “MDSC”, across different clusters (Fig. 5I).

Construction and validation of prognostic model

Lasso is an algorithm for the construction of Cox proportional risk regression models widely used in survival analysis, and a more refined model is obtained by constructing a penalty coefficient [24]. We performed Lasso on prognosis-related NK-cell-related DEGs and obtained the 4 genes with the highest predictive capacity (Fig. 7A, B). Subsequently, the model underwent optimization, and the genes identified by Lasso were subjected to Multivariable Cox regression analysis to derive the optimal model. Ultimately, three highly significant genes were identified for the development of a novel prognostic model. Single-cell resolution analysis revealed the distinct expression landscapes of the model genes (Supplementary Fig. 4). HM13 and RPL23 were robustly expressed in malignant hepatocytes. In contrast, AP1S3 exhibited a sparse but highly cell-type-specific distribution, being exclusively detected within the NK cell cluster. Despite its low transcript abundance, the enrichment of AP1S3 in NK cells aligns with its specialized role in regulating cytotoxic granule trafficking. The coefficients of these genes were utilized to estimate the risk score of patients with HCC, as follows: RiskScore = (0.7225×ExpAP1S3) + (0.2808×ExpRPL23) + (0.4369×ExpHM13). Subsequently, the patients were segregated into high- and low-risk groups. The risk score exhibited a statistically significant variance in cluster A and B (p < 2.22e-16, Fig. 7C), with cluster A displaying a higher risk score, indicative of an unfavorable prognosis. The survival curves evinced noteworthy disparities in survival rates across the distinct risk groups, both in the training set (p < 0.001, Fig. 7D) and were also suitably validated in the test set (p = 0.002, Fig. 7E). The risk curves and heatmaps demonstrated that the model genes were expressed at higher levels in the high-risk group in both the train and test sets. Additionally, as the risk score increased, patient mortality increased, with more patients eventually dying in high-risk group (Fig. 7F, G). The ROC curve was utilized to assess the prognostic model’s predictive capacity. The AUC values for the training set were 0.707, 0.700, and 0.726 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 7H). Similarly, the AUC values for the test set were 0.643, 0.591, and 0.586 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 7I). To further evaluate the clinical generalizability, we applied the fixed cutoff to an independent ICGC cohort. As expected, the model effectively stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups (p = 0.006). Furthermore, the model exhibited favorable predictive performance in the independent ICGC cohort, with 1- and 3-year AUCs of 0.721 and 0.700, respectively. While the 5-year AUC (0.597) showed a slight decrease, the overall results support the generalizability of the 3-gene signature (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Constructing clinically applicable nomogram

An independent prognostic analysis revealed that Stage III, Stage IV, and risk score were independent prognostic factors that could serve as independent predictors for patients with HCC (Fig. 8A). A novel nomogram was constructed to predict patient survival at 1, 3, and 5 years based on clinical characteristics and risk score (Fig. 8B), with Stage and risk score demonstrating greater contributions to the prognosis of HCC patients. The calibration curves indicated a high level of agreement between the predicted and actual values of the nomogram model (Fig. 8C). The cumulative risk curve demonstrated a gradual escalation in risk for patients over time, with a more pronounced increase in risk observed among high-risk group patients (Fig. 8D). The DCA can provide insight into the net clinical benefit that patients may attain, as evidenced by the nomogram utilized to forecast patient prognosis, which identifies the patients who stand to gain the most from clinical intervention (Fig. 8E). Subsequently, we conducted an analysis of the specific distribution of risk score in relation to clinical characteristics. The findings indicated that patients Age < = 65 had a slightly higher risk score, although this difference did not reach statistical significance when compared to patients Age < = 65 (p = 0.065, Fig. 8F). Assessment of tumor immune microenvironment status

The TME is composed of a diverse array of immune and stromal cells, and its heterogeneity plays a significant role in tumor metastasis, recurrence, and drug resistance. The pathophysiology of HCC is a complex, multi-step process characterized by a heterogeneous mutational landscape and histological features. The relative amounts of each immune cell infiltration in high- and low-risk groups were illustrated in Fig. 9A. As the risk score increased, there was a corresponding increase in the proportion of resting NK cells (R = 0.29, p = 0.011, Fig. 9B). The correlation heatmap illustrated the interdependence among the 22 immune cells (Fig. 9C). The enrichment of immune cells in varying risk groups was demonstrated through using of a violin plot (Fig. 9D). The overall TME in different risk groups was evaluated using ESTIMATE, and the results indicated a statistically significant difference in stromal score (Fig. 9E). The risk score exhibited a significant negative correlation with the stromal score (R = − 0.13, p = 0.005, Fig. 9G). However, the positive correlation trend between risk score and immune score was not statistically significant (R = 0.036, p = 0.43, Fig. 9F). This lack of significance may be attributed to the focus of this study on NK cells and the insufficient reflection of the degree of immune cell infiltration in HCC samples grouped by immune cell-related genes. In order to examine the intricate mutation profiles, we utilized waterfall plots to visually analyze the mutational landscape of frequently mutated genes. Our findings indicated that TP53, CTNNB1, and TTN were the most commonly mutated genes in HCC (Fig. 9H, I).

Validation of model genes

Through the analysis of high-throughput data, we identified critical NK cell marker genes (AP1S3, RPL23, and HM13) that are associated with HCC and utilized them for the development of prognostic models. To further validate these genes, we investigated their differential expression profiles in normal and HCC tissues. Our findings indicated that AP1S3 (p < 0.001, Fig. 11A), RPL23 (p < 0.001, Fig. 11B), and HM13 (p < 0.001, Fig. 11C) were significantly upregulated in HCC compared to normal tissues. After completing the survival curves, it was observed that the survival rates in the AP1S3 (p < 0.001, Fig. 11D), RPL23 (p < 0.001, Fig. 11E), and HM13 (p < 0.001, Fig. 11F) genes high expression groups were lower, thus providing further evidence that these genes are risk factors for HCC. The transcriptional expression of these model genes in both hepatocellular carcinoma cells and normal liver cells was confirmed through using RT-qPCR assay. The present study observed that the expression levels of AP1S3 (Fig. 11G, H), RPL23 (Fig. 11J, K), and HM13 (Fig. 11M, N) were significantly elevated in both HepG2 and HuH-7 hepatocellular carcinoma cells compared to WRL68 liver normal cells. Discussion

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is characterized by high malignancy and a poor prognosis. Despite hepatectomy being the preferred treatment option, the postoperative recurrence rate remains high. The phenotypic and molecular heterogeneity of HCC renders it resistant to conventional chemotherapy and targeted therapies [25]. Consequently, there is a pressing need for effective biomarkers to guide treatment and prognostic assessment. However, extant prediction methods often lack specificity and overlook the decisive influence of the TME [26]. Additionally, the immune microenvironment significantly impacts the effectiveness of immunotherapy [27]. The interaction between HCC cells and their TME is a critical area of research, with growing interest in targeting the microenvironment for therapy.

The HCC microenvironment comprises cellular elements, including tumor cells and immune cells, and non-cellular components like the extracellular matrix and cytokines [28]. Among these, NK cells are crucial. The liver is a reservoir for NK cells, which execute immune surveillance as the primary defense against tumorigenesis. In HCC, NK cell numbers are significantly reduced, and their function is severely impaired, resulting in numerical deficiency and dysfunction. However, therapeutic intervention has been shown to restore NK cell activity [12]. Thus, increasing NK cell numbers and alleviating immunosuppression represent promising strategies for improving survival. TME is a crucial mechanism restricting NK cell-mediated tumor clearance. It fosters a milieu for proliferation and immune evasion while impacting NK cell functional states through ligand regulation and the secretion of inhibitory cytokines [29–31]. Notably, our results indicated a significant correlation between the risk score and TME scores. Specifically, a negative correlation was observed with the stromal score, which likely reflects the aggressive expansion of malignant hepatocytes in high-risk HCC. This rapid proliferation increases tumor purity and potentially ‘dilutes’ the stromal component, creating a tumor-dominant architecture that lacks supportive niches for immune cell infiltration. However, the role of the stroma is complex. While a lower stromal fraction characterizes our high-risk group, a high stromal component in other contexts typically implies an abundance of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and a dense extracellular matrix. This can act as a physical barrier restricting NK cell infiltration—a phenomenon known as ‘immune exclusion’ [32]. Furthermore, stromal cells secrete immunosuppressive cytokines like TGF-β, which directly impair NK cell cytotoxicity and promote an exhausted phenotype [33]. Therefore, both the loss of supportive stroma in high-risk tumors and the presence of inhibitory stromal barriers in others can synergistically contribute to NK cell dysfunction.

Our functional enrichment analysis provides further mechanistic insight into this phenotypic switch. GSVA and GSEA results revealed that the favorable immune landscape in low-risk patients is underpinned by active metabolic pathways, specifically fatty acid metabolism and monocarboxylic acid transmembrane transporter activity. These pathways are essential for maintaining the metabolic flux required for NK cell effector functions. In contrast, the high-risk group exhibits a marked depletion of these homeostatic metabolic processes. We speculate that the overexpressed RPL23 and HM13 in tumor cells drive an aggressive metabolic competition, leading to a nutrient-deprived and potentially acidic TME. This metabolic stress likely forces infiltrating NK cells into a ‘resting’ or functionally quiescent state as a survival mechanism to minimize energy expenditure, ultimately facilitating immune evasion. This is consistent with recent findings that metabolic flexibility is a primary determinant of NK cell functional fate within the hostile HCC microenvironment [12, 13].

In this study, we constructed a prognostic signature based on three NK-cell-related genes (AP1S3, RPL23, and HM13). Our single-cell analysis suggests that these genes drive NK cell dysfunction through distinct intrinsic and extrinsic pathways. Specifically, AP1S3, an adaptor protein linked to cancer invasiveness [34, 35], was uniquely enriched in the NK cell cluster. We speculate it acts as an intrinsic regulator of vesicular trafficking. Consequently, its aberrant expression may impair the mobilization or docking of cytotoxic granules, explaining the observed degranulation defects. Conversely, RPL23 and HM13 likely exert extrinsic pressure within the TME. Our HCC samples showed significant upregulation of both RPL23, a ribosomal protein involved in p53/c-Myc signaling and multidrug resistance [36–39], and HM13, an endoplasmic reticulum-located protease associated with HCC progression and PI3K-AKT-mTOR activation [40, 41]. We hypothesize that their overexpression creates a hostile milieu characterized by metabolic competition or inhibitory signaling crosstalk, driving NK cells toward the resting and exhausted phenotypes identified in our high-risk group.

The clinical relevance of this signature was confirmed by stratifying HCC samples into two distinct risk groups. The high-risk group exhibited significantly poorer survival and an immunosuppressive TME, characterized by a marked increase in resting NK cells. This aligns with the principle that NK cell activity depends on the balance of TME-derived signals [42]. Importantly, while our prognostic signature is closely associated with NK cell dynamics, it is crucial to recognize that it likely represents an NK-cell-related mixed signature rather than reflecting exclusively NK-cell-specific markers. In the complex TME, innate immune cells do not function in isolation; instead, they engage in profound cellular cross-talk and broader TME remodeling programs. A paradigmatic example of this is the cGAS-STING pathway, a central hub of innate immune sensing [43]. Recent evidence highlights that STING activation not only mediates NK cell-driven tumor regression but also extensively shapes the TME by orchestrating the recruitment of various immune cells and modulating immunotherapy responses. Therefore, the dysregulation of the three NK-related genes identified in our model might concurrently reflect these broader innate immune sensing networks and tumor-immune interactions, collectively dictating the clinical outcomes of HCC patients.

TME heterogeneity heavily restricts the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in HCC [44, 45]. In our study, the low-risk group exhibited enriched γδT cells, which are known for their rapid expansion, stable cytotoxicity, and potential as immunotherapeutic agents [46–48]. Conversely, while the high-risk group showed elevated expression of PD-1 and CTLA-4, they paradoxically exhibited significantly higher TIDE scores. This reflects a robust immune-evasive landscape mediated by T-cell dysfunction and exclusion, consistent with recent HCC molecular classifications [49]. Despite this predicted resistance to ICI monotherapy, our oncoPredict analysis revealed that high-risk patients had significantly lower IC50 values for systemic agents like sorafenib and 5-fluorouracil. This suggests that while high-risk patients may evade T-cell-mediated immunity, they represent a vulnerable population that could benefit from aggressive targeted or combinatorial regimens designed to bypass the immune exclusion barrier. Mechanistically, innate immune sensing pathways, particularly the cGAS-STING axis, are increasingly recognized as pivotal determinants of this therapy resistance. Suppression of these pathways fosters an immunosuppressive microenvironment, mediating resistance to various immunotherapies [43]. Our finding that high-risk patients exhibit a ‘resting’ NK cell phenotype aligns perfectly with such innate immune sensing-mediated resistance programs, highlighting the potential of modulating these pathways to overcome therapy resistance in HCC.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations remain. First, while the prognostic signature has been robustly validated across different populations using the TCGA and ICGC (LIRI-JP) cohorts, the scRNA-seq analysis still involved a relatively small number of samples. Future studies with larger, multi-center single-cell cohorts are needed to fully capture NK cell heterogeneity. Second, although we have incorporated external validation for the prognostic model, our evaluations of immunotherapy efficacy (via TIDE) and drug sensitivity still rely on computational algorithms. These specific functional findings remain hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the lack of prospective clinical trials and in vitro/in vivo experimental validation (e.g., PCR or flow cytometry) remains a constraint. Future wet-lab studies are warranted to further elucidate the complex biological mechanisms of these NK-cell-related genes in the TME.

Conclusion

In summary, by combining scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq, we identified NK-cell-related genes and developed a prognostic model for HCC. Our analysis characterized the immune microenvironment across risk groups and estimated potential responses to immunotherapy and chemotherapy, offering insights for personalized treatment. While these findings are informative, they are limited by a small sample size and incomplete clinical data. Future studies with larger, more comprehensive cohorts are needed to further validate these results.
