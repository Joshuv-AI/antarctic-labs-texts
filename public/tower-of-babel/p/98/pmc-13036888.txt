Estimating the baseline age when longitudinal changes in biomarkers accelerate: application to an imaging study of preclinical Alzheimer disease
Authors: Chengjie Xiong, Folasade Agboola, Jingqin Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBiomarkers are routinely measured from human biospecimens and imaging scans in Alzheimer disease (AD) research. Age is a well-known risk factor for AD. Detecting the baseline age at which the longitudinal change in biomarkers starts to accelerate is important to design preventive interventions.MethodsWe analyzed longitudinal biomarker data by a random intercept and random slope model where the slope (longitudinal rate of change) was modeled as a piecewise linear and continuous function of baseline age. We proposed to estimate the baseline age at the intersection of the two linear functions by multiple methods: maximum (profile) likelihood, minimum squared pseudo bias, minimum variance, minimum mean square error (MSE), and a two-stage method. We simulated large numbers of data sets to evaluate the performance of these estimators. Finally, we implemented them to analyze the longitudinal white matter hypointensity from brain magnetic resonance imaging scans in an AD cohort study of 616 participants conducted by the Washington University (WU) Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center (ADRC) to estimate the baseline age when the longitudinal rate of change starts to accelerate.ResultsOur simulations indicated that performance was universally poor for all point estimators and confidence interval (CI) estimates when the true baseline age corresponding to the accelerated longitudinal change was near the boundary or when the sample size was small (N = 100). Yet, the proposed estimators became approximately unbiased and showed relatively small mean squared error when the sample size increased (N > 200) and the true baseline age was away from the boundary. The 95% CIs from these methods also provided good nominal coverage with large sample sizes if the true baseline age when the rate of change started to accelerate was away from the boundary. When applied to the AD biomarker study, we found that almost all methods yielded similar estimates of baseline age from 59.19 years to 65.78 years, but the profile likelihood approach led to a much later estimate.ConclusionsWe proposed a novel longitudinal statistical model to estimate the baseline age when the rate of change in a biomarker started to accelerate and presented multiple methods to estimate the parameter. Our proposed estimators performed reasonably well, especially when the true parameter was away from the boundary and the sample sizes were large. When applied to an imaging study of preclinical AD, our methods revealed a largely consistent baseline age when the longitudinal change in white matter hypointensity started to accelerate. Further research is needed to tackle more complex challenges, i.e., multiple accelerations in the longitudinal change that may also depend on other AD risk factors.Trial registrationN/A.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02806-3.

Background

Longitudinal biomarkers, including those measured from human biospecimens and imaging scans (e.g., brain magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) scans), are routinely collected to track the evolution of disease processes. Age is a major risk factor for many neurodegenerative diseases, for example, Alzheimer disease (AD). Because the neurodegenerative processes of AD may begin years or even decades prior to the onset of clinical and cognitive symptoms [1], prevention trials of AD must determine the baseline age interval to enroll asymptomatic (i.e., cognitively normal without manifest clinical symptoms of AD) individuals who are at an elevated risk of AD. One way to identify such high-risk individuals is to pinpoint the age when the longitudinal rates of change in AD biomarkers start to accelerate. For example, white matter hyperintensities (WM-hyper) and hypointensities (WM-hypo) measure signal abnormalities in the brain that can be visualized in the cerebral white matter on different types of MRI scans and sequences. WM-hyper and WM-hypo are highly correlated with each other [2], and both are associated with validated and established biomarkers of AD and cognition [3], especially among individuals who are cognitively normal. Figure 1 presents the spaghetti plot of 616 cognitively normal individuals at baseline from Washington University (WU) Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center (ADRC) whose WM-hypo volumes were longitudinally measured. Because ~ 79% of the 616 participants had only two to four longitudinal assessments, a linear growth model is reasonable to capture the within-individual longitudinal change, which implies that the random intercept and random slope model [4] may fit the longitudinal data well. Figure 1 clearly indicates that the longitudinal rate of change in WM-hypo, i.e., the slope in the model, depends on the baseline age in the sense that younger individuals at baseline showed minimal increases over time, whereas older individuals demonstrated an accelerated rate of increase over time. This subset of older individuals may be at a higher AD risk and represent a good target population for AD preventive trials. Because the biomarker was measured on asymptomatic individuals (at baseline), and the longitudinal follow-up was limited, age of dementia symptom onset is not the major scientific focus of this study. Of significant clinical interest is the baseline age when the longitudinal rate of change in WM-hypo starts to accelerate because this age may determine the inclusion criterion of participant enrollment for future prevention trials on AD.

The statistical challenge here is to accurately estimate the baseline age that divides individuals into two groups such that the older group will have an accelerated rate of longitudinal change, in comparison to the younger group. Such a baseline age is often called a break-point [11–18], change-point [19, 20], inflection point [11], or threshold [15] in cross sectional linear [11–13, 16] and generalized linear [14, 18, 20] regression models and also in a longitudinal setting [17], as well as in time series [21–23]. The estimation of a change-point has been studied by many authors, mostly in a cross sectional setting [11–14, 16–18, 20]. Longitudinal data are crucial for understanding the natural history of the trajectories of diseases such as AD. Detecting one or more change-points in the middle of longitudinal follow up has also been studied in HIV infection and cognitive decline [6–10], both in univariate and multivariate settings [5, 6]. However, these models only apply to studies of very long duration in the longitudinal follow up with many frequent within-individual repeated assessments over time because longitudinal data prior to and after the change-point must be available to estimate the change-point in the longitudinal and nonlinear trajectories of biomarkers. Whereas Fig. 1 does represent longitudinal data in WM-hypo across a very wide baseline age span among subjects from mid-40s to almost 100 years, the within-subject longitudinal follow up is short and the frequency of repeated measurement is only 2 to 4 time points for most subjects. Hence, our focus here is on the population-level change-point in baseline age, not the individual-level change-point in the longitudinal follow up.

The paper is organized as follows. We give details of our longitudinal model with the objective of estimating the baseline age corresponding to an accelerated rate of change of a longitudinal biomarker in Methods. We then propose various methods to estimate the baseline age when the rate of change starts to accelerate and construct the confidence intervals (CI). Simulation studies are conducted to compare the proposed estimators in Results. In Discussion, we return to the real-world AD biomarker study as presented in Fig. 1 and apply the proposed algorithms to estimate the baseline age when the rate of change of WM-hypo started to accelerate. We conclude the paper with discussions in Conclusion.

Methods

We focus on observational studies and leverage the longitudinal design feature as represented in Fig. 1 and address the statistical challenge of detecting the baseline age when the longitudinal rate of change in WM-hypo started to accelerate. Here the baseline age is defined as the age at the first assessment of WM-hypo. Note that the main parameter is the baseline age corresponding to an acceleration in the longitudinal rate of change, which differs from the age of symptom onset. Because of the short follow up and low frequency of within-subject repeated measurements, we conceptualize a random intercept and random slope model for the longitudinally assessed AD biomarker. Because of the very long age span at baseline across the subjects, we further conceptualize that the longitudinal rate of change is a nonlinear function of baseline age, which is approximated by a piecewise linear and continuous function (Fig. 2, left panel). Estimating the baseline age when longitudinal change in white matter hypointensities started to accelerate

On computed tomography (CT) and T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), white matter lesions appear dark and have been termed as white matter hypointensities (WM‐hypo), while on fluid‐sensitive MRI sequences such as fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), they appear bright and are referred to as white matter hyperintensities (WM‐hyper). WM-hyper and WM-hypo are highly correlated [2], and both are associated with other validated biomarkers of AD and cognition [2, 3]. Whereas WM-hyper has been well reported to increase with age and predict an increased risk of dementia and cognitive decline [27, 28], WM-hypo was much less studied, especially with longitudinal assessments, which is the focus of this application.

Our longitudinal data of WM-hypo are from the Washinton University Knight AD Research Center (ADRC). One of the major scientific aims of the Knight ADRC is to detect the earliest possible biological changes associated with AD. Both cognitively normal middle aged and old individuals and those diagnosed with early symptomatic stages of AD dementia were enrolled for longitudinal clinical and cognitive assessments. Every two to three years, participants also underwent MRI and other imaging (e.g., positron emission tomography) assessments. MRI brain scan acquisition and processing were previously described [27]. The WM-hyper (on fluid-attenuated inversion recovery scans) and WM-hypo were quantified using the SPM8 lesion segmentation toolbox [29].

Because changes in many biomarkers of AD start many years prior to the onset of clinical symptoms [1], the asymptomatic stage becomes the most crucial time window for designing clinical trials to test potential AD prevention therapies. An important inclusion criterion of these trials is the baseline age window that indicates an elevated risk of AD progression. We therefore focused on 616 Knight ADRC participants who were cognitively normal at baseline, as defined by a global Clinical Dementia Rating® (CDR®) [30, 31] of zero, and estimated the baseline age that was associated with an accelerated rate of longitudinal change in WM-hypo. Among the 616 participants, 240 participants had 2 longitudinal follow up visits, 160 had 3 visits, 88 had 4 visits, 59 had 5 visits, and 69 had at least 6 longitudinal data points. The baseline age distribution of the participants was shown in Supplemental Fig. 1. The longitudinal trajectories of WM-hypo across the participants as functions of age at longitudinal assessments were shown in Fig. 1. A visual examination of Fig. 1 indicated a potential baseline age between 60 and 75 years when the longitudinal rates of change in WM-hypo started to accelerate. We set the search grid of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:\:$$\end{document}between 50 and 80 years with an increment of 0.01 years to identify the baseline age corresponding to the acceleration in the rate of change, using the proposed methods in this paper.

The estimates of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:\:$$\end{document}were presented in Table 1. The estimate by maximizing the profile likelihood (maxLogLike) was 79.7 years, near the right boundary of the search grid. Minimizing Delta method-based variance (minVar) yielded a much earlier estimate at 59.19 years, and minimizing mean square error (minMSE) led to a close estimate at 60.37 years. Estimates from minimizing the squared pseudo bias (minBias) and the two-stage approach were similar (Table 1), at around 63.29 years and 65.78 years (Fig. 7), respectively. The proposed profile likelihood-based 95% CI was very narrow (79.06 ~ 79.8 years), and the other 95% CIs were similar in length. Because most of our methods (other than the profile likelihood method) performed consistently, the likely true value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:\:$$\end{document}is somewhere between 59 years and 66 years. Given that the existence of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:$$\end{document} is the most important assumption in Model (1), we further sought to statistically confirm it, beyond a simple visualization, by comparing the slope of WM-hypo (i.e., per year longitudinal change) among participants with baseline age prior to and post the estimated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:.\:$$\end{document} The Davies test (see A Two-Stage Estimator) from the two-stage method with an estimate of 65.78 years gave a p-value of 0.004, the Wald test (Alternative Point and Confidence Interval (CI) Estimators) and other methods all led to a p-value of p < 0.0001 (Table 1), all supporting the existence of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:$$\end{document}. In summary, whereas all other methods led to similar estimates, an extreme and outlier estimate was given by the profile likelihood approach. This is consistent with what we observed in our simulation study, especially with small sample sizes. Because the simulation study suggested that the likelihood of extreme and outlier estimates decreased as the sample size increased, it is possible that a much larger sample size is needed (than the current sample size in our AD study) to avoid the extreme estimate from the MLE through profile likelihood.

Discussion

This paper addressed a statistical challenge arising from a real-world biomarker study of AD. Because age is the most important risk factor of AD, older individuals have a higher risk. Prevention trials on AD are conducted on individuals who are cognitively normal but at an elevated risk of AD, and hence must determine at what minimum age, participants should be enrolled in the trials. One important consideration is to find the baseline age when cognitively normal individuals start to show an elevated risk of AD. Because imaging biomarkers of AD are functions of age, we conceptualized that an elevated risk of AD may be represented by a threshold in baseline age so that individuals older than the threshold will exhibit an accelerated longitudinal change than the younger individuals in these biomarkers. This simple conceptualization not only makes clinical sense but is also well supported by the real-world biomarker study of AD in which longitudinal changes in WM-hypo, as an important biomarker of AD, clearly demonstrated an accelerated longitudinal increase as a function of baseline age (Fig. 1).

The statistical challenge is then how to best estimate the baseline age when longitudinal rate of change in WM-hypo started to accelerate. Although AD is a decades-long neurodegenerative disease, most of the AD biomarker studies have thus far accumulated longitudinal data only over a relatively short time window with a limited number of repeated measurements (2, 3, or 4 visits), partly due to the fact that many of the AD biomarkers are only recently developed and also costly, and some of the procedures may also be perceived as invasive by participants. The limited longitudinal follow up (both in duration and frequency of assessments) prevented us from identifying an individual-level accelerated change in the middle of longitudinal follow up. We therefore opted to fit the random intercept and random slope models that reasonably captured mostly linear longitudinal changes within individuals (Fig. 1) but leveraged on the wide window of baseline age that spanned from mid 40 s to almost 100 years to assess how it predicted the longitudinal changes in the biomarker (Fig. 1). Our models accounted for these important design and analytic features and incorporated a threshold in baseline age (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:)\:$$\end{document} when the longitudinal rate of change in the biomarker started to accelerate. Our models are in contrast to several longitudinal models that were proposed in literature, especially for modeling longitudinal cognitive changes whose primary goal was to identify individual-level break points when cognitive decline accelerated in the middle of longitudinal follow up [6–10]. These types of longitudinal change-point models typically require a very long follow-up and many within-individual repeated assessments over time because there must be adequate longitudinal data both prior to and post the break point. Whereas these methods have been adequately applied to the analysis of extensive longitudinal cognitive data (which are already available in many AD studies) [5–10], they do not directly apply to most biomarker studies in AD from which only short-term longitudinal data with very few repeated assessments are currently available. Further, our conceptualization led to a unique analytic challenge because the likelihood function is no longer a smooth function of the model parameters, which then questions the validity of standard MLE-based asymptotic inferences, especially about \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:,\:$$\end{document}the baseline age corresponding to the accelerated rate of change, the main interest of our study. Given that similar models have been well studied in literature under a cross sectional design with regression models [11–13, 16], our proposed model is novel in that it can be viewed as a bridge between the cross sectional [5–10] break-point models and the individual-level longitudinal change-point models [5–10] that require extensive longitudinal follow up. Importantly, modeling the annual rate of change directly as a piecewise linear function of baseline age in our proposed model is not only novel but also reflects the fundamental biomedical science that age is the biggest risk factor of AD [32]. The fact that many AD biomarkers are functions of age [3, 33, 34] facilitates the establishment of age-related inclusion/exclusion criteria for designing future AD prevention trials.

We provided multiple methods to estimate the baseline age when the longitudinal change of a biomarker started to accelerate and constructed the corresponding CIs. Because these methods require intensive computations, we provided specific algorithms to implement them. We further evaluated the performance of the proposed estimators through a comprehensive simulation study, assessing how the sample sizes, the frequency of longitudinal follow-ups, and the location of the baseline age when the accelerated longitudinal change occurred (i.e., close to vs. away from the boundary) may affect the performance. We found that performance was universally poor for all point estimators and CI estimators if the true baseline age when the accelerated longitudinal change occurred was near the boundary or if the sample size was small (N = 100). Yet, the proposed estimators became approximately unbiased and showed relatively small RMSEs when sample size increased (at least N = 200) and the true baseline age (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:)\:$$\end{document} was away from the boundary. The 95% CIs from these methods also provided good nominal coverage with large sample sizes if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\tau\:$$\end{document} was away from the boundary. We re-visited the real-world AD biomarker study that motivated this work and found that there indeed existed a baseline age corresponding to an accelerated rate of change in WM-hypo. Almost all methods yielded similar estimates from 59.19 years to 65.78 years, but the profile likelihood approach led to a much later estimate of 79.7 years. Specifically, the profile likelihood in Supplemental Fig. 2 indicated the existence of several local maxima, suggesting the possibility of multiple thresholds in baseline age that begs for further research in this direction. On the other hand, this finding is consistent with the simulation results that indicated extreme estimates could occur in our proposed methods, and larger studies are needed to best estimate the baseline age when the rate of change accelerates in WM-hypo. Nonetheless, the estimated age from 59.19 years to 65.78 years from most of our proposed methods is consistent with the visualization in the spaghetti plot (Fig. 1) and may provide age windows that longitudinal increase in WM-hypo among cognitively normal individuals started to accelerate, hence informing future prevention trials on the possible age-based inclusion/exclusion criteria, especially if WM-hypo is the target of the prevention. Specifically, because the estimated baseline age divided participants into two groups such that the older group had an accelerated increase in WM-hypo, in comparison to the younger group, the older individuals are at an elevated risk of AD and hence may be enrolled in future prevention trials. Finally, the proposed methodologies, while motivated by a real-world imaging study in AD research, could be applicable in a wide range of settings including biofluid biomarkers from cerebrospinal fluid and plasma across a spectrum of other neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., Parkinson disease, rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behavior disorder) that share age as a major risk factor.

Conclusion

We have proposed a novel longitudinal model that accounted for an accelerated rate of longitudinal change as a function of baseline age to address a real-world statistical challenge from a biomarker study of AD. We provided multiple estimation approaches and derived their CIs. Simulation studies and the real-world application indicated that these estimators performed reasonably well, especially when the true parameter is away from the boundary and the sample sizes are large. Further research is needed to tackle more complex challenges in this line of research. For example, our model only allowed a single threshold in baseline age, but the progression of AD spans over decades, and it is likely multiple thresholds may exist in the process. Further, major covariates, such as sex, APOE genotypes, and racial and ethnic groups are well established risk factors of AD [3, 33, 34], and may also affect the age when longitudinal changes in AD biomarkers accelerate. These extensions require larger sample sizes and more complex statistical models.
