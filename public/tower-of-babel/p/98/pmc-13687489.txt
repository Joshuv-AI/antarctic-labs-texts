Single Intratympanic Injection of Proprietary Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (OTO-413) in Subjects with Speech-in-Noise Hearing Impairment: a Randomized, Dose Escalating, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Phase 1/2 Study
Authors: Alan C Foster, Jeffery J Anderson, David R Moore, Morgan Oktela Fuentes, Yiwei Wang, Peter G Volsky, K Paul Boyev, Victoria A Sanchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the safety and tolerability and exploratory efficacy of OTO-413, an intratympanic-administered, sustained release formulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in a thermoreversible gel, in participants with speech-in-noise hearing difficulties.MethodsThis was a single dose, dose-ascending, randomized, double-blind, Placebo-controlled Phase 1/2 study conducted at 7 enrolling clinical sites in the U.S. 110 participants (56% female) enrolled had self-reported speech-in-noise (SIN) difficulties that were confirmed by a SIN test and were randomized to OTO-413 or Placebo. OTO-413 dose range was escalated from 0.01 mg to 1.5 mg within 7 Cohorts. Safety evaluations included monitoring for treatment-emergent adverse events, physical and audiological examinations, and monitoring for plasma BDNF and anti-BDNF antibodies. Exploratory efficacy outcomes included three SIN tests with the Digits-in-Noise Test (DIN), Words in Noise Test (WIN), and the American English Matrix Test (AEMT) and self-reported Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC).ResultsThere were no study drug-related serious adverse events, and no audiometric safety concerns at any OTO-413 dose. Plasma levels of BDNF were comparable to endogenous levels of BDNF and anti-BDNF antibodies were not detected. An exploratory responder analysis indicated a numerical advantage for OTO-413 versus placebo over the three months of observation. Improvement was particularly evident with the WIN at the 0.3 mg dose. No improvement in PGIC was noted.ConclusionThis exploratory study demonstrated that a single intratympanic injection of OTO-413 was safe and well tolerated and provided evidence for a SIN hearing treatment benefit of 0.3 mg OTO-413.Trial registrationNCT04129775.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10162-026-01061-z.Keywords: Clinical trial, Hearing loss, Intratympanic injection, Speech perception, Hidden hearing loss, Synaptopathy

Introduction

Hearing loss is a global health concern with over half a billion people affected worldwide [1], and is significantly related to decreased quality of life [2] and increased risks of age-related health conditions including cognitive decline and dementia, falls, and social isolation [3–6]. A primary complaint of individuals with hearing loss is an inability to hear speech in a noisy background, or speech-in-noise (SIN) difficulty [7], and is estimated to affect more than 20% of the adult population in the US [8]. There are no treatments and only limited technology-focused rehabilitation approaches for patients with SIN deficits [9, 10].

Several potential mechanisms have been proposed to underlie SIN difficulties, including central auditory deficits [11], dysfunction or loss of outer hair cells [12], auditory nerve demyelination [13], and cognitive deficits [14]. Recently, the loss of synaptic connections between inner hair cells (IHCs) and type 1 auditory afferents, termed cochlear synaptopathy [15, 16] has gained support as a pathophysiological mechanism that is a potential cause of SIN deficits. In preclinical studies, cochlear synaptopathy consisting of a loss of synapses formed between inner hair cells and the primary afferents of spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs) can occur after noise exposure that does not permanently alter auditory thresholds, resulting in so-called hidden hearing loss [17, 18]. Loss of afferent nerve fibers occurs in humans as they age and after noise exposure, and precedes a decline of IHCs and SGNs [19, 20]. It is correlated with poor word discrimination scores [20]. In animal models, the endogenous neurotrophins brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and neurotrophin-3 (NT-3) that are produced in the cochlea and are essential for primary afferent connections to be established during cochlear development [21] consistently show restoration of afferent nerve fibers and IHC afferent synapses after a variety of ototoxic or noise insults [22–27]. This led to the idea that a neurotrophin-based approach may be beneficial for the treatment of speech-in-noise difficulties, and OTO-413 was developed for this purpose [28]. Following the demonstration of efficacy in a series of ex-vivo and in vivo studies using cochlear models [29, 30], BDNF was selected as a clinical candidate and OTO-413 was developed as a sustained release formulation of BDNF in a thermoreversible gel, for intratympanic administration to human patients. Studies in rodent and non-rodent species indicated a sustained exposure of BDNF in the perilymph and cochlear epithelial tissue for several weeks after a single intratympanic administration of OTO-413 [30] with a highly favorable safety profile for administration to human patients.

Here, we evaluate the safety of OTO-413 and provide an initial exploration of its potential as a novel therapy for SIN impairment. We conducted a dose-escalating Phase 1/2 randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind clinical trial. The primary outcome was the safety and tolerability of intra-tympanically injected OTO-413 and secondary and exploratory analyses included the effect of OTO-413 on audiological measurements (e.g., speech perception in noise, patient-reported outcomes, electrophysiological measures). We report here a summary of the safety results (treatment exposure, treatment-emergent adverse events, audiometric evaluation), patient-reported impression, and speech perception efficacy outcomes that were measured across all cohorts in this first-ever exploratory randomized trial of an injected neurotrophin therapy for hearing impairment.

Materials and Methods

We conducted a dose escalating double-blind randomized controlled trial testing the effect of OTO-413 vs. placebo among patients with SIN hearing deficits. The trial was conducted in the US between November 2019 to September 2022. There were 7 enrolling study sites specializing in Otolaryngology and Audiology. All study procedures were approved by a central institutional review board (WIRB StudyID 1268728). Participants provided written informed consent. A safety review committee met for each dose escalation cohort to review study safety data and provide a recommendation for dose escalation. The protocol, outcomes, and analysis plan were registered at ClinicalTrials.gov ID = NCT04129775. This clinical trial followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT [31, 32]) reporting guideline along with the dose-finding extension guideline (CONSORT-DEFINE [33]) with the completed checklist available in Supplementary Material 1.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants eligible for screening were male or female aged 21 to 64 years, inclusive, with audiometrically defined normal hearing or up to moderately severe hearing loss (pure tone average of ≤ 70 dB HL at 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz) in at least one ear (study ear). Participants self-reported difficulty hearing in noisy environments for at least 6 months prior to screening. Participants also had to have a measurable SIN deficit in at least one ear (study ear; ≥ −12.5 dB signal-to-noise [SNR]), which was measured by Digits in Noise test (DIN, more thoroughly described below). Participants were native English speakers and scored ≥ 24 on the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE; [34]) indicating no cognitive impairment. Individuals with cochlear implants or currently using hearing aids were excluded, as were individuals with current or expected unavoidable intense noise exposure during the study.

Randomization, Investigational Product, and Intervention Delivery

After a participants met all prerequisites for randomization, study sites executed each randomization via the study’s interactive web randomization system (IWR). Randomization was assigned in the IWR using a permuted block randomization algorithm. Study sites were provided the information contained in the IWR randomization notification to the unblinded delegated study staff responsible for preparation of the syringe (OTO-413 or placebo).

OTO-413 is a proprietary formulation of BDNF in poloxamer 407 designed for intratympanic administration [28]. Poloxamer 407 is a triblock copolymer with thermoreversible properties that allows for OTO-413 to exist as a liquid at room temperature and then transition to a gel upon administration to the middle ear, thereby “fixing” BDNF at or near the round window membrane. BDNF concentrate is a clear to opalescent liquid, colorless to pale yellow in color. The diluents provided for dose preparation are clear, colorless solutions. The resulting OTO-413 drug product is a clear solution. The BDNF concentrate or Placebo was provided in individual Investigational Product kits along with a study detailed pharmacy manual of procedures. The BDNF concentrate was stored at −20*C until use, while the diluents and Placebo were stored at 2–8*C until use. Diluents were used to dilute the BDNF concentrate to the target concentration for each dose level. OTO-413 for intratympanic injection (0.2 mL volume injection to the study ear) was supplied as a concentrated solution and diluted to the appropriate dose level by an unblinded pharmacist or delegated and trained study staff. The following dose Cohorts were completed: Cohort 1: 0.01 mg (0.05 mg/mL) [3:1 randomization ratio of OTO-413 to Placebo], Cohort 2: 0.03 mg (0.15 mg/mL) [3:1 randomization ratio], Cohort 3: 0.10 mg (0.5 mg/mL) [3:1 randomization ratio], Cohort 4: 0.30 mg (1.5 mg/mL) [3:1 randomization ratio], Cohort 5: 0.30 mg (1.5 mg/mL) [2:1 randomization ratio], Cohort 6: 0.75 mg (3.75 mg/mL) [2:1 randomization ratio], Cohort 7: 1.50 mg (7.5 mg/mL) [2:1 randomization ratio]. The Placebo (16% P407 poloxamer solution) was administered as a single, 0.2 mL intratympanic injection across all Cohorts.

Intratympanic injection was completed by a blinded, study-delegated, site otolaryngologist who was guided by the protocol and detailed manual of operations. A 1 mL luer-lock sterile syringe was used with a 1.5 to 3.5 inches in length needle with a gauge between 25, 26, or 27 per otolaryngologist preference. The otolaryngologist anesthetized the tympanic membrane by covering the external surface of the inferior-posterior quadrant with a topical preparation containing lidocaine, prilocaine, or other related anesthetic (e.g., EMLA® cream or lidocaine spray or solution). The prepared syringe was inserted with the needle bevel facing in the inferior-posterior direction into the inferior-posterior quadrant of the tympanic membrane at the level of the round window. A ventilation hole in the tympanic membrane was not needed due to the small injection volume (0.2 mL). Participants remained in an upright sitting position for at least 10 min following injection.

Safety and Tolerability Evaluation

Safety and tolerability data were collected across study visits for all cohorts. Participants were monitored for treatment-emergent adverse events, and underwent routine physical examination with vital signs, clinical laboratory tests (hematology, serum chemistry, and urinalysis) and audiological examination with otoscopy, audiometry, and tympanometry. A Safety Review Committee comprised of an Otolaryngologist, the study’s Medical Monitor, and a clinical representative of the Sponsor, reviewed blinded safety data for dose level cohorts to ensure subject safety prior to escalating in dose. Concomitant mediations were recorded.

Pharmacokinetic Evaluation

Blood samples were obtained pre- and post-injection to measure systemic exposure to the active pharmacologic agent of OTO-413 (i.e., BDNF) and presence of anti-BDNF antibodies (anti-drug antibodies) in plasma.

Efficacy Evaluation

Efficacy measures included audiological measurements and patient-reported outcomes. Audiological measurements were taken across the Cohorts; however, as the study progressed some measurements were removed from the protocol due to efficiency (e.g., electrophysiological measurements). Measurements that were administered consistently across the Cohorts included speech perception assessments. There were three SIN hearing tests that differed with respect to speech and noise stimuli and procedural administration [see review in [35]], including a digits-based test (Digits-in-Noise test [DIN], [36], a monosyllable-word-based test (Words-in-Noise test [WIN], [37, 38], and a sentence-based test (American English Matrix test [AEMT] [39]).

The DIN is a hearing-in-noise test in which three spoken numbers (referred to as digit triplets) are presented at adaptively varying sound intensities through earphones, while a continuous, speech-shaped noise at a fixed level is presented synchronously [36, 40]. Using a web-based interface, participants listened to the digit triplets and repeated back what was heard. A threshold is determined from the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for 50% correct for whole triplets.

The WIN is a word recognition test that presents five Northwestern University Auditory Test No. 6 (NU-6) words, spoken by a female speaker in multi-talker babble background noise [37, 41]. The presentation level of the multi-talker babble was fixed, and the words are presented first at a louder level (easier SNR) then reduced every five words. Participants repeated back the word heard and the threshold was determined as the SNR at which word recognition performance is 50% as determined by the Spearman-Karber equation [42].

The AEMT is an adaptive SIN test using continuous, speech-shaped noise that is held at constant sound level as a target sentence level is varied from trial to trial [39]. The targets are grammatically correct but semantically unpredictable sentences with a fixed syntactic structure (name-verb-numeral-adjective-object, e.g., “Rachel wants four pretty chairs”). The sentences are pre-constructed by making random combinations of 5 words, one from each category of an inventory of 50 words. The low semantic predictability minimizes learning effects. Participants repeated back what they heard, and threshold was determined as the SNR at which word recognition performance is 50%.

The Patient Global Impression of Change [PGIC [43, 44] is a patient-reported outcome that evaluates the change in overall “global” hearing status as perceived by the subject. Participants were asked: “Since the beginning of the clinical study, how would you rate your ability to hear in noise?”. The beginning of the clinical study in this context is the time prior to investigational product administration. Participants selected one of seven responses (“very much improved [3]”, “much improved [2]”, “minimally improved [1]”, “no change [0]”, “minimally worse [−1]”, “much worse [−2]”, or “very much worse [−3]”).

Procedures

After signing informed consent, participants completed screening procedures to determine eligibility and provided demographic and audiological and medical history. After completing screening procedures, eligible participants continued to Baseline assessments (Day 1) which included assessments of both safety and efficacy outcomes.

Audiological assessments for safety were completed by study-delegated audiologist. Otoscopic examination captured any notable external ear, ear canal, or tympanic membrane abnormalities. Tympanometry was measured with 226 Hz probe tone with the calibrated equipment automatically varying pressure from + 200 daPa to −400 daPa. Tympanograms were noted for ear canal volume, peak admittance, and peak pressure with the clinician noting clinical significance or if the results were considered within normal limits. Audiometry was completed in a sound booth following standardized procedures ([45, 46]) and thresholds were obtained for both ears from 250 to 12,500 Hz. Eligible participants were randomized to a single intratympanic injection of either OTO-413 or Placebo after pre-dose assessments on Day 1 and observed for 12 weeks after the intratympanic injection.

Safety assessments were completed at Day 8, Day 15 (Cohorts 1–4 only), Day 29, Day 57, and Day 85 or upon early termination from the study. Audiological safety and tolerability examination measurements were collected from the study ear only. Plasma samples for pharmacokinetic (PK) analysis were obtained at Screening (pre-dose), Day 1 (1-h post-administration), and Day 8 for Cohorts 1–4. Plasma samples for anti-BDNF antibodies immunogenicity testing were obtained at Screening (pre-dose) and on Days 8, 29, and 85 for all cohorts (Cohorts 1–7). Efficacy assessments were captured for at least one or more time points at screening, baseline (Day 1), Day 8, Day 15 (Cohorts 1–4 only), Day 29, Day 57, and Day 85 or upon early termination from the study. PGIC was collected at Day 29, Day 57, and Day 85 through pen-and-paper administration. SIN tests (DIN, WIN, AEMT) were administered at Day 29, 57, and 85 in a sound booth following the administration guidelines for each test.

Analytic Plan

Data were monitored by blinded monitors and, after database lock, a blinded data review session was completed. PK results were summarized reporting first exposure results with plasma concentrations of BDNF and presence of anti-BDNF antibodies in plasma. Safety data including incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events are descriptively reported. Change in physical and audiological examinations from baseline was summarized descriptively. Additionally, audiometry was reviewed for each frequency (250–12.5 k Hz) and study-ear lower and higher pure-tone averages (PTAs) were calculated and reviewed over assessment days.

Efficacy data for this study were represented between two separate databases–one for Cohorts 1–4 and another for Cohorts 5–7. Descriptive summaries of efficacy data were categorized and presented by the Cohort and treatment they were randomized to receive. A responder analysis was conducted using change from baseline scores of ≤ −3 dB (DIN), ≤ −3.1 dB (WIN) and ≤ −2 dB (AEMT) as clinically-meaningful improvements in SIN performance, based on the literature and/or test performance [38–41], and were prespecified prior to unblinding of the data. Detailed analyses of SNR thresholds from the SIN tests were undertaken using data normalization to compare thresholds across tests (WIN, DIN, AEMT), longitudinally along test days (Days 0, 29, 57, 85), for participants receiving 0.3 mg dosing (Cohorts 4–5) with participants in the Placebo condition. To enable performance comparison between the three tests, SNR raw score thresholds were normalized relative to baseline (Day 0) Z-scores as determined for that test (i.e. Z = (SNR(DayX) – SNR(Day 0))/STDEV(SNR(Day 0)), where X = 29, 57 or 85). Finally, two-way repeated measures analyses of variance (using JASP software) compared raw data thresholds only on the WIN test, again between participants receiving 0.3 mg dosing and participants in the Placebo condition. “Day” was the repeated measure statistic (i.e. SNR threshold on Days 0, 29, 57, 85). No formal adjustments for multiplicity were conducted.

Results

Participant Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 110 patients were randomized across 7 sites over a period of 2.8 years (between Nov 2019 and Sept 2022). Figure 1 shows the participant flow diagram. Initially, each dose Cohort consisted of 8 participants receiving either OTO-413 or Placebo, in a 3:1 randomization. Cohort 4 (0.3 mg dose) was then expanded to an additional 7 participants (5 OTO-413, 2 Placebo). Following these initial cohorts, the 0.3 mg dose was examined in an additional 33 participants (Cohort 5) with an OTO-413:Placebo ratio of 2:1. Finally, two higher doses were examined (Cohort 6: 0.75 mg; Cohort 7: 1.5 mg) in an additional 38 participants with an OTO-413:Placebo ratio of 2:1. Table 1 indicates the number of participants randomized to each dose level Cohort. A total of 76 participants were randomized to and received OTO-413, and 34 participants were randomized to and received Placebo. Table 1 also reports the demographics captured at baseline. Participants’ mean age was 50 years (standard deviation [SD] = 11.6) and 56% were female. Most participants self-identified as being White (94%) and non-Hispanic (91%). In terms of educational attainment, over half of participants (58%) reported having a college degree and 42% had at least a high school diploma.

All participants self-reported difficulty hearing in background noise (inclusion criteria), but only 34% reported tinnitus and 63% reported some form of past noise exposure (Table 1). None of the participants self-reported their hearing ability as excellent, with majority reporting more than moderate trouble (Table 1). Table 1 also reports the study-ear PTA for lower frequencies [1, 2, 4 k Hz; 30 dB HL (SD = 16)] and higher frequencies [8, 10, 12.5 k Hz; 53 dB HL (SD = 23)], consistent with most having near-normal hearing to moderate hearing loss. Demographic variables and baseline hearing measures were consistent across treatment groups at enrollment (Table 1).

As can be seen in the flow diagram (Fig. 1), a total of 104 participants completed the study (one OTO-413 subject in Cohort 4 was lost to follow-up; one OTO-413 subject in Cohort 5 was lost to follow-up and one Placebo subject in Cohort 5 withdrew consent; one OTO-413 subject in Cohort 6 was discontinued by sponsor due to an adverse event; one OTO-413 subject and one Placebo subject in Cohort 7 withdrew consent).

Safety and tolerability

Adverse Events

The safety analysis dataset included all participants randomized and treated with OTO-413 or Placebo from all Cohorts (1–7). A single intratympanic injection of OTO-413 was well tolerated (Table 2). Blinded safety data were reviewed by the Safety Review Committee prior to each dose escalation and no safety concerns were identified by the committee. There were adverse events reported across the physical examination of systems and organs, with most of the adverse events associated with the study ear. The number and percentage of adverse events overall were similar between participants that received OTO-413 and participants that received Placebo. An adverse event appears in Table 2 if it was reported in at least 2 participants in the OTO-413 treatment group or 2 participants in the Placebo treatment group. There were two OTO-413 participants and two Placebo participants with events that were determined to be serious adverse events (SAEs), all considered not related to study drug, and resolved during the duration (summarized in Supplementary Material 2). One participant with a SAE discontinued the study due to the event (summarized in Supplementary Material 2).

Physical and Audiological Examinations

There were no clinically significant changes in vital signs (pulse, blood pressure), clinical laboratory measures, otoscopic examination, tympanometry, or audiometry in OTO-413 or Placebo participants. The incidence of abnormal otoscopic findings for the auricle/meatus, middle ear, and tympanic membrane in the treated ear was low in OTO-413 and Placebo participants at all timepoints, with no notable otoscopic findings after Week 8. There were no tympanic membrane perforations observed beyond Week 4 in any subject.

An important aspect of the safety evaluation of OTO-413 was to determine any potential detrimental effects on hearing sensitivity as measured through pure-tone audiometry. As shown in Fig. 2, OTO-413 showed no systematic difference from Placebo in terms of measured thresholds at frequencies from 0.25–12.5 kHz at 8, 29, 57 or 85 days after injection. The majority of excursions in mean thresholds fell within ± 5 dB compared to baseline values. The largest increase in mean values (worsening hearing) occurred in the 0.1 mg OTO-413 dose group at 10 kHz for the 8, 29, and 85 day timepoints. However, this was not evident at the one lower or four higher doses tested. Given the low number of participants in the group (N = 5) and the apparent change only at a single frequency, the result was likely spurious and considered to not represent an effect of OTO-413 on hearing sensitivity. Consequently, OTO-413 had no appreciable effect on hearing thresholds as assessed by audiometry over the dose and frequency range examined.

Pharmacokinetics and Anti-BDNF Antibodies

Endogenous plasma BDNF levels, as determined from pre-dose and Placebo samplings from Cohorts 1–4, fluctuated widely from 431 to 10,300 pg/mL. Supplementary Material 3 lists the exposure captured at baseline, Day 1 and Day 8 for Cohorts 1–4. BDNF levels measured in the different OTO-413 and Placebo treatment arms at 1 h post dose and 7 days post dose do not appear to differ from endogenous plasma BDNF levels. No evidence for anti-BDNF antibodies was observed in OTO-413-treated participants from all Cohorts at any of the intervals tested following treatment (days 8, 29, and 85; see Supplementary Material 4).

Exploratory Efficacy Outcomes

The analysis set included all randomized participants, treated with OTO-413 or placebo, who completed at least one post-dose efficacy visit (n = 68, Fig. 1). Efficacy data from cohort 1 (first and lowest OTO-413 dose evaluated) were not included due to technical issues encountered with the SIN testing procedures at the beginning of the trial.

SIN Performance

The focus of exploratory efficacy evaluations was performance in SIN tests, based on the hypothesis that restoration of inner hair cell synapses with neurotrophin therapy would lead to better SIN performance. Table 3 reports the speech recognition performance for the treatment groups and for each Cohort. No general trends across the groups, Cohorts, assessment days or tests are appreciated.

A responder analysis was adopted to evaluate the effects of OTO-413 versus Placebo. For the SIN tests, a change of ≤ −3 dB (DIN), ≤ −3.1 dB (WIN) and ≤ −2 dB (AEMT) from baseline values was considered as clinically meaningful improvements in SIN performance, based on the literature and/or test performance [38–41]. For this analysis, 7 participants were excluded (2 Placebo, 5 OTO-413) because they were lost to follow-up, withdrawn due to an adverse event, or withdrew consent. For comparison, Placebo-treated participants were pooled across Cohorts 2–7. Shown in Table 4, for the 0.03 mg and 0.1 mg OTO-413 dose groups, responders were observed, but the small number of participants in these groups (N = 5/6) precluded meaningful interpretation. For the 0.3 mg OTO-413 group the number of responders in the OTO-413-treated participants was also numerically higher than the Placebo group in all three speech-in-noise tests at all timepoints with the exception of month 1 for the AEMT (Table 4). To visualize the 0.3 mg data, Fig. 3 shows the percentage of responders for each SIN test across the assessment days (29, 57, and 85), comparing the Placebo to OTO-413 treated participants (Fig. 3a, b, c). There was a consistent numerical superiority of OTO-413 versus Placebo at Days 29, 57 and 85 in the WIN test where the responder rate in the OTO-413 participants was 26%, 39% and 42% respectively (Fig. 3). A chi square analysis revealed no significant difference between Placebo and 0.3 mg OTO-413 at any timepoint. The number of responders in the OTO-413 dose groups of 0.75 and 1.5 mg was less compared to the 0.3 mg dose, suggesting a potential “inverted-U” dose response relationship that has been noted previously with BDNF and other neurotrophins [47, 48].

Mean PGIC response scores across treatment groups were all between 0.3 and 0.7, relative to Day 0, with a small, non-significant increase across Days (Table 3). No difference was found between OTO-413 and Placebo-treated participants.

Discussion

We report the first-ever randomized controlled trial of intratympanic neurotrophic factor therapy OTO-413 (a sustained release formulation of BDNF) for potential SIN hearing impairment. OTO-413 (compared to Placebo) was safe and well tolerated, and a mild positive improvement in performance of the WIN (< 50% responders) was noted in one dose Cohort. Findings suggest that local intervention with a neurotrophic factor may be a safe and a promising therapy for SIN hearing impairment.

A single intratympanic injection of OTO-413 was safe and well tolerated with no deaths or treatment-related serious adverse events. There were four participants who experienced serious adverse events, none of which were study drug-related, and one of these participants discontinued from the study. Plasma levels of BDNF were comparable to endogenous levels of BDNF and anti-BDNF antibodies were not detected at any of the post-dose assessments. This is the first study that we are aware of to locally administer BDNF to the inner ear in humans; although there are no comparable studies, the safety profile is similar to other emerging potential BDNF neuroprotective and functionally restorative treatments in neurological and psychiatric disorders [49].

With no past clinical trials in this therapeutic area, there were no prior reports to guide the selection of speech perception outcome measurements; thus, the present study attempted to explore the performance of three somewhat similar SIN tests and better understand the use of these measurement tools for future clinical trials. Only the DIN test, instead of all three SIN measurements, was used for inclusion/exclusion criteria. The DIN was the fastest assessment (less than 3 min per ear), and had the potential to be scaled up for remote assessment or self-inquiry into the trial. For example, if this trial continued through FDA Phase II and III trials, there might have been an opportunity for patients to take the assessment at home on their smartphone or computer to encourage accessibility and effective recruitment. Versions of the DIN test are now widely available for self-assessment, such as the World Health Organization use of the hearWHO assessment [50]. The hearWHO can be administered remotely on commonly available digital devices, is available in multiple languages, including English and Spanish and has been successfully administered to almost 20,000 U.S. adults [51]. The DIN’s use of simple digit triplets keeps linguistic and memory demands low, minimizing dependence on dialect and reflective of more peripheral effects of hearing impairment [52].

Although the SIN construct was similar across the tests (DIN, WIN and AEMT), each test probed different but overlapping aspects of hearing in a noisy environment, and had other procedural differences. One favorable aspect of the WIN versus the adaptive tests (DIN and AEMT) was the ability to generate participants’ psychometric performance functions or speech intelligibility curves, providing a more complete picture of the participants’ perceptual performance across varying SNRs (examples provided in Supplementary Material 6). Furthermore, we also appreciated the test/re-test reliability of the WIN. Across all three SIN tests, some variability was observed between the thresholds obtained at screening and at baseline, prior to treatment. For WIN, the overall test/re-test between screening and baseline was highly correlated (r2 value of 0.96, Supplementary Material 7), in accordance with a previous report (Wilson and McArdle, 2007). However, it should be noted that in the sample size of 102 participants, 15 showed a variation of ≥ 4 dB between screening and baseline, a value greater than the criterion of −3.1 dB chosen to designate a responder in this test. These subjects were distributed across the groups (n = 3 in Placebo, n = 1 in Cohort 4, n = 7 in Cohort 5, n = 2 in Cohort 6, and n = 2 in Cohort 7). The reason for this variation in 14.7% of participants is unknown and likely could have been influenced by a variety of intrinsic or extrinsic factors, such as participant motivation to complete the task, variability in testing procedure from visit to visit, unreported changes in noise exposure as well as natural variability of SIN perception in some individuals. To lessen the impact of this in future trials, it is suggested that a lead-in period prior to interventional treatment be considered during which the WIN (or other SIN test) is administered more than once, and if significant variability is observed, use this as an exclusion criterion for the study. Of the three SIN tests, the WIN provided the clearest evidence for separation of responders to OTO-413 treatment versus Placebo (Figs. 3 and 4). It isn’t clear why the WIN alone among SIN tests revealed threshold improvements in the OTO-413 0.3mg treated group. The WIN having fixed stimulus levels rather than the adaptive (DIN and AEMT) could have made the WIN less sensitive. Another stimulus difference is that the WIN uses multi-talker babble noise masker rather than the less ecologically valid speech-shaped noise of the DIN and AEMT. The multi-talker babble noise influences more forward and backward masking effects and it is possible that BDNF plays some special role in the temporal processing of complex speech-in-speech hearing, but that hypothesis lacks any further basis to our knowledge. A recent National Academies Consensus Study Report on Meaningful Outcome Measures in Adult Hearing Health Care [53] emphasized the WIN as a test of choice for measuring SIN difficulties. In any case, these data support the idea that a 0.3 mg dose of OTO-413 provided modest improvements in SIN performance and that this was best reflected in the WIN test. These observations may aid future evaluations of locally administered BDNF, and inform strategies for the clinical development of a therapy for the treatment of SIN difficulties.

As this was the first clinical trial of BDNF among adults with speech-in-noise deficits, there are no other comparable clinical trials to align findings and discuss comparisons. However, as the state of science continues to develop in search of pharmacotherapeutics for auditory-vestibular disorders and dysfunctions, clinical trial standards will begin to emerge. In the present trial, we report potential evidence of SIN improvement on some but not all of the tests measured. In a clinical trial evaluating a small molecule potassium-gated ion channel modulator for speech-in-noise improvement, (AUT00063, ClinicalTrials.gov ID = NCT02345031), no change in speech-in-noise performance was observed using the Quick Speech in Noise Test (QuickSIN, [54]). Furthermore, in a clinical trial of intratympanic injected glycogen synthase kinase-3 inhibitor and valproic acid (FX-322) showed some evidence of speech perception improvement in a Phase 1b trial [55], but there was no effect of treatment on speech-in-noise performance, also using the WIN test, reported at the conclusion of their Phase 2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov ID = NCT05086276). In a recently completed randomized, double-blind, Placebo-controlled study evaluating the safety, efficacy, and pharmacokinetics of low and higher dose levels of SPI-1005 among patients with Meniere's disease, the WIN test was used as primary outcome comparing performance between baseline and 28 days of treatment (ClinicalTrials.gov ID = NCT05086276). The higher (400 mg BID) dose of SPI-1005 demonstrated improvements, with 75% of participants achieving ≥ 10% improvement and 60% achieving ≥ 4-word improvement. These are promising results of the WIN being a sensitive measure to detect changes in SIN performance across a range of patient populations. As more clinical trials for hearing are designed to evaluate novel therapies, the use of speech perception measurement tools should be guided by past trials [55, 56], recommendations [57], and selected based on properties of the therapeutic mechanism of action and study population [35].

The present trial being a Phase 1/2 was designed with initial exploration of outcome measures including electrophysiological measures (e.g., otoacoustic emissions, middle ear muscle reflexes, and evoked potentials). However, during the dose-escalation reviews decisions to streamline the protocol for operational efficiency removed these measurements across the Cohorts to reduce the burden on subjects. We report our largest exploratory data available, the SIN measurements, but encourage future trials to consider these other measurements as they continue to be further developed as biomarkers for synaptopathy and speech-in-noise impairment [17].

Similarly, due to the dose-ascending nature of the trial, there were small analytic samples for each cohort. As such, the power to assess efficacy at each dose level was limited. The number of responders and WIN SNR performance in the OTO-413 dose groups of 0.75 and 1.5 mg were less compared to the 0.3 mg dose (Table 4), suggesting that these higher doses led to a loss of OTO-413 efficacy. This may be consistent with an “inverted-U” dose-response relationship that has been noted previously with BDNF and other neurotrophins [47, 48]. Also, due to the small sample size and exploratory nature of this study, missingness was assumed to be at random with no sensitivity analyses conducted, and sex- and gender-based analyses were not conducted; future studies with larger cohorts are needed to examine potential sex and gender differences in the treatment of hearing impairment and SIN performance [58]. Overall, as efficacy was an exploratory outcome, and the study was not powered to allow for formal adjustments considering multiplicity of assessments, results should be interpreted as hypothesis generating, rather than confirmatory testing.

Another potential source of variability in therapeutic response was the characteristics of hearing impairment across the participants in the study. All participants enrolled had self-reported SIN difficulties that were confirmed with the DIN test; however, their hearing sensitivity based on audiometric thresholds varied widely from normal hearing at both low and high frequencies to moderately severe hearing loss at low frequencies and in some subjects even severe hearing loss at high frequencies. Despite this, responders to OTO-413 were present in participants that had normal through moderate low-frequency hearing loss and those with mild through severe high-frequency hearing loss. Supplementary Material 6 provides examples of responders with different audiometric configurations. Also stated on these supplemental individual highlights are the participants’ PGIC response. As seen in Table 3 where there is no difference found between treatment groups on the PGIC, and when looking at individuals that were considered responders only 4 of these 8 examples there are self-reported improvements on the PGIC that align with the measured SIN changes. One responder (0.03 mg dose group) was the closest to what could be termed “hidden hearing loss” (PTAs of 22 for low frequencies and 27 for high frequencies). Yet many other responders demonstrated moderate to severe hearing loss; another example in Supplementary Material 6 shows one subject that had PTAs of 47 for low frequencies and 78 for high frequencies. Consequently, no pattern emerged that could define responders to OTO-413 based on hearing thresholds. This may suggest that a neurotrophin therapy has the potential to improve SIN performance over a range of hearing impairments. This is in accordance with recent human postmortem data that indicates cochlear synaptopathy occurs across participants with age-related and noise-induced hearing loss [20].

bThe DIN is a hearing-in-noise test in which three spoken numbers (referred to as digit triplets) are presented at adaptively varying sound intensities through earphones, while a continuous, speech-shaped noise at a fixed level is presented synchronously. Using a web-based interface, participants listened to the digit triplets and repeated back what was heard. A threshold is determined from the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for 50% correct for whole triplets

cThe AEMT is an adaptive SIN test using continuous, speech-shaped noise that is held at constant sound level as a target sentence level is varied from trial-to-trial. The targets are grammatically correct but semantically unpredictable sentences with a fixed syntactic structure (name-verb-numeral-adjective-object, e.g., “Rachel wants four pretty chairs”). The sentences are pre-constructed by making random combinations of 5 words, one from each category of an inventory of 50 words. The low semantic predictability minimizes learning effects. Participants repeated back what they heard, and threshold was determined as the SNR at which word recognition performance is 50%

dPatients were asked “Since the beginning of the clinical study, how would you rate your ability to hear in noise?” and patients responded on a 7-point Likert scale, “+3 very much improved”, +2 “much improved”, +1 “minimally improved”, 0 “no change”, −1 “minimally worse”, −2 “much worse”, or −3 “very much worse”

Overall, this study indicated some evidence for a treatment benefit of 0.3 mg OTO-413, in participants with SIN hearing impairment. At this dose, there was a statistically significant numerical benefit on the WIN SNR threshold and an advantage of responders relative to Placebo controls in all three SIN tests across days 29, 57 and 85, with the exception of the AEMT test at day 29. This responder analysis was supported by the absolute change from baseline in SIN threshold for WIN at days 29, 57 and 85 for all OTO-413-treated participants (not just those designated as responders) which showed an improvement in SIN for OTO-413 versus Placebo (Figs. 3d and 4). Like the responder analysis, the change from baseline improvements in the WIN test for OTO-413-treated participants increased over time, reaching a mean improvement versus Placebo of 1.8 dB at day 85 (Fig. 3d). This suggests that the effects of OTO-413 on WIN scores increased over a 3 month period, consistent with a neurotrophin mechanism that reconnected synapses over time. However, since the results reported did not include physiological measures of synaptopathy, any interpretation is inferential and the precise mechanism of such an effect in subjects with SIN difficulties remains to be determined.

We report here a summary of the safety and tolerability results (exposure, treatment-emergent adverse events), and speech perception exploratory efficacy outcomes that were measured across all Cohorts in the first-ever randomized trial of an injected neurotrophic therapy for hearing loss. We hope that these data will be helpful in informing future clinical investigation of neurotrophin therapies for hearing loss and treatments for SIN hearing impairment.
