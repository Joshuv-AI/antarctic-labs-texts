Understanding Heterogeneity in Patients’ Conceptualisation of Treatment for Rheumatoid Arthritis: A Cluster Analysis
Authors: Betty Hsiao, Julie S Downs, Mandy Lanyon, Susan J Blalock, Jeffrey R Curtis, Leslie R Harrold, William Benjamin Nowell, Carole Wiedmeyer, Shilpa Venkatachalam, Mark T Patterson, Kelly Gavigan, Laura Stradford, Danielle Ali, Liana Fraenkel
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveUptake of treat-to-target (TTT) strategies for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) management is low. Our objective was to understand the heterogeneity in patients’ conceptualisation of RA treatment to inform interventions improving TTT uptake.DesignEligible participants recruited from an online research registry rated 56 items (on 5-point scales) reflecting concepts raised from patient interviews. Using items describing adhering to physician recommendations to create a binary criterion variable for medication adherence, we conducted a principal components analysis on the remaining items using Varimax rotation, describing how these factors predict adherence over and above demographic characteristics. We further use optimal sets in regression to identify the individual concepts that are most predictive of medication adherence.ResultsWe found significant heterogeneity in patients’ conceptualisation of RA treatment among 621 persons with RA. A scree plot revealed a four-factor solution explained 38.4% of the variance. The four factors expected to facilitate TTT uptake were (% variance explained): (1) Access to high quality care and support (11.3%); (2) low decisional conflict related to changing disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) (10.1%); (3) endorsement of a favourable DMARD risk/benefit ratio (9.9%); and (4) confidence that testing reflects disease activity (7.2%). These factors account for 13.8% of the variance in full medication adherence, fully explaining the only significant demographic predictor, age of the patient. The individual items most predictive of poor adherence centre on the lack of effective patient–physician communication, specifically insufficient access to information from rheumatologists, along with the need to seek information elsewhere.ConclusionPatients’ conceptualisation of RA treatment varies; however, almost all patients have difficulty escalating DMARDs, even with access to quality information and an understanding of the benefits of TTT. Tailored interventions are needed to address patient hesitancy to escalate DMARDs.

Introduction

Despite proven benefits and widespread endorsement, implementation of treat-to-target (TTT) strategies in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) in the USA is low, with most studies demonstrating appropriate escalation of treatment in less than 50% of patients with moderate to high disease activity.1–3 TTT strategies have been incorporated into real-world protocols and carefully tested in several recently published, high-profile studies.4 These protocols require selecting a disease activity metric, setting a target goal based on that metric and escalation of disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) for patients not at target. Commonly stated reasons for poor adherence to these protocols include discordance between rheumatologists’ and/or patients’ perceived disease activity and the composite disease activity score, patient comorbidities, medication lag (ie, the delay until DMARDs become fully effective) and patient reluctance.2 5 6 Of these, only patient reluctance represents a modifiable opportunity to escalate treatment. Therefore, in order to increase adherence to TTT, a better understanding of how patients conceptualise treatment for RA is required.

To effectively communicate the benefits and risks associated with TTT, interventions must reflect in-depth understanding of the complex influences on patient decision-making, such as culture, family, peers, media, social group, as well as cognitive and emotional factors. Using the Mental Models Approach to Risk Communication derived from decision science, we developed a mental model identifying ways in which patients’ conceptualisation of their treatment diverged from that of providers, based on in-depth qualitative interviews with patients with RA.7 Beyond the current and projected impact of RA on their function and quality of life, patients discussed numerous additional factors that influence their decision-making, including the impact of barriers (eg, insurance coverage, access to rheumatologists), the importance of feeling adequately informed by both their rheumatologists as well as other patients facing similar challenges and the significant trade-offs related to switching DMARDs. The objective of this study was to quantify the importance of each factor in the model and to identify the individual concepts that are most predictive of medication adherence.

Methods

Participants

Patients were recruited via email invitations for an online survey through ArthritisPower, a research registry created by Global Healthy Living Foundation (GHLF)/CreakyJoints and rheumatology researchers at University of Alabama at Birmingham.8–10 Eligible patients were at least 21 years of age, fluent in English, lived in the USA, reported having a diagnosis of RA made by a physician, were taking one or more DMARDs and were currently under the care of a rheumatologist. Patients participating in the survey were compensated US$25. We sent 16 321 email invitations each with a unique survey link; 4681 of emails were opened (28.7%) and 1354 of survey links were accessed (8.3% of invitations, 28.9% of opened emails), resulting in 804 completed online eligibility screenings. Of the completed screenings, 640 were eligible for participation and provided with a survey link. We received a total of 636 completed surveys. Four IDs were used in the eligibility screening multiple times with different responses and an additional three IDs were used to complete the survey multiple times. One survey was completed without an ID and could not be linked to the eligibility screener using time stamps. Because we cannot be certain which of these surveys was from intended participants in the GHLF network, 15 responses using these 8 IDs (2.4%) were omitted, leaving 621 responses.

Survey

We developed survey items to reflect the concepts that emerged in the patients’ mental model of RA treatment. Topics were identified by reviewing the content codes that were assigned during coding of interviews with patients.7 A subset of authors (JSD, BH, ML and LF) reviewed all assigned codes and quotations from participants associated with each topic, creating survey items that captured the core concepts. Item wording reflected the phraseology used by participants in the open-ended interview data, to best map onto how patients naturally discussed specific topics. Each survey item was written narrowly, avoiding double-barreled structures or contingencies. Survey items were reviewed, ensuring that each content code was represented by at least one survey item and that codes that were heavily represented in the patient model were reflected by one or more items in the survey corresponding to the nuances of the interview results. Cognitive interviews were performed with six consecutive patients with RA and the survey was modified between sessions to clarify wording. No additional recommendations were made by the sixth participant. Items were asked on a 5-point Likert-type scale, with labels corresponding to equal-spaced intervals to allow for parametric analyses.11 The final survey included 56 items: Difficulty in accessing care (6 items), experiences with getting information (4 items), feelings about RA medication (6 items), feelings about side effects of medications (4 items), feelings about changing medications (6 items), feelings about managing RA over long periods of time (6 items), worries about RA and medications (5 items), past treatment decisions (8 items), beliefs about lab work and imaging (8 items) and goals surrounding RA and its treatment (3 items). Finally, an open-ended item gave participants an opportunity to share any thoughts that were not covered in the survey. Answers to this item were previously reported elsewhere.7

Patient and public involvement

Patients were involved in the design of the survey and conduct of the study. The survey was modified after performing cognitive interviews with six patients with RA.

Statistical analysis

We combined two questions related to past treatment decisions (n=8) as a measure of medication adherence: ‘I take the medication that my rheumatologist recommends’, and ‘I decide to reduce or stop taking my RA medication without consulting my rheumatologist’. Participants responding ‘always’ to the first and ‘never’ to the second (55%) were considered consistently adherent. This single, binary item serves as our key criterion in a series of linear probability model regressions, transformed to the two values of 100=consistently adherent and 0=not consistently adherent to facilitate interpretation so that each unit is equivalent to one percentage-point increase in likelihood of treatment adherence. The predictors for each of these regressions are described below.

We first predicted our binary adherence outcome measure from the available demographic variables: age, sex, race (non-Hispanic white vs others) and education (4-year college degree vs not), labelling this ‘Model 1’. These results suggest the degree of predictability that a clinician may have from knowing just this basic information about a patient. We then conduct three additional regression models, each using this demographics-only model as a first stage, to determine how much additional predictive power can be gained from harnessing, (Model 2) the variance explained by a principal components analysis of the remaining 48 mental-models survey question, (Model 3) the most predictive individual items from that same set of 48 survey items and (Model 4) the most predictive individual items from the remaining six items from the past treatment decisions that were not used to calculate the adherence criterion variable.

In preparation for Model 2, we performed data reduction using the 48 mental-models items not directly assessing treatment decisions by conducting a principal components analysis on these survey items, using Varimax rotation. We report one decimal place for percentages and two decimal places for means and SD. We used these results to calculate factor scores, weighted by how heavily each item loaded onto its highest-loading factor. We then entered these factor scores of the mental models into a predictive model (Model 2), testing whether, and to what degree, the factor contributed additional predictive power beyond the demographics-only model described above.

For the final two predictive models, we conducted an optimal subset analysis using the leaps package in R to identify the individual terms that best predict medication adherence. Using a forward-step procedure, optimal subsets exhaustively compare all linear models of a common size using Bayes information criterion for model selection. We extract items in descending order of their predictive power, testing the additional predictive power of each additional item. As a threshold for when to stop adding predictors, we selected alpha=0.05 for the additional predictive power gained. Each of these analyses identifies a subset of items to be used in Model 3. We then conducted an optimal subset analysis using the six items from the past treatment decisions section that were not used to calculate adherence, using the same threshold, identifying a subset of treatment decision items to be used in Model 4.

Results

Participants characteristics

Participants (N=621) ranged in age from 22 to 93, with a mean of 57.68 years old (SD=11.50). Most participants (88.6%) identified as female, 11.1% as male and 2 (0.4%) as transgender. Most participants reported themselves to be non-Hispanic white (89.4%), the remainder as black (3.2%), Hispanic or Latino (3.4%) multiple races (1.4%), American Indian (1.1%), Asian (0.8%) and others (0.8%). Participants were relatively well-educated, with 6.6% reported stopping at a high school diploma, 38.2% some college, trade school or associate degree, 28.2% a bachelor’s degree and 27.1% a postgraduate degree.

Principal components analysis

A scree plot revealed that a four-factor solution explaining 38.4% of the variance would provide desirable interpretability for self-reported survey items, with a discontinuous drop in eigenvalues for additional factors slowly tapering and adding little discriminability between later solutions. Of the 48 items, 7 loaded at less than 0.40, but all were retained in the analyses. The four factors were: (1) Access to high quality care and support, (2) low decisional conflict related to changing DMARDs, (3) endorsement of a favourable DMARD risk/benefit ratio and (4) confidence that testing reflects disease activity. Predictive models

Model 1 predicts adherence from our available demographic measures, revealing that demographic measures alone do not significantly predict adherence and explain less than 1% of the variance (table 2). Patient age is only a reliable predictor and is relatively weak in magnitude, with each additional year of age accounting for about a half of a percentage point (0.47) greater likelihood of reporting full adherence with prescribed medication.

In Model 2, which added the four factors that emerged in the principal components analysis of the mental models, these mental-models factors add to the demographics to explain 13.6% of the variance. Age is no longer significant in this model. Notably, each of the four factors significantly predict adherence (table 2), with large effect sizes. For example, a 1 SD change in access to high quality care and support, holding other factors constant, is associated with an 11.4% point increase in likelihood of being consistently adherent. The other three factors have smaller but still substantial predictive power.

Discussion

In this study, we were able to identify patterns of patient characteristics reflecting variance on factors that may influence uptake of TTT. The means displayed in table 1 reflect some general tendencies across participants. For example, views regarding the role of DMARDs in RA treatment were relatively favourable, with high agreement on the need for and benefits of medication and little sentiment that RA medication is too risky.

Despite endorsing DMARDs as a critical component of treatment, about half of respondents described experiencing some hesitation in adhering to TTT. On average respondents expressed moderate or high levels of decisional conflict related to adding or switching DMARDs, hovering around the middle of the scale across many items tapping conflict and worry. This finding is consistent with the lower than recommended rates of TTT in clinical practice. For rheumatologists, the decision to escalate care is often straightforward. Rheumatologists struggle less with whether a patient with moderate to high disease activity should add or switch DMARDs; for them the benefits greatly exceed potential risk, and best practices offer clear guidance. Some patients, on the other hand, perceive this decision as involving difficult trade-offs involving possible increased pain while waiting for a new medication to work, uncertainty of response to new medication and the potential risk of new toxicities.7

Our results generate several important insights regarding adherence. At a general level, we describe four factors along which the mental-models concepts vary, each of which significantly and strongly predicts adherence with medication as prescribed, well above the limited power of demographics. Importantly, even though endorsement of a favourable DMARD risk-benefit ratio elicited generally high ratings across the sample, variance on this factor was the strongest predictor of adherence. Thus, even very slight deviations from full endorsement of the benefits of DMARDs may be associated with reluctance to adhere to TTT. When recommending treatment escalation, therefore, rheumatologists should be aware that even patients seeming to hold generally favourable views regarding DMARDs may be resistant to adding or switching medication, making such a persuasive case challenging.

A similarly strong predictor of adherence was access to high quality care and support. Importantly, overall responses to these items were less positive, suggesting more room for movement through intervention. Addressing structural and systemic barriers may serve as a better target to improve adherence not only because there is room for improvement, but also because these issues may be more tractable than increasing patients’ comfort with medication. Similarly, confidence that testing reflects disease activity was moderately predictive and similarly shows considerable room for improvement. Decisional conflict was found to be the weakest predictor and is likely the most intractable of the four factors, making it less attractive as a target for intervention.

We also identify the individual items most predictive of adherence, identifying a few key concepts that may serve as red flags for poor adherence. Notably, three of the four most predictive items in Model 3 come from the index on experiences with getting information, suggesting that patients who feel like they are given sufficient time to ask questions and information from their doctor, for example, do not seek out information by doing ‘research’ on their own, presumably on the internet, are more likely to adhere to the medication as prescribed by their doctor. From Model 4, the largest behavioural barrier appears to be cost and insurance, rather than concerns related to side effects. This finding underscores the tractability of systemic or structural barriers to care as another important target of intervention.

Among the factors predicting poor adherence, a general dislike of medication stands out and is likely difficult to counteract; however, it is instructive and suggests that it is not solely the long-term fears of the medication that appear to be driving the effect. Notably, insufficient access to information from the rheumatologist, combined with information gleaned elsewhere, are the very strongest predictors. These findings resonate with the patient mental model interviews in which we learnt there are distinct patient phenotypes: those who feel they lack effective communication with their rheumatologists in receiving sufficient information so they seek out information on their own as well as patients who feel they receive relevant information from their rheumatologists but are still hesitant to escalate treatment, citing the need to also hear about other patients’ experiences with medications prior to making a decision. We learnt that these patients have a need for information from two distinct sources, both accurate and current medical advice from their rheumatologists as well as treatment experiences from their peers, which was viewed as a critical component in their decision-making process that could not be satisfied by their rheumatologists.7 This underscores the need for additional information and decision support beyond what rheumatologists typically provide during a clinic visit in order to increase TTT uptake. Previously, decision support tools such as decision aids have been developed to help patients with RA engage with their rheumatologists in effective shared decision-making and make informed choices that are aligned with their values and preferences. There is also evidence that the inclusion of patient narratives, that is, testimonials that provide examples of the experiences that are relevant to the decision being made, may improve the effectiveness of the decision aid.12–14 Further research is needed in evaluating whether the use of decision aids/patient narratives will result in actual increase in TTT implementation.

Some caution is noted in interpretating this study’s findings. The factors that emerged from the principal component analysis only explained 38% of variance in survey responses. Because this descriptive approach aims to identify novel concepts relevant to decision- making, it will not produce analyses that explain the majority of the variance as might be the case in, for example, scale development of a highly specific construct. Rather, this approach aims to identify concepts deserving of further study, and to begin to characterise the population of patients with RA in meaningful ways that may guide further investigation and decision-making for clinical care.

There are several additional important limitations to our study. First, participants were recruited from an online patient community, thus they may be more likely to have strong information preferences and prefer taking an active role in their care. They may also be more experienced or have longer-standing RA diagnoses, although unfortunately we lack this information in our data set. Additionally, our sample consists of volunteers who responded to an email invitation to participate. The study participants were very well-educated and predominantly white and women. Thus, their views may not be generalisable to the greater RA population. The over-representation of women, may be partially attributed to sex differences in the prevalence of RA, but also to the increased likelihood of women opting into studies than men.15 The large majority of women may also have limited our ability to detect any impact of sex on adherence.

Conclusion

In this study, while we found that patients’ conceptualisation of RA treatment varies, hesitancy to escalate care is common even among patients who have access to high quality care and understand the benefits associated with DMARDs. Thus, interventions to increase TTT rates should tackle structural and systemic barriers to adherence and provide access to high-quality information that further complements the necessarily limited information communicated by providers. We are currently evaluating the value of patient testimonials specifically developed based on a patient model of RA7 as a decision support tool to decrease decisional conflict and facilitate treatment escalation among patients with RA who are hesitant to initiate, add or change DMARDs. Further research is necessary to understand residual variance not explained by our model.
