Hypothesis Generation via Interpretable Machine Learning: A Case Study on Risk Factors for Postradiation Therapy Lung Cancer Recurrence
Authors: Zheng Zhang, Sang Ho Lee, Rich Caruana, Harsha Nori, Yong Fan, Xingmei Wang, Nikhil Yegya-Raman, Charles B Simone, Gary D Kao, Steven J Feigenberg, Ying Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeInterpretability is highly desirable for oncologic outcome prediction, as it increases the level of transparency and trustworthiness of the model. This model characteristic is particularly relevant in the setting of modest sample size. Existing work has focused on Shapley Additive Explanations to provide post-hoc explanations on black-box models. These models are not intrinsically interpretable. In this study, we investigated the applicability of an intrinsically interpretable glass-box model, Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM), for hypothesis generation from an early-stage lung cancer data set.Methods and MaterialsWe applied EBM to a stripped data set on postradiation therapy lung cancer recurrence, aiming to extract as much information as possible by using pristine EBM configurations. We compared the key features ranked by EBM with those identified through univariate statistical analysis. Additionally, we benchmarked its performance against logistic regression and random forest models, while also evaluating the hypotheses generated by EBM. This study was approved by an institutional review board at the University of Pennsylvania.ResultsEBM identified primary tumor size and body mass index as the most prognostic features, aligning with the results of the univariate analysis. Its interpretability provides safeguards against misinterpretation; the model revealed potential age-related bias in this single-arm data set and possible confounding interactions between race and body mass index. EBM yielded competitive performances and more interpretable insights compared with logistic regression and random forest but was not immune from generalizability challenges arising from limited data.ConclusionsThe modest performance prevents EBM from being used as a clinical decision support tool, when applied to limited data. However, its interpretable, glass-box nature makes it useful for hypothesis generation.

Introduction

Background

Predictive analytics offers significant potential for patient-centered precision cancer care by refining clinical risk stratification,1 improving population health,2 and enhancing the value of care.3 In recent years, machine-learning-based methods to predict post-radiation therapy oncologic outcomes have become a mainstream analytical methodology, spanning a wide range of endpoints (eg, survival,4 treatment response,5 and recurrence risks6). The robustness of these methods hinges on the quality of data, particularly when the use of small data sets continues to be a common characteristic of preliminary radiation oncology outcomes studies.7 This raises concerns about model bias and challenges the trustworthiness of machine learning models.8

Interpretable machine learning is seen as a promising technique to address these concerns and challenges. Recent studies have highlighted the utility of interpretable machine learning in oncology broadly,9,10 and in radiation therapy specifically.11, 12, 13 Black-box models, such as gradient boost and random forest, were used in these studies, and Shapley Additive Explanation (SHAP) was applied to derive both global and local explanations. Although SHAP is a widely accepted method, it requires an additional step to examine a model, which may have already embedded data flaws.14,15

The Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM) is a novel glass-box model that is inherently interpretable,16,17 making it useful for detecting and analyzing flaws in data sets.18,19 It has been previously shown that, on large benchmark data sets where automatic pairwise feature interactions are applicable,17 EBM can even be as accurate as state-of-the-art techniques such as random forest (RF) and gradient boosted trees (XGBoost).20

Explainable Boosting Machine

EBM belongs to a broad class of Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) originally proposed by Hastie and Tibshirani21 in the 1980s. It takes the mathematical form of:

where xj and y are the training features and target, respectively. g is the link function that adapts the model to either classification or regression. β0 is a scalar value referred to as the intercept. fj is termed the shape function of feature xj.

GAM can be viewed as an extension of the generalized linear model, because the response function g(y) in the former model is the addition of a set of nonparametric functions fj of the independent variable xj, wherein each fj is allowed arbitrary level of complexity. This design fundamentally provides GAM enough flexibility to model complex phenomena, while retaining the interpretability of linear models. In a way analogous to logistic regression, the contribution of each feature xj is expressed as log odds, and the link function converts log odds to probability. It is worth noting that, in the edge case of all features being categorical, EBM reduces to logistic regression. In each iteration of training, every feature is learned sequentially. EBM purposefully uses a low learning rate to avoid the influence of the training order on the feature set. Many iterations are performed to reduce the effects of colinearity.

Compared with traditional GAMs, EBM incorporates modern machine learning techniques such as bagging and boosting to improve accuracy and allows pairwise interactions in the form of Σfij(xi,xj) as indicated below.

To enhance intelligibility, EBM prioritizes the univariate shape functions (fjs) over the interaction terms (fijs). In limited data regime, noise in the data set prevents extraction of useful information from the interaction terms. To evaluate the extent of its influence, we tested EBM using different number of interaction pairs (Figure E1).

Study objective

In this study, we explored the applicability of EBM to limited data sets for outcomes modeling. We modeled on a manually stripped data set of cancer recurrence after lung radiation therapy, with the goal of predicting recurrence risks of any kind at time of diagnosis. Our objective was to apply interpretable machine learning to learn as much as possible from a modestly sized data set, while assessing limitations that would arise from limited data.

Methods and Materials

Data set

We used data from a previously published clinical trial that examined serum biomarkers and circulating tumor cells (CTCs) for the early detection of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) recurrences after stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT).22 In this study, 92 participants with early-stage NSCLC were prospectively enrolled between August 2013 and October 2018. Individuals with a prior nonlung malignancy within the past 5 years, except for treated nonmelanoma skin cancer, were ineligible for enrollment. However, those with a previous history of NSCLC were eligible if the principal investigator determined their initial disease was cured. The trial validated that the CTC assay is prognostic for lung cancer recurrence. For our analysis, we excluded the CTC counts to focus on insights from the other features. Given the additive nature of features in EBM, this intentional exclusion would not affect the relative importance ranking of the other features, but it might negatively impact the overall model performance.

The treatment outcomes collected in this trial encompass local, regional, and distant recurrences, in addition to death. Recurrence events were identified through follow-up imaging, using computed tomography or positron emission tomography, conducted every 3 months for the first 2 years and at longer intervals thereafter. Recurrent disease was distinguished from metachronous primary tumors based on the criteria established by Martini and Melamed.23

Missing data handling and feature selection

All types of recurrence—local, nodal, or distant failure—were consolidated into a single category for analysis.

Eleven parameters were included for modeling: body mass index (BMI), age at enrollment, pack-years, primary tumor size, total dose in cGy, number of fractions, race, sex, smoking status, T stage, and histology. Among these, 5 were classified as categorical features: sex, race, smoking status, T stage, and histology.

In the original data set, some patients had missing CTC counts due to missed study timepoints and/or loss to follow-up. We excluded this parameter from modeling. Although this approach excluded a potentially prognostic feature, we believe this was appropriate to avoid bias arising from data imputation.

Model training

We trained 3 binary classification models: logistic regression (LR), RF, and EBM. The former 2 models were trained using scikit-learn (1.7.0).24 The EBM model was trained using the open-source package InterpretML (0.6.2).25 This package also provides other state-of-the-art interpretable learning methods under a unified application programming interface to improve robustness and ease of use.

For EBM, the most relevant training parameters were learning rate = 0.015, bagging = 14, max rounds = 50,000, L1 regularization = 0, L2 regularization =0, max leaves = 2, interaction = 0. We experimented with EBM-specific hyperparameters interactions and max_leaves and found the default parameter set to be suitable (Figs. E1 and E2).

Our EBM model takes 11 clinicopathological parameters as the input and produces the log odds of recurrence as a single binary output. For consistency, the same categorical/continuous classifications of the variables were applied in EBM as they were in the descriptive statistical analysis. EBM natively supports training of categorical features without manual encoding. However, to allow ease of cross-comparison with the other 2 models, we applied one-hot coding in the ascending order of predicted recurrence risks. For instance, T Stage was coded as: 1.0-IA, 1.5-IB, 2.0-IIA, 3.0-III. Details of the one-hot encoding scheme is provided in Table E1.

Model evaluation

We evaluated the model performance through cross-validation and external validation using a threshold value of 0.25 for the binary classification. This threshold was chosen by optimizing Youden’s index in the cross-validation. Receiver operating characteristic curves are shown in Fig. E3. For the former, we calculated performance metrics over 100 rounds of bootstrap resampling. In each round, a 75/25 train-to-test random split was applied. For the latter, the entire data set (n = 92) was used to build the models, and performance metrics were calculated by testing on 100 sets of bootstrapped samples from an independent data set. We reported the model performances as the mean and standard deviations among the bootstrapped rounds for all 3 models.

The independent data set for external validation is a retrospective cohort of 186 consecutive early-stage-NSCLC patients who underwent SBRT treatments at the same institution from June 2009 to July 2013, with a median follow-up of 4.0 years.26 In this data set, a total of 44 (24%) patients had recurrence, comparable with the training data set in terms of percentage.

In addition, we compared the important features ranked by EBM with those identified from the univariate statistical analysis. Working code is provided in Appendix E1.

Results and Discussion

Univariate analysis on prognostic factors

As shown in Table 1, the cohort includes 92 patients with ES-NSCLC who were prospectively enrolled and treated with SBRT between August 2013 and October 2018. The incidence of recurrence (n = 19) was 20.6% for the cohort, which is roughly consistent with the tumor control rate in RTOG 0236.27 A majority of patients in this recurrence group experienced more than 1 mode of recurrence (n = 13). More specifically, there were a total of 14 (15.2%) events of local failure, 13 (14.1%) events of nodal failure, and 12 (13%) events of distant failure.

On univariate analysis using a threshold of P ≤ .05, 3 variables were identified as significant predictors for recurrence: BMI (P = .0013), primary tumor size (P = .0010), and race (P = .0154).

Prognostic features from EBM

We plot the feature importance rankings bar chart in Fig. 1 based on the averaged feature importance scores as predicted by EBM over 100 independent experiments (ie, 100 different random seeds). The importance score for each feature is first normalized within each independent experiment, such that the most important feature has a score of 1. Subsequently, the mean and standard deviation of the normalized scores were calculated among the 100 experiments.

Average feature importance among 100 independent experiments using Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM). The error bar represents standard deviation.

In addition, we show the feature graphs in Fig. 2. The risk scores (upper subplot) and sample density distribution (lower subplot) for the 4 most prognostic features as seen in Fig. 1 are included. The scores of continuous features are represented as line plots, and those of the categorical features are represented as bar charts. Each graph represents the ensemble average of all the trees learned for its corresponding feature during iterative training. The uncertainty in the risk estimate is shown as gray shades or error bars, depending on the feature type and is inversely correlated with the density.

Risk scores and sample density distributions for the 4 most prognostic factors.

In both Figure 1, Figure 2, larger scores stand for higher risks of recurrence. We note that a risk score of zero does not infer zero risks, but rather the baseline established by all the rest features.

As seen in Fig. 1, the most prognostic features identified by the EBM model are largely consistent with the univariate analysis: primary tumor size, BMI, and race were among the highest ranked features.

Both approaches expectedly identified the primary tumor size as the most significant prognostic factor. This is consistent with previous literature showing primary tumor size being an independent prognostic factor for NSCLC.28, 29, 30 Furthermore, the EBM model reveals a monotonic and gradual increase in recurrence risks as function of primary tumor size (Fig. 2A), an insight that would be difficult to discover with descriptive statistics.

Both approaches also identified BMI to be the second most prognostic factor. Despite the univariate analysis showing a P value associated with BMI (P = .0013) similar to that with the primary tumor size (P = .0010), Fig. 1 shows a much larger standard deviation associated with the former, which warrants caution in interpretation. Indeed, previous studies on the effects of BMI as an independent prognostic factor for radiation oncology patients have been inconclusive: depending on the disease site, a high BMI may be either a positive factor,31 a negative factor,32,33 or has no impact on the treatment outcome at all.34 One notable difference with the cohorts in these literature studies is that the current study focuses on SBRT, which specifies high localization.35 Considering this context, as well as the EBM-generated feature score graph (Fig. 2B), which shows an increased recurrence risk as BMI increases, we hypothesize that the increased recurrence risk may be a result of increased daily localization variation among larger patients.36

The univariate analysis and EBM appear to disagree on the importance of age on recurrence. Fig. 1 shows age to be at least as important as race, even though it is found to be insignificant in the univariate analysis with a P value of .2834. The feature-specific graph in Fig. 2C may aid in our interpretation—even though this graph is largely a flat line, we see a decreased recurrence risk above age 85 years. This may suggest an age-related bias in this single-arm trial data set, which is likely related to a combination of differences in tumor biology and aggressiveness by age, differences in immune response to SBRT by age, and life expectancy and being able to live long enough to develop recurrences. This observation highlights the unique advantage of EBM in analyzing small, limited data set, where confounding by treatment is a common challenge.37

Finally, we examined race as a predictor for recurrence. A naïve interpretation of Table 1, assuming no correlation among the features, would conclude that race is a significant prognosticator. This represents a well-known pitfall of univariate analysis when multiple features are intercorrelated.38 The standard approach to address this limitation is via multi-variate analyses, which would require an upfront analytical plan. Since EBM heuristically handles the interactions between features, it opens an opportunity for easier discovery of confounding factors, with no upfront hypotheses—association between race and other features can be conveniently discovered by predicting race from all the other features. In Fig. 3, we show the feature importance rankings, where a higher score indicates a higher level of association with race. Figure 3 suggests race to be strongly correlated with BMI. Prompted by this observation, we calculated the mean and interquartile range of difference races and found White (26.1, 22.9-29.9) and Black (24.3, 22.4-29.5) patients to have lower BMIs than patients of other races (32.0, 30.29-33.8), which validates the EBM experiment.

Averaged feature importance among 100 independent experiments using (EBM), predicting the patient’s race from all other clinicopathological characteristics.

We note that this analysis on race should be interpreted within the context of this data set and study only. It serves primarily to demonstrate EBM's capability for identifying confounding factors rather than to draw definitive conclusions about racial disparities. Critically, our data set lacks social determinants of health (SDOH) variables (eg, socioeconomic status, educational attainment, healthcare access, environmental exposures, social and community context), which are necessary for understanding the complex relationships between race and health outcomes. Without these SDOH variables, EBM alone is unable to distinguish between the independent contribution of self-identified race and social factors that are not captured in our data set. Recent work has shown that incorporating SDOH into EBM can reveal more nuanced associations.39

We found that EBM generally outperformed LR during training and cross-validation, but slightly underperformed compared to RF. This performance gap between EBM and RF is interesting: although previous studies of larger data sets have shown that EBM achieved comparable performance with black-box models,20,40,41 this characteristic may not readily translate to the limited data regime. Furthermore, the slightly lower scores in cross-validation when compared with training may be attributable to overfitting. However, considering that this performance drop is on par with each performance metric’s standard deviation, the degree of overfitting was likely mild.

In the external validation, a rather significant performance decrease was observed for all 3 models. Interestingly, we noticed that this performance decrease in EBM is smaller than RF. While the eroded performance may be partly attributed to differences in the patient cohort and treatment practice patterns—the validation data set predated our main data set and marked the early and exploratory era of SBRT—this nevertheless provides a cautionary tale on generalizability of models developed in the limited data regime. This modest performance suggests that EBM is not suitable for clinical decision support, when applied to limited data.

Limitations

The data in this study originated from a single-institution, single-arm study. The sample size was modest (<100 patients). The prognostic factors uncovered in this work may not be generalizable. However, independent of this specific patient population and our study results, our primary objective was more generally to highlight EBM as an interpretable tool for hypothesis generation.

Conclusions

EBM achieved better performance compared with LR, but worse performance compared with RF, in a limited data regime. However, EBM by itself produces more interpretability insights than LR or RF. It identified primary tumor size and BMI as the most predictive features, aligning with our statistical analysis. In addition, EBM uncovered possible age-related and race-related biases in the data set. Considering the very modest performance in external validation, EBM trained with limited data is not suitable for clinical decision support. However, in the absence of a mature, big data infrastructure supporting predictive modeling, the interpretable and transparent nature of EBM makes it suitable for hypothesis generation from modestly sized data sets.
