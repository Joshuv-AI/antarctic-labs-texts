The role of habit strength in the theory of planned behavior for fruit and vegetable intake among women in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Rasha Alshaalan, Walaa Essam Alhassani, Sarah A Alasmari, Rowida Khader Allily, Nouf Abdullah Alharbi, Huda Wazzan, Rola Adnan Jalloun, Majd Jan, Mahitab A Hanbazaza, Nada Benajiba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow fruit and vegetable (F&V) consumption is prevalent among Saudi women, despite global health recommendations. To identify the psychological determinants of F&V intake using the theory of planned behavior (TPB) and to examine the moderating role of habit. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 467 adult Saudi women. The questionnaire measured TPB constructs—attitude, subjective norm (SN), perceived behavioral control (PBC), intention, and behavior—alongside habit strength using a validated self-report habit index. Structural equation modeling (SEM) tested the TPB model, and multi-group analysis examined differences across low, medium, and high habit levels. Only 22.3% of women met the WHO recommendation of ≥ 5 F&V servings daily, with significant variation by habit level (10.2% low-habit vs. 30.5% high-habit; p < 0.001). The high habit group showed significantly better knowledge of WHO recommendations (p = 0.013), healthier dietary perceptions (p < 0.001), and more frequent purchasing and preparation of F&V (p < 0.001). Physical activity also increased with habit strength (p < 0.001). Compared to the low-habit group, women in the high-habit group reported higher scores for all the TPB constructs (p < 0.001). SEM results indicated that the TPB model explained 31% of the variance in behavior and 75% in intention. In the overall sample, both intention (β = 0.32, p = 0.005) and PBC (β = 0.26, p = 0.032) predicted F&V intake. PBC was the strongest predictor of intention (β = 0.66, p < 0.001), followed by SN and attitude. Across habit levels, PBC consistently predicted intention; attitude was significant in the low-habit group, and SN in the high-habit group. Perceived control strongly influences both intention and behavior, but habit moderates these relationships. Interventions should enhance self-efficacy and establish regular F&V consumption among Saudi women.Keywords: Fruit and vegetable consumption, Theory of planned behavior, Habit, Saudi women, Dietary behaviorSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends consuming at least 400 g of fruits and vegetables (F&V) per day to promote health and reduce the risk of non-communicable diseases. This recommendation, originally established in 2003, remains current and is reaffirmed in contemporary WHO guidance1. The recommendation to consume 400 g of F&V each day was derived from dietary patterns seen in certain Southern European nations, where rates of chronic diseases tend to be low1. An individual serving is estimated at 80 g, leading to the general guideline of eating a minimum of five servings of F&V per day1,2. This recommendation, widely supported by initiatives such as the global “Five-a-Day” campaign3, reflects the growing evidence linking adequate F&V intake with a protective effect against chronic conditions, including cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, and stroke4, and with a low risk of all-cause mortality5. Furthermore, F&V play a vital role in supporting healthy weight management and decreasing the risk of colon cancer and depression6. However, global consumption patterns continue to fall short of this recommendation7. In 2017, 3.9 million deaths worldwide were attributed to inadequate F&V intake2. This fact highlights the urgent need for comprehensive public health strategies to bridge this gap and encourage healthier dietary behaviors.

To better understand and address this public health concern, researchers have increasingly applied behavioral theories. One of the most widely used models is the theory of planned behavior (TPB), which posits that health-related behaviors are influenced by attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioral control, and behavioral intentions8. A growing body of literature supports the TPB’s effectiveness in explaining dietary behaviors9. However, although TPB explains a substantial proportion of variance in behavioral intention, its ability to predict actual behavior is often modest10. This discrepancy, commonly referred to as the intention–behavior gap, suggests that while TPB is effective, it may not fully capture all determinants influencing dietary practices. Therefore, researchers have proposed extending the TPB by incorporating additional constructs to enhance its explanatory power in the context of health-related behaviors. In fact, habitual processes play a crucial role in dietary behaviors11, including F&V consumption, since these behaviors are frequently repeated in stable contexts and may become automatic over time. Habit strength reflects the extent to which a behavior is performed frequently and with little conscious thought, often triggered by environmental cues12. Integrating habit into health behavior models can improve understanding of why people maintain or fail to maintain healthful eating patterns. Hence, empirical research directly supports the importance of habit for F&V intake. Studies that include habit strength alongside traditional psychosocial predictors show that habit contributes to fruit consumption beyond intention and other TPB constructs13,14. In Dutch adults, habit strength significantly predicted fruit intake over time and explained variance in consumption in addition to TPB variables such as attitudes and intention15. Similarly, research using implicit measures found that individuals with stronger habitual fruit consumption had more positive automatic associations with eating fruit, indicating a robust connection between habit and fruit intake behavior16. Further, multi-group analyses in similar frameworks have shown that habit moderates the intention–behavior relationship: intention predicts fruit consumption when habits are weak or moderate, but not when habits are strong, suggesting that habit can override deliberative motivational processes17. Together, these findings indicate that habit is a distinct and influential determinant of fruit and vegetable consumption and support its inclusion alongside TPB constructs to better explain actual dietary behavior.

In Arab societies, including Saudi Arabia, women play a pivotal role in shaping family dietary habits. Traditionally and culturally, women are primary decision-makers in food purchasing, meal planning, and preparation, directly influencing the nutritional behaviors of spouses, children, and extended family members18,19. This influence extends beyond mere provision to shaping nutritional norms and beliefs, as mothers’ food practices and knowledge are key transmitters of dietary culture within the household20,21. Consequently, a mother’s own consumption behaviors, including her intake of F&V, are strong predictors of her children’s dietary habits and weight status22,23. Furthermore, the social and cultural context, where family cohesion and shared meals are highly valued, amplifies this influence, making women’s dietary choices a cornerstone for the family’s collective lifestyle24. Therefore, promoting healthy eating among women is not only an individual health imperative but a strategic intervention with a multiplier effect, capable of improving dietary quality across the household. However, despite this influential role, evidence indicates that Saudi women themselves consistently fail to meet recommended dietary guidelines. Studies show alarmingly low adherence, with only 1.4% of women attending primary healthcare centers meeting the ‘five-a-day’ recommendation and the vast majority consuming just 1–3 servings daily25. Alsunni et al. showed that 4% of female university students reported consuming five or more servings daily26. This critical disconnect—between their powerful influence on family nutrition and their own inadequate F&V intake—creates a significant public health leverage point. Addressing the determinants of F&V consumption in this population is therefore essential for designing interventions that can resonate through the family unit and effect broader dietary change.

Nevertheless, despite its proven utility, the TPB has not yet been applied to explore the determinants of F&V consumption specifically among Saudi women. Given Saudi women’s influential role in shaping family nutrition and the high prevalence of inadequate F&V intake among this population, there is a pressing need for theory-driven research to guide effective intervention design. Therefore, the present study aims to fill this gap by investigating the behavioral determinants of F&V consumption among adult Saudi women using the TPB framework. In more detail, this study aims first to assess the applicability of TPB constructs in predicting F&V among Saudi women, and then to explore how habit may influence the connection between the different constructs of the TPB and behavior. Such an exploration will offer insights that can inform culturally tailored interventions to promote healthier dietary habits—not only among women, but across entire families in Saudi society.

In addition to the primary TPB constructs and habit, this study assesses several key contextual and behavioral factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the environment in which F&V consumption occurs. Nutritional knowledge is measured, as awareness of dietary recommendations is a foundational prerequisite for forming accurate attitudes and intentions toward a health behavior and has been shown to correlate with healthier food choices and intake15,27. Furthermore, given that health behaviors often cluster, the frequency of physical activity is assessed to explore its association with F&V consumption, a pattern well-documented in the literature and indicative of a broader healthy lifestyle orientation28,29. Finally, variables related to food procurement and preparation (e.g., purchasing and cooking F&V) are included because they represent tangible, real-world facilitators or barriers that directly operationalize the concept of perceived behavioral control (PBC) and are critical for translating intention into actual behavior30,31. Analyzing these factors alongside the TPB model allows for a richer interpretation of the psychological predictors and offers practical insights for designing multi-component interventions that address both motivation and capability.

Methods

Study population and study design

A descriptive cross-sectional study was carried out throughout various regions of Saudi Arabia between May and October 2024. Women were eligible for inclusion if they were Saudi nationals, aged 18 years or older, and currently residing within the country. Individuals were excluded from participation if they were pregnant or breastfeeding, following medically prescribed diets, or diagnosed with chronic health conditions that could substantially affect their consumption of F&V. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of King Abdulaziz University (IRB Log No. 15-24). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All research methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Prior to enrollment, all participants provided informed consent, which was presented on the initial page of the online questionnaire. Participants received comprehensive information regarding the study’s objectives, their rights as voluntary participants, and the nature of their involvement. They were also informed that they could withdraw at any stage while completing the survey, without any repercussions. To protect privacy, no identifying personal data was collected, and all responses were anonymized and stored securely. The data obtained were used solely for academic research and were treated with strict confidentiality throughout the study process.

Sampling technique and sample size calculation

Participants were recruited through an online, self-administered questionnaire hosted on Google Forms. The survey link was disseminated via popular social media platforms, including WhatsApp and Telegram, to reach a large number of eligible participants. Due to the nature of the distribution method, the sampling method is considered a non-probability convenience sampling approach.

To estimate the sample size, an assumed prevalence of 50% rate of daily consumption of five servings of F&V was used, since no national estimates were available for Saudi adults.

Given the online mode of recruitment, a non-probability convenience sampling strategy was adopted. Previous findings from the 2013 Saudi Health Interview Survey indicated that 97.4% of Saudi adults consumed fewer than five daily servings of F&V32. However, these data are more than a decade old and may not accurately reflect current dietary patterns. In the absence of recent and nationally representative estimates, a prevalence of 50% was assumed33. This 50% value represents the expected population proportion (p) meeting the recommended intake of five daily servings of F&V.

The sample size calculation for a cross-sectional study indicated that at least 385 respondents were needed, using the adult Saudi population of approximately 13 million34. To address possible incomplete responses or participants’ dropouts, an additional 10% was added, resulting in a required minimum sample size of 422. Ultimately, a total of 467 participants completed the survey and were included in the final analysis.

Data collection tool

The study employed a structured questionnaire divided into five main sections14, as described below.

Demographic and background information

This section gathered key participant characteristics such as age, marital status, education level, geographic region of residence, and monthly income in Saudi Riyals (SAR). Participants also reported their height (cm) and weight (kg); these two parameters were used to calculate the body mass index (kg/m2).

Nutrition knowledge

Comprising two targeted questions, this section assessed participants’ understanding of nutrition. It specifically evaluated their awareness of nutrition information sources and their knowledge of the recommended daily intake F&V, based on the guidelines issued by the Saudi Ministry of Health35.

Lifestyle and dietary behaviors

This section included several items designed to explore participants’ lifestyle patterns, including frequency of physical activity, habits related to sharing meals with family members, typical dietary selections, and their involvement in purchasing and preparing F&V.

The TPB constructs

According to the TPB framework36, the Target, Action, Context, and Time (TACT) principle was employed to specify the behavior of interest: “consuming 5 servings of F&V daily over the upcoming week”, which is the recommendation by the Saudi Ministry of Health35 and WHO1. The attitude was measured using four semantic differential items evaluating participants’ experience of eating at least five servings of F&V per day during the next week (e.g., bad/good, enjoyable/not enjoyable, beneficial/unbeneficial, easy/difficult to digest). The SN was assessed with two items reflecting perceived social pressure, specifically whether family and friends expect the participant to consume five daily servings of F&V. Two items measured participants’ PBC to consume five servings daily in the next week, while the intention was evaluated with three items capturing the participant’s intent, certainty, and goal to achieve the five-serving target next week. For the behavior, the actual consumption was assessed by two questions addressing the number of servings consumed per day and the frequency of meeting the five-serving target in the past week14.

The habit of F&V intake

This section aimed to assess the habitual aspect of F&V consumption by utilizing a brief version of the self-report habit index (SRHI)12,37. The tool comprised four items representing key components of habit formation, including behavioral automaticity, identity relevance, emotional response, and integration into daily routines14. This measure helped assess the extent to which F&V consumption had become a stable, automatic behavior among participants.

Each item in the TPB constructs and the habit of F&V intake was rated using a five-point Likert scale, where responses range from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree). For each construct within the TPB a total score was calculated by summing the responses for each relevant item. These total scores were computed individually for each participant to quantify the strength of each psychological determinant associated with F&V consumption.

Face and content validity

The face validity of the questionnaire was evaluated by a panel of 11 experts in nutrition, public health, and psychology. The procedures followed established guidelines for instrument development and validation as described by Mokkink et al.38 and Ehrenbrusthoff et al.39. A similar expert-review methodology used in these studies was implemented. The experts revised the tool for clarity, comprehensiveness, and the estimated time required for completion. Face validity was assessed using a 4-point rating scale (1 = not clear/comprehensive at all to 4 = extremely clear/comprehensive), and experts were also asked to provide qualitative comments for each item. The evaluation revealed that 88.9% of experts rated the content as comprehensive and clear. The appropriateness of questionnaire length was evaluated on a scale of 10, with 1 not at all appropriate and 10 very appropriate. The average score obtained by the reviewers was 8.9 out of 10. They also provided qualitative feedback that was used to refine item wording and improve clarity.

For content validity, experts rated each item using a 4-point relevance scale (1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant, 3 = quite relevant, 4 = highly relevant), consistent with recommendations for CVI calculation40,41. The Item-level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) ranged from 0.82 to 1.00, while the Scale-level Content Validity Index/Average (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.933 (93.3%). The overall agreement rate was 97.1%, and the universal agreement method (S-CVI/UA) was 0.774 (77.4%). An I-CVI value ≥ 0.78 for panels of six or more experts and an S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 were considered indicative of excellent content validity, based on established methodological standards40,41. These findings therefore support that the instrument demonstrates strong content validity and is appropriate for assessing fruit and vegetable consumption among the Saudi population.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software. To explore differences in psychosocial constructs regarding F&V consumption across levels of habit strength, participants were defined based on tertiles of the mean index score, resulting in nearly similar group sizes17,37. The three groups were low habit (< 2.5, n = 137), moderate habit (2.6–3.24, n = 143), and high habit (≥ 3.25, n = 187). Splitting participants into groups based on habit strength (low, moderate, high) allows investigating whether the relationship between TPB constructs and F&V consumption differs depending on how automatic the behavior is17,37. Internal consistency across TPB constructs was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. To examine differences across habit strength groups (low, medium, and high), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for continuous variables that met normality assumptions. For all categorical variables, the Chi-square test of independence was employed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Initial analyses explored variations in TPB-related variables across the different habit groups and examined the correlations between key TPB constructs. To test the study’s hypotheses, structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed. Although the sample size was primarily determined for the main SEM analysis, post-hoc power considerations indicate that the achieved sample of N = 467 provides adequate power (≥ 0.80) to detect medium effects (f2 ≥ 0.15) in the overall SEM model42. For the multi-group moderation analyses, the subsample sizes (n = 137–187 per group) are sufficient to detect medium to large effects in the structural paths across groups but may be underpowered for small interaction effects43. This limitation was considered when interpreting non-significant findings in the subgroup analyses.

Model fit was evaluated using multiple complementary indices in line with structural equation modeling recommendations. Specifically, we examined the chi-square statistic (χ2) and its degrees of freedom, the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with its 95% confidence interval, and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Consistent with established guidelines, CFI and TLI values ≥ 0.90 indicate acceptable fit and ≥ 0.95 indicate good fit, RMSEA values ≤ 0.08 indicate acceptable fit and ≤ 0.06 indicate good fit, and SRMR values ≤ 0.08 indicate adequate model fit44,45. Given the sensitivity of χ2 to sample size, greater emphasis was placed on incremental and residual-based fit indices when determining overall model adequacy.

Maximum likelihood estimation was used to estimate all model parameters. To investigate the moderating effect of habit on the relationships between PBC, behavioral intentions, and actual behavior, a multi-group structural equation modeling (SEM) approach was employed. The analysis began with an evaluation of the baseline (configural) model separately within each of the three habit groups to establish a valid model structure across groups. This was followed by a simultaneous test to assess cross-group equivalence. Subsequently, measurement invariance testing was conducted to ensure that individual items operated equivalently across the different habit groups. Once configural invariance was confirmed, metric and scalar invariance were sequentially examined. These steps ensured that both the factor loadings and item intercepts were comparable across groups.

Following the confirmation of measurement invariance, structural invariance was tested by comparing factor covariances and structural path coefficients across groups. The assessment of invariance relied on multiple fit indices: the chi-square difference test (Δχ2), changes in the comparative fit index (ΔCFI) and Tucker–Lewis index (ΔTLI), as well as changes in root mean square error of approximation (ΔRMSEA) and standardized root mean square residual (ΔSRMR). Invariance was considered acceptable when Δχ2 was non-significant, ΔCFI and ΔTLI were both less than 0.01, ΔRMSEA was below 0.015, and ΔSRMR was under 0.02. This multi-step procedure ensured that observed differences in structural relationships could be attributed to meaningful variations across habit levels rather than measurement bias.

In Table 2 are presented the results of the knowledge, dietary practices, and physical activity of Saudi women (n = 467) in relation to their F&V consumption habit level. Social media was the most common source of nutrition knowledge overall, at 45.6%, followed by health professionals at 26.8%. Women in the high-habit group were more likely to report frequent activity (16.04% often, 4.81% always), compared with low-habit women, where almost half (44.53%) reported never engaging in physical activity.

Table 3 presents the reliability coefficients (Cronbach’s alpha), mean scores, and standard deviations of the TPB constructs, along with reported F&V consumption among Saudi women, categorized by habit level. All TPB constructs showed good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.72 for behavior to 0.92 for intention. There were significant differences across habit levels for all constructs (p < 0.001). Women in the high-habit group reported more positive scores compared to those in the medium- and low-habit groups. Attitude scores increased from 3.89 ± 0.68 in the low-habit group to 4.42 ± 0.51 in the high-habit group. A similar trend was obtained for SN (3.06 ± 1.00 vs. 3.87 ± 0.81) and PBC (3.15 ± 0.95 vs. 4.10 ± 0.70). Intention scores also increased significantly through the habit groups, from 2.82 ± 0.98 among low-habit women to 4.14 ± 0.62 among high-habit women. In terms of behavior, the high-habit group reported both more daily servings of F&V (3.74 ± 0.92 vs. 2.88 ± 0.80 in the low-habit group) and higher consumption frequency (3.32 ± 1.22 vs. 1.64 ± 0.81, respectively). Less than a quarter (22.3%) of women reported consuming at least five servings of fruits and vegetables daily, in line with WHO recommendations. This proportion varied significantly across habit strength levels (p < 0.001): it was lowest in the low-habit group (10.2%), intermediate in the medium-habit group (23.1%), and highest in the high-habit group (30.5%).

Prediction of the F&V consumption using the TPB

Table 4 summarizes the results of the SEM, examining predictors of behavior and intention across the total sample and by levels of habit of F&V consumption. For the total sample, the model explained 31% of the variance of the studied behavior (R2 = 0.31). Both intention (β = 0.32, p = 0.005) and PBC (β = 0.26, p = 0.032) significantly predicted behavior, indicating that higher intention and greater PBC were associated with increased consumption. In contrast, the predictive power of the model declined across subgroups. In the low habit group, only PBC was a significant predictor (β = 0.42, p = 0.015), while intention had no significant effect (p = 0.776), and the explained variance was modest (R2 = 0.20). In the medium habit group and high habit groups, the model explained 16% and 1% of the variance in behavior, respectively, but neither intention nor PBC significantly predicted behavior (p > 0.05). The intention model showed strong explanatory power in the total sample (R2 = 0.75), and among the low (R2 = 0.68), medium (R2 = 0.74), and high (R2 = 0.69) habit groups. For the total sample, PBC was the strongest predictor of intention (β = 0.66, p < 0.001), followed by SN (β = 0.18, p = 0.003) and attitude (β = 0.16, p = 0.008). In the low habit group, PBC remained a strong predictor (β = 0.59, p = 0.002), while attitude (β = 0.30, p = 0.013) was also significant; SN was not statistically significant. In the medium habit group, only PBC was significant (β = 0.78, p < 0.001), with attitude and SN not reaching significance. In the high habit group, again, PBC was a strong predictor (β = 0.60, p < 0.001), while subjective norm also emerged as significant (β = 0.38, p = 0.013), but attitude was not statistically significant.

Discussion

Few studies have used the TPB to explore the factors affecting F&V consumption among Saudi women. This study filled that gap by exploring how attitudes, SN, and PBC impact intentions and behaviors related to this dietary pattern. It also examined the impactful role of habit strength. Consistent with previous research, the findings showed that overall F&V intake among Saudi women remains below recommended levels25,46. Therefore, understanding the social and behavioral factors affecting F&V consumption is vital for creating effective and culturally suitable intervention programs. In fact, factors such as correct nutritional knowledge, family meals and eating habits, and physical activity can influence food choices47–50. In this study, when participants were stratified by habit strength, those in the high-habit group—characterized by more automatic consumption patterns—displayed significantly higher nutritional knowledge and healthier lifestyle behaviors, including physical activity, compared to those with weaker habits. Thus, these findings support the idea that these behaviors work together in maintaining a healthy lifestyle. Therefore, these interrelated behaviors should be considered in public health efforts.

The finding that women with stronger F&V habits were generally older suggests that age plays a role in creating stable, health-focused routines. Older adults often show more consistent eating habits due to their life stability, experience, and awareness of health51,52. As people age, they may focus more on preventing diseases and maintaining long-term wellness, which leads to better adherence to dietary guidelines53. Additionally, habit strength tends to grow when behaviors are repeated in stable environments, and such conditions are more common in later adulthood54.

Across the total sample, both intention and PBC significantly predicted behavior. This connection shows that stronger motivation and greater perceived control are associated with higher F&V consumption, which aligns with previous TPB-based dietary studies14. However, when analyzing the habit groups, PBC predicted behavior only among women with weaker habits. This observation emphasizes the moderating effect of habit strength: as F&V consumption becomes more automatic, behavior is less influenced by conscious intentions or perceptions of control. This pattern aligns with dual-process theories of behavior, which differentiate between reflective (intentional) and automatic (habitual) processes55,56. Strong habits may therefore bypass conscious regulation, highlighting the need for programs that encourage automatic healthy behaviors instead of relying solely on motivational strategies14,57.

In line with the TPB framework, this study found that attitude, SN, and PBC were significant predictors of the intention to consume F&V, confirming earlier findings14,58,59. Among these factors, PBC consistently appeared as the strongest predictor, even within the low-habit group. This finding suggests that confidence in one’s ability to obtain, prepare, and eat F&V is crucial for forming intentions. Prior research has identified PBC as the most important predictor of dietary intentions, overshadowing attitudinal and normative factors9,60. In Saudi Arabia, this might reflect cultural and environmental limitations affecting women’s choices in food purchasing and meal preparation. Although Saudi women traditionally handle household meals, their options can still be restricted by food availability, family preferences, and economic factors, all of which influence their sense of control18. Interestingly, SN significantly predicted intention only among women with high habit strength. In contrast, attitude was significant only among those with low habit strength. This suggests that as F&V consumption becomes routine, social influences may strengthen behavior through societal expectations. However, during early habit formation, personal attitudes and perceived benefits may play a larger motivational role.

An interesting finding was that SN significantly predicted intention only among participants in the high habit group. One possible explanation is that individuals who already exhibit stronger F&V consumption habits may be more responsive to perceived social expectations from significant others. Within the TPB, SN reflect perceived social pressure to perform a behavior8. Repeated exposure to supportive social environments may reinforce intentions and contribute to behavioral consistency over time. Furthermore, habit formation theory suggests that behaviors performed repeatedly in stable contexts become automatic and less reliant on deliberative processes12,56. It is plausible that social normative influence initially shapes intentional engagement in F&V consumption, and through repeated enactment, these behaviors become habitual. In this perspective, SN may indirectly contribute to the development of strong habits by motivating repeated performance of the behavior. This interpretation is also consistent with theories of internalization, which propose that socially endorsed behaviors can become integrated into one’s self-regulatory system over time61. Thus, habit may function as a potential mediating mechanism linking SN and sustained dietary behavior. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the present study, causal direction cannot be established. Longitudinal research is needed to examine whether SN prospectively predict habit formation and whether habit mediates the relationship between SN and future F&V consumption.

From a theoretical perspective, incorporating habit into the TPB framework in this study provides valuable insights into how both deliberate and automatic processes dictate eating behavior. Regular F&V consumption can help individuals manage motivational fluctuations, ensuring better long-term adherence to dietary goals13. Therefore, interventions should consider skill development—such as planning and budgeting for F&V—with environmental signals—such as establishing family mealtime routines and including F&V in these meals—to enhance both perceived control and habit formation. Culturally relevant strategies that support Saudi women in integrating F&V into their daily lives may lead to particularly lasting outcomes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, using self-reported data may lead to recall and social desirability biases, which can cause an overestimation of healthy behaviors. However, this limitation is common in TPB-based studies. Second, the online recruitment method likely attracted younger, more educated, and health-conscious participants, which limits the ability to generalize findings to the broader Saudi female population. Third, the cross-sectional design means causal inferences cannot be made. Therefore, the relationships between TPB constructs and behavior were interpreted carefully. Future studies could use longitudinal or experimental designs to explore how behavioral intentions and habits change over time and respond to interventions. Finally, this study also relied on self-reported dietary intake, which is subject to well-documented limitations inherent to food recall methods. Recall bias may arise from inaccuracies in memory, difficulties in estimating portion sizes, and telescoping effects, particularly when participants are asked to report habitual intake62. In addition, social desirability bias may lead to over-reporting of healthy foods such as fruits and vegetables and under-reporting of less healthy foods63,64. Therefore, the reported intake levels should be interpreted with caution, as actual consumption may be either overestimated or underestimated. Despite these limitations, the TPB model in this study showed good predictive validity, explaining 31% of the variance in behavior and 75% of the variance in intention. This supports its value as a framework for understanding health-related behaviors in specific cultural groups.

Conclusion

This study is the first to use the theory of planned behavior to explore fruit and vegetable consumption among Saudi women, focusing on how habit strength plays a role. The findings show that only 22.3% of women meet the recommended five daily servings, highlighting a serious public health issue. They also show that habit shapes how psychological factors interrelate. For women with low habits, perceived behavioral control and personal attitudes influence their intentions and behaviors. This suggests that interventions should focus on building confidence, practical skills like shopping and preparation, and positive attitudes towards fruits and vegetables. On the other hand, for women with high habit, conscious intentions and perceived control become less important. Their behavior becomes automatic, guided by environmental cues and SN. For this group, interventions should focus on maintaining supportive routines, such as family meals, and utilizing social influences rather than just motivational messages. These findings support dual-process models of behavior and stress the need for individualized interventions. Public health strategies in Saudi Arabia should be tailored for different habits: targeting conscious motivation and skill development for those with low habits, while reinforcing automatic behaviors and social settings for those with high habits. Since women play a crucial role in family nutrition, these approaches could significantly improve dietary quality in households. Future research should use long-term studies to establish cause and effect, apply objective dietary measures to lower self-reporting bias, and design and evaluate habit-focused interventions, examining habit as a direct predictor of behavior to better understand how routines, such as family mealtime practices, can support sustained behavior change.
