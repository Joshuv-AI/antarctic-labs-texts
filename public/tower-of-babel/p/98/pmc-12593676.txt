The Congenital Cardiac Diagnosis Translator (CCDT): Enhancing interoperability in congenital cardiac diagnosis terminology☆
Authors: Sarah Kübler, Ely Erez, Justin Cohen, Peter J Gruber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCongenital heart diseases (CHDs) are classified using diverse terminologies worldwide, creating barriers to effective communication and collaboration in both clinical practice and research. This project aimed to develop a tool to improve interoperability between commonly used CHD classification systems.Methods and resultsThe Congenital Cardiac Diagnosis Translator (CCDT) was developed by manually cross-matching diagnostic terms across six coding systems: Fyler, HPO, IPCCC, STS, ICD-10, and ICD-11. Terms were reviewed one system at a time. When an exact match was unavailable, the most specific encompassing diagnosis was selected; in cases where no suitable match existed, no translation was provided. Mappings were consolidated into a curated master lookup table. The CCDT was implemented as a web-based tool with a graphical user interface, enabling term-specific searches. Translations were qualitatively validated by clinical experts in congenital cardiology to ensure accuracy and relevance. The CCDT includes 505 unique congenital heart diagnoses cross-mapped across six coding systems. Qualitative feedback indicated that the translator is intuitive, accurate, and easy to use.ConclusionsThe CCDT provides a scalable, expert-informed solution for improving interoperability across CHD classification systems. By standardizing diagnostic terminology, it supports global collaboration and data sharing in congenital cardiology. The translator is freely accessible and will continue to evolve alongside diagnostic systems.Keywords: Congenital cardiac diagnosis, Interoperability, Terminology translation, CCDT, Clinical informatics

Introduction

Congenital heart diseases (CHDs) encompass a broad spectrum of often complex and rare conditions [1,2]. The heterogeneity and complexity of CHDs has led to the development of multiple coding systems across institutions and domains. Modern CHD nomenclature traces back to Maude Abbott, whose Atlas of Congenital Heart Disease [3] established the foundation for classification. These early efforts were followed by institution-specific systems such as the Fyler Coding System (FCS) [4], developed by Donald Fyler for a regional registry in New England. Broader standardization efforts began with the parallel development of the European Paediatric Cardiac Code (EPCC) [5] and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) [6] nomenclature, which ultimately led to the creation of the International Paediatric and Congenital Cardiac Code (IPCCC) [7,8] by the International Society for Nomenclature of Paediatric and Congenital Heart Disease (ISNPCHD). In parallel, administrative coding systems such as ICD-10 [9] and ICD-11 [10] were developed for statistical and billing purposes, while research-focused ontologies such as the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) [11] were introduced to support phenotypic annotation in genomic databases.

The coexistence of multiple, often non-interoperable systems has resulted in fragmented data, limiting cross-institutional research, hindering data integration into electronic health records, and complicating collaborative efforts across clinical, administrative, and research domains [12]. To address this persistent fragmentation, we developed a tool to enable interoperability across widely used CHD classification systems. Our solution, the Congenital Cardiac Diagnosis Translator (CCDT), allows translation across systems, supporting data integration, and global collaborative research.

Methods

Design and development

The CCDT was developed by a multidisciplinary team of pediatric cardiac surgeons, bioinformaticians, and trainees. The CCDT centers on a curated master lookup table that enables translation of diagnostic terms across six major coding systems: FCS, HPO, 2021 IPCCC, STS Short List version 3.3, ICD-10 and ICD-11. For each coding system both the diagnostic term and corresponding code were included. Cross mapping of the IPCCC, STS Short List, ICD-10 and ICD-11 relied on manual review of existing cross-references provided by the ISNPCHD [13]. FCS and HPO terms were manually mapped to the other four coding systems. When an exact match was unavailable, the most specific encompassing diagnosis was selected; if no suitable match existed, no translation was provided. In cases of one-to-many translations the translator provides all relevant terms in the target coding system. Entries were reviewed and refined by a senior pediatric cardiac surgeon to ensure semantic accuracy and alignment across terms.

Tool architecture

The CCDT is a lightweight web application built with an HTML-based user interface and a Python Flask backend, enabling efficient query processing. The interface features dropdown menus for selecting the source and target ontologies, along with a search box for entering the desired term. As users begin typing, the system dynamically generates a list of matching terms in the source coding system, supporting partial matches and abbreviations to enhance usability. Once a term is selected, the corresponding term from the target coding is displayed. The application works by querying the master lookup table that maps terms across ontologies. Designed for simplicity and efficiency, the interface presents results in a clear, organized format to support rapid, accurate translations.

Evaluation

To inform tool refinement, qualitative feedback was collected from clinicians and researchers during the Pediatric Cardiac Genomics Consortium (PCGC) meeting in 2023. Participants were invited to explore the tool using diagnostic terms relevant to their clinical or research work.

Ethical considerations

Informed consent was not required as the study did not involve human participants.

Results

The Congenital Cardiac Diagnosis Translator (CCDT) includes 505 unique congenital heart diagnoses cross-mapped across six coding systems and is a freely accessible tool at https://fyler.pcgcid.org. Feedback garnered at the 2023 PCGC meeting indicated that translations were generally accurate and contextually appropriate, particularly for commonly used terms. Users highlighted the tool's potential to facilitate cross-institutional communication and support dataset harmonization for collaborative research. Several suggestions, particularly regarding rare or institution-specific terms, were incorporated into subsequent updates of the lookup table.

Discussion

The CCDT addresses a critical need for interoperability among congenital cardiac diagnostic systems, representing a significant step toward global standardization in CHD terminology.

The tool's ability to map over 500 terms across multiple coding systems demonstrates its potential to streamline workflows and enhance the accuracy of cross-system data integration. Initial feedback from experts suggests that the CCDT meets key clinical and research needs, particularly for common diagnoses like ASD and TOF. These results underscore its practical utility in bridging gaps between classification systems, facilitating international collaborations, and supporting unified data analysis.

Nomenclature fragmentation has plagued CHD classification for decades [12]. This challenge motivated the formation of the ISNPCHD and the development of the IPCCC, intended as a unified coding system to prevent future interoperability issues [7]. This effort was further advanced through a partnership between the ISNPCHD and World Health Organization (WHO), culminating in the joint development of the CHD section of the ICD-11 [14], which now includes 367 CHD terms—significantly more than the 73 available in ICD-10. The resulting unified IPCCC ICD-11 Nomenclature marks the first time that clinical nomenclature for pediatric and congenital cardiac care has been harmonized with administrative coding systems. To further support interoperability, the ISNPCHD provides cross-mappings between the IPCCC, ICD-11, and the STS Short List [13], while the WHO maintains mappings between ICD-10 and ICD-11 [15].

Despite these advances, several challenges persist. The Fyler Coding System, which contributed to the development of the IPCCC and remains in active use at multiple institutions, lacks a formal cross-map to other systems. Similarly, the HPO classification system, widely used in congenital genomic research, does not map accurately to the IPCCC or ICD-11 [16]. In developing the CCDT we bridge these gaps, enabling direct translation between Fyler, HPO, and other commonly used CHD coding systems. Looking ahead, future improvements may include integrating additional terminologies and coding systems, such as OMIM.

Limitations

Translation mismatches may occur due to the differences in the specificity and classification across coding systems. Ongoing feedback from a wider range of users will be essential to refine functionality and ensure effectiveness in real-world applications.

Conclusion

The CCDT offers a practical solution to the long-standing challenge of inconsistent CHD terminology, enabling clearer communication and more reliable data integration across institutions. With ongoing development-particularly in expanding terminology coverage, improving translation accuracy and supporting multilingual access - it has the potential to become a valuable resource for both clinical practice and collaborative research in pediatric cardiology and cardiac surgery. Continued user feedback will be essential to guide its refinement and ensure broad, real-word applicability.
