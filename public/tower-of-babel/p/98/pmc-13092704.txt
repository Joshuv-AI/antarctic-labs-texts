Coordinated immune response distinguishes duration of SARS-CoV-2 viral particle shedding in humans

ABSTRACT
SummarySARS-CoV-2 acquisition induces a spectrum of COVID-19 disease severity and duration among people. Prolonged viral antigen shedding (PVS) is defined as when a person tests positive by direct antigen tests performed a minimum of 21 days apart. It is unclear why some people exhibit delayed SARS-CoV-2 viral antigen clearance. In this cross-sectional observational study enrolling vaccine naive adults from the USA and Peru following recovery from SARS-CoV-2 acquisition, we examined a suite of blood-derived immune measures to understand differences between participants with PVS (n = 36) and without PVS (non-PVS, n = 223). While PVS participants demonstrated lower overall SARS-CoV-2-specific antibody magnitudes, over-representation analysis revealed that immune feature magnitude alone could not fully explain cohort differences. Hierarchical clustering and network analyses identified more highly coordinated antibody responses, T cell activation, and Fc-mediated function among non-PVS compared to PVS participants. These findings link control of viral antigen shedding to a coordinated immune response.

Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) continues to be transmitted globally, raising concerns over the ability of future variants to subvert vaccine-elicited protection, even though it is no longer classified as a public health emergency.1,2 One striking feature of the early pandemic was the heterogeneity in symptoms among individuals who acquired the virus, ranging from asymptomatic cases to severe COVID-19 and even death. Early in the pandemic, PCR-based oropharyngeal swab tests, rapid antigen detection testing, and serological testing became standard diagnostic methods.3 Serial testing identified a subset of the population, ranging from 5.4 to 25.7% in prior studies of adults not vaccinated against SARS-CoV-2, who exhibited positive PCR/antigen tests for over three weeks.4,5,6,7 Ongoing research seeks to understand why a subset of adults continue to test positive for 21 days or more after their first positive PCR/antigen test [termed prolonged viral shedding participants (PVS)] while the majority of others convert to negative tests sooner [termed non-prolonged viral shedding participants (non-PVS)].

While prolonged shedding of viral antigens into the upper respiratory tract is no longer a significant concern for transmission in the general population, it may act as a barometer of immune system health, predisposition to persistent immune activation, or biasing influence on immune responses to future coronavirus infections. The literature reports many immune features contributing to prolonged or short viral antigen shedding. Most studies have focused on people with known immunocompromised states,8,9,10 concurrent infections,11 advanced age,5,12 and malignancies.13,14,15 Others have focused on testing during the symptomatic period following acquisition, where acute phase reactants and inflammatory cytokines may bias predictive modeling.6,16 Interestingly, the upper respiratory tract is just one of the many anatomic locations, along with the intestine, central nervous system, and skin, where viral proteins or nucleic acids have been detected.17 Furthermore, influenza and SARS-CoV-2 have been implicated in potentially detrimental immune imprinting, which opens the possibility of an expanded “exposome” experienced by people with prolonged viral shedding to tune future immune responses to SARS-CoV-2.18,19

Given the complexity of the immune response to SARS-CoV-2 acquisition, and the suite of immune features that can be measured with laboratory-based assays, one challenge is sorting the data into interpretable and testable signatures. Such computational analyses to understand critical immune signatures of “long COVID,” vaccination, and viral pathogens, including hepatitis C and influenza, have been previously published.20,21,22,23 A similar analysis is lacking for PVS following SARS-CoV-2 acquisition. In this study, we evaluated the humoral and cellular immune responses of convalescent PVS and non-PVS participants in the weeks after SARS-CoV-2 acquisition in order to determine the immune signatures associated with clearance of viral antigens. Computational approaches were applied in order to identify a minimum discriminating immune feature set, to identify clusters of correlated immune features, and to capture the coordination of those features distinguishing the two phenotypes. Results from this study serve to benchmark immune signatures in the convalescent period, provide a comparison for ongoing acute infection studies, and identify a subset of the population with a previously unrecognized, relatively uncoordinated immune response that may influence susceptibility to future viral infections.

Results

Enrollment of a multi-national adult cohort in the convalescent period

Three hundred and eighty-five adults (age ≥ 18 years) from the US and Peru were enrolled in HVTN 405/HPTN 1901 (NCT04403880) between May and October of 2020 based on self-reported positive direct viral detection test(s) (antigen or molecular detection) for SARS-CoV-2. Participants were enrolled 1–8 weeks or 2–10 weeks after diagnosis if symptomatic or asymptomatic, respectively. Importantly, participants were unvaccinated against SARS-CoV-2 and had not received any anti-SARS-CoV-2 monoclonal antibodies. Those participants reporting sequential positive tests at least 21 days apart were categorized as PVS (n = 36). All others were considered non-PVS and selected for inclusion to generate a well-matched comparison group (n = 223). PVS and non-PVS participants were well-matched for age (mean 44.3 years vs. 49.1 years, p = 0.060), sex assigned at birth (50% vs. 43.5% female, p = 0.584), medical comorbidities (hypertension, COPD/emphysema/asthma, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, immune system disorders, and HIV-1) and smoking habits (Table 1). Immunomodulatory drug usage was not different between groups (PVS: 11.1% vs. non-PVS: 16.1%, p = 0.598). No differences were found between PVS and non-PVS participants in known SARS-CoV-2 environmental exposures which could have prompted their initial testing (asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2: 38 (65.5%) non-PVS and 1 (100%) PVS, p = 1.000; symptomatic SARS-CoV-2: 58 (80.6%) non-PVS and 24 (82.8%) PVS, p = 1.000) (Table 1). Days since SAR-CoV-2 diagnosis, however, were significantly longer for PVS compared to non-PVS (median 64.5 days (IQR 50, 75.2) vs. 48 days (IQR 35, 64), p = 0.003). PVS participants demonstrated lower levels of SARS-CoV-2-specific antibodies with comparable neutralizing activity

Participants were evaluated for their SARS-CoV-2-specific serum antibody levels in order to assess persistent humoral immune responses in the weeks after initial diagnosis. Potentially influential characteristics, including peak COVID-19 symptom severity, age, sex assigned at birth, region, and days since SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis, were adjusted for in our comparisons of immune measures among PVS and non-PVS participants. Response magnitude of nucleoprotein (NP)-specific total IgG, N terminal domain (NTD)- and HexaPro stabilized (6P) spike-specific IgG1, and NTD-specific IgG3 above a positivity threshold were significantly lower among PVS compared to non-PVS participants (total IgG NP: GMR 0.56, p-value = 0.037, q-value = 0.111; IgG1 NTD: GMR 0.42, p-value = 0.011, q-value = 0.027; IgG1 6P: GMR 0.43, p-value = 0.002, q-value = 0.01; IgG3 NTD: GMR 0.52, p-value = 0.006, q-value = 0.03) (Figure 1A; Figures S1 and S2; Table S1). The degree of maturation of the polyclonal antibody response, measured as serum binding kinetic dissociations of receptor binding domain (RBD)-specific antibodies, from a subset of symptomatic non-hospitalized participants, was evaluated and found to be similar between the two groups (GMR 1.22, p-value 0.227, Figure S2). Additionally, the ability of antibodies to neutralize virus, was evaluated using a pseudotyped virus assay and ACE2 receptor blocking assay (Figure 1B; Figure S3; Table S2). In both cases, non-statistically significant differences were present between PVS and non-PVS participants (ID50 GMR 0.77, p-value = 0.238; ID80 GMR 0.78, p-value = 0.238; ACE2 blocking GMR 0.95, p-value 0.916). Endemic coronavirus responses are similar or higher in PVS compared to non-PVS participants

Prior studies suggesting that endemic coronavirus-specific antibodies may cross-react with SARS-CoV-2 antigens, leading to detrimental imprinting, provide a possible mechanism for altered viral clearance differences.19,24,25 In order to assess the impact of prior endemic coronavirus exposure on SARS-CoV-2-specific responses, binding magnitudes to four human endemic coronaviruses (229E, HKU1, OC43, NL63) RBDs were compared between PVS and non-PVS participants (Figure 1C; Figure S4; Table S3). Overall, the two groups were similar except for significantly higher binding response magnitudes detected in PVS compared to non-PVS participants for 229E-RBD- and NL63-RBD-specific IgG1 (229E: GMR 1.63, p-value = 0.039, q-value = 0.083; NL63: GMR 1.54, p-value = 0.042, q-value = 0.083).

Antibody-dependent effector functions are not significantly different between PVS and non-PVS participants

Antibody-dependent effector functions, including antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), are known to be important mediators of anti-viral immunity and sources of protection from re-acquisition by variants of concern.26,27,28,29 PVS participants demonstrated a significantly increased ADCP response rate (OR: 17.37, 36/36 vs. 170/203, p = 0.004, q-value = 0.016), but no significant difference in median phagocytosis score among positive responders (GMR = 0.92, p = 0.32), as compared to non-PVS participants (Figure 1B; Figure S5; Table S4). Next, two complementary methods for testing ADCC using SARS-CoV-2 infected cells (I-ADCC) and spike-transfected cells (T-ADCC) were tested (221 non-PVS, 35 PVS). While both demonstrated a trend toward lower ADCC among PVS participants, neither reached significance after adjusting for potential confounding factors. A spike-expressing cellular antibody binding assay (SECABA) tested the ability of antibodies to bind the budding SARS-CoV-2 spike trimer on the surface of infected cells. Though the response rate was significantly increased among PVS participants (OR: 14.86, 35/35 vs. 192/221, p-value = 0.009, q-value = 0.018), the magnitude among positive responders did not differ significantly from non-PVS participants (Figure 1B; Figure S5; Table S4). These results suggest that, at least during the convalescent period, SARS-CoV-2 antigen-specific antibodies do not elicit distinguishing levels of Fc-mediated effector functions based on prior viral antigen clearance duration.

PVS participants have increased SARS-CoV-2-specific B cell subsets but comparable SARS-CoV-2-specific T cells to non-PVS participants

Based on prior literature identifying SARS-CoV-2-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the majority of convalescent patients, linking CD4+ T cell responses to antibody levels and T cell clonal expansion to COVID-19 recovery, we next evaluated the T and B cell response differences between PVS and non-PVS participants.30,31 SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell response rates did not significantly differ between the groups for any peptide stimulation (S, N, E&M, ORF3a6, ORF7a7b8) (Figure 1D; Figure S6; Table S5). PVS participants demonstrated a trend toward increased frequencies of SARS-CoV-2 E and M-specific CD8+ T cells compared to non-PVS participants (GMR 5.47, p = 0.021, but q = 0.206), though the number of positive responders was low (6% vs. 9%). The highest frequencies of SARS-CoV-2-specific CD4+ T cells were to spike (non-PVS: 68%, PVS: 69%) and NP (non-PVS: 60%, PVS: 56%), while the highest frequency of CD8+ T cells was to NP (response rates for non-PVS: 53% and PVS: 61%). Frequencies of spike-specific IgA+, IgG+, and IgM+ total B cells (IgA+: GMR 1.78, p-value = 0.013, q-value = 0.076; IgG+: GMR 1.85, p-value = 0.02, q-value = 0.076; IgM+: GMR 1.56, p-value = 0.05, q-value = 0.081), spike-specific IgA+ and IgG+ memory B cells (IgA+: GMR 2.49, p-value = 0.005, q-value = 0.065; IgG+: GMR 1.97, p-value = 0.023, q-value = 0.076), and RBD-specific IgG+ memory B cells (GMR 2.25, p-value = 0.04, q-value = 0.081) were significantly higher for PVS compared to non-PVS (Figures 1E and S7; Table S5). Together with the humoral responses, these results support the conclusion that participants with PVS develop comparable SARS-CoV-2-specific T cells and increased class-switched B cells compared to non-PVS participants.

Computational analysis identifies differences between PVS and non-PVS participants, but suggests those differences cannot be explained by individual immune features

Understanding the immune features contributing to short and prolonged viral antigen shedding (PVS) has the potential to reveal critical components of viral clearance with application to future viral infection susceptibility and vaccination response. Given the complexity of the immune response and the large number of immune features measured, a multi-pronged computational analysis was applied to the systems immunology suite of data. To this end, 56 immune markers collected from each participant were visualized as a mean percentile of the total dataset and stratified by shedding status (Figure 2). Interestingly, mean percentile response magnitudes of spike- and RBD-specific IgM+ memory B cells, spike- and NP-specific CD8+ T cells, and endemic coronavirus-specific IgG1 and IgG3 were higher among PVS compared to non-PVS participants. At the global level, the distributions of the two high-dimensional feature sets were compared using a two-sample hypothesis test and found to be significantly different (direction-projection-permutation (DIPROPERM) test, p = 0.005). To quantify the separation between groups and determine the immune features most important in driving the shedding status differences, cross-validation classification modeling algorithms were applied to rank and down-select features. PVS could be distinguished from non-PVS participants with a cross-validated area under the receiver operator curve (CV-AUC) of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.84–0.93) based on increased spike-specific IgM+ memory B cells and spike-specific CD8+ T cells, and decreased RBD-specific total IgG antibody levels, 2P-spike-specific IgG1 antibody levels, NTD-specific IgG3 antibody levels, NP-specific IgA antibody levels, E&M-specific CD8+ T cells, and ORF7a7b8-specific CD8+ T cells (Figure 3). These results suggest that even though PVS participants tended to have higher frequencies of circulating class-switched spike- and RBD-specific memory B cells, they have lower class-switched serum antibody levels and structural protein-reactive CD8+ T cell responses following SARS-CoV-2 acquisition than those who promptly cleared the virus. Clustering and correlation-based coordination of immune features provides critical insight

In order to consider the potential coordinated movement of immune features, the high-dimensional data were first reduced and then evaluated for biased enrichment. Applying t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) dimensionality reduction revealed clear patterns related to response magnitude for -individual features based on shedding status (Figure 4A). Over-representation analysis revealed distinct clusters for PVS and non-PVS participants distinguished by response magnitudes (Figure 4B). Non-PVS participants were over-represented in both a low magnitude response cluster (top left) and a high magnitude response cluster (bottom left), while PVS were over-represented in a single cluster with a mid-range magnitude response. This finding suggests that viral clearance cannot be fully explained by immune feature magnitude/quantity, and that the quality of the response is also associated with this outcome. Clustering of immune features was next evaluated using orthogonal computational approaches in order to assess the coordinated response of multiple features across different segments of the immune system. The first technique was built on Euclidean distances and the McQuitty clustering method between 56 immune markers (Figures 5A and 5B).32 The bimodal distribution of correlations among non-PVS participants suggests a collection of highly correlated immune features moving together with values between 0.5 and 1.0 in addition to the lower correlations between −0.25 and 0.5 (Figure 5B). In comparison, PVS participants form a unimodal distribution centered at or below 0.5. An alternative way to present the coordinated movement of immune features is to plot correlation coefficients between pairs of single immune features from PVS and non-PVS participants on a contour map where each dot represents one pair. Dark green dots represent pairs of immune features for which the association decreased from high among non-PVS to low among PVS participants (Figure 5D). Next, the full immune feature list was consolidated into 12 immune feature groups (Figure 5G) based on dendrogram clustering (Figures 5A and 5B), with I-ADCC and T-ADCC intentionally binned together for biological interpretation, in order to test for correlational differences between PVS and non-PVS participants (Figures 5F and 5G). Binned groups 7, 9, and 10 were found to be significantly different between PVS and non-PVS participants, suggesting more coordination between CD4+ T cell features, NP-specific antibody features, and ADCC among non-PVS participants (Figure 5H; purple boxes represent p-value ≤0.05). Interestingly, endemic coronavirus-specific IgG3 responses (group 5) correlated strongly with SARS-CoV-2 IgG3 responses (group 8) for PVS in a manner distinct from non-PVS (Figure 5H). To understand which immune features correlated with others across the adaptive immune response, network interactions between immune features among PVS and non-PVS participants were evaluated using a Spearman’s rank correlation-based matrix (Figure 6). Immune features act as nodes, while the correlations between nodes are represented as edges. The magnitude of that correlation informed the metric of weighted degree, where higher values represent stronger correlation links between nodes. The highest 20% of connections were defined as “hubs” for the network and represent immune features with the most connectivity across the network, or immune system (Table 2). Both PVS and non-PVS participant hubs (11 immune features) were enriched for SARS-CoV-2 antibody measures (8/11 and 6/11, respectively) and neutralizing features (both ACE2 blocking and pseudovirus neutralization for both). This enrichment suggests the immune network of PVS depends more heavily on SARS-CoV-2 antibody measures than that of non-PVS. B cell features, endemic coronavirus antibody measures, T cells, and functional response (ADCP, I-ADCC, T-ADCC, and SECABA) were not enriched in either group, though non-PVS overall had a more diverse feature set of nodes. The average weighted degree of hubs was lower for non-PVS compared to PVS participants (19.56 vs. 24.50), suggesting a greater distribution of correlations across all features rather than a relative concentration among selected hubs. “Bottlenecks” describe nodes (immune features) that most commonly link other nodes, much like interstate highways link cities across a country, and govern the overall connection of correlations across the network. Non-PVS bottlenecks were over-represented for B cell features, with seven of the 11 features being B cell-related. Discussion

PVS following SARS-CoV-2 acquisition is found in a subset of the population and challenges our conventional understanding of protective immune features. PVS has previously been attributed to various factors, including sex assigned at birth, co-morbidities, COVID-19 symptom severity, and delayed or truncated immune responses to prolonged viral clearance.36,37,38,39,40,41 Employing a suite of immunological assays, including SARS-CoV-2-specific isotype and subclass antibody responses, endemic coronavirus antibody responses, antibody-dependent effector functions, and T and B cell responses, generating up to 56 immune markers for each study participant, we aimed to define a unique signature distinguishing viral antigen shedding status. Adult participants recovered from SARS-CoV-2 acquisition, prior to vaccination rollout, with prolonged (≥21 days) or non-prolonged (<21 days) viral antigen shedding were compared to identify both important individual immune features and coordinated elements of the immune response that contribute to shedding status.

Considerable heterogeneity exists in the literature regarding demographic and co-morbidity factors associated with persistent viral shedding.6,7,38,39,42,43 Previous studies have found PVS rates ranging from 5.4 to 25.7%, depending on the time during the pandemic when the studies were conducted and how participants were selected and tested. This encompasses the previously published rate of 9.28% among a larger pool of HVTN 405/HPTN 1901 participants from Peru and the USA, from which the participants for this study were drawn.4,5,6,7,44 While repeated positive direct viral antigen detection tests at least 21 days apart were used as our definition of PVS, our participant groups were considerably further removed from diagnosis [median 48 (non-PVS) and 64.5 (PVS) days]. Though that potentially leaves time for reinfection as opposed to persistent viral shedding, studies focused on pre-Omicron, pre-vaccination acquisitions, suggest the risk of reinfection within the first six months post-acquisition is less than 5% (or fewer than 2 false PVS participants in our population).44,45,46,47,48 PVS participants in our study were disproportionately recovered from symptomatic outpatient (80.6%) disease as opposed to COVID-19 requiring hospitalization (25.3%), while non-PVS participants tended to be higher acuity (32.3% vs. 55.6%, respectively). Our analysis controlled for peak symptom severity and hospitalization status due to prior reports linking prolonged shedding and the magnitude of immune responses to increased COVID-19 severity.16,43,49

PVS informs our understanding of underlying immune system function. Multiple studies have tested the kinetics of culturable virus from acute infection, with the majority of cases not exceeding two weeks post-infection.37,50,51 In our cohort, dichotomized by shedding detected at 21 days post-infection, prolonged shedding reveals either delayed clearance of viral debris from an extinguished infection or evidence of a smoldering infection out of reach of a nasal swab.17,52,53,54 Unlike cases of known immunosuppression with chronic SARS-CoV-2 infection and even intra-host genetic mutations,55,56 our study population lacked prior immunodeficiency diagnoses, which would favor the former over the latter possibility. Nonetheless, it would be informative to evaluate the immune responses to other pathogens or vaccinations in our PVS participants in order to understand if their delayed clearance is SARS-CoV-2-specific or a systems-specific response.

Total IgG, IgG1, IgG3, and IgA antibody responses specific to SARS-CoV-2 and endemic coronavirus antigens were analyzed at the time of enrollment in order to better understand the humoral immune landscape of persons with prolonged and non-PVS. Overall, PVS participants had a lower response magnitude, adjusting for symptom severity and days since diagnosis, across SARS-CoV-2-specific subclasses and isotypes, with IgG1 and IgG3 reaching statistical significance against all SARS-CoV-2 antigens tested and NTD, respectively. Yates et al.57 previously demonstrated increased SARS-CoV-2-specific antibody magnitudes with increased symptom severity at a mean of 41 days post-onset, and Karuna et al.58 previously demonstrated increased SARS-CoV-2 neutralization titers in the convalescent period with increased peak COVID-19 severity. Looking specifically at associations with PVS participants, Karuna et al.58 did not find a correlation between prolonged shedding and neutralizing titers, and Vibholm et al.7 found no differences in total SARS-CoV-2-specific IgG, IgA, or IgM by prolonged viral shedding status. Interestingly, when comparing a relatively small group of individuals very early in the pandemic who shed virus antigens for 36 days to a group who shed for only 9 days, Ao et al. found no difference in clinical symptoms or serum cytokine levels, but did note significantly decreased SARS-CoV-2-specific IgG and IgM levels among the PVS participants.59 However, as the threshold for hospitalization evolved throughout the pandemic and varied by clinical research site depending on the availability and accessibility of inpatient resources, differences in symptom severity categories may have influenced these findings. Future work exploring SARS-CoV-2-specific antibody magnitudes at the site of nasal swabbing, the nasopharyngeal mucosa, and other potential viral reservoir tissue sites may help to clarify the impact of antibody kinetics and location on prolonged shedding.

Antibody effector functions, especially in the case of non-neutralizing antiviral antibodies, are known to be a critical factor for disease progression in acute infection and for protection against variants of concern post-vaccination.29,60,61,62,63 In the convalescent period, however, our study demonstrated only trends, without statistical significance, in differences for ADCC and ADCP between PVS and non-PVS participants, even though PVS participants had a significantly increased response rate for ADCP. PVS participants were also found to have a higher response rate for binding to spike-expressing cells, which is the first step in facilitating innate immune cell engagement with an infected cell.64 The lack of differences in effector function response magnitudes suggests that viral antigen clearance likely does not exclusively depend on innate cell-mediated functions. These findings corroborate a recent Cambodian convalescent cohort from Vo et al., in which viral shedding duration did not correlate with humoral or cellular immune features out to 9 months post-acquisition.65 However, in that study, increased symptom severity led to increased ADCC 6–9 months later among all study participants, and our univariate analysis controlled for that symptom severity. In other pre-clinical and clinical studies, Fc-mediated effector functions tracked with post-vaccination humoral immunity and protection from viral challenge.62,66,67 Controlling for symptom severity and testing at a single time point during the convalescent period, as in our study, necessitates the consideration of effector functions in the context of other immune measures.

Inconsistent reports have emerged regarding the SARS-CoV-2-specific B and T cell frequencies among prolonged viral shedders.7,68,69 In the convalescent period, we found either similar or increased frequency of spike-specific total and memory B cells among PVS participants in comparison to non-PVS participants. The largest differences were seen for class-switched spike- and RBD-specific IgG+ and IgA+ memory B cells. Previous longitudinal studies following primary SARS-CoV-2 acquisition demonstrated that spike and RBD-specific class-switched memory B cells can continue to increase out to 180 days or more post-acquisition, whereas serum antibodies peak and then begin to decay around 30 days after acquisition.4,25 Thus, the disconnect between the higher frequencies of SARS-CoV-2-specific memory B cells among the PVS participants may be a result of prolonged antigenic stimulation of B cells, and potentially a defect in the generation of long-lived plasma cells during the prolonged viral shedding period post-acute infection.

Spike- and structural proteins E− and M-specific CD8+ T cell response rates trended higher among PVS participants in our study, though cell frequencies were not statistically different. More in-depth profiling of CD8+ T cell subsets by Yang B et al.43 and Kusnadi et al.70 identified an exhausted T cell phenotype among PVS participants, which was not measured in our study, suggestive of a less effective virus-clearing cytotoxic T cell population. Another study by Thieme et al. found no significant difference in CD4+ or CD8+ T cells based on symptom severity or shedding status at time of hospital discharge, though group sizes were small and classification as a prolonged shedder was less restrictive than in our study.71 From the classification analysis, we found that the final selection of 8 features discriminating shedding status identified decreased humoral responses and increased CD8+ T cell and memory B cell responses among positive responders to be predictive (mean CV-AUC = 0.89) of PVS status. While waning antibody levels are expected in the weeks after acquisition, the increased cellular responses are more aligned with increased antigen exposure previously demonstrated in studies of repeated antigen exposure from hybrid immunity or repeated vaccination.72,73 Prior literature is mixed, with studies finding either no correlation between shedding status and response magnitude across antibodies and cells,58,65 or increased exhausted T cells68,70 and increased CD8+ T cell magnitude and breadth with PVS.7 Inconsistent cutoffs for days post-diagnosis categorized as PVS, along with small sample sizes and heterogeneous testing time points, likely contribute to these differences. While we used 21 days as our definition of prolonged shedding, both groups were enrolled several weeks after that. Additionally, many of the features selected demonstrated small relative effect sizes or small numbers of positive responders on univariate analysis; NTD-specific IgG3 has the most convincing discrimination. As a result, our down-selected set of 8 features warrants future validation in an acute cohort with longitudinal monitoring.

Subsequent analysis identified qualitative features of the immune response to be important differentiators of prolonged shedding status. Non-PVS participants were found to be over-represented in immune clusters of both high and low overall magnitude immune responses, suggesting viral clearance was not fully explained by the magnitude of humoral or cellular responses. Rather, the quality and coordination of responses are likely critical characteristics. While increased COVID-19 symptom severity tends to track with increased response magnitudes into the convalescent period, less functional and less coordinated immune responses in the acute phase post-acquisition are known to favor more severe clinical courses.28,61,74 Additionally, an early, robust, and more highly coordinated immune response tracks with viremia clearance rather than persistence and more severe disease.67 Lastly, non-PVS participants have been shown to recover from either asymptomatic or symptomatic COVID-19 disease, even though more severe disease boosts the early response, supporting the idea that coordination rather than magnitude drives viral clearance.75 Non-PVS participants demonstrated more highly coordinated immune features than PVS participants, supporting the importance of immune arm interactions synergizing to clear viral antigen shedding. Non-PVS participants demonstrated a more heterogeneous set of hubs and the largest community composed of functional, humoral, and cellular features. Taken together, the immune response during the convalescent period among participants who promptly cleared viral antigens was multifaceted and more highly coordinated.

Clustering to understand larger coordinated features contributing to shedding status revealed a focused set of immune classes. Interestingly, CD4+ T cell features, NP-specific antibody features, and ADCC were significantly different in their correlations between non-PVS and PVS participants. The importance of a coordinated CD4+ T cell and serum antibody response for protection from reinfection is well established in SARS-CoV-2 and other viral diseases, including hepatitis C.21,76,77 This suggests non-PVS people mount a more coordinated immune response to counter lingering viral antigens. Additionally, among PVS participants, endemic coronavirus-specific IgG3 is strongly correlated with SARS-CoV-2-specific IgG3, suggesting a detrimental effect of prior antigen exposure and cross-reactivity. While this phenomenon of detrimental back-boosting of endemic coronaviruses has been shown for RBD-specific total IgG,19 the granularity of IgG3 in this study will require validation in future studies.

Findings from this paper provide a foundation for hypothesizing a model of how immune system coordination leads to prompt viral antigen clearance. Based on prior literature, early and robust IFN-γ-producing SARS-CoV-2-specific T cell responses associate with milder symptoms and quicker viral clearance.78,79 CD4+ T cells, in particular, coordinate with the B cell response to drive an antibody response capable of neutralizing or opsonizing the pathogen.80 The kinetics of this response play an important role with earlier responses tracking with less severe disease78 and being associated with immunosuppressed states.63,80,81 Interestingly, a sufficiently robust early IFN-γ T cell response leads to a lower humoral immune response, presumably due to less time or amount of pathogen stimulation of the immune response, which helps explain the bimodal immune response magnitudes found among non-PVS.78 A recent study from Li et al. on SARS-CoV-2 infection in people with histories of hematological malignancy or transplant, B cell suppression, or autoimmune disease without B cell depletion, concluded that suppressed B and T cell responses synergized for the longest shedding duration and most intra-host viral mutations.56 People with B cell deficiencies mounted lower antibody responses, higher T cell responses, and shed for an intermediate time, while people without immunosuppression more quickly cleared the virus.56 These findings suggest that the early coordination of the immune response is required to quickly clear viral shedding rather than shedding duration influencing coordination. While our convalescent timepoint data collection precludes the direct assessment of the early coordination of cellular and humoral immunity, the lingering coordination of immune system components among non-PVS supports the underlying early immune coordination, facilitating prompt and effective viral antigen clearance.

Limitations of the study

Our results have limitations due to the convalescent nature of study participants, including a survivorship bias and the inability to assess immune responses during active infection. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, no conclusions about humoral or cellular immune kinetics may be drawn, and conclusions are built on association without the ability to directly test PVS as a cause or effect of immune coordination. Direct viral detection testing for initial diagnosis (e.g., antigen or molecular test) was self-reported by participants at the time of enrollment as a categorical outcome, no viral samples were available for sequencing to determine SARS-CoV-2 variants or peak viral loads, and a lack of daily testing prevents the treatment of shedding as a continuous variable. However, enrollment dates fell during the period from May to October 2020, and therefore, D614G was the most likely circulating variant being transmitted (https://gisaid.org/hcov19-mutation-dashboard/). Further analysis of virus shedding duration as a continuous variable is planned in a follow-up acute infection cohort that collected these data. While this early variant and lack of vaccination may limit application to current global populations, this cohort was shielded from the ever-increasing complexity of SARS-CoV-2 hybrid immunity that rapidly developed in the months and years that followed.

In conclusion, adults recovered from SARS-CoV-2 acquisition and/or COVID-19 who were able to clear shedding viral antigens within 21 days demonstrated a more coordinated antibody response, T cell activation, and Fc-mediated antiviral function in the convalescent period. We deployed systems and computational immunology techniques to identify unique signatures among people with prolonged and non-prolonged SARS-CoV-2 viral shedding. The underlying etiology of the lower magnitude and less coordinated immune response among participants with prolonged shedding remains to be determined. Ongoing acute infection studies with longitudinal follow-up are needed to bolster these results and to confirm the application of clustered immune features in a broader range of antiviral responses. Knowledge gained from this study should inform clinical testing and public health decision making, as well as vaccine designs to minimize viral shedding related to SARS-CoV-2 and future viral epidemics.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

Study participants were enrolled from the USA and Peru. Assignment to the PVS group (n = 36) and non-PVS group (n = 223) was based on self-reported testing results. The PVS group was composed of 50% male participants and the non-PVS was composed of 56.5% males. The non-PVS group was generated to balance the groups when controlling for potential confounding variables such as age, sex assigned at birth, region, days since SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis, and peak COVID-19 symptom severity.

Plasma samples: Serum samples from study participants were obtained at the study enrollment clinic visit after obtaining informed consent. Seronegative samples used for positivity calls/baseline comparisons were obtained from commercial vendor BioIVT and balanced for sex of the sample.

Cell cultures

THP-1 monocytes, used for ADCP, were obtained from ATCC. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 cell media (Life Technologies +10% FBS (Cell Generation) + 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco, Penicillin/Streptomycin 10x)) in a T75 flask. Cells are maintained at 37°C, 5% CO2, and a density of 3 × 105–1.0 × 106 cells/mL. All samples were tested using cells from the same passage number and culture flask to limit variability. Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Vero E6 cells, used for I-ADCC, were cultured in MEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 μg/mL Penicillin and Streptomycin solution at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination.293T cells, used for T-ADCC, were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 μg/mL Penicillin and Streptomycin solution at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination.293F cells, used for SECABA, were cultured in Freestyle 293 expression media (Thermofisher) at 37 °C and 8% CO2. Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination.

293T cells, used for T-ADCC, were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 μg/mL Penicillin and Streptomycin solution at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination.

293T cells, used for T-ADCC, were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 μg/mL Penicillin and Streptomycin solution at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination.

293F cells, used for SECABA, were cultured in Freestyle 293 expression media (Thermofisher) at 37 °C and 8% CO2. Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination.

293F cells, used for SECABA, were cultured in Freestyle 293 expression media (Thermofisher) at 37 °C and 8% CO2. Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Vero cells, used for the VSV assay, were cultured in DMEM cell media (Gibco), supplemented with 10% FBS (Cytiva), Penicillin/Streptomycin (Gibco), 10 mM HEPES (Gibco), 1 mM Sodium Pyruvate (Gibco), 0.075% Sodium Bicarbonate (Gibco). Cells were maintained at 37oC, 5% CO2 and passaged every other day using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco). Cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Method details

Clinical trial information and study conduct

A total of 385 participants from the United States and Peru were enrolled between May and October 2020 during the 2–10 weeks following SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis as previously described.58 In brief, convalescent adults (≥18 years old) who had acquired SARS-CoV-2 were enrolled in the HVTN 405/HPTN 1901 observational cohort study (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier NCT04403880) led by the COVID-19 Prevention Trials Network (CoVPN). Two hundred and fifty-nine participants were selected for this sub-study, including 36 participants classified as prolonged viral shedders based on self-reported sequential positive direct viral detection tests (e.g., antigen or molecular test) at least 21 days apart. Detailed information on demographics, co-morbidities, and behaviors were collected at time of enrollment (Table 1). Samples were collected at the time of enrollment. Participants had not received SARS-CoV-2 vaccination or anti-SARS-CoV-2 monoclonal antibodies prior to sample collection.

Antibody measurements (IgG1, IgG3, IgA)

Antibody specificities and magnitude were measured by a Binding Antibody Multiplex Assay (BAMA) as previously reported.88,89,90,91,92 Briefly, antigens (see Table S6) were bound to NeutrAvidin-coupled fluorescent microspheres (MagPlex, Luminex Corp, Austin, TX) with a biotinylated rabbit anti-6x His-tag antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) to ensure the directional binding and the F′(ab) arm to be oriented outward. Antigen-bound microspheres were then incubated with human sera and controls, diluted in assay diluent, for 2 h at room temperature (RT) while being shaken. Subclass detection by a mouse anti-human IgG1 (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) or mouse anti-human IgG3 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) were followed by a goat anti-mouse IgG-PE (SouthernBiotech, Birmingham, AL) to detect bound IgG1 and IgG3, respectively. IgA detection by a goat anti-human IgA-PE (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA) was performed after serum IgG depletion with a protein G MultiTrapTM plate (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB, Uppsala, Sweden). Fluorescence readout of antibody concentrations were acquired on Bio-Plex 200 Systems (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). CR3022 IgG (kindly provided by Institute for Protein Design, University of Washington, David Baker), anti-SARS Nucleoprotein IgG (Cedarlane, Burlington, Canada), Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG) and normal human serum (NHS) (Sigma-Aldrich) served as controls. Commercially available SARS-CoV-2 seronegative samples (collected prior to November 2019) were used to establish isotype- and antigen-specific positivity cutoffs (95th percentile and ≥100 net MFI) (BioIVT, Westerbury, NY) at a 1:50 dilution. SARS-CoV-2-specific IgG1 antibody responses were tested at 1:50, 1:1 000, 1:10 000, and 1:25 000 dilutions, while IgG3 and IgA were tested at 1:50 and 1:250 dilutions. All samples, controls and standards were tested in duplicate, and the mean value was reported. Negative controls and uncoupled microspheres (blank) were included in each assay to ensure specificity. Levey-Jennings plots were used to track antigen performance across assays over time.93 Samples tested were n = 36 PVS and n = 223 non-PVS, based on sample availability and quality.

MSD four-Plex SARS-CoV-2 IgG binding assay

SARS-CoV-2 Spike-, S1 RBD-, and nucleocapsid-specific IgG in serum samples were quantitatively measured using the V-PLEX SARS-CoV-2 384 Panel 1 (IgG) kit as previously described,94 according to manufacturer’s instructions (Meso Scale Discovery (MSD), Rockville, MD). Briefly, pre-coated MULTI-SPOT 384-Well plates were blocked (Blocker A solution) for 1 h at 20-26oC. Plates were washed with MSD Wash Buffer and samples were added to the plate, tested in duplicate at 1:500, 1:10,000, 1:200,000, and 1:4,000,000 dilutions. Plates were washed after 4 h, and binding was detected using a mouse anti-human IgG conjugated to MSD SULFO-TAG. Following addition of MSD GOLD Read Buffer B, plates were read on a MESO SECTOR S 600 MM instrument. Sixty-six SARS-CoV-2 seronegative serum samples were tested at a 1:500 dilution and processed to establish the positivity cut-off (mean plus 3 standard deviations) (BioIVT, Westbury, NY). Magnitude of binding in arbitrary units per milliliter (AU/mL) was calculated at each sample dilution by backfitting to a 7-place calibration curve run in duplicate on each plate. The median AU/mL from all dilutions in the linear range of the curve was used to calculate the final AU/mL for each sample. Conversion to WHO/NIBSC International Standard Units of Binding Antibody Units (BAU/mL) was calculated with MSD units (AU/mL) × a conversion factor for Reference Standard 1 (Lot A00V004) (0.00236, 0.0272, 0.00901 for nucleocapsid, RBD, and Spike, respectively) available through MSD. Samples tested were n = 36 PVS and n = 223 non-PVS, based on sample availability and quality.

MSD ACE2 blocking assay

Antibodies that block binding of SARS-CoV-2 Spike to ACE2 were quantitatively measured using the V-PLEX SARS-CoV-2 Panel 2 (ACE2) kit according to manufacturer’s instructions (MSD, Rockville, MD). Briefly, SARS-CoV-2 spike-coated MULTI-SPOT 96-Well plates were blocked and washed as above. Samples were tested in duplicate at a dilution of 1:250. A 7-place calibration curve and blank well were run in duplicate on each plate as well as a positive control mutant ACE2 protein (4-fold, 4-place dilution starting at 6 μg/mL). Samples were incubated with human ACE2 protein conjugated to MSD SULFO-TAG, washed, and read as above. Seventy-two SARS-CoV-2 seronegative samples were tested at a 1:250 dilution and processed to establish the positivity cut-off (mean plus 3 standard deviations, after truncating all negative values to zero). Percent blocking for samples was calculated from the calibration curve using the following equation: (1 – (Sample electrochemiluminescent (ECL) Signal Mean - Calibrator 1 ECL Signal Mean)/(Blank well ECL Signal Mean – Calibrator 1 ECL Signal Mean)) × 100. Samples tested were n = 36 PVS and n = 223 non-PVS, based on sample availability and quality.

Biolayer interferometry (BLI) antibody binding kinetics

The BLI assays for RBD binding of serum antibodies of study participants were carried out using Octet Red 384 and Octet HTX instruments and NTA biosensors (Fortebio- Biologics by Sartorius, San Jose, CA). Both data acquisition and analyses were performed with the United States Food and Drug Administration’s Title 21 Code of Federal Regulations Part 11 (FDA Title 21 CFR Part 11) compliant software versions (Data Acquisition 12.0 and Data Analysis HT 12.0 packages). Briefly, recombinant SARS-CoV-2 RBD and negative control protein human serum albumin, HSA (Abcam) both containing a six-Histidine tag were loaded onto NTA biosensors with a threshold set to not exceed Δλ = 3 nm. The RBD and HSA loaded biosensors were re-racked onto different sensor trays and were kept soaked in phosphate buffered saline pH 7.4, PBS (ThermoFisher Scientific) filled in a 96 well plate placed underneath the sensor trays. The assay development for RBD binding of serum antibodies is detailed in Figure S9.

For RBD binding, study participants serum at 1:50 dilution in 1x kinetics buffer, positive control mAb CR3022 at 33.33 nM, negative control mAb CH65 at 33.33 nM and 1x kinetics buffer were plated in triplicate into tilted bottom 384 wells microplate (Sartorius). Reagents for equilibration (PBS), baseline and dissociation (1x kinetics buffer, Sartorius) steps were plated into appropriate wells. During binding assay, 96 NTA biosensors immobilized with RBD were equilibrated in phosphate buffered saline pH 7.4, PBS, dipped into wells containing 1x kinetics buffer for 300 s to obtain baseline followed by dipping into wells containing study participants’ serum at 1:50 dilution in 1x kinetics buffer for 300 s to measure association time course. The dissociation time course was collected for 300 s immediately after the end of association by dipping RBD immobilized biosensors back into the 1x kinetics buffer wells used for baseline step to enable inter-step correction. Binding response due to non-specific binding during baseline, association and dissociation steps was measured using 96 NTA biosensors immobilized with HSA dipping into same wells as the RBD immobilized biosensors. The RBD specific binding time courses were obtained by subtracting out responses from the HSA immobilized NTA biosensors using subtract by tray function in Data Analysis HT 12.0 (CFR11) after normalizing data from the last 10 s of the baseline step and applying inter-step correction. Kinetics analysis of the specific binding time courses yielded binding response (average of last 5 s of the association step) and dissociation rate constant (kd) obtained by fitting dissociation phase to a 1:1 binding model. Antigen specific positivity limit (mean plus 3.3 times the standard deviation of 1:50 diluted reference human serum binding response) and lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ; empirically determined antigen specific binding response above which dissociation rate can be measured reliably for standard monoclonal antibody CR3022) were applied in quality controlling of data. This involved ensuring that the percent coefficient of variation (%CV) in binding responses that are positive for a given antigen (response > antigen specific positivity limit) was <20 and the variation in dissociation rates was ≤2-fold for sera with responses greater than LLOQ. For correlation analyses and the summary values in all tables, positive responders with binding responses below LLOQ were assigned a dissociation rate of 1 × 10−2 s−1. The AUC of the dissociation curve (AUCdiss) was estimated for the specific binding time course data by using the trapezoidal rule in the R package ‘caTools’. Samples tested were n = 14 PVS and n = 67 non-PVS, based on sample availability and quality.

VSV neutralization

Neutralizing antibody ID50 and ID80 values of all samples were measured by the VSV pseudovirus neutralization assay described previously.29 Briefly, Vero cells were seeded at 2 × 104 cells/well in black-walled 96-well plates 24 h before the assay was performed. A 7-point, 3-fold dilution curve was generated with a starting sample dilution of 1:20. PsVSV-Luc-D19 (3.8 × 102 TCID50) were mixed with the plasma dilutions, incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 30 min and then transferred onto Vero cells. Cells were incubated for 18–20 h. Luciferase activity was measured by Bio-Glo Luciferase Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI) using a 2030 VICTOR X3 multilabel reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA). Percent neutralization was calculated by the following equation: [1 – (RLU with sample – RLU with uninfected cells)/(RLU with infected cells – RLU with uninfected cells)] × 100. Plasma from a subject with severe, PCR-confirmed COVID-19 collected following discharge from the hospital was used as a positive control. Pooled human serum collected in 2015–2018 was used as a negative control.

VSV-pseudovirus (PsVSV-Luc-D19) were prepared using a codon-optimized gene of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein (YP_009724390.1) cloned into pcDNA3.1, provided by Dr. F.A. Lempp (Vir Biotechnologies), and VSV(G∗ΔG-luciferase) system purchased from Kerafast (Boston, MA).30,31 VSV(G∗ΔG-luciferase) pseudotyped with SARS-CoV-2 spike was produced in 293T cells and stored at −80°C. Median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) was measured using Vero cells (catalog number CCL-81; ATCC) with serial 2-fold dilutions of the prepared pseudovirus.

Positivity calls were based on whether or not any neutralization was observed through the entire titration (starting dilution 1:20) with maximum percent inhibition (MPI) between 10% and 50%. For the positive calls where the neutralization did not reach 50% or 80%, ID50/ID80 titer was first estimated by Prism v. 9.3 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA) using four-parameter logistic curve. If the estimation failed, then ID50/ID80 titer was set to be 10. For negative calls (MPI<10%), ID50/ID80 titer was set to be 5 for graphing purposes. Samples tested were n = 36 PVS and n = 223 non-PVS, based on sample availability and quality.

Antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP)

Antibody-Dependent Cellular Phagocytosis (ADCP) was performed as previously described26,95 with the following modifications. SARS-CoV-2 HexaPro spike protein (83,84 was coupled to NeutrAvidin fluorescent beads (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA) and antibody immune complexes by incubating with human serum diluted at 1:50. The dilution factor was chosen from a 5-fold, 6 place titration series starting at 1:10. The 6P spike (HexaPro) was chosen based on its highly stabilized trimer conformation (six stabilizing proline substitutions) in comparison to the 2P spike.83 Monoclonal antibodies CV24 IgG1 and CV30 IgG1,96 and CR3022 IgG1 were added as positive controls while CH65 IgG1 served as a negative control.97 Immune complexes were incubated with THP-1 cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA), and cellular fluorescence (FI) was measured using a BD LSR Fortessa instrument (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). SARS-CoV-2 seronegative samples (collected prior to November 2019) were used to establish the positivity cutoff (95th percentile and 3x the median) (BioIVT, Westerbury, NY). ADCP scores were calculated using following formula: Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) × frequency of phagocytosis-positive cells, divided by MFI × frequency of bead-positive cells in a PBS control well. Samples tested were n = 36 PVS and n = 203 non-PVS, based on sample availability and quality.

Antibody-dependent NK cell degranulation assay (infected and spike-transfected)

Cell-surface expression of CD107a was used as a marker for NK cell degranulation, a prerequisite process for ADCC,98 performed by adapting a previously described procedure.64 Briefly, target cells were either Vero E6 cells after a 2 days-infection with SARS-CoV-2 USA-WA1/2020 or 293T cells 2-day post transfection with a SARS-CoV-2 S protein (D614) expression plasmid (kindly provided by Dr. Farzan, Addgene plasmid no. 156420,99). NK cells were purified from peripheral blood of a healthy human volunteer by negative selection (Miltenyi Biotech, Germany), and were incubated with target cells at a 1:1 ratio in the presence of plasma diluted 1:500, Brefeldin A (GolgiPlug, 1 μL/mL, BD Biosciences, NJ), monensin (GolgiStop, 4 μL/6 mL, BD Biosciences, NJ), and anti-CD107a-FITC (clone H4A3, BD Biosciences, NJ) in 96-well flat bottom plates for 6 h at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. NK cells were then recovered and stained for viability prior to staining with CD56-PECy7 (Clone NCAM16.2, BD Biosciences, NJ), CD16-PacBlue (Clone 3G8, BD Biosciences, NJ), and CD69-BV785 (Clone FN50, Biolegend, CA). The NK cells from the infected cell assay were incubated with 0.2 mL of 4% Methanol-free Formaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were then resuspended in 115 μL PBS-1% paraformaldehyde. Flow cytometry data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (v10.8.0, FlowJo, OR). Data is reported as the percent of CD107a+ live NK cells (gates included singlets, lymphocytes, aqua blue, CD56+ and/or CD16+, CD107a+). All final data represent specific activity, determined by subtraction of nonspecific activity observed in assays performed with mock-infected cells and in the absence of antibodies. The readouts of percent CD107a+ NK cells for the infected cells and the transfected cells were background subtracted. The positive responses were called if the values of background-subtracted percent CD107a+ NK cells were ≥4.07 and ≥5.27 for the infected and transfected cells, respectively. The cutoffs were determined by mean + 2SD, from the SARS-CoV-2 negative controls. Samples tested were n = 35 PVS and n = 221 non-PVS, based on sample availability and quality.

Spike-expressing cell antibody binding assay (SECABA)

The SECABA was performed as previously described.64 Briefly, the assay was performed using the 293F cell line to derive target cells by transfection with plasmids designed to express the SARS-CoV-2 D614 Spike protein with a c-terminus FLAG tag (kindly provided by Dr. M Farzan, Boston Children’s Hospital; Addgene plasmid no. 15642099). Mock transfected (no plasmid) cells were used as a negative control. After resuspension, washing and counting, 1 × 105 Spike-transfected target cells were dispensed into 96-well V-bottom plates and incubated with three dilutions (1:50, 1:500, 1:5000) of human plasma. After 30 min incubation at 37°C, cells were washed twice with 250 μL/well of PBS, stained with vital dye (Live/Dead Far Red Dead Cell Stain, Invitrogen, MA) to exclude nonviable cells from subsequent analysis, washed with Wash Buffer (1%FBS-PBS; WB), permeabilized with CytoFix/CytoPerm (BD Biosciences, NJ), and stained with 1.25 μg/mL anti-human IgG Fc-PE/Cy7 (Clone HP6017; Biolegend, CA) and 5 μg/mL anti-flag-FITC (Clone M2; Sigma Aldrich, MO) in the dark for 20 min at room temperature. After three washes with Perm Wash (BD Biosciences, NJ), the cells were resuspended in 125 μL PBS1% paraformaldehyde. Samples were acquired within 24 h using a BD Fortessa cytometer and a High Throughput Sampler (HTS, BD Biosciences, NJ). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo 10 software (FlowJo, Ashland, OR). A minimum of 50,000 total events was acquired for each analysis. Gates were set to include singlet, live, flag+ (Spike+) and IgG+ events. Binding to mock infected cells was measured using the live cell gate as there were no flag+ events. All final data represent specific binding, determined by subtraction of nonspecific binding observed in assays performed with mock-transfected cells. Samples tested were n = 35 PVS and n = 221 non-PVS, based on sample availability and quality.

Intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) assay

SARS-CoV-2 specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses were measured by intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) assay as previously reported.25,85,86,87 Briefly, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were stimulated for 6 h with synthetic SARS-CoV-2 specific peptide pools. SARS-CoV-2 Spike-derived peptides (316 peptides, plus 4 peptides covering the G614 variant) were synthesized as 15 amino acids long with 11 amino acids overlap and pooled in 2 pools (S1 and S2) for testing (BioSynthesis). All other peptides were 13 amino acids overlapping by 11 amino acids (GenScript). Peptides covering the envelope (E), membrane (M) and nucleoprotein (NP) were initially combined into one peptide pool, but most of the assays were performed using a separate pool for NP and one that combined only E and M. Several of the open reading frame (ORF) peptides were combined into two pools: ORF 3a and 6, and ORF 7a, 7b and 8. All peptide pools were used at a final concentration of 1 μg/mL for each peptide. The peptide diluent (DMSO) alone served as a negative control, and the polyclonal stimulant, staphylococcal enterotoxin B (SEB), served as a positive control. IFN-g and/or IL-2 was the primary detection endpoint for CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells. Responses were calculated as background-subtracted responses to each of the individual pools. Samples were called positive if a response to the peptide pool was positive. Several criteria had to be met to determine an assay to be acceptable for statistical analysis. Viability of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) must have been ≥66% after sample thawing and overnight incubation to proceed with testing. If criteria were not met, a new sample aliquot was thawed for testing. If the viability of PBMCs was again below the threshold, the ICS assay was not performed and the sample was excluded from testing and analyses. No negative control acceptance criteria were applied. If the number of CD8+ T cells was <5,000 or CD4+ T cells was <10,000 for any of the SARS-CoV-2 peptide pools or for one of the negative control replicates for a particular sample, data for that stimulation were filtered. If both negative control replicates failed for number of T cells, the sample was retested. If one negative control replicate failed for number of T cells, the negative control replicate with sufficient T cells was used. Data were collected on an LSR II or FACS Symphony flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) using the antibody panel in Table S7, and analyzed using Flow Jo version 9.9.4 (Ashland, OR). Samples tested were n = 36 PVS and n = 222 non-PVS, based on sample availability and quality.

B-cell phenotyping (BCP)

SARS-CoV-2-specific B cell responses and B cell phenotypic data were identified and characterized with fluorescently labelled recombinant 6P-spike (provided by Roland Strong, Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center, Seattle, WA) and RBD (provided by Leonidas Stamatatos, Fred Hutchinson Cancer Research Center, Seattle, WA) in combination with a flow cytometry antibody panel as previously described (see Table S8).25,84 The S6P probes were made at a 1:1 M ratio of trimer to spike antigen. Two S6P probes, one labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen), and one labeled with Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen), were used in this panel to increase specificity of the detection of SARS-CoV-2-specific B cells. The RBD probe was prepared at a 4:1 M ratio of RBD monomers to SA, labeled with R-phycoerythrin (Invitrogen). Cryopreserved PBMCs were thawed at 37C prior to staining.96 Cells were stained first with the viability stain (Invitrogen) in PBS for 15 min at 4°C. Cells were then washed with 2% FBS/PBS and stained with a cocktail of the three probes for 30 min at 4°C. The probe cocktail was washed off with 2% FBS/PBS and the samples were stained with the remaining antibody panel and incubated for 25 min at 4°C.

Stained and 1% paraformaldehyde fixed cells were collected on an LSR II or FACS Symphony flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data was analyzed using Flow Jo version 9.9.4 (Ashland, OR). Samples (PVS n = 35, non-PVS n = 223) were included in the analysis only if the number of memory (IgD-) B cells was ≥1000.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis details are described below, and data and results are included in figure legends and in the associated results sections.

Participant characteristics were compared between participants with prolonged viral shedding (PVS) and without PVS (non-PVS) using chi-square test for categorical variables and two-sided t test for continuous variables.

Association of PVS status with immune markers adjusting for confounders

For each immune marker, response rate and magnitudes among the positive responders between PVS and non-PVS participants were compared using the Firth logistic100 and log-linear regressions, adjusting for potential confounders (peak COVID-19 symptom severity, age, sex assigned at birth, region, and days since SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis). Q-values were calculated adjusting for multiple comparisons involving multiple antigens in each type of response measures using Benjamini & Hochberg method101 to control the false positive rate (FDR). p-values ≤0.05 and q-values ≤0.2 are significant.

Immune markers normalization prior to multivariate analyses

Any highly correlated immune features (Spearman’s correlation>0.95) were first deselected resulting in the final list of 56 immune features. Next, binding and neutralizing antibody, B cell and T cell markers were log-transformed. Missing observations were imputed using the Lasso linear regression method and mice R package. The effect of days since SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis on immune markers observations was eliminated via linear regressing the marker observation on days since SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis (y ∼ α + βx) and then subtracting the effect from the marker observations (y- βx). Finally, the markers were normalized with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one.

Polar plots

The percentile scaling from 0 to 1 was calculated among all participants for each immune feature. Then the mean percentile was calculated by PVS status. Polar plots display the mean percentile of all immune markers by PVS status. The direction-projection-permutation (DIPROPERM) test was used to test whether the global distribution of all immune markers differed between PVS (n = 36) and Non-PVS participants (n = 223).102

Correlation network

Association between pairs of the 56 log-transformed and normalized immune markers was measured using Spearman’s rank correlation. Separate 56 × 56 matrices of absolute value correlations were generated for PVS (n = 36) and non-PVS participants (n = 223). An undirected, non-self-looping, network was built using Gephi Graph Visualization and Manipulation software (Gephi version 0.10.1, www.gephi.com). Hubs were defined as the highest magnitude 20% of nodes (immune markers) based on weighted degree. Bottlenecks were defined as the highest magnitude 20% of nodes based on betweenness centrality when edges (Spearman’s rank correlations) were filtered to included only ≥ abs(0.5) connections. Communities were defined using a built-in modularity algorithm set to non-randomized and using edge weights and a resolution of 0.95. Node sizes correspond to weighted degree, node color corresponds to modularity class, edge color and thicknesses correspond to Spearman’s rank correlation magnitude. The network was visualized using ForceAtlas2.

t-SNE data visualization

t-SNE is a dimensional reduction technique used to create 2-dimensional maps of 56-dimentional immune marker data. Each point on the map represents a particular immune profile for one participant. Adjacent points tend to have similar immune profiling. Point color represents magnitude values of a specific variable measurement and p-value for the enrichment tSNE plot. A density ratio test was used to determine whether immune profiles were significantly enriched among PVS or non-PVS. p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons by controlling the false discovery rate using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Classification of PVS vs. non-PVS

To avoid overfitting and to identify a minimal number of immune markers that were most predictive, we first applied the recursive feature elimination103,104 algorithm with resampling and the random forest model to down-select markers for the classification. The 10-fold cross-validation random forest method was used to select a best set of markers. This process was repeated 200 times to achieve a robust feature (immune marker) selection. We then selected the markers that appeared in the best sets of markers more than 95% out of 200 repeats. If more than 20 immune markers were selected, we repeated this down-selection process among the selected markers. Secondly, we applied a backward selection to further evaluate/eliminate any redundant features using 10-fold cross-validations using Super Learner with three prediction algorithms (random forest, xgboost, glmnet) and mean area under receiver operating characteristic curves (CV-AUC) as the criterion. Finally, the performance of classification of PVS vs. non-PVS using the final set of most predictive markers was presented with mean receiver operator curves and mean CV-AUC with 200 replicates of 10-fold cross-validations.

Clustering analysis of correlation matrices

The clustering analysis of the correlation matrices was carried out in R. For clustering analysis of individual biomarkers, the biomarker Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients of the PVS group (n = 36) and the non-PVS group (n = 223) were first organized into two separate 56 by 56 square matrices. Then the Euclidean distances were calculated using a combined rectangle matrix (112 columns × 56 rows) including both the PVS group and the non-PVS group, so that the Euclidean distance between each pair of biomarkers was calculated using 112 pairs of Spearman correlation coefficients, including 56 pairs from the PVS group and 56 pairs from the non-PVS group. Then using the “hclust” function in R, a dendrogram was generated through hierarchical clustering using the McQuitty clustering method,32 in which recursive steps were taken to cluster the branches with the smallest difference between them. Next the 56 by 56 square correlation matrices of the PVS group and the non-PVS group were individually arranged by the order of dendrogram and visualized.

For analysis of grouped immune features, the 56 features were first separated into 12 binned groups based on the results from the clustering analysis of individual biomarkers, as well as the immunological categories of the biomarkers. For each group of binned immune features, an averaged value of immune marker magnitudes was calculated for each participant using the log-transformed and normalized magnitudes for all biomarkers in the binned group. Next, the Spearman’s correlation coefficient was calculated for each pair of binned immune features for the PVS group and the non-PVS group separately, leading to two separate 12 by 12 square correlation matrices. Finally, for each pair of grouped biomarkers, Fisher’s Z test was carried out to compare using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients in the PVS group versus the non-PVS group. Then the p-values from Fisher’s Z tests were organized using the dendrogram of the binned immune feature groups.

All analyses were performed using R unless otherwise specified.105
