Phacoemulsification combined with bent ab interno needle goniectomy versus high-frequency deep sclerotomy: a comparative study of surgical outcomes in open-angle glaucoma
Authors: Ugur Yayla, Pinar Orenc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to compare intraocular pressure (IOP) reduction, surgical success, antiglaucoma medication (AGM) usage, complication profiles, and structural and functional outcomes between phacoemulsification combined with bent ab interno needle goniectomy (Phaco-BANG) and high-frequency deep sclerotomy (Phaco-HFDS) in patients with open-angle glaucoma (OAG), given the lack of direct head-to-head comparisons.MethodsThis retrospective comparative study included 70 eyes from 70 patients (35 per group). The primary outcome was the change in IOP from baseline to postoperative month 12. Secondary outcomes included surgical success rate, changes in AGM use, complications, and structural and functional parameters, such as retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness, central macular thickness (CMT), and visual field mean deviation (MD). Surgical success was defined as a reduction in AGM without an increase in IOP, or an IOP of ≤21 mmHg with a ≥ 20% reduction from baseline.ResultsAt 12 months, mean IOP decreased by −4.3 ± 5.2 mmHg in the Phaco-BANG group and −4.0 ± 4.3 mmHg in the Phaco-HFDS group. Both groups showed significant IOP reduction at all postoperative time points compared to baseline (p < 0.05), with no statistically significant differences between groups (p > 0.05). Surgical success (IOP ≤ 21 mmHg with ≥20% reduction and/or ≥1 medication reduction without IOP increase) was achieved in 80.0% and 77.1% of eyes in the Phaco-BANG and Phaco-HFDS groups, respectively, with similar Kaplan–Meier survival outcomes (all p > 0.05). AGM use was also significantly reduced in both groups, with no statistically significant differences between groups (p > 0.05). The mean reduction in AGM at 12 months was −2.1 ± 1.3 in the Phaco-BANG group and −1.4 ± 0.9 in the Phaco-HFDS group (p = 0.143). No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups regarding structural and functional outcomes. Early postoperative complications were more frequent in the Phaco-BANG group, primarily due to transient hyphema; however, no vision-threatening complications or need for additional glaucoma surgery occurred.ConclusionsBoth Phaco-BANG and Phaco-HFDS were associated with reductions in IOP and AGM use at 12 months in patients with OAG. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding IOP reduction, medication burden, surgical success rates, or structural and functional outcomes. Although transient hyphema was more common following Phaco-BANG, both procedures demonstrated favorable safety profiles.

Background

Glaucoma remains a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, and reducing IOP is the only proven strategy to prevent disease progression. Although trabeculectomy has long been considered the gold standard surgical intervention, its well-known complication profile has driven the development of minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) techniques targeting the conventional outflow pathway. These techniques have been increasingly discussed in clinical practice guidelines and the literature [1–3].

Among Schlemm’s canal–based MIGS procedures, high-frequency deep sclerotomy (HFDS) and bent ab interno needle goniectomy (BANG) aim to reduce trabecular resistance by directly communicating the anterior chamber with Schlemm’s canal. HFDS utilizes a high-frequency diathermic probe to create multiple intrascleral pockets through the trabecular meshwork and Schlemm’s canal, thereby facilitating aqueous outflow. Previous studies have demonstrated significant IOP reduction and medication burden decrease following HFDS, both as a standalone procedure and when combined with cataract surgery [4–8]. BANG is a low-cost ab interno excisional goniectomy technique using a bent hypodermic needle that avoids device dependency. Later studies reported reduced IOP and decreased medication use when BANG was combined with phacoemulsification [9–12].

While BANG has been compared with other MIGS procedures in the literature [13, 14], direct head-to-head comparisons involving HFDS remain scarce. Both procedures target the trabecular outflow pathway; however, HFDS creates intrascleral pockets that may enhance aqueous drainage beyond simple trabecular excision, whereas BANG involves the direct removal of a broader segment of the trabecular meshwork [5, 9]. Given their distinct mechanisms of action, we hypothesized that the two techniques may differ in terms of IOP reduction, medication burden, surgical success rates, and safety profiles. To our knowledge, no head-to-head comparative study has evaluated phacoemulsification combined with HFDS (Phaco-HFDS) versus phacoemulsification combined with BANG (Phaco-BANG).

The present study aims to compare surgical success rates, IOP reduction, medication burden, complication profiles, as well as structural and functional outcomes between Phaco-HFDS and Phaco-BANG in patients with open-angle glaucoma (OAG).

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective comparative study included patients with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) and pseudoexfoliative glaucoma (PXG) who underwent phacoemulsification combined with either BANG or HFDS at a single tertiary referral center. Medical records of consecutive patients who underwent surgery between December 2023 and June 2025 were retrospectively reviewed. This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul Medipol University, Istanbul, Turkey (approval number: E-10840098-202.3.02–1425). Due to the retrospective design of the study, the requirement for informed consent for participation was waived by the ethics committee.

Inclusion criteria were a diagnosis of POAG or PXG confirmed by open angles on gonioscopy and characteristic optic nerve and/or visual field changes [15], the presence of visually significant cataract requiring surgery, and a minimum follow-up of 12 months. Exclusion criteria included a history of previous glaucoma surgery or glaucoma laser procedures, congenital or juvenile glaucoma, other types of glaucoma (angle-closure, neovascular, uveitic, or secondary glaucomas other than PXG), corneal pathology affecting reliable IOP measurement, retinal diseases that could influence structural or functional assessment, neurological diseases affecting the optic nerve, and incomplete follow-up data.

The primary endpoint was assessed at 12 months postoperatively. To avoid inter-eye correlation, only one eye per patient was included in the analysis; in cases of bilateral surgery, the first operated eye was selected.

The allocation of surgical techniques was primarily determined by the intraoperative availability of the HFDS probe. The same HFDS probe was repeatedly used after resterilization, necessitating a waiting period between sterilization cycles. Patients were scheduled consecutively in routine clinical practice; when the HFDS probe was available following sterilization, Phaco-HFDS was performed, whereas Phaco-BANG was preferred during the sterilization waiting periods.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by the same experienced glaucoma surgeon (PO) under local anesthesia using the same phacoemulsification platform (OS 4, Oertli Instrumente AG, Berneck, Switzerland). In all cases, standard clear corneal phacoemulsification was performed prior to the glaucoma procedure. Surgery was carried out through a temporal clear corneal incision. After creation of a continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis, phacoemulsification of the nucleus and cortical aspiration were completed, followed by implantation of a foldable intraocular lens into the capsular bag. At the completion of cataract surgery, the ophthalmic viscosurgical device was thoroughly irrigated and aspirated from the anterior chamber. Intracameral carbachol (Miostat®, Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, TX, USA) was then administered to achieve adequate miosis and facilitate visualization of the iridocorneal angle. The anterior chamber was subsequently reformed and stabilized using a cohesive ophthalmic viscosurgical device. Under direct gonioscopic visualization using a surgical goniolens, the nasal angle structures were identified, and the glaucoma procedure was performed according to group allocation [16].

In the Phaco-BANG group, a 25-G angled needle tip was modified by bending its distal tip to create a goniectomy instrument. The procedure was based on previously described ab interno needle goniectomy techniques. The nasal trabecular meshwork was engaged and excised in a controlled manner by advancing the needle along Schlemm’s canal. Approximately 120° of trabecular meshwork was removed, extending from the nasal quadrant toward both superior and inferior directions, thereby creating a broad communication between the anterior chamber and Schlemm’s canal [10, 13].

In the Phaco-HFDS group, high-frequency deep sclerotomy was performed using a 19-gauge bipolar diathermic probe operating at approximately 500 kHz. After gonioscopic identification of the nasal trabecular meshwork, the probe tip was introduced through the trabecular meshwork into Schlemm’s canal. Six intrascleral pockets were created according to the standard technique. Each pocket measured approximately 1 mm in depth and 0.3 mm in height, resulting in a total estimated filtration area of approximately 3.6 mm2. These controlled sclerotomies were intended to enhance aqueous humor outflow through Schlemm’s canal and adjacent intrascleral spaces [4–8].

At the end of surgery, viscoelastic material was completely removed, and intracameral antibiotics were administered. Postoperatively, patients received topical moxifloxacin 0.5% and prednisolone acetate 1% five times daily for one month. AGMs were discontinued postoperatively and were reintroduced during follow-up when necessary. Target IOP levels were ≤ 12 mmHg for advanced, ≤15 mmHg for moderate, and ≤18 mmHg for early glaucoma [17].

Outcome measures

All outcomes were compared between the Phaco-BANG and Phaco-HFDS groups.

The primary outcome was the change in IOP from baseline to postoperative month 12. Secondary outcomes included surgical success rate, reduction in AGM use, complication profile, and structural and functional parameters.

In accordance with the recommendations of the American Academy of Ophthalmology Glaucoma Preferred Practice Pattern Committee (AAO PPP) for MIGS combined with cataract surgery, surgical success was defined as either a reduction of ≥ 1 AGM without an increase in IOP or achievement of an IOP ≤ 21 mmHg with a ≥ 20% reduction from baseline. Failure was defined as additional glaucoma surgery, loss of light perception, or hypotony [18]. For survival analysis, failure was defined as the first postoperative visit at which the eye no longer met any of the predefined criteria for surgical success.

As additional secondary analyses, complete success was defined as IOP ≤ 21 mmHg with a ≥ 20% reduction from baseline without AGM use. Qualified success was defined as IOP ≤ 21 mmHg with a ≥ 20% reduction from baseline irrespective of AGM use. Medication-only success was defined as a reduction of ≥ 1 AGM from baseline without achieving an IOP reduction of ≥20% from baseline and/or an IOP ≤ 21 mmHg. Pressure-based success was defined as achievement of predefined postoperative IOP thresholds without the use of AGMs and was evaluated at four thresholds (≤21, ≤18, ≤15, and ≤12 mmHg). Target IOP–based success was defined as achievement of stage-specific target IOP levels irrespective of AGM use. Target IOP levels were predefined as ≤18 mmHg for early glaucoma, ≤15 mmHg for moderate glaucoma, and ≤12 mmHg for advanced glaucoma [17–19].

IOP measurements, obtained by Goldmann applanation tonometry, and the number of AGMs were recorded at baseline and at postoperative day 1, week 1, and months 1, 3, 6, and 12. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was assessed using a Snellen chart and converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) for analysis. Structural parameters were assessed using optical coherence tomography (OCT; RTVue-100®, Optovue Inc., Fremont, CA, USA), including central macular thickness (CMT) and global peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness. Scans with a signal strength index < 60 or with segmentation or motion artifacts were excluded. Functional assessment was conducted using standard automated perimetry (Humphrey Field Analyzer 3®, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA). Tests with fixation losses ≤ 15% and false-positive and false-negative rates ≤ 33% were considered reliable. Mean deviation (MD) values were analyzed, and glaucoma severity was graded according to the Hodapp–Parrish–Anderson classification [20]. Measurements were performed at baseline and during postoperative follow-up; BCVA, RNFL, and MD were evaluated at months 0–12, while CMT was assessed at months 0–1. Postoperative complications and additional glaucoma procedures during follow-up were included in the safety analysis. A transient IOP spike was defined as an IOP > 25 mmHg at any postoperative visit [21].

Statistical analysis

Sample size was determined a priori using G*Power (version 3.1.9.4). The calculation was based on detecting a clinically meaningful difference in IOP reduction between the two groups at postoperative month 12, with a statistical power of 80–90% and a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. To account for potential loss to follow-up, at least 25 patients were planned for each group. Ultimately, 35 patients were included in each group.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values, as well as frequency and percentage. The distribution of variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Shapiro–Wilk test. For the analysis of independent quantitative variables with normal distribution, the Independent samples t-test was used. For non-normally distributed independent quantitative variables, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. For the analysis of dependent quantitative variables, the Paired samples t-test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test were used. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test, and the Fisher’s exact test was used when the assumptions for the Chi-square test were not met. Correlations between continuous variables were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to evaluate cumulative surgical success over time, and survival distributions between the groups were compared using the log-rank test. In addition, ANCOVA, propensity score matching (PSM) sensitivity analysis, and binary logistic regression analysis were performed to adjust for baseline differences, reduce potential confounding, and evaluate factors associated with surgical success, respectively. An exploratory post hoc non-inferiority analysis was additionally performed for the primary outcome of 12-month IOP reduction. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Both groups demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in visual acuity from preoperative to postoperative 12th month measurements (p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test), with no statistically significant differences between the groups at either time point or in the change from baseline (all p > 0.05, Mann–Whitney U test).

Mean IOP decreased from 18.3 ± 4.3 mmHg to 14.0 ± 2.8 mmHg in the Phaco-BANG group and from 18.1 ± 3.2 mmHg to 14.1 ± 2.4 mmHg in the Phaco-HFDS group at the 12th month. The mean reduction at the 12th month was −4.3 ± 5.2 mmHg in the Phaco-BANG group and −4.0 ± 4.3 mmHg in the Phaco-HFDS group. Both groups demonstrated a significant reduction in IOP at all postoperative time points compared to preoperative values (all p < 0.05, Wilcoxon signed-rank test) (Fig. 1). There were no statistically significant differences between the groups at any postoperative time point, including postoperative day 1, week 1, month 1, month 3, month 6, and the 12th month (all p > 0.05, Mann–Whitney U test). Similarly, the magnitude of IOP reduction from baseline was comparable between the groups at all time points (all p > 0.05, Mann–Whitney U test). Baseline preoperative IOP showed a strong positive correlation with the magnitude of postoperative IOP reduction (Pearson correlation, r = 0.838, p < 0.001). Exploratory post hoc non-inferiority analysis for 12-month IOP reduction between the two groups demonstrated a mean difference of 1.06 mmHg (95% CI: −0.97 to 3.08). An exploratory subgroup analysis was additionally performed in eyes with advanced glaucoma (stage 3, n = 23) to further evaluate the suitability of these procedures in more severe disease. Among eyes with advanced glaucoma (n = 23), mean IOP decreased significantly from 19.83 ± 4.01 mmHg preoperatively to 13.09 ± 2.23 mmHg postoperatively (paired-samples t-test, p = 0.0000018). Mean AGM burden also decreased significantly from 3.04 ± 0.98 preoperatively to 1.00 ± 1.38 postoperatively (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = 0.00000049). When advanced glaucoma eyes were analyzed according to surgical group, no statistically significant intergroup differences were observed in final postoperative IOP (p = 0.71, independent samples t-test) or final postoperative AGM burden (p = 0.77, Mann–Whitney U test).

According to the predefined surgical success criteria based on the AAO PPP recommendations, success was achieved in 80.0% of patients in the Phaco-BANG group and 77.1% in the Phaco-HFDS group. Although the primary success rate was slightly higher in the Phaco-BANG group, the difference between the groups was not statistically significant (χ2 test, p = 0.770). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated similar survival probabilities between the two groups over the 12-month follow-up period. The mean survival time was 11.17 months (95% CI: 10.58–11.76) in the Phaco-BANG group and 10.89 months (95% CI: 10.16–11.61) in the Phaco-HFDS group. Median survival time could not be estimated as the survival curves did not reach 50% during the follow-up period. The complete success rate was 54.3% (19/35) in the Phaco-BANG group and 48.6% (17/35) in the Phaco-HFDS group, and the difference was not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.811). The qualified success rate was 68.6% (24/35) in the Phaco-BANG group and 60.0% (21/35) in the Phaco-HFDS group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.618). The medication-only success rate was 28.6% (10/35) in the Phaco-BANG group and 37.1% (13/35) in the Phaco-HFDS group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.611).

Pressure-based success rates at IOP thresholds of ≤21, ≤18, ≤15, and ≤12 mmHg were 74.3%, 65.7%, 28.6%, and 22.9% in the Phaco-BANG group and 77.1%, 65.7%, 37.1%, and 25.7% in the Phaco-HFDS group, respectively, with no significant between-group differences at any threshold (all p > 0.05). Regarding target IOP outcomes according to glaucoma stage, 13 of 14 eyes (92.9%) in the Phaco-BANG group and 15 of 15 eyes (100.0%) in the Phaco-HFDS group achieved the predefined target IOP of ≤18 mmHg (Fisher’s exact test, p = 1.000). Among eyes with moderate glaucoma, target IOP achievement (≤15 mmHg) was observed in 7 of 10 eyes (70.0%) and 5 of 8 eyes (62.5%), respectively (Fisher’s exact test, p = 1.000). In eyes with advanced glaucoma, 2 of 11 eyes (18.2%) in the Phaco-BANG group and 6 of 12 eyes (50.0%) in the Phaco-HFDS group achieved the predefined target IOP of ≤12 mmHg (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.193). No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups across glaucoma severity stages.

Binary logistic regression analysis was additionally performed to evaluate independent factors associated with surgical success at 12 months. Baseline RNFL thickness (B = 0.003, OR = 1.003, p = 0.926), baseline MD (B = 0.070, OR = 1.073, p = 0.235), glaucoma type (B = −0.177, OR = 0.838, p = 0.820), and surgical group (B = 0.874, OR = 2.397, p = 0.255), were not independently associated with surgical success.

PSM was performed using 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Matching variables included age, sex, glaucoma type, glaucoma stage, preoperative IOP, preoperative AGM, baseline C/D ratio, preoperative MD, and preoperative RNFL thickness. Matching resulted in 29 matched pairs (58 eyes). After matching, baseline covariates, including preoperative RNFL thickness, demonstrated acceptable balance between the groups (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). In the matched cohort, 12-month postoperative IOP and AGM burden, as well as the magnitude of IOP and AGM reduction, remained comparable between the Phaco-BANG and Phaco-HFDS groups. Similarly, changes in MD, RNFL, and CMT were also comparable between the groups, supporting the robustness of the primary findings.

During the first postoperative week, the overall complication rate was significantly higher in the Phaco-BANG group compared to the Phaco-HFDS group (57.1% vs 31.4%, p = 0.030). This difference was mainly driven by a higher incidence of hyphema in the Phaco-BANG group (34.3% vs 5.7%, p = 0.003). In the Phaco-BANG group, one patient required anterior chamber lavage, while hyphema resolved within the first postoperative week in the remaining cases. Discussion

In the present study, both Phaco-BANG and Phaco-HFDS were associated with significant reductions in IOP and AGM use at 12 months. No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups regarding IOP reduction, AGM reduction, surgical success rates, or structural and functional outcomes during follow-up. Although early postoperative complications were more frequent in the Phaco-BANG group, primarily because of transient hyphema, no vision-threatening events or need for additional glaucoma surgery were observed in either group. Overall, both procedures demonstrated favorable safety profiles and effective IOP control when combined with phacoemulsification in patients with OAG.

Previous studies have reported favorable outcomes for both HFDS and BANG procedures individually, including significant reductions in IOP and medication burden when performed alone or in combination with cataract surgery. However, these findings are primarily derived from separate cohorts and heterogeneous study designs. Despite evaluating both techniques against other MIGS procedures, the literature notably lacks direct head-to-head comparisons between HFDS and BANG. This absence of comparative evidence complicates the determination of the relative advantages of these two trabecular outflow–targeting approaches, highlighting the clinical relevance of the present study.

Table 4 summarizes studies evaluating phacoemulsification combined with HFDS and BANG. Although few studies have 12 months of follow-up or longer, both techniques consistently reduce IOP and the use of AGM, aligning with our findings. Phacoemulsification alone has been reported to reduce IOP by approximately 2–4 mmHg in the literature [22, 23]. Since phacoemulsification alone may result in postoperative IOP reduction, it cannot be fully determined in this combined-surgery cohort how much of the observed IOP lowering (approximately 4.3 mmHg in the Phaco-BANG group and 4.0 mmHg in the Phaco-HFDS group) was attributable to the MIGS procedures themselves. Additionally, the relatively low baseline IOP in our cohort may have limited the magnitude of postoperative IOP reduction, considering that the IOP-lowering potential of trabecular MIGS procedures is generally modest [15, 24]. Consistent with this observation, baseline preoperative IOP showed a strong positive correlation with the magnitude of postoperative IOP reduction in our cohort. However, the effect of phacoemulsification alone on postoperative AGM reduction, particularly medication-free outcomes, remains less clearly defined in the literature [22, 23]. Both groups in our cohort demonstrated substantial reductions in AGM burden, with approximately 65% of patients maintaining IOP levels below 18 mmHg without medication at 12 months. Given that minimizing AGM burden has become an important therapeutic goal due to concerns regarding long-term adherence, ocular surface toxicity, systemic adverse effects, and quality of life, these findings suggest that both MIGS procedures, when combined with cataract surgery, may contribute to a durable reduction in medication burden even in the absence of a large additional IOP-lowering effect [3, 24]. These findings may support an additional therapeutic contribution of the combined MIGS procedures beyond cataract extraction itself. In the literature, postoperative IOP values following phacoemulsification combined with HFDS or BANG, similar to those observed in our study, generally cluster around 14 mmHg (Table 4). When other trabecular MIGS techniques are considered, a wide variability in surgical approaches becomes apparent. Differences in the type of MIGS procedure, the extent and quadrant of trabecular meshwork treated or bypassed, and regional variations in aqueous outflow—as recently evaluated using aqueous angiography—and baseline IOP values may all influence the magnitude of postoperative IOP reduction and final postoperative IOP outcomes [25, 26]. Interestingly, studies evaluating trabecular MIGS procedures in eyes with lower baseline IOP values, similar to those in our study, have generally reported mean IOP reductions of 3–7 mmHg. Consistent with these findings, both trabecular MIGS procedures evaluated in our cohort demonstrated postoperative IOP reductions within this reported range. In the literature, eyes with baseline IOP values above 21 mmHg have demonstrated larger IOP reductions, often exceeding 7 mmHg, although final postoperative IOP values generally remain within 13–17 mmHg [25]. This observation may partly reflect the physiological floor effect of trabecular MIGS procedures, which are limited by the conventional outflow pathway and episcleral venous pressure [24–26]. Further studies are needed to clarify this relationship. However, the magnitude of IOP reduction in our cohort appeared relatively limited compared with the reductions typically achieved after trabeculectomy, which generally results in substantially lower postoperative IOP levels. Therefore, greater caution may be warranted in patients with advanced glaucoma or those requiring single-digit target IOP levels. In the advanced glaucoma subgroup, we observed that postoperative IOP remained around 13 mmHg, with a significant reduction in AGM burden and no significant differences between the groups. However, whether lower postoperative IOP levels could have been achieved with a more intensive postoperative AGM regimen remains unclear. The limited sample size of this subgroup warrants cautious interpretation. Further prospective studies are needed to better define the role of these procedures in advanced glaucoma and eyes requiring very low target IOP levels, as well as their place within the evolving spectrum of trabecular MIGS techniques. Therefore, comparative studies among different MIGS procedures may provide valuable insights for optimal patient selection and surgical decision-making.

When comparing the MIGS procedures evaluated in our study with other MIGS techniques reported in the literature, comparative studies involving HFDS and other MIGS procedures remain limited. In contrast, BANG has been evaluated in several studies against other MIGS techniques, including GATT and excisional goniotomy [13, 14]. GATT has shown greater IOP reduction and higher complete success rates than BANG (approximately 88% vs. 61%) [13], whereas comparisons with other goniotomy-based techniques, such as Espaillat goniotomy, have reported more comparable outcomes, with success rates approaching 80% for BANG [14].

The lack of a standardized definition of surgical success in MIGS limits the ability to compare outcomes across studies; therefore, we applied AAO PPP–based criteria [18]. Using these criteria, overall success rates were approximately 80% in both groups, consistent with recommended benchmarks (65%), despite our relatively short 12-month follow-up. Medication-free success was also high at moderate IOP thresholds (≤21 and ≤18 mmHg) but declined markedly at ≤15 mmHg. In our study, MIGS procedures combined with phacoemulsification effectively achieved moderate IOP control without medication; however, reaching lower target IOP without pharmacological support remained challenging.

Given the limited comparative data and the lack of previously reported direct comparisons between HFDS and BANG, our study directly evaluates two ab interno approaches combined with phacoemulsification that enhance aqueous outflow through different mechanisms. We observed no significant differences between the two techniques regarding IOP reduction, AGM use, or surgical success at 12 months. The observed outcomes may be explained by their distinct yet complementary mechanisms: BANG removes a broad segment of the trabecular meshwork, reducing trabecular resistance, whereas HFDS creates intrascleral pockets that may enhance both conventional and uveoscleral outflow [4–14]. Despite these differences, both approaches may ultimately improve aqueous humor dynamics to a similar extent. Further studies incorporating anterior segment imaging modalities and aqueous humor angiography are needed to better elucidate these outflow pathways [8, 26].

In addition to IOP and medication outcomes, structural and functional parameters are crucial for assessing the influence of MIGS procedures on the progression of glaucoma. Most studies in the literature evaluating HFDS have primarily focused on visual field parameters [4, 5], whereas for BANG, both visual field and RNFL measurements have been evaluated in a limited number of studies [13]. One of the strengths of our study is that, to the best of our knowledge, it represents the first direct comparison of structural and functional outcomes between Phaco-BANG and Phaco-HFDS procedures. However, the significantly lower baseline RNFL thickness observed in the Phaco-BANG group may raise concerns about a potential imbalance in disease severity between the groups. Nonetheless, other structural (C/D ratio) and functional (MD and glaucoma stage distribution) indicators of disease severity were comparable between the groups despite the baseline RNFL difference. When the baseline structural imbalance was re-evaluated using sensitivity analyses, the baseline RNFL difference was not found to significantly influence postoperative structural outcomes or surgical success. Taken together, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in the magnitude of MD and RNFL changes during follow-up. Nevertheless, further prospective studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and additional visual field and OCT parameters are needed to better clarify the long-term structural and functional effects of these procedures.

An interesting finding was a significant increase in CMT at one month in the Phaco-HFDS group, whereas no significant change was observed in the Phaco-BANG group; however, the between-group difference was not statistically significant. Although data on CMT changes after MIGS are limited, studies have described similar transient increases following phacoemulsification [27]. The clinical significance of this finding remains uncertain, as CMT was assessed only at the first postoperative month and longer-term follow-up data were not available. Therefore, it was not possible to determine whether the observed increase persisted or resolved over time. The absence of a difference in visual acuity between the two groups may further suggest limited clinical relevance of this finding.

Both procedures demonstrated favorable safety profiles, with no sight-threatening complications observed. Early postoperative complications were more frequent in the Phaco-BANG group, primarily due to transient hyphema (approximately 34%), consistent with previous reports (around 30%) [11, 13]. In contrast, HFDS was associated with fewer early complications, although transient IOP elevation was occasionally observed [7]. Procedure-related complications such as trabecular meshwork readhesion after BANG, potentially due to incomplete removal of the excised strip, and peripheral anterior synechiae after HFDS, possibly related to inadvertent probe–iris contact during scleral pocket creation, have been reported [6, 7, 12], but were not observed in our series. This meticulous surgical technique may include complete removal of the trabecular strip in BANG and careful avoidance of iris contact during HFDS.

This study has several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, it represents the first direct head-to-head comparison between phacoemulsification combined with BANG and HFDS. Additionally, all procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon using a standardized surgical protocol, which minimizes variability related to surgical technique. The study provides a comprehensive evaluation encompassing IOP, medication outcomes, visual field analysis, and OCT parameters, enabling the assessment of both the functional and structural aspects of glaucoma.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective and non-randomized design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the allocation process, which was based on HFDS probe availability rather than randomization, may have introduced residual selection bias. Second, although all procedures were performed by a single surgeon, which ensures surgical standardization, this may limit the generalizability of the results to other surgeons. Third, although the sample size was relatively small, a priori power analysis was performed to ensure adequate statistical power. Fourth, the follow-up period was limited to 12 months; while this duration is clinically relevant, longer-term outcomes beyond 2 years remain important to fully assess the durability of these procedures. Finally, the absence of a phacoemulsification-only control group limits the ability to determine the independent contribution of cataract surgery; however, as phacoemulsification was performed in both groups, the comparative analysis between the two techniques remains balanced.

In conclusion, both Phaco-BANG and Phaco-HFDS were associated with reductions in IOP and AGM use and achieved high surgical success rates at 12 months in patients with OAG. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two techniques regarding IOP reduction, AGM use, surgical success, or structural and functional outcomes. Although transient hyphema was more frequent in the Phaco-BANG group, both procedures exhibited favorable safety profiles. These findings provide the first direct comparative evidence between these two approaches and suggest that both techniques are effective and safe when combined with phacoemulsification. However, given the relatively modest IOP reduction, caution may be warranted in patients requiring lower medication-free target IOP levels. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm these results.
