Molecular structure and thermal decomposition kinetics of kerogen from the Paleocene oil-shale facies in the Bikaner–Nagaur Basin, western India
Authors: Mohammed Hail Hakimi, Alok Kumar, Aref Lashin, Atif M Abass, Khairul Azlan Mustapha, Runcie Paul Mathews, Danis K Nurgaliev, S M Talha Qadri, Prakash K Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe bitumen shale facies in the Paleocene Palana Formation serves as a key oil-shale resource in the Bikaner–Nagaur Basin, Western India. It has a high organic matter concentration, which provides the necessary foundation for significant oil accumulation. Therefore, the purpose of this work is to characterize the molecular structure of kerogen within the Palana oil shale facies in order to assess the potential of shale oil resources and comprehend the mechanisms of hydrocarbon generation. In this study, comprehensive chemical analyses, including elemental analysis (CHNS), FTIR, TG/DTA, Py-GC, and kinetics of the kerogen decomposition, incorporated with microscopic investigation, were employed to decipher the elemental composition and molecular structure of Palana’s kerogen. The microscopic analysis of kerogen indicates that the Palana oil shale sediments are characterized by high abundance marine organic matter assemblages, including bituminite, fluorescence AOM, and algae, consistent with the presence of hydrogen-rich kerogen. The predominance of hydrogen-enriched kerogen, primarily classified as Type II, with moderate-to-low sulfur content is substantiated by the kerogen’s elemental profile and molecular structure. This includes a high hydrogen-to-carbon atomic ratio (H/C ˃1.40), low sulfur-to-carbon atomic ratio (S/C < 0.04), and predominance of aliphatic compounds with relatively low concentrations of aromatic compounds. The petroleum type of the Palana’s kerogen is corresponding to P–N–A oils with high wax content, and it is released at a subsurface temperature regime of 107 to 153 °C, which is consistent with the computed vitrinite reflectance values of 0.62–1.07%VRo, as demonstrated by the bulk and compositional kinetic results. The highlight results of the elemental composition and molecular structure provide a strong basis for further in-situ conversion processes and development of the shale oil system, as well as the mechanism of petroleum generation in the oil shale of the Palana Formation.Keywords: Palana formation, Hydrogen-rich kerogen, Kinetic model, FTIR spectra, Mixed organic matter, Western IndiaSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Globally, fine-grained oil shale sediments that are accumulated in both marine and non-marine (particularly lacustrine) environments have received substantial inputs of organic matter during deposition1. These organic-rich shale sediments are widely distributed across major basins in the world, gaining recognition for their significance in conventional and unconventional oil exploration and production, and enhancing national energy security2,3.

However, most of the marine and lacustrine oil shale sediments have high abundance of kerogen, with mainly include Types I, II and II-S, are defined by distinct H/C, O/C, and S/C atomic ratios, based on the chemical composition of the carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and oxygen (O), with trace elements of sulfur (S) and nitrogen (N)4,5. Therefore, the chemical composition and molecular structure of kerogen in the oil shale sediments are important in controlling the mechanism, composition, and properties of the resulting hydrocarbon products6. In this case, the molecular structural and thermodynamic properties of kerogen were recently studied to understand the mixture of organic compounds that holds huge quantities of hydrocarbons, and the development of unconventional oil and gas resources7. To characterize the molecular structural and chemical properties of kerogen fraction, a suite of analytical techniques, including X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), pyrolysis–gas chromatography (Py–GC), and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (13C NMR) spectroscopy, were performed8–12. More specifically, the Py–GC, FTIR and 13C NMR spectroscopy geochemical analyses were used to investigate the molecular structural and thermal decomposition behaviour of kerogen. These analyses focused on the differences in organic matter types, particularly the length of aliphatic carbon chains, the numbers of carbons in aromatic structures, and their relationship with thermal maturity13,14.

The present study focuses is Bikaner–Nagaur Basin, which is one of the largest sedimentary basins in western India (Fig. 1). The Bikaner–Nagaur Basin has evolved through Cretaceous and Jurassic tectonic events related to the separation of the Indian plate from the Gondwana supercontinent during the Mesozoic era15,16. However, the Bikaner–Nagaur Basin has also developed during the Cenozoic Era (i.e., Paleocene–Eocene), as a result of the Indian and Asian plates reactive convergence and collision during Himalayan Orogeny17,18.

Geological map of Rajasthan showing the Gurha mine location in the Bikaner–Nagaur Basin, northwestern India. Arc Map GIS software 10.2.2 is used to create this figure, http://www.esri.com.

The tectonic events during the development of the Bikaner–Nagaur Basin led to the formation of thick Palaeozoic sediments with a maximum thickness of 1500 m19, overlaid by a thin cover of, Mesozoic and Cenozoic sediments. In this case, sedimentary succession in the Bikaner–Nagaur Basin comprises a broad variety of clastic and non-clastic rocks, representing depositional ages from the Early Cambrian through the Recent (Fig. 2).

Generalized lithostratigraphic succession of the Bikaner Nagaur Basin, northwestern India (modified after Mathews et al20).

The current study involves the collection and analysis of oil shale samples from the Palana Formation in the Bikaner–Nagaur Basin (Fig. 2). The Palana Formation hosts the most extensive and compact lignite deposit in the region, accompanied by a sizable succession of bituminous shale (Fig. 2), which is deposited in reducing paralic to swampy envieroment20. However, the bituminous shales within the Palana Formation are rich in sapropelic OM, with minor contributions from humic and/or zoogenic material in certain locales20. The Palana bituminous shale facies is an additional important source rock that generates hydrocarbons of good-quality in the Bikaner Nagaur Basin, northwestern India21. The Palana bituminous shale facies is identified as oil-generating unit, exhibiting substantial organic matter carbon (TOC) content ranges from 4 to 33 wt.%, and hydrogen-rich Types I and II kerogen, with a hydrogen index (HI) peaking at 544 mg HC/g TOC21. However, the high abundance of hydrogen-rich kerogen for mainly oil generation potential from the Palana bituminous shale facies is consistent with the presence of phytoplankton algae and limited terrestrial organic matter input deposited under relatively anoxic environmental and warm climatic circumstances, as corroborated by previous published of Hakimi et al.22. Despite previous studies of the bituminous shale succession within the Palana Formation21,22, the impact of the molecular configuration and physicochemical characteristics of kerogen within the Palana bituminous shales has yet to be fully addressed in literature. This is due to dearth of extensive research, particularly molecular composition, maturation behaviour, and kerogen characteristics.

The prime investigation emphasis current study is to enhance our understanding of kerogen molecular structure (KMS) of the kerogen component within the Palana bituminous shales, and then used to address the existing gaps of knowledge concerning its internal architecture and petroleum generation potential based on a range of geochemical and petrological methods such as pyrolysis–gas chromatography (Py–GC), proximate (TG/DTA), elemental composition (CHNS), and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR), along with kerogen microscopic. Moreover, the bulk and compositional kinetic modeling approach is further applied to assess the subsurface temperature regimes and their effect on the kerogen conversion and petroleum formation, and thereby, decipher the petroleum exploration potential of the Palana bituminous shale facies for reducing exploration risk within the Bikaner Nagaur Basin.

Materials and methods

Materials and sample collection

Five bituminous shale samples were collected from the Palana Formation exposed in the Gurha mine (Fig. 3), located in the southern part of the Bikaner–Nagaur Basin (Fig. 1). The bituminous shale samples from outcrop section were used due to not an available core data for the studied Palana Formation for drill wells in the Bikaner–Nagaur Basin. However, the bituminous shale samples were systematically collected from the outcrop in a vertical profile, from bottom to top (Fig. 3), following Schopf23. Among these samples, the fresh samples were collected in order to reduce the impact of the weathering from surface outcrop samples that may influence the experimental data, and to ensure the reliability of the geochemical results. Water was also employed to clean the collected samples and eliminate further contamination.

Lithology of the studied Gurha mine section, including the studied bituminous shale horizons.

Analytical works

Soluble bitumen and isolated kerogen extraction

The five bituminous shale samples of the Palana Formation underwent bitumen (= retained free hydrocarbons) extraction using a Soxhlet apparatus. In this regard, the shale samples were crushed to a size range of 0.2 mm using a ShchD-2 jaw crusher, and then about 30 g of each crushed sample was dispersed in a mixture of dichloromethane and acetone and extracted for 72 h. However, the bitumen extraction process has been repeated to remove the residual bitumen contaminations, which they impact on the molecular structure of the insoluble kerogen component during the FTIR and Py-GC analyses and results.

Additionally, the insoluble kerogen component within the studied five oil shale samples was separated using the standard palynological procedure of extraction, as established by Durand and Nicaise24. In this procedure, the crushed samples were treated with hydrochloric and hydrofluoric acids to remove carbonates and silicate minerals, respectively, followed by repeated washing with distilled water to neutralize the residues. The resulting kerogen residues were subsequently sieved and prepared on glass slides utilizing glycerine-based mounting media.

Organic petrological analyses

Organic facies analysis of all studied oil shale samples was conducted using a standard polished block preparation method as outlined by Taylor et al.25. The samples were first milled into ± 18 mesh (approximately ≤ 1mm) and embedded in sample cups using epoxy resin mixed with a hardener. The resulting pellets were ground and polished for 3–5 min using a polishing machine with various grades of silicon carbide paper under continuous slow water flow, followed by final polishing with 0.05 micron MicroPolish gel. Polished blocks were analyzed under both reflected white light and UV light using LEICA DM 4600P microscope integrated with a photometric system (MSP 200).

For enhancing the characterization of OM types and their morphological features, the surface of oil shale samples was additionally investigated via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). For SEM analysis, representative chips measuring up to 1 cm were carefully extracted from the samples. These chips were analyzed using a high-resolution field-emission SEM (FESEM) with a Hitachi SU8220 system.

In addition, glass slides mounted with isolated kerogen were subjected to palynofacies analysis using LEICA DM4 microscope in transmitted mode. In this case, identifications and quantitative analysis of the different types of palynofacies, including amorphous organic matter (AOM), palynomorphs (i.e., spores, pollens, cuticles, diatoms, dinoflagellates, and algae), and phytoclasts (i.e., woody fragments) were studied under transmitted white light and UV lights.

Chemical composition

The extracted kerogen was subjected to chemical composition analysis using two analytical techniques, including proximate (TG/DTA) and ultimate elemental (CHNS). These geochemical analyses were conducted at the geochemical laboratories of the Department of Geoscience, Universiti Teknologi PETRONAS, Malaysia.

Proximate analysis was carried out to determine the moisture, fixed carbon, ash, and volatile matter contents using a Perkinelmer Diamonf Thermogravimeric/Differential Thermal Analyzer (TG/DTA). In this analysis, 5 mg of the extracted kerogen samples were heated to 900 °C through nitrogen gas. In this case, the programmed temperature was started at 25 °C, increased to 110 °C at the rate of 85 °C/min, and then holds 110 °C for 6 min. Subsequently, the temperature was increased from 110 to 900 °C at the rate of 80 °C/min and then held at 900 °C for 5 min. The results were analyzed using the instrument software and applied to measure the moisture, fixed carbon, ash, and volatile matter contents following the works of Donahue and Rais26.

The CHNS analysis was employed to specifically measure carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), and total sulfur (S) concentrations in weight percentages. Elemental analysis of the extracted kerogen fraction was performed using a Perkin Elmer (2400 Series II) analyser. Samples were placed in aluminum vials and automatically introduced into the instrument, following the ASTM standard (method: P/N 0240–1289). The samples were combusted at 1100 ºC, and the concentrations of C, N, H, and S were directly measured. The ooxygen content (O) was calculated by difference from the total elemental combination. Subsequently, atomic ratio such as H/C, S/C, O/C, and N/C were calculated.

Structural characterization

The molecular architecture of the kerogen was studied using FTIR and Py–GC analyses. These analyses were conducted in the geochemical laboratories of the Department of Geology, University of Malaya, Malaysia.

Py-GC analysis was specifically employed to discern the distribution of distinct kerogen groups and determine their petroleum generation potential. In this case, approximately 2 mg of each isolated kerogen was analysed using a double-shot Frontier Lab Pyrolyzer (PY-2020iD) coupled with an Agilent Technologies 5975 gas chromatograph for Py-GC analysis. The gas chromatography (GC) system utilized an ultra-alloy capillary column measuring 30 m in length, with an inner diameter of 0.32 mm and a film thickness of 0.25 µm, coupled with a flame ionization detector (FID). The column’s temperature was ramped gradually from 330 to 600 °C at a rate of 25 °C/min, holding at 600 °C for 3 min, as outlined by Horsfield27 and Weiss et al.28.

For ATR-FTIR analysis, 1 mg of each kerogen sample was ground to approximately 200 meshes and placed on the Diamond/ZnSe crystal plate of the PerkinElmer Universal ATR accessory. The IR spectra were recorded in both transmittance and absorbance modes over the wavelength range of 4000 to 650 cm −1. Peak assignments were made based on comparisons with published reference spectra29–33.

Kinetics analysis

Kerogen fractions isolated from three distinct oil shale specimens were analyzed through both bulk and compositional kinetic analyses. For bulk kinetics analysis, pyrolysis was performed using the Rock–Eval instrument. The kerogen samples were heated incrementally, starting at a rate of 1 °C/min at 300 °C, followed by 5, 10, 25, and finally 50 °C/min, with a maximum temperature reaching 600 °C. The resulting data were analyzed using the Kinetics 2000 and KMOD computational platforms to derive activation energy (Ea) and pre-exponential factor (A) values, in accordance with the kinetic modeling approaches proposed by Burnham et al.34,35 and Behar et al.36. Moreover, the effect of geological heating rates on the kerogen degradation behaviour, and then hydrocarbon formation predictions was estimated through the kinetic analysis using a consistent rate at a 3.3 °C/My rate, because it corresponds to an average heating rate in most of the sedimentary basins in the world37–40.

In addition, the isolated kerogen from the studied oil shale samples were also subjected to compositional kinetic modeling in order to determine the total hydrocarbons gas (C1-C5) and total liquid hydrocarbons content that was greater than C6+. The compositional kinetic analysis was carried out by the GC–MS on the basis of m/z spectra of the analyzed compounds using a deactivated metal capillary tube as a column. The MS was operated using electron impact ionization at 70 eV, with a full scan range of 15–600 Da.

Results and discussion

Organic matter characteristics

Microscopic analysis was employed to characterize the organic facies present within the studied oil shale samples of the Palana Formation. The finding reveals a predominance of liptinite macerals in the examined samples, as depicted in Fig. 4. Fluorescence excitation under blue light, producing yellow to greenish–yellow fluorescence, served as the primary criterion for distinguishing morphology and compositional characteristics of liptinite assemblages within the analyzed samples (Fig. 4). The majority of the fluorescence liptinites were identified as alginite, which was further classified into lamalginite and telalginite based on morphological attributes (Fig. 4A–D). Telalginite manifests as thick, structured alginite, as shown in Fig. 4A, whereas lamalginite, derived from algae, appears as thin laminae, flattened discs, and discrete lenses, as evidenced in Fig. 4B–D. Additional organic constituents, such as sporinite and bituminite, were also observed in the samples (Fig. 4E, F). Sporinite occurs as a ring-agglomerated form and is characterized by yellow to orange-yellow fluorescence (Fig. 4E, F). Moreover, the occurrence of indigenous structureless bitumen, with a yellow fluorescence under UV light was also commonly observed in the studied shales (Fig. 4F), which is interpreted as bituminite II, in accordance with the classification by Pickel et al.41.

In addition, the SEM results also show that the investigated oil shale samples of Palana Formation contain structured organic matter such as alginite and sporinite (Fig. 5). The alginite organic matter is mainly observed within in the studied samples and was infused with the mineral matrix (Fig. 5A). SEM shows that the alginite organic matter is mainly characterzied by a thin laminae and flattened discs (Fig. 5B). In contrast, the majority of the sporinite is represented by spongy structure based on morphological attributes (Fig. 5C, E). Moreover, the fungi skeletal constituents also observed in the studied shale samples as identified from SEM results (Fig. 5F).

Photomicrographs of the scanning electron microscope (SEM) show the different types of organic matter (OM) in the shale samples, including (A, B) alginite assemblages, (C–E) different types of spores, and (F) fungi.

In addition, the enrichment of the structured and structureless organic matter input in the studied oil shale samples is also clearly demonstrated from the whole-kerogen composition, which is visually estimated as four groups following Tyson42. The kerogen assemblages identified in the studied samples comprise several OM groups, including fluorescent amorphous organic matter (FAOM), algal-derived OM, herbaceous components such as spores and pollen, and woody OM (Fig. 6). Visual kerogen analysis reveals that FAOM is the dominant constituent, accounting for over 85.0% of the total OM, followed by notable contributions from spore-derived and algal OM (Fig. 6A). The FAOM appears as structureless, angular aggregates exhibiting organ to yellow fluorescence (Fig. 6A).

(A) Photomicrographs of the different types of structured and structureless organic matter input into the studied oil shale samples under while and UV lights, field width = 0.2 mm; including Amorphous organic matter (AOM), palynomorphs of spore and algal species, and woody fragments. (B) Ternary diagram of amorphous organic matter (AOM), palynomorphs, and phytoclasts, showing that the extracted kerogen is mainly Type II for oil generation potential.

The high abundance and fluorescence characteristics of FAOM suggest a marine origin for this kerogen fraction, which is consistent with the interpretation of Tissot and Welte43 and Sawada and Akiyama44. Moreover, the palynomorph (i.e., spore and algal) and phytoclasts, mainly woody fragments, were also obtained in the studied oil shales (Fig. 6A). Algal OM was further classified into tasmanite, prasinophyte, and pediastrum types based on morphological features (Fig. 6A). The presence of tasmanite algae, in particular, indicates the occurrence of hydrogen-rich telalginite and lamalginite algae assemblages (Fig. 4A–D). The higher fluorescent AOM more than in the palynomorphs (i.e., spores, pollens, and algae), and phytoclasts (i.e., woody fragments) is consistent with mainly Type II kerogen and its ability to generate mainly oil (Fig. 6B).

Elemental composition of kerogen

In this study, the elemental composition (CHNS) of kerogen extracted from the Palana oil shale samples was determined, encompassing carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N) and sulfur contents, as detailed in Table 1. Most of the kerogen samples exhibited C content above 30 wt. % (ranging from 30.62 to 34.93 wt. %), with one outlier displaying a lower C content of 29.31 wt. %. In contrast, the H content was relatively elevated, varying between 3.83 wt. % and 4.41 wt. % (Table 1). These compositional characteristics yield high H/C atomic ratios, ranging from 1.47 to 1.58 (Table 1), indicating the hydrogen-rich Type II kerogens. This interpretation is supported by the Van-Krevelen diagram (Fig. 7A), based on H/C and O/C atomic ratios. The plotted H/C vs. O/C values further indicates that the kerogen is derived from a mixed assemblage of marine OM (i.e., algae and tasmanites) and terrestrial-influenced liptinitic components (i.e., resintes and sporinites). This finding is confirmed by the organic facies obtained under the microscope (Figs. 4 and 6).

However, the high H contents are consistent with the Rock–Eval hydrogen index (HI), with values of up to 544 mg HC/ g TOC and further indicating mainly hydrogen-rich Type II kerogen (Fig. 7B), aligning with previous interpretations of Singh et al.21.

The ultimate elemental results also show varying values of N and S contents in the range 0.83–1.14 wt. % and 0.70–1.48 wt. %, respectively (Table 1). In this case, N and S contents are associated with the C content and employed in the computation of the N/C and S/C atomic ratios, which were obtained with values from 0.02–0.03 and 0.02–0.04, respectively (Table 1).

The S/C atomic ratio in kerogen is widely used to distinguish kerogen types45. Walters45 reported that the high S/C atomic ratio exceeding 0.04 suggests kerogen Type-II high sulfur, while a relatively low S/C atomic ratio of between 0.02 and 0.04 is indicative of Type-II kerogen. Accordingly, the lower S/C (from 0.02 to 0.04) with high H/C atomic ratio also confirms that the extracted kerogen in the studied oil shale is mainly Type II, with moderate-to-low sulfur content.

TG/DTA results

TG/DTA results for the extracted kerogen from the studied samples are illustrated in Fig. 8. The solid red curve represents the pyrolytic weight loss behaviour, which can be delineated into four stages corresponding to specific temperature intervals with a programmed heating sequence. These sections include proximate results such as moisture, volatile matter, fixed carbon, and ash, respectively, as shown in Table 1. As observed, the ash yield in the kerogen isolated from the majority of the analysed samples is notably low, ranging from 0.01 to 0.56% (Table 1). However, ash content is the residue material remaining after burning the kerogen, and is obtained from the inorganic mineral composition. In this regard, the low ash yield in the extracted kerogen from the studied oil shale sample indicates that this kerogen can generate high-purity oil HCs, as ash refers to inorganic mineral content, which can actually reduce the quality of the generated oil.

The TG/DTA results for the extracted kerogen from the studied samples.

The results indicate that the volatile matter content of the extracted kerogen from the studied samples is notably higher than that of the fixed carbon (Table 1). In this regard, the extracted kerogen with a high volatile matter content of up to 43.26% (Table 1) indicates a high volatile organic matter and a greater potential for generating oil and gas upon heating or pyrolysis. This high volatile matter of the extracted kerogen is also demonstrated by the relatively low carbon content and relatively high hydrogen content (Table 1). Moreover, the high potential for oil and gas generation during heating is further supported by the low fixed carbon of the extracted kerogen (Table 1), which refers to the residual carbon after removing the volatile matter by heating.

Molecular structure of kerogen

FTIR spectroscopy

Herein, the functional groups present in the kerogen structure were identified and interpreted based on FTIR spectral analysis. Characteristic absorption bands corresponding to aliphatic stretching were observed within 3000–2800 cm−1 range, while aromatic C–H stretching and out–of–plane aromatic bending vibrations were detected at 3100–3000 cm−1 and 1460–1350 cm−1, respectively46. Peaks between 2920 cm−1 and 2850 cm−1 are attributed to methylene (CH2) groups, whereas the absorption near ~ 1600 cm−1 is indicative of aromatic C = C stretching vibrations46. Additionally, the spectra exhibit an asymmetric CH3 stretching band at 2955 cm − 1 and carboxy/carboyl stretching peak at 1710 cm−146.

As shown in Fig. 9A, the standard FTIR spectra of the extracted kerogen from the studied oil shale samples display highly similar features, with intensity between 3000 cm−1 and 650 cm−1 wavenumbers. In this instance, the strong absorption signals at 2915.72 cm−1 and 2884.90 cm−1, arising from CH2 stretching vibrations directly indicate the dominance of CH2 moieties in the extracted kerogen. The FTIR spectra also show the presence of distinct bands in the aliphatic stretching at 718.65 cm−1 typically associated with the skeletal vibrations of long-chain alkanes (CH2). A prominent peak between 1465.59 cm−1 and 1412.75 cm−1 is attributed to the asymmetric bending and scissoring vibrations of CH2 and CH3 groups. In contrast, a distinct peak discernible at 1376.82 cm−1 mainly arises from asymmetric bending CH3 moieties. These results effectively emphasize that the number of CH2 groups in the extracted kerogen is higher than that of CH3, consistent with a structure comprising longer aliphatic chains. A broad peak centred at 1588.25 cm−1 is attributed to aromatic C = C stretching vibrations. Moreover, a broad, moderately intense band near 3363.32 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching frequencies of OH groups.

(A) FTIR spectra of the extracted kerogen from representative studied shale samples. (B) cross plot of the A-factor and C-factor of the FTIR spectra for the extracted kerogen from the studied shale samples, showing mainly Type II kerogen.

In the summary, the FTIR spectrum shows that the kerogen from the oil shale of the Paleocene Palana Formation exhibits a complex chemical structure, with a high abundance of aliphatic groups and a relatively low abundance of aromatic and oxygen-containing functional groups. These functional groups were used to calculate two factors, such as A–factor (AF) and C–factor (CF), which follow previous works29,30,47,48. AF is the ratio of aliphatic abundance to aromatic, and CF is a measure of relative abundances of carboxyl versus carboxyl and aromatics, which are helpful in the differentiation of the kerogen groups and their hydrocarbon generation potential, as well as translating kerogen fractions29,30,47. Accordingly, the extracted kerogen from the studied oil shale samples has AF and CF values in the range of 0.75–0.78 and 0.38–0.50 (Table 1), consequently, the kerogen of the analyzed oil shales is mainly Type II, as indicated by applying the plot of AF and CF factors (Fig. 9B).

Py-gas chromatography

Py–GC results have been employed to elucidate molecular structures associated with organic sulfur in hydrocarbons, such as alkyl-chain distribution and aromaticity levels, thus providing insights into kerogen classification and associated petroleum compositions49–51.

In the present study, the Py–GC chromatograms of the kerogen extracted from the Palana Formation oil shale facies exhibit unimodal distribution characterized by prominent doublets of n–alkenes and n–alkanes, ranging from C1-5 to C36 (Fig. 10A). The chromatographic profiles display a high relative abundance of light n– alkenes and n–alkanes compounds (C1-5–C14), with comparatively lower concentrations of waxy n–alkenes and n–alkanes (C15+). Toluene and xylene were the main aromatic compounds in the the studied isolated kerogen (Fig. 10A). Lower amount of 2, 3-dimethylthiophene organic sulfur compound is also observed laterally with aromatic and aliphatic saturated hydrocarbons (Fig. 10A). Moreover, the proportions of alkyl-chain, aromatic and organic sulfur compounds in the pyrolysates of the extracted kerogen within the studied oil shale samples, including carbon number 9, xylene and 2, 3-dimethylthiophene (Fig. 10A), were estimated and found to have high percentages for C9 n–alkane and xylene in the range of 42.63–55.02% and 35.38–45.98%, respectively, with low amounts of 2, 3-dimethylthiophene organic sulfur compound, with percentages between 9.05% and 12.44% (Table 1). The pyrolysate distribution patterns of the three examined components suggest that the kerogen extracted from the oil shale predominantly corresponds to Type II (Fig. 10B), as evidenced by the ternary diagram adapted from Eglinton et al.49. This hydrogen–rich Type II kerogen is inferred to primarily generate paraffinic–naphthenic–aromatic (P–N–A) hydrocarbons with elevated wax content, as supported by the relative proportions of C1– C5, C6–C14, and C15+ of n–alkane and n–alkene distributions (Fig. 10C).

(B) ternary diagram based on n-alkyl chain length distribution of the pyrolysis products of the extracted kerogen, showing mainly Type II kerogen. (C) ternary diagram based on pyrolysate compounds, i.e., total resolved C1–C5 hydrocarbons, the sum C6–C14, and C+15 of the n–alkenes/n–alkane ranges, showing that the extracted kerogen can release mainly paraffinic–naphthenic–aromatic oils with high wax content.

Kinetics of kerogen decomposition

Bulk kinetic of kerogen decomposition and its organic facies can be evaluated using activation energy (EA) distributions and frequency factors (A)35,52,53. In this case, Type I kerogen exhibits a narrow range of Ea values between 47 and 56 kcal/mol54, while Type II and II-S kerogens display a wider range of 44 to 66 kcal/mol35,53,55,56.

In this study, the EA and A values derived from the extracted kerogen of the analysed samples are presented in Fig. 11A. The Ea distribution exhibits a broad range, spanning from 42 to 68 kcal/mol, indicating heterogeneous kinetic behavior. Meanwhile, the kerogen exhibits (A) values in the range of 2.1657E + 14/1s to 1.1054E + 15/1s (Fig. 11A).

However, the values of (Ea) and (A) are related to the type of kerogen because they are largely controlled by the constants for certain temperature and thermal conductivity properties of each group of kerogen57,58. Accordingly, the broad activation energy distributions and the frequency factor between 2E + 14/1s and 1.1E + 15/1s of the extracted kerogen are indicative for Type II kerogen originated from marine organic matter, aligning with previous interpretations59.

Moreover, the constant rate (3.3 °C/My) in the kinetic model was applied to evaluate the geological heating37–40,60, hence paleo–temperatures and subsequently thermal maturity in terms of vitrinite reflectance (%VRo) value as well as the conversion ratio (TR) of the extracted kerogen from the studied oil shales were also predicatively computed, as shown in Fig. 10B. In this respect, the lower conversion ratio of % 10 TR occurs at geological temperature in the range of 107–112 °C, corresponding to a computed %VRo of approximately between 0.62–0.66, while the 50% conversion of kerogen was computed at the peak–generation window with %Ro between 0.97 and 1.07%, corresponding to geological temperatures between 148 °C and 153 °C (Fig. 11B).

In addition, multi-component kinetic models of the extracted kerogen from the studied samples were conducted in order to compare the effect of the heating on the kerogen degradation behaviour (Table 2 and Fig. 12A). The compositional kinetic results include methane (C1), wet gas (C2-C5), light oil (C6-C14), and heavy oil (C15+). In this regard, the compositional kinetic models show that the extracted kerogen released higher amounts of liquid hydrocarbons (C2 to C15+) than dry gaseous (C1) during pyrolysis, with amounts of up 332.77 mg/g (Table 2). However, light components of wet gas (C2-C5) and light oil (C6-C14) hydrocarbons prevail over heavy components of C15+, with high amounts in the range 121.95–166.24 mg/g, 63.27–104.55 mg/g, and 38.55–61.70 mg/g, respectively (Table 2). According to this perspective of the different types hydrocarbon, the extracted kerogen from the oil shale facies of the Formation can release mainly paraffinic–naphthenic–aromatic oils with high wax content during heating (Fig. 12B), which is consistent with the distributions of the n–alkane and n–alkane molecules derived from Py-GC analysis (Fig. 10C).

Conclusions

This investigation presents the first application of standard geochemical techniques, including CHNS, FTIR, TG/DTA, Py-GC, and kinetics of the kerogen decomposition in conjunction with kerogen microscopy of the Paleocene oil shale sequence within the Palana Formation, located in the Bikaner–Nagaur Basin, western India to characterize the molecular architecture of low-mature marine kerogen, and thereby enhancing the understanding of petroleum generation mechanisms. In this regard, the key conclusions are:

The Palana kerogen exhibits highly aliphatic molecules, with low quantities of aromatic compounds, reflecting its hydrogen-rich Type II kerogen, high potential for generating P–N–A oils with high wax content. The hydrogen-rich liptinitic assemblages, including algae, bituminite, and FAOM, further corroborate this assignment.

The Palana kerogen exhibits highly aliphatic molecules, with low quantities of aromatic compounds, reflecting its hydrogen-rich Type II kerogen, high potential for generating P–N–A oils with high wax content. The hydrogen-rich liptinitic assemblages, including algae, bituminite, and FAOM, further corroborate this assignment.

The results derived from the chemical composition (CHNS), and molecular structure analyses show that Palana kerogen is characterized by a high H/C atomic ratio (˃1.40), low S/C atomic ratio (0.02–0.04), elevated A-factor values (˃0.75), further indicating Type II kerogen, with moderate-to-low sulfur content.

The results derived from the chemical composition (CHNS), and molecular structure analyses show that Palana kerogen is characterized by a high H/C atomic ratio (˃1.40), low S/C atomic ratio (0.02–0.04), elevated A-factor values (˃0.75), further indicating Type II kerogen, with moderate-to-low sulfur content.

Bulk and compositional kinetic results indicate that the Palana kerogen can convert into significant amounts of P-N-A oil, with elevated wax content across a broad geological temperature between 107 and 153 °C), consistent with %VRo values ranging from 0.62 to 1.07%.
