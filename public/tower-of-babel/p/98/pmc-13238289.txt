Tissue‐level heterogeneity in FECD: Descemet's membrane phenotypes and association with TCF4 CTG18.1 expansion†
Authors: Sayo Maeno, Yoshinori Oie, Chifune Kai, Kohji Nishida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLate‐onset Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) is commonly framed as a corneal endothelial disease characterised by guttae accumulation and progressive thickening of Descemet's membrane (DM). However, clinical forms and evolutionary profiles vary widely. Vaitinadapoulé et al systematically analysed as many as 500 keratoplasty‐derived DMs from 25 European centres using flat mounts and transmitted light microscopy with structured scoring of extracellular matrix (ECM) lesions. The study revealed five FECD phenotypes and spatially organised lesion patterns and supports multiple pathological phenotypes. Radial organisation emerged as a dominant architectural theme, often accompanied by peripheral striae, with changes evident in both central and peripheral DM. Principal component analysis and unsupervised clustering showed modest separability, while manual classification identified dominant phenotypes with appreciable overlap, suggesting pathological phenotypes arise from a combination of lesion features and organisational patterns. In a genotyped subset, DM architectures differed by TCF4 CTG18.1 expansion status, linking tissue architecture to repeat biology. This ECM atlas provides a pathology‐anchored framework for FECD heterogeneity and motivates prospective clinical correlation and quantitative image analysis. © 2026 The Author(s). Late‐onset Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) is often described as a clinicopathological entity defined by guttae formation and progressive thickening of Descemet's membrane (DM), and subsequent corneal morphological change or visual disturbances [1, 2, 3]. Yet, the highly variable expressivity of FECD, in terms of both severity and speed of progression, is widely recognised by clinicians. This broad spectrum necessitates accurate classification for personalised care, and in research, poses a challenge to the development of treatment strategies.

The study by Vaitinadapoulé et al [4] provides strong support for the view that the widely recognised clinical heterogeneity of FECD is reflected in the anatomopathological diversity of DM. By systematically analysing 500 flat‐mounted DMs collected across 25 European centres, the authors were able to identify the types and frequency of extracellular matrix (ECM) lesions and propose a histological classification based on organisational patterns, including a prominent radial organisation and peripheral striae. Collectively, the work substantiates FECD heterogeneity with five phenotypes (centre, radial, epicentres, guttae only, and fused) at the tissue level and provides a framework to better capture the broad clinical spectrum of FECD.

A major strength of this work is its large, multi‐centre design combined with a standardised workflow. Using flat‐mounted DMs imaged by transmitted light microscopy, the authors applied a structured scoring system for guttae and additional ECM abnormalities and assessed the impact of pre‐analytical conditions, noting that fixation/transport could affect the visibility of the finest structures when subtle ECM features are compared across centres. The most conceptually unifying insights are spatial. Alongside the common central–ring–peripheral distribution of guttae, radial organisation emerges as a dominant architectural theme, encompassing radially aligned guttae and other radially oriented DM structures and accompanied in a substantial subset by peripheral striae. Together, these findings support the view that FECD constitutes separable yet co‐existing lesion patterns across the centre–periphery axis and reflects an ordered ECM remodelling programme rather than a purely stochastic accumulation of lesions.

Multivariate analysis supports this architectural heterogeneity. Principal component analysis (PCA) and unsupervised clustering indicate modest separability, consistent with partially overlapping phenotypic axes rather than sharply bounded subtypes, while manual grouping highlights dominant patterns (including a conventional central ECM‐covered form and a radial pattern) with appreciable overlap. Interpreted biologically, these results suggest that FECD DM pathology is best understood as phenotypes built from recurring lesion features and their organisational patterns. Together, these analyses strongly support the overarching message of the paper: tissue‐level heterogeneity is a defining feature of FECD. Importantly, in the genotyped subset, histological patterns differed by trinucleotide repeat (TNR) expansion status in transcription factor 4 (TCF4), with the non‐expansion group enriched for radial/fused components and peripheral striae. These results suggest that TNR expansion status is associated with distinct ECM architectures, with the non‐expansion group enriched for peripheral‐radial features and fused guttae, rather than simply reflecting a shift in overall disease severity. If validated in larger genotyped series, these findings provide a morphological substrate for genotype‐informed stratification and suggest that peripheral lesions may be informative indicators of biology beyond TNR expansion status.

Translating these tissue architectures into practice will require linkage to clinical phenotypes and progression. Integrating this atlas with prior work highlights both opportunity and caution. The systematic review by Oie et al [5] highlighted substantial variability in FECD diagnostic criteria and severity classifications, underscoring the need for more standardised approaches. The present study contributes a pathology‐anchored framework by introducing objective, reproducible tissue‐level dimensions that may help rationalise this inconsistency across centres. Linking these tissue patterns to a clinical phenotype will be an important next step, given the absence of detailed clinical data and the likely variation in disease stage at transplantation. The ongoing prospective study (NCT05742321; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05742321) should therefore be pivotal in determining whether the proposed DM architectures map onto distinct clinical phenotypes, differential progression patterns, or shared late‐stage pathways.

More broadly, TNR expansions show marked population differences – common in Western cohorts but less frequent in Asian patients [6, 7, 8]. Multiple expansion‐linked pathogenic mechanisms have been proposed, including dysregulated TCF4 isoform expression, RNA‐mediated toxicity with splicing misregulation, repeat‐associated non‐AUG translation of toxic peptides, and age‐ and tissue‐specific somatic instability [9]. Yet, despite these molecular hypotheses, associations between expansion status and clinical phenotype remain controversial [6, 10, 11, 12], and both expansion and non‐expansion FECD can present with similar clinical appearances. The present study raises the possibility that genotype‐associated differences may be reflected in DM organisational patterns (such as peripheral striae and fused guttae), which are not readily captured by routine in vivo metrics. Moreover, evidence of somatic instability in repeat length [13, 14, 15] offers a mechanistic bridge, raising the possibility that tissue‐specific and, potentially, region‐specific repeat dynamics may contribute to spatial variation in DM architecture. The pathophysiology of repeat non‐expansion FECD, including its causative genes, remains unknown, and further investigation is warranted.

The authors' limitations outline a clear roadmap for next steps. Prospective clinical correlation will be essential to link DM architectures to phenotype and progression. Mechanistic interpretation – particularly migration‐based hypotheses – will ultimately require cellular and spatial resolution of endothelial states and their ECM programs. Finally, automated image analysis may strengthen reproducibility and improve stratification in future cohorts. Rarer genetic causes and epigenetic mechanisms also warrant future study.

In summary, Vaitinadapoulé et al [4] have delivered the largest and most systematic DM morphology study in FECD to date and have demonstrated that FECD heterogeneity extends beyond clinical variability to become a defining, quantifiable feature of tissue pathology. The identification of pervasive radial organisation, peripheral lesions that may be under‐recognised, and architecture‐specific associations with TNR expansion status provides a practical bridge between pathology, genetics, and clinical stratification. As FECD management moves towards precision‐oriented endpoints and targeted interventions, this ECM atlas offers a timely foundation for phenotype‐aware, genotype‐informed frameworks that better reflect the disease's biological heterogeneity.
