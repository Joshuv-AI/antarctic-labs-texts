EEG‐based information transfer paths during motion discrimination: Detectable differences between normal cognition and MCI
Authors: Alina Renli, Boxin Sun, Ming Gu, Tim Martin, Voyko Kavcic, Tongtong Li, Bruno Giordani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDThis study explores how cognitive impairment can affect the effective connectivity of the brain network under task stimuli.METHODOur research focuses on task‐based electroencephalography (64‐channel), in which participants were asked to perform a motion direction discrimination task. The current dataset includes 56 consensus‐diagnosed, community‐dwelling American seniors (ages 60–90 years, 28 normal cognition [NC]; 28 mild cognitive impairment [MCI]) recruited through Wayne State University and Michigan Alzheimer's Disease Research Center. We evaluate the effective connectivity across all the possible region of interest (ROI) pairs using causalized convergent cross mapping and identify possible discrepancies between seniors with NC and MCI.RESULTSIndividuals with MCI exhibit weaker links than those with NC in some region pairs but concurrently activate more information transfer paths in other region pairs, especially in the frontal and temporal areas.CONCLUSIONOur results demonstrate the compensatory mechanism of the brain communication network for weakened links in critical regions under cognitive impairment.Highlights Existing work on brain connectivity analysis was mainly focused on functional connectivity rather than effective connectivity, making it a challenge to fully understand the dynamic interactions and causal relationships within the brain network.As an effort to address this problem, in this study, we applied the new causality model, causalized convergent cross mapping (cCCM), to electroencephalography data under motion discrimination tasks and aimed to capture the differences in brain effective connectivity between mild cognitive impairment (MCI) patients and those with normal cognition (NC).It was shown that cCCM can capture the inter‐region information transfer which may not be captured by Pearson correlation. That is, region pairs with low functional connectivity may still exhibit strong effective connectivity.Our results indicated that those with NC exhibit stronger effective connectivity than MCI individuals across certain region pairs, and the number of such region pairs (where those with NC shows more active information transfer) increases as the load imposed upon the brain increases.Concurrently, those with MCI activate many more information transfer paths than NC individuals during the motion detection task. This may reflect the compensatory mechanism of the brain communication network for weakened links under cognitive impairment.

INTRODUCTION

Existing research suggests that the abnormal brain functions in Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology are closely related to the changes in connectivity patterns across brain regions. 1 , 2 Prior work on brain connectivity analysis has largely been focused on functional connectivity, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 which quantifies the mutual dependence between brain regions via measures of Pearson correlation, or mutual information, and is inherently non‐directional.

In contrast, effective connectivity (also known as causality) assesses the causal influences one brain region exerts over another and is directional. 8 Recall that correlation may not be able to predict causality, as two times series which are weakly correlated may still have strong causal coupling between them. 9 This implies that brain regions with low functional connectivity may exhibit strong effective connectivity. Therefore, compared to functional connectivity, effective connectivity is expected to provide more insight on the impact of cognitive impairment on the direction and strength of information transfer in the brain network.

The first practical causal analysis framework was Granger causality (GC), which was proposed by Granger in 1969. 10 GC is a statistical approach that relies on a linear prediction model, and aims to determine whether the values of one time series are useful in predicting the future values of the other. Since then, other causality models have been proposed. Representative frameworks include directed information (DI, 1990) 11 and transfer entropy (TE, 2000), 12 which are both information‐theoretic models, and dynamic causal modeling (DCM, 2003), 13 which is implemented using a Bayesian model comparison procedure.

More recently, the convergent cross mapping (CCM, 2012) approach 14 , 15 is attracting more attention from the research community because it is much easier to implement than DCM, and can detect weak causal couplings which may not be identified by GC. In addition, it was shown that casualized convergent cross mapping (cCCM, 2024) 9 is conditionally equivalent to DI under Gaussian variables. Noticeably, it has been observed that in practical implementation, cCCM is generally more robust than DI in causality detection, 9 , 16 because it does not rely on probability estimation as DI does. In short, cCCM reflects the strength of directed information transfer across brain regions and can serve as an effective tool in detecting both unidirectional and bidirectional causality in the brain networks. 9

Concurrently, as a cost‐effective and non‐invasive brain activity recording technique, electroencephalography (EEG) has attracted ever‐increasing attention in the AD research community. Due to its high temporal resolution, EEG has the advantage of capturing rapid information transfer activities in the brain network. In literature, EEG‐based effective connectivity has been used as a potential biomarker for the discrimination of normal cognition (NC), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and AD. 17 , 18

In this study, we explore the differences in effective connectivity between participants with NC and those diagnosed with MCI using cCCM based on the EEG signals collected during a motion direction discrimination task. The overall objective of this research is to better understand the impact of cognitive impairment on directed information transfer within brain networks, as well as identify potential patterns pertinent to informing new insights for the early detection and treatment of MCI.

Our analysis indicates that during the motion detection task, MCI patients exhibit lower effective connectivity than those with NC in some EEG region pairs. To compensate for the weakened links, MCI patients increase the information transfer in other region pairs and also activate many more information transfer paths than those with NC during the motion detection task, particularly in the frontal and temporal areas. It was also observed that the most significant differences in brain effective connectivity between NC and MCI individuals occur in (i) the beta band, associated with working memory, focus, and analytical processing, and (ii) the low‐gamma band, associated with heightened perception, intense concentration, and learning. 19 , 20 Our findings provide an addition to the existing understanding of brain functions and compensatory mechanisms in AD pathology. 21 , 22 , 23

MATERIALS AND METHODOLOGY

Participants and demographic data

The sample set of this study includes 56 consensus‐diagnosed, community‐dwelling (senior) Americans with subjective cognitive complaints (ages 60–90 years, 28 NC and 28 MCI). All participants were recruited from the greater Detroit area in Michigan through the Institute of Gerontology at Wayne State University, the Institute of Social Research at University of Michigan, and the Michigan Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (MADRC). All participants are considered to be at high risk of cognitive decline, that is, they considered themselves to be fully functioning, but were concerned that they may have experienced a potential decline in cognitive ability over the past year. All participants were diagnosed through the MADRC consensus conference using the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) Uniform Data Set (UDS) version 3. 24 Reversible cognitive differences in each group were ruled out during the consensus conferences. Because of the small number of non‐amnestic MCI (naMCI) participants, both amnestic MCI (aMCI) and naMCI participants were first combined into a single MCI group for analysis, and then studied separately as well. All procedures were in accordance with the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki 25 and approved by the Wayne State University Institutional Review Board and the University of Michigan Medical School Institutional Review Board. After a full review, all participants signed an approved written consent document.

The demographic information of our participants is presented in the demographic table, featured in Figure 1A. There were no significant differences among NC, aMCI, and naMCI participants in terms of age and education. Two additional tables, which summarize and compare the UDS items (1) between NC and the combined MCI group (Table S1 in supporting information) and (2) between the aMCI and naMCI participants (Table S2 in supporting information) are provided in the Supplementary File in supporting information.

Non‐invasive EEG data collection

Brain Vision equipment was used to record scalp EEG activity with a non‐invasive high‐density actiCap (64 active electrodes), modified according to the International 10‐20 System. The EEG recordings were performed in a community center at the University of Michigan Detroit Center or the Institute of Gerontology at Wayne State University using the same EEG system.

During the EEG recording, 26 , 27 participants were given two 3 minute resting‐state EEG periods, between which they were asked to perform a motion direction discrimination task with random dot stimuli (Figure 1B). Successive trials were separated by a random inter‐trial interval (ITI) that varied between 1.5 and 3 seconds with a uniform distribution, and a fixation spot was shown during each ITI. Each trial started with the onset of a random dot stimulus, which lasted 500 to 1050 ms. After that, the dots began to move either to the left or the right for 500 ms. Concurrently, the participants were given the Go or No‐Go indicators randomly. At a Go trial, participants were requested to identify the direction of motion by pressing one of two buttons on a standard computer mouse after the motion stopped. At a No‐Go trial, no actions were needed. This makes the No‐Go trial a harder task, as people need to exercise greater control to refrain from pressing the button intuitively.

During the course of the task, the specific time period types being examined include: (1) Stimulus Onset to Go/No‐Go indication, (2) Go indication to Motion‐Stop, (3) No‐Go indication to Motion‐Stop, (4) the Button‐Press period. Originally, all the participants were expected to present at least 60 trials for each period type. However, during EEG signal pre‐processing, (1) all the trials in which the participant pressed the button before motion stops were discarded, and (2) all the trials contaminated by exceptional noise and/or artifacts were also discarded. As such, there are a few participants with < 50 trials for some period types. To ensure data balance and quality, as well as sample size maximization, for each participant, we only worked with up to the first 50 clean trials after the EEG pre‐processing.

This study focuses on the analysis of brain effective connectivity for both NC and MCI participants during motion direction discrimination tasks and investigates how cognitive impairment may impact the direction and strength of information transfer under task stimuli.

EEG data preprocessing and the selection of regions of interest

As in our previous work, 28 , 29 Analyzer 2 (Brain Vision, Inc.) was used for pre‐processing of the baseline EEG data following the recommendations from the Organization for Human Brain Mapping Committee on Best Practice in Data Analysis and Sharing Magnetoencephalography and EEG committee. 30 Off‐line inspection was used to identify and remove segments of EEG contaminating either excessive noise, saturation, or lack of EEG activity.

As illustrated in Figure 1C, a total of 12 regions of interest (ROIs) were selected as in our previous work: 28 , 29 right frontal—RF (Fp1, AF7, AF3, F7, F5, F3), medium frontal—MF (F1, Fz, F2, FC1, FC2), left frontal—LF (F4, Fp2, AF4, AF8, F6, F8), left temporal—LT (FT9, FT7, T7, TP7, TP9), left central—LC (FC5, FC3, C5, C3, CP5, CP3), medial central—MC (C1, Cz, C2, CP1, CPz, CP2), right central—RC (FC4, FC6, C4, C6, CP4, CP6), right temporal—RT (FT10, FT8, T8, TP8, TP10), left parietal—LP (P7, P5, P3, PO7, PO3), medial parietal—MP (P1, Pz, P2, POz), right parietal—RP (P4, P6, P8, PO4, PO8), occipital—Occ (PO9, O1, Oz, O2, PO10).

Prior to further analysis, the current source density (CSD) was computed from the scalp voltage of the EEG signal for all the ROIs using the CSD Toolbox in MATLAB. 31 , 32 , 33 CSD was used due to its reference‐free nature and superior spatial resolution compared to raw EEG. Additional details are available in the Supplemental File in supporting information.

Brain effective connectivity analysis using cCCM

In this study, the brain effective connectivity for both NC and MCI was evaluated using cCCM. cCCM was developed based on the state space reconstruction approach, CCM. 15 Consider two dynamically coupled variables X and Y, which share the same attractor manifold M. Let X n = [X 1, X 2, · · ·, Xn ] and Y n = [Y 1, Y 2, · · ·, Yn ] be the time series consisting of samples of random variables X and Y, respectively, then the shadow manifolds of M, M x and M y, can be constructed from X n and Y n, respectively. When there exists a causal coupling between X and Y, then we can predict or estimate each sample of Y based on the samples of X and the past and future values of Y itself through geometric cross mapping from M x to M. CCM derives its name from the fact that when there exists a causal coupling between X and Y, the predicted value of each sample of Y converges to its true value as long as the data length n is sufficiently large.

One limitation of CCM lies in its use of both the past and future values of X and Y to predict the current value of Y. This is inconsistent with the widely accepted definition of causality analysis, which aims to determine whether the past values of a random variable X can help predict of the future values of the other, Y, in addition to the past values of Y itself. The underlying assumption here is that the future values of one process cannot influence the past values of another. 34

If we limit ourselves to only using the past values of X and Y to predict the future values of Y, we obtain cCCM. In our previous research, 9 , 16 we demonstrated through both theoretical derivation and numerical analysis (based on experimental functional magnetic resonance imaging data) that cCCM is conditionally equivalent to DI, and therefore provides an alternative but superior way to measure the directed information transfer in the brain networks. In short, cCCM has proven to be an effective and easy‐to‐implement technique for identifying both linear and non‐linear causal coupling in neural networks, as well as a wide spectrum of other applications. 9 , 16 A detailed description on the cCCM algorithm can be found in the Supplemental File in supporting information. The MATLAB code of cCCM algorithm used here can be found in the public domain in Sun. 35

In this study, cCCM was applied to the CSD signals of all the ROI pairs to compare the differences in brain effective connectivity—which reflects the directions and strength of information transfer in the brain network—between NC and MCI during the motion direction discrimination task. In this article, we first analyzed the effective connectivity based on the whole band signals (0.1–70 Hz), 36 and then turned to individual frequency bands, including the delta (2–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (12–30 Hz), and low‐gamma (30–48 Hz) bands. 37 , 38 , 39

Because there are 12 EEG‐based ROIs and effective connectivity is directional, for each participant, the cCCM‐based effective connectivity was evaluated across all the 12 × 11 = 132 region pairs. More specifically, for each trial, effective connectivity was evaluated for every task period, including Stimulus Onset, Go to Motion Stop, No‐Go to Motion Stop, and Button Press.

After that, the resulting cCCM values (which encompassed all the trials for each subject) were used to conduct t tests between the NC and MCI groups. This was done to identify the region pairs exhibiting significant differences in the mean cCCM values between NC and MCI. For each directional region pair, the standard error of the mean (SEM) cCCM value was also calculated for both NC and MCI groups to reflect potential fluctuations of the brain effective connectivity within each group.

Those with MCI exhibit weakened effective connectivity across certain region pairs. In an effort to fulfill the task demands, MCI individuals demonstrate higher effective connectivity in numerous other region pairs as compensation. Figures 2 and 3 present the region pairs in which those with NC and MCI exhibit the most prominent differences in cCCM‐based effective connectivity, which reflects the strength of directed information transfer from one brain region to the other. More specifically, Figure 2 illustrates the mean cCCM values of NC and MCI individuals during different tasks periods, and Figure 3 shows the differences of the mean cCCM values between those with NC and MCI, D=ρcCCM,NC−ρcCCM,MCI, in all the directional ROI pairs and across all the task periods.

From our numerical results (Figure 3), it can be seen that, in all four periods, individuals with MCI exhibit lower effective connectivity in certain region pairs, especially in LT→LC, LC→LT, RC→RT, RT→RC, with P values < 10−3. This may be attributed to the fact that the temporal lobe, which encompasses the hippocampus, is largely involved in the encoding and processing of memory and is also relevant in visual recognition. 40 To compensate for this effect, MCI exhibits higher effective connectivity in other region pairs. The directed region pairs in which individuals with MCI exhibit higher mean cCCM values are shown in Figures 2 and 3.

It is also interesting to observe that in Go, No‐Go, and Button‐Press periods, those with MCI show very active effective connectivity from MP→Occ and Occ→MP, with the mean cCCM value being > 0.45, which is significantly higher than in individuals with NC (P value < 10−3). This may suggest that MCI individuals are exerting greater effort in capturing visual information (Occ) and processing/integrating the collected information (MP) during the motion detection task.

The number of region pairs in which those with NC show higher effective connectivity than MCI participants increases as the load imposed upon the brain increases during the motion detection task. From Figure 3, it is also observed that consistent with our expectations, the number of region pairs in which those with NC show higher effective connectivity (i.e., more active information transfer) than individuals with MCI increases in the order of (from lowest to highest) Stimulus Onset (18 region pairs) < No‐Go (26 region pairs) < Go (32 region pairs) < Button Press (45 region pairs), which is associated with the increased load imposed upon the brain as the task progresses.

To compensate for weakened effective connectivity links, MCI participants activate many more information transfer paths than those with NC during the motion detection task. The compensating phenomenon in MCI is elaborated with greater clarity in Figure 4, where all the active information transfer paths are illustrated for both NC and MCI participants. Here, using the NC group as the benchmark, a directed link from one region to the other is denoted an active path if its mean cCCM value is 5% higher than the average of the cCCM values corresponding to all the directed region pairs for the NC group.

It can be seen from Figure 4 that in all four task periods, NC and MCI participants share some active paths (depicted in purple). Beyond these, NC individuals recruit a few unique pathways (depicted in blue) unshared by those with MCI. Concurrently, MCI participants engage a far greater quantity of total pathways in completing the task, displaying a noticeably higher number of unique paths (depicted in red) unshared by NC. That is, to compensate for weakened effective connectivity links across certain brain regions due to neural deterioration, MCI patients activate a significantly larger number of information transfer paths than NC participants in all the periods of the motion detection task. This phenomenon may reflect the inherent diversity and self‐compensating mechanisms of the brain network, akin to navigating detours—often involving longer and less convenient routes—in real‐life scenarios in which road blockages are encountered.

From Figure 4, it can also be observed that both the NC and MCI groups show more active information transfer during the Go, No‐Go, and Button‐Press periods than the Stimulus Onset period, the last of which requires less effort for information processing and transfer.

Region‐wise, MCI patients show more active paths in frontal and temporal areas, especially during the Go and No‐Go periods. From Figure 3, it can be seen that MCI participants show lower effective activity in certain region pairs in the frontal and parietal areas, but as a compensation, MCI patients also exhibit higher effective activity in other region pairs in the same areas. This phenomenon can be observed more clearly from Figure 4—MCI participants demonstrate many more active paths in regions in frontal and temporal areas, especially during the Go and No‐Go periods. Our result is consistent with existing findings, which propose that AD pathology may alter the information transfer in the frontal and temporal areas. 41

More details are provided in the following subsections.

Alpha, delta, and theta bands

The alpha band (8–12 Hz) is generally associated with passive attention, general alerting, and ongoing visual processing tasks. 42 Its oscillations are implicated in selective attention mechanisms, as they persist in EEG even during demanding tasks. 42 Notably, the region RC appears frequently in links where NC participants exhibit significantly greater mean cCCM values than MCI patients (e.g., RC→Occ, RC→MC, RC→RT). A similar phenomenon is observed for MC (e.g., in links MC→RT, MC→LF, LT→MC), suggesting that RC and MC serve as hubs for information exchange in the brain network during the motion detection task. Thus, it is plausible that the central electrodes reflect overlapping EEG activity in the frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes—areas critical for information processing, cognitive control, attention, learning, and memory. 43

On the other hand, MCI patients exhibit higher mean cCCM values than NC participants in certain other links, as shown in Figure 5. Compellingly, a number of frontal and parietal regions are featured recurrently—namely MF/RF/LF (e.g., MF→LC, MF→LF, RF→MF, LT→RF), MP (e.g., MP→MC, MP→Occ), and RP (e.g., RP→LC, RP→RT, RP→RC). Existing research has proposed that in AD and cognitive decline, compensatory brain mechanisms may manifest in the form of greater neuronal activity in the frontal and parietal regions—a finding supported by the above results. 44

The delta band (2–4 Hz) is associated with deep, restorative sleep or relaxation corresponding to repair. 45 The NC group exhibits slightly higher mean cCCM values in temporal regions (e.g., RT→RC, LT→MF, LT→Occ, LT→RT), while the MCI group is often higher in links involving LC (e.g., LC→RT, LC→MF, LC→LF). However, despite statistical significance being recorded in all four task periods, the mean cCCM values of NC and MCI groups are essentially comparable, with no greatly observable discrepancies.

Activity within the theta band (4–8 Hz) is recognized for its correspondence with information encoding and retrieval of information, memory processes, learning, and cognitive control, among other functions. 46 Statistically significant differences in the mean cCCM values are observed between NC and MCI individuals for the theta band in all periods (P value < 10−3). Comparatively, the theta band demonstrates greater divergences between those with NC and MCI relative to that observed in the alpha and delta bands. During the four task periods, NC participants often feature higher effective connectivity in links involving RT/LT (e.g., RT→RC, RP→RT, LT→Occ, LT→LF). Concurrently, those with MCI often display higher effective connectivity in links associated with LC (e.g., LP→LC, LF→LC, LP→LC), as well as MP/RP (e.g., Occ→MP, MF→MP) and RP (RP→LP, RP→LP, Occ→RP). These differential patterns are consistent with existing literature in the field. 44

Beta and low‐gamma bands

The most significant differences between the NC and MCI participants occur in the beta and low‐gamma bands.

The beta band (12–30 Hz) is associated with active thinking, focused attention, and alertness. 47 Consistent with observations made from the entire frequency spectrum, NC individuals demonstrate stronger effective connectivity than those with MCI from LT→LC and RT→RC, indicating a more efficiently operated network in the temporal and central areas of the brain crucial to memory, information processing and retention, and task completion. Demonstrated in Figure 6A–D, all four task periods seemingly exhibit similar patterns.

Individuals with MCI exhibit significantly higher effective connectivity than NC from MP→Occ and MP→Occ in all the periods during the motion detection tasks, suggesting that those with MCI are exerting more effort in information capturing, processing, and integration. It can also be observed that MCI individuals exhibit a greater quantity of information transfer in certain regions pairs within the frontal, parietal, and temporal areas.

Note that the frontal, parietal, and temporal regions are often susceptible to age‐related alterations, marked by a posterior‐to‐anterior migration of neural activity. 48 As such, observed changes in effective connectivity within these areas are particularly noteworthy—such shifts in neural connectivity could signal a progression in AD pathology, necessitating closer scrutiny and warranting further investigation and heightened vigilance.

The low‐gamma band (30–48 Hz) corresponds to high‐level information processing and encoding, perceptual processes, attention, and memory, among other cognitive functions. 47 , 49 Highly consistent with analysis of the whole frequency spectrum, within the low‐gamma band (please refer to Figure 6E–H), the NC group exhibits heightened effective connectivity over MCI across links such as LC→LT, RC→RT, LT→MC, and RC→LF, to list a few, while the MCI group displays higher effective connectivity across MP→Occ and Occ→MP. MCI participants also show altered directed information transfer in the frontal, temporal, and parietal areas, indicating a robust compensatory mechanism at play across all four task periods for MCI patients during the motion direction detection task. Our results are consistent with existing findings. In literature, it has been reported that low‐gamma oscillations were actively involved in neural information transfer in various neuropsychological and behavioral contexts. Furthermore, gamma activity has also been associated with disorders of the central nervous system, including but not limited to MCI and AD pathology. 50

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the differences in effective connectivity between participants with NC and MCI using cCCM based on EEG signals recorded during motion direction discrimination tasks. Our analysis revealed that during the motion detection task, MCI participants appeared to exhibit weakened effective connectivity in specific region pairs. However, they also appeared to compensate for reduced strength in vital connections by increasing the information transfer in other region pairs and activating a greater quantity of information transfer paths, particularly within the frontal and temporal areas. The statistical power of the study ranged from 0.92 to 0.99, varying with the specific ROI pairs and task periods examined.

It was also shown that the most significant differences in effective connectivity between those with NC and MCI emerge in the beta and low‐gamma bands. This aligns with expectations, as the beta and low‐gamma bands are believed to be closely associated with critical cognitive functions such as problem solving and engaging, and intense concentration and learning, respectively—all integral functions involved in the motion detection tasks. Our findings are consistent with existing literature while providing novel insights into the existing understanding of brain functions and compensation mechanisms in AD pathology.

One limitation of this study is the small number of naMCI participants. The current dataset includes 28 NC and 28 MCI participants, of which 20 were aMCI and only 8 were naMCI. Due to the very limited number of naMCI participants, in our study, naMCI and aMCI participants were first combined into one MCI group and compared to the NC group. Then, with the limited data set, we also compared the cCCM‐based effective connectivity of aMCI and naMCI groups. Our analysis (Figure 7) suggests that those with aMCI and naMCI share some common active paths, but each group also has their unique paths. Compared to those with naMCI, aMCI participants demonstrate a greater number of active information transfer paths during the Go and No‐Go task periods. Our analysis also echoes existing findings 51 that naMCI participants are closer to those with NC than aMCI, which is associated with more substantial and diverse brain abnormalities. 52 We are continuing our effort to recruit more naMCI participants and hope to carry out a more comprehensive analysis on the effective connectivity of aMCI and naMCI individuals in the future.

Another limitation is the lack of analysis on the evolution of effective connectivity as a function of time. However, we are actively collecting the longitudinal data of all the returned participants through the MADRC and expect to report the finding on the evolution of effectivity connectivity in our future work.

We would like to point out that the higher cCCM values in the lower frequency bands (such as delta, theta, and alpha) do not necessarily indicate that these bands demonstrate more active information transfer than the higher frequency bands or the whole band. This can be attributed to the fact that the cCCM value is a relative measure of the strength of the directed information transfer and is sensitive to the frequency range and sampling rate under consideration, and therefore should be compared under the same frequency range and same sampling rate. Note that the sampling period for this study is 2 ms—this implies excessive oversampling for the lower frequency bands and leads to a higher density neighborhood and hence more accurate prediction or signal reconstruction, which in turn results in higher cCCM values.

In this study, a comparative analysis was performed between cCCM and Pearson correlation values across all the possible ROI region pairs during all the task periods. Notably, it was found that across every task period, there were numerous region pairs in which cCCM values were much larger than their corresponding Pearson correlation values for both NC and MCI cohorts. Take the NC group at the Go period (Figure 8), for example—119 out of the 132 directional region pairs yielded a cCCM to Pearson correlation ratio (ρcCCM/ρpearson) > 5, while 64 pairs exceeded a ratio of 10, with one instance even reaching as high as 84.87.

From an information‐theoretic perspective, the underlying argument is that Pearson correlation characterizes the instantaneous information transfer between the current values of X and Y (i.e., Xn and Yn ). cCCM, on the other hand, considers the overall impact of both the current and previous values of X (i.e., Xn, Xn ‐1, · · ·, Xn‐L ) on Yn . When the delay in information transmission from one brain region to the other is larger than one sampling period, the impact of the previous values of X on Yn (measured by the cCCM value) would be much larger than the impact of the current value of X on Yn (measured by the Pearson correlation).

Our study showed that cCCM‐based effective connectivity can capture the directed information transfer between brain regions which may not be captured by Pearson correlation, thus demonstrating the importance of studying brain effective connectivity as a complement to functional connectivity, and highlighting the unique advantages of cCCM as a metric of brain information transfer.

Overall, our results suggest that alternations—both decreases or increases—in effective connectivity may serve as indicators associated with MCI or AD pathology. Note that EEG can provide real‐time evaluation of the neural activity at individual brain regions and the connectivity across them. One possible future work, therefore, is to explore whether EEG‐based effective connectivity could be exploited as features for machine learning–driven early detection and prediction of MCI, and to evaluate the potential of EEG for personalizing the diagnosis and treatment of cognitive decline and AD.
