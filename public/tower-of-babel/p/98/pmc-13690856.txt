Evaluating the reach of restrictive dietary practices: prevalence and awareness among young females (18–30 years)
Authors: Disha Mogre, Parth Tailor, Alkama Mulla, Pooja Panchal, Atul A Gokhale, Kavitha Menon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeGrowing popularity of restrictive dietary practices exists among young women; however, their effects on health and factors driving adoption of restrictive dietary practices among young Indian women remain underexplored. The study assessed prevalence, awareness, and effects of perceived restrictive dietary practices among young adult Indian women (18–30 years).MethodsA cross-sectional pilot study using online survey was performed pan-India using a pre-tested questionnaire. A total of 230 young women who provided consent and met eligibility criteria completed the study. Information on socio-demographics, nutrition knowledge, dietary habits, and perceived effects of restrictive dietary practices were collected. Descriptive statistics and chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests were performed to investigate associations between age, education attainment status, and outcomes.ResultsThe study reported a prevalence of adoption of restrictive dietary practices was 20.9%, with higher adoption rates among women > 23 years of age (31.7%). The ketogenic diet was the most popular perceived restrictive dietary practice; however, intermittent fasting was the most widely practiced (65.6%). Personal factors, especially physical appearance, predominantly motivated the adoption of restrictive dietary practices (96.3%). Postgraduate participants demonstrated significantly higher nutrition knowledge and healthier dietary practices (p < 0.05) than graduates. Younger women and postgraduates reported significantly lower perceived negative physical and psychological effects of restrictive dieting.ConclusionThe prevalence of restrictive dieting among young adult Indian women, primarily driven by personal factors rather than social pressures. Age and education shape awareness, adoption, and perceived effects. Enhancing nutrition education, formal nutrition education, and digital literacy strategies is imperative to mitigate misconceptions and promote evidence-based dietary practices. Further larger studies with objective assessments of nutritional status and nutritional adequacy are recommended.Level of EvidenceNo level of evidence, Basic Science.

Introduction

Restrictive dietary practice is a broad term that encompasses restricting certain nutrients, timing, quantity, or overall food consumption without causing malnutrition [1]. However, long-term adherence to such diets leads to adverse health outcomes, including increased risk for eating disorders, negative body image, and nutritional deficiencies [2–4]. The most reported restrictive dietary practices include the ketogenic diet, intermittent fasting, the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, the vegan diet, and the paleo diet [5]. However, some of these diets, such as the DASH diet, have clinical evidence in the management of diseases. Although a few of these, such as intermittent fasting, vegan diet, and ketogenic diets, have limited evidence for beneficial health effects, they are nutritionally deficient and hence not recommended for long-term use [6].

Restrictive dietary practices are highly prevalent among women who adopt them for general health improvements, weight loss, prevention of diseases, or under social pressures related to their body image [6]. On the other hand, adolescents and older adults are vulnerable to restrictive dietary practices due to poor body image, low self-esteem, and susceptibility to unsubstantiated health claims [7]. The adoption of restrictive dietary practices is driven by social and media influences, with media and interpersonal factors motivating the adoption and maintenance of these diets [8].

With the rising prevalence of obesity, a surge in the adoption of these diets is observed. Among U.S. young adults, the prevalence of restrictive eating was reported to be 20–25% [9], 69% of U.S. women were on restrictive diets [10]; and among Italian young adults, the rate was around 30% [11]. Emerging evidence from India indicates a growing preference for intermittent fasting, ketogenic diets, and detox plans among urban Indian women aged 18–35 years [12–14]. Similarly, data from the Philippines and Mongolia reported intermittent fasting and the ketogenic diet to be the most adopted restrictive dietary practices, respectively [15, 16]. In India, there is limited region-specific and fragmented information available on the adoption of restrictive dietary practices among young women, which is insufficient to provide insights into their knowledge, perceptions, determinants of restrictive dietary practices, and the health effects of restrictive dietary practices. Additionally, little evidence exists on how age, education, and nutrition knowledge shape the adoption of restrictive dietary practices in the Indian context.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the prevalence of restrictive dietary practice adoption, assess awareness of restrictive dietary practices, and explore self-reported physical, psychological, and emotional effects among young adult Indian females aged 18–30 years. Further, the study examined the influence of age and educational attainment status on self-reported nutrition knowledge, attitudes, and dietary behaviors to provide comprehensive insights into an under-researched public health challenge.

Methods

Study setting

A study was conducted at the pan-India level among young adult women aged between 18 and 30 years between January 2024 and August 2025. The current study was a pilot study and aimed to provide information for larger studies on the adoption of restrictive dietary practices and associated factors among young adult Indian women. It used a cross-sectional design and followed a voluntary, online, purposive sampling of young adult (18–30 years) females. Participants who were healthy, aged between 18 and 30 years, willing to provide consent, and able to complete the online questionnaire were included in the study. Participants with self-reported health conditions such as eating disorders and metabolic disorders, such as diabetes, hypertension, and thyroid disorders that significantly influenced their dietary habits independently of the restrictive dietary practices, were excluded from the study. Additionally, participants who would not provide written consent and who were pregnant or lactating were not eligible for enrollment in the present study.

The sample size was estimated using Cochran’s formula for a prevalence of 69% adoption of restrictive dietary practices among young women aged 18–25 years [10]. The required sample size to estimate the sample size for a 69% prevalence with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error was 329. The research team planned to recruit participants until the sample size was achieved; however, due to the paucity of time to collect the data, a total of 230 adults were included in the study.

The participants were enrolled in the study after obtaining their informed consent. Participant information sheets with insights on the purpose of the study and methodology were provided, and the study was verbally narrated to them prior to seeking their recruitment. Upon their verbal consent, the written consent form was provided to record their consent to participate in the study. Data collection and tools

The participants of the study were recruited via online mode using Google Form-based questionnaires. The study was advertised through social media platforms such as Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, LinkedIn, email distribution, and word of mouth. Upon enrollment in the study, a pre-tested questionnaire with 25 questions, encompassing different domains such as socio-demography (age, education attainment, formal education on nutrition); knowledge regarding nutrition and restrictive dietary practices (level of nutrition knowledge, restrictive dietary practices, effectiveness on improving nutritional status); and attitude toward adoption of restrictive dietary practices (general attitude, had adopted any restrictive dietary practices, the adopted restrictive dietary practices, and drivers toward adoption of restrictive dietary practices). Additionally, dietary practices and physical activity (current dietary habits, skipping of meals and its frequency, frequency of weekly fast-food consumption, and weekly physical activity pattern) and perceived factors that influenced nutritional status. Further, the questionnaire elicited information on perceived effects of restrictive dietary practices on overall health such as change in the nutritional status or weight, and the impact of restrictive dietary practices on physical and mental/psychological well-being status. Pre-testing was conducted to assess clarity, reliability, time requirements, participant burden, and data collection logistics and administration. A total of 10 participants completed the pre-testing, and based on the insights gained, the questionnaire was modified.

Statistical analysis

The data were coded, entered, and cleaned using an MS Excel workbook. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio version 4.5.0 and SPSS 31.0. The dependent variable in the research included the adoption of restrictive dietary practices, while the independent variables were age, gender, education attainment, socioeconomic status, and dietary behaviors. Descriptive statistics such as the median (25th, 75th percentiles) were used to summarize continuous variables. Categorical variables are presented in numbers with percentages. Data visualization using a bar chart was employed to display the most common types of restrictive dietary practices known among the study participants. The associations of restrictive dietary practice adoption with age and educational attainment were compared using the chi-square/Fisher’s exact test. P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Ethical approval

The study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration and ethical guidelines, ensuring that informed consent, participant confidentiality, and voluntary participation were maintained [19]. Institutional Ethics Committee Approval from Symbiosis International (Deemed University) was obtained before the commencement of the study, and necessary permissions were secured from the relevant authorities (SIU/IEC/740). Additionally, approvals from the Data Access Review Committee (DARC) of the Higher Education Institute and a Non-Disclosure Agreement were obtained to ensure data security and confidentiality.

The posts included a Google Form link for participants to express their interest in participating in the study. The researcher verified the eligibility of the participants and recruited them if they complied with the inclusion criteria. The approach facilitated the recruitment of diverse and inclusive participants across Indian states within the age group of 18–30 years. The primary objective of the current research was to study and explore the perceived effects of restrictive dietary practices on the health of young Indian females aged 18–30 years.

Information on self-reported lifestyle practices showed that a substantial proportion of the participants [76.5% (n = 176)] adhered to a healthy dietary habits (i.e., diet that included balanced and varied or mostly healthy with once-a-week or less use of snacks/chocolates or fast foods) than less healthy, i.e., those who had more frequent consumption of fast foods, sweets, chocolates, and fried foods or paid less attention to their diets.

Additionally, 59.6% (n = 137) indicated less frequent (rarely, never, or once a week) consumption of fast foods. About 58.4% (n = 132) of participants reported skipping meals, with breakfast and snacks being the most omitted. Further, the prevalence of adoption of restrictive dietary practices among the study participants was 20.9% (n = 48). Among the participants who engaged in restrictive dietary practices, intermittent fasting emerged as the most opted diet [65.6% (n = 21)], the others being the ketogenic diet [18.8% (n = 6)] and detox diets [15.6% (n = 5)]. The results from the survey indicated that most participants, 96.3% (n = 314), identified personal drivers (enhancing physical appearance, boosting self-confidence, and attaining the desired clothing size) as key factors for adopting restrictive dietary practices. Conversely, only 3.7% (n = 62) reported that their motivations were derived from social factors, such as seeking approval from family and friends.

Additionally, data were collected on the factors that influenced the nutritional status of the study participants (Table 1). A quarter of the study participants (27.1%, n = 129) reported that changes in dietary habits and food choices influenced their nutritional status. About equal proportions of responses favored lifestyle changes (work schedule, travel, and family dynamics), as well as awareness and education about nutrition and dietary choices, which influenced their nutritional status. Also, more than half of the respondents, 52.2% (n = 120), acknowledged engaging in regular physical activity.

The evaluation of participants' awareness regarding popular restrictive dietary practices showed that the ketogenic diet was the most known [27.3% (n = 147)], followed by intermittent fasting [17.8% (n = 95)], detox diets [12.5% (n = 67)], and the vegan diet [8.9% (n = 48)]. Higher educational attainment (i.e., postgraduate vs. undergraduate education) was positively associated with formal nutrition education status (p = 0.010), self-reported nutrition knowledge (p = 0.000), healthier dietary habits (p = 0.034), and physical activity (p = 0.015). Conversely, higher educational attainment was negatively associated with fast-food consumption (p = 0.044) in participants regardless of formal nutritional education. Participants without formal nutrition education [27.8% (n = 64)] had significantly lower self-reported nutritional knowledge [95% (n = 61) vs. 5% (n = 3); p < 0.001] and unhealthier dietary practices [45.3% (n = 29) vs. 54.7% (n = 35); p < 0.001]. The above results indicated that participants with postgraduate education attainment had higher self-reported formal nutrition education and nutrition knowledge, healthier dietary habits with occasional indulgences in unhealthy foods, less frequent consumption of fast foods, and increased physical activity (p = 0.01) than undergraduate participants (Table 2). Additionally, age (p = 0.021) and frequency of fast-food consumption (p = 0.007) were associated with the adoption of restrictive dietary practices. It was observed that a significantly higher proportion of the older age group (> 23 and ≤ 30 years) adopted restrictive dietary practices and consumed fast foods more frequently than the younger ones.

On the other hand, chi-square analysis to explore the association of knowledge, attitudes, and practices of restrictive dietary practices with age (younger vs. older adults) and attainment of education (graduate vs. postgraduate) among participants who adopted restrictive dietary practices showed that postgraduate participants had significantly higher self-reported nutrition knowledge (i.e., a higher proportion with above-average nutrition knowledge scores (> 3 to ≤ 5; p = 0.031) than undergraduate participants (Table 2).

Data on the perceived effects of restrictive dietary practices on overall health were collected, and a chi-square analysis was conducted to explore the association between age (category: > 18 and ≤ 23 years vs. > 23 and ≤ 30 years) and attainment of education (undergraduate vs. postgraduate) of all the participants and those who adopted restrictive dietary practices (Table 3). The analysis of association for all participants reported that the attainment of education was associated with perceived effects on emotional and psychological well-being (p = 0.025). On the other hand, analysis of participants who adopted restrictive dietary practices showed that age was significantly associated with perceived negative experiences such as fatigue, digestive issues, or mood changes (p = 0.042) and perceived effects on psychological and emotional well-being (p = 0.025). The analysis indicated that younger participants demonstrated a reduced likelihood of experiencing perceived negative experiences (fatigue, digestive issues, or mood changes) and the effects on emotional and psychological well-being when compared to their older counterparts. Additionally, attainment of education was associated with perceived negative experiences such as fatigue, digestive issues, or mood changes (p = 0.002) and perceived effects on psychological and emotional well-being (p = 0.004). Discussion

What is already known on this subject?

Restrictive dietary practices consist of restricting certain nutrients, timing, quantity, or overall food consumption, without causing malnutrition. Such diets are highly popular among adolescents and young adults, adopted mostly under social media/peer influence to achieve self-set personal goals. Because of poor nutritional literacy among these groups, they adopt restrictive dietary practices, leading to nutritional inadequacy, adverse health effects, and unsustainable dietary practices.

What does this study add?

This study presents the results on the effects of restrictive dietary practices on young adults’ health, the level of awareness about restrictive dietary practices, and the prevalence of restrictive dietary practices adoption among young female adults in India. The information on the prevalence of restrictive dietary practices among young Indian women is limited to specific diets and specific regions. The salient results from the study are as follows: (1) The adoption of restrictive dietary practices and consumption of fast foods were higher among older adults; (2) The awareness of ketogenic diets was higher among the participants; however, intermittent fasting was the most adopted type of restrictive dietary practices; (3) Personal goals (enhanced appearance, building self-confidence, and attaining the desired clothing size) drove the adoption of restrictive dietary practices; (4) Postgraduate participants had more nutrition knowledge, which was reflected in higher self-reported nutrition knowledge, self-reported healthy dietary habits, and physical activity; (5) Younger adults and postgraduates were found to have fewer perceived negative experiences of fatigue, digestive issues, or mood changes as well as the same emotional and psychological well-being.

The present study from India reported the overall rate of adoption of restrictive dietary practices was 20.9%, with a higher rate of 31.7% among participants aged 23 years and above, in contrast to 16.8% among those aged 18–23 years. In the absence of comparable restrictive dietary practice adoption rates from India, the rates from low- and middle-income countries such as the Philippines and Mongolia reported higher rates of adoption of restrictive dietary practices of 69.5% and 40%, respectively, than the present study [15–17]. The lower adoption rate of restrictive dietary practices in the present study indicates that restrictive dieting is less common among young, urban, educated women, predominantly postgraduate students with some level of formal nutrition education in India.

Furthermore, the awareness patterns in the present study revealed that the ketogenic diet was the most well-known type of restrictive dietary practice, while intermittent fasting is the most widely adopted type. The findings align with global trends reported by Baldemor et al. [15] and Mondal et al. [14], where intermittent fasting is considered popular among young female adults [14, 15]. Further, an Indian study reported 15% adoption of intermittent fasting among urban women, with higher usage of the same among women of 18–35 years [14]. Although ketogenic diets were popular among the participants, actual adoption was low, possibly due to the limited nature of macronutrient distributions and perceived difficulty in adherence. Moreover, evidence-based diets, including the DASH and Mediterranean diets, were less recognized; these diets may be more difficult to integrate into Indian dietary patterns, whereas intermittent fasting aligns more readily with existing cultural practices. This is consistent with the evidence reporting that fast-spreading social media trends often garner more attention than the scientific dietary guidelines [15, 16]—the discrepancy underscores the necessity for targeted nutritional communication campaigns in India.

Adoption of restrictive dietary practices was largely driven by personal factors—primarily enhanced physical appearance, building self-confidence, and attaining the desired clothing size. The results are in line with the previous literature, as young women frequently diet due to body image concerns and societal pressures to maintain a desirable physical appearance [13, 18]. Comparing our study to the results of an earlier systematic review, which showed a significant influence of social and media elements, we found that social factors had a surprisingly smaller impact [19]. The disparities in our study’s demographics, which included exclusively female and primarily postgraduate participants could have adopted self-directed food choices and enhanced nutritional literacy leading to the interstudy discrepancies.

A major finding of the present study was the lesser perceived negative experiences, such as fatigue, digestive comfort, and mood-related symptoms, among younger and postgraduate participants than the older undergraduates. Possibly, the perceived negative impacts of restrictive dietary practices were mitigated by postgraduates’ improved nutrition knowledge and better dietary habits. The data are consistent with the results of a study that found a strong association between nutritional adequacy, favorable body image, and enhanced diet-related outcomes [20]. Similarly, the literature on psychological well-being indicated that healthy lifestyle practices and enhanced nutritional literacy lowered the likelihood of negative psychological experiences associated with restrictive dieting [10]. These results allude to the potential protective role of formal nutrition education.

Strengths and limitations

The limitations of the present study include the smaller sample size than estimated due to a paucity of time and slow responses from the participants, which limits the generalizability of the results. However, the study provides insight into the existing rates of adoption of restrictive dietary practices among young adult Indian women, most of whom had significant formal nutrition education. The study used an online data collection method, and hence, the nutritional assessment could not be performed. Additionally, participants self-reported the data; hence, results should be used cautiously. The distribution of participants across dietary patterns is very uneven. This limits the possibility of comparing participants' characteristics across different dietary patterns, although this could represent an interesting aspect to explore in future studies. However, the study provides valuable insights for further larger studies on the adoption of restrictive dietary practices and associated factors among young adult Indian women. Additionally, the present study analyzed the primary drivers for adoption of restrictive dietary practices.

Adequately powered studies with larger, more balanced dietary restriction groups from diverse educational backgrounds will help comparisons between dietary interventions and generalizability of the results. The inclusion of direct anthropometric measurements, nutrition adequacy, and a greater number of potential factors associated with the adoption of restrictive dietary practices will yield deeper insights into the adoption among young adult Indian women.

Conclusion

The present study highlights the prevalence of the adoption of restrictive dietary practices among young adult Indian women, especially among the older participants within the 18–30 years age group. Intermittent fasting was the most adopted restrictive dietary practices. The current dietary trends shaped the awareness of restrictive dietary practices more than evidence-based nutrition guidelines. Personal factors related to physical appearance drove the adoption restrictive dietary practices. Age and education attainment are associated with nutrition knowledge, dietary behaviors, and the perceived effects on both their physical and psychological and emotional well-being, with postgraduates reporting fewer negative experiences.
