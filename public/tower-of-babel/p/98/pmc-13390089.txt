Rotor-guided tailor-made pulmonary vein isolation for persistent atrial fibrillation: Identification of the left atrial septum as a common arrhythmogenic substrate
Authors: Yuya Suzuki, Kaoru Takami, Daisuke Shiraishi, Tatsuru Nomura, Takamichi Ishihata, Tomohiro Nozawa, Akihiro Yoshida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn patients with persistent atrial fibrillation (AF), additional substrate modification beyond pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is controversial, given limited durability and increased atrial tachycardias (ATs). ExTRa Mapping system is a new tool to clarify the real-time imaging of AF rotors.ObjectiveWe evaluated the efficacy of extended PVI that combines cryoballoon PVI with tailor-made radiofrequency (RF) ablation of pulmonary vein antrum rotors identified by ExTRa Mapping.Methods58 patients with persistent AF underwent initial ablation using a tailored strategy: cryoballoon PVI followed by RF line extension to encompass adjacent AF rotors identified by ExTRa Mapping (rotor-guided extended PVI group). Outcomes were compared with 2 contemporaneous control groups: (1) cryoballoon PVI alone (PVI-alone group, n = 33) and (2) RF-based PVI plus posterior wall isolation (PWI) (PVI + PWI group, n = 43). The primary endpoint was freedom from AF/AT recurrence without antiarrhythmic drugs at 12 months.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable among groups. Left atrial septal Nonpassively activated areas were observed in 70.7% of patients and were the most common location. Accordingly, in the rotor-guided extended PVI group, anterior extension from the right pulmonary vein toward the left atrial septal rotor was the most frequently applied strategy. At 12 months, freedom from AF/AT was significantly higher in the rotor-guided extended PVI group (89.7%) than the PVI-alone group (63.6%) and the PVI + PWI group (65.1%) (log-rank P = .004). Introduction

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is the catheter ablation cornerstone for atrial fibrillation (AF).1 Nonetheless, in the STAR AF II trial, neither linear ablation (roof and mitral isthmus) nor complex fractionated atrial electrogram (CFAE) ablation, added to PVI, improved clinical outcomes, which has been partly attributed to increased iatrogenic atrial tachycardia (AT) incidence and insufficient PVI durability.2

Previously, we demonstrated that CFAE-guided PVI, in which CFAEs were mapped before ablation and incorporated into an extensive encircling PVI strategy, was more effective than conventional PVI in persistent AF (PerAF) after 2 procedures, without increased ATs. Conversely, after a single procedure, both strategies yielded comparable outcomes, underscoring the importance of personalized PVI strategies tailored to individual atrial substrate characteristics.

Rotor-guided ablation strategies may overcome these limitations. ExTRa Mapping, a novel real-time phase-mapping technology developed by Nihon Kohden (Tokyo, Japan), enables intra-atrial activation visualization through an artificial intelligence (AI)–driven signal processing algorithm.3,4 ExTRa Mapping is distinct from conventional mapping systems, given that it provides rotational activity high-resolution visualization, also known as phase mapping, and it does not directly capture focal sources. Traditional activation mapping interprets electrical propagation solely based on electrogram timing. Nevertheless, during AF, rapid and disorganized electrical impulses may traverse the myocardial tissue without inducing true excitation because of atrial myocardial refractory properties. Notably, phase mapping provides a more accurate myocardial activation depiction by accounting for these electrophysiological limitations. This real-time rotor activity assessment offers a potential advantage in guiding targeted ablation strategies, particularly for PerAF. Recently, the ROTATE trial evaluated ExTRa Mapping–guided ablation targeting AF rotors in PerAF (PVI alone vs PVI plus ExTRa Mapping–guided substrate ablation). A positive trend was observed in rotor-targeted ablation beyond the PVI. However, the difference was not statistically significant because of the limited sample size.5

Regarding PVI durability, cryoballoon ablation (CBA) has demonstrated superiority over radiofrequency (RF) ablation in several studies because of its ability to create more uniform and continuous lesions.6 Moreover, pulsed field ablation (PFA) is a promising new modality; nonetheless, long-term data on lesion durability remain limited, and both PFA and CBA are less adaptable for individualized ablation approaches targeting AF substrates.

Therefore, we hypothesized that a tailored ablation strategy in which cryoballoon PVI is followed by RF line extension to encompass adjacent AF rotors identified by ExTRa Mapping would improve arrhythmia-free survival while minimizing unnecessary substrate modification and iatrogenic AT risk.

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent initial catheter ablation for PerAF at the Kita-Harima Medical Center between January 2021 and July 2024.

PerAF was defined as AF continuation for 7 days but ≤1 year without antiarrhythmic drugs. Patients were excluded if the left atrial (LA) diameter and left ventricular ejection fraction on transthoracic echocardiography were >60 mm and <30%, respectively, or if they had undergone previous open surgery.

58 patients with PerAF underwent an initial ablation procedure that included cryoballoon PVI, followed by RF line extension to encompass the adjacent AF rotors identified by ExTRa Mapping. These patients were assigned to the rotor-guided extended PVI group. The outcomes of this tailored ablation group were compared with those of 2 contemporaneous control groups: 1 who underwent cryoballoon PVI alone (PVI-alone group) and another who underwent RF-based PVI with posterior wall isolation (PWI) (PVI + PWI group).

This retrospective study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of Kita-Harima Medical Center (no. 07-43). Patients consented to the use of their anonymized clinical data for research purposes through an opt-out process.

Ablation procedure

Before AF ablation, contrast cardiac computed tomography (CT) or transesophageal echocardiography was performed to exclude thrombus formation. All patients underwent contrast-enhanced multidetector CT to evaluate the LA, right atrium (RA), and pulmonary veins (PVs). 3-dimensional multidetector CT images of the LA, RA, and PVs were reconstructed on a separate workstation and integrated using a NavX electroanatomic mapping system (Abbott, St. Paul, MN). The patients were examined under general anesthesia using a supraglottic airway. Unfractionated heparin was administered in bolus form before transseptal puncture to maintain an activated clotting time of >300 seconds.

PVI-alone group

CBA was performed using a 28-mm fourth-generation balloon (Arctic Front Advance Pro; Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN). The bidirectional PV block was confirmed using Achieve (Medtronic) and/or Reflection HD (Abbott). No adjunctive ExTRa Mapping–guided substrate modification or planned antral line expansion beyond cryoballoon PVI was performed in this group.

PVI + PWI group

RF ablation was performed using an irrigated-tip catheter (TactiCath SE ablation catheter or TactiFlex ablation catheter; Abbott) with a power output of 30–40 W, confirming the bidirectional block of the PVs and PW using an Advisor HD grid (Abbott).

ExTRa Mapping

After PVI, phase mapping using ExTRa Mapping and CFAE mapping of the LA were performed simultaneously with the NavX system using a deflectable 20-pole spiral-shaped catheter (Reflection HD; Abbott).

ExTRa Mapping uses an AI-based algorithm to visualize intra-atrial electrical activity, incorporating 41 electrograms: 20, 12, and 9 unipolar, conventional bipolar, and virtual bipolar, respectively.3,4 To enhance the accuracy and efficiency of detecting atrial activation during AF, the system integrates both a computer simulation (in silico) component and a specialized AI module.

The in silico model estimates virtual atrial action potentials using the timing of local activation derived from recorded signals and applies a computational model that reflects human nonparoxysmal AF dynamics,3 allowing for high-resolution phase mapping and reducing the risk of misinterpreting pseudo-rotational activity caused by spatial interpolation errors.

During mapping, the high-density phase movie automatically classifies the activation pattern at each time point as a “meandering rotor” when a single reentrant circuit is present and drifting, a “multiple wavelet” when 2 or more such circuits coexist, or “passive” when no rotational activity is observed. Moreover, the system calculates the nonpassively activated ratio (%NP), which quantifies the proportion of the recording period classified as a meandering rotor or a multiple wavelet, reflecting sustained rotational activity during AF. Data acquisition was performed after confirming adequate contact, and electrograms were reliably recorded from most electrodes. When the number of detectable electrograms was insufficient, the sensing threshold was adjusted from 0.03 mV to 0.01 mV to enhance signal capture. Based on 5-second wave dynamics during AF, each phase map was automatically created. Nonpassively activated areas (NPAs) were defined as regions with a %NP of ≥50%. These areas were considered indicative of regions in which AF rotors were likely to occur at a high frequency.

To assess NPA spatial distribution, the LA was anatomically divided into 7 segments: the roof, anterior wall, posterior wall, lateral wall, inferior wall, septum, and base of the LA appendage (LAA). Moreover, the septal segment of the LA was defined as the portion in direct contact with the RA on cardiac CT images reconstructed with the NavX system.

Rotor-guided extended PVI group

Patients in the rotor-guided extended PVI group underwent initial cryoballoon PVI using the same protocol as the PVI-alone group. Additional RF ablation was performed using an irrigated-tip catheter (TactiCath SE ablation catheter or TactiFlex ablation catheter; Abbott) to extend the antral isolation boundary in regions where NPAs were identified by ExTRa Mapping of the LA. When an NPA was identified, its location was recorded on the EnSite system by manually drawing a circular outline corresponding to the outer edge of the deflectable 20-pole spiral-shaped mapping catheter (Reflection HD; Abbott). The extended PVI line was planned to incorporate this footprint region within the expanded ipsilateral antral isolation boundary; when complete incorporation was not achievable, the lesion set was designed to partially transect the rotor area to include it within the isolation boundary as much as possible. RA rotor mapping/ablation was not performed. The adjunctive strategy was designed to eliminate the residual rotors in the PV antrum after PVI while avoiding iatrogenic AT and intentional PWI. Because ExTRa Mapping may show distributed sites of singularity formation within an NPA, we treated NPAs as a regional substrate rather than targeting individual “rotor cores” as focal points. RF ablation was then performed so that this outlined region was incorporated as much as possible within the expanded ipsilateral antral isolation boundary. For procedural characterization, we classified antral boundary expansion as “extended right PV (RPV) anterior,” “extended RPV posterior,” “extended left PV (LPV) anterior,” or “extended LPV posterior” when additional RF lesions expanded the ipsilateral antral isolation boundary beyond the index cryoballoon PVI line in the corresponding anterior or posterior direction of the PV antrum to incorporate/modify PV-adjacent NPAs; these labels describe anatomic expansion patterns within the rotor-guided strategy. In particular, (1) for the RPV, the antral isolation boundary was expanded anteriorly and/or posteriorly when an NPA was adjacent to the RPV antrum (most commonly anterior extension toward the LA septum), and (2) for the LPV, the boundary was expanded anteriorly and/or posteriorly as needed, with anterior extension limited to the LAA ridge to avoid inadvertent LAA isolation. When the entire NPA footprint could not be fully incorporated within the expanded antral boundary, most commonly at the RPV anterior region owing to transseptal access constraints, the ablation line was extended to cross and modify the NPA region within the achievable antral expansion. After completion of the extended line, repeat phase mapping at the same site was performed, and a reduction of %NP to <50% was confirmed as the procedural endpoint; bidirectional block across the extended PVI line was then confirmed. Conversely, NPAs situated near the mitral annulus or the LAA and not contiguous with the PV antrum were left unablated. CFAE

CFAE mapping was performed using the EnSite/NavX system for descriptive assessment. Importantly, CFAE was not used to guide ablation in this study, and no CFAE-guided ablation was performed. The parameters of the automatic algorithm were set according to a previous report.7 In the complex fractionated electrogram mean, the average time interval between consecutive deflections was 5 seconds. The atrial refractory period was set at 50 ms. A mean cycle length of <120 ms was defined as the CFAE. Moreover, we defined CFAE sites with very short cycle lengths (<50 ms) as very short CFAE (vsCFAEs). For descriptive characterization within ExTRa Mapping–defined NPA regions, a CFAE-positive NPA was defined when ≥50% of mapping sites within an NPA region met the CFAE criteria.

Induction

After completion of the group-specific ablation strategy, high-dose isoproterenol infusion was administered, and cardioversion was performed if AF persisted. If recurrence of PV and PW conduction or extra PV firing induced AF, additional ablation of the gap along the PVI or PWI line or at the extra PV focus was performed. Burst pacing of 10 beats was then performed at 3 sites (coronary sinus ostium, high RA, and distal coronary sinus). The pacing cycle length was shortened from 300 ms to 2:1 for atrial capture. If AT was induced, the origin or circuit was mapped. At the discretion of the operator, focal ablation of the AT origin, additional linear ablation, superior vena cava (SVC) isolation, and cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) ablation were performed. SVC isolation was not routinely performed. It was performed only when isoproterenol provoked reproducible SVC firing/triggered AF during induction testing. In contrast, CTI ablation was frequently performed at the operator’s discretion and was not restricted to cases with inducible typical CTI-dependent flutter.

Follow-up

Follow-up was performed according to an institutional standardized protocol in all groups. Outpatient visits were scheduled every 4–12 weeks, and a 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) was obtained at 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months after the ablation. 24-hour Holter monitoring was routinely performed at 3 and 12 months. In the case of symptomatic or suspected arrhythmia, patients could visit the clinic for additional ECG recordings without a scheduled appointment. After a 3-month blanking period, the occurrence of any sustained atrial tachyarrhythmia, including AF or AT lasting >30 seconds, was assessed without antiarrhythmic drugs.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. For group comparisons of continuous data, 1-way analysis of variance was used for normally distributed data, with post hoc pairwise comparisons performed using the Bonferroni correction. The Kruskal–Wallis test was applied for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher exact test.

Freedom from AF/AT recurrence was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with between-group differences assessed using the log-rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for AF/AT recurrence were estimated using the Cox proportional hazards model. For univariable screening, in addition to clinical covariates, we evaluated predefined procedural region variables and adjunctive lesion sets, including anterior/posterior extensions of the RPV and LPV after cryoballoon PVI, PWI, CTI ablation, SVC isolation, and a mitral isthmus line (Supplemental Table 1).

The main multivariable Cox model was used to evaluate the association between ablation strategy and AF/AT recurrence, with adjustment for clinically relevant covariates (age, sex, and AF duration). Ablation strategy was modeled as a 3-level categorical variable (rotor-guided extended PVI, PVI alone, and PVI + PWI), with the PVI-alone group as the reference category. Because extended anterior RPV represents a procedural phenotype within the rotor-guided strategy and occurred in 70.7% of rotor-guided cases, whereas no planned antral expansion was performed in the PVI-alone group, we did not include both strategy assignment and this phenotype simultaneously in the same multivariable model to minimize collinearity. Instead, we performed a phenotype-based sensitivity model in which extended anterior RPV replaced strategy assignment (Supplemental Table 2).

Statistical significance was defined as 2-tailed P < .05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY).

Results

Patient characteristics

During the study period, 58 consecutive patients were enrolled in the rotor-guided extended PVI group. During the same period, 33 patients in the PVI-alone group and 43 in the PVI + PWI group were enrolled for comparison. There were no significant differences among the 3 groups in age, sex, duration of AF, or duration of PerAF before the procedure. The LA diameter and left ventricular ejection fraction, assessed by transthoracic echocardiography, were also similar across groups (Table 1). Unscheduled visits for additional ECG recordings occurred in 8 of 58 patients (13.8%) in the rotor-guided extended PVI group, 3 of 33 (9.1%) in the PVI-alone group, and 3 of 43 (7.0%) in the PVI + PWI group (P = .56). No patient underwent extended-duration Holter monitoring (1–2 weeks) during the study period. No major procedural complications occurred.

Outcome

The AF/AT recurrence rate at 12 months was significantly lower in the rotor-guided extended PVI group (10.3%) than in the PVI-alone group (36.4%) and the PVI + PWI group (34.9%) (P = .003). The cumulative freedom from AF/AT recurrence at 12 months was significantly higher in the rotor-guided extended PVI group than in the PVI-alone group and the PVI + PWI group (log-rank P = .004) (Figure 3).

Kaplan–Meier curve of the freedom from AF/AT in each group. AF = atrial fibrillation; AT = atrial tachycardia; PVI = pulmonary vein isolation; PWI = posterior wall isolation.

Notably, the rate of AT recurrence at 12 months was significantly lower in the rotor-guided extended PVI group (3.4%) than in the PVI-alone group (15.2%) and the PVI + PWI group (20.9%) (P = .016).

All predefined procedural region variables and adjunctive lesion sets were screened in univariable Cox proportional hazards analyses (Supplemental Table 1). In these univariable analyses, both the rotor-guided extended PVI strategy and the presence of an extended anterior RPV antral expansion pattern were associated with a lower risk of AF/AT recurrence (Supplemental Table 1). In the main multivariable Cox model adjusted for age, sex, and AF duration and using a 3-level ablation strategy variable (reference = PVI alone), the rotor-guided extended PVI strategy remained independently associated with a lower risk of AF/AT recurrence (HR 0.276; 95% CI 0.101–0.756; P = .012), whereas the PVI + PWI strategy was not significantly different from PVI alone (HR 1.120; 95% CI 0.505–2.484; P = .780) (Table 3). The “extended anterior RPV” pattern should be interpreted as an anatomic procedural phenotype within the rotor-guided strategy rather than an intervention applied to the PVI-alone group; therefore, it was evaluated in a separate phenotype-based sensitivity model (Supplemental Table 2).

Discussion

Rotor ablation

Several large multicenter studies have reported lower-than-expected long-term success rates with focal impulse and rotor modulation (FIRM)–guided rotor ablation.8,9 In addition, Buch et al10 reported poor FIRM-guided ablation efficacy. Thus, the clinical effectiveness of FIRM-guided rotor ablation remains controversial. This controversy has been attributed to suboptimal rotor identification caused by poor electrode–tissue contact, low-density mapping owing to relatively large inter-electrode spacing, limited atrial coverage, and temporal rotor instability. Nonetheless, the recent ROTATE trial, which used ExTRa Mapping–guided ablation targeting AF rotors in patients with PerAF, suggested renewed potential for rotor-guided ablation.5 Furthermore, ExTRa Mapping enabled more accurate AF rotor identification, resulting in a favorable trend toward improved outcomes beyond conventional PVI. Although the difference was not statistically significant, the ROTATE trial revealed a higher incidence of induced ATs in the rotor ablation group, likely reflecting increased iatrogenic AT substrates. Although rotor ablation may effectively target AF mechanisms, it carries a risk of iatrogenic ATs, as previously reported.

Here, instead of direct rotor ablation, we extended the PVI lesion set to modify the rotor substrate to reduce AF recurrence while minimizing the risk of postprocedural ATs.

We acknowledge that the ROTATE trial used a different criterion to define higher %NP (%NP ≥35%). In the current study, we used a %NP of ≥50% as a pragmatic operational definition of NPAs to guide sequential mapping and antral boundary expansion, aiming to identify PV-adjacent regions with a relatively high burden of nonpassive activity and provide a clinically practical and more specific criterion for selecting target regions on repeat mapping at the same site. We also recognize that different %NP thresholds (eg, 35% vs 50%) have been used across published reports and institutions4,11, 12, 13; therefore, direct comparisons across cohorts should be interpreted with caution, and the optimal cutoff warrants prospective validation.

Moreover, the mechanistic relationship between NPAs and other substrate markers remains uncertain. Previous ExTRa Mapping studies have suggested that NPA/driver distributions do not necessarily correspond to CFAE or voltage-defined low-voltage areas (LVAs).3,4 In our cohort, electrogram fractionation showed limited overlap with NPA regions, suggesting that CFAE and NPAs may capture different aspects of the atrial substrate rather than representing mechanistically equivalent phenomena.

LA septum

In our cohort, most patients in the rotor-guided extended PVI group underwent anterior expansion of the RPV isolation line, targeting the rotors frequently located in the LA septum.

Yamaguchi et al14 demonstrated that atrial fibrosis in patients with AF progresses diffusely, as demonstrated by high-density voltage mapping and histologic studies. Among LA regions, the LA septum and anterior LA inherently exhibit lower bipolar voltages than other areas, even in patients without overt structural heart disease. Consequently, as fibrosis progresses, the LA septum, which is predisposed to lower voltages, tends to be incorporated earlier into an LVA. This pattern may partially explain the frequent involvement of the septal region as an arrhythmogenic substrate in PerAF.

Consistent with previous findings from the CFAE AF trial,15 in which most fractionated CFAEs were observed in the LA septum (80%), our results demonstrated a high prevalence of vsCFAE sites in this region. In particular, targeting the most fractionated CFAEs significantly prolonged the AF cycle length compared with targeting less fractionated areas, underscoring their potential role in AF maintenance.15

Cryoballoon plus RF ablation

In catheter ablation for PerAF, both cryoballoon and RF ablation targeting PVI alone have demonstrated comparable efficacy.16 Nonetheless, the effectiveness of PVI alone remains limited compared with that of paroxysmal AF. Kosmidou et al17 reported that hybrid strategies combining cryoballoon PVI with additional RF ablation failed to demonstrate clinical benefit. A potential explanation is that the additional RF ablation in their study was extensive and encompassed both CFAE ablation and linear lesions, likely contributing to the high iatrogenic AT incidence. In particular, linear lesions such as roof lines (100%), mitral isthmus lines (29%), and septal lines (14.3%) were observed in most patients, and CFAE ablation was performed in approximately 81% of patients.

To avoid increasing iatrogenic AT, we adopted a targeted strategy in which RF ablation was performed only on AF rotors near the cryoballoon PVI line. In this approach, the PVI line is selectively extended using RF ablation at sites with rotor activity, minimizing unnecessary substrate modifications while maintaining procedural efficacy. From a practical perspective, our approach combines multiple technologies (cryoablation, RF ablation, and ExTRa Mapping), which may increase procedural complexity and cost and could limit broad adoption. However, because adjunctive ablation was performed as selective antral expansion only at PV-adjacent NPA-positive sites, this strategy may help avoid unnecessary empirical substrate ablation. Whether such a targeted approach improves overall resource utilization (fewer repeat procedures) and is cost-effective requires prospective validation. In addition, given concerns regarding procedure-related complications and the need for a more streamlined approach, we are conducting a randomized controlled trial comparing PFA PVI alone with NPA-guided (rotor-guided) PFA PVI (UMIN-CTR: UMIN000059827).

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, this retrospective analysis had a relatively small sample size, potentially limiting generalizability and introducing potential selection bias. Second, continuous ECG monitoring was not routinely performed during follow-up, which may have resulted in underdetection of asymptomatic arrhythmia recurrences. However, the follow-up protocol was applied identically across all groups, and additional unscheduled ECG recordings were performed at a similar frequency; therefore, any underdetection is expected to be nondifferential. Third, follow-up was limited, and long-term outcomes require evaluation. Fourth, the absence of a randomized control group precludes definitive conclusions regarding rotor-guided extended PVI efficacy relative to other approaches. Accordingly, prospective randomized studies with larger cohorts are required to confirm these findings. Fifth, this study included a small number of patients and no procedure-related complications were observed; nevertheless, thermal ablation carries a risk of collateral injury to surrounding structures. Given concerns regarding procedure-related complications, we are conducting a randomized controlled trial comparing PFA PVI alone and rotor-guided PFA PVI to further evaluate and optimize ablation strategies. Sixth, because the rotor-guided strategy involved selective widening of the antral isolation boundary at NPA-positive sites and modification of NPAs when required, we cannot exclude the possibility that part of the observed benefit reflects a wider antral lesion set (“antrum ablation” effect) rather than NPA-guided targeting. Seventh, because different %NP thresholds have been used across studies and institutions (eg, 35% vs 50%), direct comparisons across cohorts should be interpreted with caution, and the optimal cutoff for defining NPAs warrants prospective validation. Eighth, electrograms recorded on the LA septal surface may include RA far-field components owing to the close anatomic proximity of the interatrial septum. Such far-field contamination may increase signal complexity and could contribute to apparent multiple-wavelet patterns during sequential mapping. Therefore, the impact of RA far-field signals on the identification and characterization of meandering rotational activity remains uncertain. In addition, voltage mapping during sinus rhythm before ablation was not systematically performed in this cohort; therefore, we could not evaluate the correlation between ExTRa Mapping–derived NPAs and LVAs. Moreover, because adjunctive CTI ablation was frequently performed at operator discretion and differed across groups, residual confounding related to concomitant lesion sets cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

Our strategy of rotor-guided extension of the PVI lesion set, targeting rotors predominantly located in the LA septum, demonstrated promising results in reducing AF recurrence while minimizing the risk of iatrogenic AT. Selective RF ablation, when combined with cryoballoon PVI, may be an effective and safe approach for substrate modification in patients with PerAF. Further large-scale randomized studies are warranted to confirm these findings and optimize ablation strategies.
