Identification of an Enteroarterial Fistula at a Pancreas Transplant Site on Retrograde Double Balloon Enteroscopy
Authors: Rand Kittani, Nader Al-Souky, Najib Nassani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIliac artery pseudoaneurysm is a rare complication after pancreas transplantation that can present as obscure gastrointestinal bleeding. A 56-year-old gentleman with failed pancreas transplant developed hematochezia and anemia with bidirectional endoscopy and computed tomography angiography being inconclusive. Video capsule endoscopy suggested distal small bowel bleeding, and retrograde double-balloon enteroscopy identified a solitary ulcer with a protruding pulsatile vessel. A right external iliac pseudoaneurysm was confirmed and treated with endovascular stenting that was complicated by limb ischemia and subsequently by surgical resection of the fistula. This case highlights retrograde double-balloon enteroscopy utility in identifying vascular-enteric fistulas when imaging fails.

INTRODUCTION

Vascular complications including common iliac artery pseudoaneurysm are a recognized entity after pancreatic and kidney transplants. It typically occurs early in the postoperative course affecting about 5%–12% of patients and is especially higher in patients with perioperative infective complications.1–3 When common iliac artery pseudoaneurysm fistulizes to the small bowel, it presents as painless hematochezia originating from the site of anastomosis after pancreatic transplantation.

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) is the standard diagnostic tool to identify the source of hemorrhage.2,4 However, in cases where CTA fails to detect the bleeding source, angiography can be used as both a diagnostic and therapeutic tool.5

Management of pseudoaneurysms associated with pancreatic transplant typically involves one of the following approaches: surgical repair, pancreatectomies, or endovascular management.6 We present retrograde double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE) as a novel diagnostic technique that could identify pseudoaneurysms associated with pancreatic transplant in a minimally invasive fashion when CTA is inconclusive. While pseudoaneurysms could occur early in the postoperative period, it could also present months to years later as a complication after a transplant. This case report highlights the possibility of their late occurrence.7

CASE REPORT

This case is about a 56-year-old gentleman with a medical history including type 1 diabetes mellitus, status postfailed enteric pancreas transplant in the right lower quadrant 19 years ago, kidney transplant, and a recent ischemic stroke for which he had been on dual antiplatelet therapy. He was initially admitted to the hospital due to a cerebrovascular accident where he developed hematochezia the next day during his in-patient stay resulting in acute blood loss anemia.

On admission, his antiplatelet medications were held initially, and a complete blood count demonstrated markedly decreased hemoglobin level of 7.1 g/dL from 12 g/dL at baseline requiring blood transfusion. The patient received 8 units of packed red blood cells to stabilize his hemoglobin over the course of his hospitalization given intermittent active bleeding. He denied chest pain, abdominal pain, or shortness of breath and was hemodynamically stable with a systolic blood pressure > 120 during his stay. Extensive diagnostic workup was initiated to investigate the obscure source of gastrointestinal bleeding. An esophagogastroduodenoscopy, a push enteroscopy, and 2 colonoscopies with normal appearing terminal ileum were unrevealing. A tagged red blood cell nuclear scan failed to identify an active source of bleeding. A Meckel scan was also negative. CTA of the abdomen and pelvis was performed on day 7 of the initial bleeding episode and showed distal ileal thickening and a right external iliac pseudoaneurysm without active extravasation of contrast which thought to be incidental.

With the persistent and obscure nature of the bleeding, the patient underwent video capsule endoscopy that revealed active gastrointestinal bleeding in the distal small bowel. Considering these findings, a retrograde DBE was recommended on day 20 of admission, as diagnostic workup was inconclusive. Retrograde DBE demonstrated a full-thickness erosion and fistulization of the pseudoaneurysm into the small intestine seen as a perforated ulcer on the luminal side (Figure 1). Pneumoperitoneum was not seen on cross-sectional imaging. As this type of lesion was not amenable to endoscopic therapy, the area upstream of the pulsating vessel was marked with a carbon black tattoo, and 2 hemostatic clips downstream from the lesion were placed for marking. Interventional radiology (IR) service was consulted. A repeat CTA confirmed the right external iliac artery pseudoaneurysm. An angiography was performed by IR, and the pseudoaneurysm was seen adjacent to the 2 hemostatic clips (Figure 2), and an endovascular stent was placed to cover the pseudoaneurysm. Overnight, the patient experienced right lower-extremity ischemia that was successfully managed with an embolectomy. An exploratory laparotomy revealed an erosion of the right external iliac artery pseudoaneurysm into the distal ileum, consistent with the findings noted on retrograde DBE. The affected terminal ileum was resected, and antibiotic beads were placed along the omental patch to reduce the risk of postoperative infection.

DISCUSSION

Hematochezia with acute blood loss anemia requires prompt diagnostic investigation to therapeutically target the identified etiology. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of identifying iliac artery pseudoaneurysm in a patient with a remote history of pancreatic transplant that fistulized into the terminal ileum causing obscure gastrointestinal bleeding. While CTA is typically sensitive in detecting active extravasation, it may be inadequate when bleeding is intermittent or slow underscoring the limitations of conventional diagnostic approaches in identifying vascular-enteric fistulas.

Recent literature further supports the diagnostic challenges and variable presentations of pseudoaneurysm-related gastrointestinal bleeding in transplant recipients. Ramos et al3 reported a case of massive hematochezia due to an iliac artery pseudoaneurysm eroding into the small bowel in a remote pancreas-transplant recipient, where initial imaging was unrevealing and definitive diagnosis required invasive angiography followed by successful endovascular stent grafting. Similarly, Uchima et al8 described a superior mesenteric artery pseudoaneurysm fistulizing into the small intestine after pancreas-kidney transplant, where video capsule endoscopy identified the bleeding site and guided targeted angiographic repair. In another case, Yoon et al9 demonstrated the utility of antegrade DBE in identifying an aortoenteric jejunal fistula missed on CT, allowing for precise localization before surgical management. Collectively, these reports parallel the diagnostic trajectory in our patient and emphasize that pseudoaneurysms may elude detection on CTA when bleeding is intermittent in nature. They also highlight the growing role of capsule endoscopy and subsequent DBE (upper or lower) in localizing vascular-enteric fistulas when imaging is inconclusive enabling timely IR or surgical intervention for definitive treatment.

Notably, the identification of a pulsatile arterial structure during retrograde DBE necessitates a thorough understanding of the limitation of endoscopic therapeutic options and inherently in this clinical context. Endoscopic clipping of large-caliber pulsatile vessels indicating a pseudoaneurysm carries a significant risk of iatrogenic vascular wall injury, potentially precipitating uncontrolled hemorrhage. Accordingly, recognition of the constraints of endoscopic intervention in such scenarios is paramount.

In this case, DBE served primarily and adjunctive role, facilitating subsequent management by IR through endoscopic clip placement downstream of the lesion as a fiducial landmark and by the surgical team through submucosal tattooing for intraoperative localization. Furthermore, to mitigate the risk of mechanical trauma, deliberate advancement of the double-balloon overtube beyond the region of the visualized vessel was avoided, given the potential for injury from the friction and inherent rigidity of the overtube shaft.

Endovascular and surgical management are both options for pseudoaneurysm treatment. While limited data are available, endovascular management is typically used during acute bleeding, where it provides immediate vascular control. The site of the lesion, the patient's status, and the multidisciplinary team's experience also play a role in determining the treatment modality.1 Angiography is the treatment of choice for delayed massive hemorrhages.5

This case report highlights the role of DBE in identifying external iliac artery pseudoaneurysm as a source of bleeding that has fistulized into the distal ileum nearly 20 years later after pancreatic transplantation, as well as its role as an adjunctive modality to facilitate subsequent endovascular and surgical treatment.
