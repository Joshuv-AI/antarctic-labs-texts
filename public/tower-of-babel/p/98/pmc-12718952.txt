Interleukin-10: Genetic and biochemical prediction of sepsis-induced acute kidney injury in critically ill patients in intensive care unit: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Amr A. Amin, Aseel M. Ghonaim, Hiba S. Al-Amodi, Mohammed H. Mukhtar, Reem M. Allam, Anas Dannoun, Mohamed N. Eldein, Neda M. Bogari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Sepsis is a potentially life-threatening condition that eventually causes multiorgan dysfunction in critically ill patients. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a severe life-threatening complication of sepsis, a condition termed sepsis-induced AKI (S-AKI), with poor clinical outcomes and high mortality rates. Inflammatory and immunological responses are important variables in S-AKI. This study aimed to examine the relationship of rs1518111 polymorphism in the interleukin-10 (IL-10) gene and serum/urine IL-10 levels with sepsis-induced AKI in critically ill patients in the intensive care unit (ICU).Methods:In this cross-sectional study, 310 critically ill adult patients were recruited, of whom, 197 developed S-AKI. Real-time polymerase chain reaction was performed to detect the rs1518111 polymorphism. Circulating blood and urine IL-10 levels of IL-10 were measured.Results:For rs1518111 SNP, the presence of at least one T allele increased the risk of occurrence of S-AKI (odds ratio [OR]: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.07-3.17; p < 0.001), regardless of the type of infection and severity of sepsis. Blood and urine IL-10 levels were an excellent prediction of S-AKI (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]: 0.881 and 0.953 and sensitivity: 90.2% and 97.6% at cutoff of 133.5 and 5.67 pg/mL, respectively). Regression analysis showed that white blood cell count and increased blood and urine IL-10 levels, in addition to the presence of TT genotype, are independent risk factors for S-AKI.Conclusion:rs1518111 polymorphism in the IL-10 gene is a risk factor for sepsis-induced AKI in the ICU. Serum/urine IL-10 levels may be used as predictors of S-AKI in critically ill patients with sepsis, thereby improving early management.

1. INTRODUCTION

Sepsis is a potentially fatal organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host immunological response to infection. It accounts for almost half of all acute kidney injury (AKI) cases in the intensive care unit (ICU) and is the leading cause of death in the ICU,1 with a mortality rate of 25% to 30%, whereas septic shock has a mortality rate of 45% to 63%.2

Fluid resuscitation, vasopressors, parental antibiotics, and adoption of surviving sepsis campaign care bundles are associated with a decrease in hospital mortality rates due to sepsis,3 whereas a longer time to management is associated with higher mortality rates. Although early initiation of a care management package lowers the mortality rate of sepsis-related AKI, it is unclear whether these management policies also lower its incidence.

AKI is caused by excessive production of cytokines that promote inflammation, including interleukin-1b (IL)-1b, IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), which activates the host immune system. Sepsis stimulates the sympathetic nervous system and endothelial injury, as well as vasoactive substances that redistribute blood flow and cause microcirculation disorders.4 These factors can cause damage to the kidney tissue, which is one of the most susceptible organs to sepsis, and trigger sepsis-induced AKI (S-AKI) development through complex molecular pathways. S-AKI is a primary cause of AKI, associated with high mortality rates in patients with sepsis.2 Sepsis is the leading cause of AKI, accounting for between 45% and 70% of all AKI cases (depending on AKI definition, hospital setting, and patient population).5,6 Furthermore, AKI affects approximately 60% of patients with sepsis.7 Renal function deterioration is a characteristic of S-AKI.8 Readily available and reliable early biomarkers can be used to predict the risk of S-AKI in patients with sepsis, which is essential for the timely development of targeted therapies for its prevention or treatment.9

The Kidney Disease Improving Global (KDIGO) Guidelines define AKI as having a serum creatinine increase of ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, a serum creatinine increase of 1.5 times the baseline value over the previous 7 days, or a urine output of 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours.10

S-AKI has a fair prognosis, which can be reversed within the first week of diagnosis. Further research regarding the potential role of biomarkers in the prediction of renal recovery is needed, and early prediction of AKI to detect at-risk patients could be the first step in the discovery and assessment of new therapies.11

The human IL-10 gene has five exons and four introns and is found on chromosome 1 (1q31-1q32).12 Several studies have reported on IL-10 gene variations.13,14 Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the IL-10 promoter allow it to be highly polymorphic and regulate IL-10 expression.15 rs1518111 is an intronic variant of the second and third exons of the IL-10 gene and has been reported to be associated with diabetic nephropathy (DN).16,17 Potential risk factors for sepsis and mortality include genetic variations. Patients with septic shock have been investigated for genetic variations in important immune response genes, such as IL-10.18

IL-10 is an important immune regulator produced after infection to control excessive activation of proinflammatory cytokines and stimulate tissue recovery.19 Previous studies have revealed that specific knockout of IL-10 upregulates the expression of proinflammatory factors in mice when renal function is damaged.20 Furthermore, SNPs in the IL-10 gene are considered important players in different diseases. El Helaly et al17 showed a genetic association between rs1518111 and rs3021094 polymorphisms in the IL-10 gene and the risk of DN and type 1 diabetes in Egyptian children. Other studies have reported an association between rs1800890, rs3024495, and rs3024505 polymorphisms in the IL-10 gene and the risk of rheumatoid arthritis in a Chinese population.21 Interestingly, the SNPs rs1800871, rs1800872, and rs1800896 in the IL-10 gene also influence the severity of infection in different SARS-CoV-2 variants.22 Other studies have reported a relationship between SNPs in the IL-10 gene and DN susceptibility,16 and CD4 + CD25 + FOXP3 + T cells in asthmatic children.23 The development of new therapeutic targets for various kidney diseases could result from a better understanding of IL-10 as an anti-inflammatory factor based on the improvement of tissue fibrosis, immune regulation, and inflammatory response, thereby improving the prognosis of kidney disease patients.24

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between rs1518111 polymorphism in the IL-10 gene and assess the predictive value of serum/urine IL-10 levels in the diagnosis of sepsis-induced AKI in critically ill patients in the ICU.

2. METHODS

2.1. Recruitment of the participants

We conducted a cross-sectional study involving 310 critically ill adult patients without a history of AKI admitted to the ICU of AL Noor Specialized Hospital in Makkah, Saudi Arabia, between May 2016 and October 2018. Of the 310 critically ill adult patients, 197 (63.5%) developed S-AKI after admission and 174 (56%) developed septic shock. The previous definition and advancement of the criteria for diagnosing sepsis and septic shock were established at the International Sepsis Definition Conference (American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP)/ Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM)).25 According to this consensus, severe sepsis is defined as sepsis accompanied by organ failure, whereas septic shock is defined as sepsis-induced hypotension (with systolic blood pressure [SBP] <90 mmHg or a decrease of 40 mmHg), despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Organ dysfunction is indicated by symptoms such as the need for vasopressors, The ratio of partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood to the fraction of inspiratory oxygen concentration (PaO2/FiO2) of <250, urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h, platelet count <80 000/mm3, unconsciousness, or an acidotic pH <7.3.

In our study, 63.5% of ICU patients received a diagnosis of AKI (197/310) based on the criteria established by the Risk, Injury, Failure, Loss of function, and End-stage renal disease (RIFLE) and Acute Kidney Injury Network (AKIN) associations. AKI was confirmed based on the worst criteria of creatinine or urine output for these patients.26

The exclusion criteria were as follows: end-stage renal disease (ESRD), acute pulmonary edema, active bleeding diagnoses, acute coronary syndrome; age <18 years; ICU admissions lasting <24 hours; and cases of AKI unrelated to sepsis.

The number of AKI cases referred to the hospital and the estimated prevalence at the neighboring tertiary center, King Abdulaziz University Hospital, Jeddah, were calculated at a 95% CI with a 5% confidence limit to calculate the sample size using the open-source Epi version 6 program.

Diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and SBP were measured using a traditional mercury manometer following standard procedures. Blood urea, creatinine, and lactate levels were determined using an automated biochemistry analyzer (Cobas Integra 400; Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). White blood cell (WBC) count was analyzed in a complete blood count using an automated cell counter (DxH 560, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA). Blood and random urine IL-10 levels were assayed using the Invitrogen Human IL-10 ELISA kit (Cat #KAC1321) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

All enrolled patients, or their legal representatives, provided informed consent and agreed to participate in the study. The protocol and consent form for a previous study were amended and approved by the UQU-MED Institutional Review Board (HAPO-02-K-012-2015-5-111) and the Al Noor Specialty Hospital Ethical Committee. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

2.2. Molecular analysis

The Promega Wizard Genomic DNA purification kit was used to isolate genomic DNA from 5 mL of Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) blood, which was then stored at −20°C until further use. The quantity and quality of genomic DNA were assessed using the NanoDrop spectrophotometer. For rs1518111 genotyping, real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and allele discrimination were conducted using TaqMan® Genotyping Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE).

Allelic discrimination was achieved using the fluorescence signal produced by two TaqMan fluorogenic probes, showing the allelic variation as 5’-AATTCCCTGCAATCAGGAAGCAGAG[T/C].CTCCCTTCCCTTAATCATGCTGCAC-3’.

PCR amplification and genotyping were performed using the StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under default conditions optimized by the supplier. Each well’s reaction mix comprised 2 µL of genomic DNA, 0.5 µL of TaqMan 40× Assay mix (probes and primers), 10 µL of TaqMan Genotyping master mix, and 7.5 µL of nuclease-free water, totaling a 20 µL reaction mix. The plate was sealed with a transparent adhesive film, briefly spun, and immediately placed into the machine for the run, with results obtained after 90 minutes.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Statistical data were analyzed using SPSS version 21 (Chicago, IL). Student’s t test was used to evaluate continuous variables, reported as mean ± SD. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Nominal variables were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square (χ2) test and expressed as numbers and percentage frequencies. Genotype and allele frequencies were computed using OpenEpi software, and the χ2 test determined the odds ratio (OR) with 95% CIs. Data for the three genotypes were compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA). Multivariable regression analysis assessed septic shock as a potential predictor.

4. DISCUSSION

One of the most frequent and serious complications of sepsis is AKI, which is associated with high mortality and unfavorable outcomes. Sepsis is characterized by the redistribution of blood flow and disruption of microcirculation due to sympathetic nervous system activation, endothelial damage, and the release of vasoactive chemicals such as angiotensin II, endothelin, and vasopressin.5

A careful balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory responses is necessary to generate an efficient immune response to an infection while also limiting tissue damage. IL-10 plays a crucial role in controlling the immune response and possesses strong anti-inflammatory properties. However, infections can exploit the immunosuppressive characteristics of IL-10 to aid in their own survival.27 IL-10 is an essential component of the anti-inflammatory cytokine network involved in sepsis. The long arm of chromosome 1 contains the IL-10 gene, and the promoter region contains SNPs for the gene. In a genetic association study involving 550 patients with sepsis, the IL-10 gene CGG haplotype (592C/734G/3367G) was associated with higher 28-day mortality in critically ill patients who developed sepsis from pneumonia.28 A meta-analysis suggested that −592C/A and −1082A/G polymorphisms in the IL-10 gene are associated with sepsis susceptibility in Caucasian and Asian populations, respectively.18

The purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between the risk of sepsis-induced AKI outcome in critically ill patients and the IL-10 gene rs1518111 polymorphism and circulating serum/urine IL-10 markers. Our findings indicate that, regardless of the type of infection or severity of sepsis, the presence of at least one T allele increases the likelihood of sepsis-induced AKI (OR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.07-3.17; p < 0.001). These findings align with those reported by Federspiel et al,29 who compared patients with no copies of the IL-10 CGG haplotype to those with one or two copies of the CGG haplotype and were free of AKI, concluding that the IL-10 CGG haplotype may offer protection against sepsis-related AKI.

The relationship between the IL-10 gene polymorphism and risk of DN has been investigated. Babel et al30 reported that DN susceptibility may be linked to the IL10-1082A/G polymorphism. Additionally, a meta-analysis reported that the IL10-1082A/G polymorphism potentially increases the risk of DN.31

A different meta-analysis revealed that the −1082A/G and −819G/A polymorphisms, particularly in the Asian population, have protective effects against the risk of developing DN in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.32

In a Chinese Han population, a previous study examined the relationship between DN susceptibility and the rs1518111 SNP in the IL-10 gene in a Chinese Han population. The case and control groups had statistically different mutant genotypes (OR, 3.091; 95% CI, 1.037-9.209; p = 0.042). Frequencies of the wild-type and mutant alleles were 20.37% and 79.63%, respectively, in cases and 29.23% and 70.77%, in healthy controls, respectively. They also reported a clear correlation between DN susceptibility and the rs1518111 polymorphism in the IL-10 gene and that the mutant allele was likely a risk factor for DN.16

El Helaly et al17 found that the allele of the rs1518111 SNP in the IL-10 gene could be linked to an increased risk for nephropathy in diabetic Egyptian children.

Our results of circulating IL-10 revealed that both blood and urine IL-10 levels were excellent predictors of sepsis-induced AKI in critically ill patients (AUC: 0.881 and 0.953 and sensitivity = 90.2% and 97.6% at cutoff 133.5 and 5.67 pg/mL, respectively). Blood and urine IL-10 levels were significantly correlated with each other and with WBC counts in critically ill patients with sepsis (r = 0.539, 0.270, and 0.478, respectively; p < 0.001).

These findings are consistent with and strengthened by several studies that have established the relationship between IL-10 upregulation and the pathophysiology of kidney disease, as well as how interactions with other growth factors and aberrant IL-10 expression in response to various stimuli are related to the onset and development of various kidney diseases.33

Using high diagnostic performance criteria, Zhang et al34 found that blood IL-10 is an excellent predictor for sepsis, and a combination with IL-17 and procalcitonin results in a higher overall clinical benefit rate.

Myśliwska et al35 reported that diabetic patients with DN had higher circulating IL-10 levels than those without DN. Wong et al36 revealed an association between the degree of kidney damage in DN and IL-10 levels.

In this study, individuals with the CT genotype exhibited significantly higher blood and urine IL-10 levels than those with the TT/CC genotypes (Table 3). These remarkable interindividual differences suggest that blood IL-10 levels are genetically regulated via polymorphic IL-10 variations. This significant elevation recorded in the CT genotype is in concordance with the findings of studies that recorded significant higher plasma IL-10 levels in CT genotype polymorphisms than in both the CC and TT genotypes.37,38

We believe that the findings of this study will help explore immunoregulatory signaling pathways responsible for poor outcomes in septic patients, elucidating additional biomolecular tools to describe the early risk of developing sepsis-induced AKI in critically ill patients with sepsis in the ICU. This may contribute to both faster management and development of molecular targets for AKI prevention.

In conclusion, our study suggests that early detection of the rs1518111 polymorphism in the IL-10 gene associated with high circulating IL-10 levels is a predictive marker for poor outcomes in critically ill patients. We recommend using a panel for genetic detection of the rs1518111 polymorphism in urine and/or serum IL-10 as a predictor for the development of sepsis-associated AKI in critically ill patients with sepsis in the ICU. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to describe the combined use of blood and urine IL-10 levels with molecular measurement of polymorphisms to monitor and predict early AKI in critically ill patients with sepsis. We have shown that both polymorphic changes in IL-10 associated with abnormally high serum/urine IL-10 levels markers and correlation with the WBC count may explore immunoregulatory mechanisms underlying poor critical outcomes of critically ill patients with sepsis. Early prediction of AKI can preserve the compartmental structure of the kidney and prevent possible renal failure. We also found that certain genotypes or alleles of the rs1518111 intronic polymorphism in the IL-10 gene can affect the risk of S-AKI in critically ill patients with sepsis. This, in turn, strongly implicates the clinical consequences for patients.

However, our study has some limitations. First, we did not include a control group for the genotyping results; therefore, we could not compare the study groups for S-AKI development. Second, due to the small sample size, multivariate-adjusted analyses to account for potential confounders were not possible. Larger cohort studies are required to develop multivariate risk-prediction models for sepsis-induced AKI. The results should be regarded as preliminary because this was an exploratory study, and they should be subjected to more thorough testing and replication in subsequent research, as well as in different populations.
