Impact of Isocenter Configuration and Flattening Filter Free Delivery on VMAT Planning for Synchronous Bilateral Breast Cancer
Authors: Jiyong Zhang, Danli Hong, Yong Gan, Zewei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the relative impact of isocenter configuration and flattening filter free (FFF) beam delivery on dosimetric performance, radiobiological outcomes, and treatment efficiency in volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT) for synchronous bilateral breast cancer (SBBC).MethodsTwenty-two treatment plans were generated for patients with SBBC using four VMAT configurations, including single-isocenter and dual-isocenter strategies with either flattened (FF) or FFF beams. Target coverage and plan quality were assessed using standard dose volume parameters. Doses to organs at risk (OARs), including the whole lung, heart, left anterior descending artery (LAD), aorta, liver, spinal cord, and skin, were evaluated. Treatment times and total monitor units (MUs) were recorded. Radiobiological outcomes were estimated using tumor control probability (TCP) and normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) models. Statistical comparisons were performed between planning strategies and beam delivery modes.ResultsDual-isocenter VMAT consistently achieved superior target coverage, dose homogeneity, and sparing of thoracic OARs compared with single-isocenter VMAT. In particular, dual-isocenter planning reduced the mean heart dose by approximately 35% (11.83 ± 1.41 Gy vs 7.69 ± 1.11 Gy) and decreased heart V10Gy by more than 50% (41.19 ± 8.14% vs 20.45 ± 6.27%) compared with conventional single-isocenter VMAT. These improvements translated into more favorable TCP and NTCP estimates, indicating that plan quality was primarily influenced by isocenter configuration. Within the same isocenter strategy, FFF beam delivery produced comparable target coverage, OAR doses, and radiobiological outcomes relative to FF beams. Although FFF plans required higher MUs, they did not significantly reduce treatment times despite the availability of higher dose rates.ConclusionIsocenter configuration had a greater impact on dosimetric and radiobiological outcomes than beam flattening mode. Dual-isocenter VMAT improved target coverage and OAR sparing, while FFF delivery offered limited benefit. Single-isocenter techniques may improve efficiency and simplicity, highlighting the tradeoff between plan quality and treatment efficiency.Keywords: synchronous bilateral breast cancer, volumetric modulated arc therapy, flattening filter free beam, radiobiology, dosimetry

Introduction

Synchronous bilateral breast cancer (SBBC) represents a complex clinical challenge in radiation oncology, characterized by the need to deliver therapeutic doses to two separate target volumes while minimizing radiation exposure to critical organs at risk (OARs), such as the heart, lungs, and liver.1,2 Epidemiologically, SBBC is a relatively rare but increasingly recognized clinical entity, accounting for approximately 1–3% of all breast cancer cases depending on the definition and study population.3–5 Traditional radiotherapy techniques often struggle to achieve optimal dose conformity under these constraints. Volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), with its dynamic multileaf collimation and gantry rotation, has emerged as an efficient solution for complex targets.6,7 However, recent technological advances have further enhanced VMAT through the implementation of flattening filter free (FFF) mode, offering unique dosimetric and radiobiological advantages that warrant rigorous investigation in SBBC treatment planning.

In conventional linear accelerators, a flattening filter (FF) is utilized to produce a uniform photon fluence across the treatment field. While this approach yields a flat dose profile, it also introduces several dosimetric disadvantages, including increased photon attenuation, elevated head scatter, and longer beam-on times. The advent of FFF mode has marked a significant advancement in radiotherapy delivery by removing the FF. The resulting unflattened beam is characterized by a forward-peaked intensity profile, substantially higher dose rates, and a softer energy spectrum.8 From a dosimetric perspective, FFF mode has demonstrated several advantages in VMAT delivery, including the potential for shorter treatment durations.9,10 In addition, removal of the flattening filter is associated with reduced head scatter and out-of-field dose, which may limit unnecessary irradiation of surrounding normal tissues and potentially decrease the risk of secondary malignancies.11–13 However, whether these dosimetric and radiobiological advantages can be consistently translated to the treatment of SBBC remains unclear, given the unique anatomical complexity, extended target length, and involvement of bilateral OARs.

Previous SBBC planning studies have explored different radiotherapy strategies, including 3D-CRT, IMRT, and VMAT, and have generally demonstrated that VMAT provides improved dose conformity and OAR sparing;14,15 however, there remains no consensus regarding the optimal isocenter strategy, particularly in the comparison between single- and dual-isocenter VMAT approaches. This uncertainty underscores the need for further investigation and constitutes the primary question addressed in the present study.

Beyond physical dose parameters, radiobiological models such as tumor control probability (TCP) and normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) offer critical insights into treatment efficacy and the risk of radiation-induced toxicity. These models were therefore included to complement dosimetric comparisons by enabling estimation of the potential clinical impact of different planning strategies, rather than relying on dose metrics alone. By linking dose distributions to anticipated clinical outcomes, these models provide a more comprehensive and clinically relevant framework for treatment plan evaluation.

Although FFF-VMAT presents several theoretical advantages, its implementation in the management of SBBC remains underexplored. To date, few studies have concurrently addressed the dosimetric and radiobiological implications of this technique, revealing a notable gap in the current body of research. Importantly, in clinical practice, the choice between single- and dual-isocenter planning also involves a trade-off between improved dosimetric accuracy and increased setup complexity, treatment delivery time, and potential geometric uncertainty. This study seeks to address this gap by systematically evaluating the dosimetric and radiobiological characteristics of FFF-VMAT in the treatment of SBBC, with particular emphasis on the role of isocenter configuration and beam mode. Therefore, this study aims to clarify whether FFF delivery provides additional benefit beyond isocenter optimization in SBBC VMAT planning.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

This retrospective planning study included twenty-two female patients diagnosed with SBBC who underwent radiotherapy between January 2012 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria comprised pathologically confirmed bilateral breast cancer, postoperative indication for adjuvant whole-breast irradiation, and availability of high-resolution planning CT images. The distribution of tumor stages was as follows: 7 patients (31.8%) with pT1N0M0, 5 patients (22.7%) with pT2N0M0, 6 patients (27.3%) with pT2N1M0, 2 patients (9.1%) with pT1N2M0, and 2 patients (9.1%) with pT2N2M0. Among them, the target volumes of 10 patients included the regional lymph node irradiation according to clinical indications. The inclusion of regional nodal target volumes was determined by the individual patient’s disease characteristics and remained identical across all planning techniques for that patient. The mean patient age was 48.5 years, with a range from 30 to 76 years.

All cases were completely replanned in a retrospective manner using a unified institutional planning protocol applied consistently across the entire study cohort. This ensured that all comparisons were performed under identical planning conditions, independent of the original clinical treatment plans. All CT datasets were recontoured or verified according to a consistent institutional contouring protocol to eliminate inter-era variability arising from the long study period.

Imaging and Contouring

All patients were treated in the supine position with both arms raised. Planning CT scans were acquired using a 16-slice CT simulator (Brilliance CT Big Bore, Philips Medical Systems, Amsterdam, Netherlands) with a 3 mm slice thickness. The clinical target volume (CTV) included bilateral whole breasts, with or without regional lymph nodes depending on clinical indications. Planning target volume (PTV) was generated by isotropic expansion of the CTV by 5 mm, excluding 3 mm from the skin surface. OARs, including the heart, bilateral lungs, spinal cord, esophagus, liver, and skin, were delineated according to Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) contouring guidelines. All target volumes and OARs were delineated by an experienced radiation oncologist and subsequently reviewed and approved by a senior radiation oncologist prior to plan generation. This quality assurance process ensured consistency in contour definition and adherence to institutional contouring standards across all cases.

Treatment Planning Techniques

All treatment plans were generated in the Eclipse treatment planning system (version 16.1, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA) for delivery on a Varian TrueBeam linear accelerator. To comprehensively assess the impact of beam mode and isocenter configuration on dosimetric outcomes, four techniques were designed for each patient: single-isocenter VMAT (sVMAT), dual-isocenter VMAT (dVMAT), single-isocenter FFF-VMAT (FFF-sVMAT), and dual-isocenter FFF-VMAT (FFF-dVMAT). For patients requiring regional nodal irradiation, the corresponding nodal target volumes were consistently incorporated into all four planning techniques. Consequently, all comparisons were performed using identical target volume definitions within each patient, thereby minimizing the potential influence of clinical heterogeneity on the dosimetric analysis. The prescription dose was 50 Gy in 25 fractions, covering at least 95% of the PTV. Dose calculations were performed using the Anisotropic Analytical Algorithm (AAA) with a grid resolution of 2.5 mm. The dose constraints for OARs were derived from a study of patients with unilateral breast cancer, as well as prior research on SBBC.1,16–19 The OARs dose constraints during optimization are presented in Table 1. All optimization objectives, dose constraints, smoothing parameters, and calculation settings were kept strictly identical across all four planning techniques to ensure a fully standardized comparison framework.

In the sVMAT approach, a single isocenter was positioned at the midline near the sternum to simultaneously cover both breast targets. Two pairs of partial coplanar arcs were arranged tangentially, ensuring efficient target coverage and sparing of the heart, lungs, and contralateral breast. This configuration simplified patient setup and minimized potential junction uncertainties. This single-isocenter configuration also simplified patient positioning, as both sides could be aligned under a common reference point, thereby decreasing setup time and mitigating potential dose discrepancies at field junctions caused by patient movement or rotational misalignment.

In contrast, the dVMAT technique placed two independent isocenters at the geometric centers of the left and right breast volumes. Two tangential partial arcs were designed for each breast to achieve comprehensive target coverage and enhanced sparing of adjacent organs such as the heart and ipsilateral lung. While this configuration offered greater flexibility in contour adaptation, it also required meticulous patient setup to prevent dose overlap or underdosage near the midline junction. Both sVMAT and dVMAT plans were delivered using 6 MV flattened photon beams at a constant dose rate of 600 MU/min.

The FFF-sVMAT plan followed the same geometric design as sVMAT but utilized 6 MV FFF beams with a maximum dose rate of 1400 MU/min. Similarly, the FFF-dVMAT approach combined the dual-isocenter geometry with FFF beam delivery.

All four treatment plans were generated by an experienced medical physicist within a standardized planning workflow and subsequently reviewed by a senior medical physicist prior to dosimetric evaluation. This quality assurance process ensured compliance with institutional planning objectives and minimized inter-planner variability.

Plan Evaluation

For the PTV, the reported parameters included D98%, D2%, D50%, V90%, V95%, V105%, and V110%. D98%, D2%, and D50% represented the doses received by 98%, 2%, and 50% of the target volume, respectively, while V90%, V95%, V105%, and V110% referred to the proportions of the target volume receiving 90%, 95%, 105%, and 110% of the prescribed dose. For the OARs, serial tissues were analyzed using the maximum point dose (Dmax) and the maximum volume receiving a specific dose (VxGy). The serial tissues examined included the heart, spinal cord, esophagus, trachea, and femoral heads. Parallel tissues, such as the lungs and liver, were evaluated based on the maximum volume and mean dose (Dmean). The monitor units (MUs) were quantitatively analyzed across all planning techniques, including sVMAT, dVMAT, FFF-sVMAT, and FFF-dVMAT. Correspondingly, treatment times were rigorously recorded for each plan, defined as the duration from the initiation of the first field beam-on to the termination of the final field beam-off, excluding any positioning time.

The selected values reflect commonly accepted estimates of tumor control and normal tissue response for conventionally fractionated breast irradiation. For the PTV, the model parameters were set as a=−7.2, γ50=2, and a tumor control dose TCD50 of 28 Gy. For the heart, the parameters were defined as a=3, γ50=3, and a normal tissue complication dose TD50 of 50 Gy. For the lungs, the corresponding parameters were a=1, γ50=2, and TD50=24.5 Gy. Although uncertainty exists regarding the exact parameter values reported across different patient populations and radiobiological models, a consistent parameter set was applied to all four planning techniques within each patient. Therefore, the radiobiological analysis was intended primarily to facilitate relative comparison among planning strategies rather than to predict absolute clinical outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Dosimetric parameters were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As most variables did not follow a normal distribution, overall comparisons among the four treatment techniques were performed using the Friedman test. When a significant overall difference was identified, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To account for the increased risk of type I error associated with multiple pairwise testing, P values from pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) procedure. Adjusted two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

In relation to the whole lung, dual-isocenter planning resulted in significantly lower low dose and intermediate dose volumes, including V5Gy, V10Gy, and V20Gy, along with a reduced mean lung dose. Similarly, cardiac dose was markedly decreased, with substantial reductions observed across all evaluated dose volume metrics, from V5Gy to V40Gy, as well as in mean heart dose. Dose exposure to the LAD was also significantly reduced with dual-isocenter planning.

For other critical structures, including the aorta, liver, spinal cord, and skin, dual- isocenter plans consistently achieved lower maximum dose and or mean dose values, indicating improved protection of OARs. Across all evaluated OARs, no statistically significant differences were observed between the corresponding FF and FFF plans.

Radiobiological Assessment

Discussion

In this study, we compared single-isocenter and dual-isocenter VMAT techniques, with and without FFF beams, for the treatment of SBBC. The results indicate that isocenter configuration exerts a greater influence on dosimetric and radiobiological outcomes than beam flattening mode. Specifically, dual-isocenter VMAT tended to achieve superior target coverage and OARs sparing, whereas single-isocenter VMAT was associated with shorter treatment delivery times.

Dual-isocenter VMAT plans demonstrated improved PTV coverage and plan quality, as reflected by more favorable dose homogeneity and conformity metrics. This finding is likely related to the geometric characteristics of SBBC, which often involve a large longitudinal extent of the target volume and asymmetric bilateral anatomy. Under such conditions, the use of a single isocenter requires broader fields and increased modulation to adequately cover both breast targets, which may compromise dose conformity. By contrast, the use of two separate isocenters allows more localized beam arrangements and optimization, facilitating improved dose shaping for each target.

In addition to improved target coverage, dual-isocenter techniques resulted in lower doses to critical OARs, including the whole lung, heart, LAD, aorta, liver, spinal cord, and skin. Dual-isocenter VMAT plans have been shown to reduce mean dose to the whole lung and heart compared with single-isocenter VMAT plans in treatment of SBBC, likely due to improved geometric separation of target volumes and more focused beam modulation for each side, providing results consistent with those reported by Boman et al26 Previous dosimetric studies have also reported that single-isocenter VMAT may produce greater low-dose spread to OARs, whereas dual isocenter techniques reduce unnecessary dose overlap in central thoracic regions.24 Given that SBBC irradiation inherently exposes bilateral thoracic organs, even modest reductions in dose may be clinically relevant. The dosimetric advantages observed with dual-isocenter VMAT were further reflected in lower NTCP values for the heart and lungs, suggesting a potential reduction in the risk of late radiation-induced toxicity. Although NTCP modeling is inherently dependent on selected parameters, the consistent trend across both dosimetric and radiobiological analyses supports the robustness of this finding.

Despite these advantages, single-isocenter VMAT was associated with significantly shorter treatment delivery times. This difference can be attributed to the simplified setup and uninterrupted delivery workflow inherent to single-isocenter approaches. In clinical practice, shorter treatment times may reduce patient discomfort and intrafraction motion, particularly in patients who have difficulty maintaining a stable supine position.27–29 Therefore, while dual-isocenter VMAT offers dosimetric and radiobiological benefits, single-isocenter VMAT may still represent a practical alternative in selected clinical scenarios where treatment efficiency is prioritized.

With respect to beam flattening mode, no significant differences were observed between FFF-based and flattened-beam plans within the same isocenter configuration. Although FFF beams are often associated with higher dose rates and reduced head scatter, these theoretical advantages did not translate into meaningful improvements in plan quality or radiobiological outcomes in the SBBC setting. This suggests that, in this clinical scenario, dosimetric outcomes are predominantly governed by geometric and planning-related factors rather than beam flattening mode. The wide arc span and high modulation required for bilateral targets may offset the theoretical benefits of FFF through increased MLC modulation, resulting in comparable dose distributions to surrounding normal tissues. Moreover, heart and lung doses were mainly influenced by arc geometry and beam overlap, with isocenter configuration playing a more decisive role than head scatter reduction. Consistently, treatment delivery efficiency was similar between FF and FFF plans, indicating that gantry speed and MLC motion, rather than dose rate, were the primary limiting factors. Several studies have reported that, in VMAT delivery, the maximum dose rate provided by FFF beams is not fully exploited because mechanical constraints such as gantry speed and MLC motion limit delivery efficiency. For conventional fractionation doses (200 cGy per fraction), the higher maximum dose rate of FFF modes does not result in substantial reductions in delivery time, unlike in high-dose SBRT regimens.30,31 The significantly higher MUs observed in FFF plans, consistent with the findings of Duan et al18 further suggest increased modulation complexity, potentially offsetting the expected gains in treatment efficiency. These findings further indicate that the advantages of FFF beams should not be assumed to be universally applicable across all treatment sites and planning scenarios. In large-field conventionally fractionated VMAT for SBBC, the potential benefits of higher dose rates appear to be largely neutralized by delivery-related mechanical constraints and increased modulation complexity, emphasizing the importance of site-specific evaluation when selecting beam modes.

From a clinical perspective, the findings of this study suggest that isocenter configuration should be considered a primary factor in VMAT planning for SBBC. Dual-isocenter VMAT may be preferred when optimal target coverage and OARs sparing are of primary concern, particularly in patients with large breast volumes or extended target lengths. Conversely, single-isocenter VMAT may be appropriate when treatment efficiency and workflow simplicity are prioritized, provided that dosimetric constraints can be adequately met.

Although several dosimetric endpoints remained statistically significant after BH-FDR adjustment, the magnitude of some differences was relatively small. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted solely on the basis of P values. Greater emphasis should be placed on whether the observed dosimetric improvements are likely to translate into meaningful reductions in normal tissue toxicity or improvements in tumor control. Parameters showing only marginal numerical differences, despite statistical significance, may have limited clinical impact.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small due to the rarity of SBBC and the single-institution nature of the study. However, because each patient served as their own control across all four planning techniques, inter-patient variability was minimized and the robustness of the dosimetric comparisons was enhanced. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger multicenter studies. Second, the radiobiological analysis was based on model-derived TCP and NTCP estimates rather than clinical outcome data. Third, setup uncertainty and intrafraction motion, which may differ between single- and dual-isocenter techniques, were not explicitly evaluated. Future studies incorporating robustness analysis, motion assessment, and long-term clinical follow-up are warranted to further clarify the optimal VMAT strategy for SBBC.

Another limitation relates to the dose calculation algorithm used in this study. All treatment plans were optimized and calculated using the AAA, which is widely implemented in clinical practice but may be less accurate than advanced algorithms such as Acuros XB or Monte Carlo methods in heterogeneous thoracic regions. Although this limitation is unlikely to affect the relative comparisons among the four planning techniques because all plans were generated under identical calculation conditions, future investigations employing more advanced dose calculation approaches may provide additional insight into the dosimetric impact of FF and FFF beam delivery. Furthermore, the TCP and NTCP parameters adopted in this study were derived from previously published models and may be subject to inherent uncertainties. The very low NTCP values observed for several OARs likely reflect the relatively low dose exposure achieved with contemporary VMAT techniques, which may reduce the sensitivity of existing radiobiological models to detect subtle inter-plan differences.

In summary, this study demonstrates that isocenter configuration plays a central role in determining dosimetric quality, radiobiological outcomes, and delivery efficiency in VMAT for SBBC. Dual-isocenter VMAT provides improved target coverage and OARs sparing, whereas single-isocenter VMAT offers the advantage of shorter treatment times. The use of FFF beams yields outcomes comparable to those of FF beams and does not independently confer a clear advantage in this clinical setting. Although the observed dosimetric and radiobiological differences suggest potential clinical implications, the present study was designed as a retrospective planning analysis with model-based TCP/NTCP estimation. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted primarily as technical guidance for VMAT optimization in SBBC rather than direct evidence of improved clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In VMAT-based SBBC treatment, isocenter configuration had a greater impact on dosimetric and radiobiological outcomes than beam flattening mode. Dual-isocenter VMAT improved target coverage and OAR sparing, whereas FFF delivery showed limited additional benefit. Single-isocenter techniques may offer advantages in delivery efficiency and setup simplicity, highlighting the need to balance plan quality with treatment practicality when selecting isocenter strategies.
