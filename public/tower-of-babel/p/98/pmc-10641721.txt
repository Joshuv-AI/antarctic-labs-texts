Acoustic radiation force impulse shear wave elastography quantifies upper limb muscle in patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy
Authors: Chia-Wei Lin, Jeng-Yi Shieh, Po-Hsiang Tsui, Chia-Ling Chen, Chun-Hao Lu, Yi-Hsuan Hung, Hsiao-Yuan Lee, Wen-Chin Weng, Susan Shur-Fen Gau
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Arm shear wave velocities (SWV) decreased with age in Duchenne muscular dystrophy.•Arm SWV distinguished Duchenne muscular dystrophy from controls across age groups.•Arm SWV correlated with upper limb muscle function in Duchenne muscular dystrophy.•Shear wave elastography can serve as an imaging biomarker in muscular dystrophy.Keywords: Duchenne, Muscular dystrophy, Ultrasound, Elastography, Shear wave

Introduction

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an X-linked, progressive muscular disease affecting 1 in 3600 ∼ 8000 live male births [1], [2]. Variants in the DMD gene lead to an absence or defect in the protein dystrophin [3], resulting in elevated muscle damage and progressive fibro-fatty replacement [4]. Very few studies have focused on upper limb muscle alteration and related upper limb function in patients with DMD, despite their important roles in daily living and impacts on quality of life [5], [6].

Repeated muscle biopsies are invasive and limited to the sampling site; therefore, there is a need to develop non-invasive biomarkers for detecting muscle structure alteration in DMD. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and spectroscopy (MRS) of upper extremities in patients with DMD showed higher muscle water T2 signals in younger patients [7], [8], higher intramuscular fat fraction in older patients [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], and the intramuscular fat progresses with age and negatively correlates with limb function [8], [10], [12]. Ultrasound offers some advantages over MRI, as it can be performed at the bedside, there is no need for sedation for children younger than four years old [13], and it is relatively low cost for follow-up [14]. Acoustic radiation force impulse/shear-wave elastography (ARFI/SWE), a type of ultrasound elastography, has been introduced to quantify muscle stiffness in patients with DMD [15], [16], [17]. A focused acoustic beam generates shear waves within an organ, and the shear wave velocity (SWV) can be obtained [17]. In this technique, focused acoustic radiation force pushing pulses of short duration are used to generate shear waves within an organ of interest, and the speed of the shear waves propagating away from the pushing location is measured [17]. By quantitatively measuring the shear-wave velocity (SWV), the stiffness of the examined tissue can be evaluated through this equation: G = ρcs2 (whereas G is the shear modulus, ρ is the material density, and cs is the shear wave velocity) [18]. A higher SWV indicates higher stiffness and vice versa [18]. The muscle stiffness quantified by ARFI/SWE could reflect the microstructural composition, particularly the fibrosis and fatty infiltration alteration through disease progression in patients with DMD [19], [20]. Lacourpaille et al., using ultrasound elastography, found that young patients with DMD exhibited higher stiffness in the biceps and triceps than similar-aged, typically developing controls (TDC) [21]. However, they did not correlate the degree of biceps and triceps stiffness with upper limb function [21]. Our group using ARFI/SWE has shown that decreasing leg stiffness, possibly related to fatty infiltration, correlates with ambulation function decline in patients with DMD [20].

Although ARFI/SWE was an objective measure for lower extremity stiffness found in our prior study [20] and higher stiffness in the biceps and triceps in DMD than TDC reported by Lacourpaille et al. [21], there have been no reports regarding the age effect on muscle stiffness in patients with DMD compared to TDC and the correlations between stiffness and function in the upper extremities in patients with DMD. Therefore, the study aimed to investigate whether ARFI/SWE on quantifying upper limb muscle stiffness could be a potential non-invasive biomarker for long-term exploring upper limb muscle pathophysiology and monitoring upper limb function in children, adolescents, and young adults with DMD compared to their TDC counterparts. We hypothesized that (1) ARFI/SWE on quantifying upper limb muscle stiffness differentiates patients with DMD from TDC, and (2) upper limb muscle stiffness quantified by ARFI/SWE decreases with age and correlates with upper limb function in patients with DMD. We applied the ARFI/SWE to measure and compare the SWVs of the deltoid and biceps muscle in participants with DMD and age and sex-matched TDC. In addition, we correlated the SWVs of these two muscles to upper limb function measured by the performance of upper limb modules in patients with DMD.

Materials and methods

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and participant consents

The Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan (Approval number 201503025RINC) and Hsin-Chu Branch, Hsin Chu, Taiwan (Approval number 104-060-F) approved this study before the study implementation. All participants (child asset if appropriate) and their parents provided written informed consent.

Study design

Male patients with DMD aged 3–18 years old were recruited from Multidisciplinary and Integrated Neuromuscular Clinics in the National Taiwan University Children's Hospital. All the patients with DMD had clinical presentations consistent with DMD, and their diagnosis of DMD was confirmed according to a muscle biopsy with absent dystrophin expression and/or genetic analysis of the dystrophin gene. Sex- and age-matched TDC were also recruited; none had a history or physical findings of weakness or neuromuscular disorders after clinical evaluation. Participants who had undergone surgery on their upper limbs were excluded from the study. All the participants underwent the ARFI/SWE examinations. Patients with DMD additionally received performance tests of upper limbs. The study flow chart is presented in Fig. 1.

The final sample consisted of 37 male patients with DMD and 30 TDC. Among them, 34 DMD and all TDC also participated in a different research topic, which our previous study utilizing ARFI/SWE on investigating the lower limb muscle stiffness and correlated ambulation function in DMD and their TDC counterparts[20]. Due to the broad age range of the sample across the developmental stage from early childhood to late adolescence, both DMD and TDC participants were further stratified into three age groups (3–8 years, 9–11 years, and 12–18 years) for statistical analysis. For the DMD and TDC groups, there were 13 and 17 participants in the age group of 3–8 years, 12 and 6 participants in the age group of 9–11 years, and 12 and 7 participants in the age group of 12–18 years, respectively.

Acoustic radiation force impulse shear wave elastography (ARFI/SWE) examinations

All participants underwent ARFI/SWE (Siemens ACUSON S2000 Ultrasound System, Virtual Touch Tissue Quantification Mode, 9L4 linear transducer, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) to quantitatively measure the SWVs of the deltoid and biceps muscles in the dominant limb with longitudinal scan. The longitudinal scan consisted of the middle deltoid muscle half away from the acromion to deltoid tuberosity along the muscle fiber and biceps muscle at two-thirds of the way between the acromion and the antecubital fossa (see Fig. 1).

All the measurements were performed using a standard position and protocol. The participants were placed supine, with the shoulder in external rotation, the elbow in extension, and the forearm in supination. Surface electromyography was applied throughout the examinations to ensure the muscles were resting. We first used the B-mode to confirm and determine optimal transducer location, orientation, and alignment by being able to trace several thin muscle fascicles across the image without interruption[22]. The ARFI/SWV acquisitions were performed in the longitudinal plane for each muscle. The rectangular electronic box-based region of interest (ROI 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm) was placed within the target muscle belly. The ROI was chosen carefully to avoid the muscle fascia or areas near the bone. The transducer was oriented parallel to the muscle fibers to obtain SWV measurements in the longitudinal axis [22]. To mitigate the impact of transducer pressure on muscle stiffness measurements [23], we kept a thin layer of acoustic gel on the skin, and the transducer was held stationary during SWV acquisition. An acoustic radiation force impulse was applied to induce a shear wave that traveled through the ROI. Virtual TouchTM Quantification Technology (Siemens) was used to compute the value of SWV (range, 0.5 ∼ 10.0 m/s) within the ROI. ARFI/SWV acquisition was performed at five sites within each muscle, and the mean of 5-point SWvs was calculated to represent a single value for each muscle (See Fig. 2)[24]. All ARFI/SWE measurements were made independently in different participants under a standard protocol by one of the two skilled physicians (C.W.L. and Y.H.H.), who had 6 years and 4 years of experience in musculoskeletal ultrasound, respectively, and were blinded to the participant's clinical status. To verify the inter-rater reliability of SWV measurements, three TDC participants were scanned independently by both assessors (C.W.L. and Y.H.H.). The inter-rater reliability evaluated by the intra-class correlations among three participants between the two assessors was 0.929 (Assessor 1: 2.66 [0.61] m/s vs Assessor 2: 2.45 [0.65] m/s).

Performance of upper limb module

A senior physical therapist specializing in pediatric neuromuscular disease assessed patients with DMD using the Performance of Upper Limb Module (PUL 1.2 module) [25]. The PUL 1.2 module is an externally-assessed clinical scale initially designed for patients with DMD, to measure upper limb motor performance across the spectrum of disease severity. The PUL 1.2 module has shown good inter-rater reliability with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.96 [25]. It could detect the progression of upper limb involvement in ambulant and non-ambulant DMD patients [26]. The PUL 1.2 module includes 22 items with an entry item to define the starting broad functional level and 21 items subdivided into proximal shoulder girdle level (4 items), middle elbow level (9 items), and distal wrist/hand level (8 items). Each dimension can be scored separately, with a maximum score of 16 for the proximal level, 34 for the middle level, and 24 for the distal level. A total score can be achieved by adding the three-level scores, with a maximum global score of 74 points; higher scores indicate better upper limb function and vice versa. The total PUL scores assess the general upper limb function. The PUL subscores measuring the three major level dimensions demonstrate the proximal to distal gradient loss of muscle function characterized in DMD patients [26]. In order to avoid the ceiling effect, four PUL timed performance tests were also recorded, measures (in seconds) including time to lift five light cans, time to lift five heavy cans, time to stack five light cans, and time to stack five heavy cans.

Statistical analyses

We conducted all statistical analyses using SPSS Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Shapiro-Wilk test was used to examine the normality of age and SWVs in three age groups of patients with DMD and TDC. For demographic data analysis, we used the Chi-Square test to compare the numbers of right-handedness and non-ambulates and t-test for age between patients with DMD and TDC for each age group.

Comparison of SWVs of each muscle between the DMD and TDC groups were made by Mann-Whitney test in each age group. Comparisons of SWVs of the deltoid and biceps muscles among three age groups within participants with DMD or TDC were performed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni correction for post-hoc multiple comparisons. In patients with DMD, we performed Pearson's correlations between SWVs of each muscle and muscle functional level, which included PUL scores and timed test measures (in seconds). The intra-class correlation was used to analyze the inter-rater reliability of the SWV of each muscle. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Of the 37 participants with DMD, 23(62.2 %) are ambulant patients, 25 (67.6 %) received steroids, and none participated in clinical trials for therapeutic interventions.

Upper limb ARFI-SWV comparison between DMD and TDC

The tissue composition within muscles contributes to muscle stiffness [27]. Lack of dystrophin in DMD results in disassembling the dystrophin-associated protein complex and losing the interaction between F-actin and the extracellular matrix, further leading to sarcolemma weakening, functional ischemia, cytosolic calcium overloading, and regeneration failure in muscle [28]. The inability to regenerate muscle underlies muscle wasting, fibrotic remodeling, and fatty cell replacement in DMD [28], [29]. Our findings of higher SWVs of the biceps muscle in young children (aged 3–8 years) with DMD, and lower SWVs of the biceps muscle in adolescents (age 12–18 years) with DMD, compared with their TDC counterparts could be explained by initially increased fibrosis [29], and later fatty infiltration within the muscle in patients with DMD [10].

Upper limb ARFI-SWV comparison between DMD and TDC in young children

We hypothesize that the elevated stiffness observed in the biceps muscle of young children with DMD is likely attributed to the increased fibrosis within the muscle [4], [29]. This notion is substantiated by previous muscle biopsy studies demonstrating marked perimysial and endomysial fibrosis in upper limb muscles of individuals with DMD, particularly at the time of diagnosis (i.e., before the age of 7 years); the increased fibrosis can be detected as early as 2 years old and remains constant up until the age of 6 years [30]. This hypothesis is also supported by Lacourpaille et al.’s ultrasound elastography study reporting higher shear elastic modulus in triceps and biceps muscle of young DMD patients compared to TDC [21].

Upper limb ARFI-SWV comparison between DMD and TDC in adolescents

In contrast, the decreased biceps muscle stiffness in adolescent patients with DMD over TDC observed in our study is likely contributed by increased fatty infiltration within the muscle, a characteristic of advanced stages of DMD [12], [31]. This notion is supported by prior quantitative MRI and MRS studies demonstrating higher fat fraction within the triceps [9], biceps [8], [9], [12], and forearm muscles [7], [11] in DMD patients older than 10 years old over age-matched TDC.

In contrast, our study did not find significant differences in deltoid SWVs between DMD and TDC stratified by age groups. Such discrepant findings, significant group differences in the biceps SWVs but no differences in the deltoid SWVs, could be explained as follows. First, unlike the biceps muscle, where most MRI studies consistently demonstrate an increase in fat fraction with age in DMD patients compared to their age-matched TDC, findings related to the deltoid muscle are less conclusive [8], [12], [32]. For instance, Willcocks et al’s study supported our findings by showing increased MRI-T2 measures (reflecting increased fat fraction) in the biceps muscle of DMD patients aged 11 to 16 years compared to age-matched TDC. Still, they did not observe the same increase in the deltoid muscle [8]. We speculate that this difference shown in our and Willcocks et al’s [8] studies may be attributed to the early involvement of deltoid muscle as DMD manifested from the proximal–distal [1]. The early involvement of the deltoid muscle comprises a complex process that includes inflammation/edema, fibrosis, fatty infiltration, and muscle loss. This early, intricate, and progressive process in the deltoid muscle could account for the lack of a consistent trend in SWVs changes with age. Second, the muscle SWVs quantified in our study reflect the composition among fibrosis, fatty infiltration, and muscle volume within the muscle, rather than solely related to the percentage of fatty content, as measured in Forbes et al’s study using MRI [12]. Consequently, the muscle SWVs changes quantified in our study may not exclusively represent changes in fatty content but should also consider alterations in muscle composition, including fibrosis and muscle loss. Third, despite the variability in SWVs within the deltoid muscle among DMD individuals when stratified by age, a robust association exists between deltoid SWVs and upper limb function, as measured by PUL scores in DMD patients, particularly in the ambulatory stage. Therefore, we believe that measuring deltoid muscle SWVs in DMD patients extends beyond distinguishing DMD from TDC in the late stage; it serves as a valuable tool to assess and monitor the decline in upper limb function in DMD patients.

Advantages of ARFI/SWE on upper limb muscle in distinguishing DMD and TDC

Contrary to MRI and MRS fat fraction measures, which can only distinguish between participants with DMD and TDC at the relatively late stage of disease (older than 9 years, and 4–8 years old, respectively) [12], or muscle biopsies, which are clinically obtainable only at initial diagnosis, our work highlights the advantages of ARFI/SWE on quantifying the upper limb muscle SWVs. This approach with significance can differentiate between patients with DMD and TDC from the very early stage to the advanced stage of the disease, making SWVs a potential image biomarker for upper limb muscles throughout the illness.

Upper limb ARFI-SWV comparison across age groups

Results of upper limb ARFI-SWV comparison across age groups

Regarding the age effect on SWVs of the two muscle groups, in patients with DMD, the ANOVA results demonstrated a significant difference in SWVs of biceps among three age groups (F = 10.141, P < 0.001). The post hoc comparisons using Bonferroni correction showed that the SWVs of biceps decreased in the elder age group (mean [SD], Group 3–8 years vs Group 12–18 years, 3.25[0.21] m/s vs 2.61[0.48], P = 0.001; Group 9–11 years vs Group 12–18 years, 3.26[0.46] vs 2.61[0.48], P = 0.001) but no significant difference between the two child groups (See Table 2, Fig. 3). There were no differences in deltoid SWVs across three age groups (F = 0.880, P = 0.425) (See Table 2, Fig. 3). In the TDC group, no differences were observed in the SWVs of the deltoid (F = 3.062, P = 0.063) or biceps (F = 0.640, P = 0.535) among three age groups (See Table 2, Fig. 3).

Upper limb ARFI-SWV comparison in DMD and TDC across age groups

The age group analysis demonstrated that the SWV of the biceps muscle decreased as age increased in patients with DMD, which was not observed in TDC. As discussed previously, this suggests that the reduced muscle stiffness with age in DMD could be explained by increased fibrosis initially, followed by muscle necrosis, edema, loss of typical muscle fiber, and, most importantly, gradual replacement by fatty infiltration [29], [33]. These findings align with previous MRI studies showing progressive fat fraction increase in upper limb muscles of DMD across several age groups [10], [11], [12]. Notably, a study conducted on idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, using ultrasound shear wave elastography and MRI, demonstrated an association between higher MRI scores for edema and lower SWV values in muscle [34]. However, the MRI study on DMD found that the water (1H2O) T2 did not exhibit any significant differences among age groups in DMD patients [12]. Therefore, our work demonstrates the potential of using SWV measurements to track age-related muscle pathophysiology changes, specifically the process of intra-muscular fatty replacement in DMD.

Advantages of upper limb ARFI-SWV comparison in DMD and TDC across age groups in the current study

Our study focuses on the microstructure changes in proximal upper limb muscles across a wide age range in patients with DMD, which is a unique contribution to the existing research. Earlier MRI studies focused on forearm muscles and found that fat infiltration increased linearly with age [10], [11]. Our study expands this investigation to include more proximal muscles in the upper limb, such as the deltoid and biceps muscles. These muscles are crucial for daily activities like eating and personal care, and DMD typically progresses from proximal to distal [1].

Relationships between upper limb ARFI-SWV and PUL tests

Results of upper limb ARFI-SWV and PUL tests

Among 37 participants with DMD, 9 could not participate in the performance of upper limb modules because of medical issues (6: spine surgery within one year, 2: poor respiratory conditions, 1: intellectual disability), yielding 28 (75.7 %) patients with DMD (22 (78.6 %) ambulatory participants and 6 (21.4 %) non-ambulatory participants) to complete the PUL module. The SWVs of the upper limb muscle positively correlated with PUL scores, while the SWVs of the deltoid associated with middle-level-PUL scores in ambulant DMD participants (r = 0.527, P = 0.017); the SWVs of biceps correlated with total PUL (r = 0.897, P = 0.015), proximal-level PUL (r = 0.847, P = 0.033), and middle-level PUL, r = 0.892, P = 0.017) in non-ambulant DMD participants (See Fig. 4).

Scatterplots Depicting the Correlation of Upper Limb Muscle SWVs with PUL in Patients with DMD. DMD = Duchenne muscular dystrophy; PUL = performance of upper limb.

Relationships between upper limb ARFI-SWV and PUL tests

Our analysis revealed positive correlations between SWVs of the deltoid and biceps muscles with the PUL scores. Specifically, there was a moderate correlation between the deltoid muscle and PUL scores in ambulant DMD patients. In contrast, there was a strong correlation between biceps muscle stiffness and PUL scores in non-ambulant DMD patients. The correlations observed here suggest that decreasing muscle stiffness, indicating increased adipose infiltration in upper limb muscle, correlated with worse upper limb function in DMD.

Relationships between upper limb ARFI-SWV and PUL tests in ambulant DMD patients

Previous studies using PUL have demonstrated upper limb function impairment even in ambulatory DMD patients [25], [35], particularly in the shoulder girdle function [25], [35]. MRI studies also disclosed early fatty replacement in proximal muscle, such as the deltoid, infraspinatus, and subscapularis muscle, in ambulant DMD patients [12], [32]. Forbes and colleagues utilizing MRI T2 fat fraction measures found a negative correlation between a composite of upper extremity muscles (average deltoid, triceps, biceps, and forearm) and the total PUL scores in ambulant DMD patients [12]. Our study links the early proximal muscle (deltoid) pathological involvement explicitly [8], [32] to the corresponding upper limb muscle function impairment [25], [35] in ambulant DMD patients through ARFI/SWE.

Relationships between upper limb ARFI-SWV and PUL tests in non-ambulant DMD patients

Patients with DMD typically lost ambulation and required a wheelchair before age thirteen [1], necessitating research on upper limb muscle and their function in this stage [36]. Limited upper limb movement against gravity is reported after the loss of walking ability [37]. MRI/MRS studies show increased fatty infiltration in non-ambulatory DMD patients' biceps [9] and forearm [7], [11] muscles. Our study found a strong and positive correlation between biceps SWVs and PUL scores in non-ambulatory DMD patients, indicating increased intra-muscular fatty infiltration [38], which could be a reliable biomarker for tracking upper limb function decline in this stage. Our results align with Willcocks et al.'s research, demonstrating a correlation between increasing MRI-T2 measures (reflecting fat fraction changes) in the biceps and decreasing PUL scores in DMD patients [8]. Still, our study narrowed down these results specifically to non-ambulant DMD patients.

Advantages of ARFI/SWE over PUL tests

The PUL 1.2 module used in our study is a commonly used functional measure to track upper limb function progression in DMD [25]. However, as presented in our study, functional measures are subject to patients' cooperation and clinical conditions, such as a respiratory compromise or after major surgery [39]. ARFI/SWE showed advantages over the PUL module because it could be performed in a lying position and required less energy consumption, which expands its clinical applicability in this relentlessly progressive disease.

Relationships between upper limb ARFI-SWV and PUL timed measures

Results of upper limb ARFI-SWV and PUL timed measures

We conducted two PUL timed tests to minimize the ceiling effect: lifting five light cans and five heavy cans [25]. The SWVs of the upper limb muscle negatively correlated with PUL timed measures, which the SWVs of the deltoid associated with time to lift five light cans (r = −0.542, p = 0.014), time to lift five heavy cans (r = −0.531, p = 0.016) in ambulant DMD participants; the SWVs of biceps correlated with time to lift five heavy cans (r = −0.425, p = 0.049) in ambulant DMD participants. There were no observed correlations between PUL timed measures and SWVs of upper limb muscles in non-ambulant DMD participants (See Fig. 4).

Relationship between upper limb ARFI-SWV and PUL timed measures

Previous MRI studies have established the relationship between increased fatty infiltration in lower limb muscle and poorer performance in timed function tests in DMD patients [31], [40], [41]. Our study extends this to upper limbs, where decreasing deltoid/biceps SWVs correlate with a longer time to lift cans, indicating reduced efficiency related to increased fatty infiltration in muscle [38]. Specifically, the deltoid SWVs correlated with the timed lifting of light and heavy cans, while biceps SWVs only correlated with the timed lifting of heavy cans. Physiological studies show that lifting light objects requires minimal additional weight while lifting heavy objects engages both shoulder and elbow segments muscle for stability [42], [43]. Thus, ambulant DMD patients with advanced deltoid and biceps involvement struggle to lift heavy objects efficiently. Clinicians should consider arm support devices to promote efficiency and prevent fatigue while detecting decreasing deltoid and biceps SWVs in ambulant DMD patients, anticipating upper limb function decline [44], [45].

Advantages of applying ARFI/SWE on DMD in the current study

The advantages of using ARFI/SWE on quantifying SWVs of upper limbs in DMD patients include non-invasion [46], no need for sedation [46], quick [47], good reliability [48], applicability for comprehensively evaluating multiple limbs in a short time [47], [48], and easily accessible and for follow-up [47], [48]. Our study demonstrated that ARFI/SWE on quantifying the upper limb muscle stiffness could be used as a biomarker from very early to an advanced stage in patients with DMD, which showed superiority to other muscle biomarkers, such as muscle biopsy was not obtainable during disease progression, ultrasound quantified higher calibrated backscatter has ceiling effect in older patients with DMD [49]. The measurement of intramuscular fat fraction using MRI is applicable only in patients with DMD aged 9 and above [8], [11]. Furthermore, the SWVs of biceps and deltoid muscle obtained by ARFI/SWE showed significant correlations with the clinical upper limb function tests (PUL module) in both ambulant and non-ambulant DMD patients. Therefore, ARFI/SWE has the potential to serve as an imaging biomarker for assessing the efficacy of new drugs or therapies in addressing muscle pathophysiology alteration, especially in the late disease stage when ambulation function is limited but upper limb function is relatively preserved.

Technical considerations of ARFI/SWE on muscle

Various technical factors should be considered pertaining to ARFI/SWE on muscle, and our study implemented a standard protocol by taking all considerations [48], [50]. This involved ensuring precise patient positioning [48], identifying anatomical landmarks [48], and utilizing surface EMG to confirm muscle rest state [22]. Meanwhile, skeletal muscle is anisotropic because of the arrangement of fibers, fascicles, myofibrils, and collagens, and shear wave travels faster along the direction of fibers [48]. We aligned the transducer parallel to muscle fibers in the longitudinal plane to reduce the effects of anisotropy on shear wave propagation [22], [51]. We conducted many steps to ensure acquiring the SWVs in the longitudinal axis [22], including placing the transducer longitudinal to the muscle belly [48], using a B-mode image to confirm and determine optimal transducer location, orientation, and alignment by being able to trace several thin muscle fascicles across the image [22]. We placed ROIs away from the bone and surrounding fascia [48]. The standardized protocol contributes to the current study's good intra-rater reliability (ICC = 0.929).

Applications of ARFI/SWE on muscle

There are three excitation methods in ultrasound elastography: manual compression, controlled external vibration, and ARFI. In ARFI/SWE [17], the method utilized in our study, a focused acoustic radiation force pushing pulses of short duration are used to generate shear waves within an organ of interest, and the velocity of shear waves propagating away from the pushing location is measured [17]. ARFI is more reliable than the other two excitation methods because its stress is produced by fixed ultrasound waves [50].

Appropriate ultrasound elastography measures are crucial in muscle disease. Evidence suggests that the SWV is a better measure of muscle stiffness than the Young modulus. Young modulus, calculated using the formula E = 3G = 3ρcs2, where E represents the Young modulus, G is the shear modulus, ρ is the tissue density, and cs is the SWV, assumes tissue isotropy, homogeneity, and with known tissue density [17], [48], [52]. However, the muscle system is heterogeneous and anisotropic, and tissue density is not being measured, posing challenges in calculating the Young modulus [48]. Consequently, reporting SWV (m/sec) is recommended over Young modulus (kPa) in musculoskeletal ARFI/SWE measurements [48], as clearly demonstrated in our study.

Translation of ARFI/SWE to muscle disease

Previous studies utilizing ARFI/SWE on muscle disease have shown that the muscle SWVs are related to changes in intra-muscular composition. For instance, Rosskopf et al. found that the SWVs of supraspinatus muscle decreased with increasing intra-muscular fat content graded by MRI [38]. Carpenter et al. also observed significantly lower muscle SWVs in patients with GNE-related myopathy compared to the healthy control group. This indicates detecting muscle fiber loss and fat replacement through SWV quantification [53]. These studies suggest that the muscle mechanical properties, measured as SWVs by ARFI/SWE, may reflect the intra-muscular tissue composition change, such as the composition alterations among normal muscle fiber, myopathic muscle fiber, adipose tissue, and fibrosis tissue, which are characteristics of muscle disease [38], [53], [54]. Our study further established a link between altered mechanical properties, measured as SWVs, in upper limb muscle and upper limb function in DMD. The current and previous research highlights the potential of translating this technique, ARFI/SWE, to clinical applications on muscle disease, investigating muscle pathophysiology, monitoring disease progression, and evaluating treatment effects.

Limitations

There are three major methodological limitations. First, the cross-sectional study design may not adequately address the upper limb muscle stiffness change as age increases. Therefore, further longitudinal follow-up studies were mandatory. Second, our study investigated the proximal and middle-upper limb segments in patients with DMD. Since the disease progresses from proximal to distal, further investigating distal hand function may provide more information in patients with more advanced progressive stages. Third, it is important to note that the observed association between SWVs of upper limb muscles and upper limb function, as measured by PUL, in non-ambulatory DMD patients was based on a relatively small sample of only 6 non-ambulatory patients. Such a limited sample size could potentially impact the confidence in the results and generalizability of this association. To establish a more robust relationship, future studies should strive to recruit a larger number of non-ambulatory DMD patients. However, it is worth noting that our research also highlights the advantages of utilizing ARFI/SWE for characterizing muscle pathophysiology in advanced-stage DMD. The application of ARFI/SWE to quantify upper limb muscle stiffness can be precious because patients at advanced stages may face challenges in completing upper limb clinical function tests due to the skeletal and pulmonary impairments associated with disease progression of DMD.

4 Conclusion

This study, the first to apply the ARFI/SWE for quantifying the upper limb muscle stiffness and its correlation to upper limb performance in patients with DMD and TDC, reveals three significant findings. First, the SWVs of the biceps effectively distinguish patients with DMD and TDC across wide age ranges. Second, in patients with DMD, SWVs of biceps decreased as age increased at adolescence, but there was no such age effect on muscle SWVs in TDC. Third, the SWVs of upper limb muscle correlated with upper limb function in patients with DMD. This study supported the possibility of translating ARFI/SWE on quantifying upper limb muscle stiffness as a potentially promising image biomarker in DMD.
