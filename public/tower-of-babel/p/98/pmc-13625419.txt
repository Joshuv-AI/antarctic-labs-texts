Multi-scale deformable attention fusion network with global context modeling for chest X-ray lesion segmentation
Authors: Xiao Li, Xin Huang, Jie Zhan, Zhongming Liu, Yugen Yi, Yong Luo, Yongming Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chest X-ray segmentation is a critical technique for the screening and diagnosis of common thoracic diseases such as pneumonia and COVID-19, providing essential support for clinical diagnosis and quantitative analysis. However, the inherent challenges of chest X-ray images, including blurry lesion boundaries and irregular contours, pose significant difficulties for accurate segmentation. To address this problem, we propose a Multi-Scale Deformable Attention Fusion Network (MSDAFNet) for lesion segmentation in chest X-rays. MSDAFNet adaptively perceives lesion regions of varying shapes and sizes, thereby enhancing the model’s robustness to diverse pathological tissues. Specifically, we design a multi-scale deformable convolution attention module that employs channel grouping and dilated convolutions with different dilation rates to capture multi-scale feature maps without increasing parameters. Learnable offsets are introduced, allowing the convolution kernels to adaptively adjust sampling positions. Furthermore, considering the clustered distribution characteristics of small target lesion regions, a memory module is proposed before the decoder.This module establishes semantic correlations between image regions by introducing learnable key-value pairs and an attention mechanism, thereby supplementing convolutional features with the capability of modeling long-range dependencies.Extensive experiments conducted on two large-scale medical datasets, QaTa-COV19-v1 and QaTa-COV19-v2, demonstrate that MSDAFNet achieves higher accuracy and robustness in anatomical structure segmentation than existing methods, delivering competitive performance.

Introduction

In clinical practice, Chest X-ray (CXR) imaging, with its core advantages of convenient operation, low cost, and low radiation dose, has become the most widely used imaging tool for the screening and diagnosis of common thoracic diseases such as pneumonia, tuberculosis, and COVID-19 [1–3]. Accurate segmentation of key anatomical structures, including the lung parenchyma and lesion regions, in chest radiographs is a crucial step for the automated quantitative assessment of diseases. It is also a fundamental prerequisite for building reliable computer-aided diagnosis systems and supporting disease staging and treatment efficacy monitoring [4, 5]. The accuracy of this segmentation directly determines the reliability of subsequent lesion quantification, pathological analysis, and treatment response evaluation. However, the precise identification and delineation of lesion areas in chest radiographs currently rely heavily on the subjective judgment of radiologists. This approach is not only time-consuming and labor-intensive but also suffers from inconsistent interpretation, making it difficult to meet the demand for rapid diagnosis in scenarios involving large-scale screening or public health emergencies [6]. Concurrently, with the exponential growth of medical imaging data, the demand for efficient processing and precise analysis of chest radiographs is increasingly urgent. The challenge of rapidly locating lesion areas and extracting quantitative features from a large volume of CXR images has become a key obstacle to improving the diagnostic efficiency of thoracic diseases [7]. Therefore, the development of automatic anatomical segmentation technology for chest X-ray imaging, aimed at accurately extracting anatomical structures and locating lesions, is of great significance for enhancing both diagnostic efficiency and accuracy.

Therefore, fully leveraging the global semantic information in CXR images to model anatomical structural relationships is highly valuable for improving performance in real-world applications. Traditional methods for CXR segmentation have predominantly relied on classical image processing techniques such as thresholding, region growing, and active contour models [8–10]. While these methods have alleviated the manual burden to some extent, they are generally sensitive to noise and exhibit limited generalizability in complex clinical scenarios characterized by blurry tissue boundaries and overlapping anatomical structures, struggling to cope with the high complexity of contemporary clinical images [11]. In contrast, deep learning-based approaches have demonstrated superior performance in complex segmentation scenarios. Encoder-decoder architectures, represented by the U-shaped structure and its attention-enhanced or nested variants such as SwinUNet [12], AttentionUNet [13], and Unet++ [14], have achieved remarkable segmentation accuracy in medical imaging tasks due to their efficient cross-level feature fusion mechanisms [15–18]. Despite the effective progress made by existing deep learning methods in chest radiograph segmentation, achieving high segmentation accuracy in complex clinical scenarios remains challenging. Consequently, it is crucial to accurately capture multi-scale lesion features and subtle boundary details while effectively utilizing global contextual associations to suppress background interference and adapt to individual anatomical variations [19, 20]. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes a lesion-oriented Multi-Scale Deformable Attention Fusion Network (MSDAFNet) for chest X-ray lesion segmentation. MSDAFNet is designed as a local-to-global representation framework that integrates deformable multi-scale feature aggregation, boundary-aware gated fusion, and compact memory-guided context modeling within a unified encoder-decoder architecture. The main methodological contribution of this framework lies in its lesion-oriented feature coordination strategy, which aims to simultaneously enhance lesion-scale adaptability, preserve weak boundary cues, and improve semantic discrimination between lesion regions and anatomically similar background structures. Specifically, the Multi-Scale Deformable Convolutional Attention module (MSDA) is embedded in the high-semantic decoding stage to combine multi-branch dilated convolution with deformable spatial sampling. By adaptively adjusting the receptive field according to local image structures, MSDA improves the representation of lesions with variable sizes, irregular contours, and blurry boundaries. This design enables the network to capture both fine-grained boundary details and broader contextual information, ranging from small localized opacities to large diffuse lesion regions. Subsequently, a boundary-aware gated fusion strategy is introduced into the skip connections between the encoder and decoder. Instead of directly concatenating or adding cross-level features, this strategy adaptively regulates the interaction between shallow spatial details and high-level semantic information. As a result, lesion-relevant boundary cues can be selectively enhanced, while interference from ribs, vessels, and low-contrast lung parenchyma is suppressed.Finally, a compact Global Context-aware Memory Module is incorporated at the bottleneck layer to strengthen long-range semantic consistency. By using learnable key-value memory prototypes, the module establishes global contextual associations across image regions and provides complementary semantic guidance for distinguishing true lesion areas from anatomically similar background tissues. Related work

Medical image segmentation

Medical image segmentation, as a key technology in the field of medical image analysis [21–23], involves assigning accurate category labels to each pixel in an image. The most commonly used X-ray images, due to their single grayscale channel nature, often exhibit low contrast between different tissues and chaotic structural edge information. This makes the segmentation of disease structures in chest X-ray images more challenging compared to traditional medical image segmentation tasks [24, 25]. From the perspective of the development of deep learning-based medical image segmentation technologies, current methods for chest X-ray disease structure segmentation primarily follow two technical routes: methods based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and methods based on Transformers.

Traditional U-shaped network architectures

Convolutional Neural Networks, with their powerful capability for local feature extraction, high computational efficiency, and adaptability to small sample data, have become the mainstream technical route for early chest radiograph lesion segmentation. Their core advantage lies in capturing key local features such as texture and edges through convolution operations, while effectively preserving fine-grained anatomical details via skip connections. Zhou et al. proposed U-Net++ [14], which optimizes feature fusion through a nested dense connectivity structure, strengthening the association between shallow detail features and deep semantic features. Similarly, Oktay et al. introduced Attention U-Net [13], which uses attention gates in skip connections to highlight task-relevant regions and suppress irrelevant encoder responses. Recent boundary-aware studies have further shown that attention-based feature refinement is beneficial for improving boundary discrimination in medical image segmentation [23]. Subsequently, the nnU-Net network proposed by Isensee et al. [26] significantly improved the generalizability and performance stability across different medical image segmentation tasks by automatically optimizing preprocessing pipelines, network architectures, and training strategies. Furthermore, Huang et al. proposed a method based on multi-scale interaction and deformable upsampling [27] to specifically improve the segmentation accuracy of the model for small target regions.

Improved methods based on transformers

The Transformer architecture, leveraging the long-range dependency modeling capability of the self-attention mechanism, effectively compensates for the shortcomings of CNNs in capturing global contextual information. Since Chen et al. proposed TransUnet [28] in 2021, Transformer-based segmentation methods have gradually become a research hotspot in the field of medical image segmentation. In the same year, Hu et al. introduced Swin-Unet [12], which uses a Swin Transformer based on sliding windows as the encoder. This approach maintains the ability to model long-range dependencies while reducing computational complexity through local window attention. Concurrently, Hatamizadeh et al. proposed UNETR [29], which deeply integrates a Transformer encoder with a U-Net decoder. By facilitating multi-level feature interaction, it strengthens the association between local details and global semantics, demonstrating strong potential in medical image segmentation tasks.Subsequently, Zhang et al. proposed ReT-UNet [30], which integrates a recursive feature aggregation block with a Transformer-based multi-scale global feature fusion module, balancing the capture of local details and global context, thereby enhancing the accuracy and robustness of segmentation in small-sample medical images. Additionally, Yan et al. proposed MDSTransUNet [31], which integrates ShuffleNet and Transformer modules in the encoder along with a dual-decoder branch to simultaneously segment lung structures and infected regions. These hybrid CNN–Transformer methods demonstrate that combining convolutional local feature extraction with Transformer-based global dependency modeling is an effective strategy for improving medical image segmentation performance.Nevertheless, Transformer-based and hybrid CNN–Transformer methods still face several challenges in chest radiograph lesion segmentation. First, although self-attention is effective for global semantic interaction, it usually introduces additional computational cost, especially when high-resolution feature maps are involved. Second, Transformer-based models often require sufficient training samples to fully exploit their global modeling ability, whereas pixel-level annotated chest X-ray lesion datasets are still relatively limited. Third, chest X-ray lesions are frequently characterized by low contrast, blurry boundaries, irregular morphology, and interference from ribs, vessels, and lung parenchyma. Under such conditions, global dependency modeling alone may not be sufficient to preserve weak lesion boundaries and local geometric details.Therefore, chest X-ray lesion segmentation requires a balanced representation strategy that can jointly capture global contextual relationships, local boundary details, and morphology-adaptive spatial features.

Multi-scale deformable convolution

In standard convolution kernels, the receptive field is typically a fixed rectangular region, which limits their sensitivity to geometric morphological features in CXR images [32]. To address this limitation, many studies have designed various variants of convolution operations to improve the adaptability of segmentation networks to morphological variations of target structures. Dilated convolution [33], as a classic method, expands the receptive field of the convolution kernel by setting different dilation rates, effectively enhancing the model’s ability to aggregate contextual information from targets of varying sizes. ASPP-like multi-scale context modules further extend this idea by combining several parallel dilated convolution branches to capture contextual information at different receptive-field scales. However, these methods mainly rely on predefined dilation rates, and their sampling positions remain fixed once the dilation patterns are determined. Therefore, although they can enlarge the contextual perception range, they still struggle to flexibly adapt to the complex contours and weak boundary transitions of irregular lesions in chest X-ray images. In contrast, deformable convolution dynamically learns offsets for each position in the feature map, allowing the receptive field to adaptively adjust to local image structures. This improves the adaptability of convolution kernels to complex lesion morphology. Based on this concept, Yang et al. proposed the DCU-Net model [34], which combines deformable convolution with a cascaded U-Net architecture. By flexibly adjusting the sampling positions of the convolution kernel, it better adapts to structures with complex shapes and multi-scale features. Meanwhile, Qi et al. proposed Snake Convolution, which differs from deformable convolution by introducing continuity constraints to optimize the receptive field adaptation process, effectively addressing the issue of receptive field deviation from target regions in the segmentation of long, curved tubular structures.Although existing methods have made considerable progress, they still face challenges in capturing features at the boundaries of blurry tissue structures. For instance, lesion tissues in CXR images often exhibit inherent characteristics such as a significant scale span from tiny nodules to diffuse opacities, and irregular, blurry boundaries [35]. Current methods may be limited when scale-aware contextual perception and morphology-adaptive spatial modeling are required simultaneously. Motivated by this requirement, the multi-scale deformable convolution module in this work integrates a multi-dilation-rate parallel convolution framework with deformable convolution, thereby combining the contextual perception capability of multi-scale dilated convolution with the adaptive sampling capability of deformable convolution. This design aims to improve the representation of CXR lesion tissues with scale diversity and morphological irregularity.

Method

Overall architecture

The MSDAFNet framework is based on the classic encoder-decoder architecture. Its design core lies in constructing a synergistic processing pipeline that encompasses global context enhancement, optimized feature fusion, and multi-scale detail refinement. The encoder module extracts multi-scale feature representations from the input chest X-ray image through progressive down-sampling. After encoding, the extracted features are progressively passed to the Memory Module. This module captures long-range dependencies, enabling effective modeling of global contextual features. The generated feature map is then directly used as the bottleneck layer input for the decoder. To promote cross-level information fusion and dynamically select and weight multi-scale features from the encoder, a gated attention mechanism is introduced in the skip connection stage. The decoder gradually restores spatial resolution through multi-stage up-sampling. A Multi-scale Deformable Convolution Attention Module is embedded at key stages. This module adaptively fits target morphology through deformable convolution and enhances local detail representation via a parallel multi-branch structure and attention mechanisms. Finally, the network outputs a pixel-level probability distribution map, achieving high-precision medical image segmentation. Encoder

A pre-trained mobileNetV3-large [36] backbone is employed as the encoder to leverage its efficient feature extraction capability. The input chest radiograph is transformed into a hierarchical multi-scale feature pyramid. Specifically, the encoder extracts features sequentially from five key stages, progressively reducing the spatial resolution to 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, and 1/32 of the input. In this feature hierarchy, the shallow features retain higher spatial resolution, focusing on capturing detailed structures such as edges and textures. The deeper features exhibit a significantly higher level of semantic abstraction, making them more suitable for carrying global contextual information. This representation ranging from fine-grained to coarse-grained provides a structured, multi-level information foundation for subsequent decoding and fusion processes.

Memory module

In traditional neural networks, expanding the receptive field often relies on image down-sampling strategies [37]. By progressively reducing the feature map size through pooling operations or strided convolutions, each neuron can cover a larger region in the input image. However, this approach leads to the loss of spatial details, localization ambiguity, and an inability to effectively model long-range dependencies [38]. To address this, a global context-aware Memory Module is introduced at the bottleneck layer following the encoder. A global context dictionary is constructed to supplement high-level convolutional features with global semantic information. Specifically, high-level features are passed through a 1×1 convolution to generate query keys. These keys are then matched for similarity with memory keys, acting as a global context enhancer. This design aims to address the difficulty in modeling long-range dependencies caused by scattered anatomical structures and significant variations in lesion scales within chest X-ray images. The module builds a learnable global memory bank to store a set of global prototype features, enabling feature association and information aggregation across spatial positions. Decoder

The decoder adopts a U-shaped hierarchical structure symmetrical to the encoder, consisting of five consecutive decoding blocks. Through four progressive up-sampling operations, the spatial resolution of the feature maps is gradually restored, ultimately generating a segmentation prediction map with the same dimensions as the input image. At each decoding stage, the deep semantic features from the previous layer are first up-sampled via bilinear interpolation to match the spatial dimensions of the corresponding feature map transmitted from the encoder via a skip connection.Subsequently, the two feature streams are fed into a boundary-aware gated fusion module for feature calibration. In this module, the up-sampled decoder feature provides high-level semantic guidance, while the encoder skip feature provides spatial details and boundary cues. The gating map is generated from the aligned encoder-decoder features and is used to calibrate the skip features before concatenation. This design aims to reduce background interference while preserving lesion-related weak boundary information during cross-level fusion. The structure of the gated fusion module is illustrated below:

This helps preserve weak lesion boundaries while reducing irrelevant background activations.

Finally, the calibrated features are concatenated with the up-sampled decoder features along the channel dimension, followed by a set of standard convolutional layers for local fusion and smoothing. By repeating this process level by level, the spatial resolution of the feature maps is progressively restored, and their semantic representation and detailed information are gradually enriched.

Multi-scale deformable convolutional attention module

To enhance the representation of CXR lesions with scale variation, irregular morphology, and ambiguous boundaries, a Multi-Scale Deformable Convolutional Attention module is embedded in the high-level decoding stage of the network. In chest X-ray lesion segmentation, lesion regions may appear as small localized opacities or large diffuse abnormalities, and their boundaries are often affected by low contrast, anatomical overlap, and background interference. Therefore, the decoder requires not only multi-scale contextual perception, but also the ability to adapt its sampling pattern to local lesion morphology.

To capture contextual information at different spatial ranges, MSDA first adopts parallel convolutional branches with multiple dilation rates. Unlike conventional ASPP-like modules, which mainly enlarge the receptive field through fixed atrous convolutions, the proposed MSDA module further considers the spatial alignment between sampling locations and lesion morphology. Although fixed dilation patterns can provide scale-diverse contextual representations, their sampling positions remain regular and input-independent once the dilation rates are determined. For CXR lesions with irregular contours, weak boundary transitions, or discontinuous opacity distributions, such fixed sampling patterns may introduce irrelevant background responses or fail to sufficiently cover lesion-related boundary cues.

To address this limitation, MSDA introduces a deformable sampling path after multi-scale feature aggregation. Learnable offsets are predicted from local features and used to adjust the sampling locations of convolutional kernels. In this way, the receptive field is not only expanded by the multi-branch structure, but also adaptively adjusted according to the spatial distribution of lesion-related responses. This structure-adaptive sampling mechanism is beneficial for capturing small lesions, irregular contours, and blurry boundaries, especially in regions affected by ribs, vessels, or low-contrast lung parenchyma.

After deformable feature extraction, spatial attention and channel attention are further introduced to recalibrate the aggregated features. The spatial attention branch emphasizes lesion-relevant positions and suppresses redundant background responses, while the channel attention branch adaptively reweights different feature channels after multi-scale fusion. Through this coordinated design, the MSDA module provides a boundary-sensitive multi-scale representation for CXR lesion segmentation and enhances the model’s ability to capture lesions with variable sizes, irregular shapes, and ambiguous boundaries. Dynamic spatial adaptation

Offset Prediction: The offset prediction network is composed of two convolutional layers. First, a 1× 1 convolution reduces the channel dimension of multi-scale features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{ms}$$\end{document} to C//8. Subsequently, a 3× 3 convolution outputs offsets Δ P with dimensions [2–4], corresponding to the 2D (x, y) offsets for each position in the 3× 3 sampling grid of four grouped convolution kernels: 5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\textrm{mid}} = \textrm{ReLU} (\textrm{BN} (\textrm{Conv}_{3 \times 3}(x)))$$\end{document}6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \Delta P = \mathrm{Conv}_{3 \times 3}(F_{\mathrm{mid}})$$\end{document}

Specifically, the offset Δ P assigns a pair of coordinate offsets \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\Delta x_{i,j}, \Delta y_{i,j})$$\end{document} to each spatial position (i,j) of the feature map. This adjustment shifts sampling points from regular grid positions (x,y) to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x+\Delta x_{i,j}, y+\Delta y_{i,j})$$\end{document}, guiding sampling points to move toward the edges and core regions of target structures. As a result, convolutional output features are more focused on meaningful target regions, and interference from irrelevant background information is suppressed.

Deformable Convolution and Spatial Weight Calculation: Traditional convolution adopts a fixed grid-based sampling pattern, where sampling positions strictly follow preset rules and are independent of input feature content. This makes it difficult to adapt to irregular lesion contours and blurry boundary characteristics in chest X-ray images. Although convolution kernels with fixed dilation rates expand the receptive field through preset parameters, they are still limited by these parameters and cannot dynamically respond to structural differences in different regions, leading to poor adaptability to lesions with variable shapes. As shown in Fig. 5, compared with the two traditional convolution methods mentioned above, deformable convolution breaks the inherent constraints of fixed sampling grids by introducing predicted offsets Δ P. It can adaptively adjust the spatial sampling positions and distribution of convolution kernels based on local structural information of the input feature map, thus accurately focusing on irregular target regions [40].

Deformable convolution employs a grouping strategy and adjusts sampling positions based on offsets Δ P to achieve adaptive fitting of irregular lesion structures. After obtaining the offsets Δ P from the previous step, deformable convolution is applied to the feature map X to generate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{deform}$$\end{document}, with the specific formula as follows: 7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\mathrm{def}}(p,c) = \sum_{q \in \mathcal{N}(p)} W_q \cdot X\left( p + q + \Delta p_q, c \right)$$\end{document}

where K=3 represents the size of the convolution kernel, W(k,l,c) denotes the weight parameter of the convolution kernel in the c-th channel, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{deform}$$\end{document} is the feature map output by deformable convolution. Each channel of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{deform}$$\end{document} encodes sampled local structural information.

Since the features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{deform}$$\end{document} output by deformable convolution remain multi-channel features, channel aggregation is required to extract global responses in the spatial dimension, enabling the conversion from multi-channel features to single-channel attention weights. Global average pooling is performed on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{deform}$$\end{document} across channels to integrate multi-channel target responses, generating a single-channel spatial feature map. Finally, normalization to the range [0,1] is achieved through Sigmoid activation, resulting in spatial attention weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_s$$\end{document}: 8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_s = \operatorname{sigmoid}\left( \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^C \mathrm{deformconv}(X;\Delta P)_c \right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\operatorname{sigmoid}$$\end{document} denotes the Sigmoid activation function, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{deformconv}(X;\Delta P)_c$$\end{document} represents the output of the c-th channel of deformable convolution on the input feature map X modulated by offsets Δ P.

Attention mechanism and feature fusion

After obtaining spatial attention weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{s}$$\end{document}, the module achieves multi-level information integration through systematic feature fusion. First, element-wise multiplication is performed between spatial attention weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_{s}$$\end{document} and the original input features X to realize adaptive weighting in the spatial dimension: 9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\mathrm{spatial}}=A_{s}\otimes X$$\end{document}

Subsequently, spatially enhanced features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\mathrm{spatial}}$$\end{document} are concatenated with multi-scale features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{ms}$$\end{document} along the channel dimension to fuse local details and global contextual information, yielding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\mathrm{concat}}\in R^{2C\times H\times W}$$\end{document}. In this process, a channel attention mechanism is introduced to recalibrate the concatenated features across the channel dimension for further optimization. Specifically, global average pooling (GAP) is first applied to the input feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\mathrm{spatial}}$$\end{document} to generate a channel statistical descriptor vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z\in R^{2C}$$\end{document}: 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}z &= \operatorname{GAP}\left(F_{\mathrm{spatial}}\right) \\z_{c} &= \frac{1}{H\times W}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W} F_{\mathrm{spatial,c}}(i, j)\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

where each element \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z_{c}$$\end{document} in vector z represents the global response intensity of the c-th feature channel, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\mathrm{concat,c}}$$\end{document} denotes the value of feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\mathrm{concat}}$$\end{document} at the c-th channel and spatial position (i, j). The vector z is then fed into a fully connected network to generate channel attention weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_c$$\end{document}: 11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_c = \sigma\left(W_2\cdot\delta\left(W_1\cdot z\right)\right)$$\end{document}

The obtained channel attention weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A_c\in R^{2C\times 1\times 1}$$\end{document} are multiplied with the concatenated features to achieve channel-selective feature enhancement. Finally, a 3× 3 convolutional layer is used to perform the final transformation on the enhanced features, outputting the refined feature map Y: 12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y = \mathrm{Conv2d}_{3\times 3}\left(A_c\otimes F_{\mathrm{concat}}\right)$$\end{document}

This fusion strategy ensures that multi-scale deformable convolution can simultaneously utilize the precise localization capability of spatial attention mechanisms, the contextual perception capability of multi-scale features, and the feature selection capability of channel attention mechanisms, significantly improving the representation accuracy of the model for complex structures in chest X-ray images.

Output layer

In the output layer, the high-dimensional feature map output from the decoder stage is compressed along the channel dimension by a 1×1 convolutional layer. The number of feature channels is directly mapped to the number of target classes, enabling a linear transformation from the feature space to the classification space. Subsequently, a Sigmoid activation function is applied to perform non-linear normalization on the output value of each pixel, converting it into a probability value within the range of [0,1], which represents the confidence level of each pixel belonging to the target region. Finally, bilinear interpolation is used to upsample the probability map to the original input resolution, ensuring spatial alignment between the output segmentation map and the input image. Throughout the output process, computational efficiency is guaranteed, while the multi-scale feature information extracted by the front-end network is completely preserved. A binary segmentation mask corresponding to each pixel of the input image is generated as the final output.

Loss function

In chest X-ray (CXR) lesion segmentation tasks, lesion regions typically account for only a small portion of the entire image. This severe class imbalance impairs the performance of traditional pixel-level loss functions. To address this issue, a weighted combination of Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) loss and Dice loss is adopted as the total training loss function for the network: 13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{BCE} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} [y_i \log(\hat{y}_i) + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - \hat{y}_i)]$$\end{document}

where N denotes the total number of pixels, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_i \in \{0, 1\}$$\end{document} represents the ground-truth label of the i-th pixel, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{y}_i \in [0, 1]$$\end{document} denotes the probability predicted by the model that the i-th pixel belongs to the lesion region. 14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{Dice} = 1 - \frac{2 \sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i \hat{y}_i + \epsilon}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \hat{y}_i + \epsilon}$$\end{document}

where ε is a smoothing factor, which is used to prevent the denominator from being zero and enhance the stability of numerical calculations. 15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{Total} = \lambda_1 L_{BCE} + \lambda_2 L_{Dice}$$\end{document}

Experiment

Datasets

In this study, public chest X-ray image datasets jointly provided by Qatar University and Tampere University [41, 42] (QaTa-COV19-v1, QaTa-COV19-v2) were selected as the benchmark for experimental validation of all methods. The QaTa-COV19-v1 dataset contains 2951 posteroanterior (PA) chest X-ray images of laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 positive patients and their corresponding segmentation masks. As an extended version, the QaTa-COV19-v2 dataset includes 9258 chest X-ray images of COVID-19 positive patients and their corresponding segmentation masks. To ensure the fairness and statistical significance of model performance evaluation, the dataset was divided into training, testing, and validation sets at a ratio of 7:2:1.

Experimental setup

The training and evaluation of the MSDAF network were conducted on a Linux operating system, implemented using the PyTorch framework with a software environment consisting of CUDA 11.8, PyTorch 2.0.0, and Python 3.8. The hardware configuration for training included a 12 vCPU Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8352 V CPU @ 2.10 GHz and a 4090D graphics card. The Adam optimizer was uniformly adopted during the model training phase, with an initial learning rate set to 1e-3 and a weight decay coefficient configured as 1e-4. Weight decay was used to suppress overfitting of the model. Additionally, a gradient clipping mechanism was introduced to avoid gradient explosion during training, with the maximum gradient norm limited to 5.0.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the model, multiple dimensions including pixel-level classification accuracy, regional overlap, and boundary fitting degree were considered. For this purpose, five widely recognized evaluation metrics in academia were selected, with their definitions and calculation formulas presented as follows:

Dice Similarity Coefficient [43]: The Dice coefficient is suitable for evaluating segmentation performance in class-imbalanced scenarios. It calculates the ratio of twice the intersection between the model-predicted segmentation region and the ground-truth annotated region to the sum of the pixel counts of the two regions. 16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Dice = \frac{2 \times |X \cap Y|}{|X| + |Y|} = \frac{2 \times TP}{2 \times TP + FP + FN}$$\end{document}

where TP (True Positive) denotes the number of pixels correctly predicted as infected regions, FP (False Positive) denotes the number of pixels incorrectly predicted as infected regions from the background, FN (False Negative) denotes the number of pixels incorrectly predicted as background from infected regions, and TN (True Negative) denotes the number of pixels correctly predicted as background.

Intersection over Union (IoU) [44]: It calculates the ratio of the intersection to the union of the predicted region and the ground-truth region. 17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IoU = \frac{|X \cap Y|}{|X \cup Y|} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP + FN}$$\end{document}

Precision: It measures the proportion of pixels truly positive among all pixels predicted as positive by the model. 18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Precision = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}$$\end{document}

Recall: It measures the proportion of truly positive pixels correctly predicted by the model among all truly positive pixels. 19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Recall = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}$$\end{document}

Accuracy: It calculates the proportion of correctly classified pixels among all pixels. 20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Accuracy = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}$$\end{document}

Baseline

To comprehensively validate the segmentation performance of MSDAFNet, a baseline model is constructed using MobileNetV3-large as the backbone within a basic U-Net architecture. This baseline model retains only the classic encoder-decoder structure and basic cross-level feature concatenation.

For comparison, fourteen representative segmentation networks in the medical image segmentation domain are selected as baseline models. These models cover different technical routes. Specifically, the classical CNN-based segmentation models include FCN [38], SegNet [45], UNet [15], UNet++ [14], HRNet [46], PAN [47], DeepLabV3+ [48], and PSPNet [49]. In addition, a Transformer-related lightweight hybrid segmentation network, MobileViT [50], is included to evaluate the performance of the proposed method against Transformer-based feature modeling. Furthermore, recent efficient segmentation networks involving lightweight convolutional design, attention-based decoding, and Mamba-style visual representation are also compared, including MKUNet [51], EMCADNet [52], MCADSDecoder [53], LVUNet [54], and VMUNet [55]. Among them, VMUNet is included as a representative Vision Mamba-based segmentation model, with Vision Mamba providing an efficient state-space visual representation paradigm [56].

To ensure a fair comparison, all models are trained and tested under identical experimental settings on four datasets: QaTa-COV19-v1, QaTa-COV19-v2, M-QaTa-COV19-v1, and M-QaTa-COV19-v2.

As shown in Table 6, the baseline model achieves a Dice of 0.8272 and an IoU of 0.7210. After introducing a single component, the segmentation performance improves to different degrees. The model with only the Memory Module obtains a Dice of 0.8345, indicating that global contextual guidance is beneficial for improving lesion-region discrimination. The model with only the gated skip fusion strategy achieves a Dice of 0.8329, suggesting that cross-level feature calibration contributes to the preservation of lesion-related spatial details. The model with only the MSDA module reaches a Dice of 0.8368 and an IoU of 0.7287, showing that multi-scale deformable feature aggregation is helpful for representing lesions with scale variation and irregular contours.

When two modules are combined, the performance is further improved in most cases. The combination of the Memory Module and gated skip fusion achieves a Dice of 0.8453 and a Recall of 0.8609, indicating that global semantic guidance and boundary-aware feature calibration can jointly enhance lesion recall. The combination of the Memory Module and MSDA obtains a Dice of 0.8386, while the combination of gated skip fusion and MSDA reaches a Dice of 0.8475 and an IoU of 0.7354. These results suggest that MSDA and gated skip fusion are complementary in improving boundary-sensitive feature representation and cross-level feature selection.

The complete MSDAFNet, which integrates all three components, achieves the highest Dice, IoU, Precision, Recall, and F1-score among the evaluated variants, with values of 0.8544, 0.7459, 0.8443, 0.8648, and 0.8569, respectively. Although the Accuracy values of different variants remain close due to the large proportion of background pixels in CXR segmentation, the complete model shows the most balanced performance in terms of region overlap and lesion recall.

Analysis on the Qdata-COVID19 dataset

To intuitively present the performance differences of various segmentation methods, eight representative images are selected from the dataset. These images cover typical clinical scenarios with multiple common lesion types, as shown in Fig. 9. Consistent with trends shown by quantitative experimental results, other networks generally suffer from missed lesion detection and mis-segmentation of edge regions in complex scenes. Effective segmentation is usually achieved only when the contrast between lesions and the background is high and structural interference is low. As shown in Case 1 of Fig. 10, most models can identify the general lesion region when the lesion area is large and occlusion interference is limited. Few false detections are observed in other background regions. However, in Case 2 of Fig. 10, even with a large lesion area, models that performed well in Case 1, such as MOENet and MKUNet, show large-area missed detection under indistinct edge transitions and severe internal noise. In contrast, the MSDAFNet establishes better global context relations through the memory module. The adaptability of the model to individual differences in anatomical structures is enhanced. Better robustness is achieved in distinguishing targets from background noise. Similarly, in Cases 3 and 4 of Fig. 10, traditional methods often misclassify mediastinal tissues and vascular shadows as lesions, or produce rough segmentation contours due to insufficient edge refinement. With its discriminative feature extraction scheme and more flexible convolution structure, MSDAFNet effectively covers the scale range from tiny nodules to diffuse lesions. Accurate fitting of blurred boundaries is realized when facing irregular lesions and indistinct borders between lesions and normal lung tissues. In addition, traditional methods are prone to large-area false detection in fine small-lesion segmentation tasks. This issue is caused by minimal gray differences in surrounding lung parenchyma and significant background interference from fine blood vessels. In Case 5 of Fig. 10, most models except DeeplabV3+ and MOENet show false detections in the left noise region for small-lesion segmentation in low-quality chest X-ray images. In comparison, MSDAFNet adjusts sampling positions through deformable convolution to fit occluded lesion edges. The segmented small white lesions are complete with clear boundaries, free of large-area false-positive segmentation caused by overlapping structures. In Case 7 of Fig. 10, feature signals of small lesions are further weakened by noise, artifacts, and uneven image contrast. Most models misclassify tissue interference in the left lower region as lesions due to the inability to distinguish noise from weak lesion features. Discussion

The experiments are mainly conducted on QaTa-COV19-v1, QaTa-COV19-v2, and their derived small-lesion subsets. These datasets provide useful pixel-level annotations for evaluating chest X-ray lesion segmentation, but they are still derived from the same dataset series. In practical clinical scenarios, chest X-ray images may vary across institutions due to differences in imaging protocols, equipment settings, patient populations, disease categories, image quality, and annotation criteria. Such variations may influence the transferability of segmentation models across different data sources.

Therefore, external validation on additional chest X-ray segmentation datasets with dense pixel-level annotations and multi-center clinical data remains an important direction for future work. In addition, future studies may incorporate boundary-sensitive metrics, such as HD95, ASSD, or Boundary F1, to more directly evaluate boundary delineation quality. More fine-grained analyses of small lesions, low-contrast lesions, and cases with severe anatomical interference would also be valuable for further assessing the robustness and clinical applicability of the proposed method.

Conclusion

To realize the accurate segmentation of pathological structures in chest X-ray images, this study proposes a multi-scale deformable convolutional attention fusion segmentation network, MSDAFNet. A complete segmentation processing pipeline is built on the network architecture, which synergistically integrates three core modules: the multi-scale deformable convolutional attention module, the gated attention mechanism and the global context-aware memory module. This integrated architecture innovatively tackles the key challenges in chest X-ray segmentation, including the structural complexity and blurry boundaries of lesion regions. Extensive experiments are conducted on two public datasets, QaTa-COV19-v1 and QaTa-COV19-v2, to verify the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed network. The network achieves Dice coefficients of 0.8544 and 0.8755 on the two datasets respectively, with Intersection over Union values of 0.7459 and 0.7785. These results demonstrate the competitive performance of MSDAFNet on the corresponding segmentation tasks, providing a promising solution for accurate chest X-ray pathological structure segmentation.
