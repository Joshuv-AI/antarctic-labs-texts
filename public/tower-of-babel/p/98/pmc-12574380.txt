Prevalence and correlates of hypertension among women of reproductive age in Tanzania: a nationwide cross-sectional study
Authors: Pankras Luoga, Atuganile Mtawa, Jovinary Adam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHypertension is a major public health problem and important area of research due to its high prevalence and being major risk factor for developing cardiovascular diseases.ObjectiveTo assess the prevalence and correlates of hypertension among women of reproductive age (15–49 years) in Tanzania.MethodsThis study used secondary data of a total of 7610 weighted women of reproductive age (15–49 years) drawn from the 2022 Demographic and Health Survey-Malaria Indicator Survey. The application of a standardised weight helped to account for complex sampling design. A dependent variable was a binary variable hypertension status and independent variables were the sociodemographic characteristics of a woman. The descriptive and inferential statistics were performed using Stata V.18 software to determine the distribution of participants among variable categories and determine the association between dependent and independent variables. A cut point of p value <0.05 determines the significant factor.ResultsThe overall prevalence of hypertension among women of reproductive age in Tanzania was 11.2%. After controlling for other correlates, the correlates of hypertension aged 35–49 years had higher odds of developing hypertension (adjusted OR (aOR) 4.12, 95% CI 2.86 to 5.94) compared with women aged 15–24 years. Women residing in urban areas had higher odds (aOR 2.40, 95% CI 1.09 to 5.27) of developing hypertension than women residing in rural areas. Moreover, the odds of being hypertensive were higher (aOR 1.58, 95% CI 1.17 to 2.15) for women in the rich quintile compared with their counterparts in middle and poor quintiles. Women who were separated had higher odds of having hypertension (aOR 1.56, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.36) than those who are married. Moreover, women who are obese or overweight (aOR 2.07, 95% CI 1.70 to 2.51), women who were using alcohol (aOR 1.50, 95% CI 1.11 to 2.03) and women who were smoking (aOR 2.30, 95% CI 1.40 to 6.60) had higher odds of developing hypertension.ConclusionThe overall prevalence of hypertension among Tanzanian women of reproductive age in Tanzania was 11.2%. The correlates of hypertension among women include older age, urban residency, being separated, coming from the richest households, drinking alcohol and smoking tobacco. The government and other health stakeholders should focus on designing health programmes to deal with modifiable correlates like reducing alcohol use and smoking of tobacco specifically among women as well as in the general population.

Background

Worldwide hypertension is one of the leading causes of death and disability-adjusted life years.1 2 It is the most common risk factor for cardiovascular disease and leading cause of mortality globally.3 It is an important risk factor for advanced cardiovascular disease in women such as stroke, myocardial infarction and hypertensive heart diseases, but it is often underestimated and underdiagnosed.4 This includes an increase in the number of hypertension cases in women of childbearing age, who normally have protective hormones against cardiovascular disease.5 It is a silent killer, as very rarely any symptom can be seen in its early stages until a severe medical crisis takes place, like a heart attack, stroke or chronic kidney disease.6 Hypertension is a major public health problem due to its high prevalence all around the globe. It has been reported to be a major health threat in the world, particularly in Africa and other lower-and-middle-income countries (LMICs).

In Africa, the estimated number of people with hypertension has increased by 41% in the year 2000.7 It is projected to rise by 36% in the year 2030.8 9 Hypertension is widespread in sub-Saharan Africa, with Tanzania and Kenya experiencing the highest prevalence.1012

Although the majority of patients with hypertension remain asymptomatic, some people with hypertension report headaches, lightheadedness, vertigo, altered vision or fainting episodes.13 There are several factors predisposing to hypertension which vary from country to country and even there is a difference between urban and rural regions of the same place.10 Established risk factors for hypertension include modifiable (unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, tobacco and alcohol consumption and overweight and obesity) and non-modifiable (age, coexisting morbidities, such as diabetes and kidney disease) risk factors.14 15

Evidence of hypertension in women can cause a range of complications that may greatly affect their life.14 16 Many epidemiological studies have established the risk factors for hypertension. These risk factors included age, gender, weight, body mass index, waist circumference, sedentary lifestyle, smoking and alcohol intake.17 However, most studies conducted in this region were not targeted at women of childbearing age, and few studies targeting this group of women covered small areas and did not use the recent and appropriate tool to assess it.18 19 Therefore, we aimed to investigate factors associated with hypertension in women of childbearing age using the most recent Tanzanian Demographic and Health Survey (TDHS). The study findings will inform policymakers about the underlying causes of the increased burden of hypertension among this segment of the population, which is at a crucial reproduction age.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in the United Republic of Tanzania in urban and rural areas. It is located in eastern Africa. It is bordered by Kenya and Uganda to the North; Rwanda, Burundi and the Democratic Republic of the Congo to the West; and Zambia, Malawi and Mozambique to the South. The country’s eastern border lies in the Indian Ocean, which has a coastline of 1424 km. Tanzania has a total area of 945 087 sq km, including 61 000 sq km of inland water. The data were collected from 31 geographical regions of the Tanzania mainland and Zanzibar (26 regions of the Tanzania mainland and 5 regions from Zanzibar).20

Data source

Data were extracted from the 2022 TDHS, which was conducted jointly by the Inner City Fund (ICF), Office of the Chief Government Statistician, Ministry of Health (Tanzania Mainland and Zanzibar) and National Bureau of Statistics. The survey collected information about hypertension from all women of reproductive age. The 2022 TDHS survey collected data from a nationally representative probability sample of households, women of reproductive age and men in the sampled households.

Study design and sampling procedure

The sample design for the 2022 TDHS-Malaria Indicator Survey (MIS) was intended to provide estimates for the entire country, for urban and rural areas in Tanzania Mainland and for Zanzibar. The 2022 TDHS-MIS followed a stratified two-stage sample design. The first stage involved selection of sampling points (clusters) consisting of enumeration areas (EAs) delineated for the 2012 Tanzania Population and Housing Census. The EAs were selected with a probability proportional to their size within each sampling stratum. A total of 629 clusters were selected. Among the 629 EAs, 211 were from urban areas and 418 were from rural areas. In the second stage, 26 households were selected systematically from each cluster, for a total anticipated sample size of 16 354 households for the 2022 TDHS-MIS. Of the 16 312 households selected, 15 907 were found to be occupied. A household listing operation was carried out in all the selected EAs before the main survey. All women aged 15–49 who were either usual residents or visitors in the household on the night before the survey interview were included in the 2022 TDHS-MIS and were eligible to be interviewed.21 The unity of analysis for this study was a woman.

Sample size

This study used data from the female individual recode file. This file contains data from 15 154 women of reproductive age (15–49 years). Only 7610 women responded to the hypertension question.

Study variables

Outcome variable

During the survey, respondent women were asked whether they had ever been told by a doctor or other health worker that they have high blood pressure or hypertension prior to the survey, which was measured using the following questions: ‘In the past 12 months, have you been told by a doctor or other health worker that you have high blood pressure or hypertension?’ In addition to asking questions about the history of blood pressure measurement and use of medication to control blood pressure, the 2022 TDHS-MIS also offered respondents the opportunity to have their blood pressure measured as part of the survey. Respondents whose blood pressure fell into two different categories based on their average systolic and average diastolic levels were classified according to the highest blood pressure category in which they fell on either of the two measures. Respondents were classified as having hypertension if they had a systolic blood pressure level of 140 mm Hg or above or a diastolic blood pressure level of 90 mm Hg or above at the time of the survey or were currently taking antihypertensive medication to control their blood pressure. The binary response of hypertension (yes/no) was used as our dependent variable, as was done by previous researchers using DHS data.

Exposure variable

In this study, the exposure variable is considered an independent variable, such as age group, place of residence, education level, wealth index, health insurance coverage, media exposure and distance to health facility. All these exposure variables were selected based on previous studies.2227 These variables were further categorised into covariates such as age group (15–24, 25–34 years and 35–49 years), place of residence (urban, rural), education level (no education, primary education, secondary education and above), wealth status (poor, middle and rich), marital status (never in union, married and separated), employment status (employed and unemployed), obesity or overweight (yes, no), alcohol consumption (yes, no), smoking status (yes, no), parity (≤2, ≥3) and HIV status (negative and positive). All these variables were used to determine the associations between exposure variables and hypertension among women of reproductive age in Tanzania.

Statistical data analysis

Data management and analysis were performed using Stata V.18 statistical software (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). The data were weighted using the sample weights (v005/1 000 000) as recommended in the DHS data analysis. Moreover, the ‘svy’ command in Stata was used to assign the sample weights and to adjust for clustering effects and sample stratification. The data were extracted, recoded and cleaned, and the descriptive results are presented as frequencies and percentages. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were used to assess multicollinearity, and all VIF values were below 10, with the highest being 2.9, indicating no significant multicollinearity. Model fit was evaluated using the likelihood ratio (LR) test, which compared the final model to the intercept-only model. The LR test indicated that the fitted model provided a significantly better fit than the null model (p<0.001). Calibration of the final model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p=0.2907). The χ² test was used to examine the association between the outcome variable and independent variables. Predictors with a p value <0.2 in the bivariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable weighted logistic regression model. Variables with p values less than 0.05 were considered to be independently significantly associated with hypertension. The strength of the association was assessed using the adjusted OR (aOR) along with its corresponding 95% CI.

Most of the participants resided in rural areas (65%), and nearly half (47.4%) had their wealth categorised in the rich quintile. Most had primary education (53.6%). Discussion

The study aimed at determining the prevalence and correlates of hypertension among women of reproductive age 15–49 years in Tanzania. The analysis shows that the overall prevalence of hypertension among women of reproductive age in Tanzania was 11.2%. Although the overall rate of hypertension among women and men aged 15–49 in Tanzania seems relatively low, hypertension is a serious health problem. Many Tanzanian women may suffer from hypertension but do not know it; hypertension is often termed the ‘silent killer’ because of the lack of warning signs or symptoms.21 The prevalence of hypertension in Tanzania among women is higher compared with Kenya28 and Zimbabwe,29 but is lower compared with Ghana30 and Gambia.31 The possible explanation for this variation might be differences in geographical location, living conditions and the cultural practices of the populations studied.

The study found that the Eastern zone has the higher prevalence of hypertension in Tanzania. This elevated prevalence may be attributed to specific regions within the zone, such as Pwani (19.2%) and Dar es Salaam (16.8%).21 Additionally, the availability of health facilities in these regions facilitates screening and diagnosis, resulting in a higher reported prevalence. In contrast, in other zones where access to health services remains a challenge, it is difficult to determine the status of individuals with hypertension. Previous studies have identified several major risk factors for higher hypertension prevalence, including inadequate physical activity, higher stress levels and unhealthy diets.32 33 The study findings suggest establishing community fitness programmes as well as public awareness campaigns to educate the community about healthy lifestyle choices. The study found that age, marital status, wealth status, smoking, alcohol consumption and overweight or obesity were significantly associated with hypertension among women of reproductive age in Tanzania.

The study found that women who were smoking had higher odds of having hypertension than people who have never smoked. The 2018 Tanzania Global Adult Tobacco Survey (GATS) found that 3.2% of women used tobacco (smoking and/or smokeless tobacco) (GAT, 2018). Studies conducted in Tanzania found that smoking cigarettes exposes the body to nicotine and other harmful chemicals.34 Nicotine in cigarette smoke leads to an increase in a person who smoke’s blood pressure because it triggers the release of epinephrine and norepinephrine hormones.2 35 Smoking also damages the blood vessels, making them thicker and narrower. This makes it harder for blood to flow, and also increases blood pressure and heart rate (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020). The study findings suggest strongly encouragement and provide support for women to quit smoking. This includes access to smoking cessation programmes, nicotine replacement therapy, counselling and provision of information regarding the health risks associated with tobacco use among women of reproductive age. The study findings are in line with previous studies conducted in South Kashmir and Brazil that reported people who smoke are more likely to develop severe forms of hypertension, including malignant and renovascular hypertension.36 37

The study found women aged 35–49 years had higher odds of developing hypertension compared with women aged 15–24 years. According to WHO, an estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30 years and above worldwide have hypertension, most (two-thirds) living in LMICs (WHO, 2021). Women in this age group may face various stressors, including work-related pressures, family responsibilities and societal expectations, which contribute to hypertension. The study done in Ngorongoro, Tanzania found that older age is characterised by physiological changes in the cardiovascular system, including arteriosclerotic structural alterations and calcification, leading to large artery stiffness and blood vessels losing elasticity with advancing age.38 Furthermore, the study conducted in the Monduli district underscores that age is a common non-modifiable risk factor for many non-communicable diseases; an increase in age raises the risk of hypertension.39 The study findings suggest undergoing regular blood pressure checks and other cardiovascular health assessments, such as cholesterol and blood glucose levels among women. The study findings are similar to other studies conducted elsewhere.24 40 41

Our analysis indicated that rich women had higher odds of developing hypertension compared with poor women. The possible reasons could be that rich women may have more sedentary lifestyles compared with poorer women who may be more physically active in their daily lives or through manual labour. Moreover, middle-class and wealthier women may have diets higher in processed, high-sodium foods and high salt intake, which are associated with an increased prevalence of hypertension.42 The study findings highlight the need to develop targeted educational campaigns focusing on the risks of hypertension, emphasising healthy eating, focusing on reducing salt intake, discouraging processed foods and increasing the consumption of fruits and vegetables, as well as emphasise regular health screenings for blood pressure. The study findings are in line with other studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia.4345 However, the findings are contrary to the findings reported by systematic review in high, middle and low-income countries whereby population coming from poor economic quintiles were more prone to hypertension compared with those coming from rich households.46

The study findings indicated higher odds of hypertension among urban women compared with their rural counterparts. The previous study conducted in Tanzania found higher prevalence of insufficient physical activity in urban areas, where rural areas had the highest levels of physical activity.47 Additionally, lack of regular exercise among women living in urban areas impairs the body’s ability to regulate blood pressure effectively through mechanisms like vascular function and autonomic nervous system balance.15 The increased stress levels such as job stress and financial burdens associated with urban living could also be a contributing factor to hypertension among women.48 49 The study findings suggest creating community programmes that encourage physical activity, such as walking groups or fitness challenges tailored for women, as well as introducing yoga, meditation or relaxation techniques in community centres. The study findings corroborate previous studies conducted in Ghana and Bangladesh.30 50

Moreover, the study findings revealed that women with obesity or overweight had higher odds of developing hypertension compared to women who were thin and normal. Similar findings were observed in North West Tanzania.19 Overweight or obesity often leads to metabolic syndrome, which includes insulin resistance, induced inflammation and damaged endothelial cells and increased waist circumference. These components contribute to the development of hypertension.5153 The study findings suggest early weight control and introduce interventions to overweight or obese people. Similar findings are reported elsewhere whereby people who are obese were linked to being more hypertensive compared with their counterparts who were not obese.46 50 54

The study found that women who were using alcohol had higher odds of developing hypertension compared with women who never consumed alcohol. Consumption of alcohol promotes metabolic changes, weight gain and hypertension.43 Moreover, drinking alcohol leads to the buildup of triglycerides and total cholesterol in the body, increasing the likelihood of hypertension.44 Studies found that people who regularly consume more than two alcoholic drinks (one drink contains about 30 mL of ethanol) are at a higher risk for hypertension.45 A similar finding was reported elsewhere, whereby people who drink alcohol were reported to be more hypertensive compared with their counterparts who do not take alcohol.384649

Women who were separated had higher odds of having hypertension compared with married women. This could be due to the tendency of many circumstances of separations among many marriages in Tanzania to occur in chaotic situations leading to stress among victims which eventually leads to depression. Other studies reported that stress and depression expose the victim to hypertension.53 55

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strength of this study lies in its use of nationally representative data from the 2022 TDHS-MIS. This large sample size facilitates the generation of national estimates, thereby enhancing the generalisability of the findings and offering a thorough understanding of overweight and obesity prevalence across various sociodemographic groups. Additionally, the application of sample-weighted data ensures that the results accurately represent the broader population, minimising potential biases that could occur with unweighted analyses. Since this is a cross-sectional study, it limits the ability to establish causality or identify trends over time related to hypertension. Some variables, such as physical activity and sedentary lifestyle, were not included because the survey did not capture them. In addition, the limitation of dropping cases due to missing values is acknowledged. However, the characteristics of the cases dropped were random and lacked identified patterns that could bias the results.

Conclusion

The overall prevalence of hypertension among Tanzanian women of reproductive age in Tanzania was 11.2%. The correlates of hypertension among women include older age, urban residency, being separated, coming from the richest households, drinking alcohol and smoking tobacco. The Ministry of Health and other health stakeholders should focus on designing health programmes to deal with modifiable correlates like reducing alcohol use and smoking of tobacco specifically among women as well as in the general population.
