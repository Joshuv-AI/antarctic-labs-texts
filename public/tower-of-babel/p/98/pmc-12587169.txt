Alterations in Cerebrospinal Fluid Density Following Contrast Administration in Patients With Acute Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Vadim Khasminsky, Jonathan Naftali, Gal Tsur, Rani Barnea, Ruth Eliahou, Eli Atar, Eitan Auriel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe degree of iodine clearance from the parenchyma in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) has not been determined. The aim of our study was to measure changes in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) density in patients with AIS relative to controls.MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study from a single tertiary stroke center, including any patient who underwent CT angiography (CTA) and EVT and then follow‐up noncontrast CT (NCCT) after 12–36 h. Control group included nonstroke patients who underwent CTA and a NCCT after 12–36 h. The density of CSF in Hounsfield units (HU) was measured in the frontal horns of lateral ventricles and in the third ventricle and was compared between baseline and follow‐up NCCT (ΔHU), as well as between groups. Paired t‐tests and multivariable linear regression analysis were utilized for data analysis.ResultsAltogether, 134 patients with AIS and 46 controls were included. In the AIS group, we found median (IQR) ΔHU of 1 (−0.34, 2.33) compared with −0.33 (−1, 0.33) in controls (p < 0.01). Early ischemic changes and onset‐to‐recanalization times remained significant for ΔHU following multivariable analysis.DiscussionOur findings suggest that iodine contrast administration in patients with AIS leads to increased CSF density, potentially through the glymphatic clearance systems.Keywords: acute stroke, cerebrospinal fluid density, CT, glymphatic systems, iodine

Introduction

Iodine‐based contrast media administered intravenously during computer tomography (CT) scan is not normally detected in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), as iodine does not penetrate the intact blood–brain barrier (BBB) [1, 2]. However, acute ischemic stroke (AIS) may lead to increased permeability of the BBB [3]. In AIS leaked iodine may be detected in the brain parenchyma on CT imaging showing parenchymal contrast staining [4, 5, 6, 7]. The mechanisms responsible for iodine clearance from the parenchyma in AIS, either by blood flow or by glymphatic clearance into the CSF [8, 9, 10], have not been determined.

The glymphatic system clears metabolic waste and contrast agents from the brain parenchyma by facilitating the flow of CSF through perivascular spaces, where it is mixed with interstitial fluid, enabling waste removal driven by arterial pulsations and regulated by aquaporin‐4 water channels on astrocytes [8, 10, 11].

Our hypothesis is that iodine leaked into the brain parenchyma through the disrupted BBB in AIS is subsequently cleared from the parenchyma into the CSF and leads to a slight measurable increase in Hounsfield units (HU) of the CSF on follow‐up CT imaging.

Methods

Patient Population

This is a retrospective cohort study conducted at Rabin Medical Center (RMC), a tertiary stroke care facility. We included patients over 18 years of age admitted with AIS who underwent endovascular treatment (EVT) for large vessel ischemic stroke in the anterior circulation (internal carotid artery [ICA] or middle cerebral artery [MCA]) between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2023. All patients had undergone noncontrast CT (NCCT), CT angiography (CTA), with or without CT‐perfusion (CTP) upon admission, and follow‐up NCCT 12–36 h after EVT. Exclusion criteria were the use of different CT scanners for follow‐up imaging or parenchymal hyperdensity within the infarcted area on follow‐up CT.

The control group included patients admitted to the emergency department within the same timeline who had brain NCCT, CTA with or without CTP, and follow‐up brain NCCT 12–36 h postadmission in the same protocol. They were discharged with the diagnosis of transient ischemic attack (TIA) or other nonstroke diagnosis and were not treated with thrombolysis therapy.

Imaging and Analysis

All scans were performed on a 256‐detector scanner (Philips iCT 256; Philips, Netherlands) and included brain NCCT, CTA with or without CTP at admission, and follow‐up brain NCCT after 12–36 h. A standard amount of contrast medium (OMNIPAQUE 350) was injected into each subject, including 50 cc during CTP and an additional 80 cc during CTA. Scan parameters for brain NCCT were as follows: Tube Voltage: 280 mA; Tube current: 120 kV; Slice Thickness: 1 mm; Pitch: 1.0; Rotation Time: 1 s per rotation; Collimation: 64 × 0.625 mm; Reconstruction algorithm: iDose4 [12].

After imaging work‐up, all subjects in the study group underwent EVT, which included the injection of a variable amount of contrast medium, up to 50 cc of OMNIPAQUE 350. Relevant studies were viewed on a PACS workstation (Philips PACS). The brain NCCT at admission and follow‐up was reconstructed with a slice thickness of 3 mm, without the application of postprocessing software filters, and synchronized in multiplanar reconstruction (MPR). Density in HU was measured using the standard PACS region of interest (ROI) measurement tool in the axial plane at three specific locations: bilateral frontal horns and third ventricle. Regions of the choroid plexus were not included in the analysis.

All scans were reviewed by a neuroradiologist and a neurologist who had undergone specialized training. Inter‐rater reliability was calculated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) from two‐way ANOVA analyses, which compared all readers. The ICC for average CSF density was 0.87 (95% CI 0.83–0.91) and for Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECT) was 0.92 (95% CI 0.90–0.94). Higher ICC values indicate better agreement between readers [13].

Clinical and Stroke Assessment

Variables of interest included age, sex, and previously diagnosed cardiovascular risk factors including hypertension, atrial fibrillation, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, smoking, ischemic heart disease, and a history of ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack. Stroke‐related variables consisted of baseline stroke severity measured by the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [14], use of thrombolysis (IV tPA), the ASPECT score [15], and the Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (TICI) scale for evaluating reperfusion, with good reperfusion defined as 2b‐3 [16, 17]. We also recorded time from symptoms onset to recanalization and time from baseline CT to follow‐up CT.

Outcome and Analyses

Our primary outcome was the difference in HU between baseline and follow‐up NCCT (ΔHU) at the bilateral frontal horns and third ventricle, as well as the mean Δ across all locations. Subsequently, we compared the ΔHU between the AIS group and controls. Additionally, a subgroup analysis was conducted on patients with high ASPECT scores (9–10) indicating minimal parenchymal damage and low ASPECT scores (8 and below) [15].

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics are presented descriptively. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables or median and interquartile range (IQR) for non‐normally distributed variables, while categorical variables are expressed as n (%). CSF density, although non‐normally distributed, was presented both in mean ± SD and in median (IQR) for better understanding. A t‐test was utilized to compare baseline variables for normally distributed numerical data, the Mann–Whitney test for non‐normally distributed numerical data, and Pearson's chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test to compare categorical variables.

To assess changes in HU between baseline and follow‐up scans, paired t‐tests were conducted for various anatomical locations, including the left frontal horn, right frontal horn, third ventricle, and the mean of all three locations, in both groups. For each location, the delta (Δ) between baseline and follow‐up scans was calculated. A comparison of ΔHU of scans between the AIS group and the control group was then performed using Mann–Whitney analysis.

For AIS patients only, we conducted the same subgroup analysis. We also compared patients with acute ischemic stroke who underwent both CTA and CTP before thrombectomy to those who had CTA only. Subsequently, we performed a multivariable linear regression analysis to further investigate factors associated with mean ΔHU. The model included variables potentially affecting iodine clearance (creatinine level, interval between baseline and follow‐up scans) and variables potentially affecting reperfusion and blood–brain barrier integrity (ASPECTS score, TICI score, thrombolysis treatment, and onset‐to‐recanalization time).

Two‐sided p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Patient Consents and Data Availability

The study was authorized by the RMC Institutional Review Board (study number RMC‐23‐0825). Due to the retrospective observational nature of this study, informed consent was waived. The data supporting the findings of this study are not publicly available due to privacy concerns and the lack of committee approval for data sharing.

Results

A total of 526 patients underwent EVT for anterior circulation stroke (either MCA or carotid occlusion) between January 2020 and December 2023. Of which, 317 patients were excluded as they underwent two consecutive CT scans in different scanners. An additional 75 patients were excluded due to parenchymal hyperdensity on follow‐up CT. Eventually, 134 patients were included for analysis (Figure 1). In total, 46 patients were found eligible for the control group.

Flowchart of subject selection process.

The mean age in the AIS group was 73.8 ± 13 compared with 76 ± 12.8 in the control group, p = 0.72. In the AIS group, 44% were females compared with 43% in the control group, p = 0.95. Patients with AIS had higher creatinine levels compared with controls (0.97 vs. 0.72 mg/dL, p < 0.01). Time interval between CT scans was comparable between groups (24 ± 5.1 vs. 25 ± 5.7 h mean ± SD, p = 0.65). In the AIS group, 53/134 (40%) were treated with thrombolysis, 126/134 (94%) patients had good reperfusion measured by TICI score, and 90/134 (67%) patients had an ASPECT score of 9–10 on arrival (Table 1).

CSF Density at Baseline and Follow‐Up Scans

Given the non‐normal distribution of the data, interpretation was primarily based on the median (IQR), with mean ± SD values provided for descriptive purposes. In the AIS group, we observed a median (IQR) change of 1 (−1, 3) HU between scans in the left frontal horn (p < 0.01), a change of median (IQR) 1 (0, 2) HU between scans in the right frontal horn (p < 0.01), and a change of median (IQR) 1 (−1, 3) between scans in the third ventricle (p < 0.01) (Figure 2). For the mean CSF density score, there was a change of median (IQR) 1 (−0.34, 2.33) HU between scans (mean ± SD 1.06 ± 2.23 HU) p < 0.01. In the control group, the mean CSF density score showed a change of median (IQR) −0.33 (−1, 0.33) HU between scans (mean ± SD −0.31 ± 1.61 HU) p = 0.19, with no significant differences observed at each location. When comparing the mean CSF ΔHU between the AIS group and controls, we found that the AIS group had higher ΔHU (median (IQR) 1 (−0.34, 2.33) vs. −0.33 (−1, 0.33), p < 0.01) (Table 2).

Multivariable Analysis

Following multivariable analysis examining the ΔHU of the mean of frontal horns and third ventricle in the AIS group, it was found that lower ASPECT score (p = 0.008) and onset symptoms time to recanalization (p = 0.005) were associated with greater ΔHU, while creatinine levels, thrombolysis treatment, TICI score, and time between baseline and follow‐up scans were not associated with higher ΔHU (Table 3).

Subgroup Analysis

In the AIS group, patients were divided into high ASPECT score (9–10) vs. low (8 and under). There were no differences regarding demographic, baseline characteristics, or stroke data (Table S1). When looking at patients with high ASPECT and low ASPECT scores separately, we observed significant ΔHU between scans in all regions in each group. Across groups, ΔHU was higher in the low ASPECT score group compared with high ASPECT in the left frontal horn (p = 0.014), right frontal horn (p < 0.01), third ventricle (p < 0.01), and in the mean CSF score (p < 0.02), as shown in Table S2.

In the AIS group only, we compared patients with acute ischemic stroke who underwent both CTA and CTP before thrombectomy to those who had CTA only. The median differences between scans in the left frontal horn, right frontal horn, third ventricle, and average CSF score were all significantly higher in the CTA and CTP group (ranging from 1.00 to 2.00) compared with the CTA only group, which showed medians close to zero, with all comparisons reaching statistical significance (p ≤ 0.005); see Table S3.

Discussion

Our study found that patients with AIS had a greater change in CSF density (ΔHU) compared with the control group (mean 1.06 HU vs. −0.33 HU, p < 0.01). Additionally, lower ASPECT scores were associated with higher ΔHU values (mean 0.66 HU vs. 1.67 HU, p < 0.01). To our knowledge, this is the first report indicating increased CSF density in the ventricular system in patients with AIS following IV iodine contrast administration.

The elimination half‐life of iodine contrast media is 1.8–2.3 h [18]. About 80% of the given dose is eliminated within 8 h, and practically most iodine is eliminated within 24 h [12]. Following AIS, there is damage to the integrity of BBB tight junctions, resulting in leakage of iodine into the brain parenchyma and interstitial fluid [4, 6, 7, 16, 17]. BBB disruption may also result in silent or symptomatic hemorrhagic transformation due to increased vessel wall permeability [3].

The mechanism responsible for the increased density of the CSF following AIS is still to be determined. One possible explanation is clearance by the glymphatic system [8, 10]. The glymphatic system clears waste metabolites and proteins, along with contrast agents and tracers [19] from the brain parenchyma via perivenous spaces into CSF [20]. Previous studies showed increased influx of CSF in the glymphatic system following AIS [21]. The increased influx is thought to be initiated by ischemic spreading depolarization and vasoconstriction, resulting in enlarged perivascular spaces and doubling glymphatic flow speed [21].

It should be noted that changes in CSF density may also be the result of blood leakage from parenchyma in the case of hemorrhagic transformation. To mitigate this concern, we excluded all patients with parenchymal hyperdensity on follow‐up. Doing so, we may have also excluded patients with infract enhancement and potentially significant CSF density changes. The use of virtual noncontrast (VNC) imaging may help in differentiating blood products from enhancement [22]. However, patients who underwent the VNC follow‐up scan were excluded from the study to maintain the same imaging protocol as on arrival.

Whether an increase in CSF density may predict clinical outcome was beyond the scope of the present study. The association with ASPECT score, however, might suggest that it may have prognostic value. Following multivariable analysis, onset symptoms to recanalization time were associated with ΔHU as well, also supporting the association between changes in density and worse outcomes. Using CSF hyperdensity as a prediction marker for a worse outcome is a matter for future studies.

Our subgroup analysis suggests a potential dose‐dependent effect of iodine contrast volume on CSF density changes, as patients undergoing both CTA and CTP (with higher total contrast volume) showed greater median differences compared to those with CTA alone. However, the lack of data on iodine dose administered during thrombectomy limits definitive conclusions, and further studies are needed to clarify this relationship.

Our study has several limitations. First, iodine transportation to CSF through the glymphatic system may also depend on factors such as sleep and hydration, which we did not account for [10, 20]. Secondly, the amount of iodine administered may differ across patients because of the variable amount of iodine received during EVT. Thirdly, as already noted, due to the lack of VNC imaging, we may have also excluded patients with true parenchymal iodine staining and not only due to ICH.

Conclusion

Patients with AIS who received IV iodine had a higher ΔHU in the CSF on follow‐up CT compared with controls, suggesting the transfer of iodine from brain parenchyma to CSF via the glymphatic system. This notion, however, requires further support.
