Response to “Unequal Local Anesthetic Dosing and Surgical Heterogeneity: Considerations for Interpreting the ESPB–TEA Non-Inferiority Trial” [Response to Letter]
Authors: Sooah Cho, Ho-Jin Lee, Soo-Hyuk Yoon, Sang-Youn Park, Samina Park, Hyun Joo Lee, In Kyu Park, Chang Hyun Kang, Kwon Joong Na, Susie Yoon, Young Tae Kim, Jae-Hyon Bahk
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We thank Dr. Liu and Dr. Lu for their thoughtful commentary on our trial comparing continuous erector spinae plane block (ESPB) with thoracic epidural analgesia (TEA) after thoracotomy.1 We appreciate the opportunity to address their methodological concerns and provide additional context for interpreting our findings.

Dr. Liu and Lu note that we did not perform objective sensory block assessment and raise concerns about distinguishing interfascial block effects from systemic local anesthetic absorption, particularly given the higher ropivacaine exposure in the ESPB group. As acknowledged in our manuscript, sensory block levels were not assessed as a separate study measure. However, catheter placement was confirmed under imaging guidance with fluoroscopy for TEA and ultrasound for ESPB to ensure appropriate positioning. We recognize that the clinical effectiveness of ESPB remains controversial; volunteer studies have demonstrated limited anterior thoracic sensory changes,2 and anatomical investigations have shown inconsistent ventral injectate spread following the block.3 We agree that incorporating dermatomal mapping in future trials would help distinguish the relative contributions of interfascial spread and systemic absorption.

Regarding local anesthetic dosing, the higher volume in the ESPB arm is a necessary consequence of the technique itself, not a design limitation. Neuraxial blockade achieves direct access to spinal nerve roots with small volumes, whereas fascial plane blocks depend on volume-driven interfascial spread to reach target structures.4,5 The dosing regimens in both arms were consistent with published recommendations for each technique.4,6 Our trial was designed as a pragmatic effectiveness comparison of two techniques as applied in clinical practice, not a mechanistic efficacy trial. Concerning local anesthetic systemic toxicity risk, no clinical signs were observed during the study. We agree that pharmacokinetic monitoring is a valuable addition to future investigations of continuous ESPB, particularly given the higher cumulative local anesthetic exposure compared with neuraxial techniques.

The authors correctly identify uneven distribution of surgical procedures between groups, with pneumonectomy more frequent in the TEA group at 33.3% versus 8.7% in the ESPB group. Randomization was performed without stratification by procedure type, and the observed imbalance likely represents a chance finding in a small sample, as is recognized to occur even with proper randomization when group sizes are limited.7 We recognize that the higher proportion of pneumonectomy in the TEA group could have biased the primary outcome in favor of ESPB non-inferiority, as pneumonectomy carries substantially greater chest wall trauma and postoperative pain than lobectomy. To address this concern, we performed a post-hoc sensitivity analysis re-estimating the treatment effect on the primary outcome using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) with procedure type (lobectomy, sleeve lobectomy, bilobectomy, pneumonectomy) as a categorical covariate. In the modified intention-to-treat population, the procedure-adjusted mean difference in the primary outcome (postoperative day 1 resting numeric rating scale score) was −0.96 (95% confidence interval, −2.20 to 0.28) in favor of ESPB. The corresponding estimate in the per-protocol population was −0.85 (95% confidence interval, −2.13 to 0.43). The one-sided 95% upper confidence limit lay below the prespecified non-inferiority margin of 2.0 numeric rating scale units in both populations, confirming the non-inferiority conclusion of the primary unadjusted analysis and indicating that the result is robust to imbalance in procedure type between groups. We agree that stratified randomization by operative extent should be incorporated in future trials to minimize this risk.

Regarding catheter stability, the authors note that three of 23 ESPB patients experienced catheter-related events including two accidental removals and one device malfunction, representing approximately 13% technical failure. We appreciate this observation. Our modified intention-to-treat analysis included all patients who received the allocated intervention regardless of subsequent catheter complications, meaning that the primary result already incorporates the clinical impact of these technical failures. This approach reflects the real-world effectiveness of ESPB as a technique package, including its inherent catheter-related limitations. The consistency between our modified intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses, showing unadjusted mean differences of −0.59 versus −0.61, respectively, demonstrates that catheter instability did not substantially affect our conclusions. We acknowledge that twice-daily pain assessments may not capture brief episodes of analgesic interruption, and the observed catheter event rate is within the range reported for continuous fascial plane catheters in published cohorts.8 We agree that subcutaneous tunneling may improve catheter retention and should be considered in future protocols, alongside more detailed catheter integrity monitoring.

Dr. Liu and Lu raise concerns about performance bias from unblinded proceduralists and note that rescue analgesia in the post-anesthesia care unit was required by 69.6% of ESPB patients versus 42.9% of TEA patients, a gap that did not reach statistical significance. We agree that complete blinding of proceduralists is not feasible in trials comparing catheter-based regional techniques with different insertion sites, and we acknowledged this as a limitation in our manuscript. Outcome assessment was performed by a dedicated research nurse who was separate from the clinical care team, was unaware of group allocation, and did not have access to procedural details. Regarding the difference in rescue analgesia rates, rescue medication was administered according to predefined criteria with a numeric rating scale threshold of 4 or higher rather than at the discretion of the treating clinician, which limits the potential for allocation-driven prescribing bias. While the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.127), we acknowledge that the numerical gap warrants consideration. Nevertheless, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility of performance bias, and we agree that a fully protocolized rescue pathway administered by blinded personnel would further strengthen future trial designs.

We wish to emphasize several strengths of our trial that contribute to the evolving evidence base. Our study was prospectively registered with pre-specified outcomes and employed rigorous technique standardization with imaging confirmation of catheter placement. We included comprehensive outcome assessment with pain scores at multiple timepoints, opioid consumption quantified as morphine-equivalents, quality of recovery measured with the validated QoR-15K instrument, and long-term follow-up at 3 and 6 months to assess chronic pain development. Our reporting was transparent with explicit acknowledgment of limitations including the underpowered design due to premature termination. We employed advanced statistical methods including both frequentist and Bayesian analyses, multiple sensitivity analyses, and comprehensive post-hoc power calculations to contextualize our findings in light of the reduced sample size.

We share the view that confirmation in larger trials with stratified randomization, dermatomal mapping, pharmacokinetic monitoring, and protocolized rescue pathways is warranted, and we stated as much in our manuscript describing our findings as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. The methodological concerns raised by Dr. Liu and Lu are valuable contributions to scientific discourse and will help guide the design of future investigations. However, we respectfully suggest that these considerations should be viewed as refinements for future research rather than fundamental flaws that undermine our conclusions. We believe the present study contributes a pragmatic, real-world comparison that supports ESPB as a feasible alternative to TEA in selected thoracic surgery patients, with a potentially superior safety profile characterized by fewer contraindications and lower risk of serious complications. This finding, if confirmed by larger trials with refined methodology, could expand access to effective regional analgesia for patients in whom TEA is contraindicated or in centers where neuraxial expertise is limited.

We thank Dr. Liu and Lu for their engagement with our work and look forward to the collective advancement of regional anesthesia practice through continued rigorous research and scholarly dialogue.
