Prognostic and predictive factors in advanced upper gastrointestinal cancer treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors: a systematic review and meta-analysis of the current evidence
Authors: Puxiu Wang, Ping Chen, Weiting Yang, Wenhan Yang, Wenqi Liu, Song Yue, Qiuhua Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown encouraging treatment efficacy for upper gastrointestinal cancers (UGICs). However, durable clinical responses only existed in a minority of patients. We evaluated evidence predicting survival benefits to identify the optimal population followed by ICI-based therapy.MethodsA comprehensive search was performed using PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science to identify clinical trials for UGICs with ICI-based therapy. The outcomes were objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS). The quality of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation System (GRADE).ResultsThirty-six studies comprising 12,440 patients were included for quantitative synthesis. Patients with PD-L1-positive (OR = 2.08, p < 0.00001), EBV+ (OR = 8.47, p = 0.003) tumors were more likely to respond to ICI treatment. Moreover, OS was significantly improved with the statistical subgroup difference concerning sex (p = 0.02) and region (p = 0.02). An exploratory subgroup analysis showed significantly improved OS with ICI plus chemotherapy in patients with CPS ≥ 10 (HR = 0.66, p = 0.001) and CPS ≥ 1 (HR = 0.75, p < 0.00001).ConclusionUGIC patients with PD-L1-positive, EBV + status are associated with a better therapeutic response to ICI-based therapy. The male patients and Asian patients could derive more survival benefits following ICI treatment than female and non-Asian ones. A combination of prognostic and predictive factors was suggested to help guide immunotherapy decision-making in UGIC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-12998-w.Keywords: Upper gastrointestinal cancer, Immune checkpoint inhibitor, Biomarker, Prognosis, Overall survival

Introduction

Upper gastrointestinal cancers (UGICs), including esophageal cancer (EC), gastroesophageal junction cancer (GEJC) and gastric cancer (GC), are among the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Despite advancements in treatment, progress in managing metastatic and advanced UGICs has been limited over recent decades. Current therapeutic approaches, primarily involving doublet platinum-based chemotherapy, offer modest survival benefits and are associated with poor prognoses [1–4]. As a result, nearly 60–70% UGIC patients require second-line treatments, which often lacks standardized regimens as outlined in current clinical guidelines.

Recent developments in immunotherapy have introduced immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) as a promising treatment option. ICIs have demonstrated broad-spectrum anti-tumor activity across various cancer types [5–12]. The pathological characteristics of gastroesophageal carcinoma, including chronic inflammation and DNA damage, often result in elevated levels of microsatellite instability (MSI), tumor mutation burden (TMB), and immune checkpoint proteins. These features suggest that immunotherapy could provide new and effective treatment opportunities [13–19]. Despite this potential, ICIs have only shown durable clinical responses in a minority of patients, with many experiencing no improvement in quality of life and facing risks of immune-related adverse effects (irAEs) [20–26]. Thus, there is a critical need for personalized treatment strategies to optimize the efficacy of ICIs in UGIC patients. For instance, subgroup analysis from the KEYNOTE-061 trial revealed that patients with a performance status (PS) of 0 experienced higher response rates and better survival benefits compared to those with PS 1 or 2, highlighting the importance of evaluating overall health before initiating ICI therapy [21]. Such insights are crucial for optimizing the application of ICIs and ensuring that clinical resources are allocated effectively for GC, GEJC, and EC patients.

The objective of this study is to conduct a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis to to identify predictive factors that may enhance the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs in UGIC patients. We also investigate the correlation between PD-L1 expression and the survival benefits associated with ICI-based therapies. The findings from this study are expected to inform clinical practice by identifying patient subgroups most likely to benefit from ICI therapy, thus supporting more personalized and effective treatment strategies and aiding in the optimal allocation of clinical resources.

Methods

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across multiple databases, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science online databases, and www.clinicaltrials.gov, from 1946 to August 2024 by two investigators independently. Major conference proceedings and meeting abstracts were also reviewed. The search terms contained the following keywords: Nivolumab, Pembrolizumab, Atezolizumab, Durvalumab, Avelumab, Ipilimumab, Tremelimumab, Camrelizumab, immune checkpoint inhibitor, PD-1 inhibitor, PD-L1 inhibitor, CTLA-4 inhibitor, gastric cancer (GC), gastroesophageal junction cancer (GEJC) and esophageal cancer (EC). Detailed search terms are listed in Appendix 1.

Study selection

This review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024570556). The inclusion criteria were studies evaluating patients with GC, GEJC, or EC undergoing ICI-based therapy and reported on objective response rate (ORR), overall survival (OS), and progression-free survival (PFS). Exclusion criteria encompassed studies with insufficient data, case reports, reviews, non-GC/GEJC/EC studies, or those not reporting the specified outcomes. Trials with atypical ICI dosages or those using ICIs as neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapies were also excluded.

Study selection was based on the Participant-Intervention-Comparison-Outcome (PICOS) framework [27]:

Participants: Patients with GC, GEJC, or EC received ICI-based therapy, including either ICI monotherapy or combination therapy.

Two reviewers independently screened and evaluated all studies based on titles, abstracts and full texts. Any discrepancies were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction

A standardized form was made for data extraction. The following information was extracted: first author, publication year, clinical trial phase, sample size, median follow-up duration, treatment line, primary tumor site (GC, GEJC, EC), ICI targets (PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4), method of PD-L1 assessment, and defined cut-off values. ORR data were extracted from subgroups and presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), including variables such as microsatellite status, EBV status, PD-L1 expression, tumor pathology, patient race, age, sex, treatment line, and ECOG score. OS and PFS data were extracted as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% CIs, stratified by PD-L1 expression. Data extraction for this meta-analysis was performed by multiple independent reviewers. Each reviewer independently extracted data from the included studies, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Methodology quality assessment

The risk of bias was assessed using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) and the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [28, 29]. Additionally, the Quality in Prognosis Studies (QUIPS) tool was used to evaluate the risk of bias in prognostic factor studies [27, 30, 31]. The risk of bias assessment was conducted by multiple independent reviewers. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through consensus meetings.

Quality of evidence assessment

The quality of cumulative evidence and the strength of recommendations were assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) assessment [32]. Two independent reviewers used the GRADE system to independently assess the quality of evidence for each outcome. Evidence quality was rated as ‘high’, ‘moderate’, ‘low’ or ‘very low’ according to the GRADE rating standards. Evaluation criteria included study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision of results, and other relevant factors. Publication bias for different subgroups concerning ORR and survival outcomes was statistically assessed using Harbord’s and Egger’s tests. Harbord’s test, a modified version of Egger’s test, specifically addresses small-study effects in meta-analyses of binary outcomes [33].

Data synthesis and analysis

Data retrieved from published reports underwent both quantitative and qualitative analysis. The ORR was weighted and pooled using the Meta package in R software (version 3.5.0). We performed the Q-test to assess between-study heterogeneity and calculated the I² statistic, which expresses the percentage of the total observed variability due to study heterogeneity. Pooled ORs and HRs with 95% CIs weighted by the inverse of the variance were synthesized using Review Manager software (version 5.4.1). HR and CIs were used to evaluate the association of biomarkers with the OS. To evaluate the stability of the outcomes in the meta-analysis, a sensitivity analysis was performed. All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as a p value < 0.05.

Results

Search results

A comprehensive literature search was conducted on August 31, 2024, and identified 1,085 studies. After the removal of duplicates, 162 studies were selected for full-text screening. The selection process and reasons for study exclusion were detailed in Fig. 1, following the PRISMA flowchart. All studies were prospective cohort studies published between 2016 and 2024.

Study characteristics

The systematic review and meta-analysis included 36 studies encompassing a total of 12,440 patients with advanced UGICs. The basic characteristics of the included studies are described in Table 1. Among the 36 studies, 23 were phase I/II trials [22, 23, 34–48, 59–64], and 13 were phase III trials [20, 21, 49–58, 65]. Of theses studies, 19 studies reported on ICI monotherapy [20–23, 35, 37, 38, 42–49, 51, 54–56], while 20 focused on ICI combination therapy [22, 34, 36, 39–41, 46, 50, 52, 53, 56–65]. Notably, three studies included cohorts receiving both ICI monotherapy and ICI combination therapy [22, 46, 56]. The ICI combination therapy examined included ICI plus chemotherapy (11 studies) [36, 41, 46, 50, 52, 53, 56–58, 63, 65], dual ICI inhibitors (2 studies) [22, 58], and ICI plus targeted therapies (4 studies) [34, 39, 40, 59], respectively. The CheckMate-648 study featured dual ICI combinations, specifically nivolumab plus chemotherapy and nivolumab plus ipilimumab [58]. Of the 36 studies, 33 utilized PD-1 inhibitors [20–23, 34–37, 39–41, 43–48, 50–65], 3 used PD-L1 inhibitors [38, 42, 49], and 3 used CTLA-4 inhibitors [22, 42, 58]. Fifteen studies involved first-line treatment [21, 34–36, 38, 41, 46, 50, 52, 53, 56–58, 60, 62], whereas 19 studies focused on second-line or later treatments [20, 22, 23, 37, 39, 42–49, 51, 54, 55, 61, 63, 64].

The quality assessment of the included RCTs, presented in Supplemental Fig. 1, utilized the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool. Four studies were rated as having a low risk of bias, 7 exhibited performance bias, and 1 study revealed reporting bias. Eighteen non-randomized studies were assessed with the MINORS tool (Supplemental Table 1), showing a total score between 14 and 15, indicating a low risk of bias. The risk-of-bias assessment for prognostic factors, shown in Supplemental Table 2, utilized the QUIPS tool, with 14 studies showing a low risk of bias and 11 showing a moderate risk, primarily due to issues with study participation, attrition, and confounding.

Objective response rate

Objective response rate to ICI treatment

The pooled objective response rate (ORR) for ICI treatment was 35% (95% CI = 34–36%) across 6226 UGIC patients. The strategy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy indicated a higher ORR (38%, 95% CI = 36–39%) than anti-PD-L1 (4%, 95% CI = 2–7%) and anti-CTLA-4 immunotherapy (24%, 95% CI = 20–28%). Patients receiving immunotherapy as first-line treatment had an enhanced ORR of 44% (95% CI = 42–46%) compared to those receiving it as second-line or later (21%, 95% CI = 19–22%). Additionally, ICI combination therapies (including ICI plus chemotherapy and ICI plus targeted therapy and ICI plus targeted therapy plus therapy) exhibited a better ORR (52%, 95% CI = 50–53%) compared to ICI monotherapy (14%, 95% CI = 12–15%). Specifically, ICI plus chemotherapy (e.g., cisplatin, fluorouracil, capecitabine) resulted in a higher ORR of 55%, slightly exceeding that of ICI plus targeted therapy (e.g., ramucirumab, lenvatinib, trastuzumab). GC patients and EC patients achieved ORRs of 37% (95% CI = 32–42%) and 31% (95% CI = 28–33%), respectively. Moreover, patients with PD-L1-positive expression had a higher ORR (35%, 95% CI = 32–39%) compared to those with PD-L1-negative expression. Subgroup analysis by combined positive score (CPS) revealed that patients with CPS ≥ 10 had a significantly higher ORR (46%, 95% CI = 42–50%) compared to those with CPS < 13 (8%, 95% CI = 10–18%). Similarly, patients with CPS ≥ 1 had a higher ORR (42%, 95% CI = 40–44%) than those with CPS < 1 (32%, 95% CI = 30–35%). Tumors with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) and Epstein-Barr virus-positive (EBV+) status showed better ORRs compared to non-MSI-H and EBV-negative tumors, with ORRs of 61% (95% CI = 41–78%) and 73% (95% CI = 39–93%), respectively. Furthermore, combination therapies significantly improved ORR compared to ICI monotherapy. Additional subgroup comparisons, including age, sex, and ECOG performance status, are presented in Fig. 2.

Prognostic factors for ORR following ICI treatment

Data from 5,827 UGIC patients across 26 studies were analyzed for prognostic factors including PD-L1 expression, EBV status, microsatellite status, primary tumor site, ECOG score, sex, age, race, and line of treatment. A subgroup analysis of 1,576 patients from 15 studies revealed that PD-L1-positive patients had a significantly higher ORR than PD-L1-negative patients (OR = 2.08, 95% CI = 1.66–2.60, p < 0.00001, I2 = 49%) (Fig. 3(A)) (GRADE assessment: high certainty). Patients with ESCC indicated a better ORR than those with adenocarcinoma (Fig. 3(B)). Furthermore, EBV (+) tumors (OR = 8.47, 95% CI = 2.07–34.60, p = 0.003, I2 = 48%, Fig. 3(C)) (GRADE assessment: all moderate certainty). Moreover, the use of a combined therapeutic strategy significantly predicted a better ORR compared to ICI monotherapy (Fig. 3(D)) (OR = 5.73, 95% CI = 3.93–8.35, p < 0.00001, I2 = 17%) (GRADE assessment: all high certainty). Subgroup comparisons based on sex, age, line of treatment, CPS score, ECOG score, microsatellite status, and primary tumor site did not show significant differences in ORR (Supplemental Fig. 2(A-H); Supplemental Table 3.1).

Prognostic factors for OS following ICI treatment

The prognostic factors for OS are illustrated in Table 2. First, our analysis revealed that male patients experienced increasing OS benefits followed by ICI treatment (HR = 0.75, 95% CI 0.71–0.80) compared to female patients (HR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.79–1.02). This difference was statistically significant when comparing the efficacy of ICI-based therapy between genders, with a p-value of 0.02, indicating that men may benefit more from ICI therapy compared to women. Second, Asian patients derived a substantial improvement in OS with ICI-based therapy, achieving a significantly enhanced HR of 0.75 (95% CI 0.68–0.83) compared with chemotherapy. In contrast, non-Asian patients treated with ICI therapy demonstrated no significant difference in OS compared to those receiving chemotherapy (HR = 0.90, 95% CI 0.81–1.01). The regional variation in therapeutic effectiveness was statistically significant (p = 0.02), suggesting that the effectiveness of ICI-based therapy varies by geographic region. Finally, patients with distant metastasis demonstrated a significant reduction in death risk with ICI therapy, showing a 31% decrease in mortality risk (HR = 0.69 (95% CI 0.61–0.78)) compared to those treated with chemotherapy. In contrast, patients with regional recurrence did not show a significant difference in OS benefits between ICI-based therapy and chemotherapy (HR = 0.84, 95% CI 0.62–1.13), and the p-value for these subgroup analyses did not indicate a significant difference (GRADE assessment: all high certainty). Additionally, no significant differences were observed based on other stratification factors such as age, ECOG performance status, the presence of metastasis, liver metastasis, or different primary tumor sites (Supplemental Table 3.2).

ICI plus chemotherapy vs. chemotherapy

The combination of ICI and chemotherapy resulted in significantly improved OS and PFS compared to chemotherapy alone. Specifically, the pooled analysis of randomized trials revealed that patients receiving ICI plus chemotherapy had a notable enhancement in OS (HR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.73–0.84; p < 0.00001; Fig. 4) and PFS (HR = 0.71, 95% CI = 0.64–0.78; p < 0.00001; Supplemental Fig. 3). In particular, patients with a CPS of ≥ 1 or ≥ 10 demonstrated significantly superior outcomes. For those with CPS ≥ 1, OS was improved (HR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.69–0.81; p < 0.00001) and PFS was enhanced (HR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.69–0.81; p < 0.0001) with ICI plus chemotherapy. Similarly, patients with CPS ≥ 10 showed even greater benefits, with improved OS (HR = 0.66, 95% CI 0.57–0.75; p < 0.00001) and PFS (HR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.57–0.73; p < 0.00001) (GRADE assessment: all high certainty).

Forest plot analysis of overall survival in prespecified subgroups based on CPS for ICI plus chemotherapy vs. chemotherapy. The hazard ratio for each study is presented, and horizontal lines indicate the 95% confidence interval. CPS, combined positive score

However, for patients with CPS < 1, no significant difference in OS (HR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.72–1.07; p = 0.19) or PFS (HR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.51–1.13; p = 0.17) was observed between ICI plus chemotherapy and chemotherapy alone (GRADE assessment: moderate certainty) (Supplemental Table 3.3).

ICI monotherapy vs. chemotherapy

The analysis of 2,960 patients revealed that ICI monotherapy significantly improved OS compared to chemotherapy (HR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.71–0.97; p = 0.02; Fig. 5). Patients with CPS ≥ 10 experienced the greatest benefit, with a 32% reduction in the risk of death (HR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.56–0.83; p = 0.0001). However, no significant therapeutic advantage was observed with ICI monotherapy for patients with CPS ≥ 1 (HR = 0.87, 95% CI 0.75–1.01; p = 0.06) (GRADE assessment: all high certainty) or CPS < 1 (HR = 1.04, 95% CI 0.77–1.42; p = 0.82) when compared to chemotherapy (GRADE assessment: moderate certainty).

Regarding PFS, there was no significant difference between ICI monotherapy and chemotherapy across all randomized patients (HR = 1.00, 95% CI 0.75–1.35; p = 0.98; Supplemental Fig. 4). Similarly, for patients with CPS ≥ 10, no significant difference in PFS was found (HR = 0.89, 95% CI 0.60–1.33; p = 0.57). Additionally, patients with CPS ≥ 1 (HR = 1.46, 95% CI 1.12–1.89; p = 0.005) and CPS < 1 (HR = 2.05, 95% CI 1.50–2.80; p < 0.00001) did not show significant PFS benefits from ICI monotherapy compared to chemotherapy (GRADE assessment: all high certainty) (Supplemental Table 3.4).

Publication bias and certainty in evidence

The results from Harbord’s test and Egger’s test did not indicate any publication bias (p ≥ 0.05) in the meta-analysis of all subgroups (Supplemental Figs. 5, 6). According to the GRADE assessment, the quality of evidence was high for several indicators predicting ORR including PD-L1 expression, region, sex ECOG score, primary tumor site, treatment line, and therapeutic regimen. The prognostic value of microsatellite status, EBV status, and age was rated as moderate quality for ORR (Supplemental Table 3.1).

In the comparison of ICI-based combination therapy versus chemotherapy (Supplemental Table 3.2), the evidence quality was high for CPS ≥ 1, CPS ≥ 10, CPS < 1, age < 65, age ≥ 65, sex, ECOG scores, region, organs with metastasis, liver metastasis, primary tumor site-GEJC, regional recurrence, disease stage, and OS. The evidence quality was moderate for CPS ≥ 1, CPS ≥ 10, CPS < 1, and PFS, as well as for liver metastasis and primary tumor site-GC regarding OS.

For the comparison between ICI plus chemotherapy versus chemotherapy alone (Supplemental Table 3.3), the evidence for different CPS scores, OS, and PFS was generally of high quality, except for CPS < 1 and PFS, which had moderate evidence quality. In comparing ICI monotherapy versus chemotherapy (Supplemental Table 3.4), the evidence quality was high for CPS ≥ 1 and CPS ≥ 10 with respect to OS.

Sensitivity analysis

Forest plot analysis of overall survival in prespecified subgroups based on CPS for ICI monotherapy vs. Chemotherapy are heterogeneous, so this outcome index was used for sensitivity analysis. To ensure unbiased results, we excluded the literature with the largest proportion. After reanalyzing the literature effect, ICI monotherapy significantly improved OS compared to chemotherapy, affirming the credibility of our study findings (HR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.76–0.91; p < 0.0001; Supplemental Fig. 7).

Ongoing clinical trials with ICI therapy in UGICs

By searching the website of www.clinicaltrials.gov, we identified 32 ongoing clinical trials evaluating ICI-based strategies in patients with UGICs (Table 3). These trials include 28 phase I/II studies and 4 phase III studies. The distribution of studies was as follows: 28 studies involving GC, 20 studies involving GEJC, and 3 studies involving EC. Primary endpoints for these trials include ORR, OS, PFS, disease-free survival (DFS), pathological complete response (pCR), maximum tolerated dose (MTD), adverse events (AE), major pathological response (MPR), DNA clearance rate, treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAE), event-free survival (EFS), dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs), and serious adverse events (SAE). The estimated completion dates for these trials range from January 2022 to August 2026.

Discussion

For decades, doublet platinum-based chemotherapy has been the standard first-line treatment for patients with advanced UGICs. Despite its widespread use, this approach has shown limited efficacy in improving long-term outcomes. Recent advances in immunotherapy, particularly ICIs, have emerged as promising alternatives, offering potential new avenues for treatment with encouraging results. However, the introduction of ICIs comes with the challenge of managing irAEs. Identifying prognostic factors that can predict which patients are most likely to benefit from ICI therapy is therefore of crucial clinical significance.

This study is the first to analyze a large cohort of 12,440 patients to provide a comprehensive evaluation of prognostic factors for predicting response and survival benefits of ICIs in UGICs. Our results indicate a significant association between PD-L1-positive tumors and improved OS and ORR in UGIC patients receiving ICI-based therapy. Specifically, a CPS ≥ 10 emerged as a critical predictor of therapeutic efficacy. This reinforces the notion that PD-L1 expression is a valuable biomarker for selecting patients likely to benefit from ICIs. In contrast, PD-L1-negative status is generally associated with a lack of benefit from ICI therapy, underscoring the need for accurate PD-L1 testing to guide treatment decisions [18].

Our data also show that combining ICIs with chemotherapy or antiangiogenic agents, such as cabozantinib and lenvatinib, offers a significant OS benefit. This combination approach could potentially address the delayed responses associated with ICIs and the less durable effects of traditional cytotoxic chemotherapy. Conversely, ICI monotherapy did not show substantial improvement in OS or progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with CPS ≥ 1, suggesting that a combined therapeutic strategy may be more effective for this subgroup.

In addition to PD-L1, other biomarkers such as MSI-H, and EBV status should be considered. Our study corroborates that EBV-positive and MSI-H tumors are associated with higher ORR to ICIs. MSI-H tumors, with their increased somatic mutations due to mismatch repair defects, may elicit a stronger antitumor immune response. Similarly, EBV-positive GC often exhibit robust PD-L1 expression, suggesting potential sensitivity to immunotherapy. However, while several other biomarkers such as cytosolic DNA, PTEN mutations, and tumor mutational burden (TMB) have been proposed, their clinical validation remains limited.

According to the study, EBV + and MSI-H tumors were associated with a higher response rate. This is probably because defects in mismatch repair result in an increased number of somatic mutations, which could induce an innate antitumor immune response and render tumors more responsive to ICIs [66]. Previous analysis derived from TCGA database has demonstrated that EBV + gastric cancer exhibited robust PD-L1 expression in both cancer cells and immune cells [67], serving as a proposed molecular marker of potential sensitivity to immunotherapy, which is also consistent with the results [67]. Several potential biomarkers have also been proposed in dMMR/MSI-H gastrointestinal cancer, including cytosolic DNA, PTEN mutations, and TMB [68–70]. However, the clinical evidence was obtained from a small sample size, and the necessary validation was lacking. It was also indicated that the combination of AKT1 and CDH1 mutations predicts primary resistance to immunotherapy in dMMR/MSI-H gastrointestinal cancer [71]. This suggested that a combination of biomarkers may help guide immunotherapy decision-making. Other emerging biomarkers, such as TIGIT and DKK1, are also being evaluated in ongoing trials (NCT04363801, NCT04047862) to identify patients most likely to respond to ICIs. Moreover, increased efficacy of ICI-based therapy in male versus female patients was also evident, as well as in Asian versus non-Asian patients, which may provide certain value in the selection of patients. Interestingly, the pooled results concerning survival data indicated that the ICI-based regimens provided incremental gains in OS rather than PFS. The lack of a PFS benefit with ICIs is probably due to the “delayed effect” of ICI therapy, which is consistent with some findings from other studies [21].

However, significant knowledge gaps remain, particularly regarding the validation of novel biomarkers and the mechanisms underlying primary and acquired resistance to ICIs. One of the primary limitations of this study is the heterogeneity among the included studies. This heterogeneity arises from variations in primary cancer sites, treatment regimens, and the use of different PD-L1 clones and thresholds for positivity (Supplemental Table 4). For example, studies included in this analysis utilized different scoring systems for PD-L1 expression, such as CPS, TPS, and other metrics, which may contribute to variability in reported outcomes. Additionally, differences in the baseline characteristics of the study populations, including variations in ethnicity, age, and disease stage, further complicate the interpretation of the results.

Another limitation is the potential for selection bias, as the included studies may not fully represent the broader patient population due to strict inclusion and exclusion criteria. Furthermore, the retrospective nature of some analyses and the reliance on previously published data introduce the possibility of incomplete data reporting and publication bias. It is crucial for future studies to adopt standardized methodologies and reporting guidelines to minimize these sources of bias.

Looking ahead, future research should focus on addressing these limitations by conducting well-designed, prospective studies with uniform criteria for patient selection and biomarker assessment. The development and validation of novel biomarkers, including those related to the tumor microenvironment and immune response, will be essential for improving patient stratification and predicting treatment outcomes. Additionally, ongoing clinical trials evaluating the efficacy of combination therapies and novel immunotherapeutic agents will likely provide new insights and expand the therapeutic options available to patients with UGICs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, exploring prognostic and predictive factors is necessary to provide essential information concerning long-term survival in patients with UGICs. Our findings suggest several potential factors that may predict a more pronounced immunotherapeutic effect, such as MSI, EBV and a preferable performance status. Furthermore, the male patients and Asian patients could derive more survival benefits following ICI treatment than female and non-Asian ones. Combination of ICIs with chemotherapy and antiangiogenic agents are other approaches with promising activity. Moreover, patients with CPS ≥ 10 could achieve definite survival benefits from ICIs, but the uniform standardized assessment of PD-L1 in UGICs should be associated with improved consistency to promote the clinical practice. These prognostic and predictive factors may provide a feasible approach to identify patients who could benefit from ICIs that can hopefully optimize patient outcomes.
