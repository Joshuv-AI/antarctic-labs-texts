Van der Waals integrated single-junction light-emitting diodes exceeding 10% quantum efficiency at room temperature
Authors: Zhenliang Hu, Qiang Fu, Junpeng Lu, Yong Zhang, Qi Zhang, Shixuan Wang, Zhexing Duan, Yuwei Zhang, Xiaoya Liu, Qiang Pan, Guangsheng Jiang, Tong Yang, Xu Han, Yutian Yang, Tianqi Liu, Tao Tao, Wenhui Wang, Bei Zhao, Xueyong Yuan, Dongyang Wan, Yanpeng Liu, Yumeng You, Peng Zhou, Hongwei Liu, Zhenhua Ni
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The construction of miniaturized light-emitting diodes (LEDs) with high external quantum efficiency (EQE) at room temperature remains a challenge for on-chip optoelectronics. Here, we demonstrate microsized LEDs fabricated by a dry-transfer van der Waals (vdW) integration method using typical layered Ruddlesden-Popper perovskites (RPPs). A single-crystalline layered RPP nanoflake is used as the active layer and sandwiched between two few-layer graphene contacts, forming van der Waals LEDs (vdWLEDs). Strong electroluminescence (EL) emission with a low turn-on current density of ~20 pA μm−2 and high EQE exceeding 10% is observed at room temperature, which sets the benchmark for the EQE of vdWLEDs ever recorded. Such efficient EL emission is attributed to the inherent multiple quantum well structure and high photoluminescence quantum yield (~35%) of RPPs and a low charge injection barrier of ~0.10 eV facilitated by the Fowler-Nordheim tunneling mechanism. These findings promise a scalable pathway for accessing high-performance miniaturized light sources for on-chip optical optoelectronics.

INTRODUCTION

Van der Waals light-emitting diodes (vdWLEDs), constructed from layered materials with the van der Waals (vdW) integration, have been intensively investigated because of their exceptional tunability and integration in next-generation on-chip optoelectronics. Despite the fact that notable advancements have been achieved in vdWLEDs, including the realization of single-photon emission (1), exciton-polariton observation (2), and centimeter-scale devices (3), they still suffer from several setbacks (4, 5). One of the bottlenecks is their low emission efficiency, of which the external quantum efficiency (EQE) barely reaches 1% at room temperature. The factors that determine the EQE of vdWLEDs include charge injection efficiency, radiative recombination efficiency, and light outcoupling efficiency, which are determined by how charge carriers are injected, how excitons recombine, and how photons escape the device, respectively. The considerably low EQEs of vdWLEDs based on transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) primarily stem from the poor photoluminescence (PL) quantum yields (QYs) of their active materials at high generation rates (6). While a combination of chemical or electrical doping (6, 7) and strain engineering (8) can improve the PLQYs of monolayer TMDCs, these methods are less effective in improving the EQE of LEDs (less than 1.2% at room temperature) (3), which may be due to the reduced charge injection efficiency after these modifications.

Layered Ruddlesden-Popper perovskites (RPPs) have garnered substantial attention owing to their unique exciton dynamics originating from inherent quantum well (QW) structures and controllable optoelectronic properties (9–11). The strong quantum confinement effect from the QW arrangement, combined with the long lifetime of charge carriers in layered RPPs (12–14), facilitates extraordinarily high intrinsic PLQYs exceeding 30% at high generation rates. In addition, the tunable bandgap of RPPs by controlling the QW width or component of organic ligands is responsible for tunable emission wavelengths (15, 16). These unique optoelectronic properties of RPPs render them potential emission materials for high-performance LEDs. However, microsized RPP-based LEDs (PeLEDs) constructed on pure single-crystalline layered RPPs have not been reported. Most reported midrosized PeLEDs are constructed based on thin films or quantum dot films via the spin-coating method (17, 18), usually leading to a degraded performance in downscaled microsized devices, which have huge potential in on-chip applications.

Here, we fabricated vdW integrated PeLEDs with the highest EQE above 15% among vdWLEDs at room temperature. A mechanically exfoliated single-crystalline RPP nanoflake was used as the active layer and sandwiched between two flakes of few-layer graphene (FLG), which were used as transparent electrodes, forming a kind of vdWLEDs. We observed strong EL emission with a low turn-on current density and a high EQE more than 10% under a direct current (DC) input. We attribute the superior performance of the PeLEDs to the inherent QW structure, high PLQYs at high generation rates of the layered RPPs, and efficient charge injection facilitated by the Fowler-Nordheim (F-N) tunneling.

RESULTS

RPPs are a kind of layered materials and adopt a general chemical formula of L2An−1BnX3n+1, where L is an organic ligand, A is a small cation, B is a divalent metal cation, X is a halide anion, and n is the number of inorganic octahedral slabs between the organic ligands (19). In our experiments, we used RPPs BA2MAn−1PbnI3n+1 (n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6) as the active layer, where BA and MA are CH3(CH2)3NH3+ and CH3NH3+, respectively. RPPs with different n were synthesized using a temperature-programmed crystallization method (see Materials and Methods for details). It has been widely recognized that conventional lithography and thermal deposition processes cause severe damage to perovskites due to their sensitivity to polar solvents (deionized water and acetone), electron beams, and high temperature (20). In our work, we adopted a dry transfer process for device fabrication as depicted in Fig. 1A. Initially, perovskite crystals were mechanically exfoliated and transferred onto the FLG, which was sitting on a Si/SiO2 substrate by a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)–assisted dry-transfer method (21) (Fig. 1B), followed by the transfer of another FLG to create the FLG/perovskite/FLG heterostructure (Fig. 1C). Last, prefabricated Au electrodes prepared via standard lithography and thermal deposition processes were transferred onto the heterostructure, completing the construction of microsized PeLEDs (22, 23). The transparent polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) layer used in the electrode-transferring process was retained on the device to encapsulate and isolate the device from the ambient environment in subsequent characterizations (Fig. 1D). Note that all fabrication processes were carried out in an argon-filled glovebox to prevent degradation. Figure 1 (B to D) presents optical images of the heterostructure with the vdW integration in each step, with the FLG, perovskite, and gold electrodes outlined in black, white, and yellow, respectively. The LED device exhibits a clean and uniform appearance without obvious blisters in the heterostructure region. A close inspection from the atomic force microscope image further verified the highly uniform and bubble-free morphology (fig. S1).

The single-crystalline feature of RPPs with different n (from n = 1 to 5) was confirmed by x-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization (24) (Fig. 1E and fig. S2) and ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption characterization (fig. S3). Figure 1F presents a cross-sectional image of the LED device captured by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). The lattice fringes of the top FLG can be roughly discerned. The sensitivity of perovskites to high-energy electrons prevented us from resolving the atomic structure of the perovskite layer (25, 26). The materials of the device from bottom to top are Si, SiO2, bottom FLG, perovskite, top FLG, and PMMA layers. Clean and flat interfaces observed in STEM implied high-quality contacts and free of contamination, which was attributed to the controlled environment and low-temperature annealing during the fabrication process. It is noteworthy that although the top and bottom FLG in the cross-sectional image are relatively thick for the illustration of the PeLED device structure, the thicknesses of FLG for electroluminescence (EL) characterization were controlled to minimize photon absorption. In our device, there are several advantages of using FLG as the electrodes: the atomically flat surface of FLG allows for vdW integration with a flat and clean interface, reducing the contact resistance (27–29); FLG serves as a low-barrier and gate-tunable vdW contact, further promoting charge carrier injection into perovskites (30); and transparent FLG reduces the absorption of light emission, increasing the light outcoupling efficiency in the single-junction sandwiched structure (31–33). Figure 1G compares the PLQYs of RPP (n = 4) and monolayer tungsten disulfide (WS2) under 532 nm excitation with different power densities (see details for the estimation of PLQYs in the Supplementary Materials). The pristine WS2 rolls off approximately two orders of magnitude due to exciton-exciton annihilation at high power densities. In contrast, the PLQY roll-off of RPPs is strongly suppressed at high generation rates (fig. S4) up to ~1022 cm−2 s−1. The lower roll off behavior and higher PLQYs as compared to WS2 is due to the low Auger non-radiative recombination efficiency of RPPs (34, 35) than WS2 (36–38). From this point, layered perovskites are better for efficient vdWLEDs at relatively high generation rates than excitonic semiconductors, such as TMDCs.

The characterization of PL and EL emission was carried out in a homemade vacuum chamber with a well-aligned optical system (fig. S5). To increase the signal collection efficiency, the optical path for signal collection was optimized as simply as possible. After we applied DC voltages to the FLG electrodes, the device exhibited nearly symmetric electrical behavior, implying symmetric contacts and resistances at top and bottom FLG/perovskite interfaces created during device fabrication (Fig. 2A). The EL and PL emissions of PeLEDs with n = 4 as a function of current were investigated. PL spectra of the device were measured as a function of voltage and current, and a negligible change in the PL signals in terms of intensity and wavelength was observed (fig. S6). In contrast to PL, EL emission only starts above a specific threshold voltage, which is attributed to the shift of the Fermi levels of the top and bottom FLG at a high voltage bias so that the charge carriers can be efficiently injected into the perovskites. Figure 2B presents the EL spectra under different currents ranging from 20 to 600 nA. All spectra have the same peak wavelength at approximately 665 nm, indicating the intact crystal structure or less halide migration of the perovskite flake at high currents. Figure 2C displays the EL intensity as a function of wavelength and current. As the current increased, no obvious broadening or peak shift was observed. EL emission became detectable with currents as low as approximately 20 nA. Benefitting from the natural multiple quantum well (MQW) structure of RPPs, PeLEDs could emit detectable EL signals at a low current density of approximately 20 pA μm−2 (fig. S7). This suggests that a higher proportion of injected charge carriers contributes to the radiative recombination processes.

The normalized integrated EL intensity as a function of current is plotted in Fig. 2D according to the spectra in Fig. 2B. The EL intensity exhibited a linear relationship with the tunneling current and then transitioned to sublinear behavior at higher tunneling currents, suggesting decreased quantum efficiency at high charge injection rates, which could be a result of Auger recombination (39–42). The microscopic images of working vdWLEDs are presented in fig. S8, showing the roughly uniform EL emission at the heterostructure region. EQE is an important figure of merit to evaluate the performance of vdWLEDs, defined as the number of emitted photons over the number of injected charge carriers. By meticulously calibrating the loss of the signal from the device to the spectrometer, we were able to accurately measure the EQE of PeLEDs (see details for the calibration of EQE in the Supplementary Materials). In total, at least 35 devices were fabricated with different thicknesses of the active perovskite layer, ranging from 26 to 200 nm (fig. S9). In fig. S10, we presented the EL emission spectra at different currents and the device images of some workable vdWLEDs. Most of the devices exhibited single peaks around 660 nm, indicating the pure RPP single crystal in the device. The EQEs of all PeLEDs at room temperature as a function of generation rate are presented (fig. S12A). With increasing driving current, most PeLEDs exhibit elevated EQEs, which is similar to the trend of PLQY when increasing the generation rate (Fig. 1G and fig. S4). This is due to the fact that nonradiative traps are saturated by the near-free charge carriers in the large-n perovskites and then radiative recombination becomes dominant (12). The distribution of the maximal EQE at room temperature is plotted in fig. S12B, with the EQE for the majority of devices remaining at approximately 5% and the highest is beyond 15%. Our microsized vdWLEDs exhibited higher EQE than TMDC-based LEDs, marking the highest room-temperature EQE over all the reported works of vdWLEDs to date (Fig. 2E). Note that the values of EQE (~15%) and PLQY (~35%) in our PeLEDs implies that the outcoupling efficiency is ~40%. This value is comparable to the simulated outcoupling efficiency (fig. S11), which is simulated by the three-dimensional (3D) finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method. Meanwhile, the outcoupling efficiency is reasonable for a straightforward single-junction vdW structure that only a small portion of the emitted photons would be reflected or absorbed.

It has been reported that the EQE of QW structure-based LEDs is greatly enhanced when increasing the number of QWs from single to multiple wells (43). However, we did not observe such dependence on the thickness of the perovskites (fig. S13A), which is attributed to the fact that the perovskites in all of our LEDs have over five layers, giving rise to the weak independence of the EQE on the thickness of the perovskite layer. In addition, variation in the thickness of FLG among devices led to different outcoupling efficiencies, further contributing to the EQE variability. The impact of perovskite thickness on the threshold voltage (VTH) is also studied, and we summarize the VTH of PeLEDs as a function of perovskite thickness in fig. S13B. VTH is defined as the lowest voltage required for the device to emit detectable signals. Owing to the single-crystalline property of perovskites, VTH exhibits a linear relationship to the perovskite thickness. We emphasize that the linear relation is only maintained for perovskite thickness in the F-N tunneling regime as we will discuss later.

Because of the high sensitivity of organic–inorganic hybrid perovskites, ensuring the stability of PeLEDs is a crucial challenge to address for practical applications. During fabrication and characterization, measures were taken to mitigate environmental influence, including fabricating in an argon-filled glovebox, using PMMA as the encapsulation layer, and measuring in a high-vacuum chamber. The integrated EL intensities of four PeLEDs over time are illustrated in fig. S14. PeLEDs operated normally for approximately 11 min averagely at a continuous current density of ~1.0 nA μm−2. Three devices showed stable EL intensity before sudden breakdown, indicating that the damage probably be attributed to electrical breakdown in the high electric field rather than the slow degradation during the operation. Further optimizations are required to address the stability issue.

We show in Fig. 3A and fig. S15 the electrical behavior of the PeLEDs measured with n = 4 and different perovskite thicknesses. By applying voltage to the FLG electrodes, electrons and holes from the FLG tunnel through the surface layers of butylammonium (BA) organic chains and are subsequently injected into the QW structure (30). These devices exhibited conspicuous thickness-dependent I-V behavior in which the threshold voltages varied with thickness. We investigated the tunneling mechanism over a wide range of perovskite thicknesses. As illustrated in Fig. 3B, for the 38- and 50-nm perovskites, the tunneling currents could be well described by the F-N tunneling model, Id~E2 exp (−α/E), where Id is the tunneling current, E is the electric field applied to the perovskite, and α is a coefficient related to the effective electron mass and potential barrier height. These curves closely overlap in the tunneling region, indicating nearly identical barrier heights and effective electron masses. According to the slope of the linear fitting curve and the effective mass of electrons in perovskite, the calculated injection barrier height is approximately 0.10 eV at room temperature (see details for the estimation in the Supplementary Materials). This extracted energy barrier is independent of the perovskite thickness for devices exhibiting F-N tunneling behavior and close to the predicted difference between the Fermi levels of FLG and the band edges of perovskites (30). The qualitative agreement with the F-N mechanism indicates that the potential bias across RPP is sufficient to drive electrons and holes to tunnel through the barrier and inject into perovskites where they form excitons.

Figure 3C illustrates the band diagrams of the FLG/perovskite/FLG heterostructure in unbiased and biased states. Unlike typical 2D quantum-well LEDs that use hexagonal boron nitride as the potential barrier, the intrinsic organic and inorganic layers in layered RPPs act as potential barriers and wells, forming the natural MQW structure in PeLEDs. At a certain voltage bias, the Fermi level of the top FLG (positive bias) was shifted beyond the conduction band minimum of the inorganic potential well so that the electrons could tunnel into the perovskite layer, and vice versa for the bottom FLG (ground). Because of the low potential barrier for charge injection at the interface, electrons and holes from FLG can sufficiently tunnel into the perovskite and be trapped in the inorganic wells even without specific electron- or hole-blocking layers owing to the inherent MQW structure of RPPs.

Benefitting from the straightforward single-junction vdW heterostructure, other types of layered RPPs could be used to construct PeLEDs through the same fabrication process. In our experiments, we fabricated PeLEDs using RPPs BA2MAn−1PbnI3n+1 with n = 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6. With increasing n, the PL peak position shifts from ~595 nm (n = 2) to ~710 nm (n = 6). Figure 3 (D and E) presents the normalized PL and EL spectra of PeLEDs with different n values. The EL peak positions coincide with those of PL, implying that the EL emission arises from the radiative recombination of electron-hole pairs of perovskites rather than the thermal blackbody emission. A broadening of the EL and PL spectra was observed. This is attributed to the activation of the disordering of organic cations due to electrical injection, similar to the broadening of PL induced by laser annealing (24). EL emission for PeLEDs with n = 1 was not observed, which may result from the low charge carrier mobility and high potential barrier at the interface resulting from the large bandgap of the perovskite with n = 1.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we realized the microsized PeLEDs based on single-crystalline layered RPPs. Our PeLEDs have a single-junction FLG/perovskite/FLG structure with vdW integration. The devices exhibit a low turn-on current density of ~20 pA cm−2 and a high EQE exceeding 10% at room temperature, establishing a benchmark for evaluating the EQE of vdWLEDs. Efficient light emission is enabled by the inherent MQW structure, high PLQYs of RPPs at high injection rates, and a low energy barrier of approximately 0.10 eV for charge carrier injection at the interface of the FLG/perovskite facilitated by the F-N tunneling mechanism. The construction of PeLEDs is versatile and can be extended to other layered perovskites. Our work demonstrates a method for realizing an efficient perovskite-based vdWLED, providing an opportunity for high-performance on-chip optoelectronics.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

RPP synthesis

The RPPs were prepared via a temperature-programmed solution precipitation method. For n = 1, PbO (0.57 M) and BAI (0.57 M) precursors were dissolved in a concentrated HI and H3PO2 mixture (10:1, vol/vol) in an Ar-filled glove box. For n = 2, PbO (0.59 M), BAI (0.43 M), and MAI (0.31 M) precursors were dissolved in a concentrated HI and H3PO2 mixture (9:1, vol/vol) in an Ar-filled glove box. For n = 3, PbO (0.59 M), BAI (0.19 M), and MAI (0.40 M) precursors were dispersed in a concentrated HI and H3PO2 mixture (9:1, vol/vol) in an Ar-filled glove box. For n = 4, PbO (0.69 M), BAI (0.17 M), and MAI (0.52 M) precursors were dispersed in a concentrated HI and H3PO2 mixture (7.6:1, vol/vol) in an Ar-filled glove box. Then, all the solutions were heated at 90°C with stirring for 120 min to give a clear yellow solution. The solution was quickly transferred to an oven at 90°C for 60 min and then allowed to cool to room temperature at a rate of 1°C/hour, whereupon perovskite crystals started to form. The crystals were isolated by vacuum filtration and dried in an Ar-filled vacuum chamber at room temperature. All reagents and reactants were used without further purification unless stated otherwise. PbO (99.999%), HI [57 weight % (wt %)], and H3PO2 (50 wt %) were purchased from Aladdin. MAI (99.5%) and BAI (99.0%) were purchased from Xi’an Yuri Solar Co. Ltd.

Device fabrication

To prevent perovskite flakes from the ambient environment, all device fabrication processes were carried out in an argon-filled glovebox. The LED device was made of bottom FLG/perovskite/top FLG on a Si/SiO2 substrate. Initially, the FLG flake was mechanically exfoliated on the Si/SiO2 substrate. Subsequently, the exfoliated perovskite with a proper thickness on the PDMS was dry transferred onto the graphene flake, followed by the transfer of another FLG flake onto the perovskite to complete the FLG/perovskite/FLG vertical heterostructure. To avoid damage to the heterostructure during the conventional fabrication of metal electrodes, all the Au electrodes were prefabricated and then dry transferred onto the sample at 60°C. The PMMA layer was used in the transfer process of the Au electrodes and was coated on the device as the encapsulation layer after device fabrication. After that, the device was annealed on a hotplate in a glovebox at 60°C for 10 min to enhance the interface contact. Last, the device was adhered and wire bonded to a chip carrier for subsequent characterization.

EL characterization

The LED characterization was carried out in a custom vacuum chamber with a well-aligned optical system. A voltage was applied via a Keithley 4200 semiconductor parameter analyzer, and the spectra were collected via a Horiba iHR550 spectrometer. To increase the collection efficiency, the optical path for signal collection was optimized as simply as possible (fig. S5). All the characterizations were completed under vacuum at a pressure of approximately 10−6 torr at room temperature.

Optical and structural characterizations

PL spectra were collected using the same setup used for EL characterization with a 532-nm continuous-wave laser. The thickness and morphology of the LED devices were measured via a Bruker Dimension ICON. XRD spectra were collected by means of a Rigaku SmartLab instrument. UV-Vis absorption spectra were collected with a HITACHI UV-3600 plus. Absorption was calculated based on the reflection and transmission spectra collected via a WITEC 3000RA and a home-made optical setup. STEM imaging was performed using a Titan Cubed Themis G2 300 STEM instrument (200 keV) and a JEOL JEM-ARM300CF S/STEM system (300 keV).

Fabrication of Rhodamine B film

We mixed 1 mg of Rhodamine B in 20 ml of PMMA, and then spin coated one drop of the mixture on the silicon substrate three times (3000 r/min, 30 s), followed by baking at 120°C for 2 min.

Integrating sphere setup

The absolute PLQY measurements were determined using the integrating sphere accessory (Edinburgh Instruments, FLS1000) with a detector (Edinburgh Instruments, PMT-900). The following are the relative parameters for the calibration: photomultiplier: PMT-900; spectral range: < 200 to 870 nm; dark count rate: < 50 cps (−20°C); response width: 600 ps; excitation sources: 450 W xenon arc lamp; spectral range: 200 to 1000 nm.

FDTD simulations

A perfect matching layer and full-wave incidence were applied to the modeling device configuration. The power fraction radiated to the free space was simulated using the reported optical constants, including the refractive index and absorption coefficient. The power fraction radiated to the free space was simulated by the following refractive index Si: 3.8365 + 0.0154i, SiO2: 1.4563, FLG: 2.7529 + 1.3897i, PVK: 2.3927 + 0.2940i, PMMA: 1.4880. The thicknesses of Si, SiO2, bottom FLG, RPPs (n = 4), top FLG, and PMMA are set to be 500 μm, 285 nm, 5 nm, 50 to 100 nm, 4 nm, and 500 nm, respectively.
