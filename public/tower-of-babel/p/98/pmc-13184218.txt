Machine learning integration of multi-omics data develops a sarcomatoid-related renal cell carcinoma score (SARS) for prognosis stratification and guiding therapy
Authors: Dingbang Liu, Xiaozhi Xia, Wei Wang, Tao Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) exhibits significant molecular heterogeneity, which limits the benefit of immunotherapy and underscores the urgent need for robust predictive biomarkers. The highly aggressive sarcomatoid phenotype, with its distinct treatment responses, provides a promising biological foundation for model development. This study aimed to develop and validate a machine-learning-based multi-omics score derived from sarcomatoid-associated genes [sarcomatoid-associated renal cell carcinoma score (SARS)] for prognostic stratification and prediction of therapeutic efficacy across different treatment modalities in advanced ccRCC.MethodsA machine learning ensemble algorithm was applied to sarcomatoid-associated genes to develop the SARS. The model stratification power was validated for overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) across IMmotion151, CheckMate025, and JAVELIN Renal 101 trials. Multi-omics analyses, including single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), spatial transcriptomics, and metabolic flux inference, were used to decipher the associated tumor biology, tumor microenvironment (TME), and metabolic reprogramming.ResultsThe SARS model robustly stratified OS and PFS in both the training and external validation cohorts. SARS effectively stratified patients into SARS-high and SARS-low groups across all cohorts for both OS and PFS, and stratified the efficacy of monotherapy and combination therapy. Multi-omics characterization revealed that SARS-high tumors exhibited enhanced proliferative capacity, metabolism reprogramming, and an immunosuppressive TME. We further identified DRAP1 as a core gene within the SARS signature. Spatial transcriptomics confirmed DRAP1’s specific overexpression in malignant regions, and single-cell analyses indicated its critical role in intercellular communication and early tumor progression.ConclusionsThe SARS model serves as a powerful and integrative biomarker for prognostic stratification and therapeutic guidance in advanced ccRCC. DRAP1 is a potential novel therapeutic target, while further studies are needed to elucidate its underlying mechanisms.Keywords: Machine learning, immunotherapy, sarcomatoid, clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), multi-omics

Highlight box

Key findings

• Sarcomatoid-associated renal cell carcinoma score (SARS), a machine-learning multi-omics score, predicts prognosis and stratifies immunotherapy/targeted therapy responses in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) across three phase III trials. DRAP1 is identified as a core gene overexpressed in malignant regions.

What is known and what is new?

• ccRCC heterogeneity limits immunotherapy; existing models mainly predict prognosis.

• SARS is externally validated to predict both prognosis and efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors, anti-angiogenics, mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors, and combination therapy, supported by multi-omics and spatial transcriptomics.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• SARS may guide personalized therapy. DRAP1 is a novel target. Prospective validation needed.

Introduction

While a 33% decline in U.S. cancer deaths since 1991 highlights progress, recent trends reveal a shifting burden characterized by rising incidence among younger populations and persistent disparities across racial and socioeconomic groups. These dynamics underscore why biomarker-driven stratification is now more critical than ever. Precision oncology, anchored in robust biomarkers, enables early interception, tailored therapeutic selection, and equitable delivery of care (1). Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most prevalent histologic subtype of renal cell carcinoma (RCC), accounting for 70–80% of all new RCC diagnoses, which exhibits remarkably molecular heterogeneity (2,3). Given the highly vascular nature of ccRCC, anti-angiogenic agents became a cornerstone of first-line treatment for metastatic ccRCC over the last two decades (4). Recently, the integration of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), as monotherapy or combined with anti-angiogenic agents, has improved clinical outcomes (5). However, significant intra- or inter-tumor heterogeneity and the lack of prognostic biomarkers currently limit the benefit of immunotherapy (6). Consequently, the discovery and validation of robust biomarkers to stratify therapeutic response to ICIs represent an urgent priority for personalized and optimal management strategies in advanced ccRCC.

The ideal biomarker should be uniformly expressed across and within tumor tissues, so that it can perform well in all patients despite the complex heterogeneity of ccRCC. Thus, multigene models may be a promising way to address this heterogeneity. However, a key limitation of existing multigene models in ccRCC is their focus only on stratifying patient prognosis, rather than exploring the efficacy stratification across different therapeutic arms by using clinical cohorts as testing sets (7,8). Additionally, in earlier clinical cohort studies, prognostic signatures (e.g., the angiogenesis genes) were only effective in evaluating the efficacy of a single therapeutic strategy within clinical cohorts comprising multiple treatment arms (9).

In ccRCC, sarcomatoid features represent an extreme manifestation of this heterogeneity and are one of the most aggressive tumor phenotypes (10-12). Sarcomatoid features are not an independent histological subtype but rather a dedifferentiated state that emerges during ccRCC progression (13). RCC with sarcomatoid features (sRCC) is characteristically associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), extremely high proliferative potential, and dysregulated activation of the cell cycle (14,15). This highly malignant transformation not only drives local invasion and distant metastasis but also profoundly remodels the tumor microenvironment (TME). Furthermore, retrospective analyses of key clinical trial cohorts have revealed that tumors containing sarcomatoid components exhibit unique patterns of response to treatment (16,17). This strongly suggests that molecular signatures based on the biology of sarcomatoid features hold promise as powerful tools for discriminating the efficacy of different treatment strategies.

This study employed a machine learning combinatorial algorithm to systematically screen clinical cohort data from CheckMate025, IMmotion151, and JAVELIN Renal 101 based on sarcomatoid-related genes, establishing a risk model for discriminating prognosis and therapeutic efficacy across different treatments. By integrating multi-omics data, including transcriptomic, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and spatial transcriptomic data, we systematically investigated the tumor biological behavior, metabolic reprogramming, and TME characteristics associated with this risk model. Building on these findings, we further identified DRAP1 as a core gene of the model and conducted an in-depth evaluation of its role in tumor progression. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2025-1-905/rc).

Methods

Data source

This integrated analysis utilized transcriptomic data and clinical information from three randomized controlled trials (RCTs) focusing on ccRCC. The first, the CheckMate025 trial (NCT01668784) (18), was a phase III trial comparing nivolumab to everolimus in patients with advanced ccRCC who had received prior therapy. Data also came from the IMmotion151 trial (NCT02420821) (19), a phase III study that evaluated atezolizumab plus bevacizumab versus sunitinib as a first-line treatment for metastatic ccRCC. Additionally, the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial (NCT02684006) (20), another phase III study, assessed the efficacy of first-line avelumab plus axitinib versus sunitinib in treatment-naive patients with advanced ccRCC. All transcriptomic data and clinical information were obtained from the supplementary materials and public repositories associated with their respective primary publications. We also used the KIRC_GSE171306 dataset for single-cell analysis and GSE175540-GSM5924038 data for spatial transcriptome analysis, which were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Machine learning ensemble algorithm-based prognostic model construction

Sarcomatoid differentiation for the CheckMate025 cohort was obtained from the clinical annotation files, which were determined by centralized pathological review as reported in the original study. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between sarcomatoid and non-sarcomatoid ccRCC were first identified from CheckMate025 cohort transcriptomic data (|log2fold change (FC)| >0.5 and adjusted P value <0.05). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was then employed to cluster genes into co-expression modules. Module-trait associations between module genes and sarcomatoid phenotype were analyzed. The overlapping genes between DEGs and the module showing the strongest association with the sarcomatoid trait were subsequently used for model construction. CheckMate025 (n=311) cohort was used as a training set while IMmotion151 (n=823) and JAVELIN Renal 101 (n=726) databases were used as external testing sets. The expression profile matrices of the overlapping genes in each cohort, as well as the PFS survival information, serve as the original data for model input. The 10 machine learning algorithms were selected based on their complementary strengths in handling high-dimensional omics data and survival outcomes. Cox-based models [CoxBoost, elastic network (Enet), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso), Ridge, stepwise Cox, partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox)] provide interpretable feature selection and regularization; tree-based methods [random survival forest (RSF) and generalized boosted regression modelling (GBM)] capture nonlinear interactions; and kernel-based approaches [survival support vector machine (survival-SVM) and supervised principal components (SuperPC)] address complex, high-dimensional patterns. Combining these models through an ensemble strategy improves robustness and generalizability by integrating diverse perspectives and reducing reliance on any single algorithm. Then, 101 algorithm combinations were applied to overlapping genes, a model was constructed based on the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework to actively mitigate overfitting (21,22), which were generated using the “Mime” R package (detailed documentation and hyperparameter settings are publicly available at: https://github.com/l-magnificence/Mime). The concordance index (C-index) of each model was calculated, which reflected the accuracy of the models (23). Model had the highest C-index was named as sarcomatoid-associated RCC score (SARS).

Validation of SARS

Based on the median SARS, patients in training and testing cohorts were divided into SARS-high and SARS-low groups. With obtaining the overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) data of different cohorts, Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves were plotted. Moreover, subgroup analyses were conducted based on different treatment arms to explore the efficacy discrimination of SARS for immunotherapy and targeted therapy. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed with the “timeROC” package to calculate the area under the curve (AUC). In addition, the calibration curve described the differences between SARS stratified probabilities and actual probabilities at different time points, which was plotted by the “rms” package. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to compare the clinical benefit relationship between SARS and all positive or all negative.

Single-cell analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq was employed to analyze cellular heterogeneity within the TME, allowing us to pinpoint malignant clusters and characterize cell-type-specific expression patterns of model genes. Using “Seurat” (v4.3.0), the KIRC_GSE171306 data were performed quality control [unique molecular identifier (UMI) count per cell >1,000, cell number >1,000, and gene number per cell >500], batch effect removal, cell clustering, and cell-type annotation. By calculating SARS of malignant clusters based on scRNA-seq, malignant clusters were divided into SARS-high (C9, C11, and C12) and SARS-low clusters (C6 and C15). The impact of SARS on the biological behavior of malignant cells was evaluated through differential analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). The stacked violin plot showed the expression patterns of model genes in different cell types.

Univariate Cox regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between the expression of 19 model genes and PFS of CheckMate025. DRAP1 was identified as the most significant risk factor. Then, malignant cells were stratified into DRAP1-positive (DRAP1+) and DRAP1-negative (DRAP1−) subsets. “CellChat” was employed to dissect the cell-cell communication landscape, comparing the outgoing and incoming interaction strengths of all major cell types and mapping the specific communication networks. Cellular trajectory of all malignant cells was reconstructed using “monocle2”, ordering cells by pseudotime and partitioning them into distinct states. Then, quantitatively compare the expression of DRAP1 along pseudotime.

Transcriptome analysis of CheckMate025

Bulk RNA-seq was used to capture genome-wide expression profiles to explain the biological characteristics of the sarcomatoid phenotype. According to SARS, patients of CheckMate025 were classified as SARS-high and SARS-low groups. DEGs between the SARS-high and SARS-low groups were obtained by the “Limma” package. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis was conducted with |log2FC| >0.5 and adjusted P value <0.05 DEGs. GSEA analysis was based on hallmark and all canonical pathways. Furthermore, we conducted a single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) to compare pathway enrichment profiles between SARS-high and SARS-low groups.

“METAFlux” was used to predict the abundance of metabolites, and the orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) method was used to identify key metabolites (24,25). The S-plot illustrates correlations between various metabolites and SARS. Then, the relative abundance patterns of SARS-associated metabolites were visualized using a Z-score plot.

Tracking tumor immunophenotype (TIP) explored the correlation between SARS and the anti-cancer immune cycle (26). Two algorithms were used to analyze immune infiltration and TME, including “ImmuCellAI” and “xCell”. All the above analyses were based on the expression profile of CheckMate025 (27).

Spatial transcriptome analysis of GSE175540-GSM5924038

Spatial transcriptomics was applied to preserve the architectural context of gene expression, providing direct visualization of DRAP1 localization within malignant regions and validating its spatial association with tumor boundaries. The expression pattern of DRAP1 was visualized and quantified across the spatial transcriptome dataset. Cell type annotation was performed using the “Seurat” package and the “SCTransform” function (28). Spots were subsequently classified into malignant (Mal), non-malignant (nMal), and boundary (Bdy) regions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0). Unless otherwise specified, two-sided P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The specific statistical methods applied in each analysis are described in the corresponding subsections above.

Module-trait association analysis further identified the red module as having the strongest correlation with sarcomatoid phenotype (Figure 2C). Evaluation of SARS

To evaluate the risk stratification performance of SARS, CheckMate025 cohort (n=311) served as the training set, while IMmotion151 (n=823) and JAVELIN Renal 101 (n=726) databases were used as external testing sets. In the training set, SARS performed robust risk stratification for both OS and PFS, as demonstrated by K-M curves (all P<0.001, Figure 2G,2H). When cohorts were divided into subgroups based on treatment arms, therapeutic response was discriminated in both ICIs therapy (nivolumab; P<0.001, Figure 2I,2J) and mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor therapy (everolimus; P<0.001, Figure 2K,2L). In the external validation sets, SARS also effectively discriminated prognosis, with significant PFS stratification observed in the IMmotion151 cohort (P<0.001, Figure 2M). However, SARS did not perform well in discriminating PFS among patients receiving combination therapy in the IMmotion151 cohort (P=0.08, Figure 2N), although it was able to distinguish outcomes in those treated with anti‑angiogenic agents (sunitinib; P<0.001, Figure 2O). Similarly, in the JAVELIN Renal 101 cohort, SARS stratified PFS in the overall cohort (P=0.01, Figure 2P) as well as in patients receiving combination therapy (P<0.01, Figure 2Q), but its performance was suboptimal in those treated with sunitinib (P=0.11, Figure 2R).

To further assess predictive accuracy and clinical utility, time-dependent ROC analyses were performed. In the CheckMate025 training cohort, the SARS model yielded high AUC values for both PFS (Figure 3A) and OS (Figure 3B). Consistent performance was observed in external testing sets, with 1-year AUCs of 0.681 for IMmotion151 (Figure 3C) and 0.601 for JAVELIN Renal 101 (Figure 3D). Furthermore, DCA demonstrated the clinical utility of our model across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Figure 3E), and the calibration curve revealed excellent agreement between model-stratified risk and observed survival probabilities (Figure 3F). Overall, SARS exhibited excellent accuracy and robustness in prognostic performance and treatment evaluation, which enabled its reliable application in guiding clinical decision-making.

SARS associated with tumor biological behaviors

To investigate the mechanism underlying the stratified effect of SARS, we first used the KIRC_GSE171306 dataset to explore the effect of SARS on tumor biological behaviors at the single-cell level. The expression patterns of all model genes across different cell types were visualized using a stacked violin plot (Figure 3G). The results showed that most model genes, such as TMSB10 and DRAP1, were significantly expressed in malignant cells. The dataset was processed using Seurat (v4.3.0) for quality control, clustering, and cell-type annotation (Figure 3H). Among a total of 25 clusters of cells (C0–C24), 5 clusters (C6, C9, C11, C12, and C15) were identified as malignant cell. We calculated the SARS of malignant clusters based on scRNA-seq, and classified malignant clusters into SARS-high clusters (C9, C11, and C12) and SARS-low clusters (C6 and C15) according to their score (Figure 3I). GSEA analysis based on DEGs (Figure 3J-3L) of SARS-high compared to SARS-low malignant clusters suggested that “Glycolysis”, “MYC Targets V1”, and “G2 M checkpoints” were enriched in SARS-high clusters, which indicated that the proliferative capacity of tumor cellular components was activated. In contrast, the “Angiogenesis” pathway was concurrently found to be enriched in the SARS-low group.

Based on our findings regarding the association between SARS and biological behaviors at the single-cell level, we proceeded to validate these observations using bulk RNA-seq from CheckMate025. First, DEGs between the SARS-high and SARS-low groups were obtained (Figure 4A). GO/KEGG analysis based on bulk RNA-seq data was conducted with |log2FC| >0.5 and adjusted P value <0.05 DEGs, revealing significant enrichment of immune-related pathways in the SARS-high group, including “PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer”, “immune response-regulating signaling pathway”, and “activation of immune response” (Figure 4B). These pathways collectively pointed to a state of immune checkpoint activation, which can render cytotoxic immune responses ineffective against the tumor. GSEA analysis based on hallmark and all canonical pathways revealed that cell proliferation-related pathways were upregulated in the SARS-high group, such as “E2F targets”, “glycolysis”, and “mitotic G2/M phases” (Figure 4C,4D), which was consistent with the enrichment analysis at the single-cell level. Notably, ssGSEA revealed an elevated enrichment of the angiogenesis pathway in the SARS-low group (Figure 4E). This observation aligned with our prior findings at the single-cell level (Figure 3K) and suggested that SARS-low tumors may be particularly dependent on angiogenesis. This may account for the superior response of this patient subgroup to anti-angiogenic targeted therapy.

SARS correlated with tumor metabolic reprogramming

Enrichment analyses at the single-cell level indicated that the glycolysis pathway was upregulated in the SARS-high group, so it was necessary to further explore the alterations in metabolism of ccRCC related to SARS. METAFlux is a computational framework to predict metabolite flux dynamics across different biological conditions by integrating multi-omics data. By applying this method, we predicted the abundance of metabolites based on transcriptome expression profiles of the CheckMate025 cohort. The OPLS-DA statistical method was used to obtain metabolite differences and identify key metabolites between the SARS-high and SARS-low groups. The variable importance in projection (VIP) reflected the importance of metabolites to grouping. On the basis of S-plot, glutamine, arginine, phenylalanine, and methionine were enriched in the SARS-high group, while acetate, glycerol, and succinate were enriched in the SARS-low group (Figure 4F). The Z-score plot (Figure 4G) also confirmed the elevated expression of aforementioned metabolites in the SARS-high group. Glutamine is an essential energy source and vital supplier of carbon and nitrogen for biosynthesis of tumor (29,30). Elevated glutamine level provided a critical metabolic basis for the highly aggressive phenotype seen in the SARS-high group. Phenylalanine facilitated immunosuppression by promoting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (31), while arginine and methionine are also implicated in T cell suppression (32-34). In the SARS-high group, alterations in these metabolites may be associated with altered TME and contribute to immune escape.

SARS-high correlated with enhanced immune infiltration and TME remodeling

The metabolite alterations in the SARS-high group, suggestive of TME remodeling, prompted us to conduct a deeper analysis of tumor immunity. Therefore, based on the RNA expression matrix of the CheckMate025 cohort, we explored the correlation between SARS and the anti-cancer immune cycle. The TIP plot showed that the SARS-high group exhibited enhanced T-cell recruiting and infiltration of immune cells into tumors (Figure 5A). Although T cells in the SARS-high group exhibited a more proliferating and cytotoxic state, they were also characterized by a higher prevalence of both exhaustion and regulation (Figure 5B). Then, we calculated the infiltration of immune cells through two algorithms, including ImmuCellAI and xCell (Figure 5C,5D). The SARS-high group exhibited elevated immune infiltration in total. Antigen-presenting cells (APCs), including dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages, were more abundant in the SARS-high group, which indicated higher immunogenicity. Notably, we found that natural regulatory T cells (nTregs) and induced regulatory T cells (iTregs) (Figure 5D) had increased infiltration in the SARS-high group. Enriched regulatory T cells (Tregs) acted in concert to disrupt the function of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and directly suppressed anti-tumor immunity (35). The co-enrichment and synergistic immunosuppressive network compellingly explained the heightened malignancy and potential immunotherapy resistance observed in the SARS-high group.

Screening of core gene and spatial transcriptome analysis

To find a key target of SARS, we used univariate Cox regression analysis to evaluate the association between 19 model genes and PFS of CheckMate025. DRAP1 [hazard ratio (HR) =1.333, P=0.004] was identified as the most significant risk factor (Figure 5E). DRAP1 can bind to Dr1 and enhance its transcriptional repression activity (36). Previous studies have suggested that DRAP1 can enhance the activity of mTOR, thereby promoting the development of triple-negative breast cancer (37). Additionally, DRAP1 has a certain prognostic effect on ccRCC (38), but in-depth exploration and mechanistic elucidation are still lacking. To further investigate its expression and role in ccRCC, we performed spatial transcriptome analysis on dataset GSE175540-GSM5924038. Figure 5F showed the expression patterns of DRAP1. Then, spots were annotated into cell types and then categorized into Mal, nMal, and Bdy regions (Figure 5G-5I). Subsequent quantitative comparison of DRAP1 expression across these regions revealed significantly higher levels in Mal areas compared to others (P<0.001) (Figure 5J,5K), suggesting that DRAP1, as a core gene of the SARS model, may play an important role in ccRCC pathogenesis. Additionally, in malignant cells, DRAP1+ is observed at a markedly higher frequency than negative expression based on the KIRC_GSE171306 scRNA-seq dataset (Figure 6A).

Cell-cell communication and cell trajectory analysis

To delineate the specific role of DRAP1 in ccRCC, we performed cell–cell communication analysis comparing DRAP1+ and DRAP1− malignant cells. Outgoing and incoming interaction analysis (Figure 6B) identified DRAP1+ malignant cells as possessing higher outgoing and incoming interaction strength than DRAP1−. Further analysis of cell-cell communication networks revealed that, compared to DRAP1− malignant cells, DRAP1+ exhibited significantly stronger interactions with endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and monocytes/macrophages (Figure 6C,6D). Further analysis of cell-cell communication networks revealed that DRAP1+ malignant cells play a multifaceted role in the IGF and IGFBP signaling pathway (Figure 6E,6F). Previous studies have identified that IGFBP pathways play a critical role in ccRCC progression, contributing to RCC metastasis (39,40). In addition, DRAP1+ malignant cells and monocytes/macrophages demonstrated a strong role in the CXCL signaling pathway (Figure 6G). CXCL chemokines can make a notable effect on the progression and growth of tumors (41), and CXCL5 has been found to induce the transformation from normal to tumor-related fibroblast to facilitate tumor growth in ccRCC (42).

To investigate the role of DRAP1 in tumor malignant cell development, we performed cell trajectory analysis. Malignant cells were first classified into five distinct states (Figure 6H), and a developmental trajectory was constructed along pseudotime (Figure 6I). Figure 6J illustrates the distribution of pseudotime values across these five states. Dynamic expression profiling of DRAP1 along pseudotime (Figure 6K) showed a significant decrease in its expression during late-stage progression, suggesting that DRAP1 plays a more critical role in early-stage malignant cells. In summary, the important role of the core model gene, DRAP1, in ccRCC pathogenesis underscored the biological relevance and validity of our SARS model.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and rigorously validated a novel multigene signature, the SARS, to stratify prognosis and therapeutic efficacy in advanced ccRCC. We established a model that robustly discriminated both OS and PFS across IMmotion151, CheckMate025, and JAVELIN Renal 101 cohorts. Furthermore, the model effectively distinguished differential responses to ICIs (nivolumab), anti-angiogenic agents (sunitinib), mTOR inhibitors (everolimus), and combination therapy. Multi-omics analyses revealed that SARS was closely associated with enhanced proliferative activity, metabolic reprogramming, and an immunosuppressive TME. We also identified DRAP1 as a core gene within the model and validated its potential role in ccRCC malignant progression through both single-cell and spatial transcriptome analyses.

The SARS model was developed using clinical cohorts derived from RCTs, endowing it with direct potential to inform clinical decision-making. Unlike existing models (e.g., glycosylation-related genes, ferroptosis-related genes) (43,44), SARS was based on sarcomatoid phenotype, one of the most established and powerful marks of poor prognosis (45,46). This foundation provided the model with a robust biological basis. Compared with recent ccRCC prognostic models (47-49), SARS shares the goal of transcriptome-based risk stratification but differs fundamentally in its development and validation framework. Key distinctions include the exclusive use of phase III RCT datasets and the demonstration of prognostic performance in patients actually treated with approved immunotherapy and targeted therapy regimens, rather than relying on purely in silico treatment-response stratification. The SARS model exemplifies the evolving paradigm of biomarker-driven cancer care by translating sarcomatoid-associated molecular signatures into a clinically actionable prognostic tool (50). When evaluated against contemporary frameworks for machine learning-based transcriptomic biomarkers (51), the SARS model satisfies key recommended elements, including independent external validation and biological plausibility. However, prospective validation in well-designed cohort studies or biomarker-driven clinical trials remains an essential next step prior to broad clinical implementation.

Multi-omics analyses elucidated the biological mechanisms underlying the risk stratification and guiding capacity of SARS. SARS-high tumors molecularly reproduced the hallmarks of sRCC, characterized by heightened cell proliferation (e.g., E2F and MYC targets) and metabolic reprogramming (upregulated glycolysis). The enrichment of the angiogenesis pathway in the SARS-low group provided a plausible explanation for the insensitivity of the SARS-high group to anti-angiogenic agents such as sunitinib, which was consistent with sRCC (52). More interestingly, although SARS-high tumors exhibited significant immune infiltration, they established a functionally impaired TME through multiple immunosuppressive mechanisms, including Treg enrichment, T cell exhaustion, and metabolic suppression via specific amino acid accumulation. This finding offered a compelling explanation for the limited benefit these patients derived from ICIs therapy despite high immune infiltration, aligning with the established clinical behavior of sRCC (53,54).

Based on Cox regression analysis, we further identified DRAP1 as a key gene. The exploration of DRAP1 served as a hypothesis-generating biological extension of the multigene SARS model, rather than a replacement, aimed at uncovering potential mechanistic drivers underlying the signature’s prognostic capacity. Spatial transcriptome revealed DRAP1’s specific overexpression in malignant regions, strongly supporting its direct role in driving aggressive phenotypes. Cell-cell communication analysis demonstrated that DRAP1+ malignant cells exhibited stronger signaling strength, suggesting their function as active signaling hubs in remodeling TME. Cell trajectory analysis indicated high DRAP1 expression during early malignant progression, implying its potential role in initiating or maintaining a highly invasive cell state. These results not only explained the strong prognostic capacity of SARS but also established DRAP1 as a functionally important player in ccRCC, warranting further mechanistic investigation.

There are several limitations in this study. First, its retrospective design limits evidence strength. Second, direct quantitative comparison with prior ccRCC prognostic signatures was not feasible, as existing models were developed on non-trial cohorts and not validated on our RCT datasets. Third, due to limited PFS follow-up in advanced ccRCC trials, time-dependent AUC could not be robustly estimated beyond 2 years. Fourth, while DRAP1 was identified as a top risk-associated gene, its precise molecular mechanisms require further in vitro and in vivo validation. Fifth, the clinical implementability of SARS, including assay feasibility, cost-effectiveness, and integration into diagnostic workflows, remains unaddressed; prospective trials are needed to establish its real-time utility for treatment guidance.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have established SARS as a powerful and integrative biomarker for risk stratification and treatment stratification in advanced ccRCC. It effectively captures core aspects of tumor aggressiveness, including proliferative activity, metabolic reprogramming, and an immunosuppressive TME. Furthermore, our study nominated DRAP1 as a potential novel therapeutic target, while its precise mechanistic role requires in-depth exploration.
