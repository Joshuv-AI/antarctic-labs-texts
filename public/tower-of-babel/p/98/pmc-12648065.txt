National survey on definitive radiotherapy practices for locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer in Japan: JROSG 23–3
Authors: Noriko Kishi, Nobuki Imano, Shigeo Takahashi, Masaki Nakamura, Yasuhito Hagiwara, Nobuteru Kubo, Takafumi Komiyama, Yoshizumi Kitamoto, Kayoko Tsujino, Masaki Kokubo, Tsuyoshi Takanaka, Takeshi Ebara, Yoshiyuki Shioyama, Keiko Shibuya, Hiroshi Onishi, Kazushige Hayakawa, Yasushi Nagata, Yukinori Matsuo, Tomoki Kimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis survey was conducted to examine the real-world practice of definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT) for locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer (LA-NSCLC) in Japan, aiming to standardize treatment, reduce inter-institutional disparities and identify areas for future research. A questionnaire was sent to members of the Japanese Radiation Oncology Study Group through a mailing list, with responses collected between December 15, 2023, and March 14, 2024. Responses from 112 institutions revealed that 81.2% either did not set general upper age limits or established limits at 80 years or older for definitive CRT in LA-NSCLC. The most common absolute contraindications were active interstitial pneumonia (60.7%) and contralateral hilar lymph node metastasis (42.0%). Relative contraindications involved dose-volume indices of the normal lung (70.5%). The most commonly adopted dose-volume indices were lung V20Gy < 30%, lung V5Gy < 60% and mean lung dose <20 Gy, while no definite indices were established for heart V50Gy and mean heart dose in half of the institutions. Additionally, 88.4% of institutions reported using IMRT for LA-NSCLC. Involved-field radiotherapy (IFRT) was adopted regardless of institutional size, and institutions with higher IMRT usage for LA-NSCLC also had higher IFRT adoption rates. In conclusion, this nationwide survey revealed the expanded use of definitive CRT and a growing emphasis on reducing lung dose to mitigate pulmonary toxicities, facilitated by advancements in IMRT and IFRT. Regularly conducting these surveys is essential to monitor evolving treatment strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 85% of lung cancer cases, and the prognosis for locally advanced NSCLC (LA-NSCLC) has been poor [2]. The standard treatment for unresectable LA-NSCLC is definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT), and the PACIFIC trial demonstrated that the addition of consolidation durvalumab significantly improved overall survival compared with placebo in LA-NSCLC [3, 4]. A common cause of the cessation of consolidation durvalumab, as identified in real-world data, is CRT-induced toxicities, primarily radiation pneumonitis, highlighting a critical need to reduce them.

Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) is a high-precision radiotherapy (RT) technique that enables the delivery of a conformal dose to the target volume while decreasing the irradiated dose to the organs at risk (OARs). IMRT can reduce toxicities, such as severe radiation pneumonitis, in LA-NSCLC by minimizing the irradiated dose to the normal lung [5, 6]. With the introduction of consolidation durvalumab and growing concerns about radiation pneumonitis, the adoption of IMRT for LA-NSCLC has become increasingly common.

The IMRT delivery process in LA-NSCLC is complex and includes several components that vary based on institutional or radiation oncologists’ policies: indication, simulation (including respiratory motion management), delineation, dose prescription and dose constraints. A recommendation has been published for the planning and delivery of high-dose, high-precision RT [7], as well as a guideline for target volume definition in the treatment of LA-NSCLC [8]. However, specific details such as the definition of the clinical target volume (CTV) in involved-field radiotherapy (IFRT), dose fractionation, or methods of dose prescription remain at the discretion of radiation oncologists and are not standardized when utilizing IMRT for LA-NSCLC. Furthermore, such recommendations need to adapt over time, especially with the emergence of new evidence in lung cancer treatment.

Thus, recent advancements in treatment strategies for LA-NSCLC have posed challenges for radiation oncologists due to the absence of a standardized strategy in clinical practice. This lack of standardization leads to inconsistencies in the control arms of clinical trials, potentially resulting in uncertain outcomes and suboptimal performance. Investigating real-world practices can provide radiation oncologists with valuable feedback to reduce inter-institutional disparities and identify unmet needs for future investigation. To address this, we conducted a survey to gather data on the real-world practice of treating patients with LA-NSCLC who received definitive RT in Japan, aiming to understand treatment strategies. In this report, we present the survey findings on NSCLC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The protocol of this study was approved by the Clinical Trials Review Committee of the Japanese Radiation Oncology Study Group (JROSG); approval number: JROSG 23–3. Due to the survey-based design of the study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

The questionnaire survey was conducted by N.K., N.I. and S.T., and was reviewed and approved by all members of the JROSG Lung and Mediastinal Cancer Committee. The survey included 33 questions: general information about the institutions (five questions), treatment strategies for LA-NSCLC (17 questions) and treatment strategies for small cell lung cancer (SCLC) (11 questions). In this report, we focused on general information and treatment strategies for LA-NSCLC, totaling 22 questions. To avoid an excessive manuscript length, the remaining 11 questions concerning SCLC will be reported separately.

The original questionnaire, conducted in Japanese, was attached as Supplementary Document 1, and the translated English version was presented in Supplementary Document 2. The survey was created using Google Forms and distributed via the JROSG mailing list to all members. The survey period was from December 15, 2023, to March 14, 2024. In cases of duplicate responses from the same institution, the response from the representative or senior member was selected. For duplicate responses from the same individual, the earlier response was considered. All statistical analyses were performed, and graphics were created using R software (version 4.3.0).

RESULTS

Survey response rate

We received 120 responses from 112 institutions. Among the 145 JROSG institutional members, responses were received from 89 institutions (61.4%). The remaining 23 responses were from radiation oncologists with individual JROSG membership who were not affiliated with JROSG institutional members. There were eight duplicate responses from the same institutions. As a result, 112 responses from 112 institutions were analyzed. Excluding conditional branch question items, there were 11 blank answers out of 3696 answers (0.3%).

Indications and contraindications

The general upper age limits for definitive CRT were as follows: no upper limit in 48 institutions (42.8%), 75 years in 17 institutions (15.2%), 79 years in four institutions (3.6%), 80 years in 33 institutions (29.5%), 85 years in nine institutions (8.0%) and 90 years in one institution (0.9%). The most common absolute contraindications were active interstitial pneumonia in 68 institutions (60.7%), followed by contralateral hilar lymph node metastasis in 47 institutions (42.0%). Regarding relative contraindications, the most common was dose-volume indices of the normal lung in 79 institutions (70.5%) (Fig. 2). Among 99 institutions using IMRT for LA-NSCLC, contralateral hilar lymph node metastasis was considered an absolute contraindication by 40 institutions (40.4%) and a relative contraindication by 42 institutions (42.4%). Of the 13 institutions not using IMRT, contralateral hilar lymph node metastasis was regarded as an absolute contraindication by seven institutions (53.8%) and a relative contraindication by five institutions (38.4%).

(a) Absolute contraindications and (b) relative contraindications for definitive CRT.

Abbreviations: DVI, dose-volume index; IP, interstitial pneumonia; LN, lymph node metastasis.

Chemotherapy regimens and fractionations for chemoradiotherapy

The most common regimens for CRT were as follows: weekly carboplatin (CBDCA) and paclitaxel (PTX) in 67 institutions, cisplatin (CDDP) and vinorelbine (VNR) in 25 institutions, CDDP and docetaxel (DTX) in eight institutions, CDDP and tegafur-gimeracil-oteracil potassium (S-1) in six institutions, weekly CBDCA and nab-PTX in three institutions, CDDP and pemetrexed (PEM) in one institution, daily CBDCA in one institution and unknown in one institution.

The second most common regimens for CRT were as follows: 26 institutions used weekly CBDCA and PTX; 19 used CDDP and VNR; 18 used daily CBDCA; 15 used CDDP and DTX; six used CDDP and S-1; five used CDDP and PEM; five used CBDCA and PEM; and five were categorized as ‘Other.’

The most common fractionation schedules were once-daily 60 Gy in 30 fractions (106 institutions, 94.6%), once-daily 66 Gy in 33 fractions (five institutions, 4.5%) and once-daily 66 Gy in 30 fractions (one institution, 0.9%). Ten institutions reported that the prescribed dose to the isocenter or gross tumor volume was escalated in patients with bulky primary tumors or in those where sufficiently low OAR doses could be achieved.

OAR and target volume definitions

As for OAR definitions, the most common definitions of the normal lung were as follows: bilateral lung minus gross tumor volume or internal target volume in 84 institutions (75.0%), bilateral lung alone in 12 institutions (10.7%), bilateral lung minus planning target volume (PTV) in 11 institutions (9.8%) and the automatic contouring function of the treatment planning system as is in five institutions (4.5%). The dose-volume indices to be achieved for normal lung V20Gy, V5Gy, mean lung dose, heart V50Gy and mean heart dose are shown in Fig. 3. The most common dose-volume indices were lung V20Gy < 30%, lung V5Gy < 60% and mean lung dose <20 Gy, while no definite indices were set for heart V50Gy and mean heart dose.

Dose-volume indices to be achieved for (a) normal lung V20Gy, (b) V5Gy, (c) mean lung dose, (d) heart V50Gy and (e) mean heart dose.

For the clinical target volume, 57 institutions (50.9%) adopted IFRT for all patients with LA-NSCLC receiving definitive CRT, while two institutions (1.8%) adopted elective nodal irradiation for all such patients. Among the 110 institutions adopting IFRT, in defining the CTV, adding margins to lymph node metastasis was more commonly used than including lymph node stations with lymph node metastasis (74 institutions vs 33 institutions, excluding three responses categorized as ‘Other’ or left blank).

Irradiation techniques and dose prescription in IMRT

In this survey of irradiation techniques and dose prescription in IMRT, 99 institutions (88.4%) reported using IMRT for LA-NSCLC. The common dose prescription methods were D50% or Dmean in 53 institutions (47.3%), D95% in 37 institutions (33.0%) and D90% in five institutions (4.5%), while four institutions specified other methods or left responses blank.

Additionally, a stratified analysis of IFRT and IMRT adoption rates by institutional size showed that IFRT is introduced for more than 80% of patients with LA-NSCLC receiving CRT, regardless of institutional size (Fig. 4a). Conversely, no association was observed between institutional size and the percentage of patients receiving IMRT for LA-NSCLC (Fig. 4b). Figure 4c shows the association between the adoption of IMRT and IFRT, indicating that institutions with a higher percentage of IMRT use also tend to have higher rates of IFRT adoption. Strategy after consolidation Durvalumab’s approval by the pharmaceuticals and medical devices agency in 2018

The number of institutions that increased their use of IMRT after 2018 was greater than those that did not change their irradiation techniques, with 56 institutions (50.0%) reporting an increase versus 48 institutions (42.9%) maintaining their existing methods.

Regarding the definition of CTV in IFRT, more than half of the institutions made no changes (61 institutions, 54.5%), whereas 49 institutions (43.8%) reported reducing the CTV size.

Imaging after CRT was performed mostly within two weeks in 86 institutions (76.8%), and durvalumab was initiated within two weeks after CRT in 92 institutions (82.1%).

DISCUSSION

This survey, conducted among 112 institutions across Japan, collected data on the real-world practices of treating patients with LA-NSCLC who received definitive RT. Our key findings included the indications, contraindications and regimens for CRT; definitions of target volume and OARs; irradiation techniques; and the impact of administering consolidation durvalumab on RT strategies. The survey results contributed to clarifying a standardized strategy for clinical practice in Japan and offered consistency in clinical trials by decreasing inter-institutional disparities. Furthermore, they showed the expanding indications of definitive CRT and highlighted ongoing challenges radiation oncologists face in reducing lung dose to minimize the risk of radiation pneumonitis.

The indications for definitive CRT in elderly patients are expanding in Japan, a super-aging society. In our survey, ~80% of the institutions included patients aged 80 and older as candidates for CRT or had no upper age limit. Additionally, daily low-dose CBDCA, following the JCOG 0301 protocol for elderly patients [9], was commonly used at some institutions. As a recommended chemotherapy regimen with reduced toxicity for elderly patients, JCOG1914 investigated the non-inferiority of CBDCA plus nab-PTX compared to low-dose CBDCA [10], but it failed to demonstrate this non-inferiority. There is strong anticipation for the development of novel chemotherapy regimens that enhance radiosensitization while ensuring efficacy and safety in elderly patients. Another promising treatment strategy involves combining immunotherapy with RT. Phase II trials have shown favorable results [11–13], but phase III trials, including PACIFIC-2 and CheckMate-73 L, reported that the concurrent use of immunotherapy and CRT did not improve survival outcomes compared to CRT alone [14, 15]. Further detailed results from other ongoing phase III trials are awaited [16].

The emergence of IMRT has further expanded the indication for definitive CRT in lung cancer. The findings of our survey reveal that radiation oncologists may no longer necessarily regard contralateral hilar lymph node metastasis as an absolute contraindication for definitive RT. This contraindication was historically based on expert opinion derived from previous studies, supported by only limited data regarding efficacy and toxicity [17–19]. Additionally, a previous study concluded that elective nodal irradiation of the contralateral hilar lymph node region is unnecessary, given that its dose coverage was not significantly associated with in-field recurrence [20]. However, these findings were based on 2D or 3D RT techniques and reflect concerns regarding the heightened risk of radiation pneumonitis from bilateral hilar irradiation. However, with the development of IMRT, it is now technically feasible to irradiate patients with conditions traditionally avoided as contraindications for definitive CRT, such as contralateral hilar lymph node metastasis and high dose-volume indices of the normal lung, while reducing lung doses. Accordingly, a re-evaluation of these conventional assumptions in the context of modern technology is warranted, and further investigations are necessary.

Our survey also highlighted that institutions with a higher percentage of patients receiving IMRT for LA-NSCLC are more likely to adopt IFRT as well. As for IFRT methodology, most institutions have adopted adding margins to lymph node metastasis for CTV delineation, which further reduces the irradiated volume in comparison with including lymph node stations with lymph node metastasis [21], and potentially lowers the risk of pneumonitis. The pharmaceuticals and medical devices agency’s (PMDA’s) approval of consolidation durvalumab may have encouraged the broader adoption of IMRT and IFRT, although the survey questions may not have been sufficient to fully evaluate changes in RT strategies before and after PMDA’s approval, as this timing coincided with the broader introduction of IMRT in Japan. However, the standardization of IMRT planning remains an unresolved issue. Although a D95% prescription was widely adopted in previous clinical trials using IMRT, such as the RTOG 0617 trial [22], our survey revealed that a D50% prescription is more commonly used in clinical practice in Japan. When introducing IMRT, a D50% prescription may have been preferred as a safer approach, as it results in a dose distribution comparable to the point-based prescription used in conventional 3D-CRT. In addition, the absence of a demonstrated benefit from dose escalation in the RTOG 0617 trial may have led institutions to avoid transitioning to a D95% prescription, which could result in higher delivered doses than those with the D50% prescription. Nevertheless, dose escalation using isotoxic IMRT has recently drawn attention [23], and the adoption of a D95% prescription may gradually increase in the future.

Regarding dose-volume constraints of organs at risk, which are important determinants for the indication of CRT, optimizing these is crucial for minimizing the risk of RT-induced toxicities in lung cancer. According to NCCN guidelines, lung V20Gy ≤ 35–40% and mean lung dose ≤20 Gy are recommended as the dose-volume constraints for CRT, with no definite indices for lung V5Gy [3]. In real-world practice, most Japanese institutions aim for lung V20Gy lower than the recommended dose constraints, and they also set indices for V5Gy, making considerable efforts to decrease the risk of radiation pneumonitis. On the contrary, ~50% of the institutions have not set definite indices for cardiac dose constraints. The survey revealed a greater focus on lung doses compared to cardiac doses. The NCCN guidelines recommend cardiac dose-volume constraints such as V40Gy ≤ 20% and a mean dose ≤20 Gy, which are primarily based on the long-term outcomes of the RTOG 0617 trial [24]. In the United States, the initiation of education on cardiac dose constraints and the implementation of heart dose performance metrics across a statewide consortium resulted in a significant decrease in the mean heart dose, without increasing lung or esophagus doses in patients with lung cancer receiving definitive RT [25]. Moreover, a recent study has revealed that combining lung and cardiac dose parameters can better predict survival in patients with LA-NSCLC treated with RT [26]. We believe that raising awareness of the importance of minimizing a cardiac dose and promoting the development of standardized dose constraints that consider both cardiac and pulmonary toxicity are essential in Japan. Such efforts may contribute to more refined treatment planning, and better long-term outcomes for patients.

There are several limitations to this survey. First, the respondents were primarily from JROSG institutions, which are mostly high-volume centers, such as university hospitals and cancer centers, potentially introducing selection bias. In this regard, the findings of another nationwide survey conducted in Japan at the almost same period, which targeted all facilities equipped with external beam RT systems, indicated that IMRT was used in 68.6% of the 405 responding institutions for at least one tumor, regardless of lung cancer [27]. In Japan, a shortage of radiation oncologists and facility requirements for IMRT implementation are considered the primary factors limiting the broader adoption of IMRT, thereby highlighting the need for improvement. Second, the responses were collected in March 2024, prior to the PMDA approval in May 2025 of maintenance osimertinib following definitive CRT for unresectable stage III EGFR-mutant NSCLC, an approval decision based on the findings of the LAURA trial [28]. Following this approval, the combination of EGFR-TKIs and RT has raised concerns regarding increased pulmonary toxicity. In the LAURA trial, radiation pneumonitis of grade ≥ 3 occurred in 2% of patients receiving osimertinib, compared with 0% among those receiving a placebo. A systematic literature review of studies reporting the combined administration of EGFR-TKIs and RT revealed that treatment-related pneumonitis of grade ≥ 3 has been reported in 2.3% of patients receiving definitive CRT or RT (typically with a prescribed dose of 60 Gy or higher), and in 2.9% of those receiving stereotactic body RT or palliative RT; however, data regarding these effects remain limited [29]. In addition, cardiotoxicity is a well-known adverse effect associated with osimertinib-based therapy. Given that long-term survival improves with maintenance therapy, RT-induced cardiotoxicity as a late adverse event is also receiving renewed attention. Consequently, clinical practice among radiation oncologists, particularly with respect to dose constraints, may have evolved since the time of this survey.

In conclusion, this nationwide survey revealed the expanded use of definitive CRT and a growing emphasis on reducing lung dose to mitigate pulmonary toxicities, facilitated by advancements in IMRT and IFRT. These findings are important for improving treatment consistency for LA-NSCLC in clinical practice and for improving quality control in clinical trials. Regularly conducting these surveys is essential to monitor evolving clinical practices and dynamically update treatment strategies for optimal CRT in lung cancer.
