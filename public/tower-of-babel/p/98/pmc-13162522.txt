Effects of high-oleic dietary interventions on cardiometabolic outcomes in adults: a GRADE-based systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Yetao Han, Mengsi Hu, Yingjian Wang, Rong Li, Yong Liax, Zhiwei Liu, Yanzhong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiets rich in oleic acid have been proposed as a strategy to modify cardiometabolic risk. Evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remains inconsistent, particularly for glucose-related outcomes. To assess the effects of high-oleic dietary interventions compared with lower-oleic diets on lipid profiles, and glycemic indices in adults.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched from inception to July 2025 in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. RCTs were eligible if the intervention diet provided at least 70% of total fatty acids as oleic acid, justified based on prior monounsaturated fat intervention literature, and differed from the comparator by at least 5% points. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed using weighted mean differences. Certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE framework with outcome-specific assessments reported in a Summary of Findings table.ResultsTwenty-six trials involving 1,244 participants were included. High-oleic diets were associated with modest reductions in total cholesterol (WMD: -0.14; 95% CI: -0.28 to -0.01; I2 = 80.8%), and LDL-C (WMD: -0.13; 95% CI: -0.24 to -0.03; I2 = 64.6%). No significant effects were observed for HDL-C, triglycerides, fasting blood glucose, fasting insulin, or HOMA-IR. Lipid-lowering effects were more evident in trials with higher oleic acid proportions (≥ 80%).ConclusionsHigh-oleic dietary interventions result in small but consistent improvements in selected lipid outcomes without effects on glycemic indices. Benefits are dose-dependent and most pronounced when oleic acid constitutes a substantial portion of dietary fat. Absolute changes are modest, highlighting the complementary role of high-oleic diets in cardiometabolic risk management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02136-w.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic disorders, including type 2 diabetes (T2DM), dyslipidemia, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, represent a predominant challenge to global public health and healthcare systems [1]. The persistent rise in their prevalence underscores the critical need for effective, sustainable prevention and management strategies that extend beyond pharmacological treatment [2]. Within this context, dietary modification remains a cornerstone of primary and secondary prevention, with increasing scientific consensus emphasizing that the quality of specific nutrients is as important as their overall quantity.

The role of dietary fat has been particularly reframed, moving beyond simplistic low-fat recommendations toward a more nuanced understanding of how different fatty acid classes distinctly influence metabolic pathways [3, 4]. Among these, monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) have garnered significant interest for their potentially favorable effects on cardiometabolic risk markers. Oleic acid (C18:1 n-9), the most prevalent MUFA in the human diet, serves as a key nutrient in this paradigm [5]. It is abundantly present in traditional sources like olive oil and is now a defining component of modern, bio-engineered high-oleic varieties of common vegetable oils (e.g., sunflower, canola, soybean oil), making it highly relevant for public health nutrition [6, 7].

A growing body of evidence suggests that oleic acid may beneficially modulate several physiological processes. Mechanistic studies indicate it can influence hepatic lipid metabolism, potentially reducing the synthesis and enhancing the clearance of atherogenic lipoproteins [8, 9]. Furthermore, experimental models propose that oleic acid may improve cellular insulin signaling and β-cell function, offering a plausible link to improved glycemic control [10]. Epidemiological observations, notably from populations consuming Mediterranean diets rich in olive oil, support an association between high MUFA intake and reduced cardiometabolic risk [11].

However, evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the gold standard for establishing causal dietary effects, has yielded inconsistent results, especially concerning glucose metabolism [11, 12]. This heterogeneity is likely multifactorial, stemming from variations in study design, the dose and source of oleic acid, the composition of the comparator diet, intervention duration, and the baseline metabolic health of participants. Many previous reviews have focused broadly on MUFAs without strictly defining the threshold of oleic acid exposure, potentially diluting the signal of its specific effect. Consequently, a clear, quantitative synthesis of RCTs employing a standardized, high-level oleic acid intervention is lacking. To address this evidence gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs with a strict a priori operational definition of a high-oleic diet. Our primary objective was to quantify the effects of dietary interventions providing ≥ 70% of total fatty acids from oleic acid, compared to lower-oleic diets, on a comprehensive panel of cardiometabolic risk markers: lipid profiles, apolipoproteins, and indices of glucose homeostasis. By applying the GRADE framework to assess the certainty of evidence and exploring sources of heterogeneity through subgroup and meta-regression analyses, this work aims to provide a rigorous, evidence-based assessment of the cardiometabolic impact of high-oleic dietary patterns and inform targeted nutritional recommendations.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [13]. The study protocol was prospectively registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO).

Eligibility criteria

We employed the PICOS framework to define study eligibility: Population (P): Adults (≥ 18 years) without restriction on health status (including healthy, overweight/obese, or diagnosed with dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome, or T2DM). Intervention (I): A dietary intervention where the primary fat source provided ≥ 70% of its total fatty acids as oleic acid (C18:1 n-9). The specific fatty acid composition for each intervention is detailed in Table 1. Comparator (C): A control diet utilizing a fat source with a lower oleic acid content, creating a minimum absolute difference of 5% points in oleic acid proportion of total fatty acids between groups.

Outcomes (O): Primary outcomes included lipid profiles (total cholesterol [TC], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], triglycerides [TG]), and glucose homeostasis indices (fasting blood glucose (FBG), insulin, Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance [HOMA-IR]). Study Design (S): Published and unpublished RCTs with either parallel or crossover design. Trials with an intervention duration of less than one week were excluded to ensure metabolic equilibrium.

Information sources and search strategy

A systematic search was conducted across four electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus, from their inception until July 2025. The search strategy was developed with a biomedical librarian and combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Emtree terms with free-text keywords related to three core concepts: (1) Oleic acid and MUFA, (2) RCTs, and (3) Cardiometabolic outcomes. No language or publication status restrictions were applied. The complete search strategy for PubMed is provided in Supplementary Material S1. Furthermore, we manually screened the reference lists of all included articles and relevant systematic reviews to identify additional eligible studies.

Study selection process

All retrieved records were managed using Covidence systematic review software. After duplicate removal, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. The full texts of potentially relevant articles were then obtained and assessed independently by the same reviewers. Any disagreements at any stage were resolved through discussion or by consultation with a third reviewer. Outcome data: For continuous outcomes, we extracted the mean, standard deviation (SD), and sample size for both intervention and control groups at baseline and post-intervention. Where available, mean change from baseline and its SD were extracted directly. For crossover trials, we sought paired analysis data (mean and SD of the within-subject difference).

Quality assessment

Two independent authors evaluated the methodological quality of randomized controlled trials using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2) tool [14]. They assessed the following criteria: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting; any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third author.

Synthesis methods

Effect Measures and Data Synthesis: For continuous outcomes, the pooled effect size was expressed as the weighted mean difference (WMD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI), maintaining natural units (mmol/L for lipids, pmol/L for insulin) [15]. A random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method) was used for all meta-analyses to account for anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity [16]. For crossover trials that did not report paired analysis statistics, we calculated the treatment effect using methods outlined in the Cochrane Handbook. To estimate the standard error of the mean difference, a within-subject correlation coefficient of r = 0.5 was assumed. The robustness of this assumption was tested in a sensitivity analysis using values of r = 0.25 and r = 0.75. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and the Cochran’s Q test (significance level p < 0.10) [17]. To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, we performed pre-specified subgroup analyses based on: oleic acid dose (< 80% vs. ≥80% of fatty acids), intervention duration (≤ 4 vs. >4 weeks), sample size, study design (Crossover vs. parallel) and participant baseline BMI (≤ 30 vs. >30 kg/m²). Univariate random-effects meta-regression was used to explore the association between the continuous variables (oleic acid percentage, duration) and the effect size [18]. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted by iteratively removing each study to assess its impact on the overall pooled estimate for primary outcomes [19]. For outcomes with ≥ 10 studies, potential publication bias was evaluated visually using funnel plots and formally tested with Egger’s regression test (p < 0.10 considered indicative of asymmetry) [20]. If asymmetry was detected, the trim-and-fill method was applied to estimate the effect of potentially missing studies. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software, version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Certainty of evidence

The overall certainty (quality) of evidence for each primary outcome was judged using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [21]. Evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low based on considerations of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Results

Study characteristics

The systematic search and selection process, detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1), yielded 26 RCTs for inclusion, encompassing 1,217 unique participants. The key characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. The majority of trials (n = 19) employed a crossover design, with intervention durations ranging from 3 to 24 weeks. High-oleic interventions were administered using various sources, primarily olive oil (n = 12) and high-oleic seed oils (e.g., sunflower, canola, safflower; n = 11). Comparator diets utilized fats with a lower oleic acid content, including conventional seed oils, palm oil, or dairy fats. Study populations were predominantly healthy or at-risk adults, with some trials including individuals with hypercholesterolemia, obesity, or metabolic syndrome. Sample sizes ranged from 8 to 130 participants per group. Twenty-six randomized controlled trials involving 1,244 participants met the inclusion criteria. Most trials employed crossover designs, with intervention durations ranging from three to twenty-four weeks. High-oleic interventions were delivered primarily through olive oil or high-oleic seed oils, while comparator diets varied in fatty acid composition and background dietary patterns.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

The methodological quality of the included studies, assessed using the ROB2, was heterogeneous. While the randomization process was judged to have a low risk of bias in all studies, other domains raised concerns. Deviations from intended interventions and the selection of reported results were frequently rated as having “some concerns” or a “high risk” of bias, reflecting common challenges in dietary intervention studies such as blinding and outcome reporting. Measurement of outcomes and missing outcome data were generally at low risk of bias (Table 2). According to the GRADE framework, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate for reductions in LDL-C and TC (downgraded for inconsistency), and low to moderate for neutral effects on glucose outcomes (downgraded for imprecision and indirectness). Lipid profile

Our finding revealed that High-oleic dietary interventions could reduce TC (WMD: −0.14; 95% CI: −0.28 to −0.01, p = 0.030; I2 = 80.8%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2a), and LDL-C (WMD: −0.13; 95% CI: −0.24 to −0.03, p < 0.001; I2 = 64.6%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2b), significantly. While, it could not have a significant impact on HDL-C (WMD: −0.0; 95% CI: −0.03 to 0.02, p = 0.224; I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.761) (Fig. 2c), and TG (WMD: −0.02; 95% CI: −0.06 to 0.03, p = 0.261; I2 = 5.3%, p = 0.387) (Fig. 2 d). Treatment duration, sample size, study design, BMI, and oleic intake from total fatty acids were recognized as sources of high heterogeneity following subgroup analysis (Table 4). No significant differences were observed between high-oleic and lower-oleic diets for fasting glucose, fasting insulin, or HOMA-IR. We acknowledge the lack of sensitive glucose endpoints (e.g., postprandial measures, clamps) as a limitation.

Meta-regression, and sensitivity analyses

Univariate meta-regression confirmed a significant dose-dependent relationship, where a higher percentage of oleic acid in the intervention fat was linearly associated with greater reductions in LDL-C and TG. Sensitivity analyses using the leave-one-out method and alternative correlation coefficients for crossover trials did not materially alter the pooled estimates.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes evidence from 26 RCTs to evaluate the cardiometabolic impact of diets rich in oleic acid. The principal finding is that high-oleic dietary interventions elicit modest but consistent improvements in atherogenic lipid profiles, specifically, reductions in TC and LDL-C, without significantly altering TG, HDL-C, and glycemic indices.

The observed lipid benefits align with established biological mechanisms. Replacing saturated fatty acids with oleic acid is known to upregulate hepatic LDL receptor activity, enhancing clearance of circulating LDL particles. Furthermore, oleic acid incorporation into lipoprotein particles, particularly cholesteryl oleate in LDL, may reduce their atherogenicity and propensity for oxidation. The small but significant increase in Apo A1, the major structural protein of HDL-C, suggests a potential favorable effect on HDL-C metabolism or composition, warranting further investigation [22, 23].

A critical finding from our analysis is the clear dose-response relationship. Subgroup and meta-regression analyses demonstrated that interventions providing ≥ 80% of fatty acids as oleic acid produced more substantial LDL-C lowering [24, 25]. This underscores that the magnitude of substitution is a key determinant of clinical effect, and may explain null results in trials with minimal contrast between intervention and control fats [26]. The substantial statistical heterogeneity (I² >60%) for TC and LDL-C outcomes is likely multifactorial, attributable to differences in the source of high-oleic fat (e.g., olive oil vs. seed oils), background dietary patterns, baseline cardiometabolic status of participants, and study design.

In contrast to the lipid findings, and despite promising preclinical data, we found no significant effect on FBG, insulin, or HOMA-IR. This null result may be explained by several factors: (1) the inclusion of predominantly normoglycemic participants with limited room for improvement; (2) the relatively short duration of most trials, which may be insufficient to detect changes in insulin sensitivity; and (3) the reliance on fasting indices, which are less sensitive than dynamic measures like hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamps or postprandial assessments. Therefore, the absence of evidence from fasting metrics should not be construed as evidence of no effect on glucose metabolism more broadly [27, 28].

Clinical implications and strengths/limitations

From a clinical perspective, the absolute reductions in LDL-C (~ 0.13 mmol/L or ~ 5 mg/dL), while statistically robust, are modest at the individual level. However, such reductions, if sustained, could translate into meaningful cardiovascular risk reduction at a population scale [29]. High-oleic diets should therefore be viewed as a complementary dietary strategy within a multifaceted approach to cardiometabolic health, which includes attention to overall diet quality, energy balance, and physical activity [30].

The strengths of this work include the use of a strict, pre-defined threshold for oleic acid exposure, comprehensive outcome assessment, rigorous application of the GRADE framework, and exploration of dose-response via subgroup and meta-regression. Limitations include the clinical and methodological heterogeneity of included trials, the short-to-medium term duration of interventions, and the lack of data on hard clinical endpoints (e.g., cardiovascular events). Additionally, our conclusions on glucose metabolism are limited to fasting measures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, dietary patterns emphasizing a high intake of oleic acid consistently produce small but favorable modifications to the circulating lipid profile, particularly when oleic acid constitutes a substantial proportion (≥ 80%) of dietary fatty acids. These benefits occur without adverse effects on fasting glucose regulation. The findings support the inclusion of high-oleic fats (e.g., olive oil, high-oleic seed oils) as a replacement for saturated fats in dietary guidelines for cardiometabolic risk management. Future long-term RCTs in at-risk populations, employing more sensitive measures of insulin action, are needed to definitively characterize the potential glycemic benefits of oleic acid.
