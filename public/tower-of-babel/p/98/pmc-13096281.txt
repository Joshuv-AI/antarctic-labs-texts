Frequency-division routing via spin–refractive-index locking
Authors: Yuan-Peng Peng, Shi-Yao Zhu, J Q You, Yi-Pu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHere we demonstrate spin–refractive-index locking (SRIL) in a composite right–left-handed transmission-line metamaterial, where the transverse spin of surface modes is locked to the sign of the effective refractive index. Unlike conventional spin–momentum locking (SML), the SRIL reverses the spin for the same energy propagation direction in right- and left-handed bands. Using this index-dependent spin control, we realize frequency-selective chiral photon–magnon interaction by placing a yttrium iron garnet (YIG) sphere above the metamaterial and achieving strong nonreciprocity, where magnons only couple to microwaves of matching spin and the required direction is determined by the operating band. Tuning the frequency across the two bands dynamically switches the coupling direction, enabling a compact, reconfigurable on-chip routing device for spin-selective microwave control and nonreciprocal signal processing.Subject terms: Sub-wavelength optics, Electronic and spintronic devices, Optical physics, Optics and photonics, Photonic devices

Introduction

Directional control of light and its interactions with matter is a central theme in modern photonics1–4, and transverse spin of optical fields provides a distinctive mechanism for realizing such scenarios. In surface and guided electromagnetic waves, the transverse spin5–7, arising from the rotation of the electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to the propagation direction, is an intrinsic characteristic of evanescent waves8–10. The spin direction is intrinsically locked to the wave momentum, a phenomenon known as spin–momentum locking (SML) or the photonic quantum spin Hall effect (QSHE)11–16. This effect has been widely exploited for the unidirectional excitation of surface and waveguide modes by spin-polarized sources17,18, high-sensitivity optical metrology19, and particle manipulation via lateral optical forces20–22. These advances have established the SML as a versatile tool for spin-dependent light control in photonic structures with engineered dispersion and field confinement. However, all experimental realizations of the SML have been restricted to right-handed (positive-index) media, where momentum and energy flow are co-directed7.

The concept of left-handed or negative-index materials, proposed by Veselago in the 1960s23, describes a regime where the phase velocity is reversed relative to the energy flow. This property has been experimentally confirmed in artificial metamaterials24–27 and underpins applications based on negative refraction. When the refractive index is negative, momentum and energy flow are anti-parallel, introducing an alternative avenue for controlling light. Notably, composite right-/left-handed transmission lines (CRLH-TLs)28–30 provide an accessible platform for exploring wave phenomena arising from refractive-index switching at microwave frequencies, as they support both positive- and negative-index regimes in one device. By enabling tuning between frequency bands31, CRLH-TLs allow the index sign to vary in situ, offering a practical route to engineer reconfigurable light–matter interactions. When combined with SML, refractive-index tuning provides an additional degree of freedom for spin manipulation.

To explore spin-selective light–matter interactions, both the optical field and material system must possess spin degrees of freedom. In optical fields, this can be the transverse or longitudinal spin of electromagnetic modes. In matter, spin arises from angular-momentum changes in atomic or orbital transitions or from the intrinsic angular momentum of collective spin-precession modes in magnetic media. Magnons, which are quasiparticles arising from collective spin precessions, hold great potential for energy-efficient data processing32–34 and can strongly interact with photons35,36. Magnon–photon interaction has been extensively investigated in yttrium iron garnet (YIG) platforms, including coherent and dissipative couplings between magnons and photons in three-dimensional cavities37–39, striplines40,41, coplanar waveguide resonator42–44, and planar split-ring resonators45,46. In these devices, microwave photons are typically linearly polarized, so their coupling to magnons lacks spin selectivity. However, recent studies have demonstrated spin-dependent coupling by exploiting circular polarization in substrate-integrated waveguides (SIW)47. Furthermore, by engineering the dispersion of right-handed spoof surface plasmon polaritons (SSPPs), enhanced magnon–photon interactions via slow-wave effects48 and chiral photon–magnon coupling enabled by spin–momentum locking49 have been realized.

Building upon these advances, we further integrate spin–momentum locking with composite right-/left-handed materials, which provide a unique capability to reverse the relative orientation between energy flow and momentum. This integration allows us to explore refractive-index–controlled spin phenomena in engineered light–matter interactions, thereby opening pathways toward integrated platforms where spin, momentum, and index sign are jointly harnessed.

In this Article, we report the observation of spin–refractive-index locking (SRIL) in evanescent surface modes of a CRLH-TL operating in the microwave regime, where, for a fixed energy-flow direction, the refractive-index sign uniquely sets the transverse spin orientation of guided surface modes, reversing it when the index changes the sign. Thus, waves propagating in the same direction exhibit opposite transverse spins in positive- and negative-index regimes. Leveraging this property, we integrate a YIG sphere with a CRLH-TL and realize frequency-controlled chiral photon–magnon coupling, where the coupling direction is dynamically reversed by tuning the operating frequency across the zero-index switching point. This enables tunable nonreciprocity and reconfigurable three-port signal-routing device that directs different frequencies into distinct spatial channels. Our results establish the SRIL as a distinct paradigm for controlling light–matter interactions, with potential applications in spin-selective isolation, circulation, non-reciprocal antennas, and frequency-division duplex transmission.

Results

Spin–momentum locking, spin–refractive-index locking, and spin-selective chiral magnon–photon couplings

The concepts of SML, SRIL, spin-selective and chiral photon–magnon couplings are schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a. In SML, the transverse spin S of a guided surface wave is locked to the propagation vector β, such that +β corresponds to −S and −β to +S. This phenomenon, widely observed in right-handed (n > 0) systems such as optical waveguides50,51 and spoof surface plasmon polaritons52,53, is summarized in the upper panel of Fig. 1a.

Spin–refractive-index locking concept.

a In right-handed (n > 0) and left-handed (n < 0) materials, the transverse spin S for a fixed energy-flow direction is set by the refractive-index sign, determining whether it is parallel or anti-parallel to the propagation vector β. Photon–magnon coupling (g) occurs only for matching spin handedness. b Implementation using a composite right-/left-handed transmission-line waveguide coupled with a YIG sphere under a bias magnetic field. In n > 0 media, phase velocity follows energy flow; in n < 0 media, it is opposite. The transverse spin (TS) reverses with the refractive-index sign, enabling reconfigurable chiral coupling. If coupling occurs, the signal is partially absorbed by the magnon and the transmission decreases. If decoupled, the signal propagates smoothly through the device.

In SRIL, the transverse spin remains determined by β, but the orientation of β for a fixed energy flow P depends on the sign of the refractive index. In right-handed (n > 0) media, P is parallel to β, while in left-handed (n < 0) media it is anti-parallel. This distinction, illustrated by comparing the upper and lower panels of Fig. 1a, implies that a waveguide supporting both positive- and negative-index bands can host opposite transverse spins under the same P, thereby realizing spin–index locking.

When it simultaneously supports negative- (n < 0) and positive-index (n > 0) bands, SRIL governs the local transverse spin associated with a given energy flow. In the negative-index regime, the guided-mode spin matches the magnon spin, enabling efficient coupling and strong absorption. In the positive-index regime, the spins are mismatched, suppressing the interaction and leaving the TL transparent. Reversing the microwave injection direction interchanges the results. This dual-band, index-tunable property allows a single device to reverse its chiral photon–magnon response simply via frequency tuning.

Characterization of the composite right/left-handed transmission line

To realize the concept proposed in Fig. 1, we employ a composite right-/left-handed transmission line (CRLH-TL). Our adoption of this platform addresses key challenges in realizing SML in negative-index media. Unlike locally resonant metamaterials based on isolated unit-cell resonances, the CRLH-TL platform relies on distributed circuit parameters, enabling low-loss, broadband propagation and strong field confinement. Crucially, it allows continuous switching between negative- and positive-index regimes within a single device, providing the reconfigurability required to verify the SRIL mechanism.

We first examine the fundamental properties of CRLH-TL. Figure 2a shows the unit cell, where the gap capacitor and the complementary split-ring resonator (CSRR) are patterned on opposite sides of an F4B substrate (Supplementary Section 1). A balanced CRLH-TL comprising eight unit cells is fabricated (Fig. 2b). Here the “balanced" indicates that the left- and right-handed passbands are directly connected without a frequency gap (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Fig. S2). The dispersion relation and effective refractive index of the TL are extracted from simulations (see Methods for details) and presented in Fig. 2c. The effective refractive index n is defined as n = cβ/ω, where c is the speed of light in free space, β is the magnitude of the propagation vector β, and ω is the angular frequency of the wave. The right-handed mode (red curve) is characterized by co-directional phase and group velocities (β > 0, dω/dβ > 0), corresponding to a positive-index passband. In contrast, the left-handed mode (blue curve) exhibits anti-parallel phase and group velocities (β < 0, dω/dβ > 0), indicative of a negative-index regime. As shown in Fig. 2b, microwave connectors at both ends allow direct measurement of both transmission and reflection spectra using a vector network analyzer (VNA). The measured transmission spectrum (purple solid curve) and the simulated result (gray dashed curve) are shown in Fig. 2d, exhibiting a good agreement. The total passband spans 4.9–9.4 GHz, with the left-handed band covering 4.9–5.7 GHz and the right-handed band covering 5.7–9.4 GHz.

As illustrated in Fig. 2e, the surface wave at the edge of the TL appears as an evanescent mode localized near the edges, with its amplitude decaying exponentially inward and outward. Specifically, the terms “inward” and “outward” describe the decay direction of the evanescent field, with inward corresponding to decay toward the center of the metal strip and outward to decay away from the strip into the surrounding dielectric or air. The stronger inward decay enhances confinement at the inner edge, whereas the outward decay produces weaker evanescent fields that still carry transverse spin outside the edge. To maximize the magnon–photon interaction strength, the YIG sphere is placed directly on the transmission line at the ideal coupling positions A and B shown in Fig. 2e. A sphere diameter of 1 mm, smaller than the microstrip width (w = 2.49 mm), is chosen so that the sphere couples to a determinate spin-polarized region without crossing the spin-reversal point at the strip center (see Supplementary Section 4). This evanescent wave is characterized by a complex wavevector k = β + iη, where β is the propagation vector along the y-direction and η is the decay vector along the x-direction. In this case, both quantities can be treated as scalars, where a positive β denotes phase velocity along +y-direction and a positive η denotes decay along +x-direction. When both β and η are nonzero, the wave simultaneously carries energy along its propagation direction and exhibits confinement due to the evanescent decay. To characterize the local polarization and transverse spin, we examine the fields at two edges of the transmission line (positions A and B in Fig. 2e). Specifically, Fig. 2e shows the simulated magnetic-field distribution and transverse spin characteristics for a wave propagating along the +y-direction in a positive-index band. The local transverse spin orientation yields opposite handedness at the two edges: right-circular polarization (RCP) at site A and left-circular polarization (LCP) at site B, as indicated by the time evolution of the field vectors. This spatial separation of opposite spin states provides a natural platform for realizing spin-selective coupling to the matter placed at different edges of the TL. The results in Fig. 2f show that in the positive-index regime (n > 0), reversing the propagation direction reverses the sign of Sz, consistent with the conventional SML. In the negative-index regime (n < 0), the directional dependence remains but the sign of Sz is reversed compared with the n > 0 case for the same energy-flow direction, confirming the SRIL. Near the zero-index point (n ≈ 0), the phase propagation constant β approaches zero (β → 0). Because the transverse spin is intrinsically linked to the momentum through S ∝ β × η, the vanishing β leads to the disappearance of the transverse spin component Sz. As a result, the local magnetic field at positions A and B undergoes a transition from elliptical to linear polarization (see Supplementary Section 7).

These characterizations confirm that the CRLH-TL platform enables deterministic control of the transverse spin state through tuning the operating frequency across the zero-refractive-index point, as well as by adjusting the propagation direction and spatial position (decay direction). This provides a compact and flexible foundation for realizing chiral photon–magnon coupling in integrated nonreciprocal devices.

Because the magnon linewidth (~MHz; see Fig. 3c) is orders of magnitude narrower than the broadband CRLH-TL passband (~GHz; see Fig. 2d), measurements performed at a fixed bias magnetic field probe only a narrow frequency window. To access the chiral coupling behavior across the entire passband, the bias magnetic field is therefore continuously swept to tune the magnon resonance. We define the contrast ratio (CR) between forward and backward transmissions as 20×log10∣S21/S12∣. Figure 3d plots the CR versus the bias magnetic field and probe frequency, showing that the nonreciprocity is locked to the sign of the refractive index, thereby confirming the SRIL mechanism. Near the transition frequency (ω/2π ≈ 5.7 GHz), the CR approaches zero, indicating equal leftward and rightward radiative damping (κL = κR) and thus achiral coupling. Furthermore, fitting the experimental data with Eq. (6) yields the results shown in Fig. 3e, which reveal the extrinsic damping rates of the magnon mode across the CRLH-TL passband. In the negative-index passband, the magnon couples more strongly to the rightward-energy-flow photon channel, with the leftward radiative damping generally smaller. Conversely, in the positive-index passband, the coupling to leftward-propagating photons is stronger. In addition, as the frequency increases, the coupling between the magnon mode and the leftward-propagating wave gradually strengthens and becomes significantly larger than the coupling to the rightward-propagating wave in the negative-index regime. This asymmetry is primarily attributed to enhanced field confinement at higher frequencies, which increases the coupling strength g and thus dominates the radiative damping (κ = ∣g∣2/vg). The superimposed nonmonotonic fluctuations are likely to arise from frequency-dependent variations in the local mode overlap. Reversing the direction of the magnetic bias field inverts the above experimental results (Supplementary Section 8).

The above results convey two main insights. First, the magnon mode with spin characteristics can serve as a sensitive probe for directly detecting the transverse spin of the surface wave, thereby providing an effective approach to determine the effective refractive index of the SRIL metamaterials. Second, leveraging the versatility of CRLH-TLs, our hybrid YIG–CRLH-TL system enables flexible and robust control of chiral photon–magnon coupling, opening a promising platform toward nonreciprocal microwave devices and integrated photonic–magnonic signal processing systems.

Frequency-division signal routing

To further explore the functionality of refractive-index-controlled chiral coupling, we implement a three-port YIG–CRLH-TL device, as shown in Fig. 4a. A YIG sphere is fixed at a specific position along the waveguide edge under a static magnetic bias field aligned with the +z-direction and is driven through port 3 using a log-periodic dipole antenna. Ports 1 and 2 act as output channels. In this arrangement, the propagation direction of the magnon-emitted signal is determined by the excitation frequency.

Frequency-division signal routing and circulator functionality.

a Schematic of a three-port hybrid CRLH-TL–YIG device. Port 3 is connected to a log-periodic dipole for magnon excitation under an external bias magnetic field. b Measured transmission spectrum around 4.9 GHz (left-handed band, n < 0), where signals from port 3 (port 1) are routed to port 2 (port 3), as illustrated in the inset. c Measured transmission spectrum around 8.65 GHz (right-handed band, n > 0), showing the reversed routing behavior relative to (b), with port 3 (port 2) signals redirected to port 1 (port 3), as indicated in the inset. A complete picture of the circulator functionality emerges when considered together with the results of Fig. 3c.

Figure 4b presents the measured transmission spectra ∣Sij∣ for a low-frequency excitation (4.9 GHz) within the left-handed passband. In this regime, ∣S23∣ ≫ ∣S13∣, indicating that the magnon predominantly emits into port 2 via spin-matched coupling to the rightward-energy-flow channel, while emission into port 1 is suppressed due to the spin mismatch. At a higher frequency (8.65 GHz) within the right-handed passband, the behavior reverses, with ∣S13∣ ≫ ∣S23∣ as shown in Fig. 4c, signifying that the emission is redirected toward port 1. This switching arises solely from reversing the sign of the refractive index at the corresponding operating frequency, which flips the energy-flow direction for a fixed transverse-spin orientation of the guided modes, demonstrating that SRIL enables frequency-division signal routing.

By examining Fig. 3c together with Fig. 4b, c, we identify that the device operates as a circulator whose circulation direction is determined by the sign of the refractive index of the TL. In the negative-index passband, transmission from port 1 to port 2 is strongly suppressed while that from port 2 to port 1 remains high [Fig. 3c (left)]. Combined with the nonreciprocal response in Fig. 4b, these results indicate that signals emitted from port 3 are directed to port 2 and those from port 1 are routed to port 3, forming the 3 → 2 → 1 → 3 circulation. In the positive-index passband, the behavior reverses, with transmission from port 2 to port 1 suppressed whereas that from port 1 to port 2 remains strong [Fig. 3c (right)]. Together with Fig. 4c, this shows that signals from port 3 are routed to port 1 and those from port 2 are routed to port 3, corresponding to the 3 → 1 → 2 → 3 circulation. This frequency-division reversal of circulation direction arises from the SRIL mechanism, which reverses the energy-flow direction for a fixed transverse-spin orientation of the guided modes. Unlike conventional circulators that rely on Faraday rotation and generally operate independent of frequency54,55, our device achieves frequency-selective signal routing within a compact, planar architecture.

Bandwidth expansion strategies

In addition to frequency-selective functionality, broadband operation is equally important for practical isolators and routing applications. Here we show that the instantaneous operation bandwidth of our device can be flexibly engineered and expanded using two complementary design strategies: increasing the effective magnetic volume (volume scaling) and cascading multiple magnonic resonators. The operation bandwidth is primarily governed by the radiative damping rate, which scales with the magnon–photon coupling strength and, consequently, with the volume of the magnetic material. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, replacing the standard 1-mm YIG sphere with a larger 1.5-mm sphere increases the mode overlap and enhances the radiative damping. The corresponding transmission spectra in Fig. 5b and c show that the nonreciprocal bandwidths are broadened in both the negative- and positive-index regimes compared with the 1-mm baseline (dashed curves), demonstrating that volume scaling provides a direct and effective route to bandwidth enhancement. Alternatively, a broader response can be realized by cascading multiple magnonic elements. Figure 5d depicts a configuration in which two 1-mm YIG spheres are integrated along the transmission line. By tuning their resonance frequencies to be closely spaced, a merged isolation window emerges (Fig. 5e, f) that is wider than that obtained with a single magnonic resonator. The integration of additional YIG spheres into the device is relatively straightforward and experimentally feasible. Together, these results confirm that the bandwidth of SRIL-based devices can be engineered to meet practical requirements through volume scaling or cascading strategies.

Discussion

We have experimentally demonstrated a spin–refractive-index locking (SRIL) mechanism in a composite right/left-handed transmission-line (CRLH-TL) platform hybridized with a magnonic system. By exploiting the intrinsic spin–momentum locking of surface-guided modes and the tunable refractive index of CRLH-TLs, we achieve chiral photon–magnon coupling whose directionality is determined by the refractive index sign. This controllable chirality enables two key device functionalities: frequency-dependent spin-selective magnon–photon coupling and the realization of a dual-mode microwave circulator, where the circulation direction reverses between the negative- and positive-index passbands. The demonstrated device exhibits high isolation performance comparable to commercial standards (15–18 dB), and its bandwidth and insertion loss can be further optimized through volume scaling, cascaded designs, and transmission-line optimization using low-loss printed circuit boards. Our approach establishes a compact and reconfigurable route for spin-selective nonreciprocal device and opens new avenues for reconfigurable microwave circuitry and spin-based signal processing.

The operating frequency of SRIL devices and index-controlled chiral light–matter interactions is not restricted to microwaves and can be extended to the terahertz and optical domain by selecting appropriate platforms and spinful materials. In the terahertz regime, the required signed-index (right- and left-handed) dispersion can be realized with terahertz metamaterials56, and the spinful matter can be supplied by low-loss ferrites or antiferromagnets exhibiting sub-THz to THz magnetic resonances57,58, which enables SRIL-governed chiral THz wave–magnon coupling and frequency-selective routing. In the optical domain, all-dielectric or plasmonic metamaterials that support right- and left-handed bands can provide the SRIL photonic platform59. Replacing magnons with circular-dichroic quantum emitters, such as Zeeman-resolved atomic transitions or spins in quantum dots60, preserves the same spin-matching selection rule for chiral coupling. These directions point to SRIL-based, spin-selective interfaces and frequency-division routers from THz to optical regime, with prospects for nonreciprocal integrated signal processing architectures and quantum interconnects between engineered waveguides and atomic or solid-state emitters.

Methods

Device design

The CRLH-TL shown in Fig. 2b is fabricated on a double-sided copper-clad F4BM265 substrate with a copper thickness of 0.035 mm, a dielectric thickness of 0.93 mm, and a relative permittivity of 2.65. Each unit cell consists of gap capacitors patterned on the top layer and complementary split-ring resonators (CSRRs) etched on the bottom layer, with detailed geometric parameters provided in the Supplementary Section 1. The commercially available yttrium iron garnet (YIG) spheres employed in the experiments have a diameter of 1 mm and feature ultralow intrinsic magnetic damping, making them ideal for coherent photon–magnon coupling studies.

Measurement procedure and calibration

A homogeneous static magnetic field oriented along the +z-direction is applied to the YIG sphere using an electromagnet, with the field strength precisely controlled by an external programmable DC current source (Supplementary Fig. S7). Prior to the measurements, the vector network analyzer (Keysight E5071C) is calibrated using a two-port through procedure to remove the path loss of the measurement chain and to place the reference planes at the device ports. The calibration is performed without the sample. The sample is then mounted and the complex S-parameters were recorded. The microwave transmission characteristics of the hybrid device were measured at an input power of −10 dBm to ensure operation within the linear response regime. All measurements are performed at room temperature.

CRLH transmission-line simulations

Full-wave simulations are carried out in CST Studio Suite (Microwave Studio) using the finite-element frequency-domain solver. To identify the right- and left-handed passbands, a single unit cell of the CRLH transmission line is modeled with Bloch-periodic boundary conditions along the propagation axis and symmetry boundaries in the transverse directions. Eigenmode and frequency-domain analyses with boundaries yield the dispersion β(ω). The sign of the effective index is determined from the relative orientation of the averaged Poynting vector 〈P〉 and the propagation vector β (negative index when 〈P〉 ⋅ β < 0).

For device-level transmission spectra, the full structure with input/output microstrip feeds is simulated using open (add space) boundaries and waveguide ports exciting the fundamental quasi-TEM mode. Complex S-parameters are extracted to locate the transmission passbands and compared with measurements under identical geometrical and material parameters. Conductor loss is included via the surface-impedance model, and the dielectric substrate is characterized by the permittivity and loss tangent specified in the experiment.

To visualize the spin of the edge fields relevant to SRIL, frequency-domain field monitors recorded E and H on planes parallel to the metallization near the device edges. The transverse spin density of the magnetic field is calculated in post-processing and plotted in normalized form, as depicted in Fig. 2. Reversing the propagation direction or sweeping from the left-handed to the right-handed band flips the sign of the transverse spin, confirming spin–index locking in simulation.
