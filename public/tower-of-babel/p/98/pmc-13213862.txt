Safety and Biodistribution of Topical Laquinimod, an Immunomodulator Targeting Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor: The LION Study

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the safety, tolerability, and distribution of laquinimod (LAQ) eye drops as a potential steroid-sparing anti-inflammatory therapy.DesignPhase I clinical trial conducted at the Byers Eye Institute, Stanford University. NCT06161415.ParticipantsPatients scheduled for elective pars plana vitrectomy for various noninfectious indications.MethodsEnrolled subjects were instructed on proper LAQ storage and administration and received the drops for 14 days prior to surgery. Patients were sequentially assigned into 3 groups receiving daily LAQ doses of 0.6, 1.2, and 1.8 mg, with dose escalation permitted only in the absence of safety concerns in the preceding cohort. A preoperative visit was performed following 7 days of LAQ administration. On the day of surgery, aqueous, vitreous, and plasma samples were collected for LAQ quantification. Two postoperative follow-up visits were conducted.Main Outcome MeasuresTolerability; safety of the LAQ eye drop, assessed via ophthalmic examination, multimodal imaging, laboratory tests, and electrocardiogram; and LAQ concentrations in aqueous, vitreous, and plasma.ResultsTen participants completed the 14-day topical LAQ and all study visits, except one where pars plana vitrectomy was canceled; however, subject completed LAQ course and safety follow-up. Given there were no dose-limiting toxicities, participants sequentially received daily doses of 0.6 mg (n = 4), 1.2 mg (n = 3), and 1.8 mg (n = 3). Mean total LAQ level was significantly higher in aqueous rising from 508.5 to 3640.0 ng/mL, exceeding vitreous levels that rose from 6.89 to 38.50 ng/mL. Dose-dependent drug levels were detected in vitreous, aqueous, and plasma. Laquinimod (LAQ), a first-in-class immunomodulator, is a quinoline-3-carboxamide derivative that targets the aryl hydrocarbon receptor signaling pathway which plays a key role in the pathogenesis of several ocular diseases, such as uveitis, neovascular (wet) age-related macular degeneration, and diabetic retinopathy (Fig 1A).1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Through aryl hydrocarbon receptor activation, LAQ skews the immune response toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype by suppressing NFκB signaling, reducing proinflammatory cytokine secretion, and Th1 and Th17 cell activity, while activating regulatory T-cells (Fig 1B).5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Beyond immunomodulation, LAQ promotes a sustained transition from an autoimmune-prone to a more balanced self-tolerant immune environment and enhances neuronal protection and repair mechanisms.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13Figure 1(A) Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling pathway and its involvement in various ocular disorders as shown in animal models.4 (B) Immune and neurological consequences of AhR activation.

Uveitis remains a major cause of irreversible visual impairment, accounting for 5% to 20% of legal blindness in developed countries.14, 15, 16, 17 Topical corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment for various uveitis types; however, their therapeutic effect is short-lived, and long-term use is constrained by adverse events (AEs).18, 19, 20, 21, 22 In addition, limited intraocular penetration significantly decreases their efficacy in posterior segment inflammation.22,23 Topical administration remains the least invasive and most convenient route for ocular drug delivery; however, anatomical and physiological ocular barriers pose substantial challenges.23, 24, 25, 26 Hence, a safe and effective topical steroid-sparing therapy capable of achieving adequate vitreous concentration remains an unmet clinical need.

Preclinical studies in experimental autoimmune uveitis and excessive ocular neovascularization models demonstrated that topical (eye drops) and oral (by gavage) LAQ therapy leads to a significant, dose-dependent reduction of uveitis with measurable concentrations in the posterior segment.7,8 Furthermore, it was found that LAQ might have antiangiogenic properties, reducing vessel formation in animal models with corneal neovascularization and neovascular age-related macular degeneration.9

Phase II and III studies of 0.6 mg daily oral LAQ revealed a favorable safety and tolerability profile in multiple sclerosis (MS) based on more than 14 000 patient-years of exposure. Moreover, LAQ exhibited significant effects on MS relapse-related endpoints, confirming its immunomodulatory and neuroprotective properties. The most common reported AEs included headache, arthralgia, and asymptomatic reversible liver enzyme elevation.10, 11, 12, 13

Given LAQ’s unique immunomodulatory and neuroprotective properties, along with promising ocular penetration in animal models, an innovative and proprietary hydrogel formulation for topical administration was optimized by Active Biotech to enhance distribution to the posterior segment of the eye. A phase I randomized, placebo-controlled study in healthy participants (Clinical Trial NCT05187403) demonstrated that topical LAQ (up to 0.6 mg for 21 days) was well tolerated with no clinically meaningful ocular or systemic AEs.27

To date, no topical steroid-sparing therapy has demonstrated consistent or meaningful penetration into the vitreous in humans. Hence, the index study was conducted to characterize LAQ ocular and systemic pharmacokinetics and its intraocular distribution in human subjects and to validate its safety and tolerability, as an initial step toward evaluating its therapeutic potential for ocular inflammation.

Methods

Design

The safety and biodistribution of topical Laquinimod, an ImmunomodulatOr targeting aryl hydrocarboN receptor (LION) study, is a prospective, unmasked, uncontrolled, single-center phase I clinical trial designed to evaluate the safety, tolerability, and distribution of LAQ administered as eye drops for 2 weeks in human participants, conducted at the Byers Eye Institute, Stanford University and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT06161415.

Ethical Conduct of the Study

The LION study was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration as well as the Stanford Institutional Review Board. All subjects provided written informed consent after explanation of the nature and possible consequences of the study. All study procedures adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. We certify that all applicable institutional and governmental regulations concerning the ethical use of human volunteers were followed during this research.

Study Participants and Enrollment Criteria

Eligible participants were adults (aged ≥18 years), capable of providing informed consent and scheduled for pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) for various noninfectious indications, including diagnostic vitrectomy, vitreomacular traction (VMT), and vitreous hemorrhage. Threshold for intraocular pressure (IOP) at screening was ≥5 mmHg and ≤22 mmHg. Women of childbearing potential and men were included only if they were willing to employ a reliable method of contraception.

Ocular exclusion criteria for the study eye included active periocular or ocular infectious disease (e.g., blepharitis, scleritis, conjunctivitis, keratitis, or endophthalmitis); intraocular/extraocular surgery or intravitreal steroids within 90 days of enrollment; intravitreal antivascular endothelial growth factor within 30 days of enrollment; and use of any topical cyclosporine, corticosteroid, or other calcineurin inhibitors within 14 days of enrollment.

Systemic exclusion criteria included participation in any other clinical trials within 30 days of enrollment; hepatic impairment; renal impairment with glomerular filtration rate ≤60 mL/min; positive pregnancy test; any history of HIV disease or other immunodeficiency disorder; history of acute hepatitis A (immunoglobulin M positive), hepatitis B, or hepatitis C; history of organ or bone marrow transplant; malignancy under active treatment; or any other acute or chronic medical condition that would, at the discretion of the study investigators, reasonably preclude participation in this clinical study. Moreover, use of a strong/moderate inhibitor or a strong inducer of CYP3A4 within 2 weeks of enrollment was prohibited as it may affect LAQ’s metabolism and plasma concentrations.

Study Activities

Participants were screened, enrolled, and sequentially assigned to 3 groups receiving daily LAQ doses of 0.6 (2 drops once daily), 1.2 (2 drops twice daily), or 1.8 (2 drops 3 times daily) mg, with dose escalation permitted only in the absence of safety concerns in the preceding group as illustrated in Figure 2.Figure 2Dose escalation study design. Enrolled subjects were given LAQ (10 mg/mL) in 5-mL sterile preservative-free eye droppers delivering a nominal drop size of 30 μL. Participants were instructed to store the medication refrigerated (2°C–8°C) and to allow equilibration to room temperature (15°C–25°C) before use. Furthermore, participants were educated to self-administer 2 drops (5 minutes apart) to the study eye once, twice or 3 times daily, according to their assigned group, for 14 days until the day of surgery (starting on Day 1 following medication pickup). Each participant was given a dosing card to record each drop instilled.

Rescue criteria included a ≥10-letter (≥2-line) BCVA decrease attributed to the study drug or the development of corneal epithelial defects or ocular surface inflammation (e.g., conjunctival redness, corneal infiltration, ulceration, thinning, or melting).

On the day of surgery, the final dose (2 drops) was administered 2 hours before the procedure. A 6-mL blood sample was collected within 60 minutes of last dosing. In the operating room, the study eye was anesthetized, prepared, and draped in the routine sterile fashion for ophthalmic surgery. Before commencing PPV, the retinal surgeon obtained ≥50 μL of aqueous fluid using a 30-gauge needle attached to a syringe through an anterior chamber (AC) paracentesis site. Subsequently, a standard 3-port vitrectomy was established without turning on the infusion line and ≥100 μL of undiluted vitreous fluid was collected using a vitreous cutting device. The aqueous and vitreous samples were collected within 60 minutes of the final dose, stored in sterile polypropylene collection tubes and immediately frozen in a liquid nitrogen bath, prior to being placed in a –80°C freezer. All the aqueous and vitreous samples were shipped in dry ice package to Aliri Bioanalysis (Colorado Springs, CO, USA), where a qualified liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectroscopy method was used to determine the concentrations of LAQ. Additionally, the plasma samples were shipped in dry ice package to Scantox (Gothenburg, Sweden), where a validated liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectroscopy method was used to determine LAQ plasma concentrations.

The free fraction of LAQ was estimated based on its plasma protein–binding characteristics. Laquinimod is highly protein-bound in plasma, with a free fraction of 1.8% and a bound fraction of 98.2%. It was assumed that albumin is the primary LAQ’s binding protein and it exhibits similar affinity in eye as in plasma. Plasma albumin concentration (40 mg/mL; ∼601 500 nM) and vitreous albumin concentration (0.293 mg/mL; ∼4400 nM) were used to approximate the equilibrium between bound and unbound LAQ. The plasma-derived binding constant was therefore applied to the lower albumin concentration in the vitreous to estimate the corresponding vitreous free fraction of LAQ. In both plasma and vitreous, albumin concentrations are significantly higher than LAQ concentrations, which simplify calculations. Such reasoning estimates that the free fraction of LAQ in vitreous is about 70%.

Postoperatively, subjects were followed on postoperative day (POD) 1 and again at least 7 days later with comprehensive ocular examinations (Table 1).

Intraocular and Plasma Concentrations of Laquinimod

Laquinimod was detected in aqueous and vitreous samples, demonstrating dose-dependent concentrations across the 3 dosing groups. Aqueous and vitreous samples from one subject in the first cohort and aqueous sample from another subject in the third cohort were excluded due to insufficient sample volume and improper handling. Table 2 illustrates concentrations of LAQ in aqueous, vitreous, and plasma samples from the participants.

The mean total LAQ levels were significantly higher in the aqueous compared to the vitreous across the 3 dosing groups. In the aqueous samples, mean LAQ levels increased from 508.5 ± 215.7 ng/mL at 0.6 mg LAQ/day to 1942.7 ± 1238.1 ng/mL at 1.2 mg LAQ/day and reached 3640.0 ± 3535.5 ng/mL at 1.8 mg LAQ/day, demonstrating a clear dose-dependent trend. Similarly, the mean LAQ values in the vitreous samples followed an upward trend, rising from 6.89 ± 4.69 ng/mL at 0.6 mg LAQ/day to 28.21 ± 26.12 ng/mL at 1.2 mg LAQ/day and peaking to 38.50 ± 22.78 ng/mL at 1.8 mg LAQ/day. Furthermore, the mean LAQ level in the plasma increased from 153.4 ± 9.0 ng/mL at 0.6 mg LAQ/day to 563.5 ± 281.9 ng/mL at 1.8 mg LAQ/day. Although intersubject variability was evident, particularly at higher doses, the overall results support a consistent dose-dependent pattern across the 3 sample sets as demonstrated in Figure 3. Correspondingly, mean unbound LAQ levels remained significant, increasing from 998 nM (356 ng/mL) at 0.6 mg/day LAQ to 7141 nM (2548 ng/mL) at 1.8 mg/day LAQ in aqueous and from 13 nM (4.64 ng/mL) at 0.6 mg/day LAQ to 76 nM (27.12 ng/mL) at 1.8 mg/day LAQ in vitreous. Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that topical LAQ achieves measurable vitreous concentration in a nearly linear, dose-dependent manner. No dose-limiting toxicities or medication-related AEs have been reported across the 3 dosing groups of 14-day LAQ regimen. Although plasma concentrations showed a dose-dependent pattern, they consistently remained within previously established safety ranges for all groups.28 Moreover, the unbound plasma concentrations, the pharmacologically active fraction, were consistently low across all dosing groups, which may support the favorable safety profile of LAQ.

The LION study was driven by the unmet need for a topical, steroid-sparing therapy with a favorable safety profile and sufficient ocular penetration, aiming to evaluate its therapeutic potential for ocular inflammatory diseases, particularly those involving the posterior segments. While several nonsteroidal topical agents are in early development for the treatment of anterior uveitis and ocular surface disease, none have reliably demonstrated penetration to the vitreous in humans. For instance, licaminlimab (OCS-02), an anti–TNF-α agent, demonstrated efficacy in reducing AC cells in noninfectious uveitis in a phase II randomized trial.29 Similarly, TRS01 (dazdotuftide), a TLR2 antagonist, showed comparable efficacy to steroids with a better IOP safety profile in the management of noninfectious anterior uveitis in the phase III TRS4VISION trial.30

Laquinimod reprograms antigen-presenting cells and shifts their functional profile toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype with strong preclinical data supporting its immunomodulatory effects and favorable retinal bioavailability following topical administration.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Respecting LAQ physicochemical properties and ocular delivery challenges, a novel hydrogel formulation was developed to enhance distribution and permeation of LAQ to the posterior part of the eye. The primary objective in developing the hydrogel was to increase ocular surface residence time, while the incorporation of penetration enhancers, solubilizers, and pH adjustments served to optimize corneal and scleral permeability thereby enabling effective posterior segment delivery.

The safety and tolerability outcomes in this study align with the previously completed phase I clinical trial (NCT05187403), which reported that 0.6 mg topical LAQ/day (10 mg/mL) for 21 days was well tolerated without evidence of ocular or systemic AEs.27 In our phase I cohort, daily doses of 0.6, 1.2, and 1.8 mg for 14 days were similarly well tolerated with no treatment-related AEs and no clinically meaningful alterations in any assessed parameters compared to baseline.

Notably, LAQ achieved evident ocular penetration following topical administration with dose-dependent drug levels detected in aqueous humor, vitreous, and plasma at daily doses of 0.6, 1.2, and 1.8 mg, respectively. The observed intersubject variability in drug levels can be explained by the heterogeneity of ocular coat thickness and the integrity of blood-ocular barrier which may account for the different pharmacokinetics of LAQ in the human eye. Detectable vitreous concentration of LAQ was achieved across the 3 tested doses, overcoming the challenge of attaining measurable drug levels in the posterior segment through topical delivery. In contrast, topical SAR 1118, an LFA-1 antagonist, exhibited measurable anterior-segment bioavailability but sparse vitreous concentrations below detection limit in phase Ib study (NCT01421498).31 Ultimately, it was granted US Food and Drug Administration approval as lifitegrast 5% ophthalmic solution for the management of dry eye disease.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, the only nonsteroidal topical therapy for macular edema,32 demonstrate limited penetration beyond AC. Heier et al and Schoenberger et al reported that topical ketorolac 0.4%, administered 4 times daily for 3 days prior to PPV, achieved a mean vitreous concentration of 3.2 ng/mL and 7.55 ng/mL, respectively, which was associated with a reduction in vitreous prostaglandin E2 levels.33,34 In comparison, topical LAQ achieved substantially higher vitreous exposure, with mean concentrations increasing from 6.89 to 38.50 ng/mL at 0.6 and 1.8 mg daily dosing, respectively, suggesting therapeutically relevant LAQ levels that support its potential as an anti-inflammatory treatment for posterior segment inflammation. Furthermore, the calculated mean free LAQ concentrations in the vitreous were 13, 55, and 76 nM at doses of 0.6, 1.2, and 1.8 mg, respectively. These unbound vitreous LAQ levels exceeded the estimated effective cerebrospinal fluid concentrations of free LAQ (10–20 nM) in MS patients receiving the effective oral dose of 0.6 mg daily.35

In the LION study, only one patient was pseudophakic; therefore, there were insufficient data to assess the impact of lens status on LAQ penetration. The detection of vitreous levels in phakic patients supports that topical LAQ can reach the posterior segment through an intact native lens. In addition, due to the small sample size, we could not correlate LAQ distribution with the underlying disease. Nevertheless, 3 patients with VMT showed adequate vitreous levels, supporting LAQ’s ability to penetrate intact ocular barriers which is consistent with its known capacity to cross the blood-brain barrier in MS.

The physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties of LAQ are consistent with its distribution within the intraocular space. Laquinimod’s relatively low molecular weight and moderate lipophilicity facilitate transcellular diffusion across the corneal and conjunctival epithelia. The hydrogel-based formulation enhances precorneal residence time and prolongs ocular exposure. Additionally, LAQ may also diffuse from the systemic circulation into intraocular tissues through transcellular permeability across ocular barriers.36,37

To further understand the intraocular distribution of topical LAQ, a simulation model was developed to trace its distribution into the vitreous and aqueous. The model was built using Stella Architect software (Isee Systems) using the principles of previous models that accurately predicted drug distribution from systemic circulation of rabbits and humans into the vitreous.38, 39, 40, 41 Systemic absorption from LAQ eye drops was built into the model using known pharmacokinetic parameters (volume of distribution 15 L, elimination rate constant 0.00639 h-1).36 Simulations with systemic LAQ bioavailability of 70% provided plasma levels that matched (90%–105%) the mean steady state concentrations of LAQ in plasma. Ocular LAQ distribution from plasma was simulated by taking into account LAQ concentrations in plasma, unbound drug fractions in plasma (0.018), aqueous and vitreous (0.7), volumes of drug distribution in aqueous (0.7 mL) and vitreous (4 mL), and estimated distribution clearances across blood-aqueous humor (1.2 mL/hour) and blood-retinal barriers (1.0 mL/hour).36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41 As shown earlier for statins and other drug molecules, our LAQ model assumes that only the unbound drug in plasma crosses the blood-retinal barrier. The model is highly sensitive to unbound LAQ concentration in plasma, whereas the simulations are minimally affected by the ocular model parameters.36,39,40

The simulation model accurately described plasma concentrations, and these levels were used to estimate systemic LAQ entry into the vitreous and aqueous. The observed aqueous levels were 100 to 1000 times higher than those predicted based on systemic distribution, indicating direct ocular penetration to AC. Similarly, vitreous concentrations of total and unbound LAQ were 1.6 to 3.1 and 4.5 to 9.6 times higher, respectively, than predicted from systemic absorption. These findings suggest that a major portion of unbound LAQ in both aqueous and vitreous is derived from local ocular uptake rather than distribution from systemic plasma. Delivery of LAQ to the vitreous takes place most likely through the conjunctiva, sclera, and retinal pigment epithelium–choroid complex, allowing penetration into the posterior segment of the human eye. The simulations support local administration of LAQ instead of its systemic use in ocular therapy.

As with all phase I pilot studies, the small sample size limits our ability to draw conclusions regarding interpatient differences or disease-specific effects. Further clinical studies are warranted to elucidate the clinical implications of these findings and to determine the optimal therapeutic dose as well as to evaluate anti-inflammatory efficacy and long-term safety of topical LAQ in patients with ocular inflammation.

Conclusion

Topical LAQ (10 mg/mL) agent was well tolerated at daily doses of 0.6, 1.2, and 1.8 mg, with no drug-related AEs or clinically meaningful changes in BCVA, IOP, ocular examinations, multimodal imaging, CBC, CMP, and ECG compared to baseline. Dose-dependent, therapeutically relevant LAQ levels were consistently detected in aqueous humor, vitreous, and plasma. Together, these findings support the therapeutic potential of topical LAQ as a novel steroid-sparing immunomodulatory topical agent for inflammatory eye diseases, including those involving the posterior segment.
