Highlighting Rigor, Reproducibility, and Transparency of Research in an Academic CV
Authors: Mario Malički, Zach Chandler, Steven N. Goodman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn academia, the curriculum vitae (CV) is the central document used for hiring, promotion, or tenure applications. The content and format of these CVs and accompanying documents varies among institutions and is a direct reflection of the institution's values. Few institutions consider rigor, reproducibility, or transparency practices for hiring, promotion, or tenure decisions. We developed a new CV template for Stanford's School of Medicine for researchers to highlight their use of those practices, making it possible to evaluate faculty not just on research reports, but how they do the research itself. The new additions include listing (and linking) availability of protocols, analysis plans, analytic code, data, public accessibility of results for research outputs; registration number and result reporting for clinical trials; details of peer review activities; and candidate's persistent identifier (e.g., ORCID iD). We also include a variety of practices supporting research rigor and reproducibility that can be described in a candidate's narrative statement. For those who dedicate time and resources to maximize the rigor, reproducibility, and transparency of their work, our new CV template makes these efforts visible, a precondition for valuing them. This can serve as a model for other institutions, while future advances in scholarly metadata and systems could facilitate easier collection and reporting.

Introduction

The academic curriculum vitae (CV) is a requirement for decisions about hiring, promotion, or tenure. The CV contents are typically dictated by templates and their required elements. Those elements greatly differ between countries and institutions [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7], as do criteria for advancement. Since any element not in the academic CV cannot be considered for professional advancement, the CV therefore embodies institutional values, powerfully impacting how academic faculty conduct their research and their careers. This behavior is also shaped by formal decisional criteria. While some institutions use quantitative criteria, for example, minimum four publications co‐authored with a student, principal investigator on at least one government grant, etc. [8], others provide more qualitative phrasing (e.g., “demonstrated excellence in the field”), which can be harder to assess [9].

A numerical publication requirement incentivizes faculty to maximize those numbers, potentially at the expense of quality or more impactful work (e.g., longer term projects that generate fewer publications) [10]. Practices recommended to increase biomedical research rigor, reproducibility, and transparency can increase the time needed to plan, conduct, or report research, so if institutions want their faculty to adopt such practices, they must be rewarded for their use, which is done at few institutions. Analysis of hiring and promotion criteria in 92 biomedical institutions (randomly selected from 2017 Leiden ranking of world universities) showed that only one mentioned data sharing, and none mentioned use of reporting guidelines, study preregistration, or open access [1]. Similarly, none of 15 top Canadian universities mentioned these criteria in the latest version of their guidelines, from 2004 to 2018 [2]. In 2019, out of 129 randomly selected North American and Canadian institutions, only 5% mentioned open accessibility of publications [3]. In 2020, none of 25 German medical university tenure application forms mentioned preregistration, protocol, data, code sharing, open access, or trial results reporting [5]. Finally, an analysis of 107 Universities documents from 2019 to 2021 across seven countries (Austria, Brasil, Germany, India, Portugal, UK, USA) showed no mention of open access or data sharing [6].

Recognizing this gap, we developed a new template for the Stanford School of Medicine's academic CV and candidate's statement that makes researchers' rigor, reproducibility, and transparency practices visible in the academic record.

Stanford School of Medicine CV Template and Candidate Statement

The Stanford University Faculty Handbook [9] and criteria [11] as well as Research Policy Handbook [12] are the main policy documents describing hiring, promotion, and tenure processes at Stanford University. The Stanford School of Medicine has a guide “How to Organize Your CV/Candidate's Statement” [13] that includes sample CV templates, of which the one for Professoriate consists of 15 required sections: (1) Personal Information; (2) Educational Background; (3) Professional Appointments; (4) Honors and Awards; (5) Scholarly Publications; (6) Grants; (7) Clinical Trials; (8) Patents; (9) Editorial Service; (10) Service as Grant Reviewer; (11) University Administrative Services; (12) Service to Professional Organizations; (13) Presentations; (14) Community Service; and (15) Trainees. The latest version of the template, updated in July 2021, served as our starting point for the new template development.

In addition to the academic CV, all Stanford appointment and promotion packages include a three page narrative “candidate's statement,” that provides a “faculty member the opportunity to explain their contributions, achievements and future plans in the research, clinical, teaching and administrative arenas.” The following key elements are recommended for scholarship: Major contributions and achievements.Major publications and scientific discoveries and how they have impacted knowledge/further research in the field and/or patient care.If you have performed a significant amount of collaborative work, which Stanford also values, comment on that work and your role in it.

The emphasis in the Stanford researcher assessment package is on research outputs, not how the research is conducted. But in an effort to focus on the quality of the researcher's best work, and not on the volume of work, it requests that bibliometric indicators not be part of the evaluation, stating: “The School of Medicine does not use citation metrics or impact factors (e.g., H‐index, Google Scholar score, etc.), so please do not include these.”

New CV and Candidate Statement Template Development

We know of no systematic analyses of academic CV templates from universities. In recent years, funders and other institutions in Europe and the US have piloted narrative CV templates, often building upon research assessment reform recommendations [14]. An overview of those reforms is presented in our Table 1 (updated and adapted from 2018 Moher et al. [15]). A 2025 scoping review of 22 funders' narrative CV templates indicated that only 1 (5%) recommended mentioning open source, open research, and open access, while 4 (18%) mentioned preprints, and 7 (32%) data sharing [16]. Our recommendations were developed from those, including from biosketches and templates from the National Institutes of Health (NIH), the National Science Foundation (NSF), and the Institute of Education Sciences (IES) [17]; and various public dashboards, as well as transparency indicators and badges (e.g., SCIscore [18]), Transparency audit and leaderboard [19], ResearchSquare methods and data sharing badges [20], Dimensions AI Trust Markers [21], ScholarGPS [22], and PLOS Open Science Indicators [23].

Scientific rigor is typically defined as the strict application of the scientific method to ensure robust and unbiased experimental design, methodology, analysis, interpretation, and reporting of results [24]. A study has good reproducibility if its design, data gathering, analysis, and inferences are reported with sufficient transparency and completeness so that it can be re‐run and corroborated. Reproducibility requires transparency, that is, the availability and accuracy of study protocols, registrations, statistical analysis plans, and methods. While computational reproducibility implies obtaining the same (statistical) results by re‐running the published analysis using the researchers' detailed methods and shared code or data [25, 26].

We present them here with explanatory notes. The full CV template with Rigor and Reproducibility items and the accompanying guideline document can be found at the Stanford's Office of Academic Affairs website [13]. Discussion

The academic CV is a statement of institutional values, reflecting activities and achievements that the institution deems meritorious of academic rewards (e.g., appointment, promotion, and other forms of recognition). The first among these is how the institution assesses the importance and quality of research, which in turn profoundly affects how faculty members conduct their work. A sine‐qua‐non of high quality research is that the findings or claims are true, but that can only be ascertained over times typically longer than needed for institutional decisions. More easily measured short‐term bibliometric proxies are therefore often used, such as the number of publications and journal prestige. These imperfect measures often affect research quality adversely, as has been described in many reports calling for research assessment reform [29, 30, 31]. Several investigations have also demonstrated that the quality and completeness of reporting in published papers is typically insufficient to reproduce the research or assess the quality of findings [32]. Stanford's new CV template allows research quality to be assessed not just from the published record, but from the reliability of the methods used to produce it and the openness of the research to scrutiny by others. It complements the Stanford efforts to reduce the role of bibliometrics in such assessments.

The value of research transparency is not just for external scrutiny; it might be most important for the research team itself. The same practices making it possible for others to assess the quality and integrity of the work allow team members to do the same. We believe a major contributor to non‐reproducible research is data pipelines that only specific individuals within a research group understand, or that involve multiple hand‐offs among team members. The principal investigator attesting to the trustworthiness of the data pipeline may have never observed or even fully understood it, particularly with large datasets. That attestation, recommended by ICMJE and required by some journals [33, 34], is often merely a statement that the PI trusts the people involved. Data pipelines supported by systems allowing interrogation by the entire team, or at a minimum by the research guarantor, are much more likely to withstand external scrutiny. Our new CV template allows these processes to be assessed as part of a researcher's evaluation, and can thereby be an incentive to use them.

The new template, however, cannot be fully effective in isolation. The values it embodies need to be supported by other aspects of the institutional enterprise, from education to monitoring and technical support, and even as a routine part of research presentations. Similarly, these values must be ultimately embraced by other institutions and within disciplines. Letters from external referees play a large role in faculty assessment, and if those referees come from places or disciplines that do not value the practices included in the new template, that will be reflected in their letters.

Research has also shown that students and early career researchers feel more is expected of them than of the previous generations, that is, that they require more publications for promotion and that those publications are expected to adhere to open, rigorous, and transparent practices. However, they also perceive that these practices have a positive effect on the credibility and visibility of their work, as well as future collaborations, and they welcome better alignment between academic norms and reward systems [35]. Recent increase of code and data sharing mandates or replication packages by scholarly journals [36], especially in the fields of psychology, sociology, and economics, point to cultural shifts happening across disciplines. This has also been followed by the increase in preprint uptake across disciplines, with more than a quarter of recent mathematics outputs being preprinted [37].

This Stanford new template is currently optional, to not penalize candidates who do not use the included practices. It is hoped that as its usage increases and as rigor and reproducibility practices become more widely used that many of its elements will be incorporated into the standard CV.

Limitations

We do not yet know how often faculty will use this CV format and how it will affect the adoption of the practices it reports. We will be monitoring both of those elements going forward. Also, the new CV elements need to be added manually. While more services are integrating ORCID iDs with publishing and submission systems, journals and publishers still rarely implement and share machine readable metadata for these items. Furthermore, no current reference management software allows easy addition of those items, even when they are available as relationship metadata (e.g., in Crossref, or OpenAlex). We hope that future developments will allow easy formatting of references to match the recommended or similar formats, and even expand it to include additional information (e.g., conflict of interest and ethics statement availability, funding information, CREDIT contribution, etc.). Examples of recent developments in this area include publication fact labels announced for Open Journal Systems [38], and Collaborative Metadata Enrichment Taskforce (COMET) which is working on, among other things, increasing linkage of preprints, data, and code, with peer‐reviewed publications [39].

Conclusions

The R&R CV allows faculty efforts to maximize the rigor and reproducibility of their research to be recognized and rewarded. It aligns with Stanford School of Medicine's commitment to encouraging these practices and to reducing dependence on bibliometrics in researcher assessments. The more universities that adopt models like these, the more these practices can become normative.
