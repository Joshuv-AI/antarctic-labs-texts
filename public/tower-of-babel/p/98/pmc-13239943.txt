Bayesian Evaluation of Treatment Effect of Avelumab Plus Axitinib for Advanced Renal Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Wataru Fukuokaya, Keiichiro Mori, Takafumi Yanagisawa, Kagenori Ito, Yu Imai, Fumihiko Urabe, Pawel Rajwa, Shahrokh F Shariat, Takahiro Kimura, Akihiro Hirakawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDespite not achieving statistical significance, the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial indicated a potentially clinically relevant effect size (hazard ratio [HR], 0.88; 95% confidence interval, 0.75 to 1.04) on overall survival (OS) favoring avelumab plus axitinib over sunitinib for advanced renal cell carcinoma (aRCC). To better interpret these findings, we performed a Bayesian evaluation.MethodsWe reanalyzed individual participant data from the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial using a Bayesian framework. Prior distributions were specified for varying levels of enthusiasm and skepticism for avelumab plus axitinib, and a prior from a meta‐analysis of similar trials. The posterior probability of any OS benefit with avelumab plus axitinib, Pr(HR < 1.0), and of benefit exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID; HR = 0.75), Pr(HR < MCID), were estimated using Bayesian Cox models. Bayesian hierarchical models evaluated treatment effect heterogeneity across International Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma Database Consortium risk groups and programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1) status.ResultsRegardless of the prior assumptions used, Pr(HR < 1.0) was consistently high (93.0% to 95.5%). However, Pr(HR < MCID) was low (1.0% to 3.8%). Results were similar when the prior was informed by pooled data from similar trials. In subgroup analyses, Pr(HR < MCID) varied by risk group: 63.4% for poor‐risk, 34.9% for favorable‐risk, and 0.9% for intermediate‐risk aRCC. This probability was low regardless of PD‐L1 status.ConclusionsThis exploratory Bayesian reanalysis complements the interpretation of the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial and offers a probabilistic perspective beyond a dichotomous (i.e., significant/nonsignificant) interpretation.Keywords: Bayesian analysis, immune checkpoint inhibitor, overall survival, renal cell carcinoma

Introduction

Combination therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) is one of the standards of care for patients with advanced renal cell carcinoma (aRCC), alongside ICI‐ICI combinations and, in selected patients, TKI monotherapy [1]. Four randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have evaluated this combination therapy against TKI monotherapy in this patient population [2, 3, 4, 5]. One of these, the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial, compared avelumab (an anti‐programmed death‐ligand 1 [PD‐L1] antibody) plus axitinib (a vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitor) with sunitinib (another vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitor) for aRCC [3].

The final pre‐specified frequentist analysis of the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial showed that the hazard ratio (HR) for overall survival (OS) for avelumab plus axitinib compared with sunitinib was 0.88 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.75 to 1.04; p = 0.067) for the overall trial population and 0.86 (95% CI, 0.70 to 1.06; p = 0.076) for the PD‐L1 positive population. This led the authors to conclude that. “OS analyses favored avelumab + axitinib versus sunitinib but did not reach statistical significance” [6]. This “non‐significant” conclusion, however, presents a clinical challenge: the frequentist framework does not quantify the likelihood of a treatment benefit. A Bayesian framework can directly estimate such probabilities and provide a more intuitive assessment of treatment effect.

Therefore, to complement the interpretation of the trial results, we reanalyzed the individual participant data from the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial.

Materials and Methods

This study followed the Bayesian analysis reporting guideline and the guidance for conducting and reporting a Bayesian reanalysis of a trial [7, 8].

Data Source

We analyzed individual participant data from the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02684006), an international, open‐label RCT comparing avelumab plus axitinib versus sunitinib in patients with aRCC with a clear cell component [3]. Key eligibility criteria included age 18 years or older, previously untreated aRCC, at least one measurable lesion per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1, and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 0 or 1. Patients across all International Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma Database Consortium (IMDC) prognostic risk groups were enrolled. Full eligibility criteria are detailed in the original report [3].

Bayesian Analysis

Using a Bayesian analytic framework, we reanalyzed data from the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial. Unlike frequentist methods, the Bayesian framework formally integrates preexisting evidence or assumptions (the “prior” distribution) with the observed trial data (the “likelihood”) using Bayes' theorem to yield the posterior probability distribution of the treatment effect [9, 10]. Prior beliefs are expressed as probability density distributions, where the variance (width) reflects the degree of uncertainty about the treatment effect.

Definition of Clinical Benefit Threshold

This reanalysis evaluated the posterior probability that avelumab plus axitinib exceeded two thresholds: any benefit, defined as HR < 1.0, and benefit exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). For trials with a control arm exceeding a median OS of 36 months, the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (ESMO‐MCBS) v2.0 defines an HR of 0.75 as the initial cutoff for grading treatment effect [11]. The American Society of Clinical Oncology working group similarly recommends HR thresholds of 0.75 to 0.80 for defining clinically meaningful outcomes in advanced solid tumors [12]. Based on these converging recommendations, we used an HR of 0.75 as the MCID. Hereafter, Pr(HR < 1.0) denotes the posterior probability of any benefit, and Pr(HR < MCID) denotes the posterior probability of benefit exceeding the MCID.

Priors

Three types of statistical priors were used: priors assuming no effect with large uncertainty, reference priors, and meta‐analytic predictive (MAP) priors [13]. The definitions of the first two priors are summarized in Table 1 and visually represented in Figure 1. The reference priors were defined according to a previous study [14]. In this analysis, all priors were assumed to be normally distributed on the log‐transformed HR (log[HR]) scale. Between‐prior heterogeneity was evaluated using τ2, estimated within a random‐effects meta‐analysis model.

MAP priors were constructed to incorporate evidence from similar RCTs of ICI‐TKI combinations versus TKI alone in aRCC. We systematically searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library for eligible RCTs published before January 31, 2025 (PROSPERO, CRD42025650154), following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses statement [15]. When multiple publications existed for the same trial, only the most recent data were included. Full search strategies are provided in Table S1. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool [16].

Trial‐level data were pooled using a Bayesian hierarchical model (BHM) with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling (4 chains; 20,000 warm‐up and 20,000 saved iterations); convergence was assessed by visual inspection of trace plots. The model used a weakly informative prior for the pooled effect (mean = 0, standard deviation [SD] = 2) and a half‐normal prior for between‐trial heterogeneity (mean = 0, SD = 1). The resulting MCMC samples were converted to a parametric mixture representation using the expectation–maximization algorithm [17]. The pooled trials evaluated different ICI‐TKI combinations rather than avelumab plus axitinib specifically, so the MAP prior was robustified by incorporating a weakly informative prior (mean = 0, SD = 2) to down‐weight the influence of prior studies [18]. This down‐weighting was applied to varying extents, ranging from 0% to 100%, to reflect the uncertainty about the effect estimates from earlier studies.

Bayesian Cox Proportional Hazards Regression Model

For each prior, a Bayesian Cox proportional hazards regression model estimated the posterior distribution of log(HR) for avelumab plus axitinib. Missing baseline covariate data were multiply imputed 100 times using joint modeling multiple imputation [19] posterior stacking combined draws across imputed datasets [20]. For imputation, 10,000 burn‐in MCMC samples were generated to estimate model parameters, with missing values sampled every 5000 iterations. For each imputed dataset, the Bayesian Cox proportional hazards regression model used 4 chains with 2000 burn‐in and 2000 saved iterations per chain. Treatment effect estimates and 95% Bayesian credible intervals (CrIs) were derived from the median, 2.5th, and 97.5th percentiles of the stacked posterior distribution. Posterior probabilities of exceeding prespecified thresholds were then estimated from this distribution. Models adjusted for previously identified prognostic factors: age (continuous), sex (male or female), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (0 or 1), body mass index (continuous), prior nephrectomy (no or yes), IMDC risk group (favorable, intermediate, and poor), and tumor PD‐L1 expression (low or high) [21, 22, 23, 24]. A weakly informative prior (mean = 0, SD = 2) was used for covariate coefficients, corresponding to plausible effect sizes between HRs of 0.02 and 50.4.

A separate Bayesian Cox hierarchical model assessed heterogeneity of the OS treatment effect across IMDC risk groups and tumor PD‐L1 expression. By sharing information across subgroups, this approach yields more reliable estimates of conditional treatment effects than standard subgroup analyses, particularly for smaller subgroups [25]. Prior specifications matched the main model: a weakly informative prior (mean = 0, SD = 2) for beta coefficients and a half‐normal prior (mean = 0, SD = 1) for between‐subgroup heterogeneity, with the same MCMC settings.

All analyses were conducted in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), version 4.5.0, using the brms package to run Stan [26].

Results

Bayesian Analysis Using Reference Priors

Baseline characteristics and Kaplan–Meier curves for OS are shown in Table 2 and Figure 2. Median OS was 44.8 months (95% CI, 39.7 to 51.1) with avelumab plus axitinib and 38.9 months (95% CI, 31.4 to 45.2) with sunitinib. Using reference priors, Pr(HR < 1.0) ranged from 93.0% (skeptical prior) to 95.5% (enthusiastic prior), whereas Pr(HR < MCID) ranged from 1.0% to 3.8% (Figure 3). The τ2 estimate was < 0.01, indicating minimal between‐prior heterogeneity. Trace plots suggested satisfactory MCMC convergence. (Figure S1).

Subgroup Analysis With Bayesian Cox Hierarchical Model

Posterior HR distributions by subgroup are shown in. Figure 7. Among IMDC subgroups, the median HR (95% CrI) was 0.70 (0.47 to 0.97) for poor‐risk, 0.95 (0.78 to 1.17) for intermediate‐risk, and 0.80 (0.56 to 1.11) for favorable‐risk. Pr(HR < 1.0) was 98.5% for poor‐risk, 67.9% for intermediate‐risk, and 91.7% for favorable‐risk; the corresponding Pr(HR < MCID) values were 63.4%, 0.9%, and 34.9%, respectively. For tumor PD‐L1 status, the median HR (95% CrI) was 0.85 (0.70 to 1.03) for PD‐L1 positive and 0.92 (0.71 to 1.23) for PD‐L1 negative. Pr(HR < 1.0) was 95.4% for PD‐L1 positive and 73.5% for PD‐L1 negative, with Pr(HR < MCID) of 9.7% and 6.0%, respectively. MCMC convergence was satisfactory. (Figure S6).

Discussion

This Bayesian reanalysis of the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial supplements the original frequentist analysis by quantifying the treatment effect of avelumab plus axitinib while incorporating prior knowledge and assumptions. Bayesian analysis can formally synthesize external evidence with current trial data, whereas frequentist methods analyze each trial in isolation [13]. Bayesian methods are receiving growing attention from regulators and clinicians, as reflected by the recent US Food and Drug Administration draft guidance on Bayesian methodology in clinical trials [27]. Our reanalysis yielded two main findings.

First, regardless of whether we assumed a skeptical, neutral, or optimistic view of the treatment effect, and even after incorporating evidence from similar ICI‐TKI trials, Pr(HR < 1.0) was consistently high, ranging from 93.0% to 96.3%. However, Pr(HR < MCID) (defined as HR < 0.75) was substantially lower, ranging from only 1.0% to 4.0%. These findings suggest that although avelumab plus axitinib very likely offers some OS advantage over sunitinib, this advantage is unlikely to reach a clinically meaningful magnitude. The ESMO Clinical Practice Guideline does not recommend this combination over single‐agent TKI therapy (level of evidence I, grade D; ESMO‐MCBS v1.1 score: 3) [1], and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guideline does not include it among preferred first‐line regimens [28]. Our analysis provides a probabilistic basis for these guideline positions.

Second, subgroup analysis using the Bayesian hierarchical model showed that the probability of a clinically meaningful benefit varies markedly across patient subgroups. The probability was low irrespective of PD‐L1 status, consistent with the recognized unreliability of PD‐L1 as a predictive biomarker in renal cancer [1]. By contrast, for poor‐risk aRCC, the frequentist analysis reported a favorable HR of 0.58 [6], and it showed a substantially higher probability of clinically meaningful benefit (HR < 0.75) than in the overall population.

However, the poor‐risk subgroup finding must be interpreted in the context of first‐line treatment options: other ICI‐based combinations have shown OS benefit in the overall population and carry the strongest guideline recommendations (ESMO level I, grade A) [1], with even larger effects in intermediate‐ and poor‐risk subgroups [29, 30, 31]. Accordingly, the poor‐risk observation does not support routine substitution of avelumab plus axitinib for these preferred regimens. Rather, it represents a hypothesis‐generating observation that may be considered only when preferred ICI‐based regimens are unavailable or contraindicated, and awaits prospective confirmation.

The Bayesian approach used here has several advantages over the frequentist framework [9, 14, 32, 33]. The Bayesian framework represents the treatment effect as a probability distribution and directly estimates the probability that the effect exceeds a meaningful threshold, consistent with clinical reasoning that weighs competing probabilities of benefits and harms. By contrast, the frequentist p value describes the probability of observing data at least as extreme as those observed, assuming no true treatment effect. Bayesian analysis can also incorporate diverse background beliefs or preexisting evidence through priors as a complement to traditional frequentist hypothesis testing.

Beyond these methodological advantages, the probability statements from this reanalysis may inform shared decision‐making between clinicians and patients. They may also support the design of future trials, particularly in the poor‐risk subgroup, where the posterior distribution from this reanalysis could itself serve as an informative prior.

However, this analysis has several limitations. First, Bayesian analysis requires specifying priors about the likely treatment effect before examining the data because different assumptions can lead to different conclusions. Although our results remained consistent when we varied these assumptions, some residual influence of prior choices cannot be excluded. Second, the earlier trials we used to inform our analysis evaluated different ICI‐TKI combinations rather than avelumab plus axitinib specifically. This raises the question of how directly applicable their results are to the present trial. To address this concern, we down‐weighted the influence of these earlier trials to varying degrees. Third, this Bayesian reanalysis was not preplanned but rather conducted after the original trial results were available, so the estimated probabilities should be viewed as supportive evidence rather than definitive conclusions [7]. Fourth, our subgroup analyses assume that treatment effects are related across patient subgroups and may still be limited by small sample sizes and differences between subgroups that were not measured. In particular, patients with IMDC poor risk accounted for approximately 16% of the trial population, making this the smallest of the three risk groups. Although the Bayesian hierarchical model borrows information across subgroups to produce more precise estimates, the higher probability of clinically meaningful benefit in the poor‐risk subgroup remains hypothesis‐generating and requires confirmation in an independent dataset before it should inform clinical practice. Finally, the threshold we used to define a clinically meaningful benefit (HR < 0.75) is a recognized benchmark but not universally agreed upon. In the setting of contemporary first‐line aRCC trials, in which the control arm achieves a prolonged median OS, less stringent thresholds (e.g., HR < 0.80 or HR < 0.85) might also be considered clinically meaningful. Use of such thresholds would raise the numerical posterior probabilities but would not alter the qualitative conclusion that the probability of benefit exceeding the MCID is low in the overall population.

In conclusion, this exploratory Bayesian reanalysis of the JAVELIN Renal 101 trial moves beyond the significant/nonsignificant dichotomy and complements the original frequentist results by directly estimating the probability of benefit under a range of assumptions. Our analysis consistently showed a high probability (93.0% to 96.3%) of some OS benefit (HR < 1.0), but a very low probability (1.0% to 4.0%) that this benefit reaches the clinically meaningful threshold (HR < 0.75) overall. For patients with poor‐risk disease, the probability of a clinically meaningful benefit was substantially higher. By translating the JAVELIN Renal 101 data into probability statements, this reanalysis offers clinicians a more informative basis for treatment decisions than the conventional significant/nonsignificant framework alone.
