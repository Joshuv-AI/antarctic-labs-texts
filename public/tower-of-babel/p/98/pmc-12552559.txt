Microbiota-dependent Formylated Peptide Receptor (Fpr1/2) Signaling Regulates Enteric Nervous System Development and Gastrointestinal Motility in Mice
Authors: Bindu Chandrasekharan, Huixia Wu, Charlie Smoller, Jiyoung Kim, Alexandra A. Wolfarth, Richard Eboka, Darra Boyer, Amanda J. Metzger, Caroline R. Addis, Ken Liu, Shanthi Srinivasan, Andrew J. Macpherson, Rheinallt M. Jones, Andrew S. Neish
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsFormylated peptide receptors 1 and 2 (Fpr1/2 or FPRs) are G-protein-coupled pattern recognition receptors that bind bacterial formylated peptides. The role of FPRs in enteric nervous system (ENS) development and gastrointestinal (GI) motility is unknown.MethodsWe generated mice with germline, epithelial-, and neural crest-specific deletion of the Fpr1/2 locus and assessed ENS structure and GI motility. We also employed a gestational microbiota suppression model using antibiotic-treated wild-type dams, alongside a transient gestational colonization model in pregnant germ-free mice using auxotrophic Escherichia coli, to determine whether temporary restoration of maternal microbiota-Fpr1/2 signaling can regulate embryonic ENS development. Enteric neuronal density at embryonic day 18.5, postnatal day 2 (P2), and in adult mice was assessed by CLARITY immunostaining and confocal imaging of pan neuronal markers (Tuj1, HuD, and Peripherin). Fecal formylated peptide (fMLF) content was measured by mass spectrometry. GI motility was assessed by stool frequency, total GI transit time, and FITC-dextran intestinal transit assay.ResultsGermline, epithelial, and neural crest-specific Fpr1/2 deletion resulted in reduced fetal, postnatal, and post weaning ENS density and epithelial innervation with reduced GI motility in post weaned mice. The 3-week-old pups derived from antibiotic-treated pregnant wild-type dams showed significantly reduced ENS density and GI motility, with reduced fecal fMLF. Critically, transient gestational colonization of pregnant germ-free mice with auxotrophic E. coli significantly improved enteric neuronal density and GI motility in 6-week-old offspring.ConclusionsCollectively our data show that microbiota-Fpr1/2 signaling in the murine gut is critical for normal ENS development and GI motility and identify Fpr1/2 as a potential therapeutic target to correct GI hypomotility. Our data also suggests a cautious approach to antibiotic usage during pregnancy.

SummaryMicrobiota-driven formylated peptide receptor (Fpr1/2) signaling is essential for enteric nervous system development and gastrointestinal motility. Disruption of Fpr1/2 signaling by genetic deletion or maternal antibiotic exposure impairs enteric nervous system density and function, highlighting Fpr1/2 as a potential therapeutic target for gastrointestinal hypomotility.

Summary

Microbiota-driven formylated peptide receptor (Fpr1/2) signaling is essential for enteric nervous system development and gastrointestinal motility. Disruption of Fpr1/2 signaling by genetic deletion or maternal antibiotic exposure impairs enteric nervous system density and function, highlighting Fpr1/2 as a potential therapeutic target for gastrointestinal hypomotility.

The gastrointestinal (GI) tract is inhabited by a highly diverse and dynamic gut microbiota, which play a key role in the regulation of a myriad of host physiological processes.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 These interactions serve as an elegant example of prokaryotic-eukaryotic interkingdom symbiosis, which is crucial to the health of the host. Using murine systems, we and others have shown that microbiota in the gut lumen modulates host processes that attenuate proinflammatory signaling and activate cytoprotective and reparative responses including epithelial proliferation, migration, and wound healing.5,8,9 Recently gut microbiota dysbiosis has been linked with gut motility disorders that are associated with clinical conditions including diabetes, Parkinson’s disease, autism, and constipation-predominant irritable bowel syndrome.10

Although initial enteric nervous system (ENS) development occurs during the fetal phase,11 the terminal differentiation of enteric neurons occurs during the postnatal development period, which is a process that is also sensitive to the presence of the gut microbiota.12,13 Furthermore, germ-free (GF) murine experiments verified a compelling role for the mammalian microbiota in the early development of the ENS.14 It is established that there is a progressive increase in microbial population from the gastric to the colonic end of the GI tract due to increased acidity and faster transit in the upper GI tract. In addition, fermentation of dietary fiber by commensal bacteria produces short chain fatty acids including acetic, propionic, and butyric acids that have multiple signaling effects on gut epithelia15,16 and enteric neurons.17 Specifically, it was reported that butyrate uptake through the monocarboxylic acid transporter (MCAT2) can regulate gut motility via selective induction of cholinergic neurons in a process that involves histone acetylation of the DNA of enteric neurons.18 Collectively, these data serve as predictive indicators of the roles that specific components of the microbiota may play in the development and function of the ENS.

Mechanistically, the gut can respond to bacterial pathogens and commensals via pattern recognition receptors (PPRs) expressed in epithelial cells and in the ENS.19 Among the PRRs are membrane bound G-protein coupled formylated peptide receptors (FPRs).20 The murine gut expresses 2 functional FPRs, namely Fpr1 and Fpr2. There is considerable functional conservation between the murine Fpr1 and Fpr2 and their human counterparts, hFpr1 and hFpr2, with a sequence identity of 73% and 77% respectively. FPRs were originally described as being expressed by innate immune cells such as neutrophils and macrophages, although recent work showed functional expression in neurons and glia,21 whereas we showed that FPRs can transmit signals from the gut microbiota to regulate gut epithelial homeostasis and wound closure.22,23 FPRs bind and respond to bacterial-specific translation products that have an N-terminal formyl group, especially N-formyl methionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine (fMLF) via G protein coupled interaction that culminates in redox signaling.23,24 This signaling is crucial in the recognition of bacterial (or mitochondrial) peptides and eventuates in inflammatory cell chemotaxis.25 Further, Fpr1 activation was found to modulate in vitro neuronal differentiation,26 and transit in the ileum of guinea pigs.27

We previously reported on the expression of Fpr1 in the enteric neurons and elucidated a novel role for Fpr1 in transducing microbe-derived signals across the gut epithelium to enteric neurons.28 Specifically, bacterial adhesion to the epithelium and the presence of Fpr1 was found to be necessary for the generation of microbe-mediated cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and subsequent ERK1/2 phosphorylation in enteric neurons, which induced enhanced neuronal differentiation and improved GI motility.28 Those reports pointed to a role for FPRs in the ENS in perceiving signals from the gut microbiota. The current manuscript delineates the role of Fpr1/2 in the development of the ENS, in orchestrating signaling between gut microbes and the ENS, and in mediating signaling from the maternal microbiota to the developing ENS in utero. We consider that our findings will serve as an essential foundation for defining the requirement of Fpr1 and Fpr2 in conveying signals from the microbiome to the developing embryonic and post-natal ENS to augment GI motility.

Results

Generation of Germline and Tissue-specific FPR1/2 Knockout Mouse Lines

The fpr locus in mice includes a contiguous region of DNA harboring fpr1 (7469 bp DNA), fpr2 (6129 bp DNA), and fpr3 (1220 bp), with fpr3 being an inactive pseudogene.28, 29, 30, 31 Single null alleles of fpr1 and fpr2 have been generated and exhibit similar, but not identical, phenotypes relating to inflammatory cell recruitment and function, indicating partially redundant roles.32, 33, 34 The close genetic distance between fpr1 and fpr2 precludes the generation of double knockouts by cross breeding. Therefore, to determine the role of Fpr1 and Fpr2 proteins in ENS development and gut motility, we generated a germline null mouse harboring a conditional knockout of both fpr1 and fpr2 genes. Specifically, we inserted loxP sequence sites flanking the entire murine fpr locus creating Fpr1/2flx/flx mouse that permits the knockout of the entire fpr locus upon interaction with Cre recombinase (Figure 1A). The mouse also harbored a gfp gene either side of the fpr locus. Specifically, the 5' end of the gfp gene was located adjected to one loxP sequence, and the 3' end of the gfp gene was located adjected to the other loxP sequence, with the net effect being that excision of the genetic region between the loxP sequences permitted the expression of functional gfp transcript (Figure 1A). To delete the fpr locus, we crossed the Fpr1flx/flx mouse to 3 different mice expressing the Cre transgene integrated into their genome, including the CMV-cre mouse line to generate germline Fpr1/2-/- mice (CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx), the Vil1-cre line to create intestinal epithelial cell-specific fpr1/2 deficient mice (Vil1-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx), and the Sox10-cre line to create mice with deletion of fpr1/2 in all neural crest derivatives, including the ENS, and the entire oligodendrocyte lineage (Sox10-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis confirmed excision of the fpr1/2 genetic region between the loxP sequences in the presence of Cre recombinase (Figure 1B–D). We confirmed that 6-week-old CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx, Vil1-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx, and Sox10-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice exhibited normal intestinal architecture and morphometry (Figure 1E–G). In addition, in CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx, Vil1-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx, and Sox10-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice, excision of the fpr region permits for the expression of a functional gfp transcript, and production of green fluorescent protein (GFP) which was detectable by fluorescence microscopy, where we detected global GFP fluorescence in all tissues of CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice (Figure 1H), including the myenteric plexi (Figure 1I). We detected epithelial-specific GFP expression in Vil1-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice (Figure 1J), and qPCR analysis of epithelial scrapings from Vil1-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice detected 82% (±5) loss of colonic fpr1 expression, and 70% (±4.7) loss of colonic fpr2 expression (Figure 1K), indicating the penetrance of Fpr1/2 knockout in these animals. The Sox10-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice expressed GFP specifically in enteric neurons and enteric glia as observed from co-localization of GFP with the pan neuronal markers HuD and Peripherin, and the glial cell markers GFAP and S100β (Figure 1L), whereas no GFP expression was detected in the epithelium of Sox10-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice (Figure 1M). Numerically, 74.5% (±3.92) of ENS neurons were GFP-positive, and 78.5% (±5.83) of glial cells were GFP-positive in Sox10-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice (Figure 1N). Previously, we detected the expression and protein abundance of Fpr1 on both jejunal epithelial cells and enteric neurons.28 Here, using RNAScope fluorescence hybridization and the GFAP marker, we detected the expression and protein abundance of Fpr1 in enteric glial cells (Figure 1O). Germline Deletion of Fpr1/2 Significantly Impairs Adult ENS Density and Epithelial Innervation

We first investigated the impact of germline Fpr1/2 deletion on the ENS. Confocal analysis of the ENS structure in adult CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice by CLARITY staining with neuronal marker β-3-tubulin (Tuj1), revealed significantly reduced ganglion size (Figure 2A and B) and reduced thickness of inter ganglionic nerve fibers compared with littermate wild-type controls (Figure 2C). Analysis of 3D images reconstructed from the z-stacks of the colon from CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice using the pan neuronal marker Tuj1 showed reduced myenteric density (myenteric ganglia per area), and significant reduction in epithelium innervation compared with controls (Figure 2D and E and Supplementary Videos 1 and 2). The latter was further evident from a select single z-stack slice of the cross section of intestinal crypts with neuronal endings (Figure 2F and G). Germline Deletion of Fpr1/2 Impairs GI Motility

We next assessed the impact of the germline deletion of the fpr locus on GI motility in young adult CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice (8–10 weeks old). Although CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice exhibited no outward signs of impaired growth and development, we observed a significant reduction in stool frequency (Figure 3A), and significantly prolonged total GI transit time (Figure 3B), in CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice compared with wild-type (WT) littermate controls. Furthermore, intestinal transit assay using FITC-dextran (FD-70) revealed significantly delayed transit in CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice compared with controls (Figure 3C), with a significant difference in the geometric center compared with WT littermates (Figure 3D). Critically, these physiological changes were absent in our previous studies investigating single gene knockout of fpr1 or fpr2 in mice.28 Having observed a significant reduction in GI motility in the CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice, we next analyzed changes in the major enteric neuronal subtypes. We observed that CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice displayed a significant reduction in cholinergic (choline acetyl transferase [ChAT]-containing) and nitrergic (neuronal nitric oxide synthase [nNOS]-containing) neurons (Figure 3E and F). However, we did not detect significant differences in enteric glial cell density between CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx and WT littermates (Figure 3E and F). Germline Deletion of Fpr1/2 Impairs Embryonic (E18.5) and Early Postnatal (P2) ENS Development and Epithelial Innervation

Having observed significant impairments in ENS structure and GI motility in adult CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice, we next evaluated the role of Fpr1/2 in ENS development by assessing the embryonic (E18.5) ENS structure in CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx and WT control mice. CLARITY immunostaining for the pan neuronal marker Peripherin showed significantly reduced enteric neuronal density at E18.5 (Figure 4A and D). Further, significant reductions in HuD-positive enteric neuronal cell bodies were detected in the CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx embryonic (E18.5) gut compared with age-matched WT controls (Figure 4B and E). In addition, we detected significantly fewer neuronal profiles as assessed by Tuj1 staining in the embryonic gut epithelia at E18.5 in CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx compared with WT controls (Figure 4C and F). Maternal Antibiotics During Gestation Significantly Reduced Fecal fMLP Content, Impaired ENS Structure and Gut Motility in Mouse Pups, Phenocopying the Fpr1/2 Null Phenotype

Having observed significant structural and functional ENS deficits in CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice during all stages of development (embryonic, postnatal day 2 [P2], and adult), we hypothesized that intestinal abundance of bacterial N-formylated peptides (fMLF or fMLP), the main ligands for Fpr1/2, may influence ENS development and GI motility. Our previous report showed that oral gavage of fMLP triggers Erk1/2 phosphorylation via ROS signaling in the myenteric neurons.28 To reduce the bacterial load and abundance of formylated peptides, we utilized a maternal microbiota suppression model where WT dams were administered a cocktail of broad-spectrum antibiotics (ampicillin, streptomycin, metronidazole, vancomycin) throughout gestation (Figure 6A). Analysis of the abundance of the 16S rRNA gene in the stool of dams validated the antibiotic-induced reduction in total microbiota load (Figure 6B), producing pseudo germ-free mice. Of note, following the withdrawal of antibiotics, the mice re-established a complex microbial community in the gut. Interestingly, feces from 3-week-old mouse pups derived from antibiotic-treated dams also exhibited significantly reduced fMLP content as detected by mass spectrometry, but the reason for this observation is unclear (Figure 6C). Strikingly, offspring from antibiotic-treated dams showed reduced ENS density and epithelial innervations at 3 weeks of age (Figure 6D–F) and prolonged GI transit time (Figure 6G). Indeed, we detected prolonged GI transit time in both male and female offspring derived from antibiotic-treated dams up to 5 weeks postnatally (Figure 6H). A key finding from the maternal antibiotic model was that abnormal ENS development in offspring from antibiotic-treated dams at P2 largely recapitulated the reduced ENS density in the myenteric plexus and reduced epithelial innervation detected in age-matched conventionally raised CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice, and in GF mice as captured by z-slice confocal images (Figure 6I–J). Taken together, our data shows that suppression of the microbiota with antibiotics, or the complete absence of the microbiota as in the case of GF mice, or absence of Fpr1/2 signaling can result in defective ENS development. Microbial-dependent Signaling During Pregnancy Regulates ENS Density and GI Motility

To determine if the maternal microbiota impacts fetal ENS development, we used a transient gestational colonization model in GF pregnant mice. This model has been previously exploited for immune studies in GF mice.4 This model utilizes Escherichia coli HA107A strain that is auxotrophic for the peptidoglycan components D-alanine (D-Ala) and meso-diaminopimelic acid (m-Dap). E. coli HA107 rapidly propagates in supplemented culture media but rapidly loses viability in D-Ala and m-Dap deficient environment. We generated GF pregnant mice, and transiently mono-colonized these mice with E. coli HA107 between days E8 and E14 of gestation, with a dose of 4 × 1010 CFU/mL every 2 days (Figure 7A). We detected the presence of viable bacteria at 24 hours post gavage in the fecal pellets of GF mice gavaged with E. coli HA107 using light microscopy and by culturing stool pellets on brain heart infusion (BHI) media supplemented with D-Ala and m-DAP (Figure 7B and C). However, we did not detect viable E. coli HA107 in the same mice at 48 hours following gavage (Figure 7D and E). To confirm the presence of live bacteria at 24 hours, stool pellets were inoculated into liquid BHI media supplemented with D-Ala and m-DAP and incubated overnight. We detected a turbid culture in media inoculated with pellets from mice following 24-hour gavage with E. coli HA107, but not in the stool pellets from mice at 48 hours following gavage, nor in stool pellets from GF mice (data not shown). To colonize pregnant females with E. coli HA107 between gestation days E8 and E14, mice were gavaged with E. coli HA107 at days E8, E10, E12, and E14 of gestation. By administration of final gavage of E. coli HA107 at day E14, offspring were born in a GF status. We detected that offspring derived from GF dams transiently colonized with E. coli HA107 during gestation had significantly augmented colonic neuronal density with a significant increase in neuronal cell bodies and fibers as assessed by CLARITY staining for Peripherin (Figure 7F–H), although no significant changes in enteric glial density as measured by the number of GFAP-positive cells (red) were detected in these mice (Figure 7F–I). In addition, significantly augmented colonic neuronal density was detected in offspring from GF dams transiently colonized with E. coli HA107 during gestation as assessed by CLARITY staining for HuD (red) in the longitudinal muscle myenteric plexi (LMMP) (Figure 7J and K) compared with control offspring from dams that remained GF throughout gestation. Importantly, with enhanced neuronal density, we observed a significant reduction in total GI transit time (Figure 7L) in the GF pups derived from GF dams transiently colonized with E. coli HA107 during gestation, compared with offspring from dams that remained GF throughout gestation. However, stool water content, stool frequency, small intestine and colon lengths, and body weights of the pups were not affected by maternal colonization with E. coli HA107 during gestation (Figure 7M–Q). Discussion

FPRs have been historically recognized for their immune-related functions including neutrophil migration and ROS production in response to pathogens, or sterile tissue damage resulting from mitochondrial lysis.31,35 Recent discovery of FPRs on astrocytes and glial cells in the central nervous system (CNS) has implicated their function in several CNS pathologies including Alzheimer’s disease.36 Here, we demonstrate that Fpr1 and Fpr2 play critical roles in the development of the ENS and GI motility during both embryonic and postnatal stages. Our findings show that germline deletion of both Fpr1 and Fpr2 leads to marked reductions in ENS density and epithelial innervation, resulting in impaired GI motility. Because enteric neurons originate from neural crest cells, we further investigated the impact of neural crest-specific deletion of Fpr1/2. This targeted deletion similarly caused significant deficits in ENS density, epithelial innervation, and GI motility, mirroring the phenotype observed in germline knockout models. Notably, even haploinsufficiency of Fpr1/2 in neural crest cells produced severe impairments in ENS development and GI function. In contrast, epithelial cell-specific deletion of Fpr1/2 resulted in milder deficits in ENS density, innervation, and motility compared with the germline knockout, suggesting a more limited role for epithelial Fpr1/2 in ENS development. It is important to note that the intestinal epithelium receives innervation from both intrinsic enteric neurons—such as intrinsic primary afferent neurons (IPANs) and secretomotor neurons—and a diverse array of extrinsic fibers, including sympathetic postganglionic neurons, vagal afferents, and spinal afferents. The precise identities and functional contributions of these neural projections remain incompletely characterized and warrant further investigation.

Maternal diet has been shown to significantly influence gut health and ENS development in offspring.37 Utilizing a mouse model of maternal antibiotics-induced dysbiosis, we demonstrated that broad spectrum antibiotic treatment that reduced the microbiome load by >99.9%, leads to reduced fecal levels of formylated peptides (fMLF), reduced ENS density, and altered GI motility in mice. Interestingly, the antibiotic-induced ENS phenotype largely recapitulated the ENS phenotype seen in both Fpr1/2-/- mice, and in GF mice with respect to reduced ENS density in embryonic, postnatal, and adult intestines. These findings suggest that disrupted Fpr1/2 signaling (germline null Fpr1/2 mouse model) or absence/depletion of microbiota (GF or gestational/maternal antibiotic model) lead to similar ENS developmental and epithelial innervation deficits and defective GI motility. In addition, the specific finding of an environment-induced maternal dysbiosis (antibiotics), albeit broad range and reducing >99.9% of the microbiome, having physiological consequences on GI motility in offspring may suggest caution in the excessive use of antibiotics during pregnancy.

Further, it has been demonstrated that FPRs in the placenta are critical for fetal growth and that Fpr2 knockout mice exhibit intrauterine growth retardation.38 Our GF mouse model of transient colonization elegantly revealed that a short window of gestational mono-colonization of GF dams can significantly improve the ENS structure and functions in the offspring. Hence, our data suggest that transfer of microbial signals (ie, fMLP via Fpr1/2 across the placenta during gestation) may be critical to the normal development of the ENS, and for normal GI motility in the offspring. Other studies using antibiotic-treated39 or GF dams have demonstrated that maternal microbiota can regulate fetal neurodevelopment40 with gnotobiotic colonization preventing the abnormalities in fetal brain gene expression via gut microbe-generated metabolites. Similarly, colonic ENS exhibits unique transcriptional profiles controlled by the combined effects of host genetic programs and microbial colonization, as seen with short chain fatty acids (SCFAs)18,41 or microbiota-induced aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) expression in neurons.6 Together, our findings identify Fpr1/2 as a key regulatory receptor for transducing gut luminal signals via formyl peptides for ENS development and GI functions. Hence, Fpr1/2 may serve as a novel drug target to treat GI disorders, and administration of probiotics to stimulate maternal microbiota-Fpr1/2 signaling may be exploited as a novel and safe strategy to address ENS disorders, especially in pregnancy, without other adverse side effects.

As our study limitation, we acknowledge that there is reported extensive CNS recombination in the Vil1-cre 997 founder line,42 and that the Sox10-cre line would affect all neural crest derivatives as well as the oligodendrocyte lineage,43 which have been implicated in FPR signaling. Further, in the maternal antibiotic model, in addition to the direct impact of microbiota deletion, the broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail itself could be affecting differentiation of enteric progenitors.44,45 Nevertheless, using similar genetic approaches in mice, we demonstrated that Fpr1 plays a crucial role in epithelial migration and wound healing,5 whereas others showed that Fpr1 enhances migration of neural stem cells in vitro.25 Further studies investigating the temporal effects of Fpr1 deletion during neural crest cell migration and Fpr1/2 activation by varying concentrations of formylated peptides of exogeneous (bacterial) vs endogenous (mitochondrial) origin opens a new avenue for future investigation in ENS developmental disorders.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Emory University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Conventional C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratories, and experiments were performed at the Emory Whitehead Biomedical Research Building Animal Facility. Mice at embryonic day 18.5 (E18.5), postnatal day 2 (P2), or 8 to 10 weeks old were used for the experiments. GF mice were purchased from Taconic, and experiments were performed at the Emory Gnotobiotic Animal Core (EGAC). GF mice were fed sterilized 2019 Teklad Global 19% Protein Extruded Rodent Diet (Inotiv) and sterilized water. For transient colonization experiments, GF mice were housed in hermetically sealed Techniplast ISOcage P-Bioexclusion System within EGAC. GF status of mice was confirmed by the ‘Germ-Free Complete Profile’ generic bacterial 16S rDNA PCR assay paired with sterility and anaerobic culture (Micro) test undertaken by IDXX BioAnalytics.

Generation of Fpr1/2flx/flx Mice

Fpr1/2flx/flx mice were developed by Ingenious Targeting Laboratory and by the Emory Transgenic Mouse and Gene Targeting Core. Briefly, a construct was designed in a vector that harbored the entire fpr loci, including the coding regions for Fpr1, Fpr2, and Fpr3. In the design, the fpr loci was flanked by a 34-base pair loxP sequence. The design of the construct also included a gfp coding sequence flanking the loxP sites that, upon excision of the fpr loci by Cre recombinase, yields a function transcript coding for the GFP protein under the pCAG promoter. The design of the Fpr1/2flx/flx is outlined in Figure 1A. Embryonic stem (ES) cells with the Fpr1/2flx/flx region was generated using standard homologous recombination technology by Ingenious Targeting Laboratory, and the ES cells were injected into blastocysts by the Emory Transgenic Mouse and Gene Targeting Core. Fpr1/2flx/flx mice were genotyped by Transnetyx automated genotyping PCR services (Transnetyx), using primers targeting one of the loxP sites and a region within the fpr loci.

Generation of Germline and Cell-specific Fpr1/2 Knockout Mice

We generated germline Fpr1/2 null mice (CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx) mice by crossing male Fpr1/2flx/flx to female CMV-cre mice (Stock No: 006054, B6.C-Tg (CMV-Cre)1Cgn/J) obtained from the Jackson Laboratories. The genotype was verified by tail genotyping (Transnetyx), and by PCR in our laboratory to confirm recombination of gfp gene (600 bp). For the generation of Vil1-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice, we crossed male Fpr1/2flx/flx with female Vil1-Cre mice obtained from Jackson Laboratories (B6.Cg-Tg(Vil1-cre)997Gum/J, Stock No: 004586). The Cre positive mice with floxed genotype were verified by tail genotyping (Transnetyx), followed by PCR in our laboratory to confirm recombination of GFP. The percent loss of fpr1 and fpr2 expression in the epithelia was quantified by qPCR from epithelial scrapings from the Vil1-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice and compared with the fpr1 and fpr2 expression in congenic Vil1-cre-/- Fpr1/2flx/flx mice using SYBR green mix and primers specific for fpr1 and fpr2.28 To generate Sox10-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice, we crossed male Fpr1/2flx/flx with female Sox-10 Cre+/- hemizygous mice that express Cre recombinase in the neural crest derived cells including the ENS and the oligodendrocytes (Stock No. 025807, B6; CBA-Tg(Sox10-cre)1Wdr/J, JAX) to obtain heterozygous Sox-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/+ or homozygous Sox-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx mice. The Cre-positive mice with floxed genotype were verified by tail genotyping (Transnetyx), followed by PCR in our laboratory to confirm the presence of cre, and the recombination of gfp. Furthermore, we also performed confocal microscopy to confirm GFP fluorescence in tissues of CMV-cre+/-;Fpr1/2flx/flx, Vil1-cre+/- Fpr1/2flx/flx and Sox-cre+/- Fpr1/2flx/flx mice. For Sox-cre+/- Fpr1/2flx/flx mice, we confirmed colocalization of GFP with neuronal and glial cell markers.

Maternal Model of Microbiota Suppression With Antibiotics During Gestation

Breeding cages of 8-week-old WT C57BL6 mice (Strain 000664, The Jackson Laboratory) were set up with ad libitum access to drinking water, and females were routinely checked for vaginal plugs. The plugged females were administered an antibiotic cocktail (1 g/L each of ampicillin, streptomycin, vancomycin, metronidazole) through drinking water46 throughout the gestational period (18–21 days). Independent breeding experiments were set up to obtain day 2 post-natal guts (P2). The pups were assessed for ENS density and GI motility at 3 weeks of age. The formylated peptide content (fMLP) in the fecal pellets collected from 3-week-old pups was analyzed by mass spectrometry.

Transient Gestational-only Mono-colonization of GF Mice

The Escherichia coli strain HA107A (from Dr Andrew McPherson, University of Bern, Switzerland) is auxotrophic for D-Ala and m-DAP, and has been demonstrated to transiently colonize GF mice with a single oral gavage (1 × 1011 CFU/mL), and mice return to GF status after 48 hours after gavage of E. coli strain HA107A.4,47 We established and validated this mono-colonization model at our institute. The auxotrophic E. coli cultures were grown in selective media containing Luria Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with D-Ala and m-DAP overnight at 30°C for 18 to 20 hours. The cultures were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C, and the bacterial pellet resuspended in sterile-filtered phosphate buffered saline (PBS). To colonize pregnant females with E. coli HA107 between gestation days E8 and E14, mice were gavaged with E. coli HA107 at days E8, E10, and E12 of gestation.4,47,48 Each dose contained 1 × 1010 CFU total. By administration of final gavage of E. coli HA107 at day E14, offspring were born in a GF status. Fecal pellets from pregnant GF mice were collected 24 hours and 48 hours after each gavage and resuspended in sterile filtered saline and cultured in BHI media in 37°C incubator (aerobic, overnight culture). The absorbance of overnight fecal cultures measured at 600 nm was used to validate HA107A colonization. In addition, a 102 dilution of the fecal pellets in PBS was prepared and 100 μL plated BHI agar plates (supplemented with D-Ala and m-DAP) and incubated overnight in 37°C incubator. The appearance of colonies confirmed the colonization of E. coli HA107. Fecal pellets collected 48 hours after the last gavage (on E14) to validate that the pregnant mice had returned to the GF status. Fecal samples were also collected from the ISO cage at 1 day following the birth of mice to test and verify GF status.

Mass Spectrometry

The formylated peptide content (fMLF or fMLP) of fecal samples was assessed by untargeted high-resolution Fourier transform high-resolution mass spectrometry using pure fMLF peptide as standard (m/z 438.204, Dionex Ultimate 3000, HF Q-Exactive, Thermo Scientific).49

Reagents and Antibodies Used in This Study

CLARITY Immunostaining

The colonic tissue E18.5 embryos, P2 pups, and adult mice (8–10 weeks old) were cleared and CLARITY stained as described previously28 with antibodies against pan neuronal markers (Tuj1, HuD, Peripherin, 1: 800 dilution), glial marker (glial fibrillary acid protein, GFAP, and S100β; 1: 500), overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with respective Alexa Fluor secondary antibodies and DAPI at room temperature. The tissue was mounted on glass slides in Vectashield and imaged using confocal microscopy.

LMMP Staining

LMMP was micro dissected out from the colon and stained with antibodies against GFP (1:600 dilution) and HuD (1:800 dilution) overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies and DAPI for 1 hour at room temperature. The plexi was then mounted on a glass slide with Vectashield as described previously.28

Confocal Microscopy and Arbitrary Fluorescence Quantification

The confocal imaging of CLARITY cleared and immunostained tissues was done using an Olympus FV1000 as described previously.28 The z-stacks were captured from the gut lumen towards the serosal layer to visualize enteric neuronal density and epithelial innervation, and 3D images were generated from z-stacks as described previously.28 The staining intensity of at least 8 to 10 neuronal cell bodies or myenteric ganglia per mouse and per experimental group was assessed in a blinded fashion after the composite image was split into respective color channels28 and plotted as relative arbitrary intensity units.

Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization

The colons from mice were cryofixed in OCT media and 5 μm sections were prepared on Superfrost slides, and subjected to fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) using RNA probes (RNAScope, ACD Bio) for Fpr1 and GFAP staining as described previously.28

GI Motility

GI motility assays were performed as published previously.28,50,51,52 Briefly, stool frequency (number of stool pellets extruded per mouse per hour), total GI transit time (time needed to extrude the first red pellet after red carmine gavage), and gastrointestinal transit assay (measuring the relative distribution of fluorescence intensity of FITC-Dextran-70 kD in the intestinal segments and the geometric center) were used as indices to measure GI motility.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using Graph Pad Prism software (GraphPad). Data are represented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The Shapiro-Wilk Test was used to test for normality and to validate the use of parametric statistics. Student’s unpaired 2-tailed t-test, or 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test were used to calculate statistical significance. Differences were considered significant at P < .05.
