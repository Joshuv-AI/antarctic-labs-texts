Key platelet genes play important roles in predicting the prognosis of sepsis
Authors: Leiting Shen, Chang Tao, Kun Zhu, Linghao Cai, Sisi Yang, Jingyi Jin, Yichao Ren, Yi Xiao, Yuebai Zhang, Dengming Lai, Jinfa Tou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is a life-threatening organ malfunction induced by an imbalanced immunological reaction to infection in the host. Many studies have utilized traditional RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data to identify important biological targets to predict sepsis prognosis. However, alterations in core cells and functional status cannot be effectively detected in sepsis patients. The goal of this study was to identify key cells through single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq), and combine bulk RNA-seq data and multiple algorithm analysis to construct a stable prognostic model for sepsis. The scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data from sepsis patients were collected from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The R package “Seurat” was used to process the scRNA-seq data. Cell communication was investigated using the R package “CellChat”. The pseudo-time of the cells was calculated using the R package “monocle”. The R package “limma” was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the sepsis group and the control group. Weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA) was used to identify critical modules. Eight kinds of machine learning and 90 algorithm combinations were used to construct the prognostic model for sepsis. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) was performed to determine the expression of key genes in the cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced sepsis mouse model. The immunological status and related properties of DEGs were then investigated in the high- and low-risk groups delineated by the model. By combining the scRNA-seq data from nine samples, 13 clusters and 9 cell types were identified. CellChat analysis revealed that the number and strength of interactions between platelets and a variety of cells increased. We identified key platelet genes from the scRNA-seq data and combined these genes and the results of differential analysis and WGCNA of the bulk RNA-seq data. After univariate Cox regression analysis, we calculated the Cindex of the model constructed by the combination of 90 algorithms, and we finally determined the “CoxBoost + Lasso” combination. Multivariate Cox regression was used to construct the final prognostic model. The qRT-PCR results revealed significant differences in five key prognostic genes between the CLP and sham groups. The data was classified into high- and low-risk groups based on the model score. The high-risk group had a poorer survival rate and less immune infiltration. We identified the importance of platelets in sepsis patients through scRNA-seq, and established prognostic models with key genes that were identified via scRNA-seq combined with bulk RNA-seq analysis. Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction induced by an abnormal host response to infection1. Sepsis patients with severe circulatory and cellular metabolic abnormalities were defined as having septic shock. Globally, there are approximately 47–50 million instances of sepsis each year, with at least 11 million sepsis-related deaths, accounting for almost one-fifth of all deaths2. One-third of sepsis patients admitted to the ICU die within 30 days3. Contemporary sepsis treatment is centered on supportive therapy, which includes controlling the source of infection, administering antibiotics, and providing supportive care for organ dysfunction4. However, these treatments typically fail to reverse immune system disruption and anomalies in the immunological response following treatment5. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify effective prevention, diagnostic, and therapeutic methods for sepsis.

With rapid advancements in molecular biology and immunology, clinicians now have more knowledge about the pathogenesis of sepsis. Sepsis is usually triggered by an overactive inflammatory response. When pathogens invade, the resulting pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) will activate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), causing strong activation of the innate immune system and varying degrees of damage to the organism6. Blocking the secretion of pro-inflammatory factors mitigates the harm produced by acute fulminant infections7. However, as sepsis progresses, it presents as a complex combination of an increased systemic inflammatory response and immunosuppression. Patients who require intensive care after major therapeutic measures frequently develop a chronic critical illness known as persistent inflammation, immunosuppression, and catabolism syndrome (PICS), which is characterized by muscle atrophy and cachexia8. Immunosuppression causes nosocomial infections and virus reactivation in the body, as well as a prolonged and persistent state of immune dysfunction after discharge from the hospital. About 90% of sepsis patients die during the immunoprophylaxis phase9. The prognosis of sepsis patients remains an essential global issue.

Despite more than three decades of study and more than 200 randomized controlled trials on sepsis, significant targeted treatments for sepsis remain scarce10. Furthermore, laboratory studies frequently focus on a single element of sepsis pathogenesis, while multicellular and multimolecular interactions are rarely adequately investigated. Traditional RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has emerged as a key method in transcriptomics, as genomics has advanced rapidly over the last few decades. RNA-seq has been used in an increasing number of studies to investigate the changes in important genes during disease development and to identify relevant molecular targets. This method has been widely applied in a range of disorders, particularly in oncology research11,12. Several studies have attempted to identify biomarkers for sepsis diagnosis and prognosis using bulk RNA-seq13,14. However, bulk RNA-seq is primarily employed to evaluate the “average” expression of all cells and is unable to reliably detect cellular alterations in disease. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) reveals information about the heterogeneity of cellular transcriptomes and allows us to comprehend changes in various cells and identify critical cells. Given this advantage, we evaluated scRNA-seq data of sepsis to identify important cells while combining bulk RNA-seq data and multiple machine learning methods to develop a stable and accurate prognostic model. In addition, we explored the association between the risk model and immune cells to gain a better understanding of the immunological processes that occur during sepsis progression. Our findings will help identify predictive indicators and specific treatment targets for sepsis.

Methods

Data sources and processing

To investigate potential predictive biomarkers of sepsis, scRNA-seq, and bulk RNA-seq data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database. GSE175453 is a peripheral blood mononuclear cell sequencing dataset that includes five control patients and four sepsis patients. This dataset was utilized for scRNA-Seq data analysis. Table 1 shows clinical information on samples used for scRNA-Seq data analysis, and more detailed description can be seen in the original publication of the dataset15. Bulk RNA-seq dataset GSE28750 was utilized for differential gene expression analysis and weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA). GSE65682 provides the survival time and outcomes of sepsis patients after 28 days for prognostic modeling. Figure 1 shows a flow diagram of the study design.

scRNA-Seq data processing and analysis

The scRNA-seq data was analyzed through the following steps: (1) 10× single-cell data were converted to Seurat objects by the “CreateSeuratObject” function of the R package “Seurat”16; (2) cells with a mitochondrial RNA percentage less than 40%, an RNA count greater than 1000 and an RNA feature number greater than 300 were extracted, and low-quality cells were excluded for quality control (QC) of the raw data; (3) the “NormalizeData” and “ScaleData” functions were used to normalize and standardize the data after QC, and the “FindVariableFeatures” function was applied to identify the top 2000 highly variable genes; (4) the R package “Harmony” was used to remove batch effects and perform principal component analysis, and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) were used for dimensionality reduction and cluster identification17; (5) the “FindAllMarkers” function was used to identify significant marker genes in different clusters by setting “logfc.threshold” and “min.pct” to 0.25; (6) the top 10 genes in each cluster were selected based on avg_log2FC values, and these genes were subsequently annotated to the corresponding cells in the CellMarker 2.0 database (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/) and Cell Taxonomy database (http://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/celltaxonomy/)18,19; (7) by setting “test.use” as “wilcox” and “min.pct” as 0.2, the “FindMarkers” function was applied to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell in the sepsis and control groups, and the data were filtered using p value < 0.05 and |Log2FC| > 0.5; (8) the R packages “CellChat” and “patchwork” were used for cell communication analysis, network construction and visualization, and the “computeCommunProb” function was used to calculate the probability of intercellular communication20; and (9) the R package “monocle” and the “newCellDataSet” function were used to build the monocle objects. The “orderCells” function was used for cell trajectory and pseudo-time analysis, and the analysis was downscaled with the “DDRTree” method21. Important cells were selected through CellChat analysis and Monocle analysis. The marker genes identified by the “FindAllMarkers” function and the differential genes identified by the previous “FindMarkers” function were selected as the key genes of the selected cell population.

Identification and functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

The “GEOquery” package and the “hgu133plus2.db” package were used to read and annotate the GSE28750 dataset. The “limma” package was used to analyze the differences between the 20 control patients and 21 sepsis patients. DEGs were defined as genes with a p value < 0.05 and |Log2FC| > 0.5. A volcano plot of DEGs was generated using the “ggplot2” package22. DEGs were then evaluated using Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) in the “clusterProfiler” package to explore enrichment pathways and biological processes23–26. The “ggupset” package was used to visualize the results of KEGG analysis and GSEA.

Weighted Gene Correlation Network Analysis

The “WGCNA” package was used for the weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA) of the dataset27. The top 10,000 genes in the samples were filtered using the absolute median deviation, and then the “goodSamplesGenes” function was used to assess whether the sample genes needed to be filtered. The “hclust” function was used to cluster the samples, and any unclustered samples were removed. The formal WGCNA procedure was as follows. First, the soft thresholding power β was chosen as the minimum power for the fit index of the scale-free topology to reach 0.8. Next, four processes were completed: intergenic correlation coefficient computation, gene module determination, coexpression network construction, and module-trait correlation. The “cor” and “corPvalueStudent” functions were used to calculate the correlation coefficient and p value of the feature vector and the trait of each module. The module genes with the highest positive and negative correlations in the sepsis group in all modules were then identified for further investigation.

Construction and validation of the prognostic model based on multiple machine learning algorithms

The genes identified by scRNA-seq analysis were intersected with the DEGs of bulk-seq data and the module genes extracted by WGCNA; the shared genes were considered candidate genes for further study. The “VennDiagram” package was used to construct a Venn diagram of the shared genes. The 479 samples in the GSE65682 dataset were separated into training and validation sets at a 6:4 ratio, with 6 serving as the training set (287 cases) and 4 serving as the validation set (192 cases). Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on the candidate genes, and genes with p values < 0.05 were subjected to subsequent machine learning integration analysis. To construct a stable and accurate model, we integrated 8 machine learning algorithms and 90 algorithm combinations. The integrative algorithms included the elastic network (Enet), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso), random survival forest (RSF), stepCox, CoxBoost, partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), survival support vector machine (survival-SVM), and supervised principal component (SuperPC) algorithms. We calculated Harrell’s concordance index (Cindex) of 90 models in the training set and validation set and selected a model with a high and stable Cindex. Finally, to further reduce the number of genes and construct a streamlined and efficient model, we included the genes obtained by the machine learning algorithm in multivariate Cox regression analysis. The variables with p values < 0.2 were again subjected to multivariate Cox regression analysis, and the variables with p values < 0.01 were subsequently selected to construct the risk model according to the following formula: risk score = exp1 × β1 + exp2 × β2+…+expi × βi (exp represents the gene expression value, and β represents the coefficient of the corresponding gene in the multivariate Cox analysis). All patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. The “survminer” package was used to visualize patient survival curves. Moreover, the “timeROC” package was used to plot receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to evaluate the effect of risk scores in predicting OS after 1, 2, and 3 weeks in sepsis patients. In addition, the “rms” package was applied to plot the calibration curves. Moreover, the “dcurves” and “ggplot2” packages were used to conduct decision curve analysis (DCA). Finally, the validation set was used to assess the utility of the prognostic model.

Experimental animals

C57BL/6J mice, aged 6–8 weeks, male and female random, were procured from Shanghai Slac Laboratory Animal Company (Shanghai, China). All mice were housed in a pathogen-free environment with a regulated light/dark cycle. The animal experiments were conducted following the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Medical Laboratory Animals stipulated by the Ministry of Health of China. The animal experiment was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval number ZJU20220526).

Construction of in vivo experimental models of sepsis

We used cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) to establish a mouse model of sepsis. Briefly, mice were peritoneally anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg), and then laparotomy was performed with a midline incision of approximately 1.0 cm. After the separation of the cecum, ligation with absorbable sutures was performed halfway between the distal pole and the base of the cecum. After the cecum was punctured with a sterile needle, the cecum was gently squeezed to remove a small amount of feces, which was then returned to the abdominal cavity. Finally, the peritoneum and skin were sutured. For the sham operation mice, no ligation or puncture incisions were used to expose the cecum. After surgery, the mice were resuscitated by subcutaneous injection of preheated saline. Blood was taken from the eyeballs of the mice on day 3, and the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation after blood collection.

Quantitative real-time PCR and statistical analysis

RNA was isolated from blood using TRIzol, chloroform, and the SteadyPure RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology, Changsha, China). RNA was reverse transcribed using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc., Otsu, Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) was performed for each gene using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara Bio Inc., Otsu, Shiga, Japan). At least three independent samples were made under the same conditions. The relative level of mRNA was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with β-actin as the reference gene. The data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS 21.0 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Differences between the two groups were determined by t-tests. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Bar graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The primers used were designed according to the official website of PrimeBank (https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank/), and the specific sequences are shown in Table 2.

Analysis of immune infiltration and DEGs between the high- and low-risk groups

To assess the proportion of infiltrating immune cells, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) with the “GSVA” package was used for both the high- and low-risk groups28. The “gsva” function was used for ssGSEA of immune cells by setting the “method” to ‘ssgsea’ and the “kcdf” to ‘Gaussian’. The “linkET” package was used to obtain the correlation heat map of 10 key genes and immune cells. Significant differences between the high- and low-risk groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. The “limma” package was utilized to assess the disparity between the high-risk group and the low-risk group. DEGs were identified based on a P value < 0.05 and |Log2FC| > 0.5. The Metascape database (https://Metascape.org/) was utilized to perform network enrichment analysis and protein-protein interaction network analysis and identify MCODE components of the DEGs29.

Results

Identification of cell subtypes in scRNA-seq data

First, we filtered out ineligible cells according to an RNA nCount > 1000, an RNA nFeature > 300, and a percentage of mitochondrial genes < 40 while preserving the genes with expression in at least 4 cells, resulting in 36,866 cells and 19,531 genes for further analysis (Fig. 2a). After performing analysis of variance (ANOVA) of the genes in the cells, we found 2000 highly variable genes (Fig. 2b). After applying the “harmony” package to the scRNA-seq data, the results revealed a dispersed distribution of single-cell samples with no significant batch effect (Fig. 2c). The elbow plot analysis of harmony dimensionality reduction revealed that the smoothing effect began after the 10th dimension. Therefore, for UMAP dimensionality reduction, the top 10 principal components were used (Fig. 2d). The UMAP algorithm was utilized to classify cells into 13 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 2e). Nine cell types were annotated by identifying marker genes using the CellMarker database and Cell Taxonomy database. These cell types included CD14 + monocytes, CD4 + T cells, NK cells, B cells, FCGR3A + monocytes, platelets, dendritic cells (DCs), plasmacytoid DCs, and plasma cells (Fig. 2f).

Red dots represent highly variable genes, and black dots represent non-variable genes. c The two groups were evenly distributed after running the “harmony” package. d Elbow plots were used to determine the number of principal components for subsequent analyses. e The UMAP algorithm was applied to the first 10 principal components for dimensionality reduction to obtain 13 cell clusters. f Nine cell types were annotated by the CellMarker and Cell Taxonomy databases.

Analysis of cell communication and pseudo-time in scRNA-seq data

The scRNA-seq data was used to analyze cell communication via the “CellChat” package. Cell communication network maps and cell interaction heatmaps revealed a notable increase in both the number and strength of interactions between platelets and various cells in the sepsis group compared to those in the control group (Fig. 3a-b). The analysis of ligand receptors showed that the communication of ligand receptors between platelets and various cells increased in sepsis compared with the control group (Fig. 3c). All annotated cells in the sepsis group were subjected to pseudotime analysis utilizing the “monocle” package. The findings indicated that cellular differentiation occurred in three distinct states and followed three distinct trajectories (Fig. 3d-e). Most immune cells are concentrated in state 1. However, as pseudo-time progressed, the number of a variety of immune cells diminished in state 2. Platelets were substantially more highly expressed in state 2 (Fig. 3f). According to the aforementioned findings, we proposed that alterations in platelet counts play a crucial role in late sepsis and are likely to impact patient prognosis. Ultimately, we identified the pivotal genes of platelets for further analysis.

Identification and functional enrichment analysis of DEGs in the bulk RNA-seq data

The differential analysis of bulk RNA-seq data yielded a total of 1297 genes with upregulated expression and 2131 genes with downregulated expression (Fig. 4a). Traditional GO enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs were primarily enriched in processes such as rRNA processing, mononuclear cell differentiation, secretory granule lumen, activities such as translation factor activity, RNA binding, and immune receptor activity (Fig. 4b). KEGG analysis revealed that the DEGs were primarily associated with several infectious diseases and biological processes, including coronavirus disease, human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection, Epstein‒Barr virus infection, neutrophil extracellular trap creation, PD-L1 expression, and the PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer (Fig. 4c). According to GSEA of DEGs, adaptive immune response, immune response − regulating cell surface receptor signaling, lymphocyte activation, etc., were the enriched biological processes (BPs) (Fig. 4d). More importantly, the enrichment trended downward. The cell component (CC) category showed an increase in DEGs in the cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, secretory granule, and secretory granule lumen (Fig. 4e). The DEGs were enriched in kinase binding, molecular transducer activity, and RNA polymerase II cis-regulatory region sequence-specific in the molecular function (MF) category. The enrichment trend increased (Fig. 4f). KEGG analysis via GSEA revealed that the DEGs were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection (Fig. 4g). The enrichment results suggest that compared to the control group, patients with sepsis showed an abnormal immune system response.

WGCNA of bulk RNA-seq data

Next, we performed WGCNA to identify key genes in sepsis development and progression. When constructing the coexpression network, we noted a soft threshold β power of 8 when the fit index of the scale-free topology reached 0.8 (Fig. 5a-b). Adjusting the MEDissThres parameter to a value of 0.25 and merging similar modules obtained from the analysis of the dynamic shear tree algorithm resulted in a total of 18 modules (Fig. 5c). Among all the modules, the magenta module exhibited the strongest positive correlation with sepsis, whereas the brown module showed the strongest negative correlation with sepsis (Fig. 5d-e). Hence, we selected these two as the key modules and extracted the genes within these modules for further research.

Prognostic Model Construction and Validation

A total of 213 genes were obtained by comparing the genes obtained from platelets and the DEGs and WGCNA module genes obtained from the bulk RNA-seq data (Fig. 6b). After univariate Cox regression analysis, we identified 57 candidate genes. The Cindex values sorting of the models of 90 machine learning algorithms showed that “coxboost + RSF”, “stepcox[both] + RSF”, “stepcox[backward] + RSF”, “coxboost + lasso”, and “RSF” were the top five best algorithms (Fig. 6a). However, we found that the Cindex values of the models built based on random forest-related algorithms were not high in the validation set; in other words, the difference between the training set and the validation set was too large. Therefore, we selected the “coxboost + lasso” model for subsequent analysis (Fig. 6c-d). After multivariate Cox regression analysis, we ultimately identified ten genes, namely, PPDPF, RPL11, FOS, SAT1, CTSW, MAP3K7, GIMAP4, CD36, CD93, and TRAIL, as key prognostic genes. Based on their coefficients, the risk score was calculated utilizing the following formula: risk score = PPDPF * 1.0386 + RPL11 * 1.1036 + FOS * 0.5560 + SAT1 * 2.4801 + CTSW * (-0.8606) + MAP3K7 * (-0.3117) + GIMAP4 * (-0.5125) + CD36 * (-0.2937) + CD93 * (-0.3848) + TRAIL * (-0.9004). The patients were classified into the high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score. Survival curves revealed that patients in the high-risk group had shorter overall survival (OS) than patients in the low-risk group (Fig. 7a). The ROC curve demonstrated that this prognostic model exhibited good predictive ability for survival at 1, 2, and 3 weeks (with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.88, 0.84, and 0.83, respectively) (Fig. 7b). The calibration curve also suggested high consistency between the actual survival rate and the predicted survival rate (Fig. 7c). Moreover, the DCA curve showed that the risk model was above the threshold baseline on a large scale (Fig. 7d). Consistently, we observed similar results in the validation set (Fig. 7e-f). After blood was collected from mice with CLP-induced sepsis, we performed qRT-PCR analysis. The results revealed significant differences in the expression of five genes, namely, CD36, CD93, GIMAP4, PPDPF, and MAP3K7, between the sepsis group and the sham group, suggesting that these genes may play a role in the development of sepsis (Fig. 7g). However, further research is needed to determine the relationship of these genes with the pathological and physiological progression of sepsis.

Analysis of immune infiltration and differences between the high- and low-risk groups

To explore the immune function of the high- and low-risk groups, we utilized the ssGSEA algorithm to examine differences in immune cell infiltration between the two groups. The findings indicated that the scores for multiple immune cells in the high-risk group were lower than those in the low-risk group, suggesting the existence of immunosuppression in the high-risk group (Fig. 8a). Correlation analysis showed that 10 genes had significant correlation with immune cells (Fig. 8b). By analyzing the differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups with the “limma” package, 91 genes with upregulated expression and 127 genes with downregulated expression were obtained. The Metascape database was utilized to conduct functional enrichment and module analysis of DEGs. The immune system activation and regulation were functionally enriched in DEGs, while various infections and inflammatory diseases were enriched (Fig. 8c-f). Protein interactions were further studied using MCODE, resulting in the identification of six central modules. Module 1 consisted of 16 genes: IFIT3, XAF1, IRF7, OASL, IFI6, IFIT1, IFITM3, MX1, OAS2, IFIT2, OAS1, OAS3, RSAD2, GBP2, and SAMHD1. Module 2 consisted of 6 genes: P2RY14, CXCL8, CCL5, LPAR2, GPR18, and CXCR2. Module 3 contained 3 genes: HLA-DMA, CD74, and HLA-DMB. Module 4 contained 3 genes: LCK, CD3G, and CD3D. Module 5 contained 3 genes: HLA-DRB1, HLA-DPA1, and HLA-DRB5. Module 6 included 3 genes: THBS1, ELANE, and CTSG (Fig. 8g).

Discussion

Sepsis is a systemic multiorgan and multisystem disorder induced by an imbalanced immunological response to infection. Sepsis is characterized by high morbidity and mortality worldwide. Despite significant improvements in the prognostic management of sepsis, the mortality rate remains as high as 20–50%, making sepsis one of the leading causes of death in hospitalized patients, which is a significant challenge in terms of global healthcare resources30. The APACHE-II score and the SOFA score are the two most commonly used clinical modalities for determining the prognosis of sepsis patients. However, due to the complex pathogenesis of sepsis, patient heterogeneity, and differences in survival time, both scoring indices have certain limitations. These methods are unable to accurately depict the pathological progression of sepsis patients or provide precise predictions regarding the severity and duration of survival for sepsis patients. Screening for new biomarkers to help develop patient-specific therapies and improve patient prognosis remains critical and urgent.

The course of sepsis is divided into two phases: an early immune stress phase followed by a long immunosuppressive phase. Patients will enter a prolonged anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive period after the acute phase, which makes primary infection control difficult and increases the risk of secondary and hospital-acquired infections. Most patients die during the immunosuppressive phase, primarily from disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), infectious shock, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), which are caused by coagulation problems, insufficient tissue perfusion, and immune system paralysis31. Following the initial period of acute inflammation, the patient’s immune cells become weakened, and numerous types of immune cells, including CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, B lymphocytes, and dendritic cells, undergo death in many organs, progressively leading to immunosuppression. As immunology research advances, an increasing number of studies have revealed that platelets, as key inflammatory cells, play an important role in activating immune cells, initiating inflammation, and exacerbating vascular inflammation and multiple organ injury32–34. However, in addition to activating inflammation, many studies have shown that platelets can also exert immunosuppressive effects, such as promoting macrophage and DC release of IL-10, reducing the release of IL-12 and TNF, and inhibiting cytotoxic T-cell activity through the TGF-β/GARP axis35–38. Although there is a lack of corresponding research on sepsis, platelet abnormalities are likely closely related to the altered immune status of sepsis patients. Simultaneously, the development of a network of platelets triggers the activation of thrombin and the production of a network of fibrin, which hastens the advancement of DIC and ultimately results in the death of patients. In clinical practice, platelet abnormalities are now recognized as a significant prognostic indicator39. Several studies have shown that sepsis patients who are prescribed antiplatelet medications, such as aspirin and clopidogrel, have a better long-term prognosis than those who do not take these medications40. Our analysis of the scRNA-seq data revealed a notable increase in both the number and strength of interactions between platelets and diverse types of cells. During the pseudo-time analysis, we observed a large concentration of various immune cells in state 1. However, as pseudo-time progressed, multiple immune cells decreased in state 2 and state 3. Additionally, we observed a significant increase in the platelet count in state 2. These findings indicate the crucial function of platelets in sepsis.

After analyzing the scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data, we identified 10 key genes for constructing the prognostic model: CD36, FOS, RPL11, SAT1, CD93, CTSW, MAP3K7, GIMAP4, TRAIL, and PPDPF. The platelet glycoprotein CD36 serves as a receptor for collagen and thrombospondin-1, playing a crucial role in platelet adhesion and aggregation. CD36 can activate platelets via the PLC signaling pathway, exacerbating the inflammatory response, and induce platelet hyperreactivity through the oxLDL pathway, leading to thrombosis41–43. Numerous studies have identified abnormalities in CD36 in sepsis patients, suggesting that CD36 is a crucial target for combating sepsis-related inflammation and organ damage44–48. Exploring the CD36 target on septic platelets holds promise for improving coagulation disorders and organ damage in sepsis. The FOS gene family, including FOS, FOSB, FOSL1, and FOSL2, encodes leucine zipper proteins that dimerize with JUN family proteins to form the transcription factor complex AP-1. Stimuli such as infection and stress can activate FOS49. Research has found that C-fos induced growth factor (FIGR) is abnormally expressed in infection-related sepsis50. Additionally, studies have indicated that the c-FOS/AP-1/c-JUN pathway is closely linked to platelet activation and thrombosis51. Therefore, in-depth research on FOS holds promise for future treatments targeting coagulation disorders and immune abnormalities in sepsis. RPL11, a highly conserved 60S ribosomal subunit, not only participates in protein synthesis but also plays a role in regulating cell proliferation52. Overexpression of RPL11 can induce autophagy52. Studies have shown that platelet autophagy is involved in the pathophysiological process of multiple organ failure in sepsis53,54. Autophagy can protect host cells in sepsis by maintaining mitochondrial integrity and reducing apoptosis. Future research should further elucidate the impact of changes in platelet RPL11 on tissue and immune cells in sepsis. SAT1 is a rate-limiting enzyme in polyamine catabolism that participates in arginine metabolism and reduces spermidine concentration in cells. Arginine metabolism can influence nitric oxide (NO) levels within platelets, thereby regulating platelet activation and quantity. Studies have reported that SAT1 plays a crucial role in metabolic regulation during sepsis and acute organ injury55,56. CD93 is a soluble transmembrane glycoprotein involved in membrane binding and is expressed in hematopoietic stem cells, platelets, and various immune cells. It plays a crucial role in cell migration, adhesion, phagocytosis, inflammation, and angiogenesis. In several cancers, elevated CD93 expression is linked to poor prognosis, reduced response to immunotherapy, and increased immune cell infiltration57. Studies have reported upregulation of CD93 transcripts in the blood of sepsis patients, but its specific mechanisms in sepsis remain to be further investigated58,59. CTSW, a member of the cysteine protease family, is present in various immune cells60. CTSW is linked to immune response, inflammation, cellular components, adhesion, and platelet activation and aggregation, but its mechanisms in sepsis need further investigation61,62. MAP3K7, a member of the mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase kinase (MAP3K) family, has been found to be abnormally expressed in the peripheral blood of sepsis patients. Studies suggest that miR-10a regulates the progression of inflammation in sepsis by modulating MAP3K7 expression63. Additionally, research indicates that MAP3K7 is involved in platelet activation, aggregation, and inflammatory cascade reactions, but its role in sepsis requires further elucidation64–66. GIMAP4, a recently identified member of the GTPase of the immunity-associated protein (GIMAP) family, is reported to be involved in the IFN-γ signaling pathway in Th1 cells and plays a crucial role in caspase-3 activation-induced T cell apoptosis67,68. However, the role of GIMAP4 in platelets during sepsis remains unexplored and requires further investigation. TRAIL, a member of the TNF superfamily, regulates immune responses in sepsis. Elevated TRAIL expression can induce apoptosis in various immune cells, thereby terminating immune responses69. Numerous clinical studies have found that TRAIL level is significantly reduced in sepsis patients, suggesting that TRAIL could serve as a key biomarker for assessing the severity and prognosis of sepsis70–72. Similar conclusions have been drawn from studies on COVID-19 pneumonia patients73. Changes in platelet TRAIL may influence immune responses by affecting the apoptosis of both platelets and immune cells, which requires further investigation. PPDPF is a key regulator of exocrine pancreatic development and contains potential binding sites for PDZ, SH2, SH3 domains, and GTP74. Middleton EA et al. performed transcriptome sequencing analysis of platelets from sepsis patients and CLP model mice75. The results indicated that PPDPF is abnormally expressed in both humans and mice, suggesting it may play a significant role in sepsis. Although the exact mechanism of platelet PPDPF changes in sepsis is not fully understood, studies suggest that PPDPF may reduce immune cell activation and infiltration, thus creating an immunosuppressive environment. Additionally, PPDPF appears to activate KRAS targets, which in turn regulates MAPK, PI3K, and other signaling pathways76. These pathways are closely linked to cell survival, proliferation, and cytokine release. Increased PPDPF expression has been associated with poor prognosis in various cancer types77,78. Patients with advanced sepsis often exhibit an immunosuppressive state similar to the immune microenvironment found in late-stage cancer. Therefore, the changes in platelet PPDPF expression in sepsis, along with the resulting alterations in immune cells and molecular mechanisms, require further investigation. Additionally, the GSE252275 dataset, which includes platelet sequencing data from 20 healthy controls and 20 sepsis patients, revealed differential expression of CD93, CTSW, FOS, RPL11, and TRAIL, consistent with the genes identified in our study (Supplementary Material 1). We performed qRT-PCR analysis on CLP-induced sepsis mice and observed expression differences in five genes between the sepsis and sham operation groups: CD36, CD93, GIMAP4, PPDPF, and MAP3K7. This suggests that these genes may play significant roles in sepsis progression. However, we did not detect differences in the other five genes, which could be attributed to factors such as the sepsis modeling method, timing of blood collection, rapid RNA degradation, or the small sample size of the model mice. Additionally, alternative splicing could affect gene function. We extracted exon transcripts from the hg38.gtf file and performed alternative splicing analysis using Astalavista software. Our predictions suggest that four of the five genes with no detected differences may potentially undergo alternative splicing (Supplementary Material 2). Currently, research on alternative splicing in sepsis is limited and warrants further exploration in future studies.

Previous studies have constructed diagnostic or prognostic models of sepsis using bulk RNA-seq, but the key cellular changes involved in the development of sepsis have not been identified13,14. Our study first identified the key role of platelets through scRNA-seq and combined multiple bulk RNA-seq analyses with 90 kinds of machine learning algorithms for modeling, thereby ensuring the stability and accuracy of the model while clarifying the key points. Additionally, experimental validation enhances the reliability of the selected genes in our study compared to previous research. After the prognostic model was applied to the dataset, the data were successfully categorized into high- and low-risk groups. Subsequently, a notable disparity in survival rates was observed between these two groups. Analysis of the high- and low-risk groups revealed that the high-risk group had a much lower proportion of most immune cells than the low-risk group. This indicates the serious impact of immunological paralysis on patient survival. Our study may provide insights for clinicians and researchers. For clinicians, compared to multi-gene predictive models proposed in previous literature, our study suggests that clinicians should pay more attention to patients with platelet abnormalities and consider blood gene detection for these patients to determine final prognosis. For researchers, the link between platelets and immune cells and the changes in related genes are likely to be the focus of future sepsis research.

However, there are some limitations to this study. Firstly, the “black box” nature of machine learning algorithms limits our understanding of their computational processes. Secondly, scRNA-Seq data included only four sepsis patients and five control patients. To better elucidate the underlying mechanisms, future studies should involve a larger sample size. Thirdly, although we observed gene expression differences in CLP-induced sepsis mice, using the CLP model to simulate human sepsis remains debated. While the CLP model is simple to operate, highly reproducible, and standardized, it can vary based on laboratory conditions, age, sex, ligated cecum length, needle size, and post-CLP treatment. The ISTH working group on DIC is transitioning from cecal ligation to using cecal slurry, which avoids surgery, requires only an intraperitoneal injection, and ensures standardized dosing. Future research should explore and compare various methods to advance our understanding of sepsis. Moreover, mRNA in platelets originates from megakaryocytes. Sepsis alters mRNA levels in megakaryocytes, and these changes are reflected in platelets. In addition to the mRNA and protein transferred from megakaryocytes to platelets, recent studies have also improved the understanding of platelet protein synthesis. Newly synthesized proteins in platelets under abnormal conditions may be crucial for reaction cascades79,80. Although the exact mechanisms are not fully understood, it is evident that the genetic transfer from megakaryocytes to platelets and the translation regulation within platelets are more complex than previously thought. The molecular mechanisms behind these changes in megakaryocytes, the active transport of platelets, and the translation processes during sepsis need further exploration. Finally, we are still uncertain about how these genes change during the long-term progression of sepsis. Longitudinal clinical cohort studies are necessary to collect blood at different stages of sepsis to clarify specific changes. Additionally, sepsis progression usually involves multiple organ damage, but it is unclear how these platelet gene changes exert their effects in different organs. These issues need further exploration through animal and cell experiments. With advances in precision medicine and increasing global patient needs, better detection technologies and targeted therapies are expected to emerge and be widely promoted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we identified the importance of platelets in sepsis patients through scRNA-seq and developed a novel prognostic model for sepsis patients by combining the key genes from platelets and bulk RNA-seq data. This model is closely associated with patient survival rates and immunological status, which is useful for prognostic management of sepsis.
