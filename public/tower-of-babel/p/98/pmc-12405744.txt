The Role of Cutaneous B‐Cells in Hidradenitis Suppurativa: From Preclinical Evidence to Novel Targeted Therapies
Authors: Maurizio Romagnuolo, Laura Calabrese, Martina D'Onghia, Vittoria Cioppa, Pietro Rubegni, Angelo Valerio Marzano, Chiara Moltrasio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHidradenitis Suppurativa (HS) is a chronic autoinflammatory skin disease of the terminal hair follicle, whose pathogenesis is complex and multifactorial. Alongside a predisposing genetic background, environmental/epigenetic factors, alterations in the skin microbiome, and dysregulation of both innate and adaptive immune responses contribute to the persistent inflammatory network. Despite advancing knowledge, the exact molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying HS have yet to be untangled. Moreover, the lack of experimental disease models that closely mimic disease phenotypes or pathophysiological processes has hindered the development of effective therapeutic strategies. Recent advances in immunology highlighted the important role of B cells in HS pathogenesis, not only in the chronic but also in the early stages of the disease, implying great potential for the development of novel therapeutic targets. This study gathers the preclinical and clinical scientific evidence that supports the role of cutaneous B‐cells in HS pathogenesis and the therapeutic potential of their targeting.Keywords: BTK inhibitors, cutaneous B‐cells, hidradenitis suppurativa, OX40L, rituximab

Introduction

The maintenance of cutaneous barrier integrity depends on both innate and adaptive immune responses. B cells play a wide range of crucial roles in the humoral immunity component of the adaptive immune system, including antibody production, antigen presentation and effector functions. However, the contribution of B cells in non‐lymphoid tissues, such as the skin, is still not fully understood, although emerging evidence underscores their important role both in skin homeostasis and the pathogenesis of cutaneous disorders, such as Hidradenitis Suppurativa (HS).

HS is a chronic, autoinflammatory and debilitating skin disease of the terminal hair follicle, characterised by deep‐seated inflammatory nodules, painful abscesses and interconnected dermal tunnels in apocrine gland–bearing areas, in particular axilla, anogenital and inguinal regions [1]. A significant proportion of HS patients develop irreversible tissue destruction accompanied by extensive scarring, leading to functional impairment of affected folds, psychological distress and work‐related limitations [2, 3]. According to recent estimates, HS affects approximately 1% of the population, with a female: male ratio of 3:1 in the Western world [4]. However, the complex and various clinical manifestations of HS result in under‐ and/or misdiagnosis, leading to a substantial diagnostic delay, averaging between 7 and 12 years from the first symptoms to correct diagnosis [5]. From a pathogenic perspective, HS is currently regarded as a T helper (Th)1/17 process, in which a three‐stage sequence of events—follicular hyperkeratosis, occlusion of the terminal hair follicle and inflammatory phase with the release of follicular content into the surrounding tissues—occurs within the pilosebaceous unit [1]. As HS progresses, the immune infiltrate changes and is accompanied by tissue remodelling and scarring [6]. Although the exact cellular and molecular mechanism underlying HS has not been entirely elucidated, it is believed to be a multifactorial disease caused by environmental/epigenetic factors [7], immune dysregulation and alteration in the skin microbiome [1, 8] alongside a predisposing genetic background [9, 10]. Nevertheless, understanding of how these pathogenic factors influence disease onset and progression is still limited. In addition, the lack of experimental disease models that closely mimic disease phenotypes or pathophysiological processes has greatly hindered the development of effective therapeutic interventions [11]. Only a continued increase and integration of knowledge about the HS pathomechanisms will provide a starting point for the development of novel promising therapeutic targets in the era of personalised medicine. Furthermore, given the highly inflammatory nature of HS and the relative paucity of studies focused on the immunology of this disease, it is clear that foundational knowledge of immune dysregulation in HS is urgently needed to identify specific pathways that may lead to more effective treatments. Recent evidence is shedding light on the role of B‐cells in HS pathogenesis, not only in the chronic but also in the early stages of the disease, implying great potential for the development of novel therapeutic targets.

Our narrative review aims to gather the preclinical and clinical scientific evidence in the literature supporting the role of cutaneous B‐cells in HS pathogenesis and the therapeutic potential of their targeting. A focus on the biology and function of B cells under physiological conditions is also presented.

B Cells: An Overview

B cells, also known as B lymphocytes, represent the cellular arm of adaptive humoral immunity, mainly through antibody production. They exert several functions through various mechanisms including direct virus inactivation, opsonization via neutrophils, phagocytosis, complement activation and antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity. B cells could also act as antigen presenting cells (APCs), participating in both pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory crosstalk [12].

In humans, B‐cell originates from haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow and then completes its maturation into an antibody‐secreting B cell in secondary lymphoid organs (spleen and lymph nodes) through various stages and complex molecular mechanisms, acquiring cell‐intrinsic features that regulate their ability to exit quiescence and build effective immune responses [13]. In the follicular areas of the spleen, large amounts of B‐cell activating factor (BAFF) are produced by resident dendritic cells, allowing B‐cell survival and maturation in two distinct lineages: (i) follicular B‐cell (the majority), that aggregate and form primary follicles, in which T‐mediated immune response is orchestrated and (ii) non‐circulating marginal zone B‐cell, characterised by CD21 expression and closely located to sinusoids and thus blood stream, representing a first‐line defence towards blood‐borne pathogens and also acting as APC [14].

During immune responses, naïve follicular B‐cells encounter antigens presented by follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) (via CD30 ligand) and macrophages moving towards the interfollicular zone, where they interact with the cognate antigen‐specific activated T helper (Th) cells via inducible co‐stimulatory molecule ligand (ICOS‐L) and CD40/CD40L interaction [15]. This allows them to proliferate into antibody‐producing cells: (i) plasmablast, an activated B cell that secretes antibody while remaining in the cell cycle and (ii) plasma cell, the effector cell of the humoral adaptive immune system [16]. A minority of the germinal centre B‐cells circulate between the bloodstream and the secondary lymphoid organs as long‐lasting memory B‐cells, capable of faster and highly specific immune responses upon re‐exposure to antigens related to a previous immune response [16].

B Cells and the Skin

The presence of functioning B cells in normal human skin is still a matter of debate, as it was initially hypothesized that normal skin lacked B cells [17]; however, recent studies indicate that scattered recirculating B cells could be found in the dermis, although the mechanisms by which they migrate to the skin are not fully clarified. It has been hypothesized that cutaneous lymphocyte antigen (CLA) could play a significant role, as its importance for T cell trafficking to the skin [18]. Indeed, B cells expressing CLA have been found in both healthy individuals and those with cutaneous disorders [19, 20], suggesting their skin‐homing properties [20]. Furthermore, several chemokine receptors (CCR) including, among others, CCR4/5/7 and 10, as well as C‐X‐C motif chemokine receptor (CXCR) 4 and 5, are thought to be involved in cutaneous B cells recruitment [20]. These B cells in humans are mainly conventional follicular B cells, while in mice, innate B‐1‐like cells have been found, probably contributing as adjunct first‐line defence of the skin [21].

By contrast, the contribution of cutaneous B cells to the pathogenesis of different inflammatory skin disorders, including, among others, pemphigus vulgaris (PV), lupus erythematosus, systemic sclerosis, and atopic dermatitis is now well recognised [22]. Although, except for PV, their exact functional role remains to be determined [22]. Notably, IgG4‐related skin disease (IgG4‐RD) is a recently established clinical entity hallmarked by tissue infiltration of IgG4+ plasma cells [23].

Cutaneous B cells are organised into tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs), whose exact role is still a matter of debate. These aggregates are ectopic lymphoid tissues with a B and T cell zone, vascular structure, accompanying APCs and fibroblast, which resemble a germinal centre outside the secondary lymphoid organs and drive antigen‐specific immune responses at sites of chronic inflammation [24]. It is known that chronic inflammatory stimuli could lead to an accumulation of lymphatic aggregates into the skin, for which it has been proposed the term “inducible skin‐associated lymphatic tissue” (iSALT) [25]. These structures are usually transient, disappearing when the inflammatory stimuli cease, and lack the complex lymphatic vascular structure found in TLS, but promote B cells maturation and antibodies production acting as ectopic lymphoid organs [26]. Although the effective presence of iSALT is still a matter of debate, similar structures with a predominant B cell activity have been found, among other, in dermal skin lesions of bullous pemphigoid—where IgG and IgE producing cells were also detected—in pemphigus vulgaris and in hypoderma of lupus panniculitis skin specimens [20, 27, 28, 29].

B Cells in HS Pathogenesis, Disease Progression and Treatment Response

While the exact role of B cells and their late differentiation patterns in HS onset is still to be defined, recent evidence is shedding light on their role in disease progression and treatment response [30]. One of the first pieces of evidence for the presence of B cells in HS came from an immunohistochemical study conducted by van der Zee et al. [31], in which ectopic CD20+ and CD79a+ B‐cell and CD138+ plasma cell ‘pseudo’ lymphoid follicles—probably to be considered as germinal centres—were observed in HS lesional skin, leading the authors to speculate on a secondary reaction to persistent inflammation or bacterial colonisation. Subsequently, various studies confirmed the presence and a potential significant role of B cells in HS. A transcriptomic study revealed—in terms of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)—an abundance of immunoglobulins and plasma cells in HS lesional skin compared with healthy control (HC) skin, potentially to promote opsonization [32]. More in detail, HS lesional transcriptome displayed a significant upregulation of kappa and lambda light chains, IgD, IgA1 and IgG3/IgG1; the latter led to the assumption that the previously documented overexpression of interleukin (IL)‐10, an anti‐inflammatory cytokine secreted by B cells, in HS [33] precedes the immunoglobulins production based upon triggers of B‐cell class switching (a biological mechanism that changes a B cell's production of immunoglobulin from one type to another). Subsequently, Musilova et al. [34] analysing peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by flow cytometry, found a statistically significant increase in the frequency of switched memory (CD19+ CD27+ IgD−) B cells in HS patients when compared to controls. Furthermore, a higher frequency of plasmablasts (CD38+ CD138−) and plasma cells (CD38+ CD138+) within the total B cells population in the HS cohort was detected. Then, the same authors conducted a single‐cell dissociation and cytometric analysis of skin biopsies, unravelling a significant increased frequency of CD19+ B cells and plasma cells (HLA‐DR−CD38+ CD20− CD19+) in HS lesional skin compared to HCs. The presence of B and plasma cells was also confirmed by immunostaining that revealed an increased number of CD20+ B cells and CD138+ plasma cells in HS‐involved skin. Finally, through mRNA expression levels of key B cell transcription factors, the authors supported the pathogenic perspective of HS as a B cell‐related disease, with a humoral immunity involvement. Interestingly, Byrd et al. [35], in addition to proving an increased frequency of CD86+ B cells, plasma cells and IgG in HS patients' sera, found elevated autoantibodies reactive to citrullinated proteins as well as a differential expression of peptidylarginine deiminases (PADs) 1–4, involved in the citrullination process. Of note, specific citrullinated antigens were previously reported in neutrophils and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), thus hypothesising that enhanced autoantigen generation in HS lesional skin and peripherally, through increased NET formation, could play a role in promoting an aberrant autoantibody production [35]. Some years later, the same research group [36] also demonstrated the presence of IgG autoantibodies directed against a plethora of antigens, cytokines and extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, most of which significantly enhanced in moderate‐to‐severe disease stages. Furthermore, immune complexes containing native and citrullinated antigens were able to activate both M1 and M2 macrophages, which in turn promoted the secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor (TNF)‐α, IL‐8, IL‐6, and IL‐12, thus suggesting a crucial role of autoimmune mechanisms in HS and unravelling putative therapeutic targets.

In light of this evidence, a larger number of studies have focused on an in‐depth understanding of B cells in the pathogenetic scenario of HS. Gudjonsson et al. [6], using proteomic and transcriptomic approaches, demonstrated that Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) and spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) pathway activation were crucial signal transduction cascades in HS lesional skin, thus providing preclinical evidence to consider them potential therapeutic targets in moderate‐to‐severe HS. In actual fact, a successful use of B cell signalling targeting in HS has been previously reported by Takahashi et al. [37], with rituximab—a chimeric monoclonal antibody against CD20—in a patient being treated for an unrelated immune disease. Similarly, HS skin explants treated with rituximab demonstrated a significant decrease of different pro‐inflammatory mediators [38]. However, the association of clinical improvement with rituximab should not necessarily indicate that B cells are primarily pathogenic in HS as they may act as indirect inflammatory stimuli in long‐standing disease [39]. Regarding this aspect, it has long been reported that B cells play a role in chronic long‐standing lesions, as also demonstrated by the presence of TLS in chronic inflammatory stages [37, 39, 40, 41, 42]. Moreover, a feedback mechanism between B cells and neutrophils through NETs could amplify B‐cell activity and exacerbate pre‐existing disease in HS [39]. On the other hand, consistent with an active role of B cells in HS pathogenesis was the observation that these cells were prominent in early HS lesions, as well as that treatment with TNF blockers was able to markedly attenuate B cell activation, and HS patients with enriched B‐cell activation pathways at baseline were poor responders to adalimumab [42, 43]. These findings underlined the important interaction between B cells and TNF pathway in HS pathogenesis, subsequently favouring the launch of new clinical trials. In this setting, OX‐40 L, whose increased expression was observed in HS lesions, is of particular importance. It is the ligand for OX40 (also known as CD134) and is stably expressed on many APCs, macrophages, and activated B cells with an important role in modulating inflammatory and immune processes [44]. Thus, this dual target therapy may enhance the efficacy in HS management, reducing pain and clinical manifestations, as discussed below.

In line with the observation recently discussed that B cells were prominent in early HS lesions and contrary to previous studies indicating macrophage as the predominant cells in HS lesions [45], Altman et al. [46] reported that plasma cells were the largest inflammatory cell fraction in early stage HS lesions, in association with migrating stratified squamous epithelium (MSSE), the earliest stage of post‐follicle rupture. These findings suggested that the presence of plasma cells could be due to an autoantibody response provoked by keratinocytes [35]; in addition, considering that granulocytes were more associated with degraded migrating epithelial sheets (DMES), the inflammatory response underlying HS could be the result of cyclic epithelial production, migration and degradation [46]. Dynamic interactions between leucocyte populations associated with early and late‐stage HS lesions have also been reported by Smith and colleagues [47], highlighting the role of B‐cell‐derived transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β. The latter has been reported to be produced by different cells including keratinocytes, T cells, B cells and plasma cells, playing a crucial role in the wound healing process [48], and its sustained signalling in HS [49] could act as a major driver in the development of tunnels and hypertrophic scarring [47]. Similarly, Sabat et al. [50] identified BAFF as a B‐cell‐supporting factor produced by neutrophils in HS, mainly during exposure to granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) in the presence of bacterial products. Although the pathogenic significance is still to be validated, the BAFF pathway could represent a promising therapeutic target in HS.

To in‐depth explore the B‐cell related microenvironment on HS lesional skin, Lowe et al. [51] conducted meticulous work that led them to conclusively demonstrate the presence of TLSs within HS lesions, able to support B cell affinity maturation and function. Of note, skin stroma and T‐cells, organised in a cellular niche, first supported TLSs formation, thereby promoting B cells recruitment. The model of a tissue microenvironment rich in chemokines and maintenance factors [52] has been confirmed by this and previous studies, in which upregulated B cell survival factors [42, 50, 51, 53] as well as chemokine (C–C motif) ligand (CCL) 10 and 18 [51, 53] and chemokine (C–X–C motif) ligand (CXCL) 13 [42, 51, 53] were reported. Of note, the presence of CXCL13 also implies the fibroblasts involvement in B cell recruitment [54], TLSs activity and disease progression since they are the only stromal cells that produce this mediator. Immunologically active CXCL13+ fibroblasts have also been shown to demarcate the edges of the inflammatory infiltrate in spatial proximity to B and plasma cells, potentially to limit the inflammatory response [55]. In turn, B cells may activate fibroblasts through secretion of TNF, forming a potential feedback loop, representing an attractive target for B‐cell‐directed therapies [56, 57].

Overall, these findings support multiple roles of B cells in HS, from autoantibody production to stromal remodelling, fibrosis, and therapeutic response, although both observational and functional research is needed to unravel the exact pathogenic contribution of these cells in HS. Concurrently, clinical trials are awaited as they may be useful for HS patients.

Future Therapies and Ongoing Clinical Trials

As mentioned above and as well illustrated by a recent review by Sabat et al. [58], there is a need for further safe and effective therapies, starting with translational research on the main pathomechanisms underlying HS to reach promising therapeutic targets.

The pivotal role of B cells, along with the BTK and SYK signalling pathways, in driving chronic inflammation is well supported by recent clinical evidence (Figure 1). At week 16, the results of a Phase 2 clinical trial revealed higher rates of participants on remibrutinib, an oral BTK inhibitor, achieving simplified Hidradenitis Suppurativa Clinical Response score (HiSCR50) with 72.7% (25 mg) and 48.5% (100 mg) versus 34.7% (pooled placebo) [59]. Greater reduction in draining tunnels and nodule counts, together with an improvement in skin pain, were also demonstrated. Concerning the safety profile, remibrutinib was well tolerated, with adverse event (AE) frequencies comparable between treatment arms (n = 1 per arm). The most common AE in the treatment arms was upper respiratory infections [59].

Additionally, two Phase 3 clinical trials (RECHARGE I and RECHARGE II) are now underway to assess the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of remibrutinib in adult patients with moderate‐to‐severe HS [60, 61].

Interestingly, to date, remibrutinib showed sustained clinical efficacy and overall safety in other inflammatory diseases such as chronic spontaneous urticaria and Sjögren syndrome [62, 63].

Overall, this data suggests that targeting BTK may effectively modulate the inflammatory process in HS by disrupting B cell activation and downstream signalling pathways.

Similarly, a proof‐of‐concept open‐label Phase 2 clinical study involving 20 HS patients treated with oral fostamatinib—a SYK inhibitor already approved for the treatment of immune thrombocytopenia—reported an impressive HiSCR response rate of 85% within only 12 weeks with no evidence of Grade 3 or 4 adverse events [64], further suggesting how SYK inhibition could be a promising therapeutic option for HS.

Furthermore, rituximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the CD20 antigen on B cells, has shown some efficacy in limited case series, underscoring the potential benefits of depleting B cells in HS management, although no clinical studies are currently investigating the effectiveness of this antibody for HS treatment [37, 65, 66].

As mentioned above, some studies outlined how TNF‐a non‐responders exhibited an increased expression of mediators involved in B cell‐related transcripts with an enrichment of B cell activation signalling pathways [42, 43], underscoring the interaction between B cells and the TNF pathway in HS pathogenesis. From a therapeutic perspective, a Phase 2 randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled proof‐of‐concept study investigating the role of the combined TNF‐α and OX40L targeting (Anti‐TNF‐OX40L NANOBODY Molecule, SAR442970) has recently been concluded [67, 68]. Another parallel Phase 2, 2‐arm, double‐blind, randomised, placebo‐controlled study to evaluate efficacy, safety, pharmacokinetics and biological effects of treatment of subcutaneous injection of amlitelimab [69] (SAR445229, a fully human nondepleting, noncytotoxic anti‐OX40L monoclonal antibody) is currently ongoing.

Another potential drug which targets the key molecules responsible for co‐stimulation between T and B cells (CD40‐CD40L interaction) and represented by the anti‐CD40 antibody iscalimab is currently in Phase 2 clinical trial for moderate to severe HS [70].

Notably, a broad spectrum of autoantibodies against circulating and tissue antigens has been detected in lesional skin of HS patients, particularly in advanced stages of the disease (Hurley II and III). This suggests that autoantibodies arise due to tissue destruction, which is further amplified by their externalisation through NET formation, a key feature of late‐stage disease [39]. NETs are composed of extracellular DNA and histones, which can exacerbate inflammation and serve as a source of autoantigens that perpetuate the disease [39]. Consequently, inhibition of NET formation using a novel monoclonal antibody targeting citrullinated histones H2A and H4 (CIT‐013) may represent a promising therapeutic avenue [71]. In addition to addressing NET‐related inflammation, targeting citrullinated proteins may help prevent further tissue damage, disease progression, as well as autoantibody production [71].

In the context of nodules and abscesses, neutrophil‐derived BAFF appears to play a significant role in promoting B and plasma cell survival compared to tunnel lesions. As previously mentioned, BAFF is an essential cytokine for B cell maturation and survival, and it has been found to be elevated in HS lesions. Based on these insights, a novel anti‐BAFF receptor antibody, ianalumab (VAY736), is currently under Phase 2 clinical investigation [70], offering new possibilities for HS management. The potential of BAFF inhibition to modulate the B cell response and reduce chronic inflammation underscores the need for further studies to establish its long‐term efficacy and safety.

Overall, these findings highlight the intricate interplay between immune cells and inflammatory pathways in HS, providing a rationale for future therapeutic strategies targeting B cell‐mediated mechanisms.

Major Open Questions

HS is a complex disease, whose multifactorial pathogenesis is still to be unravelled. Alongside a predisposing genetic background, ongoing questions regarding the relationship between innate and adaptive immune response as well as the contribution of B cells require further investigations. Furthermore, given the multiple roles of B cells, it will be critical to determine at which stage of the disease they compete most. It will be equally important to determine the pathogenic nature of interactions with other immune cells, mainly neutrophils, and key inflammatory signalling pathways.

Conclusions and Perspectives

Emerging evidence underscores the crucial role of B cells in the pathogenesis and progression of HS, expanding our understanding beyond the traditionally recognised T‐helper cell‐driven mechanisms. B cells might contribute to HS through multiple pathways, including autoantibody production, antigen presentation, and modulation of inflammatory responses, which collectively influence disease onset and chronicity. Despite these insights, many aspects of B cell involvement in HS remain underexplored, highlighting a crucial gap in our knowledge that necessitates further investigation. A more comprehensive understanding of B cell functions in HS could open new therapeutic fields, offering promising strategies for targeted interventions that modulate B cell activity to alleviate disease burden.

In conclusion, our review highlights the potential role of B cells in the evolving landscape of HS management. However, further research into B cell biology in HS, combined with interdisciplinary efforts integrating immunology, dermatology, and precision medicine, will be essential to improving patient outcomes and addressing the substantial unmet clinical needs associated with this debilitating condition.
