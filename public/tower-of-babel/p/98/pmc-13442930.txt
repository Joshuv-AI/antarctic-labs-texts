Breaking barriers and fighting inequality in Parkinson's disease care: Personal reflections of movement disorders clinicians
Authors: Shen-Yang Lim, Hrishikesh Kumar, Wei Luo, Jonathan Carr, Zakiyah Aldaajani, Cholpon Shambetova, Priya Jagota, Frandy Susatia, Eliza Zhunusova, Zhidong Cen, Supriyo Choudhury, Saeed Bohlega, Francisco Cardoso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parkinson's disease (PD) care remains profoundly unequal across and within countries, despite major advances in understanding and treatment. Drawing on PD specialists’ personal stories of real-world experiences from diverse regions across Asia, Africa, and South America, this paper highlights the key barriers to equitable PD care and identifies pragmatic, scalable solutions. Across settings, several major barriers consistently emerge: a critical shortage and uneven distribution of trained specialists; geographic disparities limiting access to care; substantial financial barriers, particularly for comprehensive and advanced therapies; fragmented healthcare systems lacking integration and multidisciplinary support; and low public awareness and persistent stigma, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment. Strategies to address these challenges include workforce development through training, mentorship, and international collaboration; bringing expertise and services closer to patients via outreach programs and the use of “simple” technologies such as telemedicine and mobile communication platforms; ensuring universal access to essential medications, particularly levodopa; integrating multidisciplinary care models; public awareness campaigns and support groups empowering patients and caregivers and reducing stigma; and embedding research into routine care. The experiences presented here illustrate that meaningful progress is achievable through pragmatic solutions, collaborative networks, and sustained commitment to patient-centered care.

Introduction

The care of individuals and families living with Parkinson's disease (PD) is often complex and multifaceted.1–3 In part, this is due to the progressive nature of the disease and the many symptoms that arise during the disease course, affecting not only movements but a myriad of non-motor symptoms as well,1,2,4 reflecting its complex pathophysiology. 5 The delivery of care is further compromised by significant limitations in underserved settings (regions, countries, or even segments within highly-developed societies), because of the lack of financial, infrastructural, and skilled human resources,6,7 and other societal issues such as poor health literacy and stigma.8–10 Ironically, while major scientific advances in PD and related neurodegenerative disorders are likely to promote personalized precision medicine and transform PD care in the near future, these high-tech approaches are usually resource-intensive and thus have great potential to further widen health disparities between high-income vs. lower-income groups.1,11–13

In this collaborative piece, a group of experienced movement disorders neurologists from nine countries (India, China, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Kyrgyzstan, Saudi Arabia, Brazil, and South Africa) ( Figure 1 ) share their personal and professional journeys in reducing inequity and breaking barriers for the care of patients and families living with PD. In addition to their track records in their respective local settings, most hold positions of leadership within the movement disorders field, e.g., as Past-President of the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society (MDS - FC), Past-Chairs of the MDS-African Section (AS - JC), Asian-Oceanian Section (AOS - SYL), and the MDS-AOS Middle East Working Group (ZA), Chair of the MDS-AOS Movement Disorders in Underserved Areas Committee (PJ), and Executive Committee members of the MDS-AOS (HK, WL, CS, FS), and thus bring additional insights from cross-border involvement. The narratives are arranged in geographical sequence from West to East, starting from Brazil and finishing in Indonesia. The write-ups highlight key barriers to equitable PD care and identify pragmatic, and often scalable solutions.

Francisco Cardoso, MD PhD (Belo Horizonte, Brazil)

In 1993 I returned to Brazil from my training in movement disorders at the Baylor College of Medicine (Houston, TX, USA) under the guidance of Joseph Jankovic MD. Then, the population of Brazil, whose area is 8,515,767 km2 (the fifth largest country of the world), was 156.8 million people. Considering our community-based study, 14 it was estimated that there were 401,000 people with PD. Since 1990, Brazil has had a public health system (Sistema Único de Saúde – SUS) that has universal access for free to anyone living in the country. In parallel, there is also a private system that can be used either by payments out of pocket or health insurance. It is estimated that 30% of the Brazilian population can afford private health care. 1

In 1993, there were just three neurologists in the country doing movement disorders: Luiz Augusto Franco Andrade MD PhD, Egberto dos Reis Barbosa MD PhD, and Hélio Afonso Ghizoni Teive MD PhD. Based in the wealthy south of the country, they worked both at SUS and in private practice. I felt then it was necessary to have a two-pronged approach to address the issue of a critical lack of proper care for people with PD and other movement disorders. At a local level, I created the Movement Disorders Clinic of the Federal University of Minas Gerais (MDC-UFMG) at the SUS. As our catchment area has a population of 20 million people, MDC-UFMG has grown very rapidly, branching out into several sub-units: Parkinsonism, deep brain stimulation (DBS) and other surgical therapies, Dystonia, Botulinum Toxin, Chorea, and Other Movement Disorders. Currently we have a staff of eight neurologists, one palliative care doctor, one functional neurosurgeon, a team of physiotherapists, a team of speech therapists, three fellows, and a variable number of residents (Neurology, Neurosurgery, Geriatrics, and Genetics). This has greatly improved the care of people with movement disorders both in quantitative and qualitative aspects. However, we still face long waiting times for access to our unit. Unfortunately, this is a feature SUS shares with public health systems of other countries.

The second aim of the strategy was to launch an educational campaign to disseminate knowledge of movement disorders in the country. Along with the previously mentioned colleagues and friends, in 1999 we held the first Brazilian Movement Disorders Course in the old colonial town of Ouro Preto, not far from Belo Horizonte, where I am based. We attracted 100 attendees, having Andrew Lees MD, Joseph Jankovic MD, and Bill Koller MD as foreign guests. Since then, there have been regular annual national meetings in our field. The last one took place in São Paulo on April 9–11, 2026, with 500 enthusiastic attendees under the leadership of Flávia Rolim MD, Pedro Renato Brandão MD PhD, and Rubens Cury MD PhD. In fact, the organizers had to turn down many applicants for this edition because of the capacity of the convention area. It is estimated that there are currently ∼200 movement disorders neurologists in Brazil, with specialized units in 20 of the 27 states of the country spread out from the Amazon rainforest area to the very south. This multiplication of movement disorders doctors resulting in an enthusiastic and highly skilled generation of young professionals is the most important achievement of the pioneers of the field in Brazil.

The exponential growth of the movement disorders field in Brazil resulted in changes of the SUS. At present, it provides levodopa/benserazide, levodopa/carbidopa, rasagiline, biperiden, amantadine, entacapone, pramipexole, oral and patch rivastigmine, and clozapine free-of-charge. DBS is also available in the public health system although with the caveat that the number of procedures is still below the current demand, creating long waiting periods (mean ∼18 months, but with a wide range of 6–24 months, depending on the region of the country).

Looking back, I feel happy with the progress we have made over these past 33 years. There is, though, much to be done: the number of doctors properly trained in movement disorders is clearly below the need for a population of 214 million in 2026; the waiting lists at the SUS are unacceptably long; and the access to complex therapies such as DBS is limited.

Jonathan Carr, MBChB PhD (Cape Town, South Africa)

When I first started working in Neurology it was at Groote Schuur Hospital in Cape Town, where the world's first heart transplant had been carried out 20 years previously. The wards were still segregated according to race (this was 1985–1986), and in the rather plusher white ward, the first patient with PD whom I remember was the father of a friend of my sister's from high school. Occasionally, the country's oldest political prisoner, the trade unionist and a leader of the African National Congress (ANC), Oscar Mpetha, would be admitted around the same period, but he would be held in the so-called “Black ward” under armed guard. I then worked at the Mount Sinai Medical Centre in New York City under Melvin Yahr; fortunately, back home in South Africa, things had changed (a lot!) and the ANC was shortly to form a new government. A while later, I was able to do some sabbatical at the University of British Columbia with Donald Calne and from that perspective, it was fairly obvious that exploring the genetic origins of PD was likely to be a sensible option.

We certainly hoped that we might find some unique discoveries, given the remarkably diverse genetic architecture of southern Africa, but I think it is reasonable to sum up to say that our results in Cape Town have been a little disappointing. In part, this reflects the journey of genetic discovery in PD, where there was an initial assumption that everything would turn out to be easy and autosomal dominant, but which, sadly (but not unsurprisingly in hindsight), turned out to be far from correct. Fortunately, other researchers in Africa have made some remarkable genetic discoveries,15–17 and following our Global Parkinson's Genetics Program (GP2) meeting in Nairobi earlier this year, we can certainly anticipate more and more collaborative findings.18,19

With respect to PD in Africa there is truly no doubt that the support and encouragement from the MDS have been phenomenal and groundbreaking. The fact that only five years ago, we established an African section, but already over 10% of MDS membership hails from Africa, speaks volumes for our progress as a Section, and to the encouragement from the Society. It is worth remembering that Africa is a vast continent, not only combining the land mass of USA, China, India, and most of Europe, but also a region where close to 3000 different languages are found. The MDS-AS had its origins in the Sub-Saharan Africa Interest Group, from 2009, and, driven very much by Richard Walker from Newcastle (United Kingdom), and Njide Okubadejo from Lagos, subsequently evolved into a task force and section, with huge support and encouragement from MDS Presidents Chris Goetz and Oscar Gershanik.

What is frequently not appreciated adequately is that there are fewer than a handful of PD specialists in most of Africa. 7 Even where there are neurologists, they are necessarily obliged to devote their attention to a wide range of neurological illnesses. I worry about the vicious circle of poverty: how impoverished people suffer from seemingly unstoppable infectious diseases, malnutrition, and massive social deprivation. For them, diseases of the elderly such as PD are not even visible in their universe. 10 Just getting by, avoiding personal violence, trying to ensure that their children are not sucked up into a whirlpool of drug-related criminality, searching futilely for some kind of work that offers dignity: these are the day-to-day concerns for most, and again, a reminder: the median age of Sub-Saharan Africans is 18 years; we are an extraordinarily youthful continent. However, I always remember one patient of mine with PD telling me how local youths threw stones at her shack: informal settlements in urban centres are the norm and typically consist of the most rudimentary structures, usually with walls and roof of corrugated iron. How did she respond? She wrote a letter of complaint to the state president (to which she received a reply!).

Africa is beset by problems, but this is countered by the resilience, hope and determination of its people. From Cairo to Cape Town, the African Section of the MDS is a beacon of learning and training for more and more doctors in the continent. Our continent is vast, and its needs are broad, ranging from determining the molecular characterization of a novel genetic variant to trying to ensure that basic treatment is available. However, we can already see improvements and shifts in perceptions. Above all, our next step must be to continue our path of providing education about PD, and I have little doubt that the many fine physicians who have attended training sessions now are able to recognize the importance of ensuring that levodopa is available to all who need it.

The past few years have not only been characterized by a major focus on education, but also by conspicuous engagement with Allied Health Professionals throughout Africa. If there is no neurologist, an intervention by physio- and occupational therapists may make a substantial improvement in quality of life for many patients, especially at grassroots level. In a similar vein, it is important to highlight important work from West Africa on use of mucuna powder as a readily available alternative to levodopa. 20 TraPCAf (Transforming Parkinson's Care in Africa) is an important project with many facets, including studies on prevalence, microbiome, and capacity building, 21 and is emblematic of the enormous strides which have taken place in an incredibly short time in Africa.

Zakiyah Aldaajani, MD (Dhahran, Saudi Arabia) and Saeed Bohlega, MD FRCPC (Riyadh, Saudi Arabia)

PD has been found to affect the Saudi population with a prevalence of ∼27/100,000, according to the 1993 Thugbah study by Al Rajeh et al., which marked the first epidemiological study on PD in Saudi Arabia and the Gulf region. 22 Subsequent investigators found that there was a predominance of young-onset patients with tremor-predominant presentation (78%). 23 Genetic heterogeneity has been described by Al-Mubarak et al., who found five disease-causing mutations (2 PARKIN, 2 PINK1, and 1 PARK7/DJ-1). 24

The gaps in access to care were highlighted by Khalil et al., identifying key deficiencies: 25–80 neurologists available for a population of 31.5 million, with Saudi movement disorders specialists comprising less than 1% of MDS global membership. 25

The journey of PD care in Saudi Arabia started in Riyadh, at King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Center (KFSHRC), where advanced treatments began with the first DBS surgery in 2002 for a 52- year-old male with tremor-dominant PD. Now, DBS is present across 3 major centers in Saudi Arabia, as well as in the United Arab Emirates (UAE), Kuwait, and Oman. In 2008, the KFSHRC in Riyadh performed the first levodopa-carbidopa intestinal gel (LCIG) infusion; and in 2015 they conducted the 1st LCIG study in the region on 20 patients. 26 2025 was a breakthrough in the Middle East for KFSHRC; the first adaptive DBS device was implanted detecting abnormal brain activity in real-time and reducing medication needs by up to 50% with same-day discharge. 27

The 1st Movement Disorders Program was established in 2004 at KFSHRC, however, there were long waiting times. It was mandatory to have other centers. The PD Program of the King Fahad Medical City (KFMC) in Riyadh, established in 2008 within the National Neuroscience Institute, had an integrated program, treating 87 patients in 2015 through coordinated care between neurology and rehabilitation, cutting down waiting times. 28

In 2014, the Middle East Task Force/Working Group of the MDS was created, aiming to spread PD education in the region. This represented a watershed moment. I (ZA) was fortunate to have been one of the members from its early beginning; I chaired the group for two terms, and took it upon myself to continue the journey of spreading movement disorders education and involving Middle East neurologists. We continued as a group to work and collaborate with movement disorders specialists in the region, conduct camps/schools that provided practical training in PD phenotypes, botulinum toxin treatments, DBS programming, and cutting-edge therapies. By 2025, these initiatives helped to train more than 500 neurologists in the region, impacting positively on the access to care.

My work did not stop at education. In 2015, I established the 1st PD clinic across the Military Hospitals, introduced dopaminergic infusion (levodopa-carbidopa intestinal gel) therapy, and participated in standardizing PD guidelines across Military Hospitals. I was involved in the feasibility of virtual visits study, which gave rural-living and disabled patients opportunities to access care remotely using telemedicine 29 ; this practice is now a mandated healthcare practice in Saudi Arabia, especially following COVID.

The Saudi Movement Disorders Chapter under the Saudi Neurological Society was a national transformation, and through annual Movement Disorder Chapter conferences, >500 professionals per year received training in PD and various movement disorders. Tertiary care services became well-coordinated by developing multidisciplinary clinics. Saudi Arabia now has the highest number of movement disorders specialists in the Middle East (up to 36); the development of the accredited Saudi Commission For Health Specialties (SCFHS) Movement Disorders Fellowship Program had a huge impact, as it has generated 8 certified experts since its launch, with its graduates establishing clinics in cities throughout the country.

Saudi Arabia is now the leader in DBS in the region with procedures done across 3 hospitals. Advanced infusion therapies are available in most major referral centers. The Cleveland Clinic of Abu Dhabi in UAE 30 is second in line, and King's College Hospital London in Dubai has already conducted subcutaneous fos-levodopa pump therapy. 31 Other neighboring countries with advanced therapies access include Kuwait and Oman. The PD care provided by Saudi movement disorders specialists crosses the border to neighboring countries like Bahrain, with procedures conducted in Saudi Arabia, and followed up by specialist visits to Bahrain.

The Saudi PD Society has reached out to a large number of patients through educational sessions in various cities, conducted by local PD specialists. This has dramatically increased awareness, reduced the social stigma of PD, and dispelled cultural beliefs including reliance on traditional healers. PD Day has become an annual event in all large hospitals.

Hrishikesh Kumar, MD DM DLitt and Supriyo Choudhury, MD PhD (Kolkata, India)

It was 1st January 2011, my (HK) first day as a movement disorders neurologist in Kolkata, a city of >15 million people. An elderly man was brought in reluctantly by his family, who were convinced that he was simply ageing. One glance told me otherwise: this was PD. When he started walking almost normally after taking a small dose of levodopa, he turned to his son and said, “I was telling you this is not old age. You robbed me of my last four years.” That same day, I saw another early-stage patient on multiple PD medications, including two dopamine agonists. Two patients, one morning, and the scale of the problem was already clear: a profound lack of public awareness and a critical gap in understanding of treatment. The challenge for movement disorders experts in India has been daunting. The country has >700,000 PD patients and perhaps 50 trained movement disorders specialists to serve them, which is very inadequate. 32

During my fellowship in Canada, Dr Vladimir Hachinsky (former President of the World Federation of Neurology) offered advice that would shape my career: “If you are going back, try to join a place that is new, where you can build a system rather than be constrained by one.” That guidance led me to the Institute of Neurosciences, Kolkata, then little more than a shell, built through the vision, personal funds, and determination of an expatriate neurosurgeon Dr Robin Sengupta, supported by a small group of philanthropists. The institute occupied a dilapidated property adjoining a neglected cemetery. Joining an institution in its infancy may have seemed ill-advised, but within a few years, it had grown into one of the highest-volume PD centres in the country. A self-sustaining model, made possible by patient volume and philanthropic support, funded the expansion of clinical services, basic research, a PhD programme, and post-doctoral fellowships. We could develop a strong clinical and basic research team of about 3 dozen members who have worked on novel ideas in the field of PD (vagal nerve stimulation, genetic polymorphisms underlying dyskinesias, the role of inflammation, etc.33–35).

It became evident early on that whatever any single centre could achieve was a drop in the ocean. We began holding regular in-person continuing medical education (CME) sessions for general physicians on Sundays, maintaining close dialogue with referring clinicians. The change in prescription patterns over the years has been gratifying. We also recognised that patients needed more than consultations - they needed community. This led to the founding of Eastern India's first PD support group, modelled on the highly successful group in Mumbai.

A significant number of our patients travel from Bangladesh, drawn by shared language and culture with Kolkata. Watching patients make that journey for issues that could be managed locally was difficult to accept. A breakthrough came through building relationships with neurologists across the border. We established a short-term training programme at our clinic; to date, eight neurologists from Bangladesh have completed rotations with us. It is deeply satisfying to have contributed, even modestly, to neurological care in a neighbouring country.

Closer to home, we recognised that remote communities in Bengal were reluctant, or unable, to travel to the city. The most underserved region was the Sundarbans, the vast delta shared by India and Bangladesh, where communities live in close relationship with the forest, the waterways, and the ever-present risk of tiger encounters. Through the generosity of friends - a businessman badminton buddy, and an equity investor friend - we established a screening clinic at Sundarbans for PD and other neurological disorders. It is an organic partnership of clinicians, philanthropists, and industry (providing medicines free-of-charge). This work was inspired in part by Dr Soaham Desai in Gujarat, who used to send trained volunteers to record videos of patients from remote villages and prescribe treatment based on the video information.

As our work progressed in eastern India, it was clear that excellence in PD care remained geographically patchy across the country. Bangalore, Delhi, and Mumbai had long been centres of expertise, but coverage was fragmented. Some informal conversations over coffee at the 2013 World Federation of Neurology Congress in Vienna seeded the idea that became the Movement Disorders Society of India, founded in 2014 - a landmark moment that gave a common platform for education and advocacy. A subsequent conference in Seoul produced another conversation, and another idea: the National Parkinson Network (NPN), focused on driving evidence-based treatment standards nationwide. Both initiatives shared two common ingredients: Prof. Pramod Pal as its nucleus and the peculiar productivity of corridor conversations at international meetings. The mind, it seems, works best when it escapes the conference hall.

The 2023 Asian and Oceanian Parkinson's Disease and Movement Disorders Congress (AOPMC) held in Kolkata - the largest international movement disorders event ever held in India - marked another inflection point. MDS membership from India since then (942 as of 31 December 2025) reflect an acceleration of interest in the field.

Somehow, I see a higher proportion of young-onset PD patients than I used to see in the Western hemisphere. The reasons remain unclear, but the consequences are profound: when a breadwinner or primary carer develops PD, the impact on the entire family is devastating. Societal attitudes still need to shift considerably - patients often lose their livelihoods despite retaining functional capacity for meaningful work.

Over the years, advocacy efforts of movement disorders experts directed at government and employers have yielded some results. The most significant has been the inclusion of PD in the national list of recognised disabilities, conferring benefits for patients with advanced disease. Government provision of medicines through public hospitals remains insufficient. At our institute, we could establish a dedicated fund - supported by more affluent patients and donors - to help provide medicines for PD patients in need.

In all interactions with community neurologists and general physicians, we emphasise one practical point: simplify prescriptions, particularly for lower-income patients. Expensive medications and supplements appearing on the prescription sheets of patients who cannot afford them are a direct barrier to adherence and, ultimately, to outcomes.

Over the last 2–3 decades, the barriers for PD care in India have not disappeared, but they are lower. The reach has extended. Treatment patterns are improving, not only in Kolkata, but across India. People who share this vision are finding one another and working in concert. Movement disorders practitioners, perhaps by the nature of the speciality, tend to collaborate naturally. One of the papers where we discussed the tale of three cities in India exemplifies such collaboration. 36

Cholpon Shambetova, MD, and Eliza Zhunusova, MD (Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan)

On returning to Bishkek in 2018 from Kuala Lumpur, where I (CS) had received my initial training in movement disorders, I became the first movement disorders specialist in Kyrgyzstan, working within a healthcare and medical education system shaped by Soviet legacy and subsequent decades without dedicated frameworks for neurodegenerative diseases. PD was largely neglected, diagnoses frequently delayed, access to treatment inconsistent or absent, research activity minimal, and awareness among both the public and healthcare providers low, often accompanied by stigma.

Initial efforts focused on establishing dedicated movement disorders clinic days within a public outpatient setting. However, working in isolation, managing a high patient volume, and lacking essential therapies, quickly exposed the limits of this approach. It became evident that individual clinical efforts were insufficient and that meaningful progress would require system-level coordination supported by a multidisciplinary team. Between 2018 and 2026, this work evolved from a single clinician-led initiative into an integrated platform spanning clinical care, research, and training.

The first step was therefore to build a team with patients at its core, recognising them as a critical and underutilised resource. Human resource strengthening became a central pillar. Patients were engaged through a dedicated group using widely accessible platforms such as WhatsApp, alongside the involvement and supervision of medical students. These efforts evolved into a formalised patient–professional alliance. One of the first initiatives was an MDS-AOS–funded national training course in movement disorders in 2019 with international faculty, which attracted neurologists from across the country. From the outset, training, mentorship, and progressive responsibility strengthened clinical capacity, while patient engagement enabled active participation in care, education, and advocacy, contributing to a more responsive and resilient system.

In parallel, patient-focused educational initiatives were introduced, including Parkinson Schools, alongside the gradual integration of allied health professionals. Physiotherapy emerged as a key component, evolving into community-based programmes co-developed with patients and subsequently expanding to include yoga, dance, and art-based activities.

These developments were accompanied by the first steps toward systematic data collection, enabling initial characterisation of clinical features and access to care. Data infrastructure proved critical, not only as a technical backbone, but as an enabling tool for both clinicians and patients, supporting clinical decision-making, audit, and service planning, and facilitating longitudinal follow-up. Concurrently, research evolved from a distinct activity into an integral component of routine practice, enabling the generation of locally-relevant data and participation in collaborative studies. The development of clinical and research capacity was closely linked to advocacy and policy engagement.

Although these steps were essential, they remained limited when implemented in isolation. Discrete interventions, however well designed, risk remaining fragmented unless embedded within a broader system. This realisation marked a transition from addressing individual gaps to developing an integrated model in which care, research, and training are closely aligned. At the same time, persistent resource constraints reinforced the importance of understanding PD within its local context, particularly to enable earlier diagnosis, inform context-specific risk reduction strategies, and prevent avoidable complications.

As these components matured, a structured model began to emerge. A functional PD system can be conceptualised around four interconnected elements: (i) structured clinical pathways with standardised diagnosis and phenotyping; (ii) a basic data infrastructure, including registries and governance mechanisms; (iii) integration of research within routine practice; and (iv) a workforce model supporting training, supervision, and progressive independence. These elements developed iteratively. This integrated model became operational within two years of initiating a PD genetics research programme. In practice, this model has translated into the training of two additional movement disorders specialists and the establishment of dedicated clinics in the capital and the second-largest city in the south. A clinical and research network now spans seven sites, supported by a registry of >600 patients with PD and other parkinsonian disorders, extending specialised care to both urban and rural populations.

International engagement played a critical role throughout. In particular, involvement with the MDS provided access to training, mentorship, and professional networks that were instrumental in our early development and continued growth. Partnership with the GP2 enabled alignment with global standards. This required developing data governance frameworks, training pathways, shared protocols, and equitable authorship and data-sharing practices, often from the ground up. These efforts also contributed to activating the local community, mobilising resources, and strengthening patient-led initiatives.

At this stage, resource constraints became central to decision-making. Priorities were defined not by what was theoretically optimal, but by what could be sustained, embedding cost-consciousness into system design. This approach favoured scalable, high-impact components supported by simplified and standardised protocols. Integrating clinical care with research reduced duplication and increased the value of each patient interaction, while stepwise implementation allowed the system to expand alongside local capacity. However, further expansion remains constrained by continued reliance on research funding, with limited dedicated resources for service delivery. Although a foundational system is now in place, scaling access to care will require sustained investment and closer alignment between research funding and patient-centred service delivery within the national health system.

Training, mentorship, and networking support were also extended to colleagues in neighbouring Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, and Tajikistan. Guided by a bottom-up approach, these efforts supported local leadership in understanding and addressing their own clinical and research environments. Rather than transferring predefined models, this approach enabled context-specific adaptation and facilitated shared learning across the region, contributing to a collaborative network.

Taken together, these experiences suggest that limited specialist capacity can be leveraged to build broader platforms for care and research when workforce development, patient engagement, and data systems are developed in parallel. The future of PD research and care will depend not only on technological advances, but on the ability to design systems that are efficient, adaptable, and inclusive from the outset, with implications for other resource-limited settings.

Wei Luo, MD PhD and Zhidong Cen, MD PhD (Hangzhou, China)

When I (WL) came to Hangzhou in eastern China to start my career as a neurologist with a special interest in PD, the biggest challenge in the clinic was the large number of PD patients. Time was very limited, as I needed to cover almost 30 patients in an afternoon outpatient clinic (about 4h). China has the largest population of PD patients, with an estimated five million people with PD, accounting for half of the world's total. 37 Thus, the very limited consultation time per patient is the most challenging issue we face. To solve this issue, our group built an efficient workflow for the PD and parkinsonism outpatient clinic with pre-consultation preparation (e.g., brief history collection, current drugs information), junior clinician consultation and report, senior movement disorders specialist consultation, and finally health education with take-home paper materials. In this way, our group could provide efficient, high-quality clinical services, which received ongoing positive patient feedback, enabling us to follow up and manage ∼10,000 PD patients.

However, the increasing number of patients desiring our group's clinical services exhausts us, even though we have tried our best. Over the past decade, our group in Zhejiang province has worked systematically to build local clinical capacity for PD. Through sustained clinical training, mentorship, and education with a Disease-Specific, Multi-Method Integrated Teaching Approach (similar to the Ground Rounds in the MDS Congress), we have now deployed movement disorders specialists to all 11 prefectures of Zhejiang. A patient with PD in Lishui or Zhoushan (relatively rural regions in Zhejiang) no longer needs to travel four hours to Hangzhou for expert consultation. This reduces inequalities between the large PD population and the limited specialist-level clinical resources.

But Zhejiang province is not the whole of China. Visiting underprivileged regions in China – Guizhou, Yunnan, Xinjiang, and Gansu – opened our views to a different reality. In some county hospitals, clinicians have never received formal training in movement disorders. They cannot reliably distinguish PD from essential tremor, drug-induced parkinsonism, or even normal aging. In remote mountain villages, we met patients who had been bedridden for years, not because PD had progressed beyond help, but because no one had ever prescribed them levodopa. Once we arranged delivery of the medication, some of these patients were able to walk again within weeks.

The human toll in these underprivileged regions is immense: undiagnosed and untreated people confined to their homes, families exhausted and impoverished by caregiving, and communities unaware that effective symptomatic treatment exists. In response, we have constructed pragmatic bridges across this divide. We use telemedicine networks to connect specialists in Zhejiang to remote hospitals across western China. Virtual consultations now reach counties that have never had a neurologist. Working with hospital pharmacies and logistics companies, we ship levodopa and other essential drugs to patients who would otherwise go without. A box of medication traveling 3000 kilometers can restore a person's ability to walk, eat, and speak. We created open-access educational videos on platforms such as DXY.cn, the largest online medical community in China, to teach frontline clinicians in low-resource settings how to recognize PD and perform a basic neurological examination. Three videos38–40 have been viewed tens of thousands of times. Using WeChat, a convenient phone app, we provide brief consultations to local clinicians. These efforts help bring care to individuals who would otherwise receive none, but they are patchwork and not systemic solutions. They also remain largely invisible to the research community, to funders, and to the journals that celebrate discoveries.

In addition to the within-country inequalities, there are also inequalities between China and the world. To narrow the gap, at the personal level, I have kept up with global communications and learning over the past decades. For clinical direction, I visited the University College of London Queen Square Institute of Neurology (supervised by Prof. Anthony Schapira) and the Université Grenoble Alpes (supervised by Prof. Elena Moro) to enhance expertise in PD and movement disorders diagnosis, management, and DBS. For the research direction, I visited the University of Chicago and Northwestern University for translational medicine training and collaboration. Along with annual participation in the MDS Congress and the AOPMC, I can balance clinical and research issues in PD and movement disorders. Many global collaborations are ongoing between our group and top teams worldwide.41–43 We led the formation of the first international expert consensus on Primary Brain Calcification (a heterogeneous group of conditions with varied clinical presentations that can sometimes mimic PD), and it was an honor to be acknowledged by experts worldwide. 44 Finally, to translate the research findings into the clinic, I organized a randomized clinical trial of TJ0113, a first-in-class small-molecule mitophagy activator in PD (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06596005). Referred to as the "Silicon Valley of China", Hangzhou has natural advantages in AI and related technologies (e.g., DeepSeek). We are developing and deploying AI technologies and wearable devices, 45 along with multidisciplinary team formation, 46 genetic testing applications, and cohort database construction,47,48 to improve clinical practice in PD and related disorders.

I believe all the above efforts would help us reduce the inequalities in contributions to PD and movement disorders between China and the rest of the world. From October 2025, six months into my role as a member of the MDS-AOS Executive Committee, I have seen how organization, education, leadership, and advocacy can be marshalled to reduce disparities and inequality. At the same time, my colleague Dr Zhidong Cen serves on the MDS-AOS Education Committee, and together we are working to organize a China PD Alliance – a grassroots network of clinicians, researchers, and patient advocates committed to advancing education, improving medication access, and amplifying the voices of underprivileged regions. We are learning from the MDS how to structure training programs, disseminate guidelines, and build sustainable local leadership. In return, we believe China has much to contribute, from our experience with telemedicine at scale to understanding genetics in non-European populations. I hope to serve as a bridge, bringing lessons from global PD care back to China and bringing China's voice and contributions to the table.

Shen-Yang Lim, MD FRACP (Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia)

I returned to Malaysia in 2009 after training in neurology and movement disorders in Melbourne and Toronto. An initial foray into “breaking barriers” for PD care was when I learned that patients were being rejected for “disabled person” status. This was a significant issue, since having a disability card enables free access to many aspects of healthcare in hospitals like mine (a public university-affiliated hospital), including medications like levodopa, entacapone, piribedil, selegiline, trihexyphenidyl, and laxatives. It also exempts the patient from public-clinic consultation fees, as well as a large proportion of hospitalization costs. I therefore prioritized having this anomalous situation rectified, and with some luck (and help from a journalist-cum-disability advocate) managed to engage with the Minister for welfare and social services. Armed with information, including videos, regarding PD, I explained the myriad of problems faced by patients and argued that they should - like any other citizen - qualify for disability status, should their functional status justify this. Thankfully, the Minister and her staff were very understanding and soon thereafter patients were able to access this important form of assistance. This was actually not such a difficult thing to do, but required the doctor to step out of the conventional “one-on-one” role.

Around the same time, I also quickly worked with the Malaysian PD Association (MPDA) and neurology and physiotherapist colleagues to produce a handbook of practical information on PD for patients and their caregivers, as there was a significant need for basic but accurate information. We published this in the 3 main local languages (English, Malay, and Chinese), pdf versions of which were also posted online (https://mpda.org.my) (all available free-of-charge).

Another area that was (and continues to be) in great need was the development of advanced treatment services for patients with troublesome motor response complications (MRC). As expected in a quaternary centre, we have many referred patients with complicated presentations. 49 I teamed up with a functional neurosurgeon (Prof. Kalai Muthusamy) who had also recently completed his training overseas, and utilizing the skills we gleaned from our teachers (Profs. Tipu Aziz and Elena Moro), we ran a DBS service which has provided treatment to ∼280 (mostly PD) patients. 50 One of the major limitations has been the cost of the DBS devices, 6 although with the advent of very long-lasting rechargeable batteries, and with negotiation with the device companies, the cost-effectiveness of the procedure is increasingly acceptable to patients/families. Nevertheless, as we highlighted recently, the vast majority of DBS is paid for out-of-pocket (or by private insurance), with only a small fraction (<20%) funded by government. 50 Continuous subcutaneous infusions of dopaminergic medications are even more out-of-reach, being prohibitively expensive and therefore accessible to only a handful of patients in Malaysia. This is also the situation in most developing (and even some developed) countries. 6 Thus, there is an urgent need for neurologists, patient support communities, industry, and governments, to work towards closing this gap. I believe it is not an exaggeration to say that the lack of these treatments for a large swathe of patients suffering from MRC is also, in a way, a “humanitarian crisis” that has been overlooked for too long.2,51

To promote greater awareness of these and other treatments (pharmacological and non-pharmacological), I led the writing of the Malaysian Consensus Guidelines for the Treatment of PD in 2012, on behalf of the nascent Malaysian Movement Disorders Council (MDC), which I co-founded with colleagues such as Prof. Norlinah Mohd Ibrahim. Our hope was that evidence-based practices would maximize treatment efficacy, and minimize adverse outcomes and also financial wastage (e.g., prescribing entacapone in early PD),52,53 particularly in resource-constrained settings. The MDC (and the collegiality of its members) has helped to promote movement disorders as a popular specialty among neurology trainees, and Malaysia now has ∼20 specialists with an interest in this area.

The voices of patients and caregivers are obviously crucial in medical decision-making (and, indeed, all our clinical and research endeavours), and my colleagues and I have discussed these personalized treatment approaches in detail previously. 2 Unfortunately, in our local setting, there has not yet been a pathway for nurses to become specialized as PD nurses or nurse practitioners. So, Prof. Ai Huey Tan (the other movement disorders neurologist at the University of Malaya [UM]) and I do our best to make ourselves accessible to patients by providing all our PD patients with our email contact. In this way, problems such as psychosis can be “nipped in the bud”, much preferably on an outpatient basis, before they become florid and require hospitalization, which can often lead to further complications. 2

It has been a true privilege to be involved within the MDS over almost 2 decades. This professional society has been a second family, providing wonderful opportunities for collaboration and sharing.54–56 The MDS supports a very large number of educational and training programs (https://www.movementdisorders.org), which have enabled and enhanced the training of PD specialists, including in countries and regions (e.g., Central Asia and West Asia/Middle East) where none or very little existed previously.

Another international organization is GP2 (https://gp2.org), which since its inception just 6 years ago has made major strides in improving access to genetic testing and analysis, and ramping up local capacity-building especially in underrepresented populations (URPs). The important ways in which genetics knowledge contributes to patient care (diagnosis, prognostication, treatment, family planning, etc.) have been outlined in a recent Perspective involving GP2 member-contributors, 41 and has much relevance for patients in underserved settings, including indigenous communities.57–60 In all these efforts, we also should not forget the “Parkinson-plus” disorders, some of which appear to have high prevalence in our settings, e.g., progressive supranuclear palsy among South Asians.61,62 Research collaborations in other spheres have also allowed us to obtain potentially actionable testing (e.g., unavailable auto-antibodies) free-of-charge. 63 We find that our patients are usually happy to participate in research, which often gives them added purpose or meaning. 64 Giving back to patients/families in terms of sharing their (individual- and group-level) findings is often very well received, and incentivizes continued participation in further research,65–67 which is a major pillar to improving care and breaking barriers, since research and patient care are intertwined. 13

Priya Jagota, MD (Bangkok, Thailand)

I started my journey breaking barriers for PD care working with the patient support group at our Center in Chulalongkorn. While I was pursuing my movement disorders fellowship, a young-onset PD patient was eager to start a support group. Along with my mentor, Prof. Roongroj Bhidayasiri, an informal group was formed. Upon finishing my fellowship in 2010, I was put in charge of it. The group was patient-led and very active. I supported them in their activities and guided them through. The group expanded and became very apt at what they were doing. Annual educational courses were organized for the patients and caregivers. After a few years, I realized that the patients and caregivers at our center were already quite knowledgeable. It was time to expand to the general public. However, if public education courses were to be held, the knowledge gained would be limited to the attendees, whereas education through media would capture a wide array of audiences, even those not intentionally seeking knowledge about PD. We had a few educational videos on PD already, but they did not capture a very wide audience. Therefore, we sought professionals to help us. We contacted our University's Communication Arts Alumni Association. Thus, a collaboration to create a short, highly impactful video was started. 68 They advised, created and planned the whole campaign for us. They contacted many celebrities, advertising agencies, and television channels to show the video free of charge. Within 24 h of the launch, there were already 1 million views in various Facebook pages combined. Hence, we were able to achieve the goal of widespread dissemination of PD awareness and knowledge to the public, at minimal cost.

Over the years, recognizing the challenges and burden that caregivers face, I have also attempted to form a PD caregiver group. However, interest from the caregivers were minimal as they were already very occupied and did not have “extra” time. Nevertheless, one caregiver with many years of experience volunteered to take on a listening ear where caregivers could talk to her about their problems.

I also had a great interest in the neuropsychiatry of PD. As it happens, our two fields “collide” with each other in the sense that PD medications can increase neuropsychiatric symptoms and anti-psychiatric medications in turn can aggravate PD symptoms.52,69 Advanced patients had to go back and forth between the two clinics to get proper treatment. Therefore, I collaborated with a geriatric psychiatrist with interest in PD, and together, we opened a PD neuropsychiatry clinic where both of us see the patients together once a month. It helped us discuss the treatment right there and then, which avoided delays and reduced adverse events.

I was also fortunate to work with the MDS. When I joined the Executive Committee of the MDS-AOS, I sensed that most of the activities were being done by some of the very active countries, and those were the countries that would come up with educational and other events each year. There was a gap of some countries not having any activities. Perhaps, they needed support. So, when Prof. Raymond Rosales mentioned his intention of working with the “underserved” countries in 2022, I jumped upon the opportunity. Together, we formed the Tending the Underserved Ad Hoc Committee where several countries in the AOS were included. We discussed their challenges in PD care, resources, and the support that we could offer. Together, the committee was able to increase awareness and education of PD and other movement disorders among health professionals and patients in these countries, improve access to treatment wherever we could, and reimport levodopa into Laos where it had been discontinued. In 2023, as part of Prof. Victor Fung's main goals during his Presidency of the MDS to reduce inequality in PD care, the Committee on Equality of Access to Care was formed. I was given the opportunity to Co-Chair this with Prof. Njideka Okubadejo from the African Section. Though in its early stages, the committee has been working together to break barriers and improve the lives of patients in different corners of the globe.

Even though I feel very fortunate to be given so many opportunities to work with the patients directly and indirectly, an important lesson that I have learnt is the power of one person - sometimes all it takes is just an initiative and dedication from one person, and then the work just rolls on, whether it is a patient, a caregiver, or a doctor (as with all the authors in this article).

Frandy Susatia, MD (Jakarta, Indonesia)

After completing my movement disorders Fellowship at the University of Florida in 2009, I returned to Indonesia to begin practice as a movement disorders specialist at Siloam Hospital Kebon Jeruk. Starting as the sole specialist in this field, I quickly realized that there was no dedicated access for patients to receive comprehensive movement disorders care.

In 2011, I created the hospital's first dedicated movement disorders clinic to support people living with PD and those requiring botulinum toxin injections. Building on this foundation, we launched the Siloam Hospital Kebon Jeruk Movement Disorder Center in 2016 and performed our first DBS procedure for a PD patient, with invaluable guidance and collaboration from Profs. Michael Okun, Kelly Foote, Peter Silburn, and Terry Coyne.

Over the course of my clinical practice, I became increasingly aware of the many barriers faced by people living with PD, highlighting the need for stronger collaboration and a broader professional network. This led me to actively contribute to the Movement Disorder Study Group within the Indonesian Neurological Association and MDS-AOS. In 2023, I helped to establish the Movement Disorder Society of Indonesia (MDSIDN), which was officially recognized as an Affiliate Society of the MDS-AOS.

By 2025, Indonesia's population was ∼280 million, including an estimated 200,000–400,000 people living with PD. 70 Through my analysis of PD management in Indonesia, several recurring barriers emerged - each playing a significant role in limiting effective care.

The first obstacle is access and geography. Indonesia's vast archipelagic landscape - spanning more than 17,000 islands - creates large disparities in healthcare access. Healthcare in Indonesia is concentrated in urban centers like Jakarta and Java, forcing many patients from remote areas to travel long distances for care. As a result, they often arrive at advanced stages. This represents a missed window of opportunity for early intervention, when treatment is most effective in preserving function and quality of life.

The second obstacle is blind spots in knowledge. Significant gaps in understanding of PD persist among healthcare professionals. These lead to under-recognition, misdiagnosis, and suboptimal care, with early symptoms often mistaken for aging, psychiatric condition or musculoskeletal disorders.

The third obstacle relates to cultural misconceptions and stigma. PD is often seen as a curse, hereditary, contagious, or an untreatable part of aging. This delays care and leads many patients to seek help only at advanced stages, having endured years of misunderstanding, fear, or social isolation.

The final major obstacle are the economic barriers to care. The cost of PD care is high relative to regional wages. While national health insurance helps cover key medications, major gaps persist in access to allied health therapies due to limited services, workforce shortages, and high costs. Newer treatments and DBS that could significantly transform patients’ lives remain largely inaccessible because of reimbursement issues.

We explored ways to work around some of these obstacles. We adopted telemedicine using Zoom and WhatsApp across Siloam Hospitals’ nationwide network (41 hospitals, 25 clinics) to connect neurologists, GPs, nurses, patients, and caregivers. Lower-cost teleconsultations reduce travel and have become a lifeline - expanding specialist access for remote patients while supporting local providers in managing complex cases.

We realized early on that improving access to care alone was not enough - we also needed to strengthen understanding. So, we turned to platforms people already use daily: Instagram (#pokja.gangguangerak.perdosni #perduliparkinson), YouTube (@PerduliParkinson), TikTok (perduliparkinson), and Facebook (perduliparkinson) to share short, low-bandwidth videos designed to reach even those with limited internet connectivity. These bite-sized pieces of content made by our neurologists render complex medical information easier to grasp and more widely accessible. At the same time, we bring learning into communities through seminars that deliver clear, evidence-based knowledge directly to patients, families, and healthcare providers. One important strategy was to place caregivers at the center of our efforts. We developed “Caregiver Toolkits” - simple, printed booklets of highly visual guides in Bahasa Indonesia that walk through medication management and help identify early signs of side effects such as dyskinesia. When caregivers understand not just what to do, but why it matters, adherence improves significantly. Education becomes more than instruction; it becomes a shared experience. In doing so, we close the gap between medical knowledge and everyday life - not just teaching about a disease, but giving families the tools, language, and confidence to take back control of their daily routines.

While it is clear that there are many challenges yet to overcome, it is my hope that by steadily working together with colleagues, patients and families, Indonesians living with PD can have a fair chance to access the care, resources, and support they need to live with dignity and hope.

Conclusions

We hope readers find these eclectic stories interesting and practical/applicable, since rather than speaking in purely abstract terms, they give real-world examples of what can and has been done globally to improve care for PD patients. The experiences presented here illustrate that meaningful progress is achievable through pragmatic solutions, collaborative networks, and sustained commitment to patient-centered care.
