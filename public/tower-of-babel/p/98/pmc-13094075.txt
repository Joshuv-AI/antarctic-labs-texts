Assessing T-cell responses to Epstein-Barr virus in individuals at risk for nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: Wan-Lun Hsu, Chieh-Ju Lee, Anna E Coghill, Zhiwei Liu, Kelly J Yu, Julia Simon, Allan Hildesheim, Tim Waterboer, Graham S Taylor, Jay A Berzofsky, Cheng-Ping Wang, Yi-Hsiang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElevated Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) antibody levels are associated with risk of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) and risk scores based on these antibodies can predict NPC risk with high accuracy. The immune mechanisms underlying this association remain unclear. We evaluated whether individuals with elevated EBV-NPC antibody risk scores had a reduced T-cell response to EBV proteins within a NPC multiplex family study in Taiwan.MethodsIn total, 79 cancer-free individuals from families with a history of NPC had blood tested for (i) EBV antibodies using a multiplex serology assay and (ii) T-cell responses to 7 EBV proteins (EBNA1, LMP1, LMP2, BZLF1, BRLF1, BARF1, gp350) using interferon-γ ELISPOT.ResultsExcluding individuals with low phytohemagglutinin proliferative responses, no statistically significant association was observed between lower EBV T-cell responses and higher EBV antibody-based NPC risk scores (P = 0.21). However, our findings did trend in the a-priori hypothesized direction, with lower EBV T-cell responses noted among those with higher EBV antibody-based NPC risk scores. Compared to individuals who responded to at least 3 EBV-specific T-cell peptide pools by ELISPOT, those who responded to 1–2 peptide pools had a 10% increased risk of having an EBV-NPC risk score above the median value (OR = 1.1; 95% CI = 0.3, 4.5) and those who responded to none had a 3-fold increased risk (OR = 3.0; 95% CI = 0.6, 17).ConclusionsOur study is the first to evaluate whether high EBV antibody responses observed among individuals at risk of NPC are associated with reduced T-cell responses to EBV. Our findings suggest such an association but require replication in larger studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13027-026-00743-w.Keywords: Epstein-Barr virus, Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Immune response

Introduction

Epstein Barr virus (EBV) is a necessary cause of undifferentiated nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) [1]. While EBV infection is ubiquitous and establishes lifelong infection, the virus typically remains latent in B-cells and only periodically reactivates leading to viral production and shedding in the pharyngeal spaces [2]. It has been shown that individuals with elevated levels of antibodies against EBV have elevated risk of NPC [1]. In fact, EBV antibody-based NPC risk scores (referred to henceforth as EBV-NPC risk score) have been developed that predict NPC with high accuracy [3].

While increased levels of antibodies against EBV are associated with NPC risk, the immune mechanisms underlying the association between elevated EBV antibodies and NPC risk have not been defined. It has been hypothesized that a high EBV-NPC risk score marks individuals with increased viral exposure at the nasopharynx due to more frequent lytic virus reactivation. T-cell responses are important in the immunological control of EBV [4], so we hypothesized that those with strong EBV T-cell responses would have less frequent lytic viral reactivation and/or lytic reactivation of shorter duration than do individuals with less robust EBV T-cell responses. If so, one would expect individuals with stronger T-cell responses to have lower EBV exposures (i.e., lower EBV-NPC risk scores) compared to those with weaker EBV-specific T-cell responses.

To test this hypothesis, we examined the association between T-cell responses to EBV and EBV-NPC risk scores in a group of individuals from multiplex families predisposed to NPC.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was nested within a larger NPC Multiplex Family Study (NPCFS) consisting of over 3,000 individuals from 358 NPC multiplex families who had two or more family members diagnosed with NPC recruited between 1995 and 2005 and followed through 2020, as previously described [5]. From within this cohort, 151 unaffected individuals (i.e. without an NPC diagnosis) were selected and agreed to participate in an additional study visit during which blood (> 50 ml) was collected and shipped fresh to the testing laboratory for EBV antibody and T-cell response characterization (see below) between March 18, 2015, and September 30, 2017. We prioritized inclusion of individuals from families with a larger number of NPC cases. Of the 151 individuals who agreed to the study, 79 were sampled before the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic necessitated that we stop field activities. 49.4% of these individuals were members of families with 3 + members affected by NPC. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Taiwan University Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Multiplex antibody testing and EBV-NPC risk score

Serum samples were subject to EBV antibody testing at the DKFZ laboratory in Heidelberg using a Luminex-bead based multiplex serology assay targeting 6 IgG and 7 IgA antibodies against 11 EBV antigens (VCAp18, EBNA1, BMRF1 [EA-D], BPLF1, BXLF1, LF2, BZLF1, BORF1, BFRF1, BGLF2, and BRLF1) previously shown to be strongly associated with NPC risk and whose combined score has been shown to be highly predictive of NPC [6]. IgA and IgG antibodies were assessed in separate assays, using different secondary antibodies and tested at different serum dilutions (1:10,000 for IgG and 1:100 for IgA), as previously described [7]. For quality control, 26 sera were tested blindly in duplicate. Resultant intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) from log10-transformed data for the 13 antibodies ranged from 0.755 to 0.996 (Median = 0.96). The percent agreement for the dichotomized EBV-antibody score used in our analysis (described below) was 73% for these blinded duplicates.

To generate a composite EBV-NPC risk score using the multiplex serology results, we utilized the following model, previously shown to be highly predictive of NPC risk: Composite antibody score = 1.6361×VCAp18-IgA + 1.5281×EBNA1(truncated)-IgA + 1.1913×BXLF1-IgA – 1.3314×BRLF1-IgA + 2.6507×LF2-IgA + 2.7506×BMRF1-IgA + 0.5645×BPLF1-IgA – 1.5870×BZLF1-IgG + 1.3984×BORF1-IgG + 0.1354×BFRF1-IgG + 3.9171×BGLF2-IgG – 0.3760×BXLF1-IgG + 2.7950×LF2-IgG6. For the analysis, individuals whose score was above the median of 2.21 were considered to be at higher risk of NPC; those with scores below this median were considered lower risk.

ELISPOT testing for EBV-specific T-cells

Whole blood was shipped to the testing laboratory at the Veterans General Hospital in Taipei at controlled room temperature to the testing laboratory, where PBMCs were isolated within 5-hours (median 2-hours) using standard ficoll-hypaque density gradient centrifugation and frozen for subsequent experiments). T-cell responses to EBV were measured by interferon-γ ELISPOT using previously published methods [8]. The assays targeted T-cell responses to 7 EBV proteins: a protein necessary for EBV maintenance in B-cells (EBNA1), three known/suspected oncogenes (LMP1, LMP2, BARF1), two proteins involved in lytic reactivation (BZLF1, BRLF1), and a protein important for viral entry into epithelial cells (gp350). Overlapping peptide pools (15-mers, overlapping 11aa; JPT Innovative Peptide Solutions, Berlin) for each of these proteins were used. Given its size, EBNA1 was split into 3 pools representing the N-terminus, mid-section, and C-terminus of the protein. All other proteins were tested in a single pool, leading to a total of 9 pools tested. Testing was performed in triplicate wells containing 200,000 cells per well and results were read using an automated counter (Cellometer Auto T4 Plus). Phytohemagglutinin (PHA) was used as a positive control stimulus. Individuals (N = 24) whose response to PHA was lower than 100 were excluded from analysis. The distribution of key characteristics of individuals included in the analysis and those selected but not included in the analysis were comparable (Supplementary Table 1). The frequency of antigen-specific IFN-γ-secreting cells was determined for each well and averaged across the 3 triplicate wells. Final T-cell response frequencies were obtained by taking the average frequency of the triplicate wells minus the average of triplicate DMSO-only wells (i.e., negative control wells containing no peptides) included on each plate. T-cell response to a given peptide pool were considered positive if the final frequency was higher than 20. To provide a global summary of T-cell response to EBV for each participant, we created a T-cell score (range 0–9) representing the sum of the number of positive pools observed for each individual (Supplementary Table 2).

Statistical analysis

To understand the association between EBV-specific T-cell responses and the EBV-NPC risk score, we estimated the odds ratios (ORs) for the association between T-cell response score (expressed as a three-level categorical variable; 0, 1–2, 3–9) and EBV-NPC risk score (low versus high using median level as cutoff) using logistic regression models adjusted for sex, age at blood collection, and smoking history (never, ever, and unknown) because previous reports show that these factors are associated with EBV antibody levels [9, 10]. When evaluating the association between T-cell responses to individual EBV proteins and EBV antibody-based NPC risk score (low vs. high), the chi-square or fisher exact test was used and the rate ratio (RR) was used to estimate the relative difference between groups. Statistical tests were based on the 55 individuals included in the analysis after exclusion of individuals with a low PHA response.

Results

We observed an inverse association between the EBV-NPC risk score and EBV T-cell responses (Table 1), although this association was not statistically significant at the alpha = 0.05 level. Among individuals who did not respond to any of the EBV peptide pools in the ELISPOT assay, 67% had EBV antibody-based NPC risk scores above the median. This was compared to 45% and 43% for individuals who responded to 1–2 or 3 + EBV peptide pools in the ELISPOT assay, respectively. In adjusted models, compared to individuals who responded to 3 + peptide pools, those who responded to 1–2 or none of the peptide pools had greater likelihood of having a higher EBV-NPC risk score of 10% (OR = 1.1; 95% CI = 0.3, 4.5) and three-fold (OR = 3.0; 95% CI = 0.6, 17), respectively. The dose–response relationship between increased EBV-specific T-cell responses and a higher NPC risk score was not statistically significant (P = 0.21).

Discussion

It is well established that individuals with elevated levels of antibodies targeting EBV are at increased risk of developing NPC, a cancer caused by EBV infection [1]. Given that EBV infection is ubiquitous and establishes lifelong, latent infection in B cells, one plausible explanation for this observation is that the lack of an effective T-cell response to EBV leads some individuals to have more frequent and/or durable EBV reactivation and shedding of EBV in mucosal surfaces. This, in turn, would lead to elevated antibody levels and increased risk of NPC. Consistent with this model, recent studies have linked EBV lytic reactivation and epithelial infection with oncogenic risk in NPC [11]. Multiplex serological analyses indicate that antibodies targeting EBV antigens involved in viral reactivation, such as BALF2, Zta (BZLF1), and BNLF2b, are elevated in NPC patients and show distinct associations with disease initiation and progression. The findings support the importance of repeated or dysregulated EBV reactivation at epithelial sites in NPC pathogenesis.

Studying the link between T-cell responses, antibody levels, and NPC among NPC patients has been difficult for two main reasons. Firstly, studies of cancer patients cannot distinguish between phenomenon that caused the cancer versus those that result from the cancer itself. Secondly, there are no easily identifiable NPC precursor states in which the relationship between T-cell and antibody responses to EBV can be evaluated. Recently, NPC risk prediction models have been developed and validated. These models use EBV antibody response patterns to define NPC risk and have been implemented for NPC screening in some NPC high-risk regions [3]. The ability to stratify otherwise healthy individuals without NPC into those at high versus low risk of future NPC development using an EBV-NPC risk score now allows for the assessment of whether individuals predisposed to NPC have reduced T-cell responses targeting EBV that might explain the relationship between elevated EBV antibodies and future NPC risk.

Herein, we report results from the first study to evaluate the relationship between T-cell responses to EBV and EBV-based NPC risk scores. Using IFN-γ ELISPOT assays to measure T-cell responses to 7 EBV proteins selected for their role in EBV maintenance, reactivation, oncogenicity, or viral infectivity, we did not observe a significant association between T-cell responses to EBV and NPC risk score. However, the observed point estimates did trend in the a-priori hypothesized direction, with a negative correlation noted between T-cell responses to EBV and NPC risk score. Individuals with no detectable responses to the 7 EBV proteins evaluated were found to be nearly three times more likely than those who responded to three of more EBV peptide pools to have an elevated NPC risk based on the EBV-NPC risk score. Individuals who responded to one or two EBV peptide pools were equally likely to have elevated EBV-NPC risk scores, compared to those with responses to 3 + pools. If future studies replicate these finding it would suggest that responses to a limited number of EBV epitopes suffices for protection. While trending in the hypothesized direction, our findings were not statistically significant at the 0.05 alpha level. This could well be due to the fact that our study (originally targeting 150 individuals) had to be stopped early due to the SARS-CoV2 pandemic, reducing the effective study population to 79, of which 55 were included in the analysis after exclusion of low responders to PHA.

When T-cell responses to individual protein antigen pools was considered, we noted that responses to most EBV proteins evaluated were comparable among individuals with low and high EBV-NPC risk scores. A suggestive association was observed, however, for gp350 T-cell responses, raising the possibility (if replicated in future studies) that T-cell responses targeting EBV glycoproteins are a good marker of an effective immune response to EBV and that, perhaps, T-cells targeting epitopes on full virions are particularly effective at controlling EBV infection and avoiding/limiting EBV reactivation at mucosal sites.

Our study has the unique feature of having been conducted within a population enriched for individuals predisposed to NPC, namely members of NPC multiplex families in a high-risk geographical location. Such individuals have elevated risk of developing NPC [1] and so would be most likely to show the predicted pattern of elevated EBV-specific antibodies and diminished EBV-specific T-cell immunity. We further enriched our population by prioritizing participation of individuals from NPC multiplex families with 3 + NPC cases. Our study also has limitations. In addition to the modest sample size discussed above, the inability to distinguish whether observed T-cell responses were driven by T-helper or T-cytotoxic cells limits full interpretation of the immune mechanisms underlying the observed antibody responses. Emerging evidence suggests that EBV-specific T cells may exhibit functional impairment despite preserved cell numbers, potentially accompanied by exhaustion-associated phenotypes involving inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, TIM-3, and TIGIT [12–15]. This concept is further supported by clinical observations in EBV-associated post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder, where EBV-specific T cells demonstrate immune exhaustion and functional abnormalities compared with controls [16]. Without direct assessment of broader functional outputs and exhaustion-related features beyond IFN-γ production alone, it remains difficult to determine whether the observed EBV-specific immune responses reflect quantitatively intact but qualitatively dysfunctional T-cell immunity. An additional limitation is the relatively high exclusion rate due to low mitogen (PHA) responsiveness. While exclusion of low PHA responders was necessary to ensure the technical validity of antigen-specific ELISPOT measurements, reduced mitogen responsiveness may itself reflect underlying immune heterogeneity within this high-risk population. It is therefore possible that individuals excluded from analysis represent a biologically distinct subgroup with broader immune hyporesponsiveness, which could bias estimates toward the null. However, sensitivity analyses including individuals with low PHA responses yielded similar overall patterns, suggesting that the primary findings are robust to this exclusion criterion (data not shown). This question could be addressed in future studies incorporating higher-resolution immunophenotyping approaches, such as multiparameter flow cytometry, which would allow discrimination of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell subsets and assessment of functional and exhaustion-related markers. In addition, integration of single-cell transcriptomic profiling and T-cell receptor repertoire analysis could further clarify the phenotypic heterogeneity, functional states, and clonal dynamics of EBV-specific T-cell responses. Beyond these technical considerations, emerging evidence suggests that EBV infection can induce tissue-resident memory CD8⁺ T cells at mucosal lymphoid sites relevant to primary EBV infection, including nasopharyngeal and tonsillar tissues [17]. These local immune populations may exhibit functional characteristics that differ from those of circulating T-cell subsets and therefore may not be fully captured by peripheral blood–based immune assays. As a result, analyses restricted to peripheral blood may provide an incomplete representation of EBV-specific immune control at epithelial sites relevant to NPC development.

In summary, while not statistically significant, our results suggest that individuals at high risk of NPC (i.e., above average EBV-NPC risk scores) might be more likely to have a lower EBV-specific T-cell responses. This, in turn, suggests that naturally occurring EBV antibodies in adults with EBV infection marks poor EBV control, supporting the notion that repeated and/or long-lasting reactivation of EBV is important for the development of NPC. Larger studies are needed to confirm these initial findings.
