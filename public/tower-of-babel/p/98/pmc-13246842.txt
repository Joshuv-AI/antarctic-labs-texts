Predictors of tumor conspicuity on Kupffer-phase Sonazoid-enhanced ultrasonography in liver tumors: implications for fusion-guided ablation
Authors: Dae Woong Kim, Jun Hyung Hong, Eun Ju Yoon, Tae-Hoon Kim, Hyung Joong Kim, Young Hoe Hur, Dakyum Shin, Hyun Chul Kim, Sang Gook Song, Jin Woong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the utility of the Kupffer phase in Sonazoid-enhanced ultrasonography (SZUS) for liver tumor ablation and to identify factors influencing tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase.MethodsEighty-one patients with 128 liver tumors (95 hepatocellular carcinomas [HCCs] and 33 non-HCCs) who underwent gadoxetic acid–enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), B-mode planning ultrasonography (B-US), and SZUS-guided radiofrequency ablation (RFA) were included. Two radiologists scored tumor conspicuity on the hepatobiliary phase (HBP) of MRI, B-US, and the Kupffer phase using a 4-point scale. Tumor echogenicity on B-US was classified as hypoechoic or non-hypoechoic. This study analyzed tumor conspicuity across modalities, correlations between modalities, RFA technical success rates, and predictors of high Kupffer phase conspicuity.ResultsThe mean tumor diameter was 14.2±7.0 mm, with no differences in size or location between HCCs and non-HCCs. Conspicuity scores on the Kupffer phase were significantly higher than those on B-US for both HCCs (2.87±0.85 vs. 1.79±0.71) and non-HCCs (3.06±0.79 vs. 2.00±0.75) (both P<0.001). The technical success rate of RFA under Kupffer phase guidance was 97.7%. Tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase showed a moderate correlation with that on HBP (r=0.436, P<0.001), and this correlation was stronger in non-HCCs (r=0.630, P<0.001). Multivariable analysis identified B-US hypoechogenicity (odds ratio [OR], 1.424; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.176 to 1.724; P<0.001) and good HBP conspicuity (OR, 1.317; 95% CI, 1.087 to 1.596; P=0.005) as independent predictors of good Kupffer phase conspicuity.ConclusionB-US hypoechogenicity and good HBP conspicuity are independent predictors of favorable tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase. Identifying these features may facilitate optimal patient selection for effective SZUS-guided RFA of tumors that are inconspicuous on B-US.Keywords: Liver tumors, Kupffer phase, Sonazoid-enhanced ultrasonography, Hepatobiliary phase, Radiofrequency ablation

Introduction

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is a standard treatment for small hepatic malignancies, such as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and hepatic metastases, because of its established safety and favorable clinical efficacy [1–3]. Recent guidelines from the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) recommend both RFA and surgical resection as primary therapies for early-stage HCC [4]. Ultrasonography (US) is the preferred modality for guiding hepatic interventions, including RFA, because it provides real-time imaging, does not involve ionizing radiation, is widely accessible, and is cost-effective [5]. Before ablation, planning US is essential for assessing technical feasibility and planning the electrode path, device selection, and ablation overlap [5,6]. However, poor tumor visibility on B-mode US precludes RFA in 25%–45% of HCC cases, particularly for small subphrenic lesions in cirrhotic livers [5–7]. Key factors contributing to tumor invisibility include small size, subphrenic location, and liver cirrhosis, particularly the macronodular type [6,7]. Fusion of US with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) improves lesion detection and procedural outcomes. Nonetheless, up to 68% of deep or very small lesions remain difficult to identify even with fusion guidance [8–10].

To mitigate these difficulties, fusion imaging and contrast-enhanced US have been used simultaneously to guide RFA of HCCs [11,12]. Perfluorobutane (Sonazoid, GE Healthcare, Oslo, Norway), a second-generation microbubble contrast agent, provides both vascular and Kupffer phases [13]. During the Kupffer phase, uptake by Kupffer cells enhances the liver parenchyma. Because tumors lack Kupffer cells, they appear as distinct defects [14,15]. This phase has demonstrated high sensitivity for detecting hepatic tumors and improves tumor conspicuity, thereby facilitating interventional procedures [16–19].

Nevertheless, the routine use of the Kupffer phase in Sonazoid-enhanced US (SZUS) for liver ablation remains limited by several factors, including additional cost, unpredictable improvement in tumor conspicuity, and the required scan delay. Predicting which tumors will be well visualized on the Kupffer phase could optimize contrast agent use, reduce unnecessary expenditure, and improve ablation success. Therefore, this retrospective study aimed to assess the added value of the Kupffer phase in SZUS for guiding local liver tumor ablation and to identify factors associated with tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase.

Materials and Methods

Compliance with Ethical Standards

The Institutional Review Board of Chosun University Hospital (No. 2025-08-014-001) approved this retrospective analysis and waived the requirement for informed consent. This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients

From January 2020 to July 2025, 98 patients with 152 liver tumors who underwent SZUS/gadoxetic acid–enhanced MRI (GA-MRI) fusion–guided RFA were retrospectively identified. The interval between SZUS and GA-MRI was less than 6 weeks. Seventeen patients (24 tumors) were excluded for the following reasons: (1) SZUS was performed only during the vascular phase without a subsequent Kupffer phase; and (2) combined transarterial chemoembolization and RFA were performed. Consequently, 81 patients with 128 tumors were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Tumor diagnosis was established by imaging (CT or MRI) in 120 tumors and by percutaneous biopsy in 8 tumors.

GA-MRI Protocol

Patients underwent imaging on 3-T MRI systems (Magnetom Skyra, Vida, Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany) using a routine liver protocol that included breath-hold axial and coronal half-Fourier T2-weighted single-shot imaging, axial fat-suppressed T2-weighted imaging, in-phase and opposed-phase T1-weighted imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging with apparent diffusion coefficient maps, and T1-weighted fat-suppressed gradient-echo sequences acquired before and after contrast administration with subtraction images. Gadoxetic acid (Primovist, Bayer Healthcare, Berlin, Germany) was injected intravenously at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg at a rate of 1.5 mL/s. After contrast administration, precontrast, arterial-phase, portal venous-phase, transitional-phase, and hepatobiliary-phase (HBP) images were obtained at 0, 20, 60, and 180 seconds and 20 minutes, respectively.

Planning US

One day before RFA, planning US was performed by an abdominal radiologist with 20 years of experience. The procedure was performed using a US machine (LOGIQ E10, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA) equipped with fusion imaging (Volume Navigation, GE Healthcare) to synchronize B-mode US with preexisting GA-MRI scans [20]. Tumors were localized using B-mode planning US (B-US) synchronized with real-time MRI fusion. HBP images facilitated lesion identification and anatomic landmark recognition by providing superior lesion-to-liver contrast [21]. During planning US, both tumor conspicuity and the technical feasibility of percutaneous RFA were evaluated [22].

SZUS/GA-MRI Fusion–Guided RFA

When B-US failed to clearly localize a tumor, contrast-enhanced US-MRI fusion imaging with Sonazoid was used [23]. A 2-mL bolus of reconstituted Sonazoid was injected intravenously, followed by a 10-mL saline flush. The Kupffer phase was acquired 20–30 minutes after injection using a convex probe on the LOGIQ E10 machine with a mechanical index of 0.18–0.21. Ablation procedures were performed under real-time SZUS/MRI fusion guidance. A 480-kHz three-channel radiofrequency generator (VIVA multi RF Generator, STARmed, Goyang, Korea) connected to either a 15-gauge or 17-gauge separable clustered electrode (Octopus, STARmed) was used. The ablation was performed by a dedicated specialist with 20 years of experience in RFA. For tumors abutting the diaphragm or adjacent organs, artificial ascites was instilled [24,25]. Completion of the procedure was determined when echogenic bubbles on fusion US-MRI indicated complete coverage of the tumor with an ablative margin greater than 5 mm [26]. During withdrawal, the electrode tract was thermally ablated after completion of RFA.

Image Analysis

Two abdominal radiologists, each with more than 10 years of experience, reviewed the B-US, Kupffer-phase SZUS, and HBP GA-MRI images using a picture archiving and communication system (INFINITT PACS, Infinitt Healthcare, Seoul, Korea), and all imaging assessments were determined by consensus. To minimize recall bias, B-US, Kupffer-phase SZUS, and HBP GA-MRI images were evaluated at 1-week intervals in chronological order. Tumor echogenicity on B-US was classified using a 4-point scale as follows: 1, hyperechoic; 2, isoechoic; 3, hypoechoic; and 4, mixed echoic (hyperechoic and hypoechoic). Scores of 1 and 2 were categorized as the non-hypoechoic group, whereas scores of 3 and 4 were categorized as the hypoechoic group. Tumor conspicuity on B-US, Kupffer-phase SZUS, and HBP GA-MRI was also classified using a 4-point scale: 1, invisible; 2, subtle localization of the tumor based on a difference in echogenicity or signal intensity but with indistinct margins; 3, localization with partial delineation of the margins; and 4, confident localization with clear margins (Supplementary Fig. 1). Conspicuity scores of 1 and 2 were categorized as poor conspicuity, whereas scores of 3 and 4 were categorized as good conspicuity. To evaluate image-registration accuracy, fusion quality was assessed after final point registration centered on the target tumor. This assessment was performed under real-time US to account for dynamic registration errors caused by respiratory motion. Fusion quality was classified using a 4-point scale: 1, registration failure; 2, successful registration of the central portion but with peripheral misregistration >10 mm; 3, successful registration of both central and peripheral portions with misregistration of 5–10 mm; and 4, successful registration of both the central and peripheral portions with misregistration <5 mm. The technical success of RFA was assessed using contrast-enhanced CT performed immediately after the procedure [22]. The ablative margin was evaluated by comparing the extent of the nonenhancing low-attenuation area (ablation zone) on postprocedural images with the location of the index tumor on preprocedural images [27]. Technical success was classified using a 4-point scale: score 1, residual tumor (incomplete necrosis); score 2, incomplete ablation (complete necrosis with an ablative margin <2 mm); score 3, borderline ablation (complete necrosis with an ablative margin of 2–5 mm); and score 4, adequate ablation (ablative margin >5 mm). Scores of 2, 3, and 4 were considered technically successful. Tumor size was determined by measuring the maximum diameter on HBP or T2-weighted MRI using an electronic caliper within the picture archiving and communication system [28]. To assess the influence of tumor depth on conspicuity, the skin-to-tumor distance was measured as the shortest distance from the skin surface to the center of the tumor. Because some tumors were inconspicuous on B-mode US, tumor depth was retrospectively measured on Kupffer-phase SZUS images, with MRI fusion used as a reference to localize the target lesion as a Kupffer-phase defect.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the independent two-sample t-test. Welch’s t-test was used when the assumption of equal variances was not met. Differences in categorical proportions were evaluated using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Ordinal variables, including tumor conspicuity scores and fusion scores, were compared between groups (HCCs vs. non-HCCs) using the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparisons of conspicuity scores between modalities (B-US, the Kupffer phase of SZUS, and HBP MRI) within each group were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To evaluate the effect of B-mode echogenicity on improvement in tumor conspicuity during the Kupffer phase, rates of reclassification to good conspicuity were compared between echogenicity groups using the Pearson chi-square test. Correlations between conspicuity scores across modalities were assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient (r). Correlation strength was interpreted according to conventional thresholds as follows: very weak, 0.00–0.19; weak, 0.20–0.39; moderate, 0.40–0.59; strong, 0.60–0.79; and very strong, 0.80–1.00 [29]. Tumor depth was compared between lesions with good and poor Kupffer phase conspicuity using the Mann-Whitney U test. Factors associated with good conspicuity on the Kupffer phase were identified using binary logistic regression. Tumor depth was entered as a continuous variable per 1-mm increase. For exploratory categorical analysis, depth was dichotomized at the cohort median (>median vs. ≤median). Variables with P<0.10 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariable model using a stepwise selection procedure. To address the potential nonindependence of multiple tumors within the same patient, a sensitivity analysis was performed using one index lesion per patient, defined as the first ablated tumor in chronological order according to procedural records. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of Patients and Liver Tumors

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1. A total of 81 patients with 128 liver tumors were analyzed. Most patients (72.8%) had underlying liver cirrhosis. Among patients with cirrhosis, hepatitis B virus infection was the most common etiology (37.3%). Hepatic functional reserve was generally well preserved, with 92.6% of patients classified as Child-Pugh class A. Of the 128 liver tumors, 95 (74.2%) were HCCs and 33 (25.8%) were non-HCC malignancies. The non-HCC lesions included cholangiocarcinoma (n=7) and metastases from colorectal cancer (n=13), pancreatic cancer (n=6), advanced gastric cancer (n=4), ampullary carcinoma (n=2), and lung cancer (n=1). There were no significant differences in tumor diameter (14.23±5.74 mm vs. 14.06±9.74 mm, P=0.904) or anatomic location between HCCs and non-HCCs. Artificial ascites was used in the vast majority of procedures (96.1%). Histopathologic confirmation was obtained for eight of 128 tumors (6.3%), including three HCCs and five non-HCC lesions. When pathology was available, the non-HCC lesions comprised two metastases from ampullary carcinoma, two from colorectal cancer, and one cholangiocarcinoma. The remaining 120 tumors (93.8%) were diagnosed based on multidisciplinary review integrating typical imaging findings on contrast-enhanced CT and/or GA-MRI, evidence of an extrahepatic primary malignancy, and the clinical course on follow-up imaging.

Added Value of Kupffer Phase Compared with B-Mode

As shown in Table 2, the conspicuity score of HCCs on B-mode US did not differ significantly from that of non-HCCs (1.79±0.71 vs. 2.00±0.75, P=0.144). When the same lesions were assessed on the Kupffer phase of SZUS, conspicuity increased in both diagnostic categories (2.87±0.85 vs. 3.06±0.79, P=0.274). Conspicuity on the HBP of GA-MRI was highest among the three modalities (3.13±0.87 vs. 3.27±0.76, P=0.465). Within each diagnostic group, however, Kupffer phase conspicuity scores were significantly higher than B-mode scores (HCCs and non-HCCs, both P<0.001) (Table 3), indicating a consistent incremental benefit of the Kupffer phase over B-mode US irrespective of tumor histology (Figs. 2, 3, Supplementary Figs. 2, 3).

Technical Success of RFA and Fusion Scores

Fusion performance of SZUS/MRI guidance was uniformly high in both groups (Table 2). The mean fusion score was 3.59±0.52 for HCCs and 3.67±0.48 for non-HCCs, with no significant difference between the groups (P=0.480). Consequently, the technical success rate of RFA was 97.7% (125/128), with no significant difference between HCCs (96.9%) and non-HCCs (100%) (P>0.99). A representative example of successful SZUS/MRI fusion-guided RFA for a recurrent HCC that was poorly visible on B-US but clearly delineated on the Kupffer phase of SZUS and HBP of GA-MRI is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. Only three HCCs were categorized as technical failures. All three lesions had low conspicuity scores (1 or 2) on both B-US and the Kupffer phase of SZUS, and their conspicuity scores on HBP of GA-MRI were 1, 2, and 3, respectively. These technical failures were subsequently treated with transarterial chemoembolization. A representative case is shown in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Impact of Kupffer Phase on RFA Feasibility According to B-Mode Echogenicity

As shown in Supplementary Table 3, among the 105 tumors with poor conspicuity on B-US, the Kupffer phase reclassified 69 lesions (65.7%) as having good conspicuity. When stratified by B-mode echogenicity, the proportion of lesions achieving good conspicuity on the Kupffer phase differed across the four echogenicity categories. Notably, the rate of reclassification to good conspicuity on the Kupffer phase was significantly higher in the hypoechoic group than in the non-hypoechoic group (84.4% [38/45] vs. 51.7% [31/60], P<0.001).

Correlation between Tumor Conspicuity on B-US, Kupffer Phase of SZUS, and HBP of GA-MRI

Tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase of SZUS was more closely related to conspicuity on HBP of GA-MRI than to conspicuity on B-US (Table 4). For all liver tumors, the correlation between Kupffer phase and HBP conspicuity was moderate (r=0.436, P<0.001), whereas the correlations between B-US and the Kupffer phase (r=0.351, P<0.001) and between B-US and HBP (r=0.311, P<0.001) were weak. A similar pattern was observed when the analysis was restricted to HCCs: the correlation between Kupffer phase and HBP conspicuity (r=0.369, P<0.001) was slightly stronger than the correlations between B-US and the Kupffer phase (r=0.334, P<0.001) and between B-US and HBP (r=0.288, P=0.005). In contrast, among non-HCC lesions, Kupffer phase and HBP conspicuity were strongly correlated (r=0.630, P<0.001), whereas B-US–Kupffer phase (r=0.352, P=0.044) and B-US–HBP (r=0.357, P=0.041) correlations were weak.

Factors Associated with Tumor Conspicuity on Kupffer Phase

The median tumor depth was 57.0 mm (mean±standard deviation, 61.4±26.0 mm). There was no significant difference in the skin-to-tumor center distance between the good- and poor-conspicuity groups (Supplementary Table 4). The mean distance was 61.6±26.7 mm in the good-conspicuity group and 60.9±24.5 mm in the poor-conspicuity group (P>0.99). Associations between clinical and imaging factors and tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase of SZUS are summarized in Table 5. In univariate analysis of all liver tumors, good conspicuity on B-US (P=0.027), membership in the hypoechoic group on B-US (P<0.001), and good conspicuity on HBP MRI (P=0.002) were significantly associated with good conspicuity on the Kupffer phase. In multivariable analysis, two imaging features remained independent predictors of good Kupffer phase conspicuity: membership in the hypoechoic group on B-US (odds ratio [OR], 1.424; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.176 to 1.724; P<0.001) and good conspicuity on HBP MRI (OR, 1.317; 95% CI, 1.087 to 1.596; P=0.005). Other clinical variables, including tumor depth, were not significantly associated with Kupffer phase conspicuity in the multivariable model. The main findings were unchanged in a sensitivity analysis limited to one index lesion per patient (Supplementary Table 5).

In subgroup analyses, membership in the hypoechoic group on B-US (OR, 1.368; 95% CI, 1.120 to 1.672; P=0.002) and good conspicuity on HBP MRI (OR, 1.232; 95% CI, 1.003 to 1.512; P=0.047) were identified as consistent independent predictors of good Kupffer phase conspicuity in HCCs (Supplementary Table 6). For non-HCC lesions, membership in the hypoechoic group on B-US (OR, 16.286; 95% CI, 1.653 to 160.419; P=0.017) and good conspicuity on HBP MRI (OR, 16.000; 95% CI, 2.001 to 127.925; P=0.009) were also significant predictors in univariate analysis (Supplementary Table 7). However, multivariable analysis was not performed in this subgroup because of the small sample size (n=33) and complete separation of the data. Although good conspicuity on B-US showed a trend toward significance in univariate analysis for all liver tumors and for HCCs, it was not identified as an independent predictor in the multivariable models.

Discussion

The findings of this study suggest that the Kupffer phase significantly improves conspicuity for liver tumors compared with B-US. Notably, 65.7% of tumors that were inconspicuous on B-US were reclassified as having good conspicuity on the Kupffer phase, thereby rendering RFA feasible and supporting its role as an effective salvage technique. Furthermore, the use of the Kupffer phase in SZUS/GA-MRI fusion-guided RFA yielded a high technical success rate. A moderate correlation was observed between tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase of SZUS and HBP of GA-MRI, and this correlation was notably stronger in non-HCCs. Multivariable analysis revealed that membership in the hypoechoic group on B-mode US and good conspicuity on HBP MRI were independent predictors of good tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase. Taken together, these findings highlight the Kupffer phase as an effective problem-solving tool for inconspicuous tumors and suggest that preprocedural imaging features may help guide patient selection to maximize the benefit of SZUS/GA-MRI fusion-guided RFA.

Deep-seated lesions are generally considered difficult to visualize on US because of beam attenuation and increased distance from the probe [10]. However, in this study, tumor depth was not a significant factor limiting tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase. This finding may suggest that the high contrast resolution provided by the Kupffer phase of Sonazoid, combined with real-time fusion imaging guidance, enables robust visualization of tumors despite substantial depth. Even for deep-seated tumors, the Kupffer phase maintained high conspicuity scores and facilitated successful ablation guidance.

The Kupffer phase in SZUS is often considered analogous to the HBP in GA-MRI [30]. This similarity arises because both depict postvascular liver parenchymal imaging. The Kupffer phase occurs when Sonazoid is phagocytosed by Kupffer cells, which are macrophages that line the hepatic sinusoids [20,24]. In contrast, the HBP occurs when functional hepatocytes take up gadoxetic acid through organic anion transporting polypeptide receptors before biliary excretion [31,32]. Because malignant liver tumors typically lack both functional hepatocytes and Kupffer cells, they generally appear as distinct defects during both the Kupffer phase of SZUS and the HBP of GA-MRI [30]. These findings support this lesion-level pathophysiologic resemblance. Despite variation in tumor type and baseline liver function, correlations between the Kupffer phase and HBP consistently exceeded those involving B-mode US.

Fluctuations in hepatic function can alter signal intensity during HBP imaging. Specifically, chronic liver disease, ascites, splenomegaly, and abnormal laboratory findings—such as elevated bilirubin and aspartate aminotransferase levels and reduced albumin and cholinesterase levels—are associated with diminished parenchymal enhancement on HBP of GA-MRI [33]. Likewise, liver cirrhosis impairs reticuloendothelial function, a recognized phenomenon in sulfur colloid imaging known as “colloid shift” [34]. This inactivation of the reticuloendothelial system leads to reduced parenchymal enhancement during the late phase of contrast-enhanced US [35]. In such settings, decreased contrast between the lesion and liver parenchyma increases the risk of missing small focal lesions. Importantly, patients with HCC frequently have underlying chronic liver disease, unlike those with non-HCC malignancies, which may impair lesion-to-parenchyma contrast in both imaging phases. In this study, all 61 patients diagnosed with HCCs had underlying liver cirrhosis or chronic liver disease. In contrast, only two of 20 patients diagnosed with non-HCCs had liver cirrhosis or chronic liver disease. Accordingly, it may be inferred that the correlation between tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase of SZUS and HBP of GA-MRI was stronger in non-HCCs than in HCCs in this study. Clinically, this suggests that the Kupffer phase of SZUS may be particularly advantageous for characterizing non-HCC malignancies in relatively preserved livers with high conspicuity on HBP of GA-MRI, whereas in cirrhotic livers the parenchymal background signal on both modalities may partly mask the expected lesion-to-liver contrast and attenuate the strength of the correlation.

Previous studies have suggested that hyperechoic lesions may not appear as defects during the Kupffer phase when a low mechanical index (0.2–0.3) is used, because the underlying B-mode signal can interfere with visualization [36–38]. The present findings support and extend this observation: the rate of reclassification to good conspicuity was significantly lower in the non-hypoechoic group, which included both isoechoic and hyperechoic lesions, than in the hypoechoic group. This may indicate that intrinsic hypoechogenicity provides a more favorable background for visualizing the perfusion defect, whereas higher baseline echogenicity may obscure the defect because of signal interference.

The Kupffer phase is notably stable, allowing repeated scanning and extending the imaging window to as long as 60 minutes [39]. Although the Kupffer phase of SZUS can improve tumor conspicuity, its widespread use in liver ablation remains limited by several factors, including additional cost, unpredictable improvement in conspicuity, and a prolonged waiting time of more than 10 minutes before Kupffer-phase imaging. This study’s findings suggest that preprocedural assessment of B-mode hypoechogenicity and HBP conspicuity may allow clinicians to identify patients most likely to benefit from SZUS/GA-MRI fusion-guided RFA. This selective strategy may improve resource utilization and workflow efficiency in high-volume centers by minimizing unnecessary contrast use while maintaining high technical success rates for challenging lesions.

This study has several limitations. First, because this was a retrospective study conducted at a single tertiary center, the cohort was limited in both the number of patients and the number of liver tumors. A larger prospective cohort is needed to validate these findings. Moreover, all procedures were performed by a single highly experienced operator using a specific US platform and fusion software, which may limit the generalizability of the results to centers with different levels of expertise or different equipment. Second, this study did not include liver tumors that were confidently localized with clear margins on B-US because SZUS was not routinely performed in such cases. This selection strategy enriched the cohort for challenging lesions but may have introduced spectrum bias; therefore, the incremental value of the Kupffer phase observed here may not directly apply to lesions that are already well visualized on B-mode US. Third, assessment of tumor conspicuity on B-US, the Kupffer phase, and HBP relied on visual evaluation and was therefore susceptible to subjectivity and bias. Although two experienced abdominal radiologists reached consensus, there was no assessment of interobserver variability, and the 4-point conspicuity scale used herein has not been formally validated, which may limit reproducibility. Fourth, although a 1-week interval between reading sessions was used to minimize recall bias, the fixed order of image evaluation (B-US, then the Kupffer phase, then HBP) may have introduced order or expectation bias compared with a fully randomized reading protocol. Fifth, pathologic confirmation was not obtained for most liver tumors, and diagnoses relied primarily on GA-MRI and/or contrast-enhanced CT. The non-HCC group was also heterogeneous, including various primary and metastatic malignancies, and the relatively small number of non-HCC lesions limited the power of subgroup analyses. In addition, small metastatic lesions were often poorly visualized on B-US, rendering percutaneous targeting and histopathologic confirmation technically challenging or impractical. Sixth, the precise interval between Sonazoid infusion and RFA was not evaluated. Variation in the delay between injection and Kupffer-phase acquisition may influence parenchymal enhancement and lesion conspicuity, but it was not possible to account for this factor systematically. Seventh, the use of artificial ascites may have improved acoustic visibility on SZUS, potentially influencing conspicuity assessment. Finally, this study focused primarily on conspicuity and technical success rather than long-term oncologic outcomes. Local tumor progression and survival were not systematically analyzed; therefore, the effect of Kupffer phase-guided RFA on disease control and prognosis remains to be determined.

In summary, use of the Kupffer phase significantly improved tumor conspicuity compared with B-US, facilitating RFA in most tumors that initially showed poor conspicuity on B-US and leading to a high technical success rate in this cohort. Because B-mode hypoechogenicity and good HBP conspicuity—the latter showing a moderate correlation with Kupffer phase conspicuity—were identified as independent predictors of favorable Kupffer phase conspicuity, these imaging features may help clinicians identify patients most likely to benefit from SZUS. This strategy may help optimize procedural workflow and facilitate ablation of tumors that are otherwise inconspicuous on conventional B-mode US. Further multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and refine patient-selection criteria for Kupffer phase-guided SZUS.

Key points

The Kupffer phase improved tumor conspicuity compared with B-mode planning ultrasonography (B-US) and was associated with a high technical success rate of ablation in this cohort. Tumor conspicuity on the Kupffer phase exhibited a moderate correlation with that on the hepatobiliary phase (HBP) of gadoxetic acid-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging, and this correlation was notably stronger in non–hepatocellular carcinoma malignancies. Good HBP conspicuity and hypoechogenicity on B-US were independent predictors of good Kupffer phase conspicuity.
