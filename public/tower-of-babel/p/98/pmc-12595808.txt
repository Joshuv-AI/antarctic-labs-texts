Global burden of HIV and drug-resistant tuberculosis co-infection and its attributable risk factors, 1990 to 2021, with projections to 2031
Authors: Liting Feng, Yubao Wang, Li Li, Xing Wang, Jing Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe persistent global spread of HIV and drug-resistant tuberculosis (HIV/DR-TB) co-infection poses a significant challenge to international tuberculosis control efforts. This study aimed to analyze the global burden of HIV/DR-TB co-infection across age groups, genders, and Socio-demographic Index (SDI) regions, while identifying attributable risk factors.MethodsData from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 and joinpoint regression analysis were utilized to examine epidemiological trends from 1990 to 2021 across different genders and SDI regions. The Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model was employed to forecast trends up to 2031.ResultsHIV and multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (HIV/MDR-TB) co-infection exhibited no significant gender differences in prevalence, incidence, mortality, or DALYs (P = 0.053, 0.277, 0.354, 0.212). In contrast, HIV and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (HIV/XDR-TB) co-infection showed significantly higher rates in males for all outcomes (P = 0.007, 0.003, 0.003, 0.005). Burden distribution varied by SDI quintile: Low SDI bore a greater HIV/MDR-TB co-infection burden. High-middle SDI had higher HIV/XDR-TB co-infection prevalence. Unsafe sex was the predominant risk factor for HIV/DR-TB co-infections in both genders, followed by drug use in males and intimate partner violence in females. Regional risk patterns revealed that male drug use was more strongly associated with HIV/DR-TB co-infection in high-middle SDI regions, whereas unsafe sex and intimate partner violence (in females) showed greater impact in low SDI regions. Projections indicated a steady decline in HIV/DR-TB co-infection from 2021 to 2031.ConclusionDespite observed declines, HIV/DR-TB co-infection continues to pose a significant public health challenge. Regional SDI-stratified interventions are urgently needed: low-SDI settings should prioritize healthcare system strengthening to address gender-specific vulnerabilities including unsafe sexual practices and intimate partner violence, while high-middle SDI regions require comprehensive drug abuse prevention programs combining public education with enhanced pharmaceutical controls.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11830-5.

Background

Multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis (XDR-TB) represent a critical antimicrobial resistance threat, significantly compromising treatment efficacy and overwhelming healthcare systems in endemic regions [1, 2]. Compared to drug-susceptible tuberculosis (TB) (more than 85% success rate), DR-TB treatments achieve only 68% success rates while being more costly, prolonged, and toxic [3]. Key drivers of DR-TB spread include diagnostic delays, suboptimal care models, treatment non-adherence, and limited therapeutic options [4–6].

HIV co-infection substantially worsens TB outcomes [7]. Among the 38 million people living with HIV worldwide, predominantly in Africa [8], HIV primarily spreads through unprotected sex,contaminated blood products, needle-sharing, and vertical transmission. Without treatment, HIV progressively weakens immunity over 2–10 years [9]. HIV elevates TB risks dramatically—increasing latent TB reactivation risk 20-fold [10], with 10% annual versus 5–10% lifetime reactivation risks in HIV-positive versus negative individuals respectively [8].

TB remains the leading AIDS-related cause of death [11]. The expanding HIV/DR-TB co-infection challenges global TB control. Despite accumulating epidemiological evidence, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding gender and regional disparities across Socio-demographic Index (SDI) levels—a composite development metric incorporating per capita income, educational attainment, and fertility rates, with higher values denoting advanced socioeconomic development [12]. Precise HIV-status stratified DR-TB data are essential for burden assessment and targeted interventions.

Methods

Data source

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database represents one of the largest publicly accessible global epidemiological surveys [13]. The methodologies and findings of GBD 2019 have been extensively documented in prior publications. Building upon the GBD 2019 framework, GBD 2021 incorporates new methodologies and processes to deliver detailed estimates of incidence, prevalence, and Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) for 371 health conditions. This data is segmented by sex, age, and the SDI, which reflects disparities in income, education, and fertility across different populations [14].

We accessed data through the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) query tool (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool), a user-friendly web-based platform, to gather information on population data, as well as the incidence, prevalence, mortality, DALYs and its attributable risk factors associated with HIV/DR-TB co-infections and age-standardized rates.

Joinpoint regression analysis

To identify trend changes, assess trend changes, epidemiological transitions, and detect effective public health interventions for HIV/DR-TB co-infection, we performed joinpoint regression analyses using Joinpoint software (v4.9.1.0; National Cancer Institute). The algorithm identifies significant trend inflection points (joinpoints) by iteratively fitting segmented linear models through least squares regression to minimize residual errors, while permutation testing (α = 0.05) determines the optimal number of joinpoints [15]. This method quantifies annual percentage changes (APC) and the average annual percentage change (AAPC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each segment, capturing nonlinear trends undetectable by linear regression. Positive and negative APC values indicate upward and downward trends, respectively.

Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) analysis

The Bayesian age–period–cohort (BAPC) model was selected for projections in this study. The BAPC model employs the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) method to approximate the marginal posterior distribution, effectively addressing common challenges associated with Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques, such as mixing and convergence issues, while maintaining high computational efficiency [16]. Extensively validated and applied in epidemiological research, the BAPC model is particularly adept at analyzing age-structured data and complex cohort effects, owing to its comprehensive ability to capture temporal trends [17]. By integrating prior information with sample data, it estimates posterior distributions and infers unknown parameters, yielding accurate and well-calibrated probabilistic forecasts [18].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphing for this study were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1). Additionally, Joinpoint regression software (version 4.9.1.0) was employed for temporal trend analysis. A Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) analysis was performed in R to project future trends in disease burden. Given the non-normal distribution of our data (confirmed by Shapiro–Wilk tests, p < 0.05), we applied the Scheirer-Ray-Hare test, a non-parametric alternative to two-way ANOVA, for all between-group comparisons. Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons.

Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

The temporal trends of HIV and DR-TB co-infection from 1990 to 2021

Analysis of GBD 2021 data indicates a 17-fold increase in age-standardized HIV/MDR-TB coinfection prevalence from 0.051 (95% CI: 0.032–0.084) to 0.868 per 100,000 (95% CI: 0.595–1.286) between 1990–2021, with an AAPC of 9.102% (95% CI: 8.308–9.902). The epidemic curve exhibited an inverted V-shaped trajectory (Fig. 2), peaking in 2005, which corresponds temporally with two key transitions: the expansion of HIV epidemics alongside inadequate TB control measures (1990–2005), followed by improvements post-2005 coinciding with global antiretroviral therapy (ART) scale-up and DOTS-Plus implementation. Segmented regression analysis (p < 0.01) revealed significant gender-based differences in epidemiological trends during the post-2015 decline phase, with female populations demonstrating slower rates of improvement for both ASIR (APC −2.759 versus male −3.018) and ASDR (APC −4.747 versus male −5.714) (Table S2). Comparative joinpoint analysis showed a 52.8% reduction in the rate of ASIR decline between 2005–2015 (−6.167%) and 2015–2021 (−2.913%), suggesting diminishing effectiveness of current intervention strategies.Fig. 2Joinpoint regression analysis of HIV/MDR-TB (A-D) and HIV/XDR-TB (E–H) co-infection trends by sex, 1990–2021. The peak of the HIV/MDR-TB co-infection epidemic occurred in 2005, reflecting the expansion of HIV and suboptimal tuberculosis control from 1990 to 2005. Subsequent improvements post-2005 followed the scale-up of antiretroviral therapy (ART) and the implementation of DOTS-Plus. Gender disparities were evident, with females showing slower declines in ASIR (−2.759% vs male −3.018%) and ASDR (−4.747% vs −5.714%). Notably, the ASIR decline rate attenuated by 52.8% between 2005–2015 (−6.167%) and 2015–2021 (−2.913%), suggesting diminishing intervention efficacy. The disproportionate DALY burden relative to other metrics underscores the critical need for enhanced rehabilitation services alongside treatment. HIV/XDR-TB coinfection metrics demonstrated consistently increasing trends from 1990–2021, with AAPCs of 18.444% (95% CI: 16.368–20.558) for ASPR, 19.041% (17.376–20.729) for ASIR, 15.365% (14.283–16.458) for ASDR, and 41.220% (35.104–47.613) for DALYs (Table S3). The differential decline in DALYs between females (−2.296%) and males (−3.144%) during 2005–2021 may indicate variations in access to second-line treatments or biological response differences. ASPR showed minimal decline (APC −0.248%, nonsignificant for 2009–2021), suggesting ongoing transmission despite mortality reductions (Table S2). While female mortality demonstrated accelerated decline (−7.300% during 2018–2021), the disproportionately high DALY burden (AAPC 41.220%) relative to ASPR (18.444%) and ASDR (15.365%) highlights the substantial long-term disability impact of XDR-TB, emphasizing the need for comprehensive rehabilitation services alongside acute treatment interventions (Table S3).

The SDI regions distribution of HIV and DR-TB co-infection

This study employs Joinpoint regression analysis to reveal significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity in the epidemiological trends of tuberculosis across regions with different SDI regions.

In high-SDI regions, the incidence of HIV/MDR-TB showed a significant decline from 1995 to 1998 (APC = −11.284%), likely attributable to enhanced HIV/AIDS prevention and control measures and optimized tuberculosis management. From 2005 to 2014, the mortality rate accelerated its decline (APC = −6.676%), reflecting improved access to second-line drugs and strengthened drug resistance surveillance systems. In high-middle and middle-SDI regions, the incidence of HIV/MDR-TB surged sharply in the early 1990 s (APC > 100%), followed by a slowdown in growth (e.g., middle-SDI regions from 2002 to 2007, APC = 0.866%). After 2011, mortality and DALYs began to decline at an accelerated pace (high-middle SDI regions, APC = −8.537% and −8.991%). Mortality in middle-low SDI regions began to decline after 2005 (from 2006 to 2014, APC = −9.572%). After 2014, mortality and DALYs further declined (APC = −6.657% and −7.121%). In low-SDI regions, key epidemiological indicators for HIV/MDR-TB coinfection—including ASPR, ASIR, ASDR, and DALYs—all demonstrated an epidemiological transition from increasing to decreasing trends around the year 1999–2003, possibly due to strengthened international aid programs such as the Global Fund (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Joinpoint regression analysis of ASPR, ASIR, ASDR, and ASR of DALYs for HIV/MDR-TB co-infection (panels A, B, C, D) or HIV/XDR-TB (panels E, F, G, H) across different SDI quintiles from 1990 to 2021. An analysis of the epidemiological trends of HIV/XDR-TB co-infection revealed an explosive increase in XDR-TB cases across all SDI regions in the early 1990 s, with an APC greater than 900%. This indicates the synchronous emergence of drug-resistant tuberculosis as a global threat. Regional disparity analysis showed that high-SDI regions experienced an accelerated decline in mortality after 2015 (APC = −7.097%), likely associated with the introduction of novel drugs (e.g., bedaquiline). Mortality rates and ASR of DALYs in high-middle SDI regions began to see declines after 2006 (APC = −0.119 and −3.168%), possibly due to enhanced second-line drug management. However, middle-SDI regions continued to exhibit rising XDR-TB prevalence after 2015 (APC = 2.074%), suggesting persistent gaps in drug resistance management. In middle-low SDI regions, DALYs declined at an accelerated rate after 2007 (APC = −5.143%), likely due to international aid and improved laboratory capacity. Low SDI regions showed slow declines after 2000 in DALYs (from 2001 to 2021, APC = −0.167%), reflecting weak prevention and control systems.

Attributable risk factors for HIV and DR-TB co-infection

All level 2 risk factors linked to HIV/DR-TB co-infection encompass unsafe sex, drug use, and intimate partner violence (IPV). Unsafe sex emerged as the predominant risk factor for both sexes, with an ASDR of 0.18 (0.08–0.33) and 0.01 (0.00–0.01) per 100,000 for HIV/MDR-TB and HIV/XDR-TB co-infections, respectively, in 2021 (Figures S3C). Additionally, unsafe sex was the leading contributor to the ASR of DALYs due to risk factors, amounting to 9.24 (4.14, 16.87) and 0.34 (0.15, 0.61) per 100,000 for HIV/MDR-TB and HIV/XDR-TB co-infections, respectively (Figures S3D). Drug use was identified as the second most significant contributor to the HIV/DR-TB co-infection burden among men, whereas IPV was the secondary factor for women (as depicted in Figs. 4 and 5). The mortality risks associated with various hazardous factors are consistently lowest in high SDI regions across different SDI levels, whereas females in low-SDI regions exhibit persistently higher risks from unsafe sex and intimate partner violence (IPV). This trend indicates underlying issues in healthcare access and gender equity within these regions. Discussion

Tuberculosis remains a significant global public health challenge [19]. In 2014, the World Health Organization (WHO)-endorsed End TB Strategy set an ambitious goal to eliminate tuberculosis by 2035 [20]. However, the COVID-19 pandemic led to an 18% decline in reported tuberculosis cases in 2020, with India, Indonesia, and the Philippines accounting for 93% of this reduction [21]. Such service disruptions contributed to a rebound in tuberculosis-related mortality. Notably, all forms of DR-TB exhibit transmission dynamics comparable to rifampicin-susceptible tuberculosis, driven by ongoing acquisition of resistance and human-to-human transmission. However, DR-TB presents greater diagnostic challenges, higher mortality rates, and an increased likelihood of post-tuberculosis lung damage [22, 23]. DR-TB is estimated to account for 13% of all global antimicrobial resistance-related deaths [23]. Although DR-TB constitutes only 5%–10% of total tuberculosis cases, it consumes over 50% of national tuberculosis budgets [22]. Furthermore, individuals co-infected with HIV and DR-TB experience worse clinical outcomes and significantly elevated mortality rates [24].

The period 2000–2015 witnessed substantial global investment in HIV/AIDS control, with over $500 billion allocated through Millennium Development Goal initiatives, achieving significant reductions in HIV-related mortality [25]. As a key intervention, PEPFAR has delivered remarkable outcomes since 2003, with cumulative investments exceeding $100 billion supporting annual ART provision to 20.6 million individuals, including 550,000 children. Our analysis reveals that HIV/DR-TB co-infection metrics peaked circa 2005, temporally aligned with global HIV mortality trends [26]. While enhanced control measures likely contributed to subsequent declines, the projected impact of the 2025 funding freeze raises serious concerns: a 90-day interruption may cause 60,000–74,000 excess deaths, with prolonged cuts potentially triggering 500,000 additional pediatric AIDS deaths in sub-Saharan Africa within five years [27]. These findings emphasize the indispensable role of sustained funding in preserving global health gains.

Research findings indicate that people living with HIV (PLHIV) face 1.42-fold and 2.7-fold higher risks of developing MDR-TB and primary drug resistance, respectively, compared to HIV-negative individuals [28]. It should be emphasized that while HIV infection does not directly drive the evolution of drug-resistant TB strains, the immunosuppressed state frequently results in smear-negative sputum samples, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment [29], which indirectly elevates the risks of drug resistance and mortality. The underlying indirect mechanisms include weakened immune control due to CD4 + T-cell depletion, which increases bacterial load and mutation risk [30].

Early detection, diagnosis, and treatment are essential for achieving TB control and elimination. Inappropriate or ineffective therapeutic interventions may contribute to the development of drug-resistant TB. Although culture remains the gold standard diagnostic method, its slow processing time delays potential treatment initiation and may consequently increase MDR-TB incidence [31]. In contrast, molecular techniques offer a highly sensitive and specific alternative [32]. These innovations have expanded the number of detectable mutations while reducing both turnaround time and costs associated with sequencing-based resistance mutation identification [23]. However, these technologies not only face challenges in the development of novel tools and methodologies but also require ensuring accessibility for the communities most in need. Furthermore, revolutionary changes in all-oral anti-TB drug regimens have led to more effective, less toxic, and shorter-duration treatments that are generally better tolerated. These improvements may enhance treatment adherence and outcomes, thereby reducing the emergence of drug resistance [32]. The introduction of shorter-course MDR-TB regimens has shifted treatment strategies toward injection-free protocols, with 6-month regimens now approved for both rifampicin-resistant TB and MDR-TB [3]. These developments underscore the importance of continued innovation and equitable implementation to optimize TB management and mitigate the growing threat of drug-resistant tuberculosis.

Our study found that young and middle-aged adults (25–45 years) bear the heaviest burden of HIV/DR-TB co-infection, which aligns with previous research findings [33]. This phenomenon can be attributed to this population's active social engagement, high exposure levels, engagement in high-risk behaviors, and high mobility. Notably, a small but distinct peak in both incidence and mortality of HIV/MDR-TB co-infection was observed among children under 5 years old. This may be associated with difficulties in obtaining adequate sputum samples and their immature immune systems [21, 34]. Elderly patients face greater challenges, exhibiting higher TB-related mortality and more difficult diagnosis due to immunosenescence [35]. Therapeutic drug monitoring proves particularly crucial for patients with drug-resistant TB, retreatment TB, and those with HIV co-infection [36].

Our study confirms a greater HIV/MDR-TB burden among women, consistent with prior research [37], likely due to increased household exposure to MDR-TB cases, healthcare-seeking delays, and social stigma [38]. Reproductive-aged women face heightened vulnerability, with HIV increasing active TB risk 2.56-fold during pregnancy [30], while treatment discontinuation—frequently driven by misconceptions about fetal harm or breastfeeding risks—contributes to peak mortality among women aged 20–39 years. Men show higher HIV/XDR-TB rates, potentially reflecting riskier behaviors and poorer adherence. While DR-TB's stringent protocols may reduce gender disparities through more equitable care, targeted interventions remain crucial—including integrated antenatal screening and addressing intimate partner violence [39]. These findings underscore the need for gender-specific approaches in co-infection management.

The SDI significantly influences HIV/DR-TB co-infection patterns. High-SDI regions show declining HIV/MDR-TB burdens, while low-SDI areas, particularly sub-Saharan Africa (accounting for > 75% of global cases [40]), face worsening epidemics due to diagnostic shortages, treatment gaps, and limited health education. These regions struggle with malnutrition (a TB risk factor and consequence), stigma, and poor living conditions [41–45], necessitating integrated TB/HIV services and nutrition interventions. High-middle SDI regions show higher HIV/XDR-TB co-infection, potentially linked to better molecular diagnostic access. This enables improved drug stewardship and resistance control. Effective management demands multidisciplinary (MDT)-based integrated care (nutritional/psychological/social support), alongside expanding “provider‐initiated counselling and testing” (PICT) initiatives for HIV—proven to enhance testing acceptance, uptake, and case detection [46].

GBD-based studies have limitations, including unaccounted post-TB disabilities [47] and data quality disparities in high-burden areas. To overcome these limitations, we propose incorporating data from cohort studies in the revised manuscript to refine DALY estimates, applying geospatial modeling techniques to fill data gaps in conflict-affected areas, and validating GBD estimates against national tuberculosis registries. These approaches will enhance the accuracy and timeliness of global health metrics, providing a more solid basis for future research and policy decisions.

Conclusion

Although the ASPR, ASIR, ASDR, and DALYs for HIV co-infection with DR-TB show a downward trend, the overall disease burden remains significant, particularly in resource-limited regions. Consistent vigilance and tailored interventions are essential for effectively tackling this challenge. A concerted effort to control unsafe sexual practices are required to reduce HIV and DR-TB co-infection burden. Moreover, special attention should be devoted to the transmission of HIV co-infected with XDR-TB among men. It is crucial to develop and enforce strategies to combat drug misuse among men and to protect women from the scourge of intimate partner violence.
