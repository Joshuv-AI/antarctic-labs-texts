Genomic selection of root‐knot nematode (Meloidogyne enterolobii) resistance in watermelon wild relatives (Citrullus amarus)
Authors: Anju Biswas, Homan Regmi, Benjamin D Waldo, Sandra Branham, Pat Wechter, Shaker Kousik, Diego Jarquin, Amnon Levi, William B Rutter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Meloidogyne enterolobii is a virulent root‐knot nematode (RKN) species posing a significant threat to watermelon production across the United States. The USDA, ARS, Plant Introduction (PI) collection of Citrullus amarus, a wild relative of cultivated watermelon (Citrullus lanatus), contains RKN‐resistance. However, incorporating RKN resistance into watermelon cultivars is challenging. Genomic selection could be a useful strategy for improving RKN resistance in watermelon as it is a polygenic trait primarily controlled through additive gene action, as indicated by the broad‐sense heritability being close or equal to the narrow‐sense heritability: H 2 = 0.69, h 2 = 0.23 for galling and H 2 = 0.47, h 2 = 0.46 for eggs per gram of root. Here, a genomic selection approach was used to evaluate 97 C. amarus PIs (S3 inbred lines) for RKN resistance based on two criteria: (1) root galling percentage and (2) eggs per gram of root. Variants from whole‐genome resequencing (2.1 million SNPs) of PIs were used here. Genomic prediction (GP) models, including genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP), sparse GBLUP, and the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) regression, were tested. The RKHS model based on 2.1 million SNPs had the highest prediction ability for galling (0.42) and for eggs per gram of root (0.50). The most resistant PIs (galling: PI_596692, PI_532664, PI_288316, PI_271767, and PI_542118; eggs per gram root: PI_271775, PI_485584, and PI_299379) based on both best linear unbiased estimates and genome estimated breeding values under 10% selection intensity were selected and are being used in our breeding program to enhance RKN resistance in watermelon cultivars.

INTRODUCTION

Watermelon (Citrullus lanatus) is an important agricultural crop, valued for its nutritional and economic contributions. In 2022, global production exceeded 100 million tons, supporting food security and agricultural economies worldwide (FAO, 2022). Due to intensive cultivation practices and limited availability of agricultural land, damage caused by root‐knot nematodes (RKNs) in watermelon fields has increased substantially in recent years. This problem has been exacerbated by the phase‐out of the soil fumigant methyl bromide, after it was classified as a Class I ozone‐depleting substance under the Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer and the U.S. Clean Act. The primary RKN genera (Meloidogyne spp.) invade watermelon plant roots, induce gall formation, disrupt water and nutrient uptake, and consequently reduce plant vigor and yield (Thies & Levi, 2007). Several Meloidogyne species including M. incognita, M. javanica, M. arenaria, and M. enterolobii are known to cause significant yield losses in watermelon (López‐Gómez et al., 2016; Thies & Levi, 2007; Thies et al., 2010; Waldo et al., 2023). These RKN species vary in their geographic distribution and pathogenicity. Meloidogyne incognita is a widely distributed virulent species, causing up to 50% yield losses in heavily infested fields (Hussey & Janssen, 2002). Meloidogyne javanica predominates in tropical and subtropical regions, particularly in sandy soil, and could cause 30%–60% crop yield losses (Bridge & Page, 1980). Meloidogyne arenaria, though less common, can exacerbate crop damage in high‐moisture soils and mixed infestations (Sasser & Freckman, 1987). Meloidogyne enterolobii is an aggressive species that has emerged in recent years and poses a threat to watermelon production worldwide (Kiewnick et al., 2009).

All commercial watermelon cultivars are highly susceptible to RKN, with potential yield losses of 50%–75% under field conditions (Davis, 2007; Thies et al., 2016). Furthermore, RKN infestations can interact synergistically with the soil‐borne pathogen Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. niveum, the causal agent of Fusarium wilt, exacerbating disease severity and yield loss beyond what is caused by either pathogen alone (Akhtar & Malik, 2000; Hua et al., 2019; Regmi et al., 2022). Meloidogyne enterolobii is an aggressive RKN species that has emerged in recent years and poses a significant threat to watermelon and other vegetable crop production (Kiewnick et al., 2009; Rutter et al., 2019). The RKN has been shown to overcome resistance in vegetable crops (Kiewnick et al., 2009; Rutter et al., 2019). The new threat by M. enterolobii stresses the need for developing new strategies, including new sources of resistance, for effective management of RKN species in vegetable crops including watermelon.

Current control strategies, including chemical nematicides and cultural practices, have significant limitations. Chemical control faces increasing regulatory restrictions due to environmental concerns, while cultural practices that include crop rotation and soil management are often insufficient in high‐intensity production systems (Davis, 2007). Developing RKN‐resistant cultivars represents the most sustainable long‐term solution. The citron melon, Citrullus amarus, a wild relative of watermelon, has been identified as a valuable source of RKN resistance (Levi et al., 2013; Thies & Levi, 2003, 2007; Thies et al., 2016; Waldo et al., 2023). Waldo et al. (2023) screened the entire USDA C. amarus collection for resistance to RKN, identified resistant accessions, and found genomic loci associated with resistance through a genome‐wide association study (GWAS) using variants from whole‐genome resequencing data. The study by Waldo et al. (2023) suggested that breeding watermelon for RKN resistance will be challenging due to the complex, polygenic nature of the resistance. The effort of moving multiple target genes from a crop wild relative (C. amarus) to cultivated watermelon (C. lanatus), along with the labor‐intensive phenotyping required to accurately assess resistance in large populations, underscores the difficulties in RKN‐resistance introgression. It is nearly impossible to capture all small‐effect gene loci scattered on the genome using traditional quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping and to incorporate them into a commercial cultivar genome background using marker‐assisted selection.

Genomic selection (GS) offers a promising solution to these challenges by leveraging genome‐wide marker information to predict the genetic potential of untested individuals (Crossa et al., 2017; Meuwissen et al., 2001). The GS approach facilitates efficient selection for complex traits like RKN resistance by integrating phenotypic and genomic data to identify superior genotypes at an early developmental stage, while reducing the requirement for expensive phenotyping.

The objectives of this study were to use the RKN phenotypic data from our recent study (Waldo et al., 2023) and genome‐wide variants (Katuuramu et al., 2022) to (1) assess the predictive performance of various GS models in identifying genotypes showing resistance to M. enterolobii and (2) select candidate genotypes in the USDA, ARS, C. amarus PI collection based on genomic estimated breeding value (GEBV) for prediction of RKN resistance. These genotypes can be used in breeding programs aimed at improving watermelon cultivars with RKN resistance using GS to minimize labor and enhance genetic gain.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

RKN resistance phenotyping

The root galling and (SC.1) eggs per gram of root were collected in our recent genome‐wide association study (GWAS) to identify putative gene loci associated with resistance to M. enterolobii (Waldo et al., 2023) and used here as response variables for our GS study. RKN resistance in C. amarus is a polygenic trait controlled by various gene loci with major and minor effects. Our previous attempts to incorporate the resistance from C. amarus into watermelon cultivars using molecular markers yielded insufficient resistance (unpublished data). For this reason, we are using here the GS approach as part of our attempts to increase genetic gain in incorporating RKN resistance into watermelon cultivars. Briefly, four separate phenotyping screens were performed in a greenhouse during the summer and fall of 2021, using 108 inbred C. amarus plant introductions (https://www.ars‐grin.gov/), which are part of core collection along with the susceptible control “Charleston Gray.” The experimental scheme followed a completely randomized design. Young plants were grown in sterilized substrate, inoculated with 4000 Meloidogyne enterolobii eggs, and maintained under controlled conditions. Six weeks post‐inoculation, plants were harvested to assess root galling and nematode reproduction. Nematode eggs were extracted using the Hussey and Barker (1973) method. In this set of experiments, we have set the threshold for resistance at <50% for galling and <7000 eggs per gram of root, which correspond to the level of significant difference (Tukey's HSD < 0.05) when compared to our susceptible control Charleston Grey.

Genotyping

Genomic data were obtained from our whole‐genome resequencing of C. amarus accessions (Katuuramu et al., 2022; http://www.icugi.org/v2/). Briefly, following whole‐genome resequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, reads were aligned to the C. amarus reference genome (USVL246‐FR2). Variant calling was performed using the GATK Best Practices pipeline (DePristo et al., 2011). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were filtered based on quality thresholds, including missing data, minor allele frequency (MAF), and read depth (Biswas et al., 2025; Katuuramu et al., 2022).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 3.5.2), and data normalization was applied as described in Waldo et al. (2023). Plants were classified as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant based on significant reductions in galling and nematode reproduction (p < 0.05) relative to the susceptible control. Accessions (PIs) exhibiting significant reductions (adjusted p ≤ 0.05) in all three measured phenotypes relative to the susceptible control (Charleston Gray) were classified as resistant. PIs that did not differ significantly from the susceptible control in any of the measured phenotypes were classified as susceptible. PIs with significant differences in only one or two phenotypes were classified as intermediate (Waldo et al., 2023). To assess phenotypic variation within the germplasm collection, histograms were generated for two traits: percentage of root area affected by galls and eggs per gram of root.

Genetic parameter calculation

Broad‐sense and narrow‐sense heritability were estimated for root galling and eggs per gram root to determine the Meloidogyne enterolobii resistance mode among the C. amarus PIs. Broad‐sense heritability was estimated using phenotypic data in a traditional approach, while the narrow‐sense heritability was calculated based on G‐matrix. In both cases, we used the ASReml‐R package (Butler et al., 2009) in R (Biswas et al., 2025; R Core Team, 2020). Estimated eBLUEs for each resistant family across replicates were subsequently used in calculating the prediction accuracy (PA) of the genomic prediction (GP) models.

Genomic prediction models

GP analyses were performed using four statistical models: genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP), sparse GBLUP (sGBLUP), reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) regression, and sparse RKHS (sRKHS). A 10‐fold cross‐validation (CV) scheme, repeated 10 times, was implemented to evaluate PA. The GBLUP and sGBLUP models were implemented using the rrBLUP package (Endelman, 2011) in combination with the AGHmatrix package (Amadeu et al., 2016) to compute the additive genomic relationship matrix (G‐matrix). The RKHS and sRKHS models were implemented using the BGLR package (Pérez & de los Campos, 2014), where the genomic kernel was computed using the inner product of standardized marker matrices. The sparse models (sGBLUP and sRKHS) were based on the top 10,000 SNPs selected by the highest variance among all markers.

Sparse GBLUP model

Due to the large number of markers (>2 million SNPs), an sGBLUP approach was implemented by selecting the 10,000 SNPs with the highest variance to reduce computational complexity. The 10K SNPs were selected once based on allele count variance [p*(1 − p)] across accessions after an MAF threshold of 0.05 and a missing data threshold of 50% were applied. RKHS models

In addition to GBLUP models, RKHS regression models were fitted to capture nonlinear genetic effects. Two RKHS models were tested: (1) RKHS (all markers): This model used the full set of 2.1 million markers. (2) RKHS (Sparse, 10k Markers): The same subset of 10,000 markers from sGBLUP was used here for sRKHS models to reduce computational complexity. The RKHS models were implemented using the BGLR package (Pérez & de los Campos, 2014) with Gaussian kernels with bandwidth selected via default CV to model complex genetic interactions.

Model assessment

Due to the weak relationship between root galling percentage and eggs per gram root (Waldo et al., 2023), only univariate models that deal with a single variable (root galling or eggs per gram root) have been tested in this study.

The GP models were evaluated using Pearson's correlation between observed and predicted values based on a 10‐fold CV scheme. In each iteration, 90% of the genotypes (PIs) were randomly assigned to the training set, while the remaining 10% served as the validation set. To ensure robustness and reduce variability due to random sampling, the 10‐fold CV process was repeated 10 times, generating a total of 100 independent partitions. The average correlation across all folds and replicates was reported as the final model assessment metric.

Selection intensities for capturing superior genotypes (PIs)

To identify superior genotypes based on GEBVs, three selection intensities (SIs) (selecting the top 10%, 20%, and 30% from the total population) were applied for both traits. The GEBV results from RKHS (2.1 million markers) were compared with family rankings based on eBLUEs to assess the consistency of selection and identify the top‐performing genotypes. Though 108 PIs phenotyped for M. enterolobii resistance, only 97 had both phenotypic and genotypic data available for both traits and were used for downstream analysis.

This approach evaluated the effectiveness of GS in capturing superior genotypes compared to traditional phenotype‐based selection. By comparing the overlap between GEBV‐selected and BLUEs‐ranked genotypes, we assessed the extent to which genomic predictions align with phenotypic performance, ensuring that superior genetic potential is effectively identified.

RESULTS

Distribution of galling severity and nematode reproduction among PIs

The galling percentage varied among the C. amarus PIs, showing continuous distribution, with most genotypes clustering in the range of 10%–40% of root tissue having RKN‐galls (Figure 1). On the other hand, eggs per gram of root exhibited a skewed distribution (having overall lower egg count), with approximately 78.17% of all genotypes (PIs) showing egg counts below the overall mean for the whole PI collection (13,328.14 eggs per gram of root), while only a few of the C. amarus PIs displayed high egg loads (Figure 2). As expected, the Charleston Gray cultivar (used here as a control reference) is highly susceptible with mean gall percentage of 77.11, and mean eggs per gram root was 84,382.86 (Figures 1 and 2).

Genomic prediction performance

The four GP models evaluated in this study showed moderate predictive abilities (PAs), ranging from 0.30 to 0.50 across the two resistance traits (Table 2). Model performance differed by trait. For galling percentage, both sGBLUP and RKHS models achieved the highest PA (0.42), with corresponding root mean square error (RMSE) values of ∼2.3, which represents only ∼2% error on the 0%–100% scale. For eggs per gram of root, RKHS with all markers produced the strongest prediction (PA = 0.50, RMSE ≈ 4771; ∼3.4% of the phenotypic scale), whereas sGBLUP performed slightly lower (PA = 0.40, RMSE ≈ 11,591; ∼8.3% of scale).

Across traits, RKHS using all markers consistently yielded lower RMSE compared to BLUP‐based models, particularly for eggs per gram of root where error was reduced by more than half. This indicates that RKHS more accurately approximated observed phenotypes in addition to ranking genotypes. Nevertheless, sGBLUP provided comparable PA for galling and remains an attractive option given its computational efficiency. These results highlight the importance of trait‐specific model choice: while RKHS with all markers offers the most robust framework for predicting resistance, sGBLUP provides a practical alternative with only minor trade‐offs in accuracy.

As expected, the GEBV‐based selection consistently identified fewer genotypes than BLUEs at higher SIs (i.e., more stringent selection, at 10% and 20% than at 30% SI). Based on the overlap between BLUEs (phenotyping data) and GEBV (GS data) at the 10% SI, five PIs (PI 596692, PI 532664, PI 288316, PI 271767, and PI 542118) were identified as having the highest resistance to root galling and three PIs (PI 271775, PI 485584, and PI 299379) were identified as having high resistance to egg reproduction (laying eggs per gram root). However, no PIs were found to have both RKN‐resistance traits measured here (resistance to root galling and eggs per gram root) at 10% or 20% SIs based on the overlap between BLUEs and GEBV. At 30% SI, PI 596676 and PI 171392 were consistently selected for resistance to root galling and eggs per gram root based on the overlap between BLUEs and GEBV methods, underscoring their potential as donors for improving both RKN‐resistance components (root galling and eggs per gram root) in watermelon cultivars.

DISCUSSION

The distribution in resistance levels among PIs reflects substantial variability in nematode reproduction, highlighting the potential for selecting highly RKN‐resistant C. amarus genotypes, useful in improving the resistance in watermelon cultivars. Resistance and tolerance to nematodes in watermelon is continuum since the resistance is a multigenic/quantitative trait. Therefore, any type of distinct threshold for classifying types of resistance will be arbitrary. Similarly, the yield and economic impacts of host resistance to nematodes are complex and depend on various environmental factors, including the size of initial nematode populations, seasonal temperatures, as well as what crops are planted in that field. What we can say for certain is that any reduction in the amount of root galling or any reduction in nematode population has a positive effect on yield and reduces the nematode impact on any subsequent crop planted in that field. The results in our study (Waldo et al., 2023) indicate that the C. amarus germplasm collection contains high, moderate, and low resistance levels to M. enterolobii, as has been shown in previous studies with M. arenaria (Thies & Levi, 2003, 2007). An abundance of metabolites was found in RKN‐resistant roots of Citrullus amarus PIs compared with those in watermelon cultivars (Kantor et al., 2018).

The differences in distribution patterns for M. enterolobii root galling percentage versus eggs per gram of root (Figures 1 and 2) indicate that these two resistance traits are controlled by different gene loci. Although bivariate models were explored to potentially improve prediction by leveraging cross‐trait information, they were not pursued further due to model instability. This limitation is due to the low correlation between these two RKN‐resistance traits: root galling and nematode eggs per gram of root (Waldo et al., 2023). Our recent GWAS study with the same population (Waldo et al., 2023) identified a QTL associated with reduced galling on chromosome Ca03, while separate QTL on chromosomes Ca04 and Ca08 were associated with reductions in nematode eggs per gram of root. Tzortzakakis (2010) concluded that root galling indices are not necessarily associated with numbers of nematode eggs in roots of vegetable crops and with the number of juveniles in the soil and such observations should be expected when exploring resistance to Meloidogyne spp. Thus, the scheme of breeding separately for each of these traits should be considered in incorporating RKN resistance into the genome background of watermelon cultivars. A breeding scheme may include separate backcrosses using different resistant donors and selection cycles for each of these resistance traits. Following several recurrent selection cycles, selected watermelon lines with high fruit quality as well as low or no galling might be crossed with their sister lines selected for low egg count per gram root to develop wholly RKN‐resistant watermelon lines with high fruit quality.

There are different RKN‐resistance mechanisms in crop plants involving qualitative gene loci that control production of specific phytochemicals arresting nematode penetration and or reproduction in the root plant tissues (Wubie & Temesgen, 2019). For example, major gene loci associated with RKN resistance exist in pepper and tomato (Wubie & Temesgen, 2019). Several of these qualitative gene loci were mapped and studied in different crop plants. Among them is the Mi gene of tomato (Medina‐Filho & Tanksley, 1983) or the N, Me1, and Me3 that show different effects on penetration and reproduction of four major Meloidogyne species in pepper (Hajihassani et al., 2019). On the other hand, RKN‐resistance mechanisms controlled by multiple gene loci, like in our case here with watermelon, are not well understood. It is also difficult to identify, select, and transfer the different gene loci with small effects on the resistance trait using classical breeding or marker‐assisted selection approaches. Host plant resistance controlled by multiple gene loci is considered more durable than resistance controlled by a single gene (McDonald & Linde, 2002; Parlevliet, 2002; Poland et al., 2009). For this reason, although challenging, incorporating the polygenic RKN‐resistance trait from C. amarus germplasm into a cultivar background using “genomic selection” should have long‐term value in the development of watermelon varieties with durable resistance.

This study evaluated GP models for RKN resistance in watermelon, focusing on two key traits: percentage of root system galling and eggs per gram of root. Both traits exhibited moderate to high broad‐sense (H 2) and narrow‐sense (h 2) heritability. The close alignment between H 2 and h 2 values indicates additive genetic effects are the primary contributors, especially for eggs per gram root to phenotypic variation for M. enterolobii resistance in C. amarus genotypes, supporting the application of additive‐based genomic selection strategies (Falconer & Mackay, 1996; Hallauer et al., 2010), while for root galling there might have other effects such as dominance or epistasis alongside additive effect.

Among the tested GP models, RKHS provided the highest PA for eggs per gram of root (PA = 0.50), with an RMSE of 4771, which corresponds to only ∼3.4% of the phenotypic scale. For gall formation, RKHS achieved a PA of 0.42 and an RMSE of 0.58 (∼0.6% of scale), comparable to sGBLUP (PA = 0.42; RMSE = 2.3). These results confirm that RKHS models are well‐suited to capture complex trait architectures, including potential non‐linear and non‐additive relationships (de Los Campos et al., 2010; Pérez & de Los Campos, 2014). Sparse GBLUP using 10,000 high‐variance SNP markers showed a slight improvement for gall formation (PA = 0.44; RMSE = 2.3) but reduced PA for eggs per gram of root (PA = 0.27; RMSE = 5250), highlighting the trade‐off between marker density and PA versus computational efficiency (Heslot et al., 2012).

Comparison of GEBVs with BLUEs across different SIs showed that GEBV‐based selection was more conservative at lower thresholds but became increasingly comparable to phenotypic selection as selection pressure increased, indicating that these two RKN‐resistance traits are controlled by different mechanisms and gene loci. This trend supports earlier findings that optimizing selection thresholds can improve the efficiency of genomic selection while maintaining selection accuracy (Heffner et al., 2009; Resende et al., 2012).

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates the potential of genomic selection as a breeding tool to improve RKN resistance. Multiple gene loci control the resistance, and different resistance mechanisms could affect root galling versus egg count per gram root. Moderate heritability estimates for gall formation and eggs per gram of root support the suitability of additive‐based models for advancing these traits. Among the evaluated models, RKHS provided the strongest PA for both traits, confirming its value for capturing complex genetic architectures. Although GEBV‐based selection was more conservative at lower SIs, it became increasingly comparable to phenotypic selection as the selection threshold was relaxed, highlighting its practical utility in breeding programs.

Large training populations derived from crosses between C. amarus genotypes selected in this study are being developed and will be further evaluated using advanced modeling techniques to capture most gene loci, including these with small (additive) effects. Additionally, refining marker selection strategies and integrating machine learning methods could further improve predictive accuracy and accelerate the development of RKN‐resistant watermelon cultivars.
