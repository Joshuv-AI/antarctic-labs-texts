Environmental disclosure and supply chain carbon leakage
Authors: Mengjie Li, Weijian Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAmid global carbon goals and supply chain carbon leakage, the effectiveness of corporate environmental responsibility disclosure requires reexamination. We explore how listed companies’ environmental disclosure impacts upstream and downstream carbon emissions. Results indicate that environmental disclosure increases, rather than reduces, supply chain emissions, confirming hidden emissions under explicit corporate responsibility. Mechanistically, the scale effect acts globally, while structural and technological effects are exclusively upstream and downstream, respectively. Furthermore, upstream suppliers face dual suppression from carbon transfer via digital speech and action, whereas downstream customers are affected solely by digital action. Additionally, upstream carbon transfer targets long-term stable suppliers, while downstream transfer targets nonmajor, long-term customers. This study provides an objective basis for resolving supply chain carbon transfer dilemmas and formulating targeted emission reduction policies.

Introduction

In the context of global consensus and national strategies on carbon peak and carbon neutrality goals, enterprises’ environmental responsibility (ER) fulfillment and information disclosure, as the core subject of carbon emissions, have become key indicators for measuring sustainable development capabilities.1,2 As environmental regulations have tightened and public awareness of environmental protection has increased, firms can convey their environmental image by releasing ER reports, publicly disclosing emission reduction data, and other means. These methods meet government compliance requirements and avoid reputation risks while attracting green investment and environmentally friendly consumption, creating a competitive advantage for firm operations.3,4

However, explicit responsibilities might differ from implicit emissions in ER disclosure. In current practices, although some enterprises have built an environmentally friendly image by disclosing their own emission reduction achievements, they have outsourced high-pollution production links and transferred environmental protection costs through business adjustments upstream and downstream of the supply chain. This phenomenon has led to the transfer of carbon emissions from producing enterprises to upstream and downstream enterprises with weaker supervision or lower levels of public attention, resulting in carbon leakage at the supply chain level.5,6,7 This phenomenon violates the overall emission reduction requirements of the dual carbon goals and may hinder overall carbon emission management and control of the supply chain because of the concealment of carbon transfer. Therefore, the core concern of this paper is not whether ER disclosure improves the focal firm’s own image but whether it unintentionally masks this form of “invisible pollution” through carbon emission transfer along the supply chain.

In this context, exploring the impact of listed companies’ ER disclosure on the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises in the supply chain holds significant theoretical and practical value. From a theoretical perspective, studies focus mostly on the impact of ER disclosure on enterprises’ own financial performance and emissions while paying insufficient attention to its transmission mechanism and asymmetric effects in the supply chain; specifically, the research lacks a clear analysis of the carbon transfer pathways. From a practical perspective, clarifying the relationship between ER disclosure and supply chain carbon transfer can provide policy-makers with a basis for precise supervision and control, prevent corporate greenwashing, and promote overall emission reduction in the supply chain.

The innovations of this study are reflected in three main aspects. First, this study breaks through the current research limitation of the single-subject perspective, adopts a supply chain collaboration perspective, verifies systematically the phenomenon of implicit emissions under explicit responsibilities caused by ER disclosure, and expands the research framework on the impact of ER disclosure on the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises. Second, on the basis of the analytical framework of scale, structure, and technology, the study decomposes the mechanisms through which ER disclosure affects supply chain carbon emissions and concludes that the scale effect works across the board, the structural effect works exclusively on the upstream, and the technology effect works exclusively on the downstream, thus clarifying the specific pathways and boundary conditions of carbon leakage. Third, this study introduces two moderating variables, namely, digital transformation and supply chain characteristics; reveals the inhibitory effect of digital tools on carbon leakage and the impact of supply chain concentration and stability on the direction of carbon leakage; and provides empirical support for the formulation of targeted emission reduction policies.

The remaining structure of this paper is as follows. The literature review and research hypotheses constitute the second section. The study design—which outlines the model setting, variable definitions, and data sources—constitutes the third section. The influence of ER disclosure on the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream businesses is confirmed by a benchmark regression, robustness testing, an endogeneity analysis, and a heterogeneity analysis in the fourth section, which contains the empirical data and analysis. The fifth section provides an extended discussion, which further decomposes the internal mechanism of action and explores the moderating effects of digitalization and supply chain characteristics. The sixth section presents the main conclusions.

As a carrier through which enterprises convey their environmental protection behaviors and performance, the impact of corporate ER disclosure has become a research focus in the fields of environmental economics and corporate governance, with studies focusing mainly on its effects on enterprises.8,9 With respect to the impacts on enterprises, scholars generally believe that ER disclosure can improve corporate operations through legitimacy and resource acquisition mechanisms.10,11,12 On the one hand, ER disclosure can help enterprises meet the environmental supervision requirements of the government, respond to public environmental concerns, gain legitimacy recognition, and reduce the risks of environmental penalties and reputation losses.13,14 On the other hand, high-quality ER disclosure can convey enterprises’ environmental protection commitments to investors and consumers, attract green investment and consumption from groups with environmental preferences, and thereby enhance enterprises' market competitiveness.8,15

Another important strand of the literature that should be incorporated into this discussion is the greenwashing research. Studies suggest that corporate environmental disclosure does not always reflect substantive environmental improvement faithfully. Firms may use symbolic disclosure strategically to construct an environmentally responsible image without making corresponding operational changes.16,17 ER disclosure may serve as both an information transparency mechanism and an impression management tool.18,19 The greenwashing literature has focused mainly on the decoupling between environmental communication and actual environmental performance at the focal firm level, emphasizing how firms use selective indicators and unverifiable claims to gain legitimacy from regulators, investors, and consumers.20,21

In addition, since the dual carbon goals have been proposed, an increasing number of scholars have begun to focus on carbon leakage issues at the local and even the global level.22,23,24 Carbon leakage refers to a phenomenon in which the emission reduction actions of one entity lead to the transfer of carbon emissions to other entities, thereby resulting in no reduction or even an increase in overall carbon emissions. It occurs in supply chain scenarios through carbon transfer.25,26 Studies on supply chain carbon transfer focus mainly on transfer pathways and driving factors.27,28 With respect to transfer pathways, scholars note that carbon transfer is achieved mainly through the outsourcing of production links and cost transfer.29 On the one hand, to reduce their own carbon emissions, core enterprises will outsource high-pollution production links to upstream suppliers or reduce the production of high-carbon products and purchase finished products from downstream customers, so pollution links migrate to both ends of the supply chain.30,31,32 On the other hand, core enterprises transfer environmental protection costs to upstream and downstream enterprises by decreasing procurement prices, increasing supply standards, and other means. To control total costs, the downstream enterprises must reduce investment in low-carbon technologies and maintain high-energy-consumption production, which promotes an increase in carbon emissions indirectly.33,34,35

In terms of driving factors, studies have shown that environmental regulatory pressure and enterprises’ demand for an environmentally friendly image are key incentives for supply chain carbon transfer. When the government introduces strict environmental protection policies or when public attention to the environment increases, to avoid environmental penalties or reputation losses, core enterprises accelerate the transfer of high-pollution links to upstream and downstream enterprises with weaker supervision or less public attention.36,37 In addition, as an important means of conveying an environmentally friendly image, corporate ER disclosure may become a hidden driving factor for carbon transfer.38,39

This possibility is closely related to the literature on supply chain environmental management.40 Studies show that focal firms increasingly govern the environmental behavior of suppliers and customers through green procurement standards and environmental performance assessments.41,42 However, they may also create unequal environmental responsibilities and governance pressure across supply chain members. Because listed companies often occupy dominant positions in supply chain networks, they are better able to externalize environmental compliance costs and production risks to smaller upstream and downstream firms with weaker bargaining power and lower visibility.43,44 This stream of the literature provides an important theoretical basis for understanding why ER disclosure may not simply improve environmental performance but may also reshape the distribution of carbon emissions within the supply chain.

The literature on ER disclosure, greenwashing, carbon leakage, and supply chain environmental management suggests that firms may achieve an apparent improvement in environmental reputation through genuine emission reduction and the reallocation of environmental burdens along the supply chain. This concept implies that the environmental consequences of disclosure should be evaluated beyond firm boundaries. If the focal firm’s ER disclosure is accompanied by pollution outsourcing, cost transfer, or the suppression of green upgrading among suppliers and customers, then disclosure may function as a supply chain-enabled form of greenwashing.

Building on the literature and drawing on the classic environmental economics framework of scale, structure, and technology, this study further integrates the resource-based view, legitimacy theory, and transaction cost theory to explain how the ER disclosure of listed companies may affect the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises. Legitimacy theory helps explain why firms have incentives to strengthen environmental disclosure to obtain social approval and to reduce regulatory and reputational pressures.45,46 The resource-based view suggests that high-quality disclosure may help firms acquire valuable external resources—such as green finance, consumer support, and reputational capital—thereby expanding production and market opportunities.47,48 Transaction cost theory further indicates that under the pressure to maintain legitimacy and control costs, focal firms may reorganize transactions and shift pollution-intensive activities or environmental compliance costs to supply chain partners.49,50 On the basis of this integrated framework, this study analyzes the mechanisms through which ER disclosure influences the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream firms from the perspectives of the scale, structural, and technology effects and proposes the following hypotheses.

First, the ER disclosure of listed companies may increase the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises through the scale effect. From the perspective of the resource-based view, high-quality ER disclosure can signal strong environmental commitment and governance capability to external stakeholders, thereby helping firms obtain scarce and valuable resources, including green investment and consumer recognition. These resources enhance firms’ competitive advantage and promote the expansion of production and market scale.51,52,53 As listed companies expand production, their demand for upstream raw materials and downstream distribution or processing activities also increases. To meet this increased demand, upstream and downstream enterprises are likely to expand their own production capacities. In terms of the scale effect, production expansion generally leads to increased energy consumption and carbon emissions. Therefore, ER disclosure by listed companies may increase the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises indirectly by stimulating supply chain-wide scale expansion.

Second, the ER disclosure of listed companies may increase the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises through the structural effect. From the perspective of legitimacy theory, listed companies face strong pressure from regulators, investors, and the public to maintain a responsible environmental image. To preserve consistency between their disclosed environmental commitments and observable performance, firms may reduce their direct exposure to pollution-intensive activities within their own organizational boundaries.54,55,56 As upstream and downstream enterprises undertake these pollution-intensive links, the enterprises may increase carbon emission intensity and total emissions.

Third, the ER disclosure of listed companies may increase the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises through the technology effect. From the perspective of transaction cost theory, when listed companies increase environmental investment to improve disclosure quality and maintain legitimacy, they may attempt to control total costs by transferring part of the environmental compliance burden to upstream and downstream firms through lower procurement prices, stricter environmental standards, or more demanding contractual terms.57,58 For smaller supply chain firms with limited financial resources, such cost pressure may crowd out long-term investment in green innovation and low-carbon technology upgrades. These firms may remain locked into high-carbon production modes, making it difficult to reduce carbon emissions through technological upgrading. Therefore, ER disclosure by listed companies may inhibit green technological progress among supply chain partners and thereby increase their carbon emissions.

Hypothesis 1: Supply chain emissions may occur implicitly under the explicit responsibilities in the ER disclosure of listed companies.

Hypothesis 2: The ER disclosure of listed companies may increase the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises through the scale, structural, and technology effects.

The relatively high R-squared values in columns (2) and (4) are driven mainly by the inclusion of firm-level controls and the two-way fixed-effects structure. Carbon emissions are linked closely to persistent firm characteristics such as production scale, asset intensity, and financing conditions, whereas firm fixed effects absorb time-invariant heterogeneity across enterprises, and year fixed effects capture common macro shocks. Therefore, the higher explanatory power reflects mainly the structural and persistent nature of corporate carbon emissions rather than model overfitting. More importantly, the coefficient on ER remains significantly positive after these controls are introduced, which supports the robustness of the benchmark conclusion.

In addition, in the benchmark analysis, the core explanatory variable adopts a comprehensive indicator of corporate ER disclosure. Although this indicator can well reflect the fulfillment of corporate environmental responsibilities, it may be affected by enterprises’ disclosure strategies and thus be subjective. To reduce information bias, in columns (3) and (4) of Table 2, the explanatory variable is replaced with a more objective dummy variable for environmental management system certification. The results show that the coefficients of the core explanatory variable are all significantly positive, indicating that when the more objective method of environmental certification is used to replace the comprehensive disclosure indicator, the positive impact of the ER level of listed companies on the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises remains significant.

To further rule out the interference of sample selection bias on the benchmark conclusions, this study conducts robustness tests by adjusting the research sample in three ways: using alternative matching algorithms, focusing on core supply chain relationships, and excluding unstable cooperative relationships. The findings are presented in Table 3.

In the benchmark analysis, the upstream and downstream enterprises of listed companies are identified through fuzzy matching. This method helps improve sample coverage but may also lead to matching errors and thus affect the estimation results. Therefore, in columns (1) and (2) of Table 3, the benchmark sample is replaced with the sample obtained through exact matching, and no fuzzy-matching procedure is applied. The results show that even when only the precisely matched upstream and downstream enterprises are retained, the improvement in the ER score of listed companies still increases their carbon emissions significantly.

In the benchmark analysis, the research sample includes the top 5 major upstream and downstream cooperative enterprises of listed companies. However, the degree of connection varies between different cooperative enterprises and listed companies, which may dilute the true impact of the core variable. Therefore, in columns (1) and (2), the sample is restricted to the largest supplier and the largest customer enterprises in the supply chain of listed companies. The results show that even when the focus is only on the core upstream and downstream enterprises that are connected most closely to listed companies, the improvement in the ER score of listed companies still increases their carbon emissions significantly. These results confirm that the benchmark conclusions are not driven by small-scale, weakly connected upstream and downstream enterprises and that the positive impact of listed companies’ ER on supply chain carbon emissions is derived mainly from core cooperative enterprises, which further confirms that the two share a causal relationship.

In addition, the sample used in the benchmark analysis may include upstream and downstream enterprises that collaborated with listed companies only once within the sample period before terminating the partnership. These enterprises have short cooperation cycles and poor business stability, and their carbon emission levels are more likely to be influenced by their own short-term operational fluctuations than they are by the ER practices of listed companies. Therefore, in columns (3) and (4), the sample is restricted to enterprises that maintained continuous cooperation with listed companies throughout the sample period, excluding unstable samples involving only one-time cooperation. The results show that the impact direction and the significance of the ER score ER of listed companies on the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises are consistent with those of the benchmark analysis. This finding indicates that the impact of listed companies’ ER remains robust under stable cooperative relationships, further ruling out the possibility that the benchmark conclusions are driven by short-term, incidental cooperation.

Endogeneity analysis

The benchmark regression assumes that the ER score of listed companies affects the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises. However, reverse causality may exist: the high carbon emission behavior of upstream and downstream enterprises might force listed companies to improve their ER levels. Endogeneity problems can lead to biases in the estimation of the core coefficient, making an accurate reflection of the causal relationship impossible. To address this point, this study constructs instrumental variables and uses the two-stage least squares (2SLS) method to reestimate the impact of ER disclosure on the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises in the supply chain.

In columns (1) and (3) of Table 4, public environmental attention in the regions where listed companies are located is used as the instrumental variable and is measured on the basis of the annual Baidu search index related to haze. When the public in a given region pays greater attention to environmental issues, local firms face greater pressure from public opinion, media scrutiny, and stakeholder monitoring. In response, listed companies are more likely to strengthen both the fulfillment and the disclosure of their environmental responsibilities to maintain legitimacy and protect their public image. Therefore, regional public environmental attention is expected to be correlated positively with the ER disclosure of listed companies, satisfying the relevance condition of the instrumental variable. The exclusion restriction can also be reasonably defended. Regional public environmental attention reflects mainly the intensity of local public concern about environmental quality rather than the production decisions or energy use behavior of upstream suppliers and downstream customers. Its primary effect is to increase the reputational and legitimacy pressure faced by the focal listed companies, which then induces changes in disclosure behavior. In contrast, the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream partner firms are determined more directly by their own production technologies, energy structures, contractual arrangements, and supply chain positions. In columns (2) and (4) of Table 4, government environmental attention in the regions where listed companies are located is used as the instrumental variable and is measured as the proportion of environment-related word frequency in the annual government work reports of those regions. When local governments place greater emphasis on environmental issues in official policy documents, environmental governance becomes a more salient policy objective, and listed companies are more likely to become key targets of environmental supervision. To respond to stricter policy expectations, reduce regulatory risk, and maintain political legitimacy, listed companies are more likely to strengthen the fulfillment and disclosure of ER. Therefore, regional government environmental attention is expected to be correlated positively with the ER disclosure of listed companies, satisfying the relevance condition of the instrumental variable. The exclusion restriction can also be reasonably supported. As captured by the wording of government work reports, regional government environmental attention primarily reflects the local government’s macrolevel policy orientation toward environmental governance rather than direct intervention in the production or energy use decisions of specific upstream suppliers and downstream customers. The most immediate effect is to alter the regulatory expectations and legitimacy pressures faced by listed companies located in that region, thereby influencing their disclosure behavior.

The instrumental variable results reported in Table 4 show that, regardless of whether public environmental attention or government environmental attention is employed as the instrument, ER significantly and positively affects the carbon emissions of both upstream suppliers and downstream customers, and the coefficient signs remain consistent with the baseline findings. This finding suggests that the core conclusion still holds after controlling for potential endogeneity. The first-stage results are in line with expectations. Both public environmental attention and government environmental attention are correlated with ER significantly and positively, providing strong evidence for the relevance of the instruments. Furthermore, the corresponding first-stage F statistics are all above the conventional threshold, indicating that weak-instrument concerns are unlikely to affect the estimation results.

To further address potential endogeneity concerns, this study employs system generalized method of moments (SGMMs) and difference generalized method of moment (DGMM) estimators, as reported in Table 5. These dynamic panel techniques are well-suited for controlling for unobserved heterogeneity, potential reverse causality, and the persistence of carbon emissions over time. The empirical results show that the coefficients of the first-order lagged core explanatory variable, L.ER, are all significantly positive in both the supplier and customer regressions, indicating that corporate environmental disclosure has a significant positive effect on the carbon emissions of upstream suppliers and downstream customers. To ensure the reliability of the estimations, we further conduct a series of postestimation diagnostic tests, including serial correlation and overidentification tests. The baseline analysis and robustness tests verify that listed companies with greater ER scores have upstream and downstream enterprises with greater carbon emissions. However, this heterogeneity may be due to differences in industry characteristics and regional characteristics. These differences may lead to variations in the intensity and significance of the impact of listed companies’ ER disclosure on the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises. These heterogeneous results may be due to differences in production models and energy dependence between manufacturing and nonmanufacturing enterprises. The core production links of manufacturing enterprises require a significant consumption of high-carbon energy, so the impact of environmental protection investment on production costs is far greater for manufacturing than it is for nonmanufacturing enterprises. When listed companies enhance ER disclosure and transfer environmental protection costs, upstream and downstream manufacturing enterprises are more inclined to cut environmental protection expenditures to maintain profits, leading to a significant increase in carbon emissions.

Figure 2 presents the results grouped by whether the listed companies and their upstream/downstream enterprises are located in the same city. The results of Figure 2 indicate that listed companies with higher ER scores have supplier enterprises located in different cities with more significant carbon emission increases; in contrast, the increase is more pronounced in the carbon emissions of the listed companies’ customer enterprises located in the same city. These heterogeneous results may be due to the impact of geographical distance on the spillover effect of environmental supervision and supply chain collaboration costs. Suppliers in the same city as listed companies are supervised by the same local environmental protection department. The high ER score of listed companies may drive local supervision departments to simultaneously increase environmental protection requirements for local suppliers, forcing local suppliers to increase environmental protection investment and offset part of the growth in carbon emissions. Customers in the same city have higher degrees of supply chain collaboration efficiency with listed companies. The scale expansion introduced by the improvement of listed companies’ ER disclosure can be transmitted to local customers more quickly, making it easier for local customers to expand production capacity to meet demand, thereby increasing carbon emissions.

To meet this scale expansion, upstream and downstream enterprises need to expand their production capacity simultaneously, which ultimately increases carbon emissions. The introduction of enterprise digitalization factors

Digital transformation has become a key means for enterprises to optimize resource allocation and improve environmental protection efficiency. The digitalization of listed companies may change the transmission path of their ER behaviors. If the digitalization factor is ignored, it may be impossible to fully reveal the boundary conditions of how ER disclosure affects carbon emissions, thereby affecting the applicability of the conclusions. To test the moderating effect of the digitalization factor of listed companies, this study constructs an interaction term between ER disclosure and the digitalization index and incorporates it into the benchmark regression model. The digitalization index of listed companies is constructed from two dimensions. The proportion of digital-related word frequency in the total word frequency of listed companies’ annual reports is used to measure the digital discourse of the listed companies. The annual digital patent applications of listed companies are used to measure their digital actions. These two indicators are intended to capture different layers of firm digitalization. Digital speech reflects the symbolic, strategic, and communicative dimension of digital transformation, which indicates the extent to which focal firms publicly emphasize digital development in annual reports, thereby conveying managerial attention, strategic orientation, and external commitment to digital upgrading. Digital action reflects the substantive and implementation-oriented dimensions of digitalization. Digital patent applications proxy the extent to which firms convert digital strategy into concrete technological innovation, organizational investment, and operational capability. Digital speech captures mainly a firm’s expressed digital orientation, whereas digital action captures its realized digital capability.

According to the results in columns (1) and (2) of Table 9, the moderating effects of the two types of digitalization indicators are both significantly negative. That is, regardless of whether it is at the level of discourse or action, the digitalization of listed companies can effectively weaken the transfer effect of ER disclosure on the carbon emissions of upstream supplier enterprises. A plausible reason is that upstream suppliers are typically more sensitive to the focal firm’s monitoring signals, compliance expectations, and governance requirements. When a listed company stresses digital transformation frequently in its annual reports, suppliers may interpret this information as a signal that the buyer is upgrading its management systems, traceability standards, and environmental supervision capacity. Even if such digitalization is still largely discursive, it can strengthen suppliers’ expectations of stricter screening, performance evaluation, and information disclosure requirements. As a result, suppliers have incentives to adjust production behaviors and reduce carbon-intensive responses to preserve long-term business relationships. Digital action reinforces this effect further because substantive digital innovation enables focal firms to develop supplier-management tools that are more effective. Compared with digital speech alone, digital action provides a stronger operational basis for coordinating environmental targets and mitigating one-sided cost shifting to upstream firms, which helps explain why both digital speech and digital action exhibit significant moderating effects in the supplier sample.

The data in columns (3) and (4) of Table 9 indicate that digital actions can effectively weaken the transfer effect of ER disclosure on customer enterprises’ carbon emissions. However, merely claiming to pursue digitalization in annual reports fails to inhibit this transfer effect. In other words, the moderating effect of digitalization on downstream customer enterprises is characterized by effective actions but ineffective discourse. The asymmetry between upstream and downstream partners may stem from their different positions in the supply chain and the different channels through which digitalization generates influence. Downstream customers are generally less affected by the focal firm’s strategic statements alone because the carbon-emission decisions of such customers depend more directly on whether they can obtain measurable efficiency gains, decreased coordination costs, or deployable digital tools in production and distribution. Therefore, digital speech may be insufficient to alter the cost‒benefit calculus of downstream firms, whereas digital action can effectively support data interconnection, demand forecasting, logistics optimization, and process coordination. These practical digital applications decrease adjustment costs for customers and weaken the incentive to pass environmental costs into carbon-intensive operating responses. This mechanism is consistent with the empirical finding that only digital action has a significant moderating effect for customer enterprises.

Discussion

Main conclusions

This study takes listed companies and their upstream and downstream associated enterprises as the research sample, focuses on the core contradiction of invisible supply chain emissions under explicit responsibility, and explores systematically the impact of listed companies’ ER disclosure on the carbon emissions of upstream suppliers and downstream customer enterprises. The study decomposes the internal mechanism from three effect perspectives—scale, structural, and technological—while examining the moderating roles of digitalization and supply chain characteristics. This research aims to provide an objective basis for resolving the dilemma of supply chain carbon transfer. The main conclusions are as follows. First, the ER disclosure of listed companies does not drive overall emission reduction in the supply chain; instead, it increases the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises through carbon transfer. This phenomenon confirms the existence of invisible supply chain emissions under conditions of explicit responsibility. Second, the internal mechanism through which ER disclosure affects the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises shows different effects: the scale effect works across the board, the structural effect works exclusively on upstream enterprises, and the technological effect works exclusively on downstream enterprises. Third, for upstream suppliers, both digital discourse and digital actions can inhibit carbon transfer; for downstream customers, only digital actions are effective, whereas digital discourse has no significant effect because of the lack of practical value. Fourth, upstream carbon transfer tends to target stable suppliers with long-term cooperative relationships, whereas downstream carbon transfer tends to target both noncore customers with low supply chain concentration and customers with long-term cooperative relationships.

Policy implications

On the basis of the main findings of this study, several policy implications that are more targeted can be derived. First, regulators should strengthen disclosure requirements concerning supply chain carbon transfer in corporate ER reports. Listed companies should be required to report separately whether carbon-intensive production links have been outsourced to upstream or downstream partners, how environmental compliance costs are allocated along the supply chain, and whether the firm has adopted collaborative rather than unilateral emission reduction arrangements. In addition, supply chain carbon coordination should be incorporated into the core indicators of ESG evaluation. More importantly, regulators should distinguish between substantive digital actions and symbolic digital rhetoric. Therefore, digital disclosure should be assessed on the basis not only of textual statements, slogans, or strategic commitments but also of verifiable evidence regarding actual digital deployment and collaborative carbon management practices. In this way, digital transformation can be evaluated according to whether it genuinely improves carbon governance across the supply chain rather than merely enhancing the focal firm’s public image. Second, differentiated policy support should be designed for upstream and downstream firms in accordance with the heterogeneous mechanisms identified in this study. For upstream suppliers, for whom the structural effect is more pronounced, policy priority should be given to supporting low-carbon upgrading in pollution intensive intermediate production and processing links, especially for high-energy-consumption activities that are easily transferred from focal firms. For downstream customers, for whom the technology effect is more significant and digital actions are more effective, policy support should focus on strengthening green innovation capacity. In particular, governments and financial institutions could jointly establish supply chain green innovation funds to compensate for the crowding out of R&D investment caused by cost transfer and to support low-carbon innovation in logistics, warehousing, distribution, and process optimization. Third, incentive mechanisms should be designed to encourage focal firms and their supply chain partners to coinvest in decarbonization rather than simply shift environmental costs. Matching subsidy schemes or tax credit programs could be established for joint low-carbon investment projects undertaken by listed companies together with upstream suppliers or downstream customers. Eligibility for such support should be linked to transparent cost-sharing arrangements, verifiable technology deployment, and measurable carbon-reduction outcomes. Regulators may also encourage firms to build shared digital collaboration platforms for carbon accounting, emissions tracking, green procurement, and technology diffusion so that low-carbon governance becomes embedded in interfirm coordination rather than remaining an internal compliance issue of the focal firm. Fourth, given that carbon leakage is more likely to occur within long-term and stable partnerships, regulatory and contractual governance should pay particular attention to such relationship-specific supply chain ties. For long-term upstream suppliers and downstream customers, firms should be encouraged to sign green supply chain agreements that clearly define carbon reduction responsibilities, data-sharing obligations, and environmental cost-sharing rules. In addition, environmental performance indicators could be linked to the renewal of long-term procurement contracts, preferred supplier status, the scale of customer cooperation, and access to green finance. Such arrangements would help transform long-term business relationships from channels of hidden carbon transfer into mechanisms for stable collaborative decarbonization. Finally, digital governance should be promoted as an operational tool for improving downstream carbon management and reducing supply chain greenwashing. Since this study reveals that digital actions are especially effective for downstream customers, policy design should place greater emphasis on the practical application of digital technologies in downstream carbon governance. This work includes encouraging listed companies to provide downstream firms with access to digital carbon-calculation systems, joint emission databases, smart logistics optimization tools, and online platforms for low-carbon technology sharing. Moreover, verification mechanisms should be developed to ensure that digital environmental claims are consistent with actual technological deployment and carbon reduction outcomes. Only when digital measures are observable, traceable, and auditable can they function as genuine governance instruments rather than as another form of symbolic environmental signaling.

Limitations of the study

This study has the following limitations that require further in-depth research in the future. First, the calculation of carbon emissions in this study relies on energy consumption data and does not cover the implicit carbon emissions throughout the entire life cycle of enterprises’ products, which may lead to an underestimation of the actual scale of the carbon transfer. Therefore, an important direction for future research is to find ways to improve the full-lifecycle carbon emission data by integrating enterprises’ carbon footprint reports and identifying the causal effect of ER disclosure on the supply chain carbon transfer research more accurately. Second, this study does not explore the moderating role of industrial policies on carbon transfer deeply, nor does it distinguish the differences in carbon transfer effects among different types of ER disclosure. Thus, future research should investigate further the inhibitory effect of carbon market policies and green financial instruments on supply chain carbon transfer by comparing the differences in carbon transfer between mandatory and voluntary environmental disclosure policies and refining the applicable boundaries of the conclusions. Finally, our data are from 2007 to 2016, but China’s environmental governance system changed substantially after 2016. In particular, the Central Environmental Inspection became an important nationwide accountability mechanism from 2016 onward, and China’s national emissions trading market officially began trading in July 2021 after several years of pilot exploration. These institutional changes likely strengthened environmental enforcement, increased the cost of carbon-intensive production, and altered the incentives and constraints surrounding both environmental disclosure and supply chain carbon transfer. The magnitude, channels, and even boundary conditions of the carbon leakage effect documented in this paper may differ under the current policy regime. Therefore, an important direction for future research is to extend the sample to more recent years and examine whether stronger environmental inspections, the expansion of carbon pricing, and the broader dual carbon policy framework have weakened, reinforced, or reshaped the supply chain greenwashing and carbon leakage mechanisms identified in this study.

Method details

To test the impact of environmental responsibility disclosure on the carbon emissions of upstream and downstream enterprises, benchmark analysis models are constructed, as shown in Equations 1 and 2.(Equation 1)Csupplierit=α1+β1ERft+γ1Xit+σ1i+θ1t+μ1it(Equation2)Ccustomerjt=α2+β2ERft+γ2Xjt+σ2j+θ2t+μ2jt

where f represents focal enterprises in the supply chain, i represents upstream enterprises, j represents downstream enterprises, and t represents the year. Csupplier denotes the carbon emissions of upstream supplier enterprises, and Ccustomer denotes the carbon emissions of downstream customer enterprises. ER represents the environmental responsibility score disclosed by focal enterprises in the supply chain. σ represents the individual fixed effect, θ represents the time fixed effect, and μ represents the random error term.

Specifically, the carbon emissions of upstream (downstream) supplier (customer) enterprises Csupplier (Ccustomer) are calculated using the emission factor method. The calculation is based on the consumption of primary energy sources such as coal, oil, and natural gas as well as secondary energy sources such as electricity, during the enterprise production process. The model also references Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories compiled by the IPCC and Methods for Estimating Carbon Dioxide Emission Reductions and Parameter Selection issued by the National Development and Reform Commission. The relevant data are sourced from the China Enterprise Pollution Emission Database and the China Tax Survey Database.

The environmental responsibility disclosure score of focal firms in the supply chain is measured by the environmental responsibility subscore from Hexun’s CSR rating system. Hexun’s evaluation framework assesses listed companies’ CSR performance through a multilevel indicator system and regards environmental responsibility as one of the five core first-level dimensions. According to the publicly available methodology, the environmental responsibility dimension mainly covers disclosed information on environmental awareness, environmental management system certification, environmental protection investment, the number of pollutant discharge categories, and the number of energy-saving categories. The relevant information is collected primarily from firms’ publicly disclosed CSR reports and annual reports. In addition, Hexun applies industry-differentiated weights at the first-level category level when constructing the overall CSR score. Under the default scheme, environmental responsibility accounts for 20% of the total CSR score, whereas the weight is increased to 30% for manufacturing firms and reduced to 10% for service firms. Since this study uses the environmental responsibility subscore rather than the overall CSR score, the variable captures firms’ environmental responsibility disclosure more directly.

X represents the control variables, which are specified as follows. The enterprise size variable Scale is measured by the number of employees at the end of the enterprise’s fiscal year. The fixed asset ratio variable FA is measured as the ratio of an enterprise’s fixed assets to its total assets. The financing constraint variable FC is measured as the ratio of an enterprise’s interest expenses to its fixed assets. The subsidy income variable Subsidy is a binary variable indicating whether an enterprise received subsidy income in the current year and takes a value of 1 if subsidy income exists and 0 otherwise. The enterprise export variable Export is a binary dummy variable measuring whether an enterprise engages in export activities and takes a value of 1 if the enterprise has export delivery value in the current year and 0 otherwise. The listed company indicator variable LC is determined by whether a supplier or customer enterprise is a listed company and takes a value of 1 if it is a listed company and 0 otherwise.

Data sources

The data in this paper are sourced mainly from the matched data of the China Enterprise Pollution Emission Database, China Tax Survey Database, Hexun.com, CSMAR, and China Research Data Platform. Centered on the monitoring of enterprise-level environmental behavior, the China Enterprise Pollution Emission Database covers fully the annual emission volume, emission concentration, and operation data of treatment facilities for enterprises’ characteristic pollutants while linking synchronously production and operation information related directly to pollution generation. The data come from the cross-verification of onsite monitoring by environmental supervision departments and enterprise pollution discharge declarations, providing a foundation for the estimation of corporate carbon emissions in this study. Organized and managed uniformly by the State Taxation Administration, the China Tax Survey Database includes detailed tax declaration data and the matched operation information of enterprises of various ownership types. This database serves as a source for indicators such as the financial status and operational stability of supplier and customer enterprises in the control variable system of this paper and supports the accounting of enterprises’ primary and secondary energy consumption. As an authoritative Chinese financial information platform, Hexun.com has compiled a database of listed companies’ social responsibility reports, which collects systematically specific corporate environmental information disclosure content, making it possible for this study to analyze the environmental responsibility disclosure behavior of enterprises. The CSMAR collects systematically the basic registration information, annual operation details, and multidimensional financial indicators of listed companies, providing basic support for the research on corporate growth dynamics, innovation capability evaluation, and strategic decision-making mechanisms. In addition, the China Research Data Platform focuses on the research on enterprise supply chain relationships. It included a dataset of the supply and marketing networks of listed companies and records in detail key information, such as the industrial and commercial registration information of the top five suppliers and customers of domestic listed companies, the proportion of cooperation amount, and the duration of cooperation.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data matching, cleaning, and statistical analyses were performed using Stata 15.0. In all statistical analyses, significance levels are indicated by asterisks, where ∗ denotes p < 0.10, ∗∗ denotes p < 0.05, and ∗∗∗ denotes p < 0.01. Statistical analyses were conducted using the matched panel datasets comprising 4,533 listed company-upstream supplier observations and 9,351 listed company-downstream customer observations from 2007 to 2016. N refers to the number of matched firm-year observations in the corresponding listed company-supplier or listed company-customer panel. Variable-specific N values are reported in the descriptive statistics table and in the corresponding empirical tables.

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed to summarize the distribution of the core and control variables. For descriptive statistics, the center is reported as the mean, and dispersion is reported as the standard deviation. Regression-based analyses were used to examine the association between corporate environmental responsibility disclosure and supply chain carbon transfer. Specifically, fixed-effects models were used for the analyses reported in Tables 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10, whereas two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimation was used for the endogeneity analyses reported in Tables 4 and 5. For inferential analyses, the statistical test used, exact sample size, coefficient estimates, standard errors, fixed effects, control variables, and significance levels are reported in the corresponding tables or figure legends. Statistical tests were two-sided, and precision was assessed using standard errors.

On the basis of the aforementioned databases, this study conducted data matching and cleaning procedures, with the following specific operations. First, the industrial and commercial registration data of target listed companies were retrieved and crawled through the Tianyancha platform, with a focus on processing identification information such as enterprise legal person codes and full enterprise names. To address issues related to abbreviations, former names, and differences in administrative division suffixes in enterprise names, a unified standard was established by combining manual verification with fuzzy keyword matching. Second, enterprise names, legal person codes, and observation years as matching keys were used to match the dataset of the top five suppliers and customers of listed companies from the China Research Data Platform with the China Tax Survey Database. Records of suppliers and customer enterprises that had supply–demand relationships with listed companies were screened from the tax database through exact matching. In addition, for samples for which initial matching failed, secondary supplementary matching was conducted using approximate enterprise name matching, supplemented by verification with city location and industry codes. Ultimately, panel data were compiled for listed company—upstream suppliers and listed company—downstream customers. Finally, to further decrease the missing rate of data for the core variables, this study incorporated data from the China Enterprise Pollution Emission Database. Enterprise names and observation years were used as the matching basis, and the aforementioned matched data were matched supplementarily with data such as the fossil energy consumption of enterprises from this database.

The sample period is appropriate for the purposes of this study for two main reasons. First, it captures a relatively coherent institutional stage in which corporate environmental responsibility disclosure was developing rapidly but at a time when China’s climate and environmental governance had not yet fully entered the more stringent policy regime shaped by the full rollout of the Central Environmental Inspection and the nationwide carbon market. This timing makes the period particularly suitable for identifying the underlying supply chain carbon leakage effect associated with environmental disclosure under a comparatively less binding and more heterogeneous regulatory environment. Second, although some regional carbon trading pilots were introduced beginning in 2011, the national emissions trading market was not launched officially until July 2021, and the first round of Central Environmental Inspection was initiated fully only in July 2016. Therefore, the 2007–2016 window can be regarded as a prestrengthening policy period, which helps isolate the basic relationship between environmental responsibility disclosure and supply chain carbon transfer before major nationwide institutional shocks changed firms’ regulatory constraints and compliance incentives substantially.
