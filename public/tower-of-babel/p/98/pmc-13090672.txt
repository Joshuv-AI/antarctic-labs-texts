Pueraria flavones attenuate DSS-induced colitis by regulating the microbiota-bile acid-FXR/TGR5 axis and suppressing mtDNA-cGAS-STING signaling
Authors: Liheng Kang, Chunsheng Feng, Ziyi Tang, Wanping Gu, Ying Wang, Aiyue Pei, Meihua Piao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUlcerative colitis (UC) is characterized by inflammation and impaired barrier function. Pueraria flavones (PFs) possess anti-inflammatory properties, though their underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study assessed PFs in a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis mouse model. PFs alleviated colitis symptoms, including weight loss and colon shortening, in a dose-dependent manner, while restoring barrier integrity and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines. Mechanistically, PFs inhibited cGAS-STING activation by decreasing mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release, thereby limiting cytokine production. Furthermore, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) confirmed that PFs exert protective effects through the modulation of the gut microbiota. Metabolomic analysis revealed that PFs increased bile acids, such as tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA) and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), which activated the farnesoid X receptor (FXR)/TGR5 signaling pathway to enhance barrier function. Collectively, these findings suggest that PFs mitigate colitis by remodeling the microbiota-bile acid-FXR axis and suppressing mtDNA-cGAS-STING signaling, presenting a promising therapeutic approach for UC.Subject areas: Immunology, Medicinal and aromatic plants

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), primarily encompassing ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), is characterized by chronic, relapsing inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract, manifesting as diarrhea, abdominal pain, and bloody stools.1,2 Current therapeutic approaches for IBD primarily involve aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, biologics, and immunosuppressants. However, these treatments are often hindered by adverse effects, the development of drug resistance, and significant interindividual variability in efficacy.3,4,5 In recent years, growing attention has been directed toward the potential use of natural products in the prevention and treatment of IBD. Increasing evidence suggests that natural products exert multi-target effects, exhibit low toxicity, and improve disease outcomes by modulating intestinal barrier integrity and inflammatory responses.6,7,8 Therefore, a comprehensive investigation into the potential and mechanisms of natural products as novel IBD therapies is crucial, providing new insights and treatment strategies.

Intestinal barrier dysfunction has been identified as a critical pathological feature in IBD, playing a central role in disease initiation and the perpetuation of chronic inflammation.9,10 Disruption of the intestinal barrier facilitates the penetration of pathogens, toxins, and antigens from the lumen, triggering the activation of the mucosal immune system and exacerbating local inflammation.11,12 Recent studies highlight that mitochondrial dysfunction and the abnormal release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytosol act as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), activating the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon gene (STING) signaling pathway. This amplification of type I interferon responses and pro-inflammatory cytokine production further contributes to inflammation.13 Dysregulated activation of the cGAS-STING-mtDNA axis has been implicated in intestinal inflammation and barrier damage, making it a potential novel therapeutic target in IBD.14

FXR, a bile acid-activated nuclear receptor, is highly expressed in the ileum and colon.15 Activation of FXR has been shown to effectively downregulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, inhibit immune cell infiltration, and promote the expression of tight junction (TJ) proteins such as ZO-1 and Occludin, thereby maintaining intestinal barrier integrity.16,17,18 Consequently, targeting the FXR signaling pathway has emerged as a promising strategy for restoring barrier function and treating the underlying pathogenesis of IBD.

Pueraria flavones (PFs), the principal active compounds of Pueraria lobata, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb, are naturally occurring isoflavones.19 PFs have been shown to protect pancreatic islet function and vascular endothelium by inhibiting inflammation and oxidative stress (OS) in diabetes and its complications.20 In models of neurodegenerative diseases, PFs alleviate neuronal injury through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms.21 These findings suggest that PFs possess broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory properties and a favorable safety profile, supporting their potential as a natural therapeutic agent for the prevention and treatment of IBD and other inflammation-related disorders.

Results

PFs ameliorate inflammation and tissue injury in DSS-induced colitis

The experimental design is outlined in Figure 1A. BW monitoring revealed a significant decrease in BW in the DSS group compared to the control group, while treatment with low, medium, or high doses of PFs, as well as 5-ASA, notably alleviated DSS-induced weight loss (Figure 1B). Similarly, the DAI was significantly elevated in the DSS group compared to the control group, whereas PFs at all tested doses and 5-ASA significantly reduced the DAI scores (Figure 1C). Colon length measurements showed significant shortening in the DSS group, which was markedly reversed by PFs and 5-ASA treatment (Figures 1D and 1E). Histological analysis demonstrated severe mucosal damage and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration in the DSS group, accompanied by a significant increase in histopathological scores. Treatment with PFs improved tissue damage, restored epithelial integrity, and reduced histological scores (Figures 1F and 1G). Furthermore, pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were significantly elevated in the DSS group, while PF treatment significantly suppressed their levels (Figures 1H–1J). These results suggest that PFs exert potent anti-inflammatory and gut-protective effects, effectively alleviating DSS-induced colonic injury.

PFs restore epithelial barrier damage in DSS-induced colitis in mice

To assess the impact of PFs on intestinal barrier integrity, the expression levels of TJ proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-3 in mouse colonic tissue were analyzed. Western blot analysis showed a significant reduction in ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-3 protein levels in the DSS group compared to the control group. However, high-dose PF treatment significantly upregulated the expression of these TJ proteins, with an effect comparable to that of 5-ASA (Figures 2A–2D). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed that the expression of ZO-1 and Occludin in the colonic epithelium was markedly reduced in the DSS group, indicating substantial disruption of the intestinal barrier. In contrast, PF treatment at all tested doses effectively restored the expression and localization of TJ proteins, with the high-dose group showing the most pronounced improvement (Figures 2E and S1). These results suggest that PFs ameliorate DSS-induced epithelial barrier damage by upregulating TJ protein expression and preserving epithelial structure.

PFs attenuate DSS-induced activation of the cGAS-STING pathway and oxidative stress in colonic tissue

To further investigate the mechanisms by which PFs protect against intestinal inflammation, colonic tissues from DSS-induced colitis mice were examined. DSS significantly increased the expression of iNOS and COX2, two key inflammatory mediators, while PF administration notably reduced their levels (Figures 3A–3C). Given the close association between colitis progression and OS, redox status was assessed next. Compared to the Control group, the DSS group exhibited decreased total antioxidant capacity (FRAP), a reduced GSH/GSSG ratio, increased MDA levels, and reduced CAT activity. PF treatment largely corrected these abnormalities, suggesting a restoration of redox balance (Figures 3D–3G). The cGAS-STING pathway, which senses cytosolic DNA and amplifies downstream inflammatory signaling in colitis, was then evaluated. DSS markedly upregulated the expression of cGAS, STING, and phosphorylated IRF3, whereas PF treatment significantly attenuated these increases (Figures 3H–3K). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed robust induction of p-IRF3 in DSS-treated colons, which was markedly diminished after PF administration (Figures 3L and S2). These results suggest that PFs mitigate DSS-induced colonic injury, at least in part, by inhibiting cGAS-STING-driven inflammation and restoring oxidative homeostasis.

PFs attenuate LPS-induced activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in Caco-2 cells

To explore the protective effects of PFs at the cellular level, Caco-2 cells were stimulated with LPS. LPS significantly increased the expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, while PF treatment dose-dependently suppressed these cytokines (Figure 4A). Western blot analysis revealed that LPS enhanced cGAS, STING, and phosphorylated IRF3 expression, whereas PF treatment attenuated these increases, with quantitative analysis confirming reductions in cGAS, STING, and p-IRF3/IRF3 levels (Figures 4B–4E). LPS also significantly promoted the release of mtDNA, as indicated by elevated levels of both total and cytosolic mtDNA. However, PF treatment significantly inhibited mtDNA release (Figures 4F and 4G). LPS-induced OS, as reflected by a reduced GSH/GSSG ratio, elevated MDA levels, and decreased CAT activity, was significantly alleviated by PF treatment. The GSH/GSSG ratio was restored to normal, MDA levels were markedly reduced, and CAT activity was significantly increased (Figures 4H–4J). Collectively, these results indicate that PFs alleviate LPS-induced inflammation by reducing cGAS-STING activation triggered by mtDNA release and improving redox balance.

PFs improve gut microbiota composition and diversity in DSS-induced colitis mice

Venn diagram analysis revealed that the control, DSS, and DSS + PF groups shared 328 OTUs, with 289, 301, and 93 unique OTUs, respectively (Figure 5A). Rarefaction curve analysis confirmed that the sequencing depth was sufficient to capture the majority of species (Figure 5B). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) indicated distinct differences in gut microbial community structure among the three groups. The DSS group was clearly separated from the control group, while the PF-treated group displayed partial restoration of the microbial structure toward that of the control group (Figures 5C and 5D). Alpha diversity analysis revealed that, compared to the control group, the DSS group had significantly lower Shannon and Chao1 indices and a significantly higher Simpson index, indicating reduced microbial diversity and community evenness (Figures 5E–5G). PF treatment significantly increased the Shannon and Chao1 indices and reduced the Simpson index, reversing the DSS-induced loss of microbial diversity.

Taxonomic composition analysis showed that DSS-induced colitis significantly altered the gut microbiota at the phylum, family, and genus levels, with a reduction in the relative abundance of Firmicutes and an increase in Bacteroidetes. PF treatment partially restored the microbial composition (Figures 5H–5J). LEfSe analysis further identified significantly different taxa, revealing that the DSS group had an increased abundance of Muribaculaceae and a decreased abundance of Lactobacillaceae. In contrast, PF treatment enriched beneficial taxa, such as Lactobacillus, while reducing the abundance of potentially harmful taxa (Figures 5K–5L).

PF-altered microbiota alleviates DSS-induced colitis and restores intestinal barrier function in mice

To determine whether the protective effects of PFs on colitis are mediated through the modulation of the gut microbiota, an FMT experiment was conducted, in which fecal microbiota from PF-treated mice were transplanted into DSS-induced colitis mice (Figure 6A). BW monitoring (Figure 6B) revealed that mice in the DSS-FMT group exhibited continuous weight loss compared to the control group, while weight loss was significantly attenuated in the DSS + PFs (100 mg/kg)-FMT group. Both the DAI and histological scores were markedly elevated in the DSS-FMT group, indicating severe inflammation, whereas these scores were significantly reduced in the DSS + PFs (100 mg/kg)-FMT group (Figures 6C–6F and 6G). Colon length measurement showed significant shortening in the DSS-FMT group, which was notably restored in the DSS + PFs (100 mg/kg)-FMT group (Figures 6D and 6E). Inflammatory cytokine assays revealed significantly increased levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the DSS-FMT group, which were significantly reduced in the DSS + PFs (100 mg/kg)-FMT group (Figures 6H–6J). Similarly, MPO activity was significantly elevated in the DSS-FMT group and markedly reduced in the DSS + PFs (100 mg/kg)-FMT group (Figure 6K). Western blot analysis demonstrated that, compared to the control group, the expression levels of TJ proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-3 were significantly downregulated in the DSS-FMT group. Following DSS + PFs (100 mg/kg)-FMT treatment, the expression levels of ZO-1 and Claudin-3 were markedly increased, showing a clear recovery trend, while Occludin expression remained unchanged (Figures 6L–6O). Immunofluorescence staining further revealed weakened epithelial ZO-1 and Occludin signals in the DSS-FMT group, which were significantly enhanced in the DSS + PFs (100 mg/kg)-FMT group (Figures 6P and S3). These results suggest that PFs alleviate DSS-induced inflammatory responses and barrier damage, at least in part, through the modulation of the gut microbiota.

PFs restore bile acid homeostasis in DSS-induced colitis

Metabolomic analysis showed that the bile acid metabolic pathway was significantly disrupted in DSS-induced colitis mice. Subsequent experiments demonstrated that PF treatment markedly restored bile acid pathway homeostasis (Figures 7A and 7B). The effects of different doses of PFs on the expression of key proteins in the bile acid pathway, including FXR, TGR5, SHP, and FGF15, were evaluated. The results showed a dose-dependent upregulation of these proteins, with the high-dose group exhibiting the most pronounced effect (Figures 7H–7L). Moreover, FMT experiments confirmed that the transplantation of microbiota from PF-treated mice into DSS-induced colitis mice also restored the expression of bile acid pathway-related proteins (Figures 7C–7G), indicating that PFs exert protective effects, at least in part, by modulating the bile acid metabolic pathway through gut microbiota.

Bile acids TUDCA and UDCA alleviate DSS-induced colitis and restore intestinal barrier function

Based on metabolomic analysis revealing significant bile acid metabolic disturbances in DSS-induced colitis mice, the effects of bile acid intervention on DSS-induced colitis were further evaluated. In this study, the secondary bile acids TUDCA and UDCA were administered to DSS-induced colitis mice, and their therapeutic effects were assessed (Figure 8A). BW monitoring (Figure 8B) showed that mice in the DSS group exhibited continuous weight loss compared to the control group, whereas TUDCA and UDCA treatment significantly mitigated this weight loss. DAI and colon length measurements revealed that DAI scores were elevated and colon length was markedly shortened in the DSS group, while both TUDCA and UDCA treatment significantly reduced DAI scores and increased colon length (Figure 8C). Histological examination (HE staining) showed severe mucosal damage, glandular disorganization, and prominent inflammatory cell infiltration in the DSS group. These features were significantly improved following TUDCA and UDCA treatment, with reduced inflammatory infiltration and restored tissue architecture (Figure 8F). Cytokine analysis demonstrated that pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were significantly elevated in the DSS group, whereas TUDCA and UDCA treatment significantly reduced their levels (Figures 8G–8I). MPO activity was also notably decreased following TUDCA and UDCA treatment (Figure 8J). Western blot analysis revealed that, compared to the control group, the expression of TJ proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-3 was significantly reduced in the DSS group. TUDCA treatment significantly restored ZO-1 expression, but no significant changes were observed in Occludin or Claudin-3 expression. In contrast, UDCA treatment significantly restored the expression of ZO-1 and Occludin, while the expression of Claudin-3 remained unchanged (Figures 8K–8N). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that ZO-1 and Occludin expression in the intestinal epithelium was markedly reduced in the DSS group, whereas TUDCA and UDCA treatment enhanced the expression of these barrier proteins (Figures 8O and S4). To further clarify whether TUDCA and UDCA exert their protective effects through the FXR/TGR5 pathway, the protein expression of FXR and TGR5 in colonic tissues was examined by western blot. The results showed that DSS treatment markedly suppressed the expression of FXR and TGR5 compared with the control group. In contrast, both TUDCA and UDCA supplementation significantly restored the protein levels of FXR and TGR5 (Figures S5A–S5C). These findings suggest that the protective effects of TUDCA and UDCA in DSS-induced colitis are associated with the reactivation of the FXR/TGR5 signaling axis. These results indicate that TUDCA and UDCA treatment effectively alleviates inflammatory responses and tissue damage in DSS-induced colitis while improving intestinal barrier function.

Discussion

The therapeutic effects of PFs have been demonstrated across multiple disease models, showcasing their ability to alleviate diabetic complications and neuronal injury through NF-κB pathway inhibition and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines.22,23 The present study investigated the therapeutic impact and molecular mechanisms of PFs on DSS-induced colitis in a mouse model. The results revealed that PFs significantly improved clinical symptoms, reduced inflammation, restored intestinal barrier function, and mitigated tissue damage. A compromised intestinal epithelial barrier is a key factor in the initiation and maintenance of IBD-related inflammation, with barrier integrity restoration recognized as an effective strategy for mitigating intestinal inflammation.24 The data indicated that PFs notably upregulated the expression of TJ proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-3, suggesting their efficacy in repairing epithelial barrier function after DSS-induced disruption.

Colitis progression is characterized by elevated OS, which plays a critical role in sustaining and exacerbating intestinal inflammation.25,26 Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) can damage mitochondria and induce mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to the release of mtDNA into the cytosol.27 The presence of cytosolic mtDNA, an endogenous danger signal, activates the cGAS-STING DNA-sensing pathway, triggering downstream IRF3/NF-κB signaling and pro-inflammatory cytokine production.28 Increased concentrations of cell-free mtDNA have been identified in both DSS-induced colitis mice and patients with IBD, correlating directly with disease severity. Our findings showed that PF intervention significantly alleviated OS in DSS-treated mouse colons, reducing mtDNA release and suppressing the activation of the cGAS-STING cascade. Similar effects were observed in LPS-stimulated Caco-2 cells, where PFs mitigated oxidative damage and effectively inhibited the mtDNA-cGAS-STING inflammatory axis. Together, these results suggest that PFs exert protective effects against colitis, at least in part, by interrupting the positive feedback loop linking OS to mtDNA release and cGAS-STING-mediated inflammation.

Emerging research increasingly links the development and progression of IBD to significant alterations in gut microbial populations, including microbial imbalance, reduced levels of beneficial microorganisms, and an increase in potentially harmful bacteria.29,30 Lactobacillus, a key beneficial bacterial genus, produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) that strengthen the epithelial barrier and suppress inflammatory pathways.31,32 In the present study, PF intervention partially reversed DSS-induced dysbiosis, restoring microbial diversity and enriching beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillaceae. Furthermore, PFs reduced the overgrowth of potentially pathogenic bacteria such as Muribaculaceae, thereby decreasing the risk of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) overstimulating the immune system.

After confirming the anti-inflammatory and barrier-restorative effects of PFs, further investigation was conducted to determine whether these effects depend on the modulation of the gut microbiota. Recent studies highlight FMT as a promising strategy for restoring microbial homeostasis in the treatment of IBD.33,34,35 The results from the FMT experiment showed that transferring microbiota from PF-treated mice to DSS-induced colitis recipients significantly reduced colitis symptoms, lowered inflammatory cytokine production, and increased TJ protein expression. These findings suggest that the beneficial effects of PFs are largely attributed to their modulation of the gut microbiota. Notably, the role of the cGAS-STING pathway in intestinal inflammation exhibits cell-type specificity. While STING signaling in T cells may offer protective effects, aberrant activation of cGAS-STING in the innate immune compartment is a potent driver of pro-inflammatory cytokine production and tissue damage.36,37 In our DSS-induced colitis model, characterized by severe epithelial injury, significant cytosolic mtDNA accumulation, and increased STING expression were observed, supporting the hypothesis that PFs alleviate colitis by dampening this pathological overactivation.

Based on the observed alterations in gut microbiota, targeted metabolomic analysis of intestinal metabolites was conducted, revealing significant changes in the bile acid metabolic pathway. Accumulating evidence highlights the profound influence of the gut microbiota on bile acid composition, primarily through bile acid deconjugation and the conversion of primary bile acids into secondary bile acids, processes that depend on microbial bile salt hydrolases (BSHs) and the 7α-dehydroxylation pathway.38 In the present study, PF intervention enriched bile acid-associated microbial taxa, particularly Lactobacillaceae/Lactobacillus. Lactobacillus species are recognized as key BSH-producing bacteria, facilitating the deconjugation of bile acids, a critical step for subsequent microbial-mediated bile acid transformation.39 In addition to Lactobacillaceae, PF treatment also significantly enriched Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospiraceae. Previous studies have shown that members of the Lachnospiraceae family are heavily involved in secondary bile acid production, particularly through the 7α-dehydroxylation process, and play a pivotal role in maintaining intestinal metabolic homeostasis.40 Additionally, PFs increased the abundance of Akkermansia, a bacterium linked to bile acid metabolic regulation, bile acid-related signaling, and enhancement of intestinal epithelial barrier function.41 Bile acids not only regulate lipid metabolism but also function as signaling molecules that modulate inflammation and preserve intestinal barrier integrity.18,35,42 Farnesoid X receptor (FXR), a bile acid-activated nuclear receptor, plays a central role in regulating bile acid metabolism and maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Previous studies have demonstrated that FXR activation suppresses inflammatory signaling and promotes the expression of TJ proteins, thereby safeguarding epithelial barrier integrity.43 In the present study, secondary bile acids significantly attenuated inflammatory responses in DSS-induced colitis models and increased TJ protein expression in intestinal epithelial cells. Further analyses revealed that both PF treatment and FMT significantly increased intestinal FXR expression and upregulated downstream FXR target genes, suggesting a critical role for FXR signaling in mediating PFs’ protective effects.

In conclusion, this research unveils a novel mechanism through which PFs exert their anti-inflammatory and barrier-protective effects via the “microbiota-bile acid-FXR” axis. These findings enhance the understanding of PFs’ pharmacological actions and suggest that modulating intestinal microbiota and bile acid metabolism to activate FXR signaling could serve as an effective therapeutic strategy for managing IBD.

Limitations of the study

Although this study independently highlights the importance of the microbiota-bile acid-FXR/TGR5 axis and the mtDNA-cGAS-STING pathway in PFs’ protective effects, these two pathways are likely interconnected rather than operating independently. Further, this study proposes a model in which PF-induced microbial remodeling reshapes the bile acid pool, activates FXR/TGR5 signaling, strengthens epithelial barrier function, and suppresses mucosal inflammation. This cascade of events subsequently reduces OS, mitigates mitochondrial damage, limits mtDNA leakage, and attenuates cGAS-STING activation. In turn, the inhibition of the cGAS-STING pathway further reduces inflammatory responses and oxidative injury, fostering a favorable intestinal microenvironment for microbiota restoration and bile acid homeostasis. These pathways may form a synergistic, feedforward regulatory network, though their directionality and causality warrant further validation through targeted interventions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal experiments

Male C57BL/6J mice (n = 36), aged 6-8 weeks and weighing 20 ± 2 g, were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China). These mice were of the specific pathogen-free (SPF) strain. They were housed in standard laboratory conditions at 23-26°C with ad libitum access to food and water. Following a 7-day acclimatization period, the mice were randomly assigned to six experimental groups (n = 6): Control, DSS (2.5%), 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) (100 mg/kg), and DSS + PFs (25, 50, and 100 mg/kg). The PFs dosage was determined based on prior studies.44,45 Initially, PFs and 5-ASA were administered by oral gavage for 14 days. The PFs-alone and DSS + PFs groups received the designated doses of PFs once daily for 14 consecutive days. From day 14 to day 21, DSS (2.5%) was added to the drinking water of all groups except the control and PFs-alone groups to induce colitis. Body weight (BW), food intake, and stool characteristics were monitored and recorded daily throughout the experiment. On day 21, the mice were anesthetized and euthanized. Colonic tissue samples were collected, with some segments fixed in 4% formalin for histological analysis, and others rapidly frozen at -80°C for further analysis.

Fecal microbiota transplantation experiment (FMT)

Donor mice

Donor mice were divided into two groups (n = 6 per group): 2.5% DSS and DSS + PFs (100 mg/kg). Fresh fecal samples from each group were collected and resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to achieve a concentration of 100 mg/mL. The suspensions were mixed and filtered to remove large particles before being used for gavage.

Recipient mice

Recipient mice were assigned to three groups (n = 6 per group): Control, 2.5% DSS-FMT, and DSS + PFs (100 mg/kg)-FMT. Prior to FMT, the recipient mice were administered drinking water containing a combination of antibiotics (ampicillin, neomycin, metronidazole, and vancomycin) for 5 consecutive days to eliminate their native intestinal microbiota. The mice were then randomly assigned to three groups and received daily oral gavage of 200 μL fecal suspension for 14 days, with fecal material obtained from either DSS or DSS + PFs (100 mg/kg) donor mice. Following 14 days of FMT, DSS was added to the drinking water of the recipient mice for 7 days to induce colitis in both the DSS and DSS + PFs experimental groups.

Metabolite supplementation

Mice were assigned to four cohorts (n = 6): Control, DSS, DSS + TUDCA, and DSS + UDCA. Mice in the DSS + TUDCA group received daily intragastric administration of tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA, 50 mg/kg/day) for 14 consecutive days, while those in the DSS + UDCA group were administered ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA, 50 mg/kg/day) on the same schedule. From days 15 to 21, 2.5% (w/v) dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) was added to the drinking water of all groups except the Control group to induce colitis. BW, food intake, and stool consistency were monitored daily throughout the experiment. Upon euthanization on day 21, colon samples were collected for histological analysis and microbiota investigation.

Method details

Disease activity index (DAI)

Daily assessments of BW, stool characteristics, and rectal bleeding were conducted at a consistent time to evaluate DSS-induced colitis severity. Individual scores were assigned to these parameters according to standardized guidelines, and the mean value was used to calculate the daily DAI, providing a numerical assessment of disease progression and treatment efficacy.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

At the end of the experiment, distal colon segments (approximately 1 cm) were harvested, rinsed with saline solution, gently dried, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours. The tissue samples were processed through dehydration, clearing, paraffin embedding, and longitudinal sectioning at a thickness of 4 μm. Sections were stained with H&E following standard protocols. Histological examination was performed using a light microscope at 100× magnification. Histological scoring was based on three parameters: inflammatory cell infiltration, inflammation distribution, and crypt damage, as described previously.46

Immunofluorescence staining

For immunohistochemical analysis, colon specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, followed by dehydration through graded ethanol solutions, clearing, and paraffin embedding. Longitudinal sections were cut to 4-5 μm thickness. The sections were heat-treated at 60°C for 30 minutes, deparaffinized, and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer (0.01 mol/L) with microwave treatment. After cooling to room temperature, the samples were washed three times with PBS (5 minutes per wash), and a blocking solution was applied for 60 minutes to reduce non-specific binding. Primary antibody solutions (anti-ZO-1 and anti-Occludin) were incubated overnight at 4°C in a moisture-controlled chamber. The following day, sections were washed with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG) for 60 minutes at room temperature in the dark. Finally, sections were mounted with DAPI-containing anti-fade medium and examined under a fluorescence microscope.

ELISA and oxidative stress (OS) markers analysis

Following colon tissue extraction, a measured portion was homogenized in chilled PBS to prepare a 10% tissue suspension. The mixture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C, and the resulting supernatant was stored for further analysis. Inflammatory cytokine levels, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), were measured in the colon homogenates using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were loaded into 96-well plates, incubated, washed, developed, and the reaction was stopped according to the kit protocol. Absorbance was measured at the designated wavelength using a microplate reader, and cytokine levels were calculated based on standard curve analysis. Additionally, OS markers, including total antioxidant capacity (FRAP), malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione peroxidase (GSH/GSSG), and catalase activity (CAT), were assessed in colon preparations using commercial kits, following the supplier’s protocols.

Western blot (WB) analysis

The colon samples were homogenized in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors and incubated on ice for 30 minutes. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Equal protein amounts, mixed with 5× SDS buffer, were heated at 95°C for 5 minutes for denaturation before being separated by SDS-PAGE. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (0.22 μm). These membranes were blocked with 5% milk in TBST for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with specific primary antibodies (ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-3) at 4°C. After washing with TBST three times, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. After three additional washes with TBST, enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate was applied, and immunoreactive bands were visualized using a gel documentation system. Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ, and protein expression was normalized to internal controls.

Cell culture

Caco-2 cells (human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 4.5 g/L glucose) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. When the cells reached 70-80% confluency, they were transferred to 96-well plates and 60 mm × 15 mm culture dishes. FBS was purchased from Inner Mongolia Opcel Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Total RNA isolation and RT-qPCR assays

RNA extraction from colon tissue samples was performed using TRIzol reagent (Sigma, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was carried out using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (2641A, Takara Bio Inc., Otsu, Japan). mRNA levels were measured using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kit (Takara Bio Inc., Otsu, Japan). The PCR amplification procedure consisted of an initial denaturation at 94°C for 2 minutes, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation (94°C, 10 seconds), annealing (60°C, 15 seconds), and extension (72°C, 30 seconds), with a final extension step at 72°C for 5 minutes. RT-qPCR primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Total mtDNA extraction and quantification

Total DNA was extracted using the Cell Fast DNA Extraction Kit (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols. mtDNA content was quantified by qPCR analysis, using primers specific to the D-loop region and a distinct non-NUMT mitochondrial site. The nuclear Tert gene served as the normalization reference. Detailed methodology is described in previous research.44 Primer sequences are listed in key resources table.

Cytosolic mtDNA extraction and quantification

Cytosolic fractions were isolated using the Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed twice with chilled PBS, and resuspended in cytoplasmic extraction buffer. After incubation on ice and centrifugation, the supernatant, containing the cytosolic fraction, was collected, while nuclei and intact organelles were discarded. DNA from the cytosolic fraction was purified using the Cell Fast DNA Extraction Kit (ABclonal, Wuhan, China). Cytosolic mtDNA was quantified by qPCR with primers targeting the mitochondrial D-loop and non-NUMT regions, and the nuclear Tert gene was analyzed to confirm the absence of nuclear contamination. Primer sequences are provided in key resources table.

Gut microbiota analysis

Fecal samples were immediately stored at -80°C after collection. Microbial DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341F and 806R. PCR products were purified and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw data were processed with QIIME2, and taxonomic assignment of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) was made using the SILVA database, with clustering performed at 97% identity. Microbial diversity and community structure were assessed through alpha and beta diversity analyses. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was used to identify significantly enriched taxa between groups.

Targeted metabolomic analysis of bile acids

Colonic contents were collected, and metabolites were extracted using a methanol-based protein precipitation method. Bile acid analysis was conducted via ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (Milford, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column using a mobile phase gradient of acetonitrile and water, with 0.1% formic acid. Mass spectrometry detection was performed in negative ion mode using multiple reaction monitoring. Bile acid quantification was carried out using calibration curves generated with authentic standards. Data processing and peak integration were performed using Analyst software, and bile acid profiles were statistically analyzed to identify differentially expressed metabolites across groups.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. Group comparisons were made using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01 vs. the control cohort; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01 vs. the DSS/LPS cohort.
