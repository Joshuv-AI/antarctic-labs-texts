Hydrothermal synthesis of nitrogen and sulfur co-doped reduced graphene oxide anchored cuprous oxide nanocomposite for enhancing rheological and filtration performance of water based drilling fluids
Authors: Mohamed Abu Shuheil, Basim Abdul Alrhym, A C Umamaheshwer Rao, Shivam P Chaudhary, Pardeep Singh Bains, Ripendeep Singh, Djumayeva Mahfuza, Abduvali Sottarov, Hojjat Abbasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBorehole instability driven by reactive shale hydration and excessive filtrate invasion remains a critical challenge in drilling operations. To address this, we synthesized a novel nitrogen and sulfur co-doped reduced graphene oxide/cuprous oxide (N,S-rGO/Cu2O) nanocomposite to reinforce the rheological and filtration properties of environmentally friendly water-based muds. The nanocomposite, prepared via a facile one-pot hydrothermal method, was characterized structurally and compositionally, then evaluated in both polymeric and high-solid base fluids. Performance was assessed through standard and high-pressure/high-temperature (HPHT) filtration, rheological modeling, and hot-rolling shale recovery tests. Results demonstrated that an optimal nanocomposite concentration of 1000 ppm maximized fluid efficiency, with rheological behavior conforming closely to the Herschel-Bulkley model. Crucially, the additive significantly mitigated fluid loss, achieving a 58.8% and 61.3%reduction in API filtrate volume for polymeric and high-solid fluids, respectively, alongside HPHT fluid loss reductions of 57.6% and 64.1%. Furthermore, hot-rolling experiments confirmed enhanced shale stability, yielding a 20% increase in shale recovery. These targeted improvements are attributed to the formation of a dense, hydrophobic nanoplug that physically seals pore throats and chemically inhibits clay swelling, though concentrations exceeding 1000 ppm caused particle agglomeration and diminished returns. Ultimately, the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite operates through a synergistic mechanism of robust physical plugging and rheological networking, providing a highly effective solution for stabilizing reactive shales in complex subterranean environments.Keywords: N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite; Water based drilling fluid; Shale inhibition; Rheological modeling; Filtration control

Introduction

The exploration and production of hydrocarbon resources have increasingly shifted towards complex geological environments, where maintaining wellbore stability is a paramount operational challenge. In particular, drilling through reactive shale formations, which constitute over 75% of drilled sections globally, presents significant risks including borehole collapse, stuck pipe incidents, and excessive non-productive time [1, 2]. These instability issues are primarily driven by the interaction between the water-based drilling fluid (WBDF) filtrate and the clay minerals within the shale. While oil-based muds (OBMs) have historically provided superior shale inhibition and lubrication, stringent environmental regulations and high disposal costs have necessitated the development of high-performance water-based alternatives [3, 4]. Consequently, the engineering of advanced WBDFs that can mimic the inert properties of OBMs while remaining environmentally benign is a critical objective in modern drilling engineering [5, 6].

The fundamental mechanism of shale instability in the presence of water-based fluids involves hydraulic pressure transmission and chemical hydration. Conventional bridging agents, such as sized calcium carbonate or barite, typically range from 0.1 to 100 μm in diameter, which is significantly larger than the nanometer-scale pore throats (10–30 nm) found in shale formations [7, 8]. As a result, these conventional additives fail to form a tight seal on the borehole wall, allowing filtrate to invade the formation, elevate pore pressure, and induce swelling. To address this physical mismatch, the oil and gas industry has turned its attention to nanotechnology. Nanoparticles (NPs), characterized by their high surface-area-to-volume ratio and dimensions commensurate with shale nanopores, offer a promising solution for creating impermeable filter cakes and modifying the rheological profile of drilling fluids [9, 10].

Extensive literature has documented the benefits of incorporating various discrete nanoparticles into drilling fluid formulations. For instance, hydrophilic silica (SiO2) nanoparticles have been widely reported to improve rheological stability and reduce filtration volume by plugging micro-fractures [3, 11]. Similarly, metal oxide nanoparticles such as iron oxide (Fe2O3) and zinc oxide (ZnO) have demonstrated the capacity to enhance the thermal conductivity and gel strength of fluids under high-pressure, high-temperature (HPHT) conditions [12, 13]. Furthermore, carbon-based nanomaterials, particularly multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), have shown exceptional mechanical strength and lubricating properties [12, 14]. However, while these single-component nanoparticles offer specific improvements, they often suffer from limitations such as poor dispersibility in saline environments or a tendency to agglomerate at high temperatures, which diminishes their effectiveness over time.

Recent advancements in nanotechnology have spurred the development of various nanomaterial-based additives to overcome the limitations of conventional water-based drilling fluids, particularly under extreme wellbore conditions. For instance, the use of biogenic copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO NPs) synthesized from plant extracts has been shown to improve both the lubricity (by 27%) and filtration control (by 48%) of drilling muds, offering an environmentally benign approach to stabilizing fluids against heat-induced deterioration [15]. Similarly, surface-modified and polymer-stabilized multiwalled carbon nanotubes (mMWCNTs) have demonstrated significant efficacy in high-temperature applications, reducing filtration losses by 55% and improving viscosity retention by 52% at elevated pressures and temperatures (150 °C) [16]. Furthermore, carbon-based nanomaterials like GO derived from industrial graphite waste have been evaluated as effective additives, showing enhanced rheological properties, reduced fluid loss, and improved lubricity, although functionalized variants like phosphorylated GO (PGO) can negatively impact fluid performance by significantly lowering the pH [17]. Building upon these foundational studies, the present research focuses on the synergistic potential of a novel rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite to address both fluid loss and shale instability.

Among carbon nanomaterials, GO and its derivative, reduced graphene oxide (rGO), have emerged as superior candidates due to their two-dimensional (2D) sheet-like morphology. This unique structure allows them to stack like “shingles” on the formation face, providing a more efficient physical barrier compared to spherical nanoparticles [9, 18]. Moreover, the presence of residual oxygen-containing functional groups on GO allows for surface modifications that can tailor its interaction with the drilling fluid matrix. Despite these advantages, pure rGO tends to be hydrophobic and prone to restacking (irreversible agglomeration) due to strong van der Waals forces, which limits its ability to form a stable, percolated network within the drilling fluid system.

To overcome the dispersion challenges and enhance the chemical inhibition capabilities of graphene-based additives, recent research has focused on heteroatom doping and nanocomposite formation. Doping the graphene lattice with atoms such as Nitrogen (N) and Sulfur (S) introduces defects and active sites that improve the material’s reactivity, wettability, and electronic properties. These heteroatoms facilitate stronger hydrogen bonding with the polymer chains in the drilling fluid, thereby enhancing the structural integrity of the fluid’s rheological network. However, the application of co-doped graphene in drilling fluids remains underexplored, particularly regarding its synergistic potential when combined with metal oxide nanoparticles.

A critical gap in current research is the development of a dual-functional additive that combines the physical plugging efficiency of 2D graphene sheets with the chemical inhibition and weighting properties of metal oxides [3, 19, 20]. While individual studies have investigated N-doped graphene or metal oxide composites separately, there is a lack of comprehensive studies on the hydrothermal synthesis of N, S-codoped rGO decorated with cuprous oxide (Cu2O) nanoparticles for drilling applications. The integration of Cu2O is particularly promising due to its potential to act as a spacer preventing graphene restacking, while providing additional rigid bridging points within the filter cake. Furthermore, previous works have often limited their evaluation to simple bentonite suspensions, neglecting the complex interactions in fully formulated, high-density drilling fluid systems used in deep drilling. There is a distinct need to evaluate how such advanced nanocomposites perform in different fluid bases, specifically distinguishing between polymeric systems (dominated by viscosifiers like Xanthan gum) and high-solid systems (dominated by weighting agents like Barite). Understanding the rheological modeling and shale recovery performance in these distinct environments is essential for translating laboratory synthesis to field application [21, 22].

Consequently, the importance of this research lies in synthesizing a novel N, S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite to address the dual challenges of rheological instability and shale hydration in water-based systems. As illustrated in the workflow in Fig. 1, this study proceeds from the one-pot hydrothermal synthesis and structural characterization of the nanocomposite to its strategic incorporation into two distinct drilling fluid formulations: a polymeric fluid and a high-solid weighted fluid. The hypothesis tested is that the synergistic effect of N/S heteroatom doping and Cu2O anchoring will prevent nanosheet agglomeration, thereby creating a superior “house-of-cards” rheological network and a dense, hydrophobic nanoplug barrier. The specific objectives are to optimize the nanocomposite concentration, validate the synthesis through XRD/FTIR/EDS, and comprehensively evaluate the filtration control, rheological behavior (via Herschel-Bulkley modeling), and shale stabilization efficiency of the formulated nanofluids.

Methodology

Materials

To ensure the highest structural integrity and chemical purity of the synthesized nanocomposite, all precursors were sourced from reputable global suppliers with analytical grade (AR) purity (> 99%). Natural graphite flakes (325 mesh), used as the carbon source for graphene oxide (GO) production, were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). The oxidizers and acids required for the modified Hummers’ method—including sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%), sodium nitrate (NaNO3), potassium permanganate (KmnO4), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%)—were obtained from Merck KGaA (Germany). For the in-situ doping and composite formation, copper(II) nitrate trihydrate [Cu(NO3)2·3H2O] served as the copper precursor, while thiourea (CH4N2S) was selected as the simultaneous source for nitrogen and sulfur doping as well as the reducing agent; both were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

The formulation of the polymeric drilling fluid system required high-performance additives to establish a baseline rheology. To enhance the rheological profile of the polymeric system, xanthan gum (XG) of industrial purity was utilized as the primary biopolymer viscosifier, alongside PAC-LV and PAC-R provided by CP Kelco (USA) to regulate filtration loss. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) pellets, used to adjust the pH of the mud system to the optimal range of 9.5–10.5, were obtained from Fisher Scientific.

For making drilling fluids, specific weighting materials were essential to achieve the target density. High-grade barite was obtained from Halliburton as the primary weighting agent. Calcium carbonate of varying particle sizes (fine and medium) was acquired from Baker Hughes to act as a bridging agent and secondary weighting material. This fluid system relies heavily on the interaction between the high solid content and the nanocomposite, necessitating the use of API-grade bentonite pre-hydrated in fresh water to ensure proper suspension of heavy barite particles.

Finally, general laboratory reagents including absolute ethanol (C2H5OH) and acetone, used for the washing and purification steps of the synthesized nanomaterials, were purchased from Honeywell Research Chemicals. Deionized water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm, generated via a Millipore Milli-Q system, was used throughout the synthesis of the nanocomposite and as the base fluid for all drilling mud formulations to prevent ionic interference from tap water contaminants.

The reactive shale samples utilized for the hydration, swelling, and recovery evaluations were sourced directly from native core plugs extracted from a subterranean well located in southeastern Iraq. To precisely ascertain the phase composition and reactive potential of the formation, quantitative XRD analysis and cation exchange capacity (CEC) measurements were conducted prior to fluid exposure. The XRD profile revealed a bulk mineralogy predominantly composed of quartz (35%) and calcite (12%), accompanied by a substantial total clay content of 48%. Detailed clay speciation indicated a high concentration of inherently hydratable and dispersive clays, specifically mixed-layer smectite/montmorillonite (18%) and illite (22%), alongside minor fractions of kaolinite (8%). Consistent with this prominent swelling-clay fraction, the shale exhibited a pronounced cation exchange capacity of 24 meq/100 g. This comprehensive mineralogical and chemical characterization confirms the highly reactive nature of the selected core samples, establishing them as an ideal and rigorous substrate for evaluating the shale-inhibition characteristics of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O-treated water-based drilling fluids.

Methods

The experimental methodology adopted in this study follows a sequential workflow comprising the synthesis of the nanomaterial, its structural and morphological characterization, and finally, its application in drilling fluid formulations. The synthesis phase utilized a one-pot hydrothermal approach to ensure simultaneous doping and composite formation. Following synthesis, the physicochemical properties were verified using standard spectroscopic and microscopic techniques. The performance evaluation involved the preparation of drilling fluids according to American petroleum institute (API) standards (API RP 13B-1), followed by rigorous rheological and filtration testing to quantify the additive’s efficiency in both polymeric and high-solid heavy mud systems.

Synthesis of N,S-rGO/Cu2O

The synthesis of GO was first carried out using natural graphite powder via a modified Hummers’ method. In this process, 2.0 g of graphite flakes and 1.0 g of NaNO3 were mixed in 46 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid in an ice bath. Subsequently, 6.0 g of potassium permanganate was gradually added under controlled low-temperature conditions (< 5 °C) to prevent overheating, followed by a hydrolysis step at 35 °C after the addition of 98 mL of deionized water. The resulting brownish dispersion was treated with 10 mL of 30% hydrogen peroxide to terminate the reaction, then washed repeatedly with 5% hydrochloric acid and deionized water until the pH became neutral [22].

To synthesize the final nanocomposite, 50 mg of the obtained GO was dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water (1.0 mg/mL) and ultrasonicated for 1 h to ensure complete exfoliation of the graphene sheets. Subsequently, 0.25 g of copper (II) nitrate trihydrate [Cu(NO3)2⋅3H2O] was dissolved in the GO suspension, followed by the addition of 0.50 g of thiourea (CH4N2S). Thiourea serves a dual purpose: it acts as the reducing agent to convert GO to rGO and reduce Cu2+ to Cu+, while simultaneously providing nitrogen and sulfur atoms for doping the graphene lattice.

The mixture was stirred magnetically for 30 min to ensure homogeneity and then transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. The autoclave was sealed and maintained at 180 °C for 12 h. Under these high-pressure and high-temperature conditions, the reduction of graphene oxide occurs simultaneously with the nucleation and growth of cuprous oxide (Cu2O) nanoparticles onto the graphene sheets, alongside the incorporation of N and S heteroatoms into the carbon framework.

Here, GO denotes the graphene oxide precursor, and N,S-rGO represents the nitrogen- and sulfur-co-doped reduced graphene oxide matrix. After the autoclave cooled naturally to room temperature, the resulting black precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm. The product was washed thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol to remove any unreacted ions or excess thiourea. Finally, the synthesized N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite was dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h to obtain the final powder for characterization and application. As depicted in Fig. 2, the synthesis route transforms the planar, oxygen-rich GO sheets into a crumpled, conductive rGO network decorated with cubic Cu2O particles. The schematic visualizes the transition from the exfoliation of graphite to the hydrothermal assembly, highlighting the role of thiourea in efficiently bridging the reduction and doping mechanisms.

Schematic illustration of the one-pot hydrothermal synthesis pathway for the N,S-co-doped rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite

To verify composition and phase purity of the synthesized N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite, a suite of spectroscopic and microscopic analyses was employed. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was conducted using a Shimadzu IR-Tracer 100 spectrophotometer in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻1 using KBr pellets to identify surface functional groups and confirm the reduction of graphene oxide. The crystalline phase and structural integrity were investigated via X-ray Diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over a 2θ range of 10° to 80°. Furthermore, the elemental composition and distribution were mapped using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) ensuring the successful co-doping of nitrogen and sulfur and the presence of copper.

Dispersion stability assessment

To evaluate the colloidal stability and dispersion behavior of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite in aqueous media, dynamic light scattering (DLS) was done. Various suspensions of the nanocomposite were prepared in DW [23].

The hydrodynamic diameter of the particles was determined via DLS analysis using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS. This technique measures the Brownian motion of the particles and relates it to their size using the Stokes–Einstein equation. By analyzing the fluctuations in scattered light intensity over time, the particle size distribution was generated, providing insight into the exfoliation state of the nanosheets and the tendency of the composite to re-stack or aggregate in a neutral aqueous environment. Concurrently, the surface charge of the nanoparticles was quantified by measuring the zeta potential (ζ). An electric field was applied across the dispersion, and the electrophoretic mobility of the charged particles was recorded. The magnitude of the zeta potential serves as a key indicator of stability; a high absolute value (either positive or negative) suggests strong electrostatic repulsion between particles, which is crucial for preventing sedimentation and maintaining the rheological performance of the drilling fluid over time.

Drilling fluid formulation

Two distinct water-based drilling fluid systems, a polymeric fluid and a high-solid/high-weight fluid, were made to measure performances for the synthesized additive under different operational regimes. The fluids were prepared using a Hamilton Beach multi-mixer to ensure high-shear blending. The mixing process was conducted at ambient temperature (25 ± 2 °C) with a fixed stirring speed of 6000 rpm. To facilitate proper hydration and dispersion, each additive was introduced slowly to the base fluid and allowed to mix for 10 min before the addition of the next component [24, 25].

For the preparation of the nano-fluids, the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite was added to the base muds at varying concentrations. The nanoparticle powder was added last to the fully prepared base mud and mixed for an extended period of 20 min to ensure uniform dispersion within the viscous matrix. Special care was taken to avoid the formation of “fish eyes” or unhydrated polymer clumps during the addition of viscosifiers. API filtration

The feature was assessed to determine the ability of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O additive to form a thin, impermeable filter cake and prevent fluid invasion into the formation. Static filtration tests performed according to API RP 13B-1. The cell was pressurized to 100 psi using nitrogen gas, and the test was run for 30 min at room temperature. The volume of filtrate collected was recorded to the nearest 0.1 mL.

To simulate harsh downhole conditions, high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) filtration tests were performed using a specialized HPHT filter press. The drilling fluid was heated to 250 °F (121 °C) inside a heating jacket, and a differential pressure of 500 psi was applied (600 psi applied pressure and 100 psi back pressure) to prevent boiling of the filtrate. This test evaluates the thermal stability of the polymer–nanocomposite interaction.

Following both API and HPHT tests, the permeability and physical properties of the filter cakes were visually inspected and measured. A lower filtrate volume combined with a thin, tough, and slick filter cake indicates superior filtration control. The reduction in fluid loss is attributed to the ability of the nanomaterials to plug nano-sized pore throats in the filter cake and the formation, creating a tighter seal compared to the base fluid alone.

Clay cuttings recovery

The inhibition capability of the drilling fluids against shale hydration and dispersion was evaluated using a hot-rolling cuttings recovery test. Shale samples were dried, ground, and sieved to obtain cuttings with a particle size range of 2–4 mm. The containers sealed and put in a roller oven at 105 °C for 16 h to simulate downhole circulating conditions. After aging, the cuttings were recovered using a 0.5 mm sieve and gently washed with brine to remove adhering mud. The cuttings were then placed in 105 °C to reach constant weight [25, 28].

Results and discussion

Structural and compositional analysis

The successful anchoring and uniform distribution of Cu2O onto the functionalized rGO sheets rely on strong interfacial metal-support interactions, a synergistic structural mechanism analogously observed in the rapid synthesis of multi-metallic nanocomposites [22]. The crystalline structure and phase purity of the synthesized materials were first investigated using XRD. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the XRD pattern of pristine GO displays a characteristic sharp diffraction peak at approximately 2θ = 10.5°, corresponding to the (001) crystallographic plane. This prominent peak indicates a large interlayer spacing (d-spacing) caused by the intercalation of water molecules and the dense presence of oxygen-containing functional groups (hydroxyl, epoxy, and carboxyl groups) on the basal planes and edges of the carbon sheets during the extensive oxidation process of natural graphite flakes.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of graphene oxide precursor and synthesized N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite

In stark contrast, the XRD pattern of the final N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite reveals the complete disappearance of the sharp GO peak at 10.5°, providing concrete evidence for the successful and complete reduction of GO to reduced graphene oxide (rGO) during the hydrothermal treatment with thiourea. Instead, a broad and relatively weak diffraction hump emerges around 2θ = 24°–26°, corresponding to the (002) plane of the graphitic framework. The broad nature of this peak suggests that the rGO sheets are highly exfoliated and poorly restacked, preventing the formation of bulk graphite. This behavior is a direct consequence of the successful in-situ growth of nanoparticles and the structural defects introduced by the co-doping of nitrogen and sulfur atoms into the carbon lattice.

Furthermore, the XRD pattern of the nanocomposite exhibits several distinct and sharp diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 29.6°, 36.4°, 42.3°, 61.4°, and 73.5°. These peaks are indexed to the (110), (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystallographic planes of the pure cubic phase of cuprous oxide (Cu2O), in excellent agreement with the standard JCPDS card No. 05–0667. The absence of any secondary phase peaks, such as CuO or Cu(OH)2, underscores the high purity of the synthesized material and confirms that thiourea effectively reduced Cu2⁺ precursors to highly crystalline Cu2O nanoparticles firmly anchored onto the doped rGO sheets.

To further elucidate chemical transformations and surface functional groups, Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed, with comparative spectra shown in Fig. 4. The FTIR spectrum of GO exhibits intense absorption bands characteristic of heavily oxidized carbon frameworks. A broad band around 3400 cm⁻1 is attributed to O–H stretching vibrations of intercalated water molecules and hydroxyl groups. Additional peaks at approximately 1720 cm⁻1, 1620 cm⁻1, and 1050 cm⁻1 correspond to C = O stretching of carboxyl groups, C = C skeletal vibrations of graphitic domains, and C–O stretching of alkoxy/epoxy groups, respectively.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of GO sheets and N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite

After hydrothermal synthesis, the FTIR spectrum of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite shows a marked reduction in the intensity of oxygen-related absorption bands (O–H, C = O, and C–O). This attenuation provides molecular-level confirmation of effective deoxygenation and reduction of GO to rGO. Simultaneously, new absorption features appear, confirming successful heteroatom doping. Bands in the 1150–1250 cm⁻1 region are assigned to C–N stretching vibrations, while weak bands at 600–700 cm⁻1 correspond to C–S stretching modes, indicating the incorporation of nitrogen and sulfur atoms derived from thiourea into the graphene lattice.

Most notably, a strong absorption band at approximately 620 cm⁻1 is observed, which is characteristic of Cu–O stretching vibrations in the cuprous oxide lattice. The simultaneous presence of C–N, C–S, and Cu–O bands, together with diminished oxygen functionalities, corroborates the XRD findings and confirms the successful formation of a structurally integrated, co-doped nanocomposite suitable for drilling fluid applications [29].

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was employed to quantify elemental composition and verify chemical purity, as shown in Fig. 5. The EDS spectrum displays strong emission peaks for carbon © and oxygen (O). The dominant carbon peak, observed near 0.27 keV, reflects the carbonaceous backbone of rGO sheets. Oxygen is present at a reduced atomic percentage compared to pristine GO, consistent with the reduction process confirmed by XRD and FTIR. Distinct peaks at approximately 0.39 keV and 2.3 keV correspond to nitrogen (N Kα) and sulfur (S Kα), respectively, providing definitive evidence of successful dual doping. Elemental mapping (not shown) indicates a uniform distribution of these heteroatoms across the carbon matrix, contributing to modified electronic structure and enhanced surface reactivity.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) profile of N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite

Prominent copper (Cu) peaks are also observed, including the Cu Lα peak near 0.93 keV and the Cu Kα peak at approximately 8.04 keV. The presence and relative atomic ratio of copper to oxygen further confirm the formation of Cu2O nanoparticles on the rGO surface. Importantly, no residual peaks associated with sodium, potassium, or manganese are detected, demonstrating effective washing and the high purity of the synthesized N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite [22, 30].

To evaluate the thermal endurance of the synthesized material, a critical parameter for additives utilized in high-temperature subsurface drilling operations, Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) was conducted. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the thermal degradation profile of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite exhibits distinct weight loss stages under a nitrogen atmosphere. The initial mass loss of approximately 5–8%, occurring between ambient temperature and 150 °C, is attributed to the evaporation of physically adsorbed water molecules and residual volatile solvents trapped within the porous graphene network. Following this, a second, more pronounced weight loss step is observed between 150 °C and 300 °C. This stage corresponds to the thermal decomposition of the remaining labile oxygen-containing functional groups (such as hydroxyl, epoxy, and carboxyl groups) on the surface of the reduced graphene oxide, which are eliminated as CO and CO2 gases. The relatively moderate mass loss in this region confirms that the chemical reduction process during synthesis was successful in removing a large portion of the initial oxygen functionalities.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) curve of the synthesized N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite evaluated from ambient temperature to 800 °C

Beyond 300 °C, the nanocomposite demonstrates remarkable thermal stability, characterized by a slow and gradual degradation phase. The decomposition of the bulk carbon skeleton (the graphitic backbone) primarily occurs at elevated temperatures exceeding 500 °C. The enhanced thermal resistance in this region is credited to the robust N and S heteroatom doping, which strengthens the carbon lattice, as well as the anchoring effect of the Cu2O nanoparticles that restrict the thermal vibration of the graphene sheets. At temperatures approaching 800 °C, the TGA curve plateaus, leaving a substantial residual mass. This stable inorganic residue corresponds to the inorganic copper components (predominantly reduced to metallic Cu or stable copper oxides depending on the inert atmosphere dynamics) and residual graphitic ash. The high residual mass not only corroborates the successful loading of Cu2O nanoparticles onto the rGO matrix but also definitively proves that the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite can easily withstand the harsh thermal conditions typically encountered in deep drilling applications.

Colloidal stability assessment

The prolonged colloidal stability of the fluid is mechanically driven by the robust electrostatic repulsion and surface functionalization of the nanosheets, a stabilization strategy similarly essential for preventing agglomeration in highly responsive 2D nanomaterial networks [31]. To ensure the uniform performance of the drilling fluid under subsurface conditions, the particle size distribution (PSD) and dispersion behavior of the synthesized nanomaterial must be rigorously evaluated, as premature agglomeration can severely degrade the rheological and filtration properties. Figure 8 illustrates the PSD analysis obtained via DLS for various concentrations (100, 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 ppm) of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite dispersed in an aqueous medium. Figure 8A displays the short-term PSD profiles measured exactly 1 h after ultrasonication. For the concentrations of 100, 250, 500, and 1000 ppm, the average hydrodynamic diameters were tightly clustered around 38, 41, 45, and 54 nm, respectively. These exceptionally small, monomodal distributions indicate that the nanomaterial exfoliates effectively and disperses uniformly at these loadings. However, at the highest concentration of 2000 ppm, the size distribution broadens significantly, and the average diameter rapidly increased to approximately 125 nm within the first hour. This immediate size expansion suggests that the high particle density increases the collision frequency between the nanosheets, causing van der Waals attractive forces to initiate early-stage flocculation.

DLS diagrams for the synthesized N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite at various concentrations measured A 1 h and B 3 weeks after ultrasonication

The long-term colloidal endurance and PSD evolution of these dispersions were subsequently evaluated after an extended static aging period, as depicted in Fig. 8B, which shows the DLS diagrams 3 weeks post-sonication. For the lower to moderate concentrations (100 to 1000 ppm), the PSD profiles exhibited excellent stability, with the average diameters experiencing only a modest increase and stabilizing at roughly 65, 70, 81, and 91 nm. This minimal growth demonstrates excellent long-term colloidal stability, proving that the electrostatic and steric repulsions provided by the functionalized rGO sheets successfully hinder massive agglomeration over prolonged durations. In stark contrast, the PSD of the 2000 ppm sample exhibited a catastrophic loss of stability, with the average hydrodynamic diameter ballooning to 822 nm. This massive shift in the size distribution indicates severe, irreversible aggregation and the onset of phase separation, which would be detrimental in a drilling operation. Consequently, based on these temporal PSD evaluations, a concentration of 1000 ppm is definitively identified as the optimal maximum loading, offering the highest possible additive dosage without compromising the critical long-term dispersion stability [32].

To elucidate the underlying electrochemical mechanisms governing the observed PSD stability trends, the surface charge of the dispersed nanomaterials was quantified via zeta potential analysis, with the results detailed in Table 2. Zeta potential serves as the primary indicator of the magnitude of electrostatic repulsion between adjacent particles in a colloidal suspension. As the table demonstrates, the dispersions exhibit highly negative zeta potential values at concentrations ranging from 100 to 1000 ppm, specifically measuring between − 42.5 mV and − 36.4 mV. In colloidal science, it is widely accepted that an absolute zeta potential value greater than 30 mV provides a sufficiently strong energy barrier to prevent particles from approaching each other closely. Therefore, the highly negative surface charge of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite—likely originating from residual oxygen functionalities and the specific electron-donating nature of the nitrogen and sulfur dopants—creates a robust electrostatic shield.

This significant reduction indicates a severe compression of the electrical double layer surrounding the nanoparticles, likely due to the overabundance of dispersed species and counter-ions in the localized fluid environment. Because the absolute value falls well below the critical 30 mV threshold, the weakened electrostatic repulsive forces are no longer capable of overcoming the attractive van der Waals interactions between the dispersed N,S-rGO/Cu2O sheets. This drastic loss of surface charge repulsion provides the fundamental physical explanation for the massive agglomeration (reaching 822 nm) observed in the long-term DLS analysis. Ultimately, the combined zeta potential and DLS data conclusively validate that exceeding the 1000 ppm threshold induces colloidal collapse, firmly establishing 1000 ppm as the optimal formulation limit for high-performance drilling fluid applications.

Filtration performance evaluation

The substantial reduction in fluid loss is mechanistically attributed to the nanoparticles dynamically altering the filter-cake pore structure, causing a transition from channel seepage to restricted microporous seepage [33]. Furthermore, this physical pore-plugging mechanism effectively mitigates deep core permeability damage by sealing micro-throats within the formation [34]. The capability of a drilling fluid to form a thin, highly impermeable filter cake is vital to prevent wellbore instability, minimize formation damage, and reduce the likelihood of differential pipe sticking. As detailed in Table 3, the standard API filtration tests reveal a profound, concentration-dependent improvement in fluid loss control upon the addition of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite. For the base polymeric fluid, the API filtrate volume was initially 8.5 mL, which systematically decreased to an optimal minimum of exactly 3.5 mL at a nanocomposite loading of 1000 ppm. Similarly, the high-solid fluid exhibited a reduction from 6.2 mL to a highly efficient 2.4 mL at the same 1000 ppm concentration. The physics driving this exceptional reduction lies in the nanoscale dimensions and the two-dimensional morphology of the rGO sheets. The nanosheets act as highly flexible, structural bridging agents that physically plug the nanometer-sized voids and interstitial spaces between the larger solid particles (such as barite and calcium carbonate) within the mud cake. This physical pore-plugging mechanism dramatically increases the tortuosity of the fluid flow paths, creating a tightly sealed, low-permeability barrier that effectively chokes off filtrate invasion into the surrounding formation [35, 36].

Under the considerably more severe High-Pressure High-Temperature (HPHT) testing conditions (500 psi, 250 °F), the protective mechanisms of the nanocomposite become even more pronounced. Typically, the thermal degradation of conventional polymeric additives at high temperatures leads to severe fluid loss, as evidenced by the high baseline HPHT filtrate volumes of 18.4 mL and 14.5 mL for the polymeric and high-solid base fluids, respectively. However, the introduction of the synthesized nanomaterial up to 1000 ppm slashed these HPHT fluid loss volumes to just 7.8 mL and 5.2 mL, respectively. The chemistry of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite plays a pivotal role in this thermal resilience. The nitrogen and sulfur heteroatoms, alongside the anchored crystalline Cu2O nanoparticles, introduce highly electronegative and catalytically active surface sites. These sites engage in robust intermolecular chemistry—specifically, strong hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attractions, and coordinate interactions—with the hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups of the xanthan gum and PAC polymers. This advanced chemical cross-linking reinforces the polymeric network, preventing polymer chain scission and conformational collapse under extreme thermal stress, thereby maintaining the structural integrity of the filter cake where standard additives would normally fail.

Interestingly, the filtration data in Table 3 exhibits a definitive V-shaped trend, where increasing the concentration beyond the optimal 1000 ppm threshold to 2000 ppm results in a detrimental increase in fluid loss across all fluid types and testing conditions (e.g., the HPHT filtrate for the polymeric fluid rebounds to 11.4 mL). The physics of colloidal stability and particle agglomeration perfectly dictate this performance inversion. As verified by the DLS and zeta potential analyses in the previous section, the 2000 ppm concentration exceeds the critical dispersion threshold of the fluid system, leading to severe electrostatic double-layer compression. Physically, the resulting lack of electrostatic repulsion causes the nanosheets to rapidly flocculate into large, micron-sized agglomerates. These massive agglomerates are geometrically incapable of plugging the ultra-fine nanopores of the filter cake. Instead of sealing the barrier, these bulky clusters disrupt the homogeneous packing of the cake layer, creating physical defects, highly permeable micro-fractures, and preferential flow channels. Consequently, the filter cake becomes coarse and porous, allowing elevated volumes of filtrate to escape, definitively proving that optimal nanoscale dispersion, achieved at the 1000 ppm threshold, is the fundamental physical requirement for maximizing macroscopic filtration control.

Rheological behavior and modeling

The enhanced yield point and non-Newtonian pseudoplastic behavior stem from the dynamic three-dimensional viscoelastic networks formed by the nanocomposite [37]. The fluid’s structural integrity under varying shear rates is governed by complex particle–fluid interactions, which can be accurately represented through mathematical rheological models [38]. To rigorously quantify the flow characteristics of the drilling fluids, the relationship between shear stress and shear rate was analyzed and fitted to standard rheological models. Figure 9 presents the comparative rheograms for the two base fluid formulations (polymeric and high-solid) without nanoparticles. The experimental data points, derived from rotational viscometer readings, are overlaid with regression curves corresponding to the Newtonian, Bingham Plastic, Power Law, and Herschel–Bulkley models. Visually, the Newtonian model (linear regression passing through the origin) exhibits the poorest fit, failing to account for the non-linear flow behavior and the existence of a yield stress, which is characteristic of drilling fluids. Similarly, the Power Law model, while capturing the curvature associated with shear-thinning behavior, inaccurately forces the curve through the origin, neglecting the critical threshold stress required to initiate flow.

Experimental shear stress versus shear rate rheograms for the base polymeric and high-solid fluids fitted with Newtonian, Bingham Plastic, Power Law, and Herschel–Bulkley models

The analysis reveals that the fluids exhibit distinct non-Newtonian, viscoplastic behavior. The Bingham Plastic model provides a reasonable approximation by incorporating a yield stress intercept; however, its linear slope fails to track the shear-thinning curvature observed at higher shear rates. Consequently, the Herschel–Bulkley model emerges as the superior mathematical descriptor for both fluid systems. As shown in the figure, the Herschel–Bulkley curve (R2 ≈ 0.998) perfectly traces the experimental data, accurately capturing both the low-shear yield stress region and the shear-thinning viscosity reduction at elevated shear rates. This indicates that the fluids possess a complex microstructure that requires a finite stress to break (yield point) and subsequently flows with a viscosity that decreases as the shear rate increases. While the Bingham Plastic model serves as a close second-best approximation suitable for quick field calculations, the Herschel–Bulkley model is necessary for precise hydraulic modeling.

Having established the Herschel–Bulkley model as the most accurate descriptor, the rheological parameters (τ₀, k, and n) for fluids containing varying concentrations of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite were calculated and listed in Table 4. The addition of the nanocomposite up to 1000 ppm results in a systematic enhancement of the yield stress (τ₀) and consistency index (k). For the polymeric fluid, the yield stress rises from 4.2 Pa to a peak of 12.5 Pa at 1000 ppm. This trend is physically driven by the formation of a robust, three-dimensional percolated network. The high aspect ratio of the exfoliated nanosheets allows them to bridge polymer chains and solid particles, creating a “house-of-cards” structure. Chemically, the nitrogen and sulfur functional groups on the graphene lattice facilitate strong hydrogen bonding and electrostatic cross-linking with the base fluid polymers (xanthan/PAC). This reinforced internal structure requires higher shear energy to disrupt, translating to improved hole-cleaning capabilities and suspension of drill cuttings when the pump is off.

As the system exceeds the percolation threshold and colloidal stability limit, the nanosheets agglomerate into localized clusters. Consequently, the continuity of the reinforcing network is broken; the agglomerates act as inert distinct masses rather than interconnected structural bridges. This collapse of the internal scaffolding reduces the fluid’s ability to resist initial flow (lower τ₀) and diminishes its structural viscosity. Therefore, 1000 ppm is confirmed as the rheologically optimal concentration, balancing maximum structural networking with stable dispersion.

Shale stabilization efficiency

Shale stabilization is significantly improved through the generation of structural disjoining pressure and favorable wettability alterations at the fluid-rock interface [39]. This mechanism restricts destructive water invasion and helps preserve rock mechanical properties under complex mixed-wettability conditions in the wellbore [40]. The interaction between water-based drilling fluids and reactive shale formations often leads to hydration, swelling, and the subsequent disintegration of drill cuttings, which compromises hole cleaning and wellbore stability. Table 5 presents the shale recovery performance, a direct indicator of the fluid’s inhibition capacity. The data reveals a consistent upward trend in recovery percentages as the concentration of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite increases in both polymeric and high-solid fluid systems. For the base polymeric fluid, the recovery rate was initially 78.5%, indicating moderate shale instability. However, the addition of the nanocomposite significantly enhanced this performance, culminating in a remarkable peak recovery of 96.5% at the optimal concentration of 1000 ppm. Similarly, the high-solid fluid saw its recovery improve from a baseline of 72.4% to a maximum of 94.2% at the same 1000 ppm loading.

The physics and chemistry driving this enhanced stabilization are rooted in the unique surface properties and morphology of the synthesized nanomaterial. Physically, the exfoliated nanosheets possess a lateral dimension ideally suited to seal the nano-sized pore throats and micro-fractures inherent in shale formations. By effectively plugging these entry points, the nanocomposite establishes a physical barrier that retards the transmission of pore pressure and blocks the ingress of water molecules into the clay interlayers. Chemically, the hydrophobic nature of the reduced graphene oxide basal plane repels water, creating a “hydrophobic shield” around the shale cuttings. Furthermore, the nitrogen and sulfur functional groups likely engage in adsorption mechanisms with the charged clay surfaces, neutralizing the hydration stress and preventing the osmotic swelling that typically causes shale to slough and disperse.

However, consistent with the rheological and filtration results, increasing the concentration to 2000 ppm results in a noticeable decline in inhibition performance, with recovery rates dropping to 89.3% and 85.6% for the polymeric and high-solid fluids, respectively. This regression is explained by the physics of particle size exclusion. As established in the DLS analysis, the 2000 ppm concentration leads to the formation of large agglomerates (> 800 nm). These agglomerated clusters are physically too large to enter and seal the ultra-fine nanopores of the shale matrix. Instead of forming a tight, impermeable seal, they accumulate on the surface as a porous, ineffective layer that allows filtrate to bypass and hydrate the shale. This confirms that the distinct, dispersed state achieved at 1000 ppm is essential for the nanomaterial to function as an effective pore-plugging and stabilizing agent.

Nanocomposite interaction mechanisms

At the molecular level, the primary interaction mechanism involves the nitrogen and sulfur heteroatoms on the rGO surface establishing robust hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces with the hydrophilic shale minerals [41]. Ultimately, the optimization of these fluid-mineral interactions is dictated by the nonlinear, interactive effects of drilling parameters, akin to multi-variable response optimizations in complex drilling operations [42]. To integrate the findings from the filtration, rheological, and shale recovery experiments, Fig. 10 illustrates the proposed physicochemical mechanisms governing the performance of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite. The primary physical mechanism is the formation of a dense, impermeable nanoplug. Due to their high aspect ratio and two-dimensional morphology, the dispersed nanosheets align parallel to the formation face under differential pressure. They effectively overlay the larger pore throats of the filter cake and the micro-fractures of the shale, acting as discrete shingles that seal the gaps between the coarser bentonite and barite particles. This reduces the permeability of the filter cake and creates a tortuous path that significantly hinders filtrate invasion, thereby minimizing fluid loss and preventing pore-pressure transmission into reactive shales [8, 43].

Schematic representation of the physical plugging and chemical networking mechanisms of the N,S-rGO/Cu2O nanocomposite in drilling fluids

Simultaneously, the rheological enhancements are driven by the formation of a percolated structural network within the bulk fluid. As the nanosheets disperse, they act as rigid, multifunctional cross-linkers between the flexible polymer chains (xanthan gum and PAC) and the charged clay platelets. This creates a “house-of-cards” scaffolding structure that resists sedimentation and deformation. The observed yield-stress increase at 1000 ppm is a direct result of the energy required to disrupt this interlinked microstructure. However, as noted in the results, when the concentration exceeds the percolation threshold (2000 ppm), this structured network collapses into localized agglomerates, explaining the subsequent loss of rheological integrity.

Chemically, the incorporation of nitrogen and sulfur heteroatoms, along with the Cu2O nanoparticles anchored on the graphene lattice, introduces specific active sites for molecular interaction. The functional groups (amine, pyridine-like nitrogen, and thiophene-like sulfur) facilitate strong hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction with the hydroxyl groups on the polymer chains and the negatively charged surfaces of the clay minerals. Furthermore, the partial reduction of graphene oxide restores the hydrophobicity of the basal plane. This hydrophobic character repels water molecules at the shale interface, limiting the osmotic hydration of clay layers. Thus, the material functions through a synergistic triad: physical plugging, structural networking, and chemical inhibition.

Implications, limitations, and future scope

The primary implication of this study is the development of a dual-functional additive that significantly enhances the performance of water-based drilling fluids, potentially allowing them to replace environmentally hazardous oil-based muds in challenging formations. By simultaneously providing physical plugging via the 2D nanosheet morphology and chemical inhibition through N and S heteroatom doping, the nanocomposite offers a robust solution for minimizing non-productive time associated with stuck pipe and borehole collapse. Furthermore, the successful application of the Herschel-Bulkley model to these nanofluids provides drilling engineers with reliable parameters for hydraulic optimization and hole cleaning predictions [24, 44].

Despite these promising results, the study is subject to certain limitations, primarily concerning the scalability of the synthesis process and the dispersibility of the nanomaterial at high dosages. The hydrothermal synthesis method, while effective for producing high-purity nanocomposites in a laboratory setting, may incur high production costs and logistical challenges when scaled for industrial mud volumes. Additionally, the experimental data revealed a performance ceiling at concentrations above 2000 ppm, where the Van der Waals forces between nanosheets overcame the electrostatic repulsion, leading to agglomeration that destabilized the rheological network and compromised filtration control.

Future research should focus on optimizing the synthesis pathway to reduce costs and investigating surface modification techniques to extend the colloidal stability of the nanocomposite at higher concentrations and extreme salinities. Long-term thermal stability tests under dynamic aging conditions exceeding the current operational limits would also be beneficial to validate the material’s durability in ultra-high-temperature geothermal or deep-well applications. Finally, field-scale trials are necessary to corroborate the laboratory findings and assess the interaction of the nanocomposite with common field contaminants such as cement and influx formation brines.
