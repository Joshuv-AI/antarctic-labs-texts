Impacts of land use land cover change on ecosystem services of Hoto area, Gofa zone, South Ethiopia
Authors: Medhanit Ketema, Shetie Gatew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEcosystems provide critical services essential for human well-being and environmental sustainability. This study examines the impacts of land use land cover (LULC) dynamics on ecosystem service values (ESVs) in Hoto area, South Ethiopia, from 1993 to 2023. Using multispectral Landsat imagery, analyzed with ERDAS IMAGINE 2014 and ArcGIS 10.8, six LULC classes were identified: barren land, forest, grazing land, farmland, shrubland, and settlement. The analysis reveals a significant decline in forest cover (from 24.9% to 11%, net loss of 55.69%) and grazing land (from 13.5% to 9.9%), alongside expansions in settlement (from 4.6% to 7.0%), barren land (from 2.1% to19.5%), and shrubland. Total ESVs decreased from US$ 4.35 million in 1993 to US$ 3.33 million in 2023, driven primarily by deforestation, overgrazing, and land degradation, which impaired key services like food production, water regulation, and erosion control. The decline was most pronounced in regulating services (-29.4%), which dominated the total ESV. Overall, these findings underscore the urgent need for targeted policy interventions, including reforestation of degraded lands, promotion of sustainable agroforestry, protection of remaining forest patches, and regulating grazing to restore critical ecosystem services and to improve local livelihoods in the study area.Keywords: Ecosystem service values, Hoto forest, Land use/land cover change, South Ethiopia, SustainabilitySubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Ecosystems encompass a dynamic interplay of environmental components, including flora, fauna, microorganisms, and both biotic and abiotic elements together. These components form integral units within the natural systems on Earth1. Ecosystem service refers to the direct and indirect benefits that ecosystems provide to people. These services are categorized into provisioning, regulating, supporting, and cultural services1.

Humanity relies on ecosystem for various benefits and services, fostering ongoing interactions between humans and the ecosystem, regardless of whether these interactions are beneficial or detrimental2. This reliance reflects co-evolutionary processes that have shaped ecosystems, which in turn support human life3. However, the exploitation and misuse of many benefits provided by ecosystems often lead to significant changes in land use. In many developing countries, there is an overdependence on these services, resulting in declines in both ecosystem health and human well-being4. Misconceptions about ecosystems as free resources contribute to their improper utilization.

LULC changes are primarily marked by the decline of forests and natural vegetation, alongside the expansion of agricultural lands and pastures. These changes significantly contribute to land degradation and the loss of valuable ecosystem functions and services5,6. Land cover change involves alterations in human management practices affecting ecosystems that in turn modify the biogeochemical cycles, climate, and hydrology of these systems. Additionally, such changes contribute to biodiversity loss through habitat fragmentation and destruction. The transformations resulting from land use and land cover changes adversely impact various ecosystem services, including provisioning, regulating, supporting, and cultural services7.

Studies have evaluated ecosystem service values (ESVs) using LULC change models at global, regional, and local levels8. Globally, the estimated economic value of ecosystem services’ declined from US$ 145 trillion in 2007 to US$ 125 trillion in 20119. In Sub-Saharan Africa, the estimated ESVs dropped from US$ 9 trillion in 1992 to US$ 83.7 billion by 201510. In Ethiopia, land degradation alone is projected to cost approximately US$4.3 billion annually11. These trends underscore how LULC dynamics and land degradation significantly influence changes in the structure and function of ecosystem services12.

Ethiopia has undergone substantial LULC changes, primarily driven by agricultural expansion and urban settlement, which have adversely affected forested areas. While several previous studies have examined the impact of LULC changes on ESVs in different part of Ethiopia (e.g8,10,11,13,14). Most of these studies are limited to remote sensing based ESV quantification and lack integration of local community perceptions. Furthermore, they have largely focused on the central and northern highlands, with limited attention to the southern Ethiopian region.

The present study addresses this research gap by focusing on the Hoto area in Gofa zone, south Ethiopia- a region with a distinct ecological and socio-economic context that has not been previously investigated in LULC–ESV studies. A key novelty of this study is the integration of local community perceptions of ecosystem services alongside the quantitative LULC and ESV analysis.

Stakeholder-driven approach provides a more comprehensive understanding by combining geospatial techniques with community insights, offering policy-relevant information grounded in the lived experiences and priorities of local residents. The study’s three-decade temporal scope (1993–2023) further strengthens its contribution by providing a robust longitudinal analysis of LULC changes and their implications for ecosystem services. Community perspectives on the ecosystem services associated with various land uses, including forests, can vary significantly15–17. These insights are particularly valuable for informing sustainable land management policies that are both ecologically sound and socially responsive.

Hoto Forest, located in the Gofa zone of South Ethiopia, has provided essential ecosystem services to the local community for decades. However, the forest is undergoing rapid decline, resulting in a significant reduction in its ecosystem service values. Therefore, this study investigates the impacts of LULC change on the ESVs of the Hoto area, identifies the driving forces, and quantifies the rates of change over the past three decades.

Materials and methods

Description of study area

The study area encompasses Hoto forest and the surrounding kebeles (the smallest administrative unit in Ethiopia) in the Geze Gofa woreda (the second administrative unit in Ethiopia), Gofa Zone, located 529 km southwest of Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. Geographically, the area lies between 6°15′−6°22′ N latitude and 36°47′−36°52′ E longitude, covering 4888 hectares (Fig. 1). The topography of Geze Gofa woreda is diverse, featuring highlands, plateaus, midlands, and lowlands, with the sampled kebeles characterized by highland structures ranging from low 1560 to 2640 m above mean sea level. The woreda is bordered by Demba Gofa woreda to the east, Basketo woreda to the west, Oyda woreda to the south, and Melo woreda to the north. According to the 2007 Ethiopian human population census, Geze Gofa woreda had a population of 68,650. Using the Ethiopian national population growth rate of 2.8% per year, the estimated population in 2023 is 101,327.

Map of the study area.

Ecologically, the Hoto forest is a critical biodiversity reserve in the Gofa zone, harboring diverse types of flora and fauna while providing essential regulating services such as erosion control, water regulation, and climate moderation in the rugged terrain and variable rainfall patterns.

Socio-economically, it supports the livelihoods of the local communities through provisioning services like fuelwood, timber, and non-timber forest products, which are vital for the predominantly agrarian population reliant on forest-adjacent agriculture and grazing land. The area’s proximity to expanding settlements and roads heightens its vulnerability to deforestation and land conversion, exacerbating risks like landslides and soil degradation that threatens both ecological integrity and human well-being. This unique interplay of ecological fragility and socio-economic dependence underscores the urgency of investigating the LULC dynamics in Hoto to inform sustainable management strategies.

Methods of data collection

Time series data about LULC in the study area were analyzed using Landsat imagery (TM, ETM+, and OLI/TIRS) sourced from the United States Geological Survey (Table 1). High resolution options like Sentinel-2 (10 m) were considered for 2023, However, Landsat was retained for uniformity across the time series, as Sentinel-2 lacks data for earlier periods cloud free level 1 images, acquired in 1993, 2003, and 2023, and were projected to UTM zone 37 N (WGS84 datum). Images were acquired during the dry seasons (January - February) for each study year to ensure consistent vegetation and hydrology conditions (Table 1). Ground-truth data were collected in January and February to support image classification and accuracy assessment of the 2023 Landsat imagery. To ensure reliable classification of historical Landsat images (1993 and 2003) and robust accuracy assessment where direct ground truth data were unavailable, five experienced local elders (aged 65–80 years) were interviewed. They were asked to identify land-cover features that remained stable throughout the study period (1993–2023) and to recall major landscape changes. Areas or features confirmed by at least four of the five elders were considered valid. These elder-verified locations were then cross-validated with high-resolution historical and current Google Earth imagery and used as ground control points and reference data for both training the supervised classification and for independent accuracy assessment. This combined approach of local ecological knowledge and modern remote-sensing tools significantly improved the reliability of the long-term LULC change detection. However, the reliance on elders’ memory for historical periods (1993 and 2003) has inherent limitations, including potential recall bias and reduced spatial precision for earlier time periods. To minimize these limitations, only consistently agreed upon information was used and rigorously cross validated with available historical Google Earth imagery and topographic maps.

Image processing and data analysis

Image processing and data analysis of LULC dynamics in Hoto area was conducted using standard methodologies across the three reference periods (1993, 2003, and 2023). Landsat imagery (TM, ETM+, and OLI/TIRS) underwent extraction, radiometric correction, layer stacking, geometric correction, and change detection to ensure data consistency and accuracy. Ground reference data were collected to support image interpretation, classification determination, and accuracy evaluation. Field data validated the interpretation of land use and land cover (LULC) dynamics, supported image classification, and provided qualitative descriptions of LULC categories. In this case, supervised maximum likelihood image classification was employed using the known identities of specific sites24. Satellite image processing and LULC change analysis were performed using ERDAS IMAGINE 2014 and ArcGIS 10.3. A survey conducted in January and February 2023 identified changes in land use and land cover (LULC) and facilitated the understanding of land patterns through temporal satellite imagery. A total of four Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) were carried out in four Kebeles selected by purposive sampling techniques where household interviews were conducted. FGDs facilitated by the researchers were carried out according to the procedure proposed by Hennink25, and were guided by a checklist of questions related to LULC changes and their driving forces. Each FGD consisted of eight people age between 25 and 80 years old and the discussion lasted from one to two hours. In depth face-to-face interviews were conducted with key informant women household heads, elders, knowledgeable persons, and kebele administration heads selected by purposive sampling techniques. From each kebele, nine key informants were selected, resulting in a total of 36 participants, including 21 males and 15 females. Their contribution enriched the qualitative data and complemented the quantitative analysis. Furthermore, field observations informed by FGDs revealed the historical context of land use during classification periods. Ground truth data were collected from fixed locations representing various LULC types to guide classification and evaluate accuracy for the 2023 classification. A total of 61 ground control points (GCPs) were randomly collected, taking into account area proportions for different types of LULC changes. LULC change statistics were calculated using established methods.

Accuracy assessment of the classification

The accuracy of the LULC classification for the study area was evaluated using a classification accuracy assessment (Eqs. 1, 2, 3, 4); employing the Kappa coefficient and overall accuracy metrics (Eqs. 1 and 4). The assessment utilized field GPS data, aerial photographs, FGDs, key informant interview (KIIs), and reference imagery, following established protocols26. Ground-truth data from field surveys, elder interviews, and Google Earth imagery were used to train and validate the 2023 Landsat classification. Elder interviews informed historical context but were not used for pixel specific ground truth; instead, validation relied on stratified random sampling of reference data from Google Earth and field points. Accuracy was assessed by randomly selecting a sample of pixels from the classified image and comparing their labels with reference data. The percentages of correctly classified pixels for each LULC class and the proportions of misclassified pixels were calculated and presented in an error matrix27, computed on a pixels-by-pixels basis and expressed as percentages of correctly classified ground truth pixels. The Kappa coefficient, ranging from 0 (no agreement) to 1(complete agreement), was multiplied by 100 to express classification accuracy as a percentage26. Estimation of ecosystem service values (ESV)

To estimate the values of ecosystem services, various valuation methods can be applied, including direct market-based approaches (such as market price, cost-based approaches, and production function approaches); revealed preference approaches(travel cost method and hedonic pricing); and stated preference approaches (such as contingent valuation method and choice modeling)30. In this study, the equivalent coefficient of ESV for the terrestrial environment was used to estimate ESVs. The estimation was conducted using the benefit transfer technique, which involves estimating ESVs for comparable locations without conducting site specific assessments by transferring values from similar studies a widely accepted method in ecosystem services assessments, particularly in regions where local valuation studies are scarce8,12,17,29,30.

Result and discussion

LULC change from 1993 to 2023

The Hoto area underwent significant LULC changes from 1993 to 2023, driven by natural and anthropogenic factors (Fig. 2). The changes observed across all major LULC classes were substantial and indicate a clear trend of land degradation over the three decades period. Barren land expanded substantially from a baseline of only 101 ha in 1993 to 955 ha in 2023, representing an overall increase of 846.54% (28.47 ha year−1). Although the percentage change appears extremely high, it is largely attributable to the very small baseline area in 1993 (101 ha). In absolute terms, barren land increased by 391 ha between1993-2003 and 463 ha between 2003 and 2023. This more than eight fold expansion, as supported by22, reflects sever land degradation processes. This expansion is driven by degradation pathway. Deforestation and overgrazing first remove protective vegetation cover and expose topsoil to intense seasonal rainfall on steep highland slopes. The erosion rapidly strips away nutrient rich topsoil, lowers soil organic matter, compact the surface and prevent natural regeneration of forest and convert the former forest and grazing land to barren land. Socio-economically, the population growth intensifies demand for charcoal and fuelwood and accelerating the removal of wood biomass and initiating soil erosion that directly reduces the ESVs of the area. Similar expansion (200–500%) are reported in South Ethiopia watershed, driven by erosion, and over grazing12,33. The local FGDs confirmed landslide, charcoal production and drought as key factors. Grazing land declined by 61.82% (−40.70 ha year−1) between 1993 and 2003, aligning with grassland losses in the Afar region due to intensive LULC changes32. A partial recovery of 93.63% (11.75 ha year−1) occurred from 2003 to 202334, but the net change was 26.07% loss (−5.73 ha year−1). This net decline despite partial recovery indicates persistent overgrazing pressure.

LULC maps of the Hoto area. 1993 (A), 2003 (B), and 2023 (C). Major spatial transitions include a substantial contraction of forest cover, particularly in the central and northern parts of the landscape, alongside a marked expansion of barren land. Farmland and grazing land showed net declines, while shrubland and settlement areas expanded, reflecting widespread deforestation, land degradation, and conversion processes over the three-decade period.

Farmland expanded by 58.41% (50.30 ha year−1) from 861 ha to 1364 ha between 1993 and 200335. However, from 2003 to 2023, it contracted by 71.73% (−48.85 ha year−1), resulting in a net loss of 54.93% (−15.76 ha year−1) (Table 5; Fig. 3). This drastic shift reflects abandonment of the farmland due to the landslide induced abandonment and soil exhaustion, consistent with 40–70% decline in Andassa and Munessa-Shashemene areas29,36, corroborated by KII on low productivity. Initial expansion into forest margins provided a temporary ESV gain through cropland production, but continuous rain-fed cultivation without fertilizer or conservation measures depleted soil nutrients and organic carbon. Deforestation on upslope areas simultaneously increased runoff velocity, triggering landslides that physically removed topsoil and buried farmland. The resulting abandonment converted 272.86 ha of former farmland to barren land and 145.01 ha to shrubland, producing a net loss in food-production and raw-material services while illustrating how socio-economic drivers interact with biophysical degradation pathways to reverse earlier ESV gains. This mirrors trends in the Blue Nile Basin, where farmland and settlements grew from 1973 to 1986 at the expense of Sub-Afro-alpine vegetation37 and from 1957 to 1982 in the Chemoga watershed, followed by declines38. Similarly, significant agricultural land loss was observed in Malawi’s Dedza District from 1991 to 201539.

Forest cover, despite occupying only 24.9% of the area in 1993, accounted for approximately 68–89% of the total ESV due to its high per-hectare service value (1775 US$ ha−1 yr−1). In contrast, by 2023, forest area had declined to 11.0%, contributing to a considerable reduction in its ESV share, while the expansion of barren land and shrubland (low-value classes) resulted in a substantial overall decline in total ESV.

Forestland loss accelerated from − 8.1 ha year−1 (1993–2003) to −29.8 ha year−1 (2003–2023), yielding a net decrease of 55.69% (−22.57 ha year−1), indicating ongoing deforestation driven by agricultural expansion, fuelwood extraction, and illegal logging21,40. This represents the loss of more than half of the original forest cover, with the rate of loss increasing by nearly four times in the second decade. The selective logging and charcoal production first open the canopy, reducing evapotranspiration and increase soil moisture variability; subsequent overgrazing and fire further reduce regeneration, expose slopes to erosion and convert high-value regulating services (climate regulation and erosion control) in to none value barren lands. Moreover, socio-economically, rising household demand for construction timber and fuelwood (68% of respondents) under conditions of limited alternative energy sources creates a self-reinforcing cycle that disproportionately erodes regulating and cultural services.

Settlement areas, conversely, shifted from a decline of −13.6 ha year−1 (1993–2003) to growth of 12.60 ha year−1 (2003–2023), resulting in a net increase of 51.49% (3.87 ha year−1), likely due to population growth41. The 136 ha loss (1993–2003) reflects the relocation of people due to recurrent landslides, with communities moving to safer areas or urban centers (FGD and KII insights), and the land being reclassified as shrubland. The 252 ha reversal (2003–2023) stems from returnees following stabilization and infrastructure development, as confirmed by FGDs and similar recovery patterns42,43. Settlement expansion into former forest and grazing land replaces high-ESV classes with built up areas, directly reduces provisioning, regulating, and supporting services and fragmenting remaining habitat.

Shrubland decreased by 14.94% (−27.3 ha year−1) from 1993 to 200338, but increased by 40.29% (31.25 ha year−1) from 2003 to 2023, reaching 2177 ha. The net gain of 19.28% (11.73 ha year−1) aligns with findings in37,38 and may reflect natural succession and ecological recovery following land abandonment or disturbances like road construction. Shrubland gains, largely from degraded forest and abandoned farmland, illustrate secondary succession on eroded sites; while this partially restores supporting services (soil formation, nutrient cycling). However, this cannot compensate for the lost regulating services previously supplied by closed-canopy forest.

Overall, these large percentage changes and differential rates between periods indicate significant landscape transformation, with high value land covers (forest and grazing land) being replaced by low value classes (barren land).

LULC change matrix

The LULC change matrix illustrates the dynamic and nonlinear shifts occurring in the Hoto area over the three-decade period. Notable reduction in the forest cover and corresponding increases in barren land occurred in the study area (Table 5). Forestland decreased by 677 ha, predominantly transitioning into barren, settlements, and shrubland areas from 1993 to 2023. The transition matrix further confirms that forest and farmland experienced the highest gross loss (677 ha and 473 ha respectively), with the majority converted to barren land and shrubland, reflecting intense degradation processes. This change is consistent with findings by35,37–39, who attributed deforestation to agricultural expansion, logging, and charcoal production. Grazing land, settlement, and shrubland experienced initial decline (1993–2003), followed by notable recoveries between 2003 and 2023. Grazing land losses were largely driven by overgrazing and soil degradation, with substantial conversion to barren areas40. Settlement expansion, particularly post 2003, increased by 252 ha, primarily encroaching on forest and grazing lands, indicating the influence of population pressure35,39,41. Farmland exhibited a contrasting trajectory, expanding by 503 ha from 1993 to 2003 and declining by 976 ha from 2003 to 2023, resulting in a net loss of 473 ha. A substantial portion transitioned into barren land, reflecting soil degradation and unsustainable agricultural practices. Another portion reverted to shrubland, suggesting farmland abandonment and natural regeneration processes39. Shrubland increasing overall due to gain from degraded forests and abandoned farmland from 2003 to 2023. Barren land showed the most pronounced increase, from 101 ha to 955 ha, driven by cumulative impact of deforestation, landslide, overgrazing, and unsustainable land use practices which is supported by39 and38.

The matrix diagonal (Table 7) entries indicate land cover stability; while off diagonal values represent conversions between classes. Specifically, 339.1 ha of forest converted to barren land, resulting in the complete loss of the highest ESV coefficients across all ecosystem service categories. In addition, 299.43 ha of forest and 102.79 ha of grazing land transitioned to shrubland, facilitating partial recovery of supporting services via secondary succession, while causing a permanent reduction in regulating services (e.g., water regulation). These spatially explicit land-cover transitions, driven by socio-economic pressures associated with an agrarian population facing limited off-farm livelihood opportunities, largely explain the overall 23.4% decline in total ESV observed between 1993 and 2023.

Changes in the total ecosystem service values

The assessment of ecosystem service values (ESV) in the Hoto area demonstrates substantial alterations in land use and land cover and their associated ecosystem services over the period from 1993 to 2023 (Fig. 3). The total ESV decreased from US$4346274.9 in 1993 to US$3334560.4 in 2023, representing a significant reduction of approximately 23.4% over the three-decade span (Table 8). This corresponds to an average annual loss of US$33, 924 (0.86% per year). The net decline of US$1.02 million in total ecosystem service value (ESV) was primarily driven by the replacement of high-value forest cover (1775 US$ ha−1 yr−1) with low value land-cover classes. The 677 ha net loss of forest alone accounted for approximately US$1.20 million of this reduction. These transitions, notably the conversion of 339 ha of forest to barren land and 75.63 ha to settlement, resulted in the reduction of key regulating services, including those that formerly provided critical buffering capacity against soil erosion and climate variability.

Effects of LULC changes on individual ecosystem service values

The impacts of LULC changes on individual ecosystem services in the study area between 1993 and 2023 are presented in Table 10. Throughout this period, most ecosystem services experienced a decline in value, consistent with the findings by47. The Hoto area is dominated by regulating services (climate regulation, water regulation, erosion control), which contributed nearly 50% of total ESV in 1993 (Table 10). From 1993 to 2023, total ESV declined by 23.4% (US$1.02 million), primarily driven by LULC transitions from forests and grazing lands to barren land and settlements (Table 11). This caused major losses in regulating services (− US$ 634000; −29.4%) and provisioning services (− US$ 147000; −17.4%), with adverse effects on essential functions such as food production, erosion control, and climate stability (Table 11). Supporting services increased slightly (+ 8.2%) due to shrubland expansion, which offers higher soil-formation and habitat/refuge values than replaced farmland or barren land, while cultural services declined by 11.6% (Table 11). Regulating services experienced the greatest decline (− 29.4%), primarily attributable to the extensive conversion of 339.1 ha of forest to barren land. This transition eliminated the highest per-hectare service values for erosion control, climate regulation, and water regulation. From a socio-economic perspective, population-driven fuelwood extraction removed stabilizing root networks, thereby increasing surface runoff, slope instability, and landslide frequency, which further exacerbated the loss of these regulating functions. Provisioning services decreased by 17.4%, reflecting the shift from high-value forest coefficients for food production and raw materials to lower coefficients associated with farmland and shrubland. Supporting services showed a modest net increase of 8.2%, owing to shrubland expansion, which enhanced nutrient cycling and soil formation relative to barren land; however, this gain only partially offset the substantial loss of forest-mediated habitat and refuge functions. Finally, cultural services declined by 11.6%, largely as a result of reduced aesthetic and recreational values following forest fragmentation.

Major drivers of the LULC changes and associated ESV gain and losses

Land use and land cover dynamics in the study area are predominantly shaped by anthropogenic pressures, particularly those linked to globalization and demographic expansion. Analysis of Landsat imagery confirms that human activity is the dominant force driving those transformations. Both proximate (direct) and underlying (indirect) drivers were identified through household surveys, FGD and key informant interviews (Fig. 4). Local communities consistently cited firewood collection, charcoal production, livestock grazing, settlement expansion, and timber extraction as key proximate drivers of LULC change (Fig. 4). These findings alien with prior report by41. Population growth emerged as the most significant underlying driver, intensifying the demand for land and resources (Fig. 4). While this typically results in farmland expansion into natural vegetation and steep slopes, in Hoto it manifested differently—through degradation and abandonment, further aggravated by landslides, as population pressure accelerated soil depletion29,47. These dynamics align with our findings despite the growing need for food. In addition, natural factors such as landslides, erosion, and changing climatic conditions also contributed to LULC shifts. The interaction among underlying drivers generates distinct land degradation cascades. Rapid population growth (2.8% yr−1) has increased per-household demand for fuelwood and charcoal thereby triggering selective logging and canopy opening. The resulting decline in evapotranspiration elevates soil water saturation during rainfall events, while the loss of root reinforcement reduces slope shear strength. These processes culminate in recurrent landslides that convert both forest and farmland into barren land. This degradation pathway simultaneously erodes regulating services (particularly erosion control and water regulation) and provisioning services (timber and non-timber forest products). Compounded by widespread poverty and limited off-farm livelihood options, these conditions reinforce community dependence on unsustainable resource extraction, thereby perpetuating the degradation cycle.

Driving forces of forest cover change.

Nationally, Ethiopian Green legacy initiative (since 2019) has planted over 25 billion seedlings, targeting 50 billion, with reports 1–2% forest cover increase through agroforestry and restoration49. However, local trends in Hoto show persistent forest decline, suggesting implementation gaps, recurrent landslides and erosion in the area, drought and overriding pressures like population growth and illegal logging.

The conversion of forest areas into barren land, settlements, and shrubland led to a loss of 677 ha in forest cover, resulting in substantial reductions in ecosystem ESVs. This pattern of deforestation is consistent with findings by35,48,50,51 who highlighted the role of logging and charcoal production in forest degradation. Timber extraction presents an additional challenge, with 32% of surveyed respondents reporting widespread tree cutting to meet growing construction demands—a trend similarly observed by52.

Field observation and interviews further emphasized the influence of road infrastructure, illegal logging, and fuelwood collection. Approximately 68% of respondents indicated that informal logging and fuelwood harvesting remain critical livelihood strategies (Fig. 4). Fuelwood suppliers in Bulki town transport wood using trucks, while residents living adjacent to the forest regularly collect wood, construction materials, and other forest products for subsistence and income generation. The major road to Laha town traverses the forest, intensifying access and exploitation. These findings reflect broader national patterns reported by42 and53 who documented the economic significance of forest-based resources in Ethiopia’s rural development context.

Natural factors also contribute to LULC changes. Heavy rainfall, exacerbated by deforestation and forest degradation, increases the risk of landslides and soil erosion. These hazards threaten not only forest ecosystems but also agricultural land—particularly farmland, which decreased by 473 ha, much of it transitioning into barren and shrubland classes. The destruction of infrastructure and displacement of communities have also been documented, with 23.2% of survey respondents identifying landslides as a direct consequence of forest degradation. These findings corroborate prior research on the impacts of climate variability and hydrological extremes43,54. The expansion of barren land from 101 ha to 955 ha further illustrates the cumulative effects of overgrazing, deforestation, and unsustainable land use practices39. Shrubland gains were largely due to natural regeneration processes on degraded forest plots and abandoned farmland. Settlement areas expanded by 116 ha, frequently encroaching on ecologically sensitive zones such as grazing lands and forests, an outcome of population pressure highlighted in previous studies35,39,41.

Grazing land was among the most affected, with large portions degraded and converted barren lands due to intensive livestock activity and associated soil erosion40. Among all the land cover classes, forest land recorded the highest net loss, followed by farmland. In contrast, barren land, shrubland, and settlement areas exhibited progressive expansion. These findings reflect a complex interplay between human and environmental drivers, emphasizing the urgent need for integrated land management approaches.

The cumulative effects of these transformations underline the necessity for sustainable interventions aimed at mitigating deforestation, controlling resource extraction, and preserving ecosystem services. Strategic land use planning that balances development goals with ecological integrity is essential for enhancing community resilience and ensuring long-term sustainability in the Hoto region.

The community’s perceptions of ecosystem services provided by forest land use

Local households in the study area generally hold a positive view of the ecosystem services provided by the forest land use. This is based on survey responses and insights from focus group discussions, which examined community perspectives on various ecological functions.

The calculated mean scores, accompanied by standard deviations, revealed strong consensus across most categories, particularly in relation to the forest’s role in supporting water supply and food production. These services were overwhelmingly rated as highly important. However, responses also identified areas with lower agreement, notably in erosion control and climate regulation, indicating potential knowledge or implementation gaps. Survey results indicated that participants consistently valued food production (63.5%), water supply (60.3%), and shade provision (60%) as primary benefits of forest lands (Table 13), and reflecting substantial local awareness of the tangible advantages forests offer despite ongoing land-use changes. However, the perceived importance of several regulatory and cultural services—including water regulation, erosion control, climate moderation, pollination, and cultural values—was notably lower than reported in similar assessments by55.

The community’s reliance on forest-adjacent agriculture, characterized by relatively low productivity, underscores their vulnerability to environmental disturbances. Soil erosion, driven in part by deforestation, was reported to significantly impact livelihoods—a relationship similarly documented by56,57 who linked declining soil fertility to unsustainable forest clearing.

The study highlights the critical contribution of forests to ecosystem services provision relative to other land uses. The loss of forest cover directly diminishes access to multiple services, a relationship well understood by residents living near forest boundaries. These populations often serve both as beneficiaries and as stewards of forest ecosystems, a dual role supported by the findings of58,59. Their proximity to, and reliance on, forest resources appear to foster a deep appreciation of the forest’s ecological and socioeconomic value, which in turn shapes their attitudes toward conservation. Survey results indicate strong community consensus on the importance of provisional services such as food and water supply, while recognition of supporting and regulating services particularly climate regulation and erosion control appears comparatively limited.

Limitations of the study

This study has the following limitations. First, the benefit transfer method used for ESV estimation relies on global coefficients adjusted locally, which may not fully capture site-specific values of ecosystem services in the Hoto area. Second, historical LULC classification for 1993 and 2003 depended partly on local elders’ knowledge and Google Earth imagery, which introduces potential recall bias and lower spatial precision compared to the 2023 field validation. Finally, the study did not quantify the impacts of climate variability independently from land-use change. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results, and future research should incorporate primary valuation studies and longer-term climate data.

Conclusions

Over the period 1993–2023, the Hoto area experienced notable LULC changes, characterized by reduction in forest cover (24.9% to 11.0%), grazing land (from 13.5% to 9.9%), and farmland (from 17.6% to 7.9%), accompanied by increases in barren land (from 2.1% to 19.5%) and settlements (from 4.6% to 7.0%). These shifts were associated with a 23.4% decrease in total estimated ecosystem service value (from US $4.35 million to US $3.33 million), equivalent to a net loss of approximately US $1.02 million over the period. Regulating services, which accounted for nearly 50% of the total ESV in 1993 and were predominantly supplied by forest cover, experienced the largest relative decline (− 29.4%). Provisioning services decreased by 17.4%, while supporting services showed a modest increase (+ 8.2%) linked to shrubland expansion, and cultural services declined by 11.6%.

Community survey responses aligned with these trends, indicating strong recognition of provisioning services (e.g., food 63.5%, water supply 60.3%) but comparatively lower perception of certain regulating and supporting functions (e.g., erosion control and climate regulation).

The observed patterns suggest that ongoing LULC dynamics, particularly forest loss, have contributed to reduced capacity for key ecosystem services such as water regulation and erosion control in this landscape. These changes may have implications for local water availability, soil stability, and livelihood support in an area reliant on agrarian and forest-based activities. To address these trends, integrated land management approaches such as targeted reforestation, protection of remaining forest patches, promotion of sustainable agroforestry, and regulated grazing could help maintain or restore priority ecosystem services. Further research incorporating site-specific valuation data, longer-term monitoring, and assessment of climate interactions would strengthen understanding of these dynamics and support evidence-based policy in similar highland areas of south Ethiopia.
