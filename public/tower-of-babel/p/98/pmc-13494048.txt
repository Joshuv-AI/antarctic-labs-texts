Analysis of gene co-expression connectivity dynamics implicates aberrant neuron-oligodendroglia interactions in schizophrenia
Authors: Eugenia Radulescu, Petra E. Vértes, Shizhong Han, Jennifer A. Erwin, Apuã C. M. Paquola, Thomas M. Hyde, Joel E. Kleinman, Tomoyo Sawada, Edward T. Bullmore, Daniel R. Weinberger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dysconnectivity in schizophrenia is a pervasive concept across various levels of systems biology. To better understand disrupted patterns of molecular connectivity in schizophrenia, we apply innovative approaches to gene co-expression networks starting from multiple regions of postmortem brain (bulk RNA-Seq from dorso-lateral prefrontal cortex- (DLPFC) (Ndonors=297; sex: M/F = 212/85), hippocampus (Ndonors=250; sex: M/F = 181/69) and caudate (Ndonors=349; sex: M/F = 242/107). Here we identify differentially connected genes (DCG) in schizophrenia networks that deviate from architectural relationships characteristic of neurotypical gene networks based on three network metrics- total connectivity (kTot), clustering coefficient (C), and intra-module degree (kIn). We find multiple DCG consistent across all brain regions, most of which we then independently confirm in brain single nuclei (snRNAseq) data and in four independent human iPSC-derived brain organoids. DCG specific for each network parameter shows enrichment in schizophrenia genetic signal, in pathways prominently related to neurons and oligodendroglia functionality, and in cell-type specific co-expression patterns that differ between neurotypical and schizophrenia, implicating neuronal-oligodendroglia incoordination.

Introduction

In E. Bleuler’s groundbreaking work “Dementia Praecox or the Group of Schizophrenias”1 published at the turn of the 20th century, he posited that schizophrenia involved a breakdown of coordination of mental functions, a complex and multi-faceted brain disorder affecting communication between parts of the brains. Decades of research have suggested that schizophrenia phenotypes plausibly emerge from a neurodevelopmental disorder of connectivity within the brain2,3. Though progress has been made in unraveling schizophrenia’s biological substrate4, much is yet to be learned about the mechanisms leading to this condition. Importantly, dysconnectivity in schizophrenia can be regarded as a pervasive trait apparent across various levels of systems biology, from genetic and molecular to cognition and behavior5,6. The mechanisms of dysconnectivity in schizophrenia likely include transcriptional dysregulation of various genomic features (genes, isoforms, non-coding transcripts, etc.)7–11 that affect synaptic function and compromise the optimal coordination between brain cell types and within local and distributed circuitries12,13.

Efforts to elucidate the nature of transcriptional dysregulation associated with schizophrenia, including from our group, have largely relied on analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEG), in schizophrenia brains compared to non-clinical controls14–17. However, DEG studies have notable limitations, most importantly, they are often confounded by medication effects, agonal events, aging, and RNA quality. In addition, they tend to show inconsistent alignment with genome-wide association studies (GWAS) that highlight genetic risk loci for schizophrenia18–21. Moreover, DEG tends to emphasize one-off gene expression differences, missing the obvious, that schizophrenia is a systems-level problem.

A popular complementary approach has focused on patterns of co-expression within gene networks that may reveal a systems-level dysregulation underlying schizophrenia. A well-established method for studying co-expression networks in schizophrenia is weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), which identifies modules (clusters of co-expressed genes) and tests their enrichment for schizophrenia genetic risk and biological ontologies22–28. For instance, in a previous study from our group23, we used standard WGCNA to calculate gene co-expression networks from postmortem DLPFC RNA-Seq data, and we identified and prioritized SCZ-relevant genes by exploring the overlap between GWAS loci genes and modules of co-expression that differed between CTRL and SCZ and that correlated with SCZ genomic risk score. However, while WGCNA has provided important insights, most studies with this approach have focused on clusters or modules of related genes and have not directly assessed the finer-scale connectivity of how individual genes relate to each other. It is conceivable that how genes are connected within networks may be at least, if not even more important to the biology of illness than to whom they are directly connected.

To move beyond these limitations, we here propose a refined systems biology approach to gene co-expression in the brain and in 3-D cell model systems. Specifically, we address the question of schizophrenia-associated dysconnectivity by quantifying the topological properties of co-expressed genes- both within modules and across the entire network- that deviate in patients from the properties defined in neurotypical brain networks. Our primary aim is to understand how the same genes that comprise a molecular community in the normal brain relate differently to each other in the context of the schizophrenia brain. We are interested in the dynamics of relationships within the community rather than simply community membership. In this study, therefore, we characterize each gene based on both intra-module and global network connectivity metrics in co-expression networks derived initially from bulk postmortem brain RNA-Seq data across three brain regions prominently implicated in schizophrenia-the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), caudate, and hippocampus. We submit that part of the originality of our approach is to look at the disconnection of genes in the schizophrenia brain based on the normal brain reference. While most brain functions of individuals with schizophrenia are relatively normal, something is presumably misconnected at a relatively specific systems level29,30. Analogously, by using the architecture of gene co-expression in the normal brain projected into the gene expression context of schizophrenia, we sought to highlight which genes do not properly integrate within the normative gene co-expression network’s architecture.

We then validate gene sets that differ in network metrics between controls and schizophrenia in cell-specific co-expression networks from DLPFC snRNAseq data and also from iPSC-derived 3-D organoid models. We hypothesize that schizophrenia networks exhibit reproducible deviations in connectivity patterns compared with controls despite a common network infrastructure of the same genes, reflecting underlying risk and state biology at the systems level. By quantifying these connectivity differences, we identify gene sets and mechanisms that provide new insights into pathogenic biological systems and potential therapeutic targets.

Results

General characterization of neurotypical and schizophrenia co-expression networks from bulk RNA-Seq in DLPFC, hippocampus and caudate

We created six co-expression networks from postmortem brain bulk RNA-Seq data, for 3 brain regions (DLPFC, caudate, and hippocampus) and two groups (neurotypical controls and donors with schizophrenia) (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Data 1). First, we performed module detection in each network and generated six sets of co-expression modules (Supplementary Fig. 1). Biological profiling by gene ontology analysis showed similar themes for all co-expression modules, plausibly explained by the mixture of CNS cellular types characteristic for postmortem bulk RNA-seq data. In the context of remarkably different cell composition of our regions (caudate- a nonlaminar structure, DLPFC and hippocampus- laminar structures with distinct cytoarchitectures), we examined whether any variances in our regional co-expression networks might be explained by case-control differences in cell-type composition. Consequently, we performed cell-type deconvolution and looked for case-control differences in cell-type proportions. This analysis across the three brain regions identified minimal differences in cellular composition between CTRL and SCZ, suggesting that potential changes in co-expression network architecture in SCZ are not merely explained by shifts in cell-type proportions between the two groups. In total, only five distinct cell-type proportions exceeded the statistical threshold for significance (pFDR < 0.05) across the 57 possible comparisons (19 cell types x 3 regions) (Supplementary Data 2). The differentially connected genes that we highlight in what follows were not enriched for any of these cell types.

From the co-expression networks, we calculated three network metrics: one dependent on modular partition from CTRL networks as reference (intramodular degree- kIn), and two global network parameters- clustering coefficient (C) and total degree (total connectivity) (kTot). From a biological perspective, the three co-expression network parameters likely describe various properties of interactions at cellular and molecular levels, which offer an opportunity to get a glimpse of the role of these complex properties in relation to schizophrenia. Because we assume that the biological systems represented in these co-expression networks are complex, dynamic and multidimensional, there is unlikely to be only one or two characteristics of network topology that adequately capture this complexity. Generally, the simplest two of the three is the measure of degree (kTot and kIn), which sums up the number of connections of a node (i.e., a gene) within a module or the entire network, respectively. While this is the first clue about a node’s importance within a module, or in the global network, it is rather basic due to the focus on local neighborhood, ignorant of connections’ quality and of information’s flow in the network, and the lack of sensitivity to network topology changes. What could this mean in terms of biological significance? For example, a gene can have a high intra-module degree in more than one module, which suggests functional versatility and participation in different relevant biological processes. In contrast, the clustering coefficient is a more complex metric that describes the local density in a network, or the “cliquishness“ of nodes in the network. From a biological perspective, this measure can show patterns of genes that are tightly regulated together, because they are part of biological processes relatively less variable. In other words, participating genes need to act as complexes to maintain the coordinated nature of such processes.

Gene-wise connectivity differences in schizophrenia co-expression networks relative to neurotypical controls

To explore gene-specific case-control variations in the three network metrics, we used two types of difference measures: the absolute differences (|∆| = |x1-x2 | ) that summarize the effects’ magnitude and the simple difference (∆ = x1-x2), that shows the directionality of effect, respectively, genes with higher network parameters in CTRL vs. SCZ (“positive” differences) or higher network parameters in SCZ vs. CTRL (“negative” differences). We generated three region-specific sets of |∆| and ∆ per parameter (Supplementary Data 3). To characterize and visualize distributions of case-control differences in network parameters, we focused on the absolute differences |∆| kIn, |∆| kTot and |∆| C. Across the three brain regions |∆| kIn, |∆| kTot and |∆| C each had a right-skewed distribution, with most of the values close to the left tail of the histograms (Fig. 2). We sought to determine an appropriate threshold at which the differences in network metrics become potentially significant for the alterations of co-expression architecture in schizophrenia, and consequently, for putative disruptions in relevant biological pathways. Given the right-skewed distribution of |∆| in which a majority of genes coalesce around zero or with minimal differences, whereas ~ 2000 for each measure (roughly 10%) appeared as outliers, we determined the threshold based on the IQR rule. Notably, this is a robust, distribution-free measure of spread, according to which the upper bound is defined by 1.5 times the interquartile range (IQR) above the third quartile (Q3), respectively the upper boundary before individual points (i.e., genes) are considered outliers for the |∆| kIn, |∆| kTot and |∆| C (Fig. 1C) (see “Methods”).

The next step was to select and functionally characterize the outlier genes for network metrics’ |∆| (Fig. 1C). To explore the spread of gene-wise outliers for |∆| relative to CTRL values as reference, we used density map plots for each network parameter and brain region (Fig. 3). The density map plots revealed numerous genes as outliers for each network parameter’s |∆|, especially within hippocampus, followed by the caudate and DLPFC (Fig. 3). Overall, 100 regionally consistent outlier genes were common to all parameters, while 286 were regionally consistent genes based on C (now called C-specific), and 185 were kIn-specific (Supplementary Fig. 11). For total connectivity (kTot), the vast majority of regionally consistent outliers were common between the three parameters, leaving only 9 genes specific to kTot (Supplementary Fig. 11). We, therefore, focus further on regionally consistent outliers partitioned only by kIn and C, and we create three priority sets of differentially connected genes (in short, priority DCG): C-specific, kIn-specific and C-kIn shared (genes representing the overlap between outliers of |∆| for all parameters plus genes representing the overlap between outliers of |∆| C and |∆| kIn). These priority DCGs were taken for downstream functional profiling and for replication in independent datasets (Fig. 1C).

Testing for statistical artifacts and regional specificity

While the IQR rule was a distribution-driven choice for outlier detection, we also explored genes with significant case-control differences in network parameters by performing simulations and establishing null statistics meant to ensure that differentially connected genes are not statistical artifacts. Results of this analysis showed that network deviations in SCZ are non-random. Specifically, this analysis found that the hippocampus was the most systemically disrupted region, with the highest percentage of non-random genes (i.e., the same genes showing significant differences) across all metrics (C = 0.70%, kIn = 0.45%, kTot = 0.37%). DLPFC showed non-random disruption in total connectivity (kTot = 0.47%), while clustering coefficient (C = 0.12%) and kIn (kIn = 0.05%) had relatively fewer nonrandom genes. In contrast, caudate displayed the least nonrandom structural variation (C = 0.11%, kIn = 0.15% non-random genes). Likewise, while permutations induced an apparent global “variance collapse”, the individual gene positions remained random, with 0% of genes exceeding the null boundary for caudate kTot.

We then looked for overlaps between non-random genes and the outliers for each network metric in each brain region. Notably, all non-random genes by permutation tests were found in the larger sets of IQR-based outliers (Supplementary Data 4). This suggests that in spite of the random network re-configuration, there is a low frequency of non-random outliers for network metrics that differ between CTRL and SCZ. These statistical approaches confirm that the outliers are not noise or statistically improbable.

While regional consistency was the biological pillar for selecting differentially connected genes in SCZ, we also filtered the outliers for network metrics’ |∆| that were regionally specific (labeled regDCG- defined as sets of outlying genes that did not overlap with any of the regionally consistent DCG) (Supplementary Fig. 12). We also looked at the simple differences in network metrics across the three regions and interestingly, we found that most outliers for “positive” differences overlapped with the regionally consistent outliers for network metrics’ |∆| (Supplementary Fig. 13). In other words, schizophrenia outliers (i.e., DCG) at all levels of analysis tend to be the least connected both within modules and the whole network.

Returning to our final priority set of differentially connected genes (DCG) (the regionally consistent and parameter-specific outliers for network metrics |∆|, respectively kIn-specific, C-specific and C-kIn shared), we next asked two questions: (1) are these genes related to plausible biological mechanisms underlying schizophrenia or to schizophrenia’s genetic architecture? And (2) do the DCG carry potential for finding schizophrenia biomarkers and drug targets? To answer these questions, we adopted the strategy schematized in Fig. 1D; specifically, we looked at the enrichment of DCG in biological processes by gene ontology analysis, in gene sets and pathways credibly or previously linked to schizophrenia, and enrichment of DCG in the schizophrenia GWAS signal.

Biological mapping of DCG by gene ontology (GO) analysis

GO analysis of the priority DCG suggested a complex interaction between mechanisms implicated in neurodevelopment and optimal functionality of CNS synapses (Fig. 4A). While the DCG are enriched in schizophrenia related gene sets (e.g., synaptic function, brain development), they also highlight not just the involvement of neuronal mechanisms (i.e., modulation of chemical transmission), but also a contribution of glial cells, especially oligodendroglia, as indicated by biological processes including axon ensheathment and myelination. Further, a relative functional divergence was observed between the DCG based on the specific network properties implicated, with C-specific genes more enriched in biological processes related to oligodendrocytes’ development and axon ensheathment in CNS, kIn-specific genes preferentially enriched in processes related to modulation of chemical transmission and immune functions, and C-kIn shared genes for axon ensheathment and myelin assembly (Fig. 4A). While previous studies33 suggested a relationship between oligodendrocytes and SCZ, our study uncovers common sets of genes that appear to operate trans-regionally through myelin-related pathways and mechanisms. We also compared biological processes (GO:BP) between regDCG and our priority DCG. Only caudate and hippocampal regDCG had relatively meaningful biological ontologies, mainly related to cilium organization and functionality, synaptic transmission, axon development and immune processes (Supplementary Fig. 14). Cell-type specificity estimated from co-expression networks does not explain case-control differences in connectivity

To further rule out the possibility that case-control differences in network metrics are related to changes in cell-type composition between the two diagnostic groups, we also calculated a gene-wise score of cell-type specificity (CTS) directly from our CTRL and SCZ co-expression matrices, defined as the average adjacency (or correlation) between a gene and all markers from a specific cell-type. Essentially, a gene with a high cell-type specificity (CTS) score can be linked to a certain cellular type due to the many connections with the markers of that cell type. This also suggests that the respective gene may be part of a co-regulatory network characteristic for the cell-type with which it is mostly associated. We then calculated the absolute difference (|∆|) of CTS between CTRL and SCZ and compared it with |∆| C, |∆| kIn and |∆| kTot.

In this context, we reasoned that finding linear, significant correlations between the |∆| CTS score and |∆| C, |∆| kIn, and |∆| kTot would suggest that absolute differences in network metrics are in fact differences in cell-type composition between the two groups. This correlation analysis across the three regions showed non-linear relationships between the network metrics |∆| and |∆| CTS score for various cell types. Remarkably, we noticed that only for oligodendrocytes and OPCs was there a subset of genes that had a near linear relationship between the |∆| C and the |∆| CTS (Supplementary Figs. 15–23). However, this specific correlation cannot be explained by CTRL vs. SCZ differences in oligodendrocytes and OPC ratios, as oligodendrocytes and OPC proportions did not differ between the two groups (Supplementary Data 2).

Enrichment of differentially connected genes (DCG) in gene sets and pathways credibly linked to schizophrenia

We examined the potential biological importance of our priority DCG by performing gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using curated gene sets associated with schizophrenia, including, the PGC3 prioritized (i.e., fine and functionally mapped) genes in the latest GWAS34, genes from three TWAS studies on our brain regions of interest15,16,35, sets of synaptic genes from the SynGO database36 and gene targets for schizophrenia from the Druggable Genome database, parsed by the drug development stage37. Results of the GSEA showed that kIn-specific DCG were significantly enriched in PGC3 prioritized genes (Fig. 4B). Further, kIn-specific showed significant enrichments in synaptic ontologies and druggable targets, especially for the Tbio level (i.e., targets from plausible biological pathways, however, without drug or small molecules activities). The C-kIn shared DCG were also enriched in presynaptic ontologies and druggable targets (Tbio level). In contrast, C-specific DCG did not show any significant association with our selected gene sets credibly linked to schizophrenia (Fig. 4B), again illustrating an orthogonal biology to C-specific genes.

When we jointly performed the enrichment of priority DCG and regDCG in gene sets of interest, caudate and hippocampus kIn regDCG and kIn-specific DCG were the only sets significantly enriched in PGC3 loci genes (Supplementary Fig. 24A). When using a more stringent statistical threshold (i.e., p < 0.05 after multiple comparisons correction and strong effect size- odds ratios OR > = 3), only the kIn-specific DCG showed a high confidence overlap with PGC3 loci genes (p < 0.05 and OR = 6.8) (Supplementary Fig. 24B). As expected, all regDCG were significantly enriched for various synaptic and druggable genome ontologies, while no set was enriched for TWAS genes. Nonetheless, relative to the kIn-specific DCG, the regDCG had overlaps with lower effect sizes (Supplementary Fig. 24B).

Post-GWAS analysis: MAGMA and LDSC with schizophrenia GWAS summary statistics

To look for potential relationships between DCG and mechanisms relevant for schizophrenia risk, we checked for associations between DCG and GWAS signal for schizophrenia by applying gene-set analysis with Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation (MAGMA)38, and by stratified Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression (LDSC)39,40. MAGMA analysis38 is a functional genomics method that facilitates understanding of the biological relevance of complex traits through gene-set analysis. We performed three steps of MAGMA: a) standard gene analysis (results in Supplementary Data 5A); b) basic competitive analysis- Supplementary Data 5B, C); and conditional competitive analysis- Supplementary Data 5C. In the basic competitive gene-set analysis, we used the priority DCG together with three gene-sets with synaptic ontologies36- SynGO genes related to:(1) synaptic signaling, (2) presynaptic and (3) postsynaptic processes. Adding the SynGO genes was motivated by, on one hand, an overlap between SynGO genes and our DCG sets, especially for kIn-specific and C-kIn shared, and on another hand by their strong association with genetic risk for schizophrenia, which qualifies SynGO gene sets as including potentially “causal” genes34. In the context of an overlap between a gene set of interest and a causal gene set, it has been shown previously that within the basic competitive MAGMA framework, significant results could be explained just by correlated gene properties between the set of interest and the causal set, not by a direct link with the considered phenotype38. To avoid such a scenario, we added to our MAGMA pipeline the conditional competitive step, specifically developed to mitigate significant, but possibly irrelevant genetic associations between gene sets of interest and phenotypes38.

Overall, after multiple comparisons correction (see Methods), kIn-specific and three SynGO sets were significant for association with schizophrenia GWAS hits (Supplementary Data 5B). Two DCG sets had genes with gene-level association evidence for schizophrenia at genome-wide significance (p < 2.8 × 10−6): kIn-specific- had five genes (AKT3, SNAP91, KIAA1549, DGKZ and MAP1A), and C-specific- had five genes (RFTN2, C12Orf76, ARHGAP1, SPOCK3, and GRM3). For conditional competitive GSA, we used our priority DCG, while also controlling for SynGO genes (synaptic biological processes: pre- and postsynaptic, and synaptic signaling). kIn-specific remained significant (p ≈ 0.01-0.02) after conditioning on each of the SynGO sets (Supplementary Data 5C), suggesting that the kIn genetic signal is at least partially independent of some synaptic ontologies and raising the possibility that kIn genetic association with SCZ could be related to a failure of implicated genes to establish optimal connections in co-regulated co-expression modules. In summary, MAGMA showed that C-specific, kIn-specific and C-kIn shared DCG associated with schizophrenia genetic signal, although with various strengths.

Stratified LDSC39,40 is an enrichment method based on GWAS statistics of polygenic traits (e.g., schizophrenia) that estimates the enrichment of SNP-heritability for genomic annotations (i.e., in our case C-specific and kIn-specific DCG) based on a joint model with other annotations (i.e., coding and evolutionary conserved regions, regulatory elements and histone marks). The recommendation to obtain reliable results with stratified LDSC is that the gene set of interest covers at least about 1.7% of SNPs of the whole genome41. Gene sets of interest that fulfilled this condition were C-specific, kIn-specific, and genes C-kIn shared. These gene sets were therefore used for LDSC analysis. In addition to GWAS schizophrenia PGC3 summary stats, we also used GWAS summary statistics for a selection of other general complex traits (body-mass index- BMI, height, intelligence, smoking), and psychiatric and neurological syndromes and disorders (bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder- MDD, epilepsy, insomnia, ADHD, Alzheimer’s disorder, autism, alcohol dependence). Stratified LDSC showed a mixture of suggestive enrichment in heritability of the selected traits for the DCG sets (Supplementary Data 6A).

Specifically for the SCZ genetic signal, although all DCG genes had enrichment for heritability > 1 and C-specific and kIn-specific had p-values of enrichment < 0.05, Z scores were low (less than 1.7 for one-tailed tests), indicative of a weak genetic association possibly explained by the lack of power (a low proportion of SNPs within the gene sets explaining a small fraction of heritability). Interestingly, the three DGC sets had also significant enrichment in other traits- C-specific in Alzheimer’s Disease, kIn-specific in BMI and bipolar disorder and C-kIn shared genes in epilepsy, in line with the intricate genetic architecture shared by many complex traits (Supplementary Data 6B). In summary, case-control differences in network metrics suggested various levels of altered molecular connectivity in schizophrenia, potentially explainable by incoordination of gene expression co-regulatory programs between neurons and glial cells and consequent synaptic dysfunctions.

Further validation of schizophrenia differentially connected genes (DCG) in snRNAseq data

While the DCG sets from bulk RNA-Seq showed a meaningful biological profile related to aspects of schizophrenia risk, and in light of potential confounding effects of cell-type composition, we sought to explore them in more depth in cell-type-specific gene co-expression networks from CTRL and SCZ. We thus extended our study to the single-cell level using snRNA-Seq from adult brain42, Supplementary Data 7) and looked for differences in cell-type specific gene co-expression modules between CTRL and SCZ43 and tested the overlap between our DCG and differentially co-expressed modules across various cell types.

Cell-type specific co-expression modules differed between CTRL and SCZ by module eigengenes (DMEs) (Supplementary Data 8A) and were associated with biological pathways by gene ontology analysis. Collectively, nine of those modules were significantly enriched for biological ontologies that summarize fundamental pathways potentially causally related to schizophrenia,- for example, transcriptional dysregulations starting during neurodevelopment that modify critical cell structures (i.e., actin filament, myelin sheath) and functions (i.e., modulation of chemical transmission, intracellular calcium ion homeostasis, etc.) (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, our priority DCG (C-specific, kIn-specific and C-kIn shared) were each enriched in some of the resulting modules. In addition, the distinctions between our DCG remained apparent at the single cell level, with the kIn-specific DCG enriched (at trend level) in a module from L6b layer neurons, whereas C-specific DCG were confined to an oligodendrocyte’s module (Fig. 5E), consistent with the results from the bulk tissue analysis. Although the module-centric validation analysis in independent snRNA-Seq data from CTRL and SCZ donors supports our DCG from bulk RNA-Seq co-expression networks, one question remains: would similar DCG be found in co-expression networks from snRNA-seq data despite major methodological differences? More specifically, would the bulk DCG be reproducible in pseudo-bulk co-expression networks from CTRL and SCZ snRNA-seq? To answer this question, we performed a pseudo-bulk analysis on the PsychENCODE data (Ndonors: CTRL = 24, SCZ = 24)42. This validation analysis generally followed the bulk RNA-Seq pipeline with the inherent limitations related to the number of donors, expression data “cleaning” restricted to the available demographics and technical variables, the lack of caudate and hippocampus snRNA-Seq data and a reduced number of expressed genes with sufficient abundance compared to the DLPFC bulk RNA-Seq study (14,089 v. 22,542, respectively). After calculating the C, kIn and kTot parameters in each network and the case-control absolute differences (|∆|), we identified three sets of pseudo-bulk DCG, respectively: C = 1,121, kIn = 992, and kTot = 501. We then tested the overlap between bulk and pseudo-bulk DCG. The results showed significant overlap of pseudo-bulk C and pseudo-bulk kTot DCG with all bulk DCG; in contrast, pseudo-bulk kIn did not have any significant overlap with bulk DCG, which was to be expected, because the modular partition upon which kIn is based is plausibly different for co-expression networks with vastly distinct architectures (Supplementary Fig. 25).

We next applied a more stringent threshold to filter high confidence overlaps (p < 0.05 adjusted for multiple comparisons and strong effect size OR > = 3) (results in Supplementary Fig. 25A). We also sought to identify biological processes associated with the high confidence significant overlaps between pseudo-bulk and bulk DCG and found that four of the high confidence overlaps were enriched in GO:BP annotations. From those, a set of 142 genes representing the most significant overlap between pseudo-bulk C and bulk C-kIn shared was enriched in annotations related to oligodendrocyte functions (Supplementary Fig. 25B). Interestingly, the immune processes signature of bulk kIn-specific DCG (Fig. 4), but not the synaptic functions (i.e., modulation of chemical transmission) was reproduced in the overlap between bulk kIn-specific DCG and pseudo-bulk C DCG (Supplementary Fig. 25B).

Schizophrenia differentially connected genes (DCG) in cerebral organoid models

Further, to explore a potential neurodevelopmental signature of DCG in human cells putatively free of possible treatment effects, we used four sets of human brain organoids corresponding to our three brain regions and to findings implicating oligodendrocyte function (cerebral- COs, ventral striatal (VSO), hippocampal and oligodendrocytes spheroids- hOLs)44–46. Specifically, we performed trajectory analyses in the four sets of organoids47 by which organoid cell types (identified according to similarity in gene expression profiles) were organized along a “pseudotime” trajectory approximating early stages of development. Along such a trajectory, various genes show fluctuations in gene expression, characteristic for cells in a specific neurodevelopmental state. In our study, after calculating the trajectories in all organoids, we estimated the significance of overlap between DCG and genes expressed in various cell types ordered along the “pseudotime” trajectories.

All sets of DCG showed significant neurodevelopmental signatures, that is, high confidence overlap with genes changing trajectory by “pseudotime” in various sets of organoids (high confidence: p < 0.05 adjusted for multiple comparisons and strong effect size- OR ≥ 3). Notably, the strongest effect size was for the overlap between C-kIn shared DCG and hOL genes changing trajectory by “pseudotime” (OR = 11.9; Fig. 5F). We also sought to look at the enrichment of the reliable neurodevelopmental signatures of DCG in biological processes (GO:BP). Interestingly, the overlapping genes between C-kIn shared, and hOL cells (n = 103 genes) were uniquely associated with myelination and myelin assembly. Likewise, two sets of kIn-specific reliable neurodevelopmental signatures, representing the intersections with VSO genes (fetal quiescent and neurons) shared the annotation of modulation of chemical transmission.

When looking for reliable neurodevelopmental signatures of regDCG by trajectory analysis in the sets of human brain organoids, we found that only the caudate regDCG showed reliable neurodevelopmental signatures (padj < 0/05 and OR ≥ 3) in fetal quiescent cells, in both COs and VSO. Notably, caudate regDCG with reliable neurodevelopmental signatures were enriched in annotations related to forebrain development, action potential, cytoplasmic translation, substantia nigra development, cilium movement (Supplementary Fig. 26).

Sensitivity analyses- comparison of DCG with differentially expressed genes (DEG)

We have assumed that DCG will bring complementary information about biological relevance for schizophrenia in comparison with more traditional gene lists derived from differential gene expression analysis. Concretely, we hypothesized that alterations in the dynamics of gene co-expression (i.e., connectivity) in SCZ relative to CTRL do not simply follow case-control variations in gene expression. To this end, we calculated region-specific differentially expressed genes from our bulk RNA-seq cohort (CTRL vs. SCZ) (DEG) and compared the magnitude of changes in expression between CTRL and SCZ (the log-transformed fold change- logFC) and the absolute differences (|∆|) in network connectivity parameters. We also tested the overlap between DCG (priority and regDCG) and DEG for each brain region.

Unsurprisingly, given that our cohort was included in previous studies15,16,35 we found comparable DEG between CTRL and SCZ, with a relatively lower number for DLPFC and hippocampus (Ntotal = 114, respectively 159) and a higher number for caudate (Ntotal = 2984). Notably, in striking comparison to DCG, DEG had negligible regional consistency, with only three genes shared by all three regions (Supplementary Fig. 27). The DCG-DEG enrichment comparison showed that C-specific, kIn-specific and C-kIn shared DCG significantly overlapped only with caudate DEG in various proportions (C ≈ 52%, kIn ≈ 24% and C-kIn ≈ 74%) (Supplementary Data 9). Beyond the simple overlap between DCG and DEG in caudate, visualization and comparisons between gene expression and gene connectivity variations (logFC and absolute differences for network metrics) in schizophrenia relative to controls, suggest that these differences originate from distinct biological processes (Supplementary Figs. 28–36). Specifically, scatter plots of logFC against |∆| C, |∆| kIn and |∆| kTot show a non-linear relation between the two measures, with DCG showing smaller fold changes in differential gene expression and conversely, significant DEG displaying less changes in connectivity. Further, density plots reveal different distributions of logFC and network metrics’ |∆|. Finally, we note the significant and negative correlations between network metrics’ |∆| and logFC for all three brain regions. These latter results suggest that, with some exceptions, genes experiencing significant changes in connectivity, do not suffer major changes in expression level. Taken together, these results suggest that DEG and DCG originate largely from distinct biological processes, and therefore, DCG bring additional and possibly more granular information about network dysconnectivity at cellular and molecular levels in schizophrenia.

To further demonstrate the biological distinction of DCG, we performed a functional comparative analysis of biological processes (GO:BP) associated with DCG and DEG. We tested the enrichment of DEG in gene sets of interest along with DCG; and we looked for distinct neurodevelopmental signatures of DEG in the four sets of human brain organoids: cerebral- COs, ventral striatal- VSO, hippocampal, and oligospheroids- hOL. For these additional analyses and to segregate DEG signatures from DCG signatures, we filtered the DEG to include only those not overlapping with any DCG. This filtering resulted in three sets of region-specific DEG: 68/114 DLPFC, 76/159 Hippo and 1945/2984 Caudate.

The results showed that our priority DCG- C-specific, kIn-specific and C-kIn-shared maintained their unique biological profiles, while no regional specific DEG was enriched for meaningful ontologies (GO BP). Interestingly, kIn-specific and DEG caudate were the only sets significantly enriched in PGC3 loci genes. However, by using again the more stringent statistical threshold (p < 0.05 adjusted for multiple comparisons and strong effect size OR > = 3), the overlap between DEG Caudate and PGC3 loci genes did not surpass the high confidence threshold (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 37). Likewise, although DEG caudate was the only set enriched also for synaptic ontologies and druggable genome sets, none of these enrichments were of high confidence. In contrast, kIn and C-kIn shared DCG were the only sets that showed consistent high confidence overlaps with gene sets of interest (Supplementary Fig. 37). Finally, caudate DEG and C-specific, kIn-specific and C-kIn shared DCG showed seemingly significant overlap with genes changing trajectory by “pseudotime” in various sets of organoids. However, only the DCG showed high confidence overlaps that we consider reliable neurodevelopmental signatures of DCG (Fig. 5F).

We performed additional comparative post-GWAS analyses to estimate the enrichment of DEG in the SCZ signal, relative to our DCG sets. Rather unsurprisingly, the DEG caudate was the only DEG set enriched for SCZ genetic signal. In MAGMA, however, it was interesting that DEG caudate achieved a roughly similar p- value with kIn-specific DCG, although the kIn-specific set had only 166 genes, while DEG caudate had 1451 after aligning to hg19 and European 1000 genomes. To better understand if kIn-specific DCG was indeed independently associated with SCZ signal after adding the DEG sets, we performed a conditional MAGMA analysis simultaneously accounting for major synaptic pathways (SynGO postsynaptic, presynaptic, and signaling) and DEG caudate. Even in this more stringent multi-variate model, kIn-specific DCG remained significant (p = 0.025), demonstrating that the genetic risk associated with these genes is not merely a reflection of broader synaptic or regional dysfunction (Supplementary Data 5D). In LDSC, DEG caudate was also significantly enriched for SCZ heritability (Z = 2.93, p < 6e-10) (Supplementary Data 6). As the striatum is a core brain region targeted by antipsychotics, these results suggest an overlap between molecular pathways targeted by genetic risk and antipsychotic treatment.

Discussion

The molecular architecture of the brain, across regions, cell types and developmental stages, varies in a coordinated manner depending on biological rules between genes in pathways and networks. Disruptions in these relationships are thought to underlie brain illnesses. In the present study, we aimed to identify genes in the brains of patients with schizophrenia that deviate in their connectivity from the co-expression patterns observed in neurotypical (control) networks. Accordingly, we used three network connectivity parameters to highlight those genes that most significantly differ between controls and schizophrenia in their connectivity with other genes in the networks and therefore break the normal rules of connectivity within these networks.

We explored differentially connected genes (DCG) from two angles: (1) regional consistency under the assumption that a significant gene overlap across multiple brain regions that differ in cell composition and cytoarchitecture is biologically meaningful and can implicate shared schizophrenia etiopathogenic mechanisms, and (2) parameter specificity under the assumption that network metrics, i.e., the pattern of relationships between genes, can reveal distinct aspects of the schizophrenia biological substrate at the systems level. We then looked for DCG association with schizophrenia genetic signal, and enrichment in schizophrenia related ontologies, and druggable genome sets. In addition, we substantiated our DCG results in snRNA-Seq data collected from independent case-control cohorts and in cell-type-specific developmental trajectories calculated in human brain organoids, which presumably do not share environmental exposures with brain tissue.

To illustrate the ability of co-expression network-derived parameters to bring novel information about schizophrenia in the brain and to indirectly explore the potential role of medication effects on outliers, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEG) compared with neurotypical donors and demonstrated an inverse relationship between variations in gene expression level and gene connectivity. These results, along with analogous findings in 3D cell models and secondary analyses (i.e., extended post-GWAS analyses, enrichment in relevant biological pathways and gene sets of interest) suggest that illness state epiphenomena including medication and other chronic illness effects are not principally responsible for the altered connectivity dynamics.

Our data indicate that schizophrenia differentially connected gene sets capture both common and distinct aspects of the biology underlying schizophrenia’s complex disorganization of brain co-expression network architecture. For example, in comparison with C-specific DCG, kIn-specific DCG had the strongest association with schizophrenia GWAS signal: it was the only set significantly enriched in prioritized PGC3 loci genes and associated with schizophrenia genetic signal by MAGMA, while marginally associated with schizophrenia heritability by LDSC. Interestingly, this set of DCG was also enriched with various strengths for the heritability of other complex traits, neuropsychiatric and neurological disorders, all of which showed previously shared heritability with schizophrenia48,49. These results are consistent with the fact that the kIn-specific gene set was enriched for GO sets related to synaptic transmission and to deep cortical layer neurons, which have been highlighted in many studies related to genetic risk for schizophrenia. Further, the significant enrichment of kIn-specific DCG in BMI heritability in addition to SCZ, further supports the previously suggested shared genetic architecture between the two traits48, and indicates a potential link between schizophrenia and cardiovascular disorder comorbidity50. Intriguingly, the C-specific set, while showing weak enrichment in SCZ heritability, is more strongly enriched in Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) heritability, results consistent with previous studies that demonstrated a shared genetic architecture between SCZ and AD51, although the etiopathogenic mechanisms leading to the two disorders are largely different52. In contrast to kIn-specific genes, the C-specific gene set is enriched in oligodendrocyte function, which also has been implicated in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’ Disease53. While comparisons of gene annotations in the brain and genetic association with risk are standard procedures in functional genomics studies of brain illness, the assumption that our DCG would underly genetic association signals is not intuitively obvious. How genes interact with each other in the context of illness and how they account for risk are potentially unrelated characteristics25.

Biological annotation of DCG through gene set enrichment analysis and gene ontology analysis brought additional insights about their relationship with schizophrenia vulnerability. As noted, gene ontology analysis showed an interesting pathway specialization of kIn-specific and C-specific DCG. While C-specific was biased toward oligodendrocytes differentiation and axon ensheathment, kIn-specific was enriched in a mixture of the rubrics of immune processes and modulation of chemical transmission. The apparent functional specialization of C-specific and kIn-specific DCG was further supported by the results from snRNA-seq co-expression analysis and trajectory analysis in brain organoids (COs, VSO and hippocampal) and human oligodendrocyte spheroids (hOLs). The significant overlap between C-specific DCG and C-kIn shared with a differential co-expression module from oligodendrocytes, as opposed to the overlap between kIn-specific and a differential module from the multiform, and principally cortical layer- L6b, raises the intriguing possibility that network topography measures could capture functionally distinct, cell-specific patterns of connectivity in the brain transcriptome. Furthermore, the neurodevelopmental signatures of C-specific, C-kIn shared, and kIn-specific suggested by a trajectory analysis in human brain organoids add further evidence, albeit indirect, of the association between network connectivity measures and different brain cell types. Specifically, the enrichment of C-specific and C-kIn shared DCG in a gene set whose expression kinetics varies in oligodendrocytes by “pseudotime” trajectory analysis adds also a novel interpretation of altered neurodevelopment in schizophrenia, considering that oligodendrocytes insulate cortical axons in a protracted manner. Oligodendrocyte dysfunction originating during early brain development could contribute to both altered connectivity across distributed neural circuits as they are formed and then later near the clinical onset of the disorder54. However, these varying associations need to be interpreted with some caution, as enrichment, per se, is a statistical conclusion that does not establish functional specificity.

Compared with our priority DCG in schizophrenia-based co-expression network metrics, differentially expressed genes (DEG) based on control vs. schizophrenia contrasts, and regionally specific regDCG showed a less coherent picture. First, compared with priority DCG, the DEG were regionally inconsistent, with virtually no overlap across regions, and a disproportionately higher number of DEG in caudate, compared with DLPFC and hippocampus, which might be explained by antipsychotic drug treatment, as our group previously demonstrated35,55. In this context, we emphasize the distinctiveness of our differentially connected genes (DCG), in that they do not simply mirror changes in expression level and putatively reflect different biological processes, related to higher-level co-regulatory mechanisms involving more than simple gene on-off regulation. Likewise, while we cannot rule out at least a partial medication effect on our DCG gene sets, we emphasize the higher regional consistency and orientation toward more specific cell-types that make less probable a confounding effect of AP treatment that impacts basically all brain cellular types56. The data in the organoid cell models indicate that the DCG can be independent of drug effects.

While a neuronal role in schizophrenia etiopathogenesis has been extensively documented, less is known about the contribution of glial cells, although recent studies have started to implicate a role played by glia in schizophrenia synaptic dysfunctions57,58. Oligodendrocytes, astrocytes and microglia, currently considered key players in shaping synapse structure and modulation of synaptic transmission, can be affected in multiple ways in schizophrenia, starting in neurodevelopment and continuing through adult life59. In particular, DCG based on clustering coefficient across three brain regions seems to be related to the biology of oligodendrocytes. Since glial cells in general, and oligodendrocytes in particular, play a significant role in facilitating synaptic transmission and promoting synaptic plasticity (e.g., through adaptive myelination)60, it is tempting to speculate that these results uncover a potential limitation of oligodendrocytes’ gene expression regulatory programs in schizophrenia, which translates into sub-optimal responses to neuronal activity and failure to support neuronal synapse activity through adaptive myelination. However, it should be noted that the interplay between neuronal cells and oligodendrocytes in synaptic plasticity is far more complex and involves also astrocytes and microglia61.

Our study is not without limitations. First, while we extended and innovatively adapted the standard WGCNA pipeline, we measured a limited number of network parameters. However, considering that little is known about the biological aspects captured by network metrics, our biologically meaningful results are a proof of concept that a more in-depth characterization of gene co-expression networks beyond just finding modules is worth pursuing in clinical and non-clinical populations. Second, our postmortem brain study in bulk RNA-Seq, although suggestive and well powered, is not ideal for inferring cell-type specific abnormalities related to dysconnectivity in gene co-expression networks. However, the relative validation of our results in snRNA-Seq from independent data sets of adult brain and from human 3D cell models (organoids) supports the bulk tissue findings and warrants further experimentally oriented studies to clarify how various cell types interact to generate molecular phenotypes of schizophrenia. Likewise, future studies of regulatory dynamics starting early in neurodevelopment will be necessary to clarify when the disruptions occur and how they relate to the etiopathogenesis of schizophrenia. Third, we focused in this study on the most deviant genes by connectivity in schizophrenia, the outliers for absolute difference. However, these may not be the only consequential differences in co-expression network architecture between schizophrenia and control, and it is conceivable that applying other filtering methods to highlight differentially connected genes will show a more fine-grained image of connectivity patterns altered in schizophrenia. Fourth, while we focused on regionally shared differentially connected genes, our study also suggested that numerous genes have region-specific patterns of deviance in schizophrenia vs. control. These regionally specific, differentially connected genes deserve further exploration, considering that differential signatures of gene expression can contribute to the development of structural architecture in the brain and areal functional specialization31,32. Fifth, while the genetic enrichment results were modest, these results should be interpreted in the context of a possible lack of statistical power (i.e., reduced size of the gene sets, specifically for kIn) and SCZ high polygenicity, with the signal broadly distributed across regulatory regions. This is likely especially the case with LDSC, as the marginally significant results could be explained by signal “dilution” within larger, overlapping categories in the baseline model. We also emphasize that the convergence of LDSC and MAGMA results, although modest, implicates a consistent, core genetic signal associated with SCZ. This being said, we also note that the DCG may be targeting an aspect of schizophrenia pathogenesis that complements but is not coterminate with genetic risk25.

In conclusion, our study of differentially connected genes (DCG) in schizophrenia brings further insights about possible patterns of dysregulation in this disorder, likely involving an underlying incoordination of gene expression regulatory pathways between neurons and certain glial cells, oligodendrocytes in particular, that warrant experimental validation.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study complied with all ethical regulations, and it was approved by the relevant institutional review boards and ethics regulation committees (details in the section of Data selection and processing for the bulk RNA-Seq study).

Data selection and processing for the bulk RNA-Seq study

Postmortem human brain tissue from the LIBD Human Brain Repository was used; the brain tissue was collected under standardized protocols for brain acquisition, processing, and curation (location, legal authorizations, informed consent, clinical review/ diagnosis)15,16,35. In summary, brain tissues were obtained by autopsy from: the Offices of the Chief Medical Examiner of the District of Columbia and of the Commonwealth of Virginia, Northern District (protocol 90-M-0142 approved by the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH)/National Institutes of Health (NIH) Institutional Review Board) and received by LIBD under an MTA, the Office of the Chief Medical Examiner for the State of Maryland under protocol number 12-24 from the State of Maryland Department of Health, and from the Office of the Medical Examiner, Department of Pathology, Western Michigan University Homer Stryker MD School of Medicine in Kalamazoo, Michigan, University of North Dakota Department of Pathology in Grand Forks, North Dakota, and Santa Clara County Office of the Medical Examiner-Coroner in Santa Clara County, California under protocol number 20111080 from WCG Institutional Review Board. All brain tissues were obtained after informed consent from the legal next of kin under the respective protocols, as reviewed and approved by the previously named IRBs.

Audiotaped informed consent to study brain tissue was obtained from the legal next of kin on every case collected at the NIMH and LIBD. After the next of kin provided audiotaped informed consent to brain donation, a standardized 36-item telephone screening interview was conducted (the LIBD autopsy questionnaire) to gather additional information (i.e., demographic, clinical, psychiatric, substance abuse, treatment, medical and social history). A psychiatric narrative summary was written for every donor to include data from multiple sources, including the autopsy questionnaire, medical examiner documents (investigative reports, autopsy reports and toxicology testing), macroscopic and microscopic neuropathological examinations of the brain and extensive psychiatric, detoxification and medical record reviews and/or supplemental family informant interviews using the mini international neuropsychiatric interview. Two board-certified psychiatrists independently reviewed every case to arrive at DSM-V lifetime psychiatric and substance use disorder diagnoses, and if agreement was not reached between the two reviewers, a third board-certified psychiatrist was consulted.

Only donors free from significant neuropathology, including cerebrovascular accidents and neurodegenerative diseases, were included for brain tissue collection. Brain specimens from the Clinical Brain Disorders Branch were transferred from the NIMH to the LIBD under a material transfer agreement. Available postmortem samples were selected based on RNA quality (RIN ≥ 6).

A toxicological analysis was performed in each case. The non-psychiatric non-neurological neurotypical individuals had no known history of significant psychiatric or neurological illnesses, including substance abuse. Positive toxicology was exclusionary for neurotypical individuals but not for individuals with psychiatric disorders.

The number of donors from each brain region was: DLPFC-Ndonors = 297 (control- CTRL = 182, schizophrenia- SCZ = 115); caudate-Ndonorss = 349 (CTRL = 215, SCZ = 134); hippocampus- Ndonors = 250 (CTRL = 162, SCZ = 88). Donors overlap for the three regions was Ndonors = 155. Details about donors’ demographics (i.e., age of death, sex and ancestry) are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

RNA sequencing: total RNA was extracted from DLPFC gray matter (BA9/46), hippocampus (hippocampus proper and subiculum complex) and caudate (dorsal third, lateral to the lateral ventricle)15,16,35. Sequencing libraries were constructed with the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Preparation kit with Ribo-Zero Gold ribosomal RNA depletion.

Raw sequencing reads were quality checked with FastQC62 and corrected with Trimmomatic63 if necessary. The quality checked sequencing reads were mapped to the hg38/GRCh38 human reference genome with HISAT2 and Salmon (v2.0.4)64, and expression for genes was summarized in counts based on GENCODE v25 (GRCh38.p7)65 and converted to RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads).

Genes with sufficient abundance in more than 80% of samples (RPKM greater than cutoffs determined by the inflection point of feature retention and sample consistency plots provided in expression_cutoff function from jaffelab v.0.99.30 R package66) were then normalized by log2(x + 1) transformation. Lastly, samples outliers for gene expression determined by hierarchical clustering of the Euclidean distances measured from the normalized expression data67, were removed from the analysis. After quality checking and normalization, the number of genes with enough abundance of expression per region were: DLPFC- Ngenes = 22,542, Caudate- Ngenes = 19,954 and Hippocampus- Ngenes = 20,708, and the gene overlap between the three regions was Ngenes = 18,874.

Genotyping was performed as described in ref. 15. In brief, it comprised genotype imputation with IMPUTE2 and Shape-IT after removing low-quality and rare variants. Common variants with MAF > 5% present in > 90% of samples, and in Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE p-value > 0.001) were retained. Multidimensional scaling (MDS) was performed to calculate ancestry components (genomic PCs).

Linear regression models with normalized expression data as the dependent variable and covariates of interest (diagnosis) and non-interest (biological and technical) were used to “clean” (adjust) the normalized gene expression from the three brain regions. Before applying the cleaning step, variation inflation factor (VIF)- a measure of multicollinearity– was calculated with the vif function from the R car v.3.1-3 package68. After discarding variables with VIF ≥ 10, several covariates of non-interest were retained for expression adjustment: 1. Age, sex,10 ancestry components (genomic PCs), technical covariates (RIN, mitoRate) and 11 quality surrogate variables (qSVs) for DLPFC and caudate; 2. Age, sex, 10 ancestry components, 12 qSVs and technical covariates (RIN, totalAssignedGene and mitoRate) for hippocampus.

Quality Surrogate Variables (qSVs) were calculated with Quality Surrogate Variable Analysis (QSVA) that consists of identifying transcript features most susceptible to RNA degradation by estimating a “degradation matrix” quantified as the coverage of the susceptible features in customized sequencing libraries and performing a principal component analysis on the “degradation matrix” that yields a number of k principal components named “quality surrogate variables” (qSVs). Quality surrogate variables (qSVs) were extracted from region-specific RNA degradation matrices calculated as described in ref. 20.

The number of qSVs was pre-determined with the num.sv R function that uses the method of Buja and Eyuboglu20.

Creating control and schizophrenia co-expression network with WGCNA

Prior to WGCNA, a scale-free topology analysis with 30 soft thresholding beta (β) powers (pickSoftThreshold function from WGCNA package version 1.6967,69 was applied to control and schizophrenia expression sets to select a common beta (β) power corresponding to an R^2 ≥ 0.8. After calculating the beta power, CTRL and SCZ datasets were bootstrapped by using a sampling procedure with replacement (sample.int function in base R); 50 CTRL and 50 SCZ gene expression bootstraps were then used for robust WGCNA (consensus analysis), following a procedure adapted from ref. 70.

For each group, the consensus network analysis was performed with the consensusTOM function (correlation type = bi-weight midcorrelation; type of network = signed, β powers: 10 for DLPFC and caudate, 14 for hippocampus, networkCalibration = “single quantile”, consensusQuantile = 0.5). The results were represented by two consensus networks (one for CTRL, one for SCZ) for each brain region. Consensus networks were then used to calculate the topological overlap matrix (TOM) dissimilarity (dissTOM). Finally, dissTOMs were used to detect modules by hierarchical clustering with a deep split parameter = 3 and a minimum module size of 50 nodes (genes). The modules were labeled with pre-specified colors implemented in WGCNA.

As a preliminary exploratory step, relevant biological mechanisms associated with modules of co-expression in control and schizophrenia groups were summarized for each brain region by gene ontology enrichment analysis with enrichGO function implemented in the clusterProfiler package v.4.8.371. This function calculates the gene ontology overrepresentation (i.e., biological process- GO-BP) with a hypergeometric test by which functional terms are tested for statistically significant enrichment in gene lists of interest, while using as a statistical domain the region-specific background (the total gene set: DLPFC = 22,542; caudate = 19,954, hippocampus = 20,708). False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was used for multiple comparisons correction. Parameters used for the enrichGO function were a minimum gene set to be analyzed- 100, respectively a maximum gene set-5000; method for p-value adjustment- Benjamini-Hochberg; p- value cutoff = 0.001, q-value cut-off = 0.001. Visualization of enrichment results was performed with functions from the rrvgo R package v.1.12.272.

Calculation of intra-modular and general connectivity parameters

The next step of gene co-expression network analysis was represented by the calculation of intramodular and general network connectivity parameters and their differences between control and schizophrenia (Fig. 1B). Of note, while it may be interesting to statistically compare CTRL and SCZ initial co-expression modules, interpreting any differences is complicated by the probabilistic nature of module detection methods and their sensitivity to various confounders. Therefore, we propose here to simplify the problem by applying a single modular partition of the CTRL network as a standard reference to both CTRL and SCZ co-expression networks. We then ask how every gene’s connectivity differs in schizophrenia, by using three network metrics for each gene within each region-by-diagnosis network: one intramodular connectivity measure (degree) (kIn), dependent on the CTRL modular partition, and two network-wide module-independent connectivity measures (total degree- kTot, and clustering coefficient- C) (Fig. 1B). We posit that this approach will highlight genes whose connectivity is likely deviant in schizophrenia in comparison to the connectivity metrics of the same genes in control brain, without introducing noise by running multiple module decompositions.

In total, three network metrics were calculated: the first parameter- intramodular degree (intramodular connectivity)- was dependent on modular partition and was normalized by module size (kIn). The two additional parameters were clustering coefficient (C) and total degree (total connectivity) (kTot). kIn represents the sum of connection strengths between a node (i.e., gene) and all the other nodes in the same module. C is a network parameter of cliquishness, measuring how connected the direct neighbors of a node are. kTot is the sum of connection strengths between a node and all the other nodes in the network (Fig. 1B). Using the module partition from the CTRL co-expression networks, kIn was calculated from diagnosis-specific adjacency matrices with the intramodular Connectivity function from WGCNA. We also normalized kIn by module size to correct for a bias that shows stronger connectivity in larger modules compared to smaller modules.

Clustering coefficient (C) was calculated with the clusterCoef function in WGCNA, which also takes as input adjacency matrices.

To quantify the brain region specific, gene-wise control vs. schizophrenia differences in network parameters (kIn, C and kTot), a measure of magnitude- the absolute difference (|∆|=|x1-x2 | )- was used to summarize each gene’s differences in network parameters between control and schizophrenia (Fig. 1C). To explore how consistent the distributions of absolute differences are across the three regions, robust correlations (Kendall tau) were calculated (Fig. 2).

The correlation analyses were performed and results visualized with functions from R packages PerformanceAnalytics (v.2.0.8)73 and GGally (v.2.2.1)74. The reason for selecting robust (i.e., non-parametric) correlations was dictated by the non-normal shape of the distribution for the selected absolute differences.

The most differential nodes, respectively genes outliers for the absolute differences (|∆|) in network parameters, were identified with the boxplot function in base R (Fig. 1C). Region specific patterns of |∆| distributions and outliers for each parameter are visualized in Fig. 3. The outliers were representative of genes with the most deviant pattern of intramodular or network connectivity in schizophrenia vs. control and were used in subsequent steps to determine their potential biological relevance for schizophrenia.

For gene-based association of DCG with schizophrenia genetic risk, linear regression models in MAGMA (v.1.10) were used in three steps: 1. gene analysis, 2. basic competitive and 3. conditional competitive gene-set analyses (GSA)38. Essentially, the MAGMA principle is to model the joint GWAS effects of all the SNPs in and around a gene, considering linkage disequilibrium (LD). In this manner, genes become units of analysis and can be interrogated with respect to associated biological pathways through competitive gene-set analysis. Before running MAGMA, all genes expressed in the three brain regions were aligned to the GRCh37 (hg19) assembly and used for annotation of SNPs from PGC3 SCZ GWAS, (i.e., all SNPs in a window around the transcription region of the gene: + 1 kB/ + 0.5 kB), as recommended in the MAGMA protocol. Due to the high LD, the MHC region (genes within chr6:28,477,797-33,448,354) was excluded from the analysis. First step included gene analysis of all expressed genes (N = 18090) with the SNP-wise model; the association of each gene with SCZ genetic vulnerability by considering the LD structure inferred from the European reference panel from the1000 Genomes, was performed with a Bonferroni correction α = 0.05/18090; the results from the gene-based analysis were used in a basic competitive gene-set analysis, with gene sets from priority DCG (C-specific, kIn-specific, C-kIn shared), SynGO pathways (synaptic signaling, pre- and postsynaptic processes) and for comparison, differentially expressed genes (DEG) from DLPFC, caudate and hippocampus. A Bonferroni correction α = 0.05/9 was used, where the denominator is the number of gene sets. Finally, using the same gene sets, a competitive conditional gene analysis was performed, using the SynGO processes as conditions36.

Stratified LDSC was used to test the DCG for enrichment in schizophrenia heritability. Following recommendations from the LDSC resource website (https://alkesgroup.broadinstitute.org/LDSCORE), S-LDSC was run for each gene list with the baseline LD model v2.2 that included 97 annotations to control for the LD between variants with other functional annotations in the genome. First, genome positions for gene sets were converted from hg38 to hg19 reference assembly with biomaRt (v.2.62.0)78, to be concordant with the GWAS summary statistics. Then, genomic intervals for each gene within 100 kb upstream or downstream of the gene to capture regulatory regions were defined. HapMap Project Phase3 SNPs were used as regression SNPs, and 1000 Genomes SNPs of European ancestry samples were used as reference SNPs; all SNPs were downloaded from the LDSC resource website. For this study, DCG were tested for enrichment in heritability for several complex traits: schizophrenia, the trait of interest and for comparison: ADHD, Bipolar disorder, Major Depressive Disorder, alcohol dependence, epilepsy, Alzheimer’s disorder, autism, intelligence, insomnia, smoking, height, body-mass index (BMI).

Differential gene expression

For differential gene expression in controls (CTRL) compared with schizophrenia (SCZ) based on bulk RNA-Seq data, region- specific linear regression models were fitted on log2 transformed expression matrices by using functions implemented in the limma (v.3.6.21) package79. Models for the three regions were built on the design matrices used for data cleaning that included as covariates diagnosis, age, sex, race, qSVs and genomic PCs selected after removing colinear variables (see Eq.1 in the Data selection and Processing section). After fitting the models, differentially expressed genes (DEG) between CTRL and SCZ were identified based on statistics calculated with the eBayes function and included only those statistically significant for p < 0.05 after false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment (method Benjamini-Hochberg).

Cell-type specific Differential Module Eigengene (DME) analysis with hdWGCNA

To validate the DCG from bulk RNA-Seq data and to infer a potential cell-type specificity snRNA-Seq data, DLPFC tissue from an independent cohort of donors (recently published by the PsychENCODE consortium42 was used. Specifically, the matrices of gene expression (counts) were downloaded from the brainSCOPE resource (https://brainscope.gersteinlab.org) and a subset of 48 donors was selected (24 CTRL and 24 SCZ; demographic details in Supplementary Data 7).

Expression data from CTRL and SCZ was pre-processed, merged and integrated into donor-specific Seurat objects with routines from the Seurat v.580 framework. Quality check (QC) was performed on the individual Seurat objects (i.e, filtering low-quality data and artifacts), cell annotations were added for each sample, and all individual Seurat objects were merged into a large compact object that included cell-type-specific expression data and metadata (i.e., information about the demographics and diagnosis of the donors). Standard processing steps were then applied on the merged object, according to the Seurat pipeline: normalization, finding variable features, scaling, principal component analysis (PCA) and QC visualization techniques.

The merged Seurat object was used to calculate cell-type specific gene co-expression modules with functions from the high-dimensional WGCNA (hdWGCNA v.0.4.00) package in R43, suitable for manipulating large and sparse expression matrices characteristic for single-cell data.

First, the Seurat object was subset in glia (including expression data from astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocytes precursors (OPC) and microglia), excitatory neurons (including intra-telencephalic (IT) layers: L2/3, L4, L5, L6 and the deepest layer- L6b) and inhibitory neurons (including parvalbumin (Pvalb), somatostatin (Sst) and vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (Vip). Cell types with too few cells per sample (i.e., immune cells, endothelial cells, and others) were excluded from the analysis. Each cell class object was then processed with the SetupForWGCNA function, with a gene filtering fraction of 0.05 (genes with zero counts, i.e., not expressed in at least 5% of cells, were removed for noise reduction and network quality).

Second, meta-cells were created. Of note, hdWGCNA deals with sparse and high-dimensional data by creating meta-cells (i.e., clusters of cells with similar expression profiles originating from the same sample), subsequently used as units of analysis within a workflow similar to bulk RNA-Seq WGCNA. To create meta-cells, the MetacellsByGroups function was used, and cells with similar expression profiles within specific groups (i.e., oligodendrocytes, or L2/3 IT neurons) were clustered by PCA with parameters: K-nearest neighbor- K = 25, minimum cell number = 100, and maximum number of shared cells between metacells = 5. By this dimensionality reduction method, a more robust and cleaner dataset for gene co-expression network analysis was created.

Third, meta-cells were pre-processed by scaling, normalization, a further PCA and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and finally QC-ed by visualization techniques.

Finally, expression data from meta-cells was used as input for the usual WGCNA pipeline. Notably, due to the lower number of donors and the inherent constraints of snRNA-Seq data, the pipeline was applied to CTRL + SCZ sets, followed by module eigengene calculation and comparisons (CTRL vs. SCZ). Twelve cell-type specific co-expression networks per donor were created: from glial cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocytes precursors-OPC, microglia), excitatory neurons (cortical layers: L2/3 intra-telencephalic (IT), L4 IT, L5 IT, L6 IT and L6b) and inhibitory neurons (parvalbumin- PVALB, somatostatin- SST and vasoactive intestinal polypeptide- VIP).

Due to the data non-independence, with multiple cell-type networks created from each donor, cell-type specific MEs were extracted and compared with linear mixed-effects models from lmer4 (v.1.1-37) package81, accounting for donor status (random effects), and diagnosis and other covariates (age and post-mortem interval- PMI) as fixed effects.

After identifying modules with DME for the selected cell types, a biological profiling in a similar manner with modules and gene sets from bulk RNA-Seq co-expression networks was performed by testing for enrichment in biological ontologies with enrichGO and compareCluster functions implemented in the clusterProfiler R package71.

Calculation of intra-modular and general connectivity parameters from DLPFC snRNA-Seq pseudo-bulk data

In a complementary analysis, we tested if similar sets of DCG are detectable in co-expression networks from single-nuclei RNA-seq data (snRNA-Seq). First, pseudo-bulk expression data from the PsychENCODE snRNA-Seq used for hdWGCNA was generated (Ndonors: CTRL = 24, SCZ = 24)42. Roughly, the bulk RNA-Seq study’s pipeline was applied, with inherent limitations related to the number of donors, expression data “cleaning” restricted by the available demographics and technical variables, the lack of caudate and hippocampus snRNA-Seq data and a reduced number of genes with sufficient abundance compared to the DLPFC bulk RNA-Seq study (14,089/22,542). Importantly, the pseudo-bulk expression Supplementary Data et was created across all cell types. The expression matrices were normalized by library size, to account for the fact that not all donors participate with the same number of nuclei to the pseudo-bulk construction; similar data cleaning and processing like in the bulk RNA-Seq study was applied and the residuals were used in a standard WGCNA pipeline to create one pseudo-bulk co-expression network for CTRL and one for SCZ; the intramodular connectivity was calculated using the same reference CTRL set of modules for both groups. The clustering coefficient and kTot parameters were calculated as described in the bulk RNA-seq study. Finally, the outliers for case-control absolute differences (|∆|) in network parameters- clustering coefficient (C), intramodular degree (kIn) and total degree (kTot) were extracted. These outliers were named pseudo-bulk Differentially Connected Genes (DCG). The overlap between bulk and pseudo-bulk DCG was tested with routines from GeneOveralp (v.1.42.0) R package77 as explained in the section about enrichment in SCZ relevant gene sets.

For a more rigorous assessment, a high confidence overlap between bulk and pseudo-bulk DCG was considered only for the overlaps with high statistical significance (p < 0.05 after correcting for multiple comparisons) and strong effect size (odds ratio OR > = 3). The high confidence overlaps between bulk and pseudo-bulk DCG were subsequently tested for enrichment in relevant GO:BP annotations.

Inferring a neurodevelopmental signature of DCG with Monocle trajectory analysis

To infer the neurodevelopmental signature of our DCG, we studied their enrichment in genes with cell type expression ordered along a “pseudotime” trajectory calculated with Monocle v.347 in four human 3D cell models: a. Cerebral organoids (COs) (N = 16,852 cells- donors: 4 CTRL low PRS risk and 3 SCZ high PRS risk, days of development 37, 70 and 15044; b. oligospheroids (hOLs) (N = 295 cell from two lines/ donors, days of development grouped in 15 bins between days 127 and 406; GEO accession number: GSE11501145; c. ventral striatal organoids (VSO) (N = 16,852 cells- donors: 4 CTRL low PRS risk and 3 SCZ high PRS risk, days of development 70 and 150)44, and d. hippocampal organoids (N = 31,908 cells- GEO accession number GSE264363, two single-cell samples from day 70 and day 81)46. Cells from all sets- COs, VSO, hippocampal and hOLs were first annotated with singleR (v.2.8.0)82 package using as a reference a fetal Supplementary Data et available in scRNAseq (v.2.20.0) package83. snRNA-Seq organoids data was partitioned in cell-type specific subsets (cell data sets- CDS = the objects/ units for Monocle3), then the Monocle3 workflow was applied to all CDS: pre-processing (normalization and removal of batch effects), dimensionality reduction with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), identifying cell clusters and cluster comparisons to find specific cell markers, and finally trajectory analysis. Essentially, in this latest step, the program is using graph-analytical techniques (i.e., minimum spanning tree- mst calculation) to “learn” a trajectory along which cells in development change their expression). The principle is that cells close to each other (“neighbors”) along this trajectory are in a similar developmental stage and have similar expression relative to the trajectory. Because in the COs, VSO and hippocampal cell-type specific CDS, we did not see a clear separation by days of development, we used as a starting point for trajectory one end of the mst; for hOl, where OPC and more mature oligodendrocytes were better separated along the mst, we used OPC as starting points for trajectory. By comparing expression and calculating statistics with specific non-linear methods (i.e., Moran’s I test- a statistical test for spatial autocorrelation), it is possible to infer genes with cell-type specific correlated expression explained by the developmental stage. In our case, to select the most significant genes by Moran’s I statistics, we selected again outliers by the IQR rule. We then calculated the enrichment of DCG and in addition, of caudate DEG in outlier genes with the most significant change in cell-type specific expression along the trajectories, by using the GeneOverlap package77 as explained in the section about enrichment in SCZ relevant gene sets.

Supplementary analysis to explore the relationship between intra-modular and general connectivity parameters and cell-type composition in bulk RNA-Seq

First, a cell-type deconvolution of bulk RNA-Seq data was performed, and cell-type proportions in CTRL and SCZ were calculated. For this purpose, a collection of functions from the CIBERSORT (Cell-Type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts) algorithm implemented in R was used84. CIBERSORT is a partial deconvolution method that uses a linear support vector regression (SVR) algorithm to estimate the cell type proportion from bulk RNA-Seq expression data by using as input a matrix of reference gene expression signatures representing each cell type of interest.

For consistency, the same reference of gene expression signatures was used, that is an empirically derived signature matrix based on single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data from the human brain (DLPFC)85. This matrix defines unique, non-overlapping gene expression profiles for 19 distinct cell subtypes (e.g., specific excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, and immune cells).

The bulk RNA-Seq data for each region (DLPFC, caudate, and hippocampus) were quantile normalized to ensure the expression distributions were comparable across all samples, then “unmixed” with the CIBERSORT algorithm to obtain cell-type proportions of the 19 cell types. Permutation tests implemented in CIBERSORT were used to determine the statistical significance of the estimated cell-type proportions.

Second, to address the possibility that differences in connectivity are simply related to differences in cell-type composition between CTRL and SCZ, a complementary strategy was adopted. While the differentially connected genes were generated from co-expression networks, a cell-type co-expression-based measure (more suitable in our view to addressing the relationship between the differential connectivity and cellular landscape in three regions) was calculated. More specifically, a Cell-Type Specificity score (CTS) from the same adjacency matrices used to calculate the network parameters was derived. Conceptually, the CTS score is based on the principle that genes tend to be differentially co-expressed/ co-regulated depending on their functional role in a specific biological context (i.e., a specific cell-type). To calculate the CTS score, a curated dataset of validated cellular markers was assembled from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA), PsychENCODE papers in Science and from neuronal genes previously associated with SCZ pathogenesis (i.e., DRD1, DRD2, GAD1, etc.).

Essentially, a CTS score can be defined as the average adjacency (or correlation) between a gene and all markers from a specific cell type.

After calculating the CTS score for each gene in the co-expression network, the absolute case-control differences (|∆|) in CTS scores were calculated. Correlations between case-control |∆| CTS score and |∆| C, |∆| kIn, and |∆| kTot for each brain region were calculated. For visualization, the |∆| CTS scores were plotted against |∆| C, |∆| kIn, and |∆| kTot with routines in the ggplot2 R package86.

Verifying case-control differences in co-expression-based gene connectivity by permutations testing

To evaluate whether the observed absolute differences (|∆|) in network parameters between control (CTRL) and schizophrenia (SCZ) cohorts exceed what would be expected by chance, a permutation-based null-model approach (n = 10,000 iterations) was implemented.

Importantly, an initial inspection of the raw network metrics that revealed significant differences in global variance between groups, particularly in the caudate (i.e., variance ratio CTRL/SCZ = 7.35, p < 2.2e-16), was performed. To satisfy the assumption of exchangeability- a prerequisite for valid permutation testing87- a Z-score normalization (scaling) on all metrics (C, kIn, kTot) for each region was performed. This ensured that the permutation test identified gene-specific topological shifts rather than artifacts of global variance fluctuations.

Then, for each metric, a null distribution by shuffling group labels across the entire set of genes in each region was generated. A 95% confidence boundary was established from the 97.5th percentile of the absolute differences (|∆Z | ) observed in the permuted data.
