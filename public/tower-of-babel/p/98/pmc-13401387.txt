Exploration of the Potential Mechanisms of Yinqiao Powder in Treating Atopic Dermatitis: Integrating Network Pharmacology, Single-Cell, and Bulk RNA Sequencing
Authors: Lila Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeYinqiao powder (YQP) is a traditional Chinese medicine formula that has been widely used clinically to treat infectious diseases such as respiratory tract infections, influenza, and pneumonia, exhibiting anti-inflammatory and antiviral properties. This study aimed to investigate its therapeutic potential for atopic dermatitis (AD) and to identify its key target genes and active components.MethodsWe performed mRNA transcriptome sequencing on blood samples collected from 15 adult patients with AD and 15 adult healthy controls. Potential targets were identified by intersecting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with known YQP target genes. Key genes were pinpointed using protein-protein interaction networks and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. We estimated immune cell proportions, constructed regulatory networks, and used molecular docking to predict active ingredients. Single-cell RNA sequencing data (GSE180885) was also analyzed to identify key cell types and track gene expression.ResultsFifty-two potential target genes were identified. From these, four key genes—CTNNB1, ERBB2, TP53, and HIF1A—were highlighted, potentially involved in carbon metabolism and organelle biosynthesis. A nomogram model based on these genes showed strong predictive power for AD. Immune analysis linked resting CD4+ memory T cells with TP53 and HIF1A. Molecular docking suggested beta-carotene, aloe-emodin, and quercetin as potential active ingredients binding to these key targets. Single-cell analysis identified keratinocytes as a key population, with key gene expression varying during their differentiation.ConclusionThis study indicates that CTNNB1, ERBB2, TP53, and HIF1A are key targets of YQP in AD treatment, with beta-carotene, aloe-emodin, and quercetin as likely active components. These findings provide a theoretical basis for using YQP in AD therapy.Keywords: atopic dermatitis, Yinqiao powder, single-cell RNA sequencing, regulatory network, molecular docking

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD), often triggered by a dysregulated immune response to specific allergens, manifests as a chronic and recurrent skin condition characterized by inflammation, pruritus, and erythema.1 The persistence of clinical symptoms and potential dermatological complications significantly contribute to the psychological burden, adversely impacting the social lives of patients.2,3 The intricate pathogenesis of AD involves a dynamic interplay among genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, disturbed skin barrier, microbial abnormalities, and neuroimmune pathways.4,5 The general treatment for AD typically involves the use of topical corticosteroids and emollients to alleviate inflammation, supplemented with oral antihistamines for itching relief.6 Despite advancements in targeted cytokine therapies, receptor blocking, and JAK inhibitors, challenges remain in achieving optimal therapeutic outcomes for AD.7–9 First, AD is characterized by a high recurrence rate, which is higher than 20% even in patients receiving standard treatment after drug withdrawal.10 Second, some patients do not respond well to available therapies, especially those with moderate to severe AD, with a failure rate or inadequate response to biologic agents of approximately 40%.11 In addition, the safety and potential side effects associated with emerging therapies cannot be ignored, including skin infection, asthma exacerbation, and the risk of skin cancer that may be increased by long-term UV phototherapy.1,12 Therefore, it is of great clinical significance to explore alternative or complementary treatment strategies with definite efficacy, good safety and long-term use.

YQP, a TCM formulation, is comprised of diverse herbs, including Bohe, Dandouchi, Gancao, Light bamboo leaves, Jiegeng, Jingjiesui, Jinyinhua, Lianqiao, Lugen, and Niubangzi. These active ingredients, which are renowned for their anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, and detoxifying properties, collectively contribute to YQP’s multifaceted functions. Extensively employed in TCM, YQP has been demonstrated efficacy in treating various respiratory and infectious diseases.13–15 Studies confirmed that YQP contributed to promoting apoptosis in neutrophils and suppressing the TLR4/NF-κB pathway.16 Additionally, Lianhua Qingwen Capsule, incorporating YQP as a key ingredient, has been shown to reduce the excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor -alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL-6), and IL-8 induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS).17 Regarding inflammatory dermatoses, it has been demonstrated that the bioactive constituents of Yinqiao Powder (YQP), including chlorogenic acid, phillyrin, and quercetin, exert inhibitory effects on the expression of pivotal atopic dermatitis (AD)-associated cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13.18–21 These findings provide a preliminary scientific rationale for the clinical application of YQP in AD management, with its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties offering promise for ameliorating cutaneous manifestations. Furthermore, consistent with traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) theory, YQP is posited to possess heat-clearing, detoxifying, and pathogen-resolving functions,22 which may contribute to alleviating AD-related pruritus, erythema, and edema. Nevertheless, current investigations into YQP for AD remain limited, particularly regarding comprehensive mechanistic insights. Existing studies predominantly focus on isolated components or singular signaling pathways, thereby failing to elucidate the holistic regulatory network underlying YQP’s “multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway” synergistic action as a complex herbal formula. Consequently, a thorough understanding of AD pathogenesis and a systematic investigation into the therapeutic potential of YQP—including its molecular mechanisms—are imperative to refine treatment strategies and fully harness its capabilities in managing dermatological disorders, including AD.

Network pharmacology, emerging as a potent tool for comprehending drug actions within complex biological systems,23,24 aligns with the holistic philosophy of TCM, emphasizing integrated regulation across multiple components, targets, and pathways in the body. Hence, the application of network pharmacology serves as a precious avenue for elucidating how the diverse components of YQP influence skin disorders. For example, in the study of licorice root, by constructing a “component-target-pathway” network, it was predicted that its active components might exert anti-AD effects by regulating inflammatory-related signaling pathways such as PI3K-Akt, MAPK and TNF.25 Meanwhile, Single-cell sequencing is an advanced technology that provides in-depth insights into gene expression and cellular heterogeneity. By identifying distinct cell types, states, and functional dynamics within tissues, it enhances our understanding of the complex cellular landscape of diseases. Notably, recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies have revealed important immune cell subsets and their roles in AD pathogenesis,26,27 deepening our understanding of the molecular mechanisms involved and uncovering potential therapeutic targets. Furthermore, several relevant studies have employed integrative analyses to cross-compare the potential targets of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) compounds with differentially expressed genes identified in cells via single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), thereby identifying candidate drugs and elucidating potential molecular mechanisms for the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease (AD).28 This suggests that the integration of network pharmacology with single-cell sequencing holds promise for mapping the core therapeutic targets predicted by network pharmacology to the key pathogenic cell types revealed by scRNA-seq data, and for systematically elucidating the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of Yinqiao San in AD.

Based on the current background, this study proposes the following hypothesis: Yinqiao Powder (YQP) may exert its therapeutic effects on Alzheimer’s disease (AD) by acting on multiple key targets through its diverse bioactive components, thereby modulating immune-inflammatory pathways and cellular functions associated with AD. Accordingly, we will employ an integrated approach combining network pharmacology, bulk RNA sequencing, and single-cell RNA sequencing technologies to systematically identify the potential active ingredients and core target genes of YQP in the treatment of AD. We will further investigate its regulatory roles in immune cell infiltration and functional alterations across distinct cell types. Molecular docking simulations and experimental validation will be conducted to confirm the binding affinities and expression changes of these targets. The aim of this study is to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms of YQP in AD therapy by identifying critical target genes, biological pathways, and potential molecular targets based on its active constituents. This will provide a theoretical foundation for the clinical application of YQP in AD treatment and establish a basis for future in-depth mechanistic investigations.

Materials and Methods

Sequencing and Data Pre-Processing

The overall research workflow is illustrated in Supplementary Figure 1. First, blood samples were collected from 15 AD patients and 15 healthy individuals for transcriptome sequencing. In short, total RNA of 30 samples was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, CA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. After detecting their concentrations, purity and integrity, the mRNA was specifically captured with oligo(dT)25 beads (Thermo Fisher, USA) by two cycles of purification. The mRNA fragments were yielded at 94°C for 5 ~ 7 minutes by NEBNext® Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (New England Biolabs, USA), followed by synthesis of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) with the flat end with the help of Invitrogen SuperScriptTM II Reverse Transcriptase (CA, USA), E. coli DNA polymerase I and RNase H (NEB, USA), and dUTP Solution (Thermo Fisher, CA, USA). In the wake of trailing “A” base, the magnetic beads were used to filter and purify dsDNA fragments. At last, the Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) was performed to yield the sequencing library (pre-denaturation at 95°C for three min; denaturation at 98°C for 15s, annealing at 60°C for 15s, extension at 72°C for 30s, eight cycles; final extension at 72°C for five min). The illumina Novaseq™ 6000 (LC Bio Technology CO., Ltd. Hangzhou, China) was employed for paired-end sequencing (PE150).

Identification and Functional Enrichment Analysis of Potential Target Genes

Based on aforementioned transcriptome sequencing data, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between AD and controls were recognized by “DESeq2” package (version 3.44.3) (|log2fold-change (FC)| > 1 and adj.P < 0.05).29 This threshold is widely accepted for defining significant changes, as gene expression alterations below this threshold are more likely to be affected by technical noise. The volcano plot and heatmap of DEGs were exhibited by “ggplot2” (version 3.3.0) and “pheatmap” (version 0.7.7) packages, respectively. Afterwards, the active ingredients of YQP (Oral Bioavailability > 30, Drug-like properties > 0.18) and their target genes were collected from TCM Systems Pharmacology database. Importantly, the DEGs and target genes were intersected to identify potential target genes, followed by enrichment analysis based on Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases (adj.P < 0.05). In addition, a network involving herb-active ingredient-target gene was structured a network involving herb-active ingredient-target gene was constructed, which helped to deeply understand the underlying molecular mechanisms by which the YQP exerted its pharmacological effects.

Identification of Candidate Target Genes Based on Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) Network

Firstly, a PPI network of potential target genes was constructed through the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database to explore their relationships (Confidence > 0.4). Subsequently, five algorithms (Degree, Edge Percolated Component, Maximal Clique Centrality, Radiality and Betweenness) in the cytohubba plug-in of Cytoscape software (version 3.8.2) were employed to screen candidate target genes by overlapping the top 10 potential genes of each algorithm.30 The correlation among candidate target genes was evaluated with the the help of Spearman correlation analysis.

Construction of Nomogram Model and Subcellular Localization

For further screening the key target genes of YQP for AD treatment, the ability of candidate target genes to distinguish AD from control samples was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with the pROC package (v 1.18.4).31 The genes were regarded to be key target genes when the area under curves (AUCs) of them were greater than 0.7. Furthermore, a nomogram model was established to predict the attacked probability by AD using “RMS” package (version 5.1–4),32 and then its estimated performance was appraised through calibration curve and ROC curve. Additionally, the location of key target genes in the cell was predicted by means of mRNALocater database (http://bio-bigdata.cn/mRNALocater). Incidentally, the expression levels of key target genes in AD and control groups were estimated and compared by Wilcoxon’s test (P < 0.05).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and Immune Infiltration Analysis

In order to investigate the regulatory pathways and molecular functions referred by key target genes, the correlation coefficients between key target genes and other genes were firstly computed and ranked, and sorted genes were brought into the single gene GSEA carried out by “clusterProfiler” package (version 3.8.1)33 referencing to GO and KEGG gene sets (adj.P < 0.05). Moreover, the immune infiltration analysis was further proceeded to understand the role of key target genes in immune cells. The abundance of 22 immune cells in each AD and control sample was estimated using “CIBERSORT” approach.34 The correlations among discrepant immune cells were examined by Spearman correlation analysis, as well as between discrepant immune cells and key target genes.

Construction of a Regulatory Network and Molecular Docking

For exploring the regulatory mechanism of key target genes in the onset of AD, a regulatory network including transcription factors (TFs) and kinases associated with key target genes was built based on computational prediction via eXpression2Kinases database (https://www.maayanlab.net/X2K/). Furthermore, the molecular docking was adopted to research the interactions between key target genes in AD and active ingredients of YQP, and to project their binding affinity through AutoDockvina software (version 1.1.2).35 By the way, this analysis was proceeded based on the crystal structure corresponding to key target genes and three-dimensional structure of active ingredients, which were obtained from Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) and Pubchem databases (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), respectively.

To assess the binding strength between the active components of YQP and target proteins, the clinically commonly used AD therapeutic agents Abrocitinib36 and Upadacitinib37 were introduced as positive controls in this study. The three-dimensional structures of both drugs were obtained from the PubChem database, and molecular docking with the key target proteins was performed separately. By comparing the binding affinities of the active components of YQP with those of the positive control drugs, their potential as active ingredients for AD treatment was evaluated.

Analysis of Single-Cell Sequencing Data

The expression matrix and metadata information file of the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset, namely GSE180885 (GPL20301), were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; accessible at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database. This dataset encompassed four samples of lesional skin tissue from AD patients and three control skin tissue samples.

The Seurat package (v 5.1.0)38 was harnessed to dissect the scRNA-seq data. The analytical process primarily consisted of several key steps: creating the object, normalizing the data to a unified scale, reducing the data dimensionality followed by clustering, and pinpointing marker genes. Initially, cells possessing fewer than 200 genes and genes covered by less than three cells were excluded from further analysis (while retaining cells with nFeature RNA ranging from 300 to 5000, nCount RNA below 30,000, and mitochondrial proportion under 10%). The NormalizeData and FindVariableFeatures functions were deployed to standardize the data and single out the top 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs). By leveraging the ScaleData and JackStrawPlot functions, the principal component analysis (PCA) outcomes were scrutinized to identify the top principal components (PCs) with statistical significance (p < 0.05). Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was then utilized in combination with the top PCs to curtail the data dimensionality, and clusters across these cells were demarcated via the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions (resolution = 0.5). Finally, the references39 were consulted to recognize prominent marker genes within the annotated cells (Supplementary Table 1).

Enrichment Analysis and Pseudo-Time Analysis of Key Cell Types

Identification of key cell types was performed using a two-stage strategy. In the first stage, candidate key cell populations were identified based on cell abundance. By comparing the distribution of each cell type between the AD group and the control group, cell types that exhibited the highest cell count and proportion in the AD group, along with the most significant intergroup distribution difference (assessed by chi-square test), were defined as candidate key cell populations. In the second stage, target gene expression was validated. After determining the candidate key cell populations, the expression differences of the previously screened key target genes in this specific cell population between the AD group and the control group were independently evaluated. If the expression levels of these key genes in the GSE180885 dataset showed statistically significant differences between the AD and control groups, the corresponding cell type was identified as the key cell type. Additionally, pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the annotated cells using the ReactomeGSA package (v 1.12.0).40

With the intention of discerning the fluctuations in the expression of key target genes during the cell state transition of every cell, a dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis (resolution = 0.5) was successively implemented on the key cells, and the cells were reclassified into diverse subtypes. Subsequently, the Monocle package (v 2.30.0)41 was enlisted for pseudo-temporal analysis to probe into their differentiation trajectories and the alterations in the expression levels of key target genes throughout the differentiation process. Finally, cell communication analysis was performed to investigate the intercellular interactions among annotated cell types in the AD group and the control group using the CellChat package (v1.6.1)42 and explore potential ligand-receptor interactions.

Experimental Validation by RT-qPCR

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Zunyi City, approval number NO.2023-1-169. The patients signed written informed consent forms in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration to participate in the study. Total RNA was extracted from blood samples of 5 AD patients and 5 controls using Trizol reagent (Ambion, Texas, USA). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized from the RNA using the SweScript First Strand cDNA synthesis kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). PCR amplification was performed using a 10 μL reaction system containing 3 μL cDNA, 5 μL 2x Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix, and 1 μL each of forward and reverse primers. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 minute, followed by 20 seconds at 95°C for denaturation, 30 seconds at 55°C for annealing, and 30 seconds at 72°C for extension, repeated for 40 cycles. Relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2, and GAPDH was employed as the internal reference gene to ensure proper normalization.

Statistical Analysis

R software (version 4.1.1) was used for all bioinformatics analyses, and GraphPad Prism 5.0 was employed for partial data visualization. Corresponding statistical methods were adopted based on different analytical objectives: the DESeq2 package was used for differential expression analysis of transcriptome sequencing data to identify differentially expressed genes between AD patients and healthy controls, with significance thresholds set at adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1; the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparisons between two independent samples, specifically including the analysis of differences in the infiltration abundance of 22 immune cell types between the AD group and the control group, as well as the validation of key gene expression levels between the two groups; Spearman correlation test was used to evaluate correlations between variables, including correlations among candidate key genes, among differentially infiltrated immune cells, and between key genes and the abundance of differentially infiltrated immune cells; ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of candidate key genes, with the AUC serving as the evaluation metric; the Benjamini-Hochberg correction method was used for multiple hypothesis testing correction in GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to control the false discovery rate at < 0.05; the chi-square test was used to compare the distribution of cell types between the AD group and the control group in single-cell RNA sequencing data; and the two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for intergroup comparison of relative expression levels of key genes in real-time quantitative PCR experiments. Unless otherwise specified, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 3705 DEGs between AD and controls were acquired, including 2886 up-regulation and 819 down-regulation (Figure 1a and b). Thereinto, a sum of 52 DEGs were screened out after overlapping 3705 DEGs with 259 target genes of YQP active ingredients named as potential target genes (Figure 1c). The active ingredients and target numbers of Yinqiao powder were detailed in Table 1. An additional form file showed this in more detail (Supplementary Table 3). With respect to GO enrichment analysis of these potential target genes, we discovered that they mainly participated in response to oxygen levels, response to hypoxia, response to decreased oxygen levels, etc, in biological process items; transcription regulator complex, membrane raft, membrane microdomain, etc, in cellular component items; and DNA-binding transcription factor binding, RNA polymerase II-specific DNA-binding, etc, in molecular function items (Figure 1d and Supplementary Table 4). In KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, these 52 potential target genes were principally enriched in endocrine resistance, AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis and so forth (Figure 1e and Supplementary Table 5). Although these pathways were not traditionally considered classical pathways in AD, their roles in inflammation, oxidative stress, and cardiovascular disease are gaining increased attention—processes that constitute relevant links in the pathogenesis of AD.43–45 Besides, the herb-active ingredient-target gene network was made up of nine herbs, 114 active ingredients of YQP and 52 potential target genes (Figure 1f), such as Bohe-luteolin-EGFR, Gancao-quercetin-HIF1A, Jiegeng-acacetin-TP53.

HIF1A, TP53, ERBB2 and CTNNB1 Were Identified as Key Target Genes

In regard to interactions among 52 potential target genes, a total of 365 relation pairs were ascertained, including EGFR-RUNX1T1, TP53-NFE2L2, ERBB2-ESR2 and so on (Figure 2a). After filtering via five algorithms in cytohubba plug-in, five intersection genes (HIF1A, TP53, CTNNB1, ERBB2 and EGFR) were screened out, named as candidate target genes (Supplementary Figure 2). Moreover, the most strong correlation was detected among HIF1A, TP53 and ERBB2 (|Cor| > 0.55), and CTNNB1 was only connected with HIF1A with slight correlation (Cor = 0.55) (Figure 2b). Further ROC curve indicated that four candidate target genes were equipped with excellently diagnostic efficiency except EGFR (AUC = 0.58) due to their AUC values of greater than 0.7 (Figure 2c). Therefore, the nomogram model embraced four key target genes, namely HIF1A, TP53, CTNNB1 and ERBB2 (Figure 2d), and this model could effectively predict the risk of developing AD (R2 = 0.878, AUC = 0.97) (Figure 2e and f). Compared with control samples, the expression levels of HIF1A and CTNNB1 were significantly higher in AD samples (P < 0.05), while the expression levels of TP53 and ERBB2 were markedly lower in AD samples (P < 0.001) (Figure 2g). By the way, up-regulated key target genes mainly located in cell nucleus, and another two down-regulated key target genes basically existed in cytoplasm (Figure 2h).

Differential Resting CD4(+) Memory T Cells Were the Strongest Cells Associated with Key Target Genes

The proportion of 22 immune cells in AD and control samples was shown in Figure 4a, and the significant differences in the abundance of five immune cells were detected (Figure 4b). The abundance of resting CD4(+) memory T cells, activated dendritic cells (DCs) and neutrophils in AD samples was higher, on the contrary, the proportion of CD8(+) T cells and resting natural killer (NK) cells was lower in AD samples than those in control samples. With respect to correlation analysis between differential immune cells, the most strongly passive correlation was discovered between resting NK cells and neutrophils (Cor = −0.89), as well as the highest positive correlation between resting CD4(+) memory T cells and activated DCs (Cor = 0.68) (Figure 4c). Simultaneously, the resting CD4(+) memory T cells were the most strongest cells related to HIF1A (Cor = 0.75) and TP53 (Cor = −0.76) (Figure 4d).

Regulation Network of Key Target Genes Included Multiple TFs and Kinases

The TF-mRNA-kinase regulatory network incorporated 60 TFs and 98 kinases corresponding to four key target genes, forming 230 relation pairs (Figure 4e). For instance, the expression of HIF1A and CTNNB1 might be regulated by EGR, SMARCA4 and KDM5B TFs and ATM, CSNK1D, CSNK2A1 kinases at the same time. Both TFs (SOX2, GABP, NANOG, etc) and kinases (PTK2, PRKCA, TP53RK, etc) could target mRNA TP53 and ERBB2 to regulate their expression.

The binding energy of CTNNB1 with beta-carotene was −6.4 kcal/mol (Figure 4g), TP53 with aloe-emodin was −6.1 kcal/mol (Figure 4h), and ERBB2 and HIF1A with quercetin were −6.3 and −7.4 kcal/mol, respectively (Figure 4i and j). These findings indicate that beta-carotene, aloe-emodin, and quercetin exhibit strong binding potential with AD key target proteins, suggesting they may be the active components responsible for the therapeutic effects of YQP.

Cell Clusters Identification in scRNA-Seq Dataset GSE180885

First, after integrating and filtering the original data, following quality control (QC), the data consisted of 17,771 cells and 20,534 genes (Supplementary Figure 5). The top 2000 HVGs were selected for subsequent analysis. Figure 5a presents and labels the top 10 HVGs, as well as the overall cell distribution in GSE180885. The PCA replacement test and scree plot were employed to determine that the most optimal and relatively stable dimensionality was 30. Consequently, the top 30 PCs were retained for further analyses (Figure 5b and c). After eliminating the batch effect, dimensionality reduction and clustering were performed again. As a result, 16 distinct cell clusters were co-clustered (Figure 5d). Subsequently, a bubble plot was utilized to display the expression of marker genes, and nine cell clusters were annotated. Among the identified cell populations were cutaneous innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), NK cells, T cells, keratinocytes, B cells, phagocytes, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and melanocytes (Figure 5e and f).

Potential Mechanisms of Key Target Genes in Keratinocytes

Within the scRNA-seq dataset GSE180885, keratinocytes boasted the highest cell count and accounted for the greatest proportion within the AD group, as evidenced by Figure 6a and b. The chi-square test outcomes unequivocally demonstrated that the disparity in keratinocyte distribution between the control and AD groups was the most pronounced, leading to their classification as the Candidate key cell type (Supplementary Table 7). Conversely, the expression profile of the key target gene ERBB2 manifested an initial upward surge, trailed by a subsequent downward descent during the diverse developmental phases of different keratinocytes subtypes (Figure 6h).

Discussion

AD, as we know, poses a global health burden.46 Although conventional treatments such as topical corticosteroids and immunomodulators have elucidated limited efficacy in managing AD,47,48 the intricate web of predisposing and triggering factors complicates therapeutic progress.49 YQP, a traditional Chinese regimen gaining attentions for its potential in addressing inflammatory conditions, presenting an intriguing avenue for exploration.50. In our current study, we screened key target genes associated with the active ingredients of YQP and investigated their latent biological functions utilizing transcriptomics in conjunction with network pharmacology for the sake of unveiling the therapeutic roles and explore the potential mechanisms of YQP in AD.

Yinqiao Powder originates from “Dongheng Liangge Powder”. In terms of its formulation and functions, it inherits the method of using pungent and cool herbs to relieve the exterior syndrome, while also drawing from Yu Jiayan’s theory of “using aromatic herbs to eliminate foulness”. As a result, Yinqiao Powder is a formula that lightly disperses the qi level. It is known as the “mild pungent-cool formula” and has the effects of promoting the flow of qi and expelling heat outward. The formula employs both warm and cool herbs, primarily utilizing acrid-cool substances. Wu Jutong described it as “acrid” without using “acrid-warm” herbs, and “cool” without employing “bitter-cold” herbs. In the formula, Forsythia (Lianqiao) and Honeysuckle (Jinyinhua), which are cool and slightly cold in nature, serve as the sovereign herbs to dispel wind-heat and provide a pathway for expelling pathogenic factors. Acrid-cool herbs such as Peppermint (Bohe) release the exterior and clear the head and eyes, while acrid-cool Arctium (Niubangzi) clears heat and alleviates swelling and pain, both acting as minister herbs. The acrid-warm herbs, such as Schizonepeta Spike (Jingjiesui) and Fermented Soybean (Dandouchi), enhance the formula’s ability to release the exterior and expel pathogens. Secondly, the Yinqiao San formula employs the method of “combining sweet and bitter flavors to generate fluid”. Sweet herbs like Honeysuckle (Jinyinhua), Raw Licorice (Sheng Gancao), Fermented Soybean (Dandouchi), and Bamboo Leaf (Zhuye) are paired with bitter herbs such as Forsythia (Lianqiao), Arctium (Niubangzi), and Platycodon (Jiegeng) to clear heat and promote fluid production. This combination clears heat while preventing damage to body fluids, ensuring that nourishing yin does not retain pathogens and expelling pathogens does not harm healthy qi. Together, they achieve the effect of “sweet and bitter combination generating fluid”. In summary, the combination of herbs in Yinqiao San works to acridly disperse, release the exterior, dispel wind, clear heat, and expel pathogens, while simultaneously preserving and promoting the production of body fluids. In our investigation, we unveiled 52 potential target genes of YQP related to AD, with a particular focus on four key target genes (TP53, ERBB2, CTNNB1 and HIF1A) distinguished by high scores in both topology and diagnostic value. TP53, a tumor suppressor gene crucial for DNA repair and cell cycle regulation, has been implicated in various cancers widely, including skin cancer.51–53 Despite its reported involvement in protecting against oxidative stress and its potential impact on acne vulgaris which is a chronic inflammatory skin disease, its specific role in AD remains elusive.54 Given the reported function of TP53 in adaptation to challenging conditions such as cold, hypoxia, and viral infections,55 we inferred that the downregulation of TP53 raised questions about the compromised ability of dermatitis patients to respond to stress and maintain genomic stability. Notably, our study uniquely identified the downregulation of ERBB2 in AD patients. ERBB2, commonly referred to as human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), is a member of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) family. It holds a pivotal position in regulating cell proliferation and differentiation. In contrast to the extensive research on its oncogenic activation in carcinogenesis, little is known about its normal physiological functions in the skin.56–59 Studies have unveiled the essential role of ERBB2 in normal cardiac physiology, where the loss of cardiac ERBB2 leads to cardiomyopathic expansion in adult mice.60 Interestingly, Justin G et al proposed a role for normal ERBB2 expression in skin wound healing, supported by its protective overexpression in radiation-induced skin ulcers. However, this upregulation also posed an elevated risk of skin cancer, indicating complex and sometimes conflicting roles for ERBB2 signaling in the skin.61 As for CTNNB1, which encodes β-catenin, it serves as a crucial regulator of cell adhesion, Wnt signaling, gene transcription and cell cycle, playing diverse roles in maintaining cellular and tissue integrity. While extensively explored in the context of cancer,62,63 our findings now introduced a potential role for upregulated CTNNB1 in the intricate molecular landscape of AD, offering a novel perspective on its involvement in dermatological conditions. Besides, our investigation revealed the upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1A), a transcription factor known for inducing cytokine expression under pathological conditions. Consistent with our findings, this increased expression correlated with the direct involvement of HIF1A in UVB-mediated TSLP induction, which plays a pivotal role in the initiation and perpetuation of allergic inflammatory diseases.64 Collectively, these findings encouraged a deeper exploration of the intricate connections involved in improving AD through multiple targets.

Additionally, these four genes may exhibit mutual regulatory relationships, collectively forming a functional network. The core of this network may be associated with the interaction between TP53 and HIF1A. Studies have shown that p53 promotes the degradation of HIF1A,65 while HIF1A can conversely inhibit p53 activity.66 Both are crucial for cellular adaptation to the inflammatory microenvironment.67,68 Therefore, in AD, the downregulation of TP53 may alleviate its inhibitory control over HIF1A, leading to the accumulation and activation of HIF1A. The activation of HIF1A may, in turn, affect the other two genes to some extent. On one hand, HIF1A indirectly participates in the β-catenin signaling pathway encoded by CTNNB1,69 co-regulating downstream target genes. This implies that in the AD environment, HIF1A may engage with β-catenin, which is originally involved in maintaining tissue integrity, redirecting its function toward inflammatory regulation. This could explain why barrier function remains impaired despite the upregulation of CTNNB1 expression. On the other hand, HIF1A is regulated by the upstream ERBB2/PI3K/Akt pathway.70 This forms a potential closed loop: downregulation of TP53 initiates network imbalance and relieves the inhibition on HIF1A; accumulated HIF1A interacts with β-catenin, shifting its function toward inflammatory regulation; the HIF1A-driven inflammatory microenvironment further suppresses TP53; while the downregulation of ERBB2 further exacerbates the imbalance in HIF1A expression, amplifying the inflammatory response. Through these three connecting lines—the interplay between TP53 and HIF1A, the functional impact of HIF1A on CTNNB1, and the regulatory effect of ERBB2 on HIF1A expression—the four genes collectively drive the inflammatory response in AD. Of course, this integrative model still requires validation through AD-specific experiments.

To unravel the intricate biological mechanisms of YQP in the treatment of AD, GSEA and regulatory network establishment were conducted, focusing on shared targets to comprehensively elucidate the therapeutic effects of YQP. Our investigation unearthed a significant association of HIF1A and CTNNB1 with mRNA processing, highlighting their pivotal roles in governing gene expression, particularly in skin cell communication and signaling. Delving deeper, our study revealed noteworthy pathway enrichments during YQP treatment, encompassing Herpes simplex virus 1 infection and nucleocytoplasmic transport. These pathways emerged as critical regulators of immune cell activity in skin and any dysregulation might precipitate the production of inflammatory mediators such as IL-13, IL-33, and IL-31.71 This underscored the importance of maintaining precise immune responses, particularly in the face of environmental challenges. Furthermore, our regulatory network analysis unveiled the coordinated influence of TFs (EGR, SMARCA4, KDM5B) and kinases (ATM, CSNK1D, CSNK2A1) on the expression of HIF1A and CTNNB1. As key mediators, these regulatory elements intricately maintain the delicate balance of skin cells and steer their responses to external stimuli. In exploring the regulation of TP53 and ERBB2, we uncovered their control by TFs like SOX2, GABP, NANOG, along with kinases such as PTK2, PRKCA, TP53RK. These regulatory networks, as indicated by GO functional analysis, contributed to the regulation of protein-containing complexes, mitochondrial inner membrane, and mitochondrial matrix, all pivotal for skin homeostasis and immune responses. The energy production heavily relies on the crucial roles played by the mitochondrial inner membrane, while the mitochondrial matrix serves as the vital site for energy metabolism. Delving into the realm of mitochondria and metabolism, we emphasized the critical importance of mitochondrial function and cellular metabolic states in our study. Studies indicating a reduced abundance of mitochondrial proteins in the affected epidermis suggested mitochondria as potential therapeutic targets in AD.72,73 The dysregulation of these functions might escalate oxidative stress and intensify cutaneous inflammatory responses.74,75 Additionally, KEGG analysis underscored significant enrichments in pathways like cofactor biosynthesis, carbon metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and thermogenesis. Cofactor biosynthesis directly impacts skin cell metabolism, while pathways related to carbon metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and thermogenesis collectively regulate the energy balance and metabolic state of skin cells. Disruptions in these pathways may exert direct or indirect effects on the development and progression of AD. In essence, the identified target genes intricately participate in the mechanisms underlying AD, affecting crucial pathways related to immune responses, energy metabolism, and the production of inflammatory mediators. This holistic understanding sheds light on the multifaceted impacts of YQP in the intricate network governing skin health and disease progression.

In the complex immunoinfiltrating landscape of AD, the intricate dynamics of immune system involvement, particularly concerning CD4(+) T cells.73,76 Various CD4(+) T cells, encompassing subsets like T helper1 (Th)1, Th2, Th17, Th22, and Treg, orchestrate a multifaceted immune response.76,77 While Th2 responses are universally activated in AD, evidence also points to the involvement of Th1 cytokine pathways.78 Additionally, Th17 cells secrete IL-17, IL-6, and TNF-α, actively participating in the regulation of inflammation. Gonzalez et al found that FLG deficiency, associated with an increased risk of AD, might result in deviations in the CD4(+) T cell response to commensal bacteria rather than regulatory T cells, ultimately causing long-term effects on skin immune homeostasis.79 In line with these findings, our study identified an elevated abundance of CD4(+) T cells in response to inflammation of AD, unveiling a positive correlation with HIF1A and a negative correlation with TP53. Remarkably, research has demonstrated that changes in HIF1A expression can activate CD4(+) T cells and impact the differentiation of naive T cells, promoting Th17 while suppressing Treg development through targeted proteasomal degradation.80,81 Additionally, a research involving post-traumatic stress disorder, often accompanied by inflammatory conditions, the downregulation of the TP53/let-7a axis could lead to an increased Th17 inflammatory phenotype.82 Therefore, we have proposed a hypothesis that in response to hypoxia and inflammation in AD, the differentiation and function of CD4(+) T cells could be intricately modulated through the regulatory mechanisms involving HIF1A and TP53. Research on the mechanisms of AD has predominantly centered around CD4(+) cells, with fewer studies focusing on other immune cells. Our findings indicated increased activation of DCs and neutrophils, coupled with decreased CD8(+) T cells and resting NK cells in AD. Our results aligned with studies emphasizing the integral role of neutrophils in triggering the itch-associated chemokine CXCL10, while DCs likely contributed significantly to the groundwork for itch and inflammation preceding neutrophil infiltration.83 In a recent single-cell immune analysis of AD patients, our findings correlated with the research, indicating that inflammation was linked to a decrease in activated CD8(+) T cells and an increase in exhausted CD8(+) T cells, implying a compromised ability to directly eliminate infected or abnormal cells, potentially influencing disease pathogenesis. However, the marginal 20.4% increase in NK cells contradicted the decrease we observed, highlighting a disparity between this study and our findings.84 Considering the potential stages of disease development, the diminished NK cells in our research might stem from abnormal immune activation and inflammation, leading to immune suppression and cytokine imbalances. Further investigation is necessary to delve into the intricate immune system interactions shaping the course of AD.

Keratinocytes are the key components of the skin barrier, responsible for maintaining skin structural integrity and participating in immune responses.85,86 Our single-cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed that the distribution of keratinocytes in the AD group differs from that in control group, indicating that keratinocytes play a crucial role in the immune response and pathological processes of AD. A recent study emphasized the importance of the skin microbiome in wound healing and hair follicle regeneration by modulating keratinocyte HIF1A signaling pathway.87 Based on this, we hypothesize that expression changes of HIF1A in keratinocytes may reflect their adaptation to the compromised skin barrier in AD and their response to environmental stresses, such as hypoxia and inflammation. Given that CTNNB1 plays a role in cell proliferation, differentiation and survival,88 its upregulation may lead to disrupted adhesion function in keratinocytes, thus compromising the integrity of the skin barrier. The expression changes of TP53 and ERBB2 in keratinocytes may be linked to their response to DNA damage and inflammatory stress within the skin environment.89 In addition, pseudotime analysis further uncovered the developmental trajectory of keratinocytes in AD, revealing distinct stages of differentiation. Notably, the expression of HIF1A, CTNNB1, and TP53 gradually decreased during keratinocyte differentiation, which may reflect a transition away from stress-responsive states as keratinocytes adapt to their roles in barrier formation and maintenance within the compromised AD environment. The decline in HIF1A expression could be associated with keratinocytes transitioning out of a stress-responsive state, while the decrease in CTNNB1 may indicate the restoration and stabilization of cell adhesion functions. The reduction in TP53 expression might reflect a transition of keratinocytes from an active state to a quiescent state. In contrast, ERBB2 expression initially increased and then decreased, suggesting that it plays a pivotal role in keratinocyte proliferation and maturation. The initial increase in ERBB2 could relate to keratinocyte activation and proliferation, while the subsequent decrease may correspond to the maturation stage during differentiation and barrier repair. Our study also identified significant alterations in biological pathways associated with ion channel dysfunction, immune cell abnormalities, lipid mediator synthesis, and steroid metabolism. These changes highlight the complexity of AD pathology. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms and further investigation are crucial for the development of more targeted treatment strategies in the future.

Based on molecular docking analysis, we preliminarily predicted the binding potential of the active components of Yinqiao Powder (YQP) with four key target genes. The results indicated that β-carotene, aloe-emodin, and quercetin may be the potential active components in YQP that target CTNNB1, TP53, ERBB2, and HIF1A to alleviate AD. Among these, the combined application of β-carotene and quercetin is particularly noteworthy, as they may jointly intervene in multiple pathological aspects of atopic dermatitis through complementary molecular mechanisms: β-carotene may primarily act on skin barrier repair, while quercetin may focus on immunoinflammatory regulation. Existing research provides a preliminary theoretical basis for this perspective. In terms of barrier function, β-carotene is an integral part of vitamin A biosynthesis, possesses antioxidant properties, and offers potential benefits for skin health.90 Although one study did not observe a significant decline in β-carotene concentration in AD patients,91 animal studies have reported its ability to inhibit inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-4, reinforce the skin barrier, and confer potential benefits to patients.92–94 In terms of immunomodulation, quercetin has been shown to combat inflammation by inhibiting TNF-α in macrophages and mast cells, inhibiting histamine production, regulating the Th1/Th2 balance, and reducing the release of antigen-specific IgE antibodies.95,96 Furthermore, its anti-inflammatory effects are closely related to its activity in regulating multiple inflammatory signaling pathways.97 The molecular docking results of this study provide further theoretical clues for the above perspective. We identified potential binding sites between β-carotene and CTNNB1, as well as between quercetin and ERBB2 and HIF1A. Based on existing literature, CTNNB1 is a core component of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, involved in keratinocyte differentiation and skin barrier formation;98,99 while ERBB2 and HIF1A play important roles in inflammatory responses and immune regulation.100–103 Based on this, we hypothesize that β-carotene may influence the Wnt signaling pathway by acting on CTNNB1, thereby promoting keratinocyte differentiation and filaggrin expression, and enhancing skin barrier integrity. Quercetin may intervene in inflammatory signaling axes by binding to ERBB2 and HIF1A, inhibiting the expression of Th2-type cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13, and potentially exerting antipruritic effects by affecting the IL-31 pathway.104 The synergy between the two may both promote downstream skin barrier repair and inhibit upstream inflammation and itch signals, forming a potential multi-target intervention model. Additionally, this study also identified a potential binding between aloe-emodin and TP53, suggesting its antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects may exert adjuvant therapeutic benefits through pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK.105–107 It is important to emphasize that the above mechanistic exploration is primarily based on molecular docking predictions and existing preclinical research evidence, and remains a preliminary inference. The direct interaction between β-carotene and CTNNB1, quercetin and ERBB2/HIF1A, and their downstream biological effects, require further validation through experiments such as surface plasmon resonance and cellular thermal shift assays, and their specific molecular regulatory mechanisms need to be further elucidated using in vitro cell models and in vivo animal experiments. This study merely provides preliminary theoretical clues for these potential mechanisms, laying the foundation for subsequent in-depth research.

There are two methodological aspects of this study that warrant discussion. First, we employed two datasets derived from different sources: bulk RNA sequencing data from the peripheral blood of AD patients were used to screen for differentially expressed genes, while single-cell sequencing dataset GSE146026 from skin tissue was utilized to validate the expression localization of key genes at the cellular level. Peripheral blood transcriptomic analysis is justified in AD research, because although AD is characterized by cutaneous manifestations, it is fundamentally a systemic disorder accompanied by systemic immune dysregulation.108 Therefore, screening for systemic molecular markers using peripheral blood transcriptomes enables the capture of disease-related immune-inflammatory signals at a holistic level. Subsequently, introducing single-cell data from skin tissue allows for the localization of candidate genes to key cell types within lesions, forming a complementary analytical framework that transitions from systemic screening to localized validation. However, it is undeniable that there are inherent differences in cellular composition and microenvironment between peripheral blood and skin tissue; genes identified from blood do not necessarily perform equivalent functions in the skin, and the functional correlation between these compartments requires further confirmation through in vitro cellular assays and animal models. Second, when selecting key genes for the nomogram model, EGFR was excluded due to its AUC value of 0.58. It is important to emphasize that this exclusion was based solely on its efficacy as a diagnostic biomarker and does not negate the potential value of EGFR in the pathogenesis of AD or its role in the therapeutic effects of YQP. As a member of the epidermal growth factor receptor family, EGFR plays a crucial role in keratinocyte proliferation, differentiation, and skin barrier maintenance,109 and abnormalities in its signaling pathway are associated with various inflammatory skin diseases.110 In the “active ingredient–target” network of this study, EGFR exhibited potential interactions with multiple active components of YQP, suggesting it may be one of the targets of YQP. The diagnostic efficacy of a gene is influenced by multiple factors such as sample size, disease heterogeneity, and fold change in expression. Changes in EGFR expression in peripheral blood may not suffice as an ideal diagnostic marker, but this does not preclude its important functions within local skin lesions. Subsequent research could employ tissue-level validation and paired sample analysis to further investigate the expression localization of EGFR in AD lesions and its interactions with the active components of YQP, while also experimentally verifying the functional correlations of gene expression between blood and skin tissue.

In summary, this study employed a multi-omics integration strategy to reveal the key targets of Yinqiao Powder in the treatment of atopic dermatitis, although certain limitations exist. At the validation level, RT-qPCR was performed using only five patients and five controls, a sample size considerably smaller than the cohort of 15 cases used for transcriptome sequencing, which may lead to a discrepancy between computational predictions and experimental evidence. More importantly, the current conclusions are primarily based on bioinformatics predictions and molecular docking simulations. Although a relatively complete regulatory network was constructed, direct validation of the mechanisms of key genes and the pharmacodynamic effects of active ingredients through functional experiments is still lacking. Furthermore, the transcriptome sequencing and single-cell data were derived from peripheral blood and skin tissue, respectively, and tissue heterogeneity may limit the precise interpretation of the mechanisms underlying local skin lesions. Future efforts should focus on expanding independent validation cohorts, conducting functional validation of the four key genes using AD animal models and cellular experiments, and employing technologies such as spatial transcriptomics to analyze their expression distribution and cellular interaction networks in situ within tissues, thereby providing a more robust experimental basis for the clinical translation of Yinqiao Powder.

Conclusion

Our study underscores the promising therapeutic potential of YQP for AD treatment by synergistically modulating key target genes (CTNNB1, ERBB2, TP53 and HIF1A) and pathways. The efficacy might be enhanced by active ingredients such as β-carotene, aloe-emodin, and quercetin. Recognising the need for further validation, we propose additional functional experiments to verify key target genes and deepen our understanding of YQP’s mechanisms. Despite these limitations, our research establishes a solid foundation for clinical applications and fundamental investigations, emphasizing YQP’s significance in AD therapy.
