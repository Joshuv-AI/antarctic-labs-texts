A near telomere-to-telomere genome assembly of the tobacco root rot pathogen Fusarium oxysporum
Authors: Rui Wang, Xiaotong Gai, Wei Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFusarium oxysporum is a critically important pathogen with significant impacts on both agriculture and public health. In agriculture, it causes vascular wilt and root rot in numerous crops, resulting in substantial economic losses. This threat is compounded by the fungus’s high genetic diversity, adaptability, and persistence in the environment, which make its control particularly challenging. In this study, we report a near telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome assembly of F. oxysporum strain Fo-129, a highly virulent strain causing tobacco root rot in China. It achieved a BUSCO completeness of 96.9%. The genome contains 24 identifiable telomeres, 9.40% repeat sequences, 15,102 protein-coding genes, 370 ncRNAs, and 538 candidate effector protein-coding genes. Additionally, 52 secondary metabolite gene clusters and 714 carbohydrate-active enzymes were also identified. This near T2T genome assembly of a pathogenic F. oxysporum strain provides a crucial genomic resource for advancing research into fungal pathogenicity, host adaptation, accessory chromosome evolution, and the molecular basis of host-pathogen interactions.

Background & Summary

Fusarium is a globally distributed genus of filamentous fungi that colonize diverse habitats including water, soil, and plant surfaces1,2. As primary or secondary invaders, they infect plant tissues and cause a variety of diseases, while producing numerous mycotoxins that not only reduce crop yield and quality but also pose threats to animal and human health3,4. The genus exhibits extremely high species diversity, comprising approximately 300 phylogenetically independent species, most of which have been grouped into monophyletic species complexes (e.g., the Fusarium oxysporum species complex, FOSC)5. FOSC includes hundreds of pathogenic strains that cause vascular wilts in important crops and is regarded as one of the most economically impactful groups5. Among them, the FOSC constitutes a key group of soil-borne plant pathogens with a broad host range, infecting major economic crops such as cotton, tomato, banana, and watermelon, leading to severe agricultural losses6. This monophyletic species complex encompasses both pathogenic and non-pathogenic strains5. Pathogenic strains not only infect plants but can also cause opportunistic infections ranging from localized to systemic diseases in immunocompromised humans and other mammals7,8. In contrast, some non-pathogenic strains demonstrate potential for biological control9,10. Although the species complex exhibits a wide host range, individual strains often show high host specificity, sometimes infecting only particular cultivars. Consequently, pathogenic strains are commonly classified into more than 120 formae speciales (ff. sp.) based on their host ranges11. Moreover, F. oxysporum is a natural producer of various toxic metabolites, including fusaric acid, which further threatens plant development and human health9,12. Therefore, systematically elucidating the molecular basis and evolutionary patterns of pathogenicity in F. oxysporum is of great significance for ensuring agricultural production safety and public health.

In the field of molecular plant pathology, F. oxysporum is listed as one of the top ten fungal pathogens due to its significant scientific and economic impacts12. This fungus exhibits a dynamic genome architecture, consisting of conserved “core” chromosomes and lineage-specific “accessory” chromosomes, and the accessory chromosomes are enriched in transposable elements (typically accounting for >74% of repetitive sequences), which complicates genome assembly and hampers the acquisition of accurate, complete sequences13,14. Although a chromosome-level genome of F. oxysporum f. sp. conglutinans strain Fo5176 has been constructed based on PacBio Sequel long-read and Illumina short-read data14, genomic resources for different formae speciales of F. oxysporum that infect distinct host plants remain limited. This gap restricts systematic elucidation of their pathogenic mechanisms and genetic evolutionary dynamics. Moreover, existing genome assemblies often lack complete annotation of telomere and centromere, leaving the structure and function of these complex genomic regions poorly understood.

F. oxysporum strain Fo-129 was isolated from tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) and exhibits strong pathogenicity to tobacco, causing tobacco root rot15 (Fig. 1). The near telomere-to-telomere (T2T) reference genome of strain Fo-129 constructed in this study will provide an important data foundation for in-depth investigation of the molecular and evolutionary mechanisms underlying fungal growth, development and pathogenicity.

To gain deeper insights into the genome structure and evolutionary characteristics of F. oxysporum, this study integrated multiple cutting-edge sequencing technologies, including Illumina short-read, PacBio high-fidelity (HiFi) long-read, Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) ultra-long reads, and Hi-C chromatin conformation capture, to successfully construct a near T2T genome for this strain (the sequencing data output see Table 1). With the assistance of Hi-C data for scaffolding, approximately 98.46% (52.75 Mb) of the assembled sequences were accurately anchored onto 14 gap-free pseudochromosomes (Table 2, Fig. 2). The final genome assembly has a total length of 52.87 Mb with an N50 of 4.61 Mb, comprising 14 chromosomes and 1 unanchored scaffold (Table 2, Fig. 3), including 11 core chromosomes and 3 accessory chromosomes (Chr07, Chr08, and Chr09, respectively). The near T2T gap-free genome captured all centromeres and 24 telomeres on the 14 chromosomes (Fig. 4a). Genome annotation identified 4,967,992 bp (9.40%) of repetitive sequences (Table 3), 15,102 protein-coding genes, and 370 non-coding RNA genes, including 3 miRNAs, 311 tRNAs, 28 rRNAs, and 28 snRNAs (Table 4, Fig. 4a). 14,779 (97.86%) protein-coding genes were functionally annotated by alignment against multiple databases (Table 5, Fig. 4b). Furthermore, the study identified 52 secondary metabolite gene clusters, 714 Carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes), and 538 fungal candidate effectors. The high-quality F. oxysporum genome presented here not only represents a significant advance in the genomics research of this species, but also provides a crucial data foundation for further elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms and genetic evolutionary dynamics of Fusarium.

Methods

Strain collection and DNA extraction

F. oxysporum strain Fo-129, originally isolated from symptomatic N. tabacum plants in Yunnan, China, was used in this study. The isolate was routinely cultured on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) plates and stored long-term at −80 °C. For genomic DNA extraction, the spores and mycelia of F. oxysporum Fo-129 were harvested from a 3-day-old Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB) liquid culture by filtration, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method16.

Illumina, PacBio HiFi and ONT ultra long sequencing

To obtain a more precise and complete genomic map, this study employed a multi-platform sequencing strategy, integrating Illumina short-read, PacBio HiFi long-read, ONT ultra-long-read, and Hi-C technologies to collectively construct a high-quality genome. For Illumina short-read sequencing, the NEB Next® Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit was used to construct a 350 bp insert library, followed by sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq platform, yielding 8.50 Gb (150.94×) of raw data. For PacBio HiFi sequencing, the Template Prep Kit 3.0 was used to prepare the SMRTbell library, and sequencing was performed on the PacBio Revio platform, producing 6.10 Gb (113.21×) of high-accuracy long-read data. For ONT ultra-long-read sequencing based on the 50 K library construction protocol, the R9.4.1 flow cell and ULK001 kit were used to prepare the library, followed by sequencing on the PromethION platform, resulting in 74.28 Gb (1396.23×) of ultra-long-read data. The detailed statistical results of sequencing data from each platform are summarized in Table 1.

Hi-C sequencing

Hi-C library construction of F. oxysporum was prepared from cross-linked chromatins of fungal mycelia using a standard Hi-C protocol. After adding A-tails to the fragment ends and following ligation by the Illumina paired-end (PE) sequencing adapters, Hi-C sequencing libraries were amplified by PCR (12–14 cycles) and sequenced on Illumina PE150. The raw sequencing data of 14.5 Gb (264.15×) was subjected to quality control and filtering. Subsequently, 10,000 high-quality reads (5,000 each from read1 and read2) were randomly sampled from the filtered data. These reads were aligned against the NCBI nucleotide database (NT database) to remove exogenous contamination.

RNA extraction and sequencing

To annotate protein-coding genes, RNA sequencing of the hyphae of F. oxysporum Fo-129 was performed (with 2 biological replicates). Total RNA was extracted from 3 days PDB hyphae of Fo-129 using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol17. RNA integrity was assessed using the Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The library was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. The total raw sequencing data of 2 replicates amounted to 12.81 Gb, and after quality control and filtering using fastp v.0.23.218, 12.46 Gb of clean data were obtained (Table 1).

Genome assembly

The genome de novo assembly was used Hifiasm v0.19.819. Combined with Hi-C sequencing data, the resulting contigs/scaffolds were clustered, oriented, and ordered at the chromosome-level with Allhic v0.9.820 (parameters: enz = DpnII, CLUSTER = n), elevating the assembly to near chromosome-level. Subsequently, manual correction of the assembly was performed using Juicebox v1.11.0821 based on chromosomal interaction intensities, resulting in a chromosome-level genome. This assembly was further refined by gap filling with ONT ultra-long data using Novogene’s proprietary in-house GAP-filling technology (Patent Authorization No. CN202310944042.X)22 and TGS-GapCloser v1.2.123, followed by error correction with Racon v1.4.2024, yielding the final optimized genome assembly. After Hi-C assisted assembly, gap filling with ONT ultra-long data, and sequence error correction, the genome was assembled into 14 chromosome-level sequences, with one sequence remaining unplaced at the chromosome level. The total genome length is 52,869,277 bp, with a contig N50 length of 4,605,390 bp. The assembly results are summarized in Table 2, and the chromosomal genome characteristics were visualized using Circos v0.6925 (Fig. 3).

Repeat and structure annotation

RepeatModeler v2.0.326 was used to perform de novo prediction of repetitive sequences in the genome and construct a species-specific repetitive sequence library. Based on the RepBase database27 and the above-mentioned self-built library, RepeatMasker v4.1.628 was applied to carry out fine annotation and sequence soft-masked of repetitive sequences.

To predict protein-coding genes in the genome, we integrated three strategies: ab initio prediction, homologous protein-based prediction, and RNA-seq evidence. For ab initio prediction, Augustus v3.529 and SNAP v2013-11-2930 were used in our automated gene prediction pipeline. For homologous prediction, known protein-coding sequences from closely related species were aligned to the genome using TBLASTN v2.2.2631 (e-value ≤ 1e−5). The matched protein sequences were then aligned with genomic regions for refinement, and gene structures within each homologous region were predicted using GeneWise v2.4.132. For RNA-Seq evidence, Trinity v2.8.533 was used to assemble transcriptome data for genome annotation. To further optimize the annotation, RNA-Seq reads were aligned to the genome sequence using Hisat v2.2.134 to identify exon regions and splicing sites. The alignment results were then input into StringTie v2.2.135 for genome-guided transcript assembly. Finally, the predictions from the three methods, along with the transcriptome alignment data, were integrated using EvidenceModeler v1.1.136. The resulting gene set was further polished with PASA37 by incorporating transcriptome assembly results, thereby supplementing annotation information such as UTRs and alternative splicing to generate a final, high-quality gene set. All centromeres and telomeres (parameters:–string TTAGGG/CCCTAA -w 100000) were identified using tidk v0.2.6538 and trf v.4.09.139 (parameters: 2 7 7 80 10 90 2000 -d -m -l 2) and validated with visual inspection in IGV v2.19.740. Finally, 14 centromeres and 24 telomeres were identified on the 14 chromosomes. Although telomeric signals were detected at both ends of each chromosome, manual inspection of the terminal sequences revealed that the starting ends of Chr03, Chr04, and Chr13, as well as the terminal end of Chr14, lacked contiguous canonical TTAGGG/CCCTAA repeats. The genomic distributions of telomeres, centromeres, and gene density were visualized using RIdeogram41.

Non-coding RNA Annotation

The annotation of non-coding RNAs primarily includes four categories: tRNAs, rRNAs, miRNAs, and snRNAs. Based on the conserved secondary structural features of tRNAs, this study utilized the tRNAscan-SE v2.0.1242 to identify tRNAs within the genome. Highly conserved rRNAs from closely related species were used as references to search for rRNAs loci in the genome via BLAST alignment. Furthermore, based on the covariance models from the Rfam database, Infernal v1.1.243 was employed for genome-wide systematic prediction of miRNAs and snRNAs. We also visualized the genomic distributions of tRNAs, rRNAs, and miRNAs using RIdeogram41.

Gene functional annotation

Gene functional annotation refers to the process of obtaining functional information for genes by aligning the gene set obtained from gene structure annotation with known protein databases using alignment software. Specifically, protein motifs and domains were annotated using InterProScan v5.3944, and Gene Ontology (GO)45 identifiers were assigned to each gene according to the corresponding InterPro entries. Protein functions were predicted by aligning with the closest BLAST homologous sequences (e-value ≤ 1e−5) in both the UniProtKB/Swiss-prot46 and NR databases. Meanwhile, the gene set was mapped to the KEGG pathway database47 to determine the optimal pathway match for each gene. Furthermore, eggNOG-mapper v2.1.1248 was also performed for functional annotation, based on eggNOG orthology data49, sequence searches were performed using Diamond50, and total 14,594 genes were predicted. The functional annotation results are summarized in the upset plot made using the UpsetR51. Ultimately, functional predictions were obtained for 14,779 (97.86%) of the genes, with statistical results shown in Table 5 and Fig. 4b.

Secondary metabolism gene clusters were annotated using the ‘relaxed’ strictness model in antiSMASH fungi v8.0.452. The analysis yielded 52 predicted clusters. Among these were 4 NRPS, 10 PKS, 8 NRPS-like genes, 12 terpenes, 3 terpene-precursors, 2 indoles, 2 betalactones, 1 isocyanide, 1 phosphonate, 1 cytokinin, and 8 hybrid clusters. It is noteworthy that no such clusters were found on chromosomes 08 or 09. CAZymes were annotated using the dbCAN3 pipeline53 (HMMER, e-value < 1e−15, coverage > 0.35), and the output results annotation identified 714 genes categorized into six functional classes, including 134 auxiliary activities (AAs), 351 glycoside hydrolases (GHs), 113 glycosyl transferases (GTs), 29 polysaccharide lyases (PLs), 53 carbohydrate esterases (CEs), and 34 carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs). For effector prediction, the secretion proteins in the Fo-129 proteome were initially identified using SignalP v5.054. Subsequently, sequences containing transmembrane domains were excluded using TMHMM v2.055. Finally, EffectorP v3.0.0-beta56 was employed to predict candidate effectors. A total of 1,471 mature fasta sequences and 538 fungal candidate effectors were ultimately predicted.

Technical Validation

Genome survey

The k-mer frequency method was employed to estimate the genome size. Jellyfish v2.3.166 (count -C -m 15; histo -h 10000) was used to compute a histogram of 15 k-mer frequencies via Illumina short-read. The heterozygosity level was calculated using GenomeScope v2.067. As a result, the estimated genome length of Fo-129 was 50.84 Mb, with a homozygous rate of 100% (Fig. 5a).

Accuracy assessment

Merqury v1.368 analysis of Illumina short-read data yielded a quality value (QV) of 40.76 (Table 2).

Completeness assessment

BUSCO v5.3.269 software and fungal single-copy homologous gene set (fungi_odb10) were used to evaluate the completeness of the predicted gene sets of strain Fo-129 (C: 96.9%, S: 96.2%, D: 0.7%, F: 0.3%, M: 2.8%, n: 758) (Fig. 5b; Table 2).

Consistency assessment

To evaluate assembly accuracy, Illumina short reads were aligned to the assembled genome using BWA v0.7.17 (r1188)70, yielding a read mapping rate of approximately 99.79% and genome coverage of about 99.69% (Table 2), which indicates a high degree of consistency between the assembly and the sequencing data.

These comprehensive assessments confirm that the genome of F. oxysporum strain Fo-129 assembly has achieved a high level in terms of consistency, completeness, and accuracy.
