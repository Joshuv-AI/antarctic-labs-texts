Personalized recommendation and short-form video addiction tendency: the mediating effect of flow and the moderating effect of self-control
Authors: Libing Zhang, Jing Wang, Xiuli Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, short-form video addiction has severely impacted college students’ academic performance, physical and mental health, and social functioning. It has become a widespread public concern that remains unresolved. Existing research has predominantly focused on individual and family environmental factors influencing short video addiction tendency, as a pivotal technological feature of short-form video platforms, the relationship between personalized recommendation and short video addiction tendency still requires further empirical investigation. Guided by the Stimulus-Organism-Response theory and the Individual-Context Interaction theory as the primary theoretical frameworks, this study constructed a moderated mediation model to examine the relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency, along with the mediating role of flow and the moderating role of self-control.MethodsA random sampling method was used to recruit 2,646 college students (1,585 female, 1,061 male; Mean age = 18.83, SD = 1.13) in China to complete online questionnaires assessing personalized recommendations, short-form video addiction tendency, flow, and self-control. Data were analyzed using Model 15 of the SPSS macro PROCESS to test the moderated mediation model.ResultsAfter controlling for gender, age, and average daily usage time, personalized recommendations not only directly and positively predicted short-form video addiction tendency but also indirectly predicted it through the mediation of flow. Self-control moderated both the positive relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency and the positive relationship between flow and short-form video addiction tendency.ConclusionThis study provides empirical evidence for the positive relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency. By investigating flow as a mediator and self-control as a moderator, this study contributes to a systematic understanding of short-form video addiction tendency antecedents and offers insights for future interventions. Future research could employ longitudinal or experimental designs to further examine the relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency, as well as compare the differential effects of perceived versus objective personalization on short-form video addiction tendency.Keywords: Short-form video addiction tendency, Personalized Recommendation, Flow, Self-Control

Introduction

With the rapid development of information technology, short-form videos featuring personalized recommendations as the core technology have gained widespread popularity among Chinese internet users. According to data from the China Internet Network Information Center [1] as of 2025, 95.1% of internet users use short-form videos. As digital natives, college students characterized by flexible schedules, openness to new technologies, and lack of external supervision constitute a key user group of short-form videos. However, while enjoying the satisfaction brought by short-form videos, college students also experience the distress of being “unable to stop,” making them a high-risk group for short-form video addiction tendency [2].

Short-form video addiction tendency refers to a problematic behavior characterized by compulsive viewing of short-form videos, which leads to obvious behavioral loss of control or attention disorders, and causes difficulties in interpersonal adaptation, academic performance, and work adjustment [3]. Multiple studies have shown that short-form video addiction severely affects individuals’ emotional health, sleep quality, cognitive function, academic motivation, and sense of well-being [4–6]. Therefore, exploring the formation mechanism of short-form video addiction tendency among college students is crucial for formulating effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Research on the factors influencing short-form video addiction has focused primarily on individual factors, such as negative emotional states (e.g., depression and anxiety [7]) and personality traits like neuroticism [8], as well as family environmental factors, including parental phubbing [9] and parental neglect [10]. Nevertheless, several issues remain underexplored and warrant further investigation.

First, the relationship between personalized recommendation and short-form video addiction tendency warrants focused attention. Digital technology constitutes a significant environmental factor within the individual’s microsystem [11]. In industry practice, personalized recommendation is often regarded as a key technology for enhancing user retention on short-form video platforms [12, 13]; the academic literature also acknowledges a positive relationship between personalized recommendation and user engagement behaviors [14, 15]; however, such theoretical propositions still require empirical validation. Therefore, from the perspective of technological environmental factors, this study employs an empirical approach to examine whether a positive association exists between personalized recommendation and Short-form video addiction tendency.

Second, it is essential to further explore the mediating and moderating factors in the relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency. The Stimulus-Organism-Response(S-O-R) theory posits that external technological stimuli can influence user behavior by evoking individuals’ affective [16]. Guided by this theory, in the present study, we conceptualize personalized recommendations as an external technological stimulus (S), short-form video addiction tendency as the behavioral response (R), and introduce flow as a key emotional state (O), thereby examining its mediating role between the two. Meanwhile, the Individual-Context Interaction theory suggests that the impact of environmental factors on individual outcomes may be moderated by personal characteristics [17]. Accordingly, in this study, we introduce self-control as an individual trait to examine whether it moderates both the direct and indirect relationships between personalized recommendations and short form video addiction tendency. To simultaneously examine the mediating role of flow and the moderating role of self-control, the present study draws on both the Stimulus–Organism–Response theory and Individual-Context Interaction theory as its primary theoretical frameworks. A moderated mediation model was constructed to systematically investigate the relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency.

Personalized recommendation and short-form video addiction tendency

Personalized recommendation is a technology centered on algorithms that relies on big data analytics and information filtering mechanisms to accurately match information content to users based on their preferences and needs. By leveraging user information, it can recommend content that users are interested in but which is difficult to discover [18]. Scholars have posited in theoretical articles that personalized recommendation serves as a core competitive advantage for short-form video platforms and constitutes a key technological factor contributing to high user retention [19, 20]. This can be interpreted as follows: the stronger the personalization of recommendations on a short-form video platform, that is, the more closely the recommended content aligns with users’ interests, the more likely users are to sustain prolonged engagement with the platform. Furthermore, research documenting positive associations between other technological features and smartphone addiction [21], online gaming addiction [22], social media addiction [23], and short‑form video addiction [24] provides indirect evidence supporting a potential link between personalized recommendation and short‑form video addiction tendency. Additionally, qualitative studies based on user interviews have identified personalized video as a critical factor driving addictive usage patterns among short‑form video users [25, 26]. Integrating the theoretical arguments and indirect evidence, we hypothesize that a higher degree of personalized recommendation is associated with a stronger short-form video addiction tendency.

The mediating effect of flow

Flow refers to a positive emotional experience in which individuals show a strong interest in an activity or thing and are motivated to fully engage in it [27]. When individuals experience flow, they focus intensely on the task at hand, completely ignoring other task-irrelevant perceptions, tuning out the surrounding environment, feeling that time flies, and repeatedly engaging in the current behavior to maintain this pleasant emotion.

Stimulus-Organism-Response theory posits that external stimuli can influence an individual’s behavior by arousing emotions or affective states [16]. In other words, an individual’s emotions or affective experiences play a mediating role in the relationship between external technological stimuli and individuals’ usage behavior. According to this theory, personalized recommendations, as a key technological feature of short-form video platforms, can be conceptualized as an external techno-environmental stimulus (S). Short-form video addiction tendency can be regarded as the organismic response (R), while flow, as an emotional experience, corresponds to the organismic state (O). Accordingly, we hypothesize that flow mediates the relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency.

On the one hand, personalized recommendation may enhance flow. Compared with traditional text and image social media, short-form videos, as a new type of social media, deliver personalized recommended content integrated with dynamic audio, visual, and video effects, making them more appealing to users and fostering a deeper sense of flow [17]. According to the Self-Reference theory, individuals automatically exhibit positive responses to stimuli that are related to themselves [28]. The video content recommended on short-form video platforms aligns with users’ personalized needs, thereby making users more likely to experience positive emotional reactions. Moreover, existing research has found that the entertainment, interactivity, and convenience of short-form video platforms are significantly and positively associated with flow [6, 29]. In addition, various personalized feature settings in social media, convenient technical characteristics of smartphones, and even the information quality and system quality of the Internet have all enhanced users’ flow [30, 31]. Thus, it can be inferred that a higher degree of personalized recommendation is associated with a stronger sense of flow in individuals.

On the other hand, flow may promote short-form video addiction tendency. According to Rational Addiction theory, individuals engage in repeated activities or behaviors to maximize their utility [32]. When individuals experience flow, a positive emotional state, they may engage in repeated viewing of short‑form videos, thereby increasing their short-form video addiction tendency. Furthermore, existing studies have found that flow is significantly positively correlated with internet addiction, smartphone and addiction [33, 34]. It can therefore be hypothesized that the stronger the flow experienced by an individual, the greater their short-form video addiction tendency.

The moderating effect of self-control

Self-control refers to the ability of individuals to proactively regulate their behaviors in accordance with personal values and social expectations [35]. Individuals with higher self-control tend to demonstrate better physical health [36], superior emotion regulation skills, higher-quality friendships [35], and greater well-being [37]. Moreover, they are less likely to engage in risky behaviors [38] and addictive behaviors [39]. Self‑control is regarded as a core protective factor against various addictive behaviors [40–42].

The Individual-Context Interaction theory proposes that the influence of environmental factors on individuals is not uniform; rather, behavioral outcomes are shaped by the interactive effects of environmental factors and individual characteristics [17]. Based on this theoretical perspective and applied specifically to the present study, the relationship between personalized recommendations (an environmental factor) and short-form video addiction tendency (an individual behavior) may also vary depending on differences in self-control (a personal characteristic). Therefore, we propose that self‑control plays a moderating role in the proposed model via two distinct pathways.

First, we hypothesize that self‑control moderates the relationship between personalized recommendation and short‑form video addiction tendency. The Risk-Buffering Hypothesis suggests that positive individual characteristics can attenuate or buffer the adverse effects of risk factors [43]. Personalized videos are highly enticing to users, who may find it difficult to resist watching them; thus, personalized recommendation can be viewed as a tempting risk factor. As a positive individual characteristic, high self-control may buffer the risk of addiction within personalized recommendation environments. Conversely, individuals with low self‑control possess weaker inhibitory systems, making them more susceptible to addictive tendencies. Moreover, empirical studies have found that self‑control plays a similar protective role when individuals are exposed to other media temptations [44]. Based on this, we speculate that self‑control may buffer the positive predictive effect of personalized recommendation on short‑form video addiction tendency.

Second, we hypothesize that self-control moderates the relationship between flow and short-form video addiction. The Dual-Systems theory of self-control suggests that individual behavior results from the interaction between an impulsive system and a reflective system [45]. When flow in short-form videos, the impulsive system is activated, leading to an urge to continue watching and increasing the risk of addiction. However, individuals with high self-control tend to be dominated by the reflective system, which enables earlier detection of their emotional state and the initiation of control strategies, thereby reducing the likelihood that a flowed individual will progress toward addiction. Additionally, existing empirical research has found that self-control also moderates the relationship between flow and other technology-related addictions [43]. Based on this, we speculate that self-control may buffer the positive predictive effect of flow on short-form video addiction tendency.

The present study

The prevalence of short-form video use and the severe consequences of short-form video addiction among college students have compelled us to explore its influencing factors. As a core competitive technology of short-form video platforms, personalized recommendation is widely regarded by both industry and academia as being positively associated with user retention. Nevertheless, the relationship between this feature and short-form video addiction tendency still requires empirical evidence, and its mediating and moderating mechanisms warrant in‑depth investigation. Therefore, this study explores the issues hierarchically based on existing theories. To begin with, to simultaneously test the mediating role of flow and the moderating role of self-control, this study adopts the Stimulus-Organism-Response (S-O-R) theory and the Individual-Context Interaction theory as the primary theoretical frameworks to construct a moderated mediation model (the theoretical model is shown in Fig. 1). Furthermore, to examine the relationships between variables in each pathway in detail, hypotheses are derived based on specific theories. Specifically, based on Uses and Gratifications theory, personalized recommendation may be positively correlated with short‑form video addiction tendency. Self-Reference theory and Rational Addiction theory provide the theoretical basis for the positive correlations between personalized recommendation and flow, flow and short‑form video addiction tendency, respectively. The moderating role of self-control in the relationship between personalized recommendation and short‑form video addiction tendency, as well as between flow and short‑form video addiction tendency, is proposed based on the Risk-Buffering Hypothesis and Dual-Systems theory of self-control, respectively. Methods

Participants

The study utilized a random sampling method, recruiting university students from five institutions located in Henan, Zhejiang, Guangdong, and Shandong provinces as well as the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region in China. After obtaining informed consent from both the course instructors and the students themselves, an online questionnaire link was distributed to participants during class breaks. Trained research assistants read the instructions aloud prior to the survey. A total of 2,900 university students were initially invited to participate. After excluding invalid responses (e.g., patterned answers), 2,646 valid questionnaires were retained, resulting in an effective response rate of 91%. The sample consisted of 1,585 female students (59.9%) and 1,061 male students (40.1%). In terms of geographical background, 1,384 participants were from urban areas (52.3%) and 1,262 were from towns or rural areas (47.7%). Regarding academic discipline, 1,102 were liberal arts majors (42.8%) and 1,470 were science majors (57.2%). The age of participants ranged from 17 to 23 years old (M = 18.83, SD = 1.13).

Measures

Personalized recommendation questionnaire

Personalized recommendation was measured using the Personalized Recommendation Questionnaire [46]. Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was translated into Chinese, and the term “website” in the original scale was revised to “short-form video platform” to better fit the research context. In the study, personalized recommendation was conceptualized as users’ perceived level of personalization in the videos recommended by short-form video platforms (e.g., “the short-form platform understands my specific needs and recommends content accordingly”). The questionnaire consists of 4 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Completely inconsistent, 5 = Completely consistent), with higher scores indicating a higher level of personalized recommendation. The fit indices of the confirmatory factor analysis were acceptable(χ2/df = 4.50, RMSEA = 0.02, CFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.94, SRMR = 0.02),indicating good structural validity of the questionnaire. In the present study, the questionnaire yielded a Cronbach’s α of 0.86.

Short-form video addiction tendency Scale

Short-form video addiction tendency was measured using the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS) [47]. Prior to data collection, the scale was translated into Chinese, and the term “social media” in the original scale was revised to “short-form videos” (e.g., “I have tried to reduce my short-form video use but failed”). The scale consists of 6 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Completely inconsistent, 5 = Completely consistent). Higher scores indicate a stronger tendency toward short-form video addiction tendency. The fit indices of the confirmatory factor analysis were acceptable(χ2/df = 4.86, RMSEA = 0.04, CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.98, SRMR = 0.01),indicating good structural validity of the questionnaire. In the present study, the scale yielded a Cronbach’s α of 0.72.

Flow scale

Flow during short-form video use was measured using the Flow Scale [48]. Prior to data collection, the scale was translated into Chinese, and the term “social media” in the original scale was revised to “short-form videos” (e.g., “When I use short-form videos, I feel time flies”). The scale consists of 11 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Completely inconsistent, 5 = Completely consistent), with higher scores indicating a higher-level flow. The fit indices of the confirmatory factor analysis were acceptable (χ2/df = 3.27, RMSEA = 0.03, CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, SRMR = 0.02), indicating good structural validity of the questionnaire. In the present study, the scale yielded a Cronbach’s α of 0.77.

Self-control scale

Self-control was measured using the Brief Self-Control Scale (BSCS) [49], a revised version of the Self-Control Scale (SCS) [35]. Prior to the measurement, the questionnaires were translated into Chinese (e.g., “I am good at resisting temptation”). The scale consists of 7 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Completely inconsistent, 5 = Completely consistent), with higher scores indicating a higher level of self-control. The fit indices of the confirmatory factor analysis were acceptable(χ2/df = 3.82, RMSEA = 0.03, CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, SRMR = 0.01), indicating good structural validity of the questionnaire. In the present study, the scale yielded a Cronbach’s α of 0.75.

Data analysis

First, Mplus 8.3 was used to conduct a common method bias test on the data. Subsequently, descriptive statistics and correlation analysis were performed for all measured variables with SPSS 25.0. Finally, the SPSS macro PROCESS was employed to test the moderated mediation model [50], in which flow served as the mediating variable and self-control as the moderating variable.

Results

Common method bias test

Since data for multiple variables were collected from the same participants, potential common method bias might exist. To mitigate the impact of common method bias, procedural controls were implemented, such as anonymous responses and reverse-coded items for some questions. Additionally, following the approach of existing studies [51], prior to formal data analysis, we first conducted a common method bias test using the unmeasured latent method factor approach. The model fit indices after adding the latent method factor (χ²/df = 23.44, RMSEA = 0.09, CFI = 0.67, TLI = 0.64, SRMR = 0.10) were significantly worse than those of the hypothesized four-factor model (χ²/df = 4.61, RMSEA = 0.04, CFI = 0.96, TLI = 0.94, SRMR = 0.06). According to the unmeasured latent method factor approach, if severe common method bias were present, the model incorporating a common method factor should demonstrate significantly better fit indices compared to the model without such a factor [52]. However, the results above indicate that the fit indices of the model did not improve substantially after adding the common method factor. Therefore, it can be concluded that no serious common method bias was present in the measurements.

Test of moderated mediation effect

The present study used Model 15 of the SPSS macro PROCESS developed by Hayes AF to test the moderated mediation model [50]. Existing research has identified gender- and age-based differences in short-form video addiction [53, 54], and has also demonstrated a strong association between usage duration and such addiction tendency [53]. Therefore, to more clearly elucidate the relationship between personalized recommendation and short-form video addiction tendency as well as the mediating and moderating factors involved, gender, age, and usage duration were included in the model as control variables. The results (see Table 2) showed that personalized recommendation had a significant positive predictive effect on flow (β = 0.39, t = 22.46, p < 0.001), supporting Hypothesis1. Flow significantly positively predicted short-form video addiction tendency (β = 0.33, t = 19.40, p < 0.001), and personalized recommendation also had a significant positive predictive effect on short-form video addiction tendency (β = 0.06, t = 3.68, p < 0.05). These findings indicated that flow mediates the relationship between personalized recommendation and short-form video addiction tendency, thus supporting Hypothesis 2. Additionally, the interaction between personalized recommendations and self-control, as well as the interaction between flow and self-control, were both significantly negatively predicted short-form video addiction tendency (β = -0.12, p < 0.001; β = -0.12, p < 0.001). This indicated that both the direct and indirect effects of personalized recommendation on short-form video addiction tendency were moderated by self-control, thus supporting Hypothesis 3 and Hypothesis 4.

Discussion

Guided by the Stimulus-Organism-Response theory and the Individual-Context Interaction theory as the primary theoretical frameworks, this study constructed a moderated mediation model to examine the relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency, along with the mediating role of flow and the moderating role of self-control. The results indicate that: personalized recommendation significantly and positively predicts short-form video addiction tendency; flow positively mediates the relationship between personalized recommendation and short-form video addiction tendency, and self-control negatively moderates the relationship between personalized recommendation and short-form video addiction tendency, and the relationship between flow and short-form video addiction tendency. These findings not only contribute to a deeper understanding of the antecedents of short-form video addiction tendency but also extend the applicability of traditional psychological theories to the context of short-form video usage, while offering valuable implications for future interventions targeting short-form video addiction tendencies among university students.

Personalized recommendation and short-form video addiction tendency

The findings revealed that personalized recommendation significantly and positively predicted short-form video addiction, meaning that a higher level of personalized recommendation was associated with a greater short-form video addiction tendency. This result is consistent with prior research [55]. According to the Uses and Gratifications theory [56], personalized recommendations not only fulfill users’ need to “obtain maximum reward with minimal effort” [26, 57], but also align video content with their preferences. The gratification of such needs may heighten the risk of addictive usage. Thus, a higher degree of personalized recommendation is associated with a greater short-form video addiction tendency among users. It is worth noting that, compared to flow and self-control, the direct predictive effect of personalized recommendation on short video addiction tendency had a relatively modest effect size in the findings. This finding suggests that although the predictive effect of personalized recommendations on short-form video addiction tendency cannot be overlooked, the roles of flow and self-control warrant significant attention.

One point that warrants clarification is that the present study focuses on perceived personalization rather than objective personalization. Perceived personalization refers to the user’s subjective judgment of whether information aligns with their individual needs, whereas objective personalization refers to the personalized content delivered by the platform based on predictive algorithms derived from user preference data [58]. Given that perceived personalization does not perfectly correspond with objective personalization, the relationship between objective personalization and the tendency toward short-form video addiction cannot be definitively determined in this investigation [20]. However, based on the recommendation mechanisms employed by short-form video platforms, the personalization level of the videos pushed to users is continuously adjusted based on their usage data. Therefore, if a user perceives a high level of personalization, it is reasonable to infer that the objective personalization implemented by the platform is also substantial. Consequently, the relationship between objective personalization and the tendency toward short-form video addiction may likewise be positive. Nevertheless, a conclusive answer will require further empirical investigation.

The mediating effect of flow

The results indicated that flow played a positive mediating role between personalized recommendation and short-form video addiction. In other words, a higher degree of personalization in recommended videos was associated with stronger feelings of flow, which in turn predicted a greater short-form video addiction tendency. This finding aligns with previous research. For example, flow has been shown to mediate the relationship between online gaming features and online gaming addiction [59].

Personalized recommendation significantly and positively predicts flow, which may be associated with the self-reference effect [28]. Content recommended on short-form video platforms often aligns with users’ interests or reflects what people connected to them are viewing. When users encounter videos that match their preferences, they tend to experience pleasure and devote time involuntarily. Similarly, content viewed by people they are related to can spark curiosity or elicit positive reactions [60], both of which are integral components of flow. Thus, a higher degree of personalization is associated with a stronger sense of flow experienced by individuals.

Flow significantly and positively predicts short-form video addiction tendency, which may be a result of individuals’ rational choice [32]. In this study, flow is regarded as a positive emotional state. To sustain this positive emotion, users tend to consume more short-form videos, thereby strengthening their short-form video addiction tendency. Csikszentmihalyi has similarly pointed out that almost any activity capable of inducing a state of flow can become addictive [61]; for instance, flow in online gaming has been shown to contribute to gaming addiction [62]. Consequently, individuals who experience a stronger sense of flow tend to exhibit a greater short-form video addiction tendency.

Examining the mediating role of flow not only supports the Stimulus–Organism–Response theory, but also deepens our understanding of how personalized recommendations as an environmental stimulus indirectly relate to short‑form video addiction tendency as a behavioral outcome.

The moderating effect of self-control

The findings revealed that self‑control buffered the positive predictive effect of personalized recommendation on short-form video addiction tendency. Specifically, the positive association between personalized recommendation and addiction tendency was weaker among individuals with higher self‑control. This result aligns with previous research [44]. From the platform designer’s perspective, personalized recommendation may serve as a positive factor for enhancing user retention; however, from the user’s perspective, since personalized recommendation significantly predicts the tendency toward short‑form video addiction, it can be regarded as a negative or risk factor in this context. Individuals with higher self‑control possess greater psychological resources to counteract the adverse effects of risk factors [43], thereby attenuating the positive predictive effect of personalized recommendation on short‑form video addiction tendency.

The results further indicated that self‑control also buffered the positive predictive effect of flow on the tendency toward short‑form video addiction tendency. That is, the relationship between flow and addiction tendency was weaker among individuals with higher self‑control. This finding is consistent with prior studies [63]. When faced with high flow experiences, individuals with high self-control are characterized by a prevailing reflective system [45], which allows them to restrain the impulse to continue watching short‑form videos, lowering their short‑form video addiction tendency. Thus, the positive predictive effect of flow on short‑form video addiction tendency is buffered by self-control. Examining the moderating role of self‑control not only supports the Individual-Context Interaction theory, but also extends its application to the context of short‑form video use.

Implications and limitations

Implications

This study found that personalized recommendation positively predicts short-form video addiction tendency, with flow serving as a positive mediator between the two. Self-control moderates both the relationship between personalized recommendation and short-form video addiction tendency, as well as between flow and short-form video addiction tendency. These findings provide theoretical implications and references for future intervention research regarding short-form video addiction tendency among university students.

Theoretical implications

First, this study found that personalized recommendations are positively associated with short-form video addiction tendency. This result not only offers empirical evidence regarding the antecedents of short-form video addiction tendency but also extends the observed positive relationship between technological features and user behavior to the context of short-form video use.

Second, by examining the mediating role of flow and the moderating role of self-control, this study clarifies the indirect relationship and boundary conditions between personalized recommendations and short-form video tendency. It also offers empirical support from the context of short-form video use for the Stimulus-Organism-Response theory and the Individual-Context Interaction theory, while extending their explanatory scope and practical relevance in contemporary digital settings.

Practical implications

First, this study found that personalized recommendations are directly and indirectly (via flow) positively associated with short‑form video addiction tendency. Based on these results, it is tentatively proposed that personalized recommendations might be considered a potential intervention direction for short‑form video addiction tendency. Designers should consider the positive association between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency when applying such technology. Educators may utilize various channels to help students recognize the direct and indirect links among personalized recommendations, flow, and short-form video addiction tendency, thereby enhancing their objective understanding of short-form video usage. Considering the relatively modest effect size of the direct predictive effect of personalized recommendations on short-form video addiction tendency in this study, personalized recommendation may only be one of the influencing factors of short-form video addiction tendency. Therefore, when designers and educators examine its effects in future practice, it is also necessary to pay attention to the roles of other factors, such as flow and self-control.

Second, the findings indicate that self-control negatively moderates the associations between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency, as well as between flow and addiction tendency. Based on these results, it is tentatively proposed that self-control may represent another potential direction for intervention. Educators and users could consider designing and implementing strategies to enhance self-control based on existing research findings [64–66], thereby improving their own self-control capabilities, and leveraging the protective role of self-control. Since this study employed a cross-sectional correlational design, the potential intervention directions mentioned above are only inferences based on the data from this study and are not empirically validated intervention conclusions. Effective intervention strategies for short-form video addiction still require further empirical support from intervention experiments.

Limitations

While this study supported the theoretical hypotheses and provided suggests potential directions for future interventions of short‑form video addiction tendency, it is not without limitations.

First, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes causal inferences regarding the relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency. Future research could employ longitudinal or experimental designs to examine causality.

Second, this study relied on self-reported measures of perceived personalization, which may diverge from objective personalized recommendations. Future studies could incorporate more objective indicators to examine the relationship between algorithm-based personalization and addiction tendency.

Third, as the sample predominantly consisted of first- and second-year undergraduates, the findings cannot be generalized to other academic years or age groups. Further research is needed to examine the robustness of the conclusions across more diverse populations.

Conclusion

This study systematically examined the relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency among university students by constructing a moderated mediation model. The findings reveal that personalized recommendations are not only directly and positively associated with short-form video addiction tendency but also indirectly linked to it through the mediating role of flow. More importantly, self-control was found to buffer both the association between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency and the relationship between flow and short-form video addiction tendency. These results deepen the systematic understanding of the antecedents of short-form video addiction and provided suggests insights for future interventions. Future research could employ longitudinal or experimental designs to further examine the relationship between personalized recommendations and short-form video addiction tendency, as well as compare the differential effects of perceived versus objective personalization on short-form video addiction tendency.
