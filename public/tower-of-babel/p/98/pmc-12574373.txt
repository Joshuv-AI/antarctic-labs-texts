Counting the seconds: a quality improvement initiative to accelerate intraoperative results for arthrocentesis cell counts in a paediatric tertiary care hospital
Authors: Sarah Rose Purtell, Madeline Hornfeck, Patrick Carry, Tyler Winkler, Sumeet Garg, Julia Skye Sanders
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn paediatric patients with concern for septic arthritis, arthrocentesis may be performed under anaesthesia with intraoperative cell count determining need for surgical intervention. Shorter turnaround time (TAT) between collection and result minimises anaesthesia exposure for the patient and surgical time for the treatment team. This study evaluated a quality improvement (QI) initiative to decrease TAT to <1 hour by improving interdisciplinary communication and placing visual reminders (‘stat card’) to indicate priority handling.Methods206 consecutive paediatric patients who underwent arthrocentesis for the diagnosis of septic arthritis were identified. Midway through the collection period, the QI intervention (stat card) was implemented. We collected the date and time of arthrocentesis and sample verification (TAT) and calculated the proportion of TAT <1 hour preintervention and postintervention. We collected variables related to the affected joint and whether the stat card was used. Operative time was calculated for those samples collected in the Operating Room (OR).ResultsThe final study population included 109 patients preintervention and 88 patients postintervention. Postintervention, the stat card was used in only 44% (20/45) of eligible cases. Compliance was highest in aspirations of the hip (75%). After adjusting for the affected joint, the odds of TAT <1 hour were higher in the postintervention stat card group compared with the postintervention no stat card group (odds ratio 7.10, p=0.0147) and to the preintervention group (odds ratio 3.63, p=0.0810). There was no difference between the postintervention no stat card versus the preintervention groups (odds ratio 0.51, p=0.2524). TAT was significantly decreased when the stat card was used (42 min) compared with when it was not used (84 min) (mean difference −39.5%, p=0.0178). After adjusting for the affected joint, there was no difference in operative time across the three groups (p=0.2531).ConclusionA multidisciplinary QI initiative for the intraoperative diagnosis of septic arthritis was effective in reducing cell count TAT but demonstrated poor compliance and failed to reduce operative time.

Introduction

Paediatric orthopaedic surgeons often use synovial fluid (SF) cell counts to determine the need for formal incision and drainage in a patient with a joint effusion and signs of infection concerning for septic arthritis. To minimise anaesthetic exposure, arthrocentesis may be done under the same anaesthetic as the surgical intervention, if a cell count can be done efficiently. Laboratories use automated or manual analysis to determine white blood cell (WBC) and polymorphonuclear leucocytes (%) counts in SF samples, with traditionally accepted cut-offs of >50 000 WBCs/μL indicating septic arthritis and need for open surgery. SF specimen collection, transport, analysis and resulting directly involves multiple teams and staff members, often with no direct personal contact. In response to the extended time from specimen submission to result, known as turnaround time (TAT), surgeons may choose to skip an arthrocentesis and perform incision and drainage on a joint with clinical concern for septic arthritis, rather than wait under anaesthesia for the result. With a goal to reduce TAT to <1 hour, a multidisciplinary quality improvement (QI) initiative was developed with involvement from orthopaedic surgeons, nurses, operating room (OR) technologists and laboratory staff (microbiology and pathology). Barriers were identified and a process was delineated to streamline communication and clearly identify priority samples. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of the QI intervention in reducing TAT to <1 hour.

Methods

SF cell count TAT was identified as an opportunity for a multidisciplinary QI project after anecdotal reports of delays in the OR at a tertiary care paediatric hospital. Process review by key stakeholders from the OR, orthopaedic surgery and clinical laboratory teams identified several barriers, including specimen ordering and labelling, specimen transport, prioritisation of testing within the lab and communication of results. After multidisciplinary discussion, changes to the specimen collection and resulting process were made in September 2020: the surgical OR team called to alert the haematology laboratory of incoming emergent (stat) cell count, the surgical team maintained responsibility for transportation of the specimen, haematology immediately processed and notified the OR team with results, and haematology then was responsible for transportation of the specimen to microbiology for further testing. In July of 2022, a coloured paper tag was added to samples (‘stat card’) that served as a visual reminder to laboratory staff that the sample was to be prioritised (figure 1).

QI Intervention Stat Card. OR, operating room; QI, quality improvement.

To determine the effectiveness of the intervention, consecutive patients who underwent an arthrocentesis between September 2020 and September 2023 were identified. Patients who underwent arthrocentesis prior to July 2022 were included in the preintervention group, and patients who underwent a joint aspiration after July 2022 were included in the postintervention group. Samples in the postintervention group were divided into those with the ‘stat card’ and those without.

We collected the date and time when the sample was obtained and the date and time when the sample was first verified by the laboratory (when the OR team was notified of results). The duration of time between sample acquisition and verification was the primary variable of interest (TAT). We calculated the proportion of samples with TAT <1 hour. We also collected the sample collection location (OR, floor or emergency department), affected joint, how the order was placed in the electronic medical record (high priority vs routine), as well as whether the ‘stat card’ was used (yes vs no). All patients who had the stat card sent were cases in which the operating surgeon waited for the cell count results before continuing with surgery, while in cases where the stat card was not used, the operating surgeon proceeded with surgery based on clinical findings rather than waiting for the cell count results. Operative time was only calculated for those subjects under general anaesthesia. Patients from the emergency department or floor were included as a comparison group, but operative time was not calculated.

A subset of patients (n=28) contributed multiple samples to the dataset (multiple joints sampled or the same joint sampled multiple times). In this subset of patients, we preferentially selected the sample that was flagged as a priority for each individual patient. If multiple samples were flagged as high priority or, conversely, if multiple samples were flagged as routine priority, we preferentially selected the sample that was obtained first for inclusion in the final dataset. This process ensured that all observations were independent.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the clinical characteristics. A multiple variable linear regression model, adjusted for collection department, joint and priority level, was used to test for differences in TAT before versus after implementation of the QI intervention (‘stat card’). The time variable was log-transformed due to the skewed nature of this outcome variable. Parameter estimates from the model, the average difference between groups, were exponentiated and interpreted as the per cent change in TAT preintervention versus postintervention. Multiple variable linear regression models were also used to test whether differences between groups depended on sample priority level. A secondary logistic regression model was also used to compare the percentage of samples in which TAT was <1 hour across these three groups. Finally, among priority samples collected in the OR during the postintervention period, we compared differences in clinical characteristics among samples when the ‘stat card’ was used versus when the ‘stat card’ was not used.

Results

The final study population included n=109 unique patients in the preintervention group and n=88 unique patients in the postintervention group. There was no difference in the department where the sample was collected or the joint affected between the preintervention and postintervention groups. There was a difference in the percentage of the samples ordered as high priority (p=0.0085) (table 1), indicating that more samples were ordered as high priority (STAT) after the QI intervention.

Given the relatively higher compliance of the stat card in the hip, we performed a secondary analysis of only these patients. When comparing the mean operative time preintervention to postintervention (64.8 and 54.3 min, respectively) and postintervention with and without stat card use (53.3 and 55.8 min, respectively), there was no statistically significant difference (p=0.5065 and p=0.8343). Similarly, when we compared the mean TAT preintervention to postintervention (65.7 and 59.3 min, respectively), there was no statistically significant difference (p=0.6111). However, when we compared the TAT postintervention with the stat card to without (42.8 and 82.8 min, respectively), there was a statistically significant difference (p=0.0101), indicating that even in this subgroup, decreased TAT did not correlate with a decrease in operative time.

Discussion

SF cell count remains the gold standard for differentiating between non-inflammatory, inflammatory and infectious joint disorders13 and aiding in surgical decision-making. Cell counts >50 000 WBCs/mm3 are indicative of septic arthritis4 and guide decision-making for surgeons managing these patients, although more recent evidence suggests lower cell cut-offs such as 33 000 may be more sensitive.5 Arthrocentesis may be performed in the ED, on the ward or in the OR. Given the higher likelihood of needing sedation in paediatric patients, arthrocentesis may be performed under general anaesthesia, with the ability to proceed directly to open incision and drainage if indicated by elevated cell counts. Avoiding a second anaesthetic has many advantages, including hospital resource utilisation and risk mitigation, particularly in neonates and infants who may experience cardiovascular and pulmonary complications from anaesthesia, with some studies suggesting potential long-term neurodevelopmental effects.6

To effectively use single setting arthrocentesis and surgical drainage, TAT must be minimised. Extended time places surgeons in a challenging position, forcing them to decide between proceeding with potentially unnecessary open procedures or taking the risk of bringing the patient back for a separate surgery and anaesthesia once cell count results are available. TAT serves as a metric for evaluating not only laboratory efficiency but also the overall effectiveness of hospital programmes.7 A shorter TAT carries direct benefits: it reduces the length of time spent under anaesthesia, aids in optimising OR time, decreases length of stay, increases patient safety and generally does so in a cost-effective fashion.8 In this study, the primary goal was to consistently reduce the time from joint sample acquisition to first verification by the laboratory team to <1 hour with the hopes of subsequently reducing the total time paediatric patients spend under general anaesthesia in the orthopaedic setting.

The primary barriers identified by our multidisciplinary QI team were related to communication. Our formal intervention, the ‘stat card’ (figure 1), provided clear instruction to both the OR and laboratory teams. Although simple, our intervention was consistent with a systematic review that found that the top 25% of their top performing orders implemented the use of different coloured labels for urgent specimens and regularly trained lab staff to expedite urgent samples into protocols.9

While the stat card resulted in a higher proportion of TAT <1 hour, it did not result in decreased operative time. Compliance was limited, with only 45% of eligible cases using the card, which could explain these findings. Another explanation may be the surgeon waiting for SF results in cases where the stat card was used, while proceeding directly to surgery based on clinical findings in cases where the stat card was not used, resulting in shorter median operative times for this group. The increased usage of hip joint aspirations is likely due to the diagnostic challenge that septic arthritis of the hip presents. Infections in other joints usually have a more specific clinical exam with effusions easier to visualise, palpate and identify; therefore, surgeons may feel more comfortable moving forward with operative incision and drainage in those cases without a diagnostic aspiration. However, our subgroup analysis of the hip cases did not demonstrate a decrease in operative time corresponding with a decrease in TAT with stat card use, which we would have expected. Statistically significant changes in TAT could also simply not be large enough to affect overall operative time, given the innumerable factors contributing to a patient’s time spent in the operating suite. These findings highlight the joint-specific and case-specific nuances, as well as challenges in process changes within a busy tertiary care referral centre, where processes must be shared, understood and then used by fluctuating teams.

As we experienced, limitations commonly associated with improvement projects in healthcare settings include staffing complications, such as turnover and variable knowledge levels, inconsistent communication practices between providers and staff, variability in electronic medical record systems and inconsistency in training programmes for procedures. These limitations highlight the importance of recognising the broader systemic factors that influence QI initiatives and emphasise the need for a comprehensive and adaptable approach to overcome these challenges in the evolving landscape of healthcare improvement. Another limitation was the variation in the clinical scenarios included. The decision to operate or wait for cell count results, regardless of stat card use, was at the discretion of the operating surgeon. This study was further limited by potential human errors in calculation and potential Hawthorne effect following implementation of the stat card process. Finally, these findings may not be generalisable to hospital settings without the capability for intraoperative SF analysis.

Conclusions

A multidisciplinary QI initiative for the intraoperative diagnosis of septic arthritis was effective in reducing cell count TAT but demonstrated poor compliance and failed to reduce operative time. Future work will focus on identifying barriers to compliance as well as understanding the factors driving operative time and clinical decision-making.
