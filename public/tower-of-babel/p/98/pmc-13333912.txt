Ultrasound-guided closed-loop control of magnetic hydrogel microrobots with adaptive gait switching
Authors: Xiaofeng Lang, Lang Chu, Lei Chi, Cheng Qi, Tiantian Kong, Zhou Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soft hydrogel microrobots offer unique advantages for safe, minimally invasive biomedical interventions, yet precise control in tissue-like media remains challenging due to weak ultrasound echoes and actuation uncertainty. Here, we use a millimeter-scale magnetic hydrogel robot as a proof-of-concept platform to develop an ultrasound-guided closed-loop control strategy for adaptive navigation of soft hydrogel microrobots. Real-time localization is achieved by combining inter-frame differencing with a correlated-noise Kalman filter, providing stable feedback even under low acoustic contrast. A hierarchical controller—model predictive control (MPC) for swimming and proportional–integral–derivative (PID) control for rolling—executes trajectories and switches gaits under a rigorously validated stability rule. Inspired by the crown-of-thorns starfish, the hydrogel robot adaptively transitions between swimming and rolling in response to local environmental conditions. In heterogeneous tissue-mimicking phantoms, the system achieves sub-4 mm trajectory-tracking error, climbs graded surfaces, clears vertical steps through adaptive gait switching, and explores unseen obstacles without precomputed maps. By integrating ultrasound sensing, adaptive feedback, and multimodal locomotion, this work transforms compliant hydrogels from passive movers into predictable robotic agents, providing a generalizable framework for imaging-integrated control of soft microrobots.

Introduction

Microrobots hold great promise for performing precise interventions deep within the human body, ranging from targeted drug delivery to minimally invasive surgical procedures. Among them, soft hydrogel microrobots are particularly attractive due to their material compliance1–7, biocompatibility8–11, ability to interact safely with delicate tissues, and facile functionalization for bio-integration12–20. However, realizing these advantages requires more than suitable materials. Effective biomedical microrobots must navigate complex, dynamic tissue environments with high positional accuracy21. Such navigation demands real-time control22,23, continuous feedback24–26, and adaptive locomotion that can adjust to heterogeneous biological terrains27. Unlike rigid microrobots28, hydrogels deform under flow and exhibit strong coupling with surrounding fluids, leading to nonlinear, time-dependent dynamics that complicate both tracking and control29,30. For example, jellyfish-inspired soft robots experience fluid resistance that counteracts their intended shape changes31,32, underscoring the need for robust, adaptive control frameworks capable of handling deformable bodies in viscous environments.

Magnetic propulsion combined with ultrasound imaging offers a practical solution for achieving closed-loop operation. Magnetic actuation is non-invasive33–35, remotely controllable, and compatible with clinical systems36,37, while ultrasound provides real-time, deep-tissue feedback at high resolution and low cost. These complementary modalities have already enabled closed-loop control in rigid magnetic microparticles, such as colloidal particles38–40, clusters41,42, and helical swimmers43. For instance, swarms of magnetic nanoparticles have been steered through blood vessels using ultrasound Doppler imaging21, maintaining control within highly dynamic physiological flows44,45. Similarly, single helical microrobots have navigated upstream against blood flow under ultrasound guidance46, demonstrating the effectiveness of ultrasound-guided magnetic control for rigid microrobotic systems47,48. However, extending these strategies to soft hydrogel robots remains a major challenge. Fully autonomous, ultrasound-guided navigation of deformable microrobots has yet to be achieved, and closed-loop switching among multiple gaits has been only minimally explored. Realizing such adaptive behavioral transitions would bring artificial soft robots closer to the natural agility of biological organisms.

In this study, we extend ultrasound-guided closed-loop control to soft hydrogel microrobots by addressing two long-standing challenges: unstable ultrasound localization and mode-limited actuation. First, we establish a real-time ultrasound localization pipeline that combines inter-frame differencing with adaptive Kalman filtering to stabilize weak echoes and suppress tissue artifacts. Second, we design a hierarchical controller integrating model predictive control (MPC) for swimming and proportional–integral–derivative (PID) control for rolling, with a mathematically validated switching rule ensuring stable transitions. As a proof of concept, we fabricate crown-of-thorns starfish (COTS)-inspired millimeter-scale magnetic hydrogel robots using digital light processing (DLP) printing of PEGDA hydrogels embedded with magnetic nanoparticles49. Driven by modulated magnetic fields, the robots adaptively switch between omnidirectional swimming (sawtooth excitation) and surface-conforming rolling (phase-offset sinusoidal excitation). Results

We selected the crown-of-thorns starfish (COTS) as the biological model because it naturally exhibits two distinct gaits: larvae swim by beating surface cilia, whereas adults roll by curling their body into a compact, spheroidal shape that provides stable, surface-conforming motion under flowing water (Fig. 1a). Inspired by these behaviors, we designed a star-shaped hydrogel microrobot capable of switching between swimming and rolling on demand. The biomimetic robot was fabricated using digital light processing (DLP) 3D printing of a poly (ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA) hydrogel mixed with acrylamide (AAm) crosslinkers and embedded magnetic nanoparticles (Fig. 1b). After UV curing, the printed millimeter-scale hydrogel robots, with a cross-sectional diameter of 8 mm and a thickness of 1.2 mm in their flat state, were magnetized under a uniform magnetic field generated between two opposing poles (N–S), during which the embedded magnetic nanoparticles aligned along the field direction. Therefore, the present device is a millimeter-scale proof-of-concept prototype rather than a true micro-scale robot, and it was used here to validate the ultrasound-guided closed-loop sensing and control architecture. This magnetization step established a well-defined net magnetic moment within the hydrogel structure, enabling magnetic actuation.

In the absence of a magnetic field, the robot remains flat due to internal elastic forces and structural symmetry. When driven by Helmholtz-coil magnetic fields, the embedded nanoparticles induce localized bending that deforms the body into locomotion-specific shapes (for the detailed process of force analysis and modeling for the hydrogel microrobot, please refer to Supplementary Note S5). Controlled waveform modulation allows seamless switching between modes: orthogonal sawtooth excitations along the x- and y-axes drive coordinated rhythmic bending for omnidirectional swimming, while sinusoidal fields with a 90° phase offset induce curling and a rolling gait suited for surface navigation and confined spaces (Fig. 1c). The locomotion speed in both modes scales monotonically with excitation frequency, providing a continuous and tunable actuation response (validated by the frequency–velocity curves in Supplementary Figs. S1–S2).

To evaluate real-time tracking performance in tissue-like environments, the hydrogel microrobot was tested in a cellulose–gelatin phantom that mimics the acoustic properties of soft tissue while maintaining high contrast to water, enabling continuous B-mode ultrasound visualization (Fig. 2a). Segmented ultrasound frames were used to construct a map comprising obstacles, free space χ, and contact-permissive boundaries—regions that the robot can touch but not traverse (Fig. 2b). Based on this map, a Rapidly-exploring Random Tree (RRT*) planner computed a collision-free route between designated start and end points. The route was positioned slightly above the contact plane, allowing the microrobot to swim through free fluid and switch to rolling near boundaries where contact is advantageous. To regulate locomotion, we modeled both swimming and rolling modes within a unified Lie-group framework, the Special Euclidean group SE(2), which compactly describes the robot’s pose [x,y,θ]. Each gait was characterized by mode-specific Lie-algebra velocities, enabling efficient switching between kinematic representations. Model Predictive Control (MPC) was employed for swimming to handle nonlinear constraints, while Proportional–Integral–Derivative (PID) control was used for rolling, where the dynamics are near-rigid. Stable switching between swimming and rolling was guaranteed through Average Dwell Time (ADT) analysis. Mode-specific Lyapunov functions were used to derive a critical dwell-time threshold, proving that the system remains globally uniformly asymptotically stable as long as switching frequencies remain below this bound. Consequently, the robot could adaptively determine when and how to switch based on real-time ultrasound feedback. In experiments, the robot rolled efficiently along contact-permissive surfaces and swam through fluid regions, transitioning smoothly between gaits. This adaptive, feedback-driven control achieves consistent trajectory tracking across diverse environments, advancing soft microrobots toward adaptive operation in complex biomedical tasks (see Supplementary Note S4 for Lyapunov analysis and the ADT-based switching condition).

Having established real-time tracking, estimation, and closed-loop path following, we next quantified the conditions under which each locomotion mode is advantageous and examined how the controller exploits both modes to navigate structured environments under ultrasound guidance. To isolate the mechanics of rolling, we first evaluated the robot on an inclined plane (Fig. 3a). Starting from the base in water, the robot ascended solely by rolling along the slope. By sweeping the magnetic-field frequency, we generated a speed–angle phase diagram (Fig. 3b), in which the green region indicates successful ascents and the red region indicates failure when the required rolling speed exceeded the controllable range at steeper slopes. As the gradient increased, a higher rolling speed was needed to sustain upward motion, but at inclinations above 30°, stable ascent was no longer achievable. Ultrasound snapshots further revealed that controlled descent remained stable once climbing was feasible, confirming the mechanical reliability and efficiency of rolling on graded surfaces. We next examined a vertical step mimicking abrupt tissue interfaces (Fig. 3c). In rolling mode (phase-offset sinusoidal excitation), the robot was unable to surmount the step; even at elevated rolling speeds, only brief and uncontrolled vertical excursions were observed. Switching the actuation waveform to sawtooth excitation triggered swimming, producing sustained upward propulsion that enabled the robot to clear the barrier. These observations highlight the complementary nature of the two modes: rolling provides speed and stability on continuous contact surfaces, whereas swimming is essential for overcoming discontinuities that are otherwise impassable (see Supplementary Fig. S5 for additional step-crossing sequences contrasting low-/high-speed rolling with swimming).

To further demonstrate the need for multimodal locomotion, we compared the two gaits in a staircase-shaped phantom and in an obstacle-free control environment (Fig. 3d). In the obstacle-rich terrain, the hydrogel robot traveled from a predefined start to an endpoint across multiple steps, while in the control setting it moved laterally in free fluid. Under closed-loop control, rolling completed the trajectory faster, with smaller velocity variance and lower average tracking error (ATE) than swimming in both scenarios. The difference was most pronounced in the presence of obstacles, where intermittent contact destabilized swimming, whereas rolling maintained consistent, planar motion. Together with the step-crossing test, these results illustrate the cooperative roles of both gaits: rolling for rapid, accurate motion on surfaces and swimming for vertical traversal and obstacle crossing. The controller’s on-the-fly switching leverages both behaviors to accomplish tasks that neither mode could achieve alone (see representative ultrasound frames for obstacle-rich and obstacle-free environments in Supplementary Fig. S6, and time-lapse sequences over staircase-shaped obstacles in Supplementary Fig. S7).

Closed-loop tracking and adaptive gait switching enable the microrobot to follow planned trajectories when obstacles are visible in ultrasound and can be segmented from the image. For future biological applications, however, some structures may be poorly resolved, preventing complete environmental mapping. To emulate this incomplete-information scenario in vitro, we implemented an exploration mode in a tissue-mimicking phantom containing a physical step-like obstacle. The obstacle was present in the workspace but its geometry and location were deliberately withheld from the controller. Before execution, the workspace was treated as fully traversable, and the controller received only the ultrasound-derived centroid position of the robot and a predefined four-sector search rule.

In this mode, the robot follows a four-sector search policy, lower → right → upper → left, to bracket hidden geometry while maintaining continuous ultrasound localization (Fig. 4a). Obstacle contact is inferred from motion stagnation: when continued actuation produces little or no displacement in the commanded direction over a short observation interval, the controller interprets this discrepancy between expected and measured motion as contact with an unseen obstacle. This event triggers a transition from rolling to swimming, with an upward velocity component for vertical lift and a lateral component for probing the obstacle height. A representative trial is shown in Fig. 4b. In Stage 1, the robot rolls toward the lower sector, but between 1.6 s and 3.6 s, its displacement nearly ceases, indicating contact with an unseen obstacle. The controller then adaptively switches to swimming mode, assigning upward and leftward velocity components (Stage 2). The upward motion provides vertical lift, while the leftward component allows the robot to probe the obstacle’s height. By approximately 9 s, sustained leftward motion confirms that the robot has bypassed the obstacle crest. Subsequently, the vertical velocity is reduced to enable contour tracking along the upper surface (Stage 3). Between 9.6 s and 10 s, the robot clears the peak and then reorients downward to reacquire the floor or terminate at the goal (Stage 4). Throughout this process, no precomputed map or trajectory is used—the robot’s decisions arise entirely from instantaneous position feedback and the search policy.

The reconstructed planar trajectory (Fig. 4c) confirms a vertical bypass followed by reentry into the workspace. For quantification, we report a distance error defined as the Euclidean deviation from the nominal path generated for a known map (used only as a reference to score exploration). Errors remain small in Stages 1-2, spike during Stage 3 as the robot deliberately scans laterally to satisfy the exploration condition, and transiently increase in Stage 4 while rejoining the global route (Fig. 4d). These results show that, coupled with ultrasound localization, the hydrogel microrobot can detect and cross unseen obstacles by adaptively switching between rolling and swimming, suggesting utility as an embedded, position-sensing agent when environmental information is incomplete. The adaptive exploration described herein does not rely on real-time inference or online learning; instead, it is governed by a predefined rule-based framework that ensures deterministic decision-making.

Conclusion

We have extended ultrasound-guided magnetic control to soft hydrogel microrobots and demonstrated that coupling high-rate imaging feedback with adaptive gait switching enables precise navigation in tissue-like media. A real-time localization framework—combining inter-frame differencing with an adaptive, correlated-noise Kalman filter—stabilizes weak echoes and yields reliable state estimation. Built on this feedback, a hierarchical controller that integrates model predictive control (MPC) for swimming and proportional–integral–derivative (PID) control for rolling executes trajectories and switches gaits under a stability-verified policy. In tissue-mimicking phantoms, the system achieves sub-4-mm tracking accuracy, ascends graded surfaces, clears vertical barriers via swimming transitions, and adaptively explores unseen obstacles without precomputed maps. Together, these results establish a closed-loop strategy that transforms the intrinsic material advantages of hydrogels—compliance, biocompatibility, and adaptability—into predictable, controllable robotic functions.

Rather than introducing a new imaging or actuation modality, this work adapts the ultrasound–magnetics pairing to the softest and most challenging class of microrobots, providing the sensing and control advances necessary to make it practical. Looking forward, future research will focus on validation in more complex and dynamic environments, in vivo testing under physiological flow conditions, volumetric ultrasound for full three-dimensional tracking, and miniaturization with integrated therapeutic payloads. Additionally, incorporating learning-based planners could enable faster, safer exploration and dynamic environment adaptation. Collectively, these developments will move hydrogel microrobots from laboratory demonstrations toward clinically relevant, adaptive biomedical interventions.

Discussion

Microrobots offer vast potential in clinical applications, as demonstrated by existing research on swarms for drug delivery and micro-object transport; yet, soft robots significantly expand this scope by enabling more delicate and complex operations. In this study, we introduce a dynamic operator in the modeling process to serve as a bridge between unobservable microscopic deformation and controllable macroscopic motion, considering the diverse environmental disturbances across physiological regimes. The proposed system is primarily designed for the treatment of the urinary tract, where the core challenge lies in managing the dynamical switching on unstructured soft interfaces. Localized flow variations and the complex topography caused by mucosal rugae directly interfere with the Effective Propulsive Factor, which acts as a functional of the deformation history. Since the deformation field undergoes significant reconfiguration when transitioning from swimming to rolling, our controller identifies these transient jumps online and adjusts magnetic gradients to compensate for the propulsion efficiency loss induced by wall-related viscous drag. To ensure stability during frequent gait transitions, we utilize the Average Dwell Time (ADT) criterion to guarantee that the system Lyapunov function remains convergent when switching between the fluid-reaction swimming model and the solid-contact rolling model, thus avoiding control oscillations on mucosal surfaces.

To advance the clinical translation of microrobotic systems, true micro-scale agents may be required for applications involving narrower anatomical spaces or more localized interventions. The present 8-mm robot should therefore be viewed as a millimeter-scale proof-of-concept prototype for validating the ultrasound-guided closed-loop control architecture. Miniaturization is feasible in principle, but it will introduce several coupled physical and imaging challenges. Reducing the robot size will decrease the magnetic moment and may reduce the actuation-to-drag ratio, requiring optimization of magnetic nanoparticle loading, magnetization strategy, and hydrogel geometry. At the same time, smaller structures may produce weaker ultrasound echoes, making acoustic contrast enhancement, such as incorporating echogenic particles or microbubble-based contrast features, important for maintaining reliable localization.

The proposed control architecture is conceptually scalable because it relies on real-time ultrasound feedback and model-based compensation rather than on a fixed robot size. However, after miniaturization, the physical parameters in the propulsion model, the Effective Propulsive Factor, the magnetic actuation conditions, and the ultrasound localization pipeline would need to be recalibrated experimentally. Thus, true micro-scale implementation represents a feasible but nontrivial future direction, rather than a direct scale-down of the current millimeter-scale prototype.

In the clinical context of the urinary tract, our robot can act as an active carrier to physically guide kidney stone fragments toward the urethra, monitor biomarkers, or perform targeted drug delivery to reduce non-target toxicity, addressing the limitations of traditional cystoscopy and irrigation therapies.

Despite the promise of this framework, several technical barriers to clinical translation within the urinary system remain that warrant further investigation. First, the shear-thinning behavior of inflammatory urine complicates the decoupling of the nonlinear relationship between the propulsive factor and deformation rate, which may lead to cumulative prediction errors during high-speed maneuvers. Second, the potential for signal loss in ultrasound shadow zones, particularly near the lateral bladder walls, could invalidate the ADT-based stability proofs due to sampling interruptions, causing the robot to drift into irrecoverable blind spots. Finally, the compliance of biological walls means that the transition in propulsion is not a discrete step but a time-dependent function due to viscoelastic tissue indentation. This may induce low-frequency drifts by violating the assumption of deterministic dynamics during switching.

Transient ultrasound signal loss may occur when the robot passes through acoustic shadow zones or regions with weak backscatter. In the current framework, such events can be handled as short missing-measurement intervals in the EKF-based estimator. When no reliable robot centroid is detected, the measurement-update step is skipped, and the state is propagated using the nominal kinematic model, the most recent magnetic actuation command, and the calibrated Effective Propulsive Factor. During this prediction-only interval, the state-estimation covariance is allowed to increase according to the process-noise model, explicitly representing the growth of positional uncertainty.

This strategy is intended only for short, transient dropouts. If the signal-loss duration or covariance exceeds a preset safety threshold, the controller should reduce or pause actuation and initiate a conservative relocalization maneuver rather than continuing open-loop navigation. Once the ultrasound signal is reacquired, the EKF measurement update corrects the predicted state through the innovation residual, after which closed-loop control can resume. Thus, the framework may bridge localized sensing gaps, but prolonged shadowing remains a limitation that will require future validation using three-dimensional ultrasound, shadow-aware path planning, and anatomically realistic phantoms.

Transitioning from static phantoms to authentic physiological environments introduces complex fluid-structure interactions that fundamentally modulate the microrobot’s propulsion. In controlled static media, the Effective Propulsive Factor (EPF) can be accurately characterized as a pre-calibrated nominal parameter. However, in vivo, dynamic physiological flows and the viscoelastic nature of mucosal interfaces generate spatiotemporally varying convective forces and state-dependent drag profiles. According to our dynamic modeling, these extreme environmental conditions induce continuous, unobservable structural deformations of the soft body. Under this physical context, the EPF is no longer a simple static constant; rather, it evolves into a nonlinear, time-varying variable governed by deformation-induced energy dissipation.

This physical evolution causes the actual state transition and input matrices to experience continuous parametric drift relative to the nominal model utilized in Model Predictive Control (MPC). Nevertheless, the proposed control architecture analytically bounds the resulting prediction error by formulating this complex structural deformation as an unmeasurable slow variable. The correlated-noise Extended Kalman Filter mathematically isolates the kinematic deviations arising from this parametric drift as bounded stochastic disturbances. Coupled with real-time ultrasound feedback to actively compensate for the degradation in propulsion efficiency, this mechanism effectively precludes kinematic divergence without requiring real-time parameter modifications.

Furthermore, to ensure system stability during frequent gait transitions across complex physiological interfaces, we implement the Average Dwell Time (ADT) criterion. This mechanism guarantees that the system’s Lyapunov function remains convergent when switching between the fluid-reaction swimming model and the solid-contact rolling model, thereby mathematically suppressing control oscillations caused by mucosal heterogeneity. While this robust disturbance rejection strategy ensures navigation stability across typical physiological regimes, relying strictly on a time-invariant nominal model may marginally compromise steady-state control optimality under extreme non-Newtonian conditions. Therefore, future research will focus on developing online system identification techniques to dynamically tune the EPF mapping via real-time, data-driven parameter estimation, aiming to achieve absolute control optimality across the full spectrum of biological hydrodynamics.

In conclusion, the proposed physics-compensated control framework provides a robust foundation for navigating complex physiological environments within the urinary tract. By addressing the fundamental coupling between soft-body deformation and environmental interaction, this work offers a versatile platform that bridges the gap between theoretical soft robotics and practical clinical interventions in urological applications.
