Annual Patient EXposure of residents in training over 20 years (APEX-trial): a retrospective analysis from a public teaching hospital
Authors: Carina Sturm Kunz, Veronika Gerhards, Florence Huong Ballmer, Andrea Gantenbein, Markus Schwendinger, Jürg-Hans Beer, Maria M Wertli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the clinical patient exposure of residents over 20 years following the statutory introduction of a maximum working week of 50 hours in 2005.DesignRetrospective analysis of longitudinal cohorts of residents who worked on the medical wards between 2001 and 2023 or the emergency department (ED) between 2007 and 2023.SettingCantonal Hospital of Baden, a representative, large Swiss teaching hospital affiliated with the University Hospital of Zurich.ParticipantsResidents in training working on the internal medical wards and the ED.Primary outcome measuresAnnual Patient Exposure (APEX) of residents, which is the ratio of annual patients to residents and describes the annual number of patients treated per resident.Methods and analysisWe assessed time trends of the APEX in annual cohorts of residents in training on the internal medicine wards (2001–2023) and the ED (2007–2023). We analysed the annual case severity (Case Mix Index and Emergency Severity Index (ESI)), patient age and the length of hospital stay (LOS) as a proxy for changes in the patient population over time and the readmission rate as a quality indicator.ResultsThe medical ward APEX even increased by 5.2% from 325 patients to 342, despite the workhour restrictions and the increased number of residents (+90%). Inpatients increased by 100% (from 3250 to 6507). Their LOS decreased from 12.4 to 7.5 days and the readmission rate decreased from 4.2% to 3.3%.In the ED, the APEX decreased by 21% (from 3261 to 2576), but the case complexity (ESI) and the LOS increased substantially.ConclusionsIn contrast to the general belief, work hour restrictions and the increased number of residents did not reduce the inpatient APEX over 20 years. The reduced APEX in the ED may be compensated by the increase in case complexity and the LOS.

Introduction

Over the past decades, the hospital workday structure of residents has changed dramatically, as the amount of clinical data and administrative workload has increased immensely. Time spent in direct patient contact ranges internationally from 9% to 27.5%, with a much larger proportion of time spent on documentation, indirect patient care, communication and organisation, up to 66%.15 In particular, in internal medicine, recent studies showed that residents spend three times more time in front of the computer than in direct patient contact6 and the most relevant patient contact occurred during the daily ward rounds.7 Besides, there is an ever-increasing complexity of hospital medicine a resident has to deal with. Patients in the medical wards are usually older and have multiple comorbidities. In addition, their illnesses are chronic and not self-limiting. Apart from finding a diagnosis and initiating the treatment pathway, multidisciplinary and time-intensive coordination tasks are also part of the patient care.8 9

In many countries, the working hours of residents are above the average for many other professions. In addition, the medical profession is not a nine to five job; it includes late shifts, night shifts and weekend duties.10 Residents are under immense stress and pressure, which can affect their mental and physical health, as well as the quality of care provision over time.11 In order to curb these negative effects, many countries have reduced the working hours of residents in recent years. However, there is still a wide range internationally. In the USA, the maximum working week was set at 80 hours in 2003, whereas in Estonia, Latvia and the Czech Republic, the maximum working week is just half that, at 40 hours per week.10

In 2005, a national law came into effect in Switzerland that limits the working hours of residents to 50 hours per week. Further, individual overtime should not exceed 2 hours per day and a cumulative total of 140 hours per year, except in emergency situations.12 Recently, the Association of Swiss Assistant and Senior Physicians (VSAO), a lobby which represents their interests, has called for a further reduction in weekly working hours.13 The impact of a reduction in working hours remains a matter of debate. Given the relevant reduction of the weekly working hours14 and increasing administrative workload,15 some experts warn that the clinical experience would diminish as the caseload per doctor would decrease substantially.1618 Although several studies assessed the impact of residents’ working hours on their health and patients safety in different countries over time,19 20 none of them has examined the clinical patients exposure relative to the caseload per resident (patients/resident/year) in internal medicine.

The main aim of our long-term study was to test the hypothesis that the average patient exposure and the experience of residents has decreased significantly over time in one large Swiss teaching hospital. Further, we also assessed the change in patient complexity, patient age and the length of stay which may contribute to the teaching value of our residents.

Materials and methods

Study design

Retrospective analysis of longitudinal cohorts of residents who worked on the medical ward between 2001 and 2023 or the emergency department (ED) between 2007 and 2023. Patients were included when they were admitted to the ED between 2007 and 2023 and/or hospitalised in the medical wards between 2001 and 2023. The study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement21 for reporting observational studies.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Setting and population under study

This study was conducted using long-term data from the Cantonal Hospital of Baden, a representative, large Swiss teaching hospital affiliated with the University Hospital of Zurich. We examined the data from the 140-bed Department of Internal Medicine and the ED, one of the largest in Switzerland, based on the patient frequencies.22 The Cantonal Hospital Baden presented a unique opportunity to study patient exposure among general internal medicine residents. While many hospitals and healthcare systems experience substantial changes over time, the Department of Internal Medicine and the ED of the Cantonal Hospital Baden remained under the same leadership (J-HB) between 2001 and 2022. Many physicians have worked and taught at the hospital for 15 years or more. Furthermore, reforms focusing on competency-based training are currently being implemented in Swiss medical schools, but not yet in training hospitals.23 Although patient complexity increased and internal medicine subspecialties developed over time, the core of residency training remained essentially unchanged. Consequently, clinical processes, the teaching environment and organisational structures remained largely consistent. Due to an increase in administrative burden, more administrative staff (mainly medical practice assistants (MPAs)) were employed to assist residents with their tasks. Furthermore, social care workers took over organisational tasks related to hospital discharge.24 Speech recognition tools have recently been implemented to increase the efficiency and timeliness of medical reports, particularly in the ED. Other quality improvement efforts have included introducing the I-PASS bundle in 2022, a standardised, structured handover scheme that improves communication during shift changes and reduces treatment errors (Illness Severity, Patient Summary, Action Items, Situation Awareness, Synthesis). It is primarily used to increase the safety and efficiency of patient handovers. Standardised handoff of patients has been shown to increase clarity and staff satisfaction while reducing the number of preventable medical errors.

The management and organisation of the ED remained unchanged during the study period. Patients suffering from internal medical conditions were seen by internal medicine residents and senior physicians. A structural adjustment took place as part of the transition in departmental leadership after 2022. With a new interdisciplinary ED, process adjustments were implemented and rolled out mainly in 2023. In the ED, we counted the actual number of residents scheduled in the work plan for each month and calculated mean and SD. We calculated the average number of residents based on the mean of regular working days, weekends and holidays. Short-term absences due to illnesses and other reasons were not considered, since it would keep the exposure in a similar range.

Data sources/measurement

Patient data originate from the annual report of Cantonal Hospital of Baden, Department of Internal Medicine and Department of Emergency. Most data we obtained were annual means. The mean annual number of residents in the ED was calculated by using the work schedule of the department. For the internal medicine wards, we used the full-time equivalents employed per year.

Patient exposure of residents over time

Our main outcome measure was the Annual Patient Exposure (APEX) of residents, which is the ratio of annual patients to residents. In the Swiss healthcare setting, in particular in public teaching hospitals, all patients are initially assessed and managed by residents. Their duties comprised, among others, obtaining the patients’ medical history, performing the clinical examination, managing patients during their stay, consulting with specialists and documenting the case in a written report. Depending on patients’ insurance level, supervision is provided by a senior physician, attending physician or the department head. On the medical ward, patient data were available between 2001 and 2023. For the ED, patient data were available between 2007 and 2023.

Patient complexity

With the introduction of the diagnosis related group system in Switzerland (SwissDRG)25 in 2012, the case severity of each hospitalised patient was calculated. Each patient case is assigned to a DRG based on the main and secondary diagnosis, procedures, age, gender, etc. Each DRG is assigned to a fixed relative weight that reflects the average treatment expenditure (costs). The Case Mix Index (CMI) is a measure of average case complexity. It is calculated as the additive sum of all relative weights (valuation ratio) divided by the additive sum of treatment cases. The CMI serves as a benchmark for comparison between hospitals and has an influence on remuneration, as more complex cases lead to higher remuneration. A CMI>1 means that, on average, the hospital treats more complex and costly cases than the national average, whereas a CMI<1 treats fewer complex cases than the national average. The CMI is used internationally as a key figure for measuring average case complexity.26 27 In this study, we used the annual CMI for patients hospitalised in the internal medicine department.

For ED patients, the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) was introduced in 2011. The ESI is used to triage ED patients based on defined criteria into five severity levels.28 The triage algorithm consists of four decision points which are followed by the triage specialist. In this way, patients with complex health problems can be distinguished from those with less complex ones and assigned to the appropriate treatment pathways. ESI level 1 indicates immediate life-saving support is required, whereas ESI 5 means no resources are necessary for stabilisation. The German version of the ESI has been shown to be reliable and valid.29

Length of stay

We compared the annual average length of stay in hospitalised patients and the average annual length of stay of patients in the ED department over the 20-year study period.

Patients age

We analysed the average patient age from the ED over a period of 13 years (2011–2023). The data regarding the average age from the medical ward were not available.

Satisfaction of residents

Annually, the Swiss Institute for Medical Education30 conducted a survey that assessed the satisfaction of residents with their current training and the work conditions. We assessed the annual global satisfaction and whether residents were satisfied with their time to perform their daily tasks. Satisfaction of both items was rated on a 0 to 6-point scale, with six points indicating very satisfied. We assessed the annual ratings for both indicators from 2011 on.

Readmission rate

As a quality indicator of medical care, we assessed the annual readmission rate of inpatients discharged from internal medicine wards. Readmissions are based on the SwissDRG definition for case-based billing and a quality indicator.31 In Switzerland, the SwissDRG inpatient acute somatic reimbursement rate was introduced in 2012, and thus, from this time point onwards, highly reliable data on readmissions are available. DRG readmission is defined as readmission for the same major diagnostic category within 18 days after hospital discharge and considered a quality indicator.

Statistical analysis

We calculated mean and SD for continuous variables, absolute numbers and percentages of total for categorical variables. We built annual cohorts of residents and patients between 2001 and 2023 for internal medicine wards and between 2007 and 2023 for ED patients separately. We calculated the annual APEX for internal medicine ward by dividing the number of inpatients in a given year by the number of residents working on the internal medicine wards. We calculated the APEX for ED by dividing the number of inpatients and outpatients presenting to the ED and the number of residents undergoing training in the ED. We used the mean annual CMI and calculated the mean annual ESI. We compared global satisfaction and work-time satisfaction in years with APEX≥400 to years with APEX<400 using the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test. Statistical analyses were conducted using the open source statistical software R.32

Missing data

Missing data are reported in online supplemental table 1. For unknown reasons, the length of hospital stay (LOS) data for 2010 on the internal medical ward were missing. For this year, we took the average of 2009 and 2011.

Results

Between 2001 and 2023, a total of 301 residents worked on the internal medical wards and 119 167 patients were hospitalised (online supplemental table 1). On the ED 165, residents worked between 2007 and 2023 and 512 413 patients underwent inpatient or outpatient treatment in the ED (online supplemental table 2).

Discussion

The main finding of our study is, in sharp contrast to our hypothesis, an increase in APEX of +5.2% for internal medicine residents working on the inpatient wards over the past 23 years. Despite the introduction of the national law to limit working hours to 50 hours per week in 2005,12 we observed a substantial increase in the APEX until 2013. Although there was an increase in the workforce by almost 100%, the APEX remained above the values observed in 2001 indicating that residents see more patients with increased complexity over a shorter hospital stay. Over the study period, the number of inpatients more than doubled, inpatient LOS decreased from initially 12.4 days to an average of 7.5 days (−40%), and case complexity increased. Despite an increase in case complexity, a reduction in the annual readmission rate on the acute medical wards was observed between 2012 and 2023. Further, residents’ global satisfaction of their residency training and work-time satisfaction was higher during years with an APEX below 400 compared with years with a higher APEX. The APEX for emergency residents decreased and the proportion of severe cases increased.

Training residents for their future professional activity is the crucial goal of a residency programme in teaching hospitals. Over the course of the past decades, the healthcare system and residency training changed substantially.33 Whereas in other countries, residency training shifted towards competency-based training, in Switzerland, reforms are under way in medical schools but have yet to be implemented in teaching hospitals.23 Residency programmes in large training hospitals need to address increasingly fragmented care and complex medical and psychosocial conditions. Studies have shown that senior residents have better patient outcomes and higher patient satisfaction scores compared with junior residents.34 The impact of work-hour restrictions on the experience gained by residents remains a matter of debate. Opinions differ on if and how reductions in residents’ working hours affect patient care, patient safety, as well as the work quality and job satisfaction of residents.3538 After work hour restrictions in Switzerland, a study in patients undergoing surgical procedures suggested a higher postsurgical complication rate,39 raising concerns about more fragmented care. While we cannot assess the direct impact of work-hour restriction on rehospitalisation rates, our analysis of readmission rates since 2012, a key indicator of quality of care, showed a decrease in readmission rates over time, suggesting that a fragmentation in care did not result in a decreased standard of care.

Burnout remains a significant concern in medicine,40 particularly among residents.41 In residents in internal medicine, 60% reported feelings of burnout and 30% depressing feelings.42 This group is especially vulnerable due to prolonged working hours, high workload and the demanding nature of caring for acutely ill patients, often in settings with limited supervision and support.41 A study conducted in Japan demonstrated a significant association between working hours and burnout among residents. Interestingly, the study also reported that these residents worked an average of 63.3 hours per week but exhibited a similar prevalence of burnout to that observed among residents in the USA, who work an average of 80 hours per week. These findings suggest that working hours alone may not fully account for burnout symptoms, and other factors are likely contributing to the development of burnout in residents.43 Our study indicates that not only may work-hour restrictions be relevant but also the case load residents need to handle. Given that many healthcare systems, including ours, have observed a substantial reduction in hospital LOS,44 45 more complex patients need to be managed in less time. The increased efficiency will lead to an increased burden for healthcare staff4648 if not addressed otherwise. Negative factors that may result in dissatisfaction and burnout include a high burden of temporal work stressors and time pressure.49 Indeed, a time motion study showed that residents switched tasks 15 times per hour.6 50 In ED residents, an independent predictor for burnout was the average number of night shifts per month.51 Organisational interventions are important to improve working conditions and reduce physician burnout.52 Further, a higher job autonomy and the work climate had a stress-buffering effect.49

In order to improve work conditions for residents, many hospitals, including ours, employed administrative staff that specifically support residents in their everyday tasks. In Switzerland, MPAs mainly trained in outpatient practices support physicians by taking over organisational, administrative and limited clinical tasks. Further, allied healthcare professionals increasingly perform tasks that have been traditionally physicians’ tasks, including discharge planning and social planning. In many countries, including the USA, England and Canada, physicians’ assistants increasingly take over clinical work. In Switzerland, the role of physician assistants has not been defined and not all hospitals employ physician assistants.53 Further, standardised communication during hand-off procedures may improve patient care. In our department, we introduced the I-PASS handoff bundle54 that consisted of communication training, standardising hand-off using a mnemonic and organisational adjustments. A survey showed an increased satisfaction of residents after the introduction of the standardised handoff and a reduction in preventable adverse events (manuscript currently in preparation). New digital options such as voice to text recordings,55 speech recognition tools56 57 and artificial intelligence (AI) based support algorithms may help to manage the administrative workload. However, the reliability and accuracy of such AI-based algorithms need to be first validated in clinical care and questions of governance need to be addressed.58

In the USA, projections suggest a shortfall of up to 40 000 primary care physicians by 2030.59 Although Switzerland is much smaller, similar trends are observed. A recent study estimated that about 2300 additional full-time physicians will be required by 2033. This growing shortage is influenced by an ageing medical workforce, limited training capacity and increasing part-time preferences among younger doctors.60 It is therefore of urgent importance to train a sufficient number of residents to address and fill these workforce gaps. Access to Swiss medical schools is currently limited by an aptitude test, the Numerus Clausus, which is under revision. Despite national programmes to expand medical education, infrastructure and postgraduate training opportunities remain limited. With the growing shortage of physicians, providing high-quality training for residents is crucial to ensure the standard of medical care. The APEX may serve as a proxy to assess patient exposure of residents over time and thus, the clinical experience a resident has over the postgraduate training. Given the ongoing reform and structural changes in our hospital, we plan to use the APEX to assess changes in patient load as a proxy for potential burn-out and dissatisfaction. To date, residents in Switzerland report their satisfaction about the quality of training by completing a questionnaire that will be published publicly. This benchmark among training facilities is important to recruit talents. Future studies should assess whether the APEX correlates with the residents’ satisfaction about training and thus help institutions to adapt before their rating of training quality decreases.

On admission to the emergency ward, patients are classified by the triage nurse into ESI levels depending on the severity of the illness and the urgency of the treatment; this five-level triage system introduced in 1998, classifies patients into five levels from ESI 1 (patient’s life is in danger) to ESI 5 (patient is stable).61 According to our study, there was an increase in disease severity, in particular with increasing proportions of ESI level 2 and 3, which indicates an increased workload, work complexity and experience of a resident.

Life expectancy of the Swiss population is steadily increasing,62 which was also observed in our study. The mean age of patients in the ED increased by 6 years from 48.4 to 54.9 years between 2011 and 2023. An ageing and multimorbid patient population results in an increased use of the healthcare system and is also more complex to treat.63 George et al investigated the impact of an ageing population on ED workload and confirmed that patients over 70 years of age take longer to treat, require more examinations and are more likely to be admitted as inpatients and they require a longer length of stay.64 Patient care is not only more complex due to the increasing options and advances in healthcare, but also because of the need for more integrated care. During the decision process, patient and family involvement as well as shared decision making are key principles in patient care.65 Residents need to be trained in skills and knowledge to improve their skills and willingness to engage in a shared decision process.66 Further, educating patients to improve their health literacy and willingness to adhere to recommended treatments is an important aspect that needs to be addressed.67

Our results contribute to a long and lively discussion on the optimal clinical education and experience and the role of the weekly working hours. Further reductions of the weekly hours are planned and are already a fact in many Western European countries. Arguments in favour of a reduction in working hours include less sleep deprivation and more quality of care,68 69 improved medical performance70 and cognitive performance and thus, emotional stability71 and less medical errors.72 However, according to a study, failed to show a reduction in surgical complication rates after the reduced working hours.73 Similarly, a systematic review showed no clear results of the reduction in working hours on the surgical experience and perceived training quality of the residents.74 Interestingly, outcome measurements in a systematic analysis found that physicians’ clinical experience was correlated with safer care,75 whereas safety is defined as one of the six dimensions of healthcare quality.76 In this regard, our APEX data provide a rationale for this often intuitive argumentation. Our data showed that despite a reduction in working hours, residents on medical wards did not lose patient contact and were exposed to an even higher number of more complex patients. Further, higher patient numbers resulted in decreased satisfaction with the overall training and work-time satisfaction. Our findings provided new data for the discussion of reducing residents’ working hours like the idea of 42+4.

Limitations

Although we were able to assess a long-term development in clinical training over 23 years of a large teaching hospital, there are several limitations that need to be discussed: First, using a single-centre design inherently limits the generalisability of the results. A particular strength of our study setting was that the residency programme was under the same leadership over more than 10 years. Thus, the training of core competencies in internal medicine remained essentially unchanged. In other hospitals, reforms and the introduction of physician assistants may result in other challenges. Of course, we cannot exclude other potential sources of bias, as structural and managerial changes in healthcare delivery during this time may have influenced the observed trends but could not be fully captured in this retrospective design. Second, not all variables were available for the entire study period, as some metrics (eg, CMI, ESI) were introduced only later in the observation window. In particular, earlier results on readmission rates were not available because the DRG system was introduced in 2012. Third, we had no measure of quality of the residency training and the APEX is only a proxy for patient exposure. Thus, the APEX cannot be a substitute for other measures of quality of training and staff satisfaction.

Summary

In contrast to the general belief and to our hypothesis, the inpatient exposure even increased over the past 20 years, despite the reduction in working hours and despite the residents doubling in number. During the same period, the APEX decreased in the ED, accompanied by a substantial increase in case complexity and LOS. APEX may be a useful quantitative parameter for the patient exposure and likely the clinical training of our residents. It has the potential of a planning tool for medical education.
