Helix-fixation atrial leadless pacing for rhythm-mediated cardiogenic shock after acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Shuhei Arai, Yuya Nakamura, Taku Asano, Toshiro Shinke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiogenic shock complicating inferior acute myocardial infarction is usually attributed to pump failure, but rhythm-mediated mechanisms may predominate when ischemia involving the sinoatrial nodal artery produces sinus node dysfunction, chronotropic incompetence, and junctional rhythm–induced atrioventricular dyssynchrony. We report a 79-year-old man who developed recurrent cardiogenic shock several hours after successful revascularization for inferior ST-elevation myocardial infarction despite only mildly reduced left ventricular systolic function. Invasive hemodynamic assessment demonstrated that increasing the temporary right ventricular pacing rate failed to improve left ventricular systolic pressure, whereas right atrial appendage pacing immediately restored hemodynamics, establishing chronotropic incompetence and loss of atrioventricular synchrony as the dominant contributors. Because durable atrial pacing was required and prolonged temporary atrial pacing was impractical, a helix-fixation atrial leadless pacemaker was implanted despite markedly adverse acute ischemic atrial electrical parameters, including blunted local electrograms, minimal current of injury, and a high initial pacing threshold. Pacing thresholds improved progressively after implantation, and hemodynamic stability was maintained without escalation to advanced mechanical circulatory support. To our knowledge, this is the first reported use of atrial leadless pacing in the acute phase of myocardial infarction.

Introduction

Cardiogenic shock (CS) complicating acute myocardial infarction (AMI) most commonly reflects primary pump failure [1], but in inferior AMI involving the right coronary artery (RCA), rhythm-mediated mechanisms can be the dominant contributors to hemodynamic collapse. Ischemia of the SA nodal artery may cause sick sinus syndrome (SSS) with chronotropic incompetence (CI); emergence of a junctional escape rhythm further abolishes AV synchrony. In selected patients without concomitant AV block, preserved AV conduction may make atrial pacing both feasible and hemodynamically effective. The hemodynamic benefit of restoring atrial contribution in right ventricular or inferior AMI has been recognized since early reports demonstrating that atrial or AV sequential pacing—unlike ventricular-only pacing—could reverse hypotension and cardiogenic shock in this setting [2]. Despite this recognized benefit, atrial pacing has been underutilized in the acute setting, largely because temporary transvenous atrial leads are mechanically less stable and vulnerable to dislodgment, carry risks of cardiac perforation and infection, and complicate nursing care during prolonged use in the intensive care unit. The recent introduction of the helix-fixation dual-chamber leadless pacemaker system (Aveir DR; Abbott Medical) now provides the first clinically available option for stable, durable, pocket- and lead-free right atrial appendage (RAA) pacing [3]. Successful atrial leadless pacemaker (aLP) implantation nonetheless depends on adequate atrial electrical substrate, and whether implantation is feasible during the acute ischemic phase of AMI—when that substrate may be profoundly impaired—has not been reported [4,5]. We describe, to our knowledge, the first such case, including the invasive hemodynamic rationale, the strategic choice of aLP over other modalities, and the implantation challenges encountered.

Case presentation

A 79-year-old man with diabetes mellitus and dyslipidemia presented with cardiogenic shock and severe bradycardia (29 bpm) in the setting of inferior ST-elevation AMI. Emergency coronary angiography revealed critical proximal RCA stenosis (99%) as the culprit lesion with advanced multivessel disease (Fig. 1A, B, C). Emergency PCI was performed under temporary transvenous RV pacing and intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) support, restoring sinus rhythm and initial hemodynamic stability. Several hours after successful revascularization, CS recurred. Rhythm monitoring demonstrated spontaneous junctional rhythm at 50 bpm, suggesting ischemic SSS involving the SA nodal artery territory of the RCA, resulting in both chronotropic incompetence and loss of AV synchrony in the absence of AV block. Echocardiography showed a mid-range left ventricular ejection fraction of 41% with inferior wall motion abnormality, and repeat angiography confirmed no re-occlusion. The discordance between only mildly reduced systolic function and profound hemodynamic collapse suggested that ischemic sinus node dysfunction—manifesting as chronotropic incompetence and junctional rhythm–induced loss of atrioventricular synchrony—was the dominant mechanism of shock. Neither atropine nor isoprenaline was administered. Atropine was avoided because of concern that its anticholinergic effect could aggravate the patient's pre-existing glaucoma, whereas isoprenaline was withheld because its peripheral vasodilatory effect could further worsen the profound hypotension. Invasive hemodynamic assessment was therefore prioritized to define the mechanism of shock and guide reliable pacing-based circulatory support (Fig. 2A and B). Even after increasing the temporary RV pacing rate, LV systolic pressure remained critically depressed at approximately 40 mmHg—indicating ineffective support, likely due in part to interventricular dyssynchrony imposed by RV pacing [6]. Repositioning the temporary lead to the RAA for atrial pacing immediately restored LV systolic pressure to approximately 80 mmHg, establishing chronotropic incompetence and junctional rhythm-induced AV dyssynchrony as the dominant contributors to shock. Given the need for durable atrial pacing and the limitations of prolonged temporary transvenous atrial pacing, implantation of a helix-fixation aLP was undertaken. Pre-mapping of the RAA, however, revealed markedly adverse atrial electrical substrate (Fig. 3A and B): multiple candidate sites demonstrated elevated pacing thresholds with frequent loss of capture, consistent with acute ischemic atrial injury. Stable capture was obtained only at the lateral RAA, where the local electrogram amplitude was 0.5 mV, current of injury was <1.0 mV, and pacing threshold was 5.0 V/0.4 ms—clearly suboptimal by standard criteria (Fig. 3C) [4]. With no superior site available, the aLP was implanted at this location given the clinical imperative for hemodynamic rescue. Pacing threshold improved progressively after implantation, from 3.5 V/1.5 ms at procedure end to 1.0 V/1.5 ms (1.75 V/0.4 ms) by day 3, supporting early partial recovery of the acute ischemic atrial electrical dysfunction. Stable aLP position was confirmed fluoroscopically (Fig. 4A and B). With IABP support held constant across pacing configurations, native arterial systolic pressure under atrial leadless pacing recovered to >120 mmHg (Fig. 4C), indicating that the hemodynamic gain was attributable to restoration of AV synchrony rather than to mechanical augmentation. Hemodynamic stability was maintained throughout hospitalization without escalation to advanced MCS. An acceptable intrinsic sinus rate recovered on hospital day 12, underscoring the prolonged duration of chronotropic incompetence and junctional rhythm during the acute phase. After stabilization of the cardiac condition, the patient was transferred to a regional rehabilitation hospital because of severe deconditioning. Discussion

This is, to our knowledge, the first reported case of aLP implantation in the acute phase of AMI. Four key observations merit discussion. First, accurate mechanistic characterization of shock is essential. In inferior AMI with RCA involvement, ischemia of the SA and AV nodal arteries frequently causes bradyarrhythmias; junctional escape rhythm in particular abolishes AV synchrony, compounding the hemodynamic burden beyond that of rate reduction alone [6]. The loss of AV synchrony can reduce cardiac output substantially in the setting of impaired myocardial compliance, an effect that RV pacing cannot overcome and may in fact worsen through interventricular dyssynchrony. As demonstrated invasively in this case, LV systolic pressure failed to recover with increased RV pacing rates but doubled immediately upon restoration of atrial pacing—consistent with rhythm-mediated rather than pump-failure CS. This pattern aligns with classical observations by Topol et al. and Love et al., who demonstrated that atrial or AV sequential pacing—not ventricular pacing—reverses hypotension and cardiogenic shock in right ventricular infarction and inferior AMI [2,7], and with earlier reports by Chamberlain et al. showing hemodynamic superiority of sequential AV pacing over ventricular pacing in AMI-associated heart block [8]. Escalation to vasopressors or advanced MCS addresses neither chronotropic incompetence nor junctional rhythm-induced AV dyssynchrony, underscoring the importance of invasive hemodynamic characterization to direct appropriate therapy. Second, the prolonged 12-day duration of chronotropic incompetence in this case warrants mechanistic consideration. Although early reperfusion would be expected to reverse ischemic SA nodal dysfunction, recovery was notably delayed. Beyond direct SA nodal ischemia and reperfusion-related stunning, enhanced vagal tone via the Bezold-Jarisch reflex—preferentially activated by inferoposterior ischemia through C-fiber cardiac afferents—may suppress SA and AV nodal function beyond the acute infarct window [9]. This combined mechanism of direct nodal injury and persistent autonomic dysregulation provides a plausible explanation for the protracted course observed, and helps define the clinical duration over which durable, stable atrial pacing must be maintained. Third, this therapeutic strategy was made feasible by the recent availability of the helix-fixation aLP. A semipermanent pacing strategy using an active-fixation transvenous atrial lead connected to an externalized pulse generator would have been a reasonable and potentially more cost-effective bridge for a bradyarrhythmia expected to be reversible. However, this approach would still require transvenous and externalized hardware and would therefore retain risks of infection, cardiac perforation, lead dislodgment, restricted mobility, and increased nursing complexity during prolonged critical care. In the present patient, the duration of atrial pacing requirement was uncertain, and stable atrial support was required during ongoing hemodynamic instability and IABP treatment. These considerations favored a fully intracardiac leadless strategy. The single-component tine-fixation leadless pacemaker with accelerometer-based atrial tracking was not a suitable alternative because it provides only VDD-mode ventricular pacing synchronized to mechanically sensed atrial contractions; it can address AV block with preserved sinus rhythm but cannot pace the atrium and therefore cannot treat SSS with chronotropic incompetence [10]. The aLP thus occupies a distinct therapeutic niche by providing stable, pocket-free, active-fixation atrial pacing. Its theoretical retrievability may offer an additional advantage when the long-term need for permanent pacing remains uncertain. Fourth, implantation in the acute ischemic phase presents substrate-specific challenges. The atrial electrical parameters encountered (threshold 5.0 V/0.4 ms, electrogram 0.5 mV, current of injury <1.0 mV) were clearly suboptimal [4], and reflect the acute ischemic milieu of the peri-infarct atrium. Nonetheless, progressive threshold improvement to 1.0 V/1.5 ms by day 3 supports early partial recovery of the acute ischemic atrial electrical dysfunction and the biological plausibility of proceeding despite adverse initial parameters. Recovery of an acceptable intrinsic sinus rate by hospital day 12 further suggests improvement in sinus node function during the index hospitalization. However, because longitudinal device interrogation was not available after transfer, the subsequent atrial pacing requirement and the extent of late sinus node recovery remain uncertain. When aLP implantation is performed in this setting, programming output at a substantial safety margin and close post-implant surveillance of pacing parameters are warranted during the recovery period.

Conclusion

This case suggests that helix-fixation aLP implantation may be feasible during the acute ischemic phase of AMI despite markedly adverse atrial electrical substrate, and that acute ischemic atrial electrical dysfunction may show early partial recovery after reperfusion. In selected patients with inferior AMI and cardiogenic shock in whom junctional rhythm causes chronotropic incompetence and AV dyssynchrony without concomitant AV block, invasive hemodynamic characterization may help identify a rhythm-mediated etiology amenable to atrial pacing. The recent availability of the helix-fixation aLP may provide a new therapeutic option for durable hemodynamic rescue in this setting. Further experience is needed to define the role of this strategy.
