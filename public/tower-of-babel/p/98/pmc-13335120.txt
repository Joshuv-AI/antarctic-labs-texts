Anastomosing hemangioma (AH) is a rare benign vascular neoplasm first described by Montgomery and Epstein in 2009 in the genitourinary tract, particularly the kidney.1 Histologically, AH is characterized by tightly packed, capillary-sized vascular channels arranged in an anastomosing or sinusoidal pattern and lined by hobnail endothelial cells.

Although these features may raise concern for malignancy, AH lacks endothelial multilayering and significant mitotic activity. Importantly, AH can closely mimic well-differentiated angiosarcoma, making accurate recognition essential to avoid overtreatment.1,2 Since its initial description, AH has been reported in a wide range of anatomical sites, including the kidney, testis, adrenal gland, ovary, liver, gastrointestinal tract, and paraspinal soft tissues.2–8 Notably, John and Folpe reported a series of 17 soft tissue AH cases, of which 13 (76%) arose near the vertebral column, demonstrating a striking predilection for the paraspinal region.2 More recently, Fujita et al. described the first case of AH in the spinal epidural space.9

AH was subsequently incorporated into the 2020 WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumors, 5th edition, as a newly recognized benign vascular neoplasm.10,11 Immunohistochemically, AH typically shows positivity for CD31, CD34, and ETS-related gene (ERG).10,11 Molecular studies have identified recurrent activating mutations in GNAQ, GNA14, and GNA11 in 70%–91% of cases, supporting its distinction from angiosarcoma.12,13

Despite increasing recognition, primary intraosseous AH remains exceedingly rare. Prior reports have described vertebral involvement with focal osseous extension,2 and one intraosseous spinal case has been identified in a molecular study without detailed clinical characterization.13 To our knowledge, no prior cases of primary intraosseous AH specifically involving the sacrum have been reported.

We present such a case, initially suspected to represent a malignant sacral tumor, and provide a comprehensive clinical description including preoperative imaging, embolization, surgical management with spinopelvic reconstruction, and histopathological findings, along with a review of the relevant literature and discussion of postoperative surveillance.

Illustrative Case

A 79-year-old woman presented with a 6-month history of progressive left-sided sacral pain, which was particularly severe when lying supine or turning in bed. She denied bowel or bladder dysfunction, lower extremity weakness, or sensory disturbance. Her past medical history was unremarkable, with no prior malignancy or radiation exposure. Physical examination revealed tenderness over the left sacral region. Neurological examination of the lower extremities was normal.

MRI of the lumbosacral spine demonstrated a large mass measuring 60.2 × 48.5 × 39.8 mm (width × height × anteroposterior) with osteolytic changes and focal cortical thinning with limited soft tissue extension involving the midline and left sacral ala at the level of the sacralized L5 vertebra. The lesion was hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging, hypointense on T1-weighted imaging, and showed heterogeneous enhancement after gadolinium administration, consistent with a hypervascular neoplasm (Fig. 1A– D). No similar abnormality had been identified on imaging performed 11 years previously. CT further delineated bony involvement with an osteolytic lesion causing cortical thinning and partial destruction of the sacral body and left sacral ala (Fig. 1E–H). 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/CT demonstrated increased FDG uptake (maximum standardized uptake value [SUVmax] 3.8) in the osteolytic lesion involving the L5 vertebra and left sacral ala, suggesting a low-grade neoplasm, although malignant tumors remained in the differential diagnosis (Fig. 2A–C). Whole-body bone scintigraphy showed increased bone remodeling at the L5 vertebra and sacrum corresponding to the known tumor site (Fig. 2D). For initial staging, enhanced CT of the neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvis was performed, which revealed no evidence of distant metastasis, pathological lymphadenopathy, or other primary malignancy. Fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous biopsy was attempted but was aborted prematurely due to profuse intralesional hemorrhage, which precluded adequate tissue sampling. No procedure-related complications occurred. Given the hypervascular nature of the lesion, preoperative transarterial embolization was performed the day before surgery. Via right common femoral artery access, selective angiography of the left internal iliac artery revealed tumor supply from the lateral sacral artery originating from the left superior gluteal artery, which was embolized using polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles (350–500 μm) (Fig. 3A). An aortogram subsequently demonstrated additional tumor feeding from the left L5 lumbar artery; this vessel was selectively catheterized and the distal segment was occluded with a 2-mm coil, followed by PVA particle embolization. Final angiography confirmed complete devascularization with no residual tumor staining (Fig. 3B). FIG. 3.Preoperative angiography, embolization, and intraoperative findings. Tumor resection was performed via a posterior approach under continuous intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, including somatosensory evoked potentials and electromyography. After adequate exposure and decompression, the lesion was resected under microscopic visualization using an ultrasonic aspirator (Sonopet, Stryker) and a high-speed drill. The mass was reddish, friable, and highly vascular, although notably less hemorrhagic than anticipated, reflecting the effectiveness of preoperative embolization and allowing safe and controlled resection. The intraosseous component extended to the left superior articular process and sacral endplate of the sacralized L5 vertebra (transitional lumbosacral anatomy), with focal cortical involvement without cortical breakthrough. Laterally, the lesion partially involved the left sacroiliac joint, and inferior extension reached the S1–2 level, consistent with preoperative imaging findings. Along the S1 nerve root trajectory, tumor tissue with indistinct margins encased the root; this was meticulously dissected and completely removed while preserving the S1 nerve root. At the ventral aspect of the resection cavity, the posterior longitudinal ligament was intact; however, focal tumor adhesion was noted on its ventral surface (Fig. 3C), which was carefully excised in its entirety. Gross-total resection (GTR) was achieved. The resultant large bony defect was reconstructed using a fibular strut allograft for structural support and beta-tricalcium phosphate as an osteoconductive scaffold. A titanium mesh plate was applied to reinforce the posterior sacral wall defect. Spinal stabilization was achieved with pedicle screw fixation from L3–4 to S1 (sacralized L5), with extension to bilateral iliac screws for spinopelvic reconstruction. The total operative time was 360 minutes. Estimated blood loss was 300 mL, and the patient received 1 unit of packed red blood cells and 2 units of fresh-frozen plasma intraoperatively.

Postoperatively, the patient developed transient paresthesia of the left anterior thigh, which resolved within a few weeks. No motor weakness or sphincter dysfunction occurred. MRI obtained on postoperative day 2 confirmed GTR without residual enhancing tumor, hematoma, or other complications (Fig. 4A–D). Postoperative radiographs demonstrated appropriate alignment and positioning of the spinopelvic fixation construct (Fig. 4E and F). The patient remains under clinical follow-up, with no clinical evidence of recurrence at 10 months postoperatively. A 12-month follow-up MRI is scheduled for radiological surveillance. Microscopically, the tumor was composed of capillary-sized vascular channels with a characteristic anastomosing architecture. These channels were lined by a single layer of endothelial cells with focal hobnail features. The endothelial cells exhibited mild nuclear atypia without significant mitotic activity. Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells were positive for CD31 and CD34, findings consistent with the diagnosis of AH (Fig. 5). ERG immunostaining, another endothelial marker frequently reported in AH, was not performed as it is not part of the routine immunohistochemical panel at our institution; however, the combined positivity of CD31 and CD34 was considered sufficient for diagnosis. No features suggestive of malignancy, such as multilayering, necrosis, or increased mitotic activity, were identified. Although AH most commonly arises in the kidney and paraspinal soft tissues, rare cases involving vertebral bone have been reported, supporting the diagnosis despite this unusual intraosseous presentation. Discussion

AH is a relatively recently recognized benign vascular neoplasm that has been increasingly reported since its initial description in 2009.1 In the 2020 WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumors, 5th edition, AH was recognized as a distinct benign vascular entity, reflecting its established clinicopathological significance.10,11 Histologically, AH is characterized by a complex network of capillary-sized anastomosing vascular channels lined by hobnail endothelial cells, with scattered fibrin thrombi. Additional features may include extramedullary hematopoiesis and adipocytic metaplasia.1,2,10 The tumor lacks significant mitotic activity, endothelial multilayering, and necrosis—features distinguishing it from angiosarcoma.2,14 Despite this histological resemblance, AH consistently follows a benign clinical course.

The anatomical distribution of AH has expanded considerably since Montgomery and Epstein’s original report of 6 genitourinary cases.1 The kidney remains the most commonly affected organ, particularly in the setting of end-stage renal disease.3,15,16 AH has also been reported in a wide range of extra-genitourinary sites, including the adrenal gland,4,17 ovary,5 liver and gastrointestinal tract,6,18 testis,7 spermatic cord, larynx, left atrium, and breast.8,14,19 Of particular relevance to the present case, John and Folpe demonstrated that 76% of their 17 nongenitourinary AH cases arose in or near the vertebral column, including the paravertebral, psoas, costovertebral, para-aortic, paracaval, parasacral, and retroperitoneal regions.2 One case in their series showed focal extension into the adjacent vertebral body, suggesting that osseous involvement, while uncommon, can occur.2 Fujita et al. subsequently reported the first AH arising in the spinal epidural space at the thoracic level, presenting with cord compression and horn-like projections on axial MRI.9

Although rare, osseous involvement by AH has been documented. John and Folpe reported 2 cases involving vertebral locations and 1 case with focal extension into the adjacent vertebral body among their 17 soft tissue cases, with 3 cases showing focal bone erosion.2 Bean et al. explicitly described 1 case as intraosseous spinal AH in their molecular study of 13 cases, although no detailed clinical, radiological, or surgical description was provided.13 More recently, primary intraosseous AH of the skull has also been reported (Supplemental Table 1), further supporting that osseous involvement represents an underrecognized manifestation of AH. The present case is notable because it represents, to our knowledge, the first primary intraosseous AH specifically involving the sacrum and the first comprehensively documented case of osseous spinal AH including detailed radiological, endovascular, surgical, and histopathological findings. Unlike previously reported parasacral soft tissue or epidural cases, the lesion in our patient originated within the sacral bone itself, with focal cortical involvement without cortical breakthrough and limited soft tissue extension.

This case expands the recognized anatomical spectrum of AH and highlights a potential diagnostic pitfall, as such lesions may closely mimic aggressive sacral tumors including chordoma, giant cell tumor, or metastatic disease. Furthermore, it underscores important surgical considerations in the management of hypervascular sacral tumors, particularly the role of preoperative embolization in facilitating safe and controlled resection.

Observations

Differential Diagnosis

The radiological appearance of sacral AH poses a significant diagnostic challenge. In our case, the T2-hyperintense, heterogeneously enhancing, hypervascular mass with increased FDG uptake (SUVmax 3.8) on PET/CT was initially considered to represent chordoma, giant cell tumor, metastatic carcinoma, or primary bone sarcoma. PET/CT suggested a low-grade tumor; however, malignancy remained in the differential diagnosis, contributing to the preoperative suspicion.

This diagnostic difficulty is consistent with previous reports. John and Folpe noted that imaging studies of soft tissue AH were not considered diagnostic of hemangioma by musculoskeletal radiologists in 11 of 13 cases, and the radiographic differential diagnosis frequently included sarcoma, peripheral nerve sheath tumor, and lymphoma.2 Shanbhogue et al. also emphasized the variable and often nonspecific imaging features of AH across multiple organ systems.20 Notably, PET/CT findings in AH have been rarely described in the literature. The moderate FDG uptake (SUVmax 3.8) observed in our case likely reflects the metabolic activity of proliferating endothelial cells and inflammatory infiltrates within the vascular channels, a phenomenon well recognized in benign vascular tumors. This highlights the potential for AH to demonstrate metabolic activity that may be misinterpreted as suggesting malignancy.

Histologically, the primary differential diagnosis is angiosarcoma, given the overlapping anastomosing vascular architecture.1,2,14 As emphasized in prior studies and the WHO classification, the characteristic vascular anastomosing pattern of AH must be carefully distinguished from angiosarcoma. AH lacks the hallmarks of malignancy: features such as significant cytological atypia, endothelial multilayering, brisk mitotic activity, and necrosis are absent.1,2,10 Additional histological clues favoring AH include the presence of adipocytic metaplasia (53% of cases), extramedullary hematopoiesis (41%), and small fibrin thrombi.2 Molecularly, recurrent activating mutations in the Gq family of G-protein alpha subunits (GNAQ, GNA14, GNA11) have been identified in 70%–91% of AH cases.12,13 These mutations have not been reported in angiosarcomas, which instead harbor mutations in PTPRB and PLCG1, providing a molecular basis for their distinction.12,13

Other vascular neoplasms in the differential include cavernous hemangioma, sinusoidal hemangioma, retiform hemangioendothelioma, and hobnail hemangioma.2,14 Cavernous hemangioma of the epidural space may show similar radiological features to AH but differs histologically, with larger gaping vessels and lobulated architecture.9,21–23

It is important to distinguish AH from conventional vertebral hemangioma, a common incidental finding with a prevalence of 10%–12% in the general population.24 Unlike AH, this entity represents a vascular malformation or hamartomatous lesion composed of cavernous, thin-walled vascular channels interspersed with adipose tissue and thickened bony trabeculae.24 On imaging, it characteristically demonstrates high signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted MRI sequences (due to its high fat content) and a pathognomonic polka-dot pattern on axial CT or corduroy pattern on sagittal CT, reflecting the vertically oriented thickened trabeculae.24 These features were entirely absent in our case, which instead showed T1 hypointensity, heterogeneous T2 hyperintensity, avid gadolinium enhancement, and osteolytic bone destruction—findings more suggestive of an aggressive neoplasm. Histologically, the distinction is also clear: conventional vertebral hemangioma consists of large cavernous vascular spaces, whereas AH is composed of anastomosing capillary-sized vessels lined by hobnail endothelial cells with fibrin thrombi.1,2,10,24 Furthermore, conventional vertebral hemangioma lacks the recurrent GNAQ, GNA14, and GNA11 mutations that characterize AH as a clonal vascular neoplasm.12,13 The 2020 WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumors recognizes AH as a distinct benign vascular neoplasm, whereas conventional vertebral hemangioma is not classified as a neoplastic entity.10 Clinicians should therefore recognize that, despite sharing the term “hemangioma,” these entities differ fundamentally in their pathogenesis, imaging characteristics, histology, and clinical behavior, and misinterpretation may lead to inappropriate management.

Role of Preoperative Embolization

The profound hypervascularity of the lesion in our case, as evidenced by the aborted percutaneous biopsy due to profuse bleeding, underscores the importance of preoperative planning for suspected hypervascular sacral tumors. Preoperative transarterial embolization is widely accepted as an effective strategy to reduce intraoperative blood loss and improve surgical resectability for hypervascular spinal and sacral tumors.25,26 In a series of 60 patients with sacral tumors, complete embolization of tumor-feeding arteries was achieved in all cases, and no procedure-related symptomatic complications were reported.26 Similarly, a larger series of spinal tumors demonstrated that complete or near-complete embolization was achievable in more than 98% of cases.25 In our case, embolization of the lateral sacral artery and L5 lumbar artery feeders resulted in a markedly less hemorrhagic surgical field than anticipated, enabling GTR without intraoperative hemodynamic instability. These findings support the routine consideration of preoperative embolization for suspected hypervascular sacral lesions, particularly when a vascular tumor such as AH is included in the differential diagnosis.

Surgical Considerations and Prognosis

AH is consistently associated with benign biological behavior and an excellent prognosis. In the original genitourinary series by Montgomery and Epstein, no recurrences were observed following excision.1 Subsequent studies, including the series by John and Folpe and the spinal epidural case reported by Fujita et al., have similarly demonstrated no local recurrence or metastasis after complete resection.2,9 A comprehensive review by Lappa and Drakos confirmed the benign nature of AH and emphasized that accurate diagnosis could prevent unnecessary overtreatment.14 Given this favorable prognosis, excision is generally considered the treatment of choice when symptomatic or when the diagnosis is uncertain. Adjuvant radiation therapy or chemotherapy is not required.2,9

In our case, GTR was achieved with spinopelvic fixation and reconstruction. The decision for aggressive surgical intervention was driven by the strong preoperative suspicion of malignancy and the patient’s progressive, debilitating pain. The patient’s postoperative course was favorable, with resolution of pain and only transient sensory deficit, consistent with the expected benign clinical course of AH.

Although no formal surveillance guidelines exist for AH, the published literature uniformly supports a benign course with no documented recurrences after complete resection.1,2,9,10 We recommend postoperative MRI surveillance at 12 and 24 months following complete resection; if no recurrence is identified, further imaging may be unnecessary given the consistently benign behavior of this entity. For cases with subtotal resection or atypical histological features such as increased mitotic activity or cytological atypia, more frequent imaging (e.g., every 6 months) would be prudent, although such features are rare. Radiation therapy has not been described in the management of AH, and given the characteristically low Ki-67 index and absent mitotic activity, adjuvant radiotherapy does not appear warranted even in cases with atypical features.

Lessons

This report has several limitations inherent to a single case study. The follow-up period is relatively short, although existing literature supports the benign nature of AH with no documented malignant transformation or metastasis. Molecular analysis (GNAQ, GNA14, and GNA11 mutation testing) was not performed due to limited availability and cost constraints at our institution, which could have provided additional diagnostic confirmation; however, the characteristic histopathological features and immunophenotype were considered sufficient for a definitive diagnosis. Given the rarity of this entity, management strategies remain individualized.

We report a case of primary intraosseous AH of the sacrum, the first comprehensively documented case in this location. This rare benign vascular tumor can closely mimic aggressive sacral neoplasms, posing a significant diagnostic challenge. Accurate histopathological diagnosis is essential to avoid overtreatment, and AH should be included in the differential diagnosis of hypervascular sacral lesions. Preoperative transarterial embolization may facilitate safer resection by reducing intraoperative bleeding, and complete excision is curative without the need for adjuvant therapy.

This case expands the recognized anatomical spectrum of AH and highlights the importance of careful radiological-pathological correlation in the evaluation of hypervascular sacral tumors.
