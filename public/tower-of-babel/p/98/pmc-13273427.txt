Discovery of novel InhA inhibitors through structural bioinformatics and machine learning-driven QSAR screening of natural products
Authors: Godswill Imolele Anthony, Samuel Nzube Nwosu, Adedoyin John-Joy Owolade, Temitope Olubanjo Kehinde, Richard Gamah Teye, Samuel Eneojo Akor, Favour Segun Fabuyi, Ifeoma Roseline Nwafor, Precious Kelechi Oleh, Toluwalope Daniel Ilori, Damilola Samuel Bodun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTuberculosis (TB) persists as a formidable global health challenge, particularly due to the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) variants that limit the effectiveness of existing therapies. These variants limit therapeutic options, prolong treatment duration and increase the risk of treatment failure. Additionally, the antitubercular drug discovery remains relatively limited; moreover, the rise in drug-resistant strains necessitates the need to identify and develop novel drug candidates that can overcome these resistance patterns. This study aims to design novel InhA inhibitors that can bind to and inhibit InhA and circumvent the resistance pathway in the KatG enzyme of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. We sourced an extensive library of 276 518 natural products from the LOTUS database and screened the compounds through a series of rigorous computational approaches such as drug-likeness filtration, molecular docking, MM-GBSA analysis, and ADMET prediction. We developed a machine learning model using a Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN). The MPNN was trained to predict bioactivity profiles of 31 597 natural products against the InhA enzyme. Molecular dynamics simulations further confirmed the stability of these interactions over 100-nanoseconds. Out of the screened compounds, four novel drug candidates exhibited strong binding affinities with binding energies of −12.204 kcal/mol, −11.926 kcal/mol, −11.624 kcal/mol, and −11.548 kcal/mol, respectively, surpassing the co-crystallized ligand (−8.895 kcal/mol), and the standard drug, Isoniazid (−12.204 kcal/mol). The top hit molecules demonstrated high and considerable structural stability during molecular dynamics simulations. Additionally, the pharmacokinetic profile of LTS0161715 exhibited low toxicity. positioning LTS0161715 for further investigation. These research findings elucidate the potential of direct InhA inhibitors, particularly LTS0161715, as a promising drug candidate for anti-tubercular drug development, while also highlighting the need for further optimization to enhance safety and efficacy.Keywords: tuberculosis, multidrug-resistant, InhA inhibitors, machine learning, molecular docking, QSAR, natural products

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) continues to pose a significant global health burden, compounded by the rising prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains [1]. Despite decades of treatment advances, the disease continues to persist as a major global threat, with approximately 8.2 million individuals diagnosed with TB yearly, and reported death cases of over 1.25 million globally [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), over 10.6 million people have been diagnosed with tuberculosis, equivalent to 133 incident cases in a 100, 000 population. South-East Asia, with a 46% prevalence rate, appears to be the most common region among these many instances, with Africa following closely after at 23%. Other WHO regions include the Western Pacific (18%), the Eastern Mediterranean (8.1%), the Americas (3.1%) and Europe (2.2%) [3].

Numerous efforts have been deployed to significantly reduce the incidence of TB by 1.9% globally through the use of anti-TB medications such as Rifampicin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, and Ethambutol [4]. However, the ongoing emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains has hampered progress and prevented the WHO from reaching its 4%–5% reduction target [5]. This is because long-term use of these medications has led to low patient compliance and, consequently, the establishment of strains that are more resistant to the anti-TB drugs [6]. Numerous investigations have been carried out to comprehend the processes underlying drug resistance in Mycobacterium TB [7]. These studies demonstrated that drug resistance in Mycobacterium TB results from recurrent mutations in several genes that encode for resistance rather than from a single homogenous genetic unit. Additionally, its resistance to anti-TB drugs is significantly increased by its slow metabolism during a protracted latent state [7–9].

M. tuberculosis has evolved distinct metabolic regulatory mechanisms of growth and replication, including cholesterol and fatty acid metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, nucleotide biosynthesis, and mycolic acid synthesis [10]. Among them, the FAS-II metabolic pathway produces fatty acids for the cell wall, with the InhA enzyme being a key target.

InhA is a 2-trans enoyl-ACP reductase that reduces the double bond in the elongated fatty acid chain, making it saturated. InhA, the enoyl-ACP reductase in Mycobacterium tuberculosis, is a therapeutic target for the design of novel drugs against tuberculosis. Different classes of direct InhA inhibitors have been discovered and reported in scholarly articles, demonstrating varying degrees of potency and selectivity. For example, N-benzyl-4-((heteroaryl)methyl) benzamide derivatives have demonstrated remarkable inhibitory activity, with reported IC50 values in the range of 0.04–0.05 μM, indicating strong enzyme affinity [11]. Similarly, 4-hydroxy-2-pyridone derivatives have shown potent inhibition, with an IC50 value of 4-hydroxy-6-isobutyl-3-phenylpyridin-2(1H)-one, demonstrating moderate activity against Mtb H37Rv (MIC50 1.5 μM) [12]. The representative chemical structures and their reported activity values are shown in Fig. 1. In 2018, another new family of InhA inhibitors was announced, and AN12855 was the lead drug in this class. With a reported IC50 value of 0.03 μM (Fig. 1), AN12855 demonstrated potent bactericidal action against strains of drug-resistant M. tuberculosis [13]. High-throughput screening also found new inhibitors, such as Genz-10850 (GEQ), which had an IC50 of 0.16 μM (Fig. 1). This led to the creation of derivatives that had InhA inhibitory action in the nanomolar range [13]. Moreover, Isoniazid is one of the most effective first-line antituberculosis drugs, demonstrating a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 0.01–0.2 μg/ml (0.0729–1.458 μM) [14]. The drug isoniazid (INH) is activated by KatG and inhibits InhA, preventing mycolic acid synthesis and causing cell death [15]. However, resistance to frontline drugs like isoniazid, which targets InhA through its activated form, has limited its effectiveness. The growth of INH resistance, primarily due to mutations in KatG, has led to the development of novel direct inhibitors of InhA. However, none of them has received approval for clinical usage [16]. Therefore, the development of novel direct inhibitors that can circumvent INH resistance is necessary. Through a structure-based drug design approach, novel direct inhibitors that bypass resistance mechanisms linked to prodrug activation can be discovered. This study aims to help develop next-generation antitubercular drugs that are more effective against drug-resistant and drug-susceptible strains by specifically targeting InhA.

Methodology

Materials and procedure

The materials and procedures used in this study are summarized in Fig. 2.

The workflow of this study.

Virtual screening of target library data set

The LOTUS database (https://lotus.naturalproducts.net/), which contains 276 518 natural products, was used to identify potential lead compounds. The screening process involved several steps. First, the compound data from the database was imported as a CSV file into a Jupyter notebook (see Supplementary File) and converted into the RDKit Mol format. Next, molecular descriptors, the Quantitative Estimate of Drug Likeness (QED), and the Synthetic Accessibility Score (SAScore) were calculated for all compounds using the RDKit library. Compounds with QED values of ≥ 0.6 and SAScore of ≤ 4 were selected, resulting in a final set of 31 597 compounds.

Generation of InhA inhibitors from CHEMBL

The CHEMBL database provided the experimental data on InhA inhibitor activity (n = 21 000). Using the 5VRN FASTA sequence of the target protein that was obtained from the PDB, the antagonists were found in the CHEMBL database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/). Following this, the inhibitor list was preprocessed to include only the compound’s pIC50 value, SMILES, and CHEMBL ID in a CSV file [17].

Virtual screening using machine learning model

We developed a machine learning (ML) model for the InhA inhibitor list (n = 21 000) obtained from the CHEMBL database using ChemProp feature, a deep learning tool which uses Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN); a type of graph neural network that processes molecular graphs (atoms and bonds) by aggregating local neighbor features into learned representation. Initially, the compounds were cleaned and preprocessed, then categorized as active or inactive based on their pChEMBL values. Compounds with pChEMBL ≥ 6.5 were classified as active, while those below this threshold were labeled inactive [18]. This resulted in an imbalanced dataset with 77 inactive and 46 active compounds. To address this imbalance, the minority class (active) was oversampled to 77 using the resample function from the sklearn library. The balanced dataset was then split into training, testing, and validation sets in an 80:10:10 ratio. The model was trained for 100 epochs. Model convergence was monitored using epoch–loss plots to ensure adequate training and prevent overfitting. In addition, the applicability domain of the model was evaluated using Tanimoto similarity to the training dataset, with a similarity threshold of 0.5 used to define the model’s chemical space.

After training, it was used to predict the activity of the filtered library of compounds (n = 31 597). Classification metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and specificity were assessed to evaluate the ML model’s performance. The ML model classified the dataset into 21 043 strong inhibitors and 10 554 weak inhibitors of InhA. The 21 043 predicted strong inhibitors were moved to a CSV file and considered for the next stage of virtual screening (molecular docking study).

Molecular docking study

Ligand preparation

The predicted 21 043 strong inhibitors of InhA were prepared using the LigPrep feature in the Schrodinger software tool, employing the OPLS3 force field. The purpose of ligand preparation is to ensure that the ligand’s structure accurately reflects its physical and chemical properties, which is crucial for predicting the binding mode and affinity with a target protein [19]. Using the LigPrep feature, the ligand’s 2D structures were transformed into their 3D structures, and correct atom types and partial charges were assigned to the atoms [17]. Stereoisomer computation was restricted to one isomer per ligand, and tautomer formation was not included. At physiological pH (i.e. pH 7.4), the ligands were either deprotonated or protonated to conform to their predicted condition in a biological setting. The maestro output format was used to format the results. Additionally, the co-crystallized ligand and the standard drug, isoniazid, were prepared and used as a reference for a comparison study [20].

Protein preparation

The target protein (5VRN) was retrieved from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/). The Protein Data Bank (PDB) provides access to a wide range of experimentally determined three-dimensional (3D) structures of biological macromolecules such as proteins, RNA, and DNA [21]. We utilized the “protein preparation wizard” feature of the Schrodinger software tool to prepare the protein for the docking investigation. This feature provides the display hydrogen polar only option; selecting this option will display just the polar hydrogen atoms [21]. Following its download in PDB (PDB ID: 5VRN) format, we imported the protein into the software’s workspace [22]. The target protein was then pre-processed, with the necessary atom types and charges added, as well as side chains and missing loops filled in. Water molecules were then removed during the chain refining process. Protein optimization was achieved by using PROPKA pH 7.5, followed by protein reduction and water removal beyond a 5 Å radius (Fig. 2) [17, 21].

Receptor grid generation

According to Ban et al. [23], the receptor grid is a search area that offers details on the force field surrounding the target protein. This receptor grid’s creation enables the search space to be explored and ligand-receptor complex structures to be predicted, hence assessing the ligand-receptor complex’s binding affinity [22]. In this study, we prepared the protein grid on the protein’s binding site using the Receptor Grid Generation function in the Schrodinger software tool, which allowed us to identify the InhA enzyme’s active (binding) region. The co-crystallized ligand was selected at the protein’s active site in order to determine the binding site’s location. The active site’s amino acid residues were all included in an artificially produced cubic grid box. Three-dimensional coordinates x, y, and z of the produced grid were 9.15, 22.77, and 80.14 for the target protein (5VRN), respectively.

Structure-based virtual screening (docking study)

In this study, we employed three distinct stages: high-throughput virtual screening (HTVS), standard precision (SP), and extra precision (XP). The self-same scoring function is used by HTVS and SP to rank and assess ligand binding postures. However, XP minimizes the intermediate conformations and the extent of the torsional refinement and sampling to ensure high accuracy in predicting binding poses and affinities with the protein binding cavity [21, 22]. Using the HTVS docking technique, which makes use of the self-same scoring function, predicted 21 043 strong inhibitors of InhA were docked with the reference ligand (co-crystallized ligand) and the standard drug (isoniazid) against the target protein’s binding site (InhA). We selected the top 20% performing molecules of the docking results and performed around the docking study using the SP docking approach. The list was then whittled down to 227 compounds using Standard Precision (SP) docking, which had a more stringent threshold score of −6.5 kcal/mol. The docking results obtained from the SP docking were subjected to XP docking to obtain top hit molecules with excellent docking scores. Throughout the process, the binding affinity of each top hit molecule was evaluated against the co-crystallized ligand and the standard drug, isoniazid. A measure of binding energy or affinity, the docking score is given in kcal/mol [24, 25].

Evaluation of drug likeness and ADMET properties

The top hit compounds with excellent docking profiles were subjected to an absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) study after binding affinity and energy estimates were evaluated. This stage is essential in order to assess the overall pharmacokinetic profile of the compounds. Advanced computational techniques, such as SwissADME and ProTox-II, were used to accomplish a thorough assessment [27, 28]. These platforms provide crucial information on the chosen compounds’ physicochemical characteristics, pharmacokinetic behavior, drug-likeness, and possible toxicity hazards. In drug discovery, ADMET screening is essential because it allows for a thorough assessment of a compound’s absorption, systemic distribution, metabolic transformation, elimination, and potential toxicological consequences [27]. This stage is essential for determining the compounds’ safety and effectiveness and confirming that they are suitable for subsequent development in the pharmaceutical pipeline.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was used to study the dynamic characteristics of the biological environment, such as lipid membranes and aqueous systems. These simulations, which use Newtonian mechanics, shed light on the mobility and interactions of different molecular constituents, such as water, ions, tiny molecules, and macromolecular structures [29].

In this study, the top four compounds based on docking scores with the co-crystalized ligand were subjected to a 100 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation using the Desmond tool of the Schrodinger (version 2017-1). Based on previous docking research, protein–ligand complexes that provided predicted insights into molecular behavior were chosen for simulation. TIP3P was used as the solvent model in an orthorhombic box, and the OPLS4 force field was used for the simulations. NaCl (0.15 M) was supplied to mimic physiological circumstances, and counter ions were added to establish charge neutrality [21]. The Martyna–Tuckerman–Klein Barostat was used to apply the NPT ensemble at 300K temperature and atmospheric pressure. Relaxation measures were carried out to maximize system stability prior to starting the simulation [22]. Every run’s trajectory was captured for further examination at 100 ns intervals. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the protein and ligand structures over time was computed in order to evaluate stability. The protein–ligand interaction profiling and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) were also carried out to improve comprehension of molecular activity in the simulated system [30].

Results and discussion

The discovery of novel InhA inhibitors provides a more realistic solution to combat multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) tuberculosis (TB) strains by targeting the InhA enzyme directly, which bypasses the mutations in the KatG gene that confer resistance to the first-line drug isoniazid (INH) [31]. According to Manjunatha et al. [32], these inhibitors are essential because the growing number of MDR and XDR strains renders therapy more difficult and necessitates the discovery of novel drugs with distinct modes of action. According to research, direct inhibitors of the enoyl-ACP-reductase InhA may produce potential clinical prospects for the discovery of novel antitubercular drugs [33].

While many studies have identified a variety of phytochemicals, including secondary metabolites derived from plants, such as flavonoids or alkaloids, as potential inhibitors of InhA, relatively few studies have focused on natural products, which include bioactive molecules from microorganisms, marine organisms, and other biological sources in addition to plant-derived compounds, providing a much greater chemical diversity. In this study, we adopted a broader strategy by focusing on natural products because they possess structural complexity, which contributes to higher target selectivity [34]. We sourced an extensive library of 276 518 natural products from the LOTUS database and screened the compounds through a series of rigorous computational approaches, such as drug-likeness filtration, molecular docking, MM-GBSA analysis, and ADMET prediction. We developed a machine learning model using a Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN). The MPNN was trained to predict bioactivity profiles of 31 597 natural products against the InhA enzyme. Molecular dynamics simulations further confirmed the stability of these interactions over 100-nanoseconds.

LOTUS database filtration using SAScore and QED

In the initial step of the virtual screening process, the vast collection of 276 518 natural products from the LOTUS database was filtered utilizing assessment measures such as synthetic accessibility score (SAScore) and quantitative estimates of drug-likeness (QED). The Quantitative Estimate of Drug-likeness (QED) assesses how closely a compound’s physicochemical properties align with currently approved oral drugs. QED values vary from zero, which indicates all qualities are unfavorable, to one, which indicates all attributes are favorable [35]. The QED parameter was set to less than or equal to 0.6; compounds with QED values less than or equal to 0.6 were excluded; this suggests that these compounds may not meet the criteria for a good candidate and may have a lower probability of having characteristics that contribute to successful drug development [36]. This validates the findings of Bickerton et al. [35] that compounds with QED values below 0.6 are indicative of compounds with reduced overall drug-likeness.

While it may be realistic for a potential drug molecule to possess a high QED score, which would indicate strong drug-like characteristics, it may also be feasible for the compound to be difficult or challenging to synthesize, possessing a very low SAScore because of its high structural complexity. As a result, we chose to add SAScore in this study, another crucial indicator, to the initial assessment metric, QED. Subsequently, we established two key criteria: (i) molecules having an SAScore of at least 4, and (ii). QED < 0.6 compounds will be screened out and eliminated. This filtration yielded a refined library of 31 597 compounds suitable for downstream analyses. According to Ertl and Schuffenhauer [37], SAScore is significantly essential in virtual screening because this metric can effectively rank large molecular databases by predicting their ease of synthesis. This enables researchers to prioritize compounds for experimental screening and concentrate resources on molecules that are practically viable for follow-up in medicinal chemistry. Baber and Feher [38] demonstrated that a higher SAScore indicates a higher level of ease of synthesis or synthetic accessibility. Consequently, the drug filtration analysis resulted in the elimination of 244 921 compounds, while 31 597 compounds were retained and subsequently advanced to the next stage of virtual screening.

This model may eventually be used to classify the compounds during training, testing, and validation (Table 1). We also used confusion matrices and classification report metrics to evaluate the model’s performance on the test and validation sets (Fig. 3).

In terms of accuracy, which represents the proportion of correct predictions, the model demonstrates strong performance on the training and test sets, achieving 90% and 88%, respectively. However, its performance declines considerably on the validation set, where the accuracy is reduced to 60%. Precision, which reflects the proportion of positive predictions that are correct (true positives divided by the sum of true positives and false positives), is also high on the training and test sets, at 91% and 100%, respectively. Nevertheless, precision decreases to 75% on the validation set, suggesting challenges in accurately identifying positive instances. Recall (or sensitivity), which measures the model’s ability to correctly identify actual positive instances (true positives divided by the sum of true positives and false negatives), is strong in the training set (88%), but declines on the test set (75%) and further decreases on the validation set (60%).

The confusion matrices provide additional insights into classification performance by presenting the distribution of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives (Fig. 3). Similarly, the ROC curve, which evaluates overall classification ability, indicates robust performance across the training, test, and validation sets, with an area under the curve (AUC) exceeding 0.75 in all cases. A curve positioned closer to the top-left corner, along with a higher AUC, reflects better model performance. The reduced performance observed on the validation set may be attributed to limitations in data quantity or the model’s insufficient generalization to unseen examples. Nonetheless, the model demonstrates promise, particularly in the training and test sets, where it achieves consistently strong performance across all evaluated metrics.

Applicability domain analysis of the QSAR model

To evaluate the reliability of predictions generated by the machine learning model, an applicability domain (AD) analysis was performed using Tanimoto similarity relative to the training dataset. Establishing the applicability domain ensures that model predictions are made within the chemical space represented by the training compounds and are therefore considered reliable.

The distribution of maximum Tanimoto similarity values between screened compounds and the training set is presented in Fig. 5. A similarity threshold of 0.5 was adopted as the boundary for the applicability domain. Compounds with similarity values above this threshold are considered to fall within the model’s learned chemical space, while those below the threshold are regarded as outside the domain and interpreted with caution.

Molecular docking and MMGBSA analysis

The docking study was conducted in three different stages, namely the HTVS, SP and XP. Even though HTVS and SP docking use the same scoring function and docking algorithm, they have different mechanisms. According to Greenfield et al. [39], the HTVS approach is less stringent and ultimately eliminates ligands from the same pool, especially when their volume exceeds the volume of the binding site. SP and XP, on the other hand, are slower but more robust. It starts with SP sampling before beginning its own anchor-and-grow procedure; however, XP does more sampling than SP. XP uses a more complex scoring algorithm that is more demanding than SP and has higher standards for compatibility between ligand and receptor shapes. This eliminates false positives that SP allows to pass through [40].

21 043 strong inhibitors obtained from the machine learning-based classification were subjected to HTVS. Following the HTVS approach, compounds with docking scores below −7.00 kcal/mol were identified for a further docking round utilizing the Standard Precision (SP) approach. The SP approach yielded 29 compounds with docking scores below −10.00 kcal/mol. The XP approach was employed for the final round of molecular docking, which revealed the top five compounds with docking scores of ≤ −12.00 kcal/mol. Table 2 shows the results of the XP docking, revealing the top compounds with excellent docking scores. These docking scores were compared with Isoniazid (-6.410 kcal/mol) and that of the co-crystallized ligand, with a docking score of -8.419 kcal/mol.

The top four drug candidates, namely LTS0161715, LTS0078946, LTS0064866, and LTS0023082 had excellent docking profiles with binding energies of −12.204 kcal/mol, −11.926 kcal/mol, −11.624 kcal/mol, and −11.548 kcal/mol respectively, surpassing the co-crystallized ligand (−8.895 kcal/mol), and the standard drug, Isoniazid (−12.204 kcal/mol) (Table 2). The docking score, an indication of the binding affinity or energy, is expressed in kcal/mol. A more negative docking score indicates stronger binding interaction, suggesting a more favorable energy state for the ligand-protein complex [24]. Based on the findings of the docking study, the top four compounds had more negative scores than the standard drug, Isoniazid and the co-crystallized ligand (Table 2). This implies that the top hit molecules can successfully bind to the protein’s active site more firmly and effectively inhibit the target protein’s function.

When comparing our findings with those of other previously published research articles, the molecular docking scores of the most promising compounds in this study fall within the range expected for InhA enzyme strong binders. For example, a recent study by Gupta et al. [41] revealed that three strong inhibitors of InhA enzyme derived from plants had docking scores ranging from –6.3 kcal/mol to −9.16 kcal/mol. A similar study by Singh et al. [42] assessed 80 active compounds from Ruta graveolens and citrus plants against InhA and found that the top molecule, gravacridonediol, a major glycosylated natural alkaloid from Ruta graveolens, might possess promising inhibitory potential against InhA, with a binding energy of -10.80 kcal/mol. While previous studies have primarily focused on phytochemicals, this present study broadens the scope by employing natural products from various sources, highlighting the uniqueness of the research. With the use of natural products, the study’s findings identified lead compounds that demonstrated higher docking scores and more favorable binding interactions compared to reports in previous literature. This highlights the potential of naturally derived scaffolds as promising candidates for the development of novel anti-tubercular agents, particularly against resistant strains.

Structure-based screening

A total of 227 compounds were prepared using the LigPrep module, after which the protein active site was defined based on the position of the co-crystallized ligand through grid generation. Molecular docking was then carried out in a sequential manner, beginning with High Throughput Virtual Screening (HTVS). Compounds that achieved docking scores below −6.5 kcal/mol were subsequently re-docked using Standard Precision (SP). To confirm the reliability of the docking protocol, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) was calculated between the native and re-docked conformations of the co-crystallized ligand, ensuring methodological accuracy (Fig. 7). The calculated RMSD value of 0.8278 Å showed that the top compound had better molecular interactions with the biological target; moreover, a compound is considered to be docked accurately if the RMSD with its crystal structure is ≤ 2.0 Å [43]. The close overlap of the two ligand poses, as indicated by their alignment, suggests a minimal deviation in spatial orientation. This shows that the docking protocol used in this study is reliable and accurately creates the experimentally determined binding mode of the ligand. The low RMSD value obtained further supports the validity of the docking approach.

Evaluation of drug likeness and ADMET prediction

The concept of “drug-likeness” refers to an overall assessment of a drug’s potential to succeed in clinical trials by ultimately eliminating compounds with undesirable features from screening libraries and lowering the risk of attrition at later stages of drug discovery [46]. Drug-likeness can be defined by the representative substructures or building blocks present in medicinal products or drug-like compounds [47]. A common kind of drug-likeness evaluation method is property-based rules, which define the acceptable bounds on the physicochemical properties of drug candidates [46]. The Rule of Five (RO5) rule is the oldest and most well-known of the four empirical rules that have been refined from clinical phase II drugs [48]: (a) molecular weight < 500, (b) octanol/water partition coefficient ≤ 5, (c) number of hydrogen bond donors ≤ 5, and (d) number of hydrogen bond acceptors ≤ 10. Three of these criteria are met by compounds that are regarded as drug-like and have good oral absorption through passive transport [46, 48]. In drug design, the drug likeness parameters include physicochemical properties like molecular weight, hydrophobicity (LogP), hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, aqueous solubility, and topological polar surface area (TPSA), which are often assessed using Lipinski’s Rule of Five. This study assessed the drug likeness of the top four hit molecules, the standard drug, and the co-crystallized ligand using these parameters (Table 4).

The findings of the study demonstrated that all compounds assessed had molecular weights below the 500 Da threshold (Table 4), suggesting good potential for oral absorption. TPSA values ranged between 68.01–108.61 Å2, which is within the acceptable limit (<140 Å2) for adequate membrane permeability, although compounds such as LTS0064866 and LTS0023082 with TPSA values above 100 Å2 may have relatively reduced passive diffusion compared to other drug candidates. Regarding PAINS filters, all other compounds were free of PAINS alerts, suggesting higher reliability as potential lead compounds. However, LTS0161715 and LTS0078946 triggered catechol_A alerts, indicating possible assay interference and the need for further validation before advancing in drug development pipelines. The Lipinski’s rule of five suggests that potential drug candidates should not violate more than one of the established rules [49]. If a drug violates more than one criterion of Lipinski’s Rule of Five, it indicates a poor oral bioavailability and a low probability of being absorbed effectively after oral administration. This suggests that the orally active drug might be unsuitable for the oral administration route, and may require an alternative drug delivery method to be considered a viable drug candidate [50].

The findings of this study demonstrated that all the drug candidates satisfied Lipinski’s Rule of Five with zero violations, reinforcing their oral drug-like potential. According to Sun et al. [51], the logarithm of the n-octanol-water partition coefficient (logP) is frequently used to assess lipophilicity, which shows how soluble and permeable a substance is via biological membranes. A higher log P value is correlated with a greater hydrophobicity. The high Log P value suggests that the drug candidate may have poor bioavailability, accumulate in fatty tissues, and potentially cause systemic toxicity [52]. According to Lipinski’s Rule of 5, an oral drug should have a LogP value less than five (5), more preferably between 1.35–1.8 for good oral and intestinal absorption [53]. Additionally, a fundamental chemical characteristic used to estimate the oral bioavailability of small drug molecules is the number of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors [54]. An excess of hydrogen bond donors/acceptors can negatively affect the membrane partition and permeability of the drug molecule [55]. These polar groups have the ability to both raise the water desolvation penalty during drug penetration and decrease affinity toward the hydrophobic membrane area [54, 55]. A drug must exhibit favorable pharmacokinetics in addition to the necessary bioactivity; therefore, it is essential to strike the right balance between hydrophilicity and lipophilicity. According to the Lipinski rule of five, a drug is considered to have good pharmacokinetics if the number of hydrogen bond donors is ≤ 5, and the number of hydrogen bond acceptors is ≤ 10 [46]. The findings of this study demonstrated that the hydrogen bonding profiles of all compounds (nHA: 4–7; nHD: 2–4) were well within the acceptable range, balancing solubility with membrane permeability (Table 4).

According to Wang et al. [56], the Caco-2 permeability model is used to evaluate the permeability of medication candidates, providing essential details about their potential for oral absorption and bioavailability. The apparent permeability coefficient is a measure of a compound’s ease of passage across the intestinal epithelium [56]. According to Kus et al. [57], A drug candidate is considered a high-permeability molecule if its permeability is greater than 10×10-6cm/s. Consequently, a permeability of less than 1×10-6cm/s is a characteristic of low-permeability drugs. Drugs with values between 1 and 10×10-6cm/s are also categorized as moderate. P-glycoprotein (P-gp) is a drug efflux pump that removes drugs absorbed in the intestines to the gut lumen, hence influencing drug bioavailability. Drugs can be divided into substrates and inhibitors according to how they interact with P-glycoprotein (P-gp). The transport protein (p-gp) typically transports P-gp substrates by actively removing them from the cells. Because of this efflux, there is less medication accessible for systemic absorption, which lowers bioavailability and may result in decreased effectiveness [58]. P-gp inhibitors, on the other hand, disrupt the protein’s (p-gp) activity, which may improve the drug’s distribution and absorption. The findings of the study showed that the top-ranked ligands (LTS0161715, LTS0078946, LTS0064866, and LTS0023082) were predicted to be P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrates, unlike isoniazid and the co-crystallized ligand. While P-gp recognition may enhance efflux and reduce intracellular accumulation, this finding also implies a potential risk of reduced bioavailability and possible drug–drug interactions in multi-drug therapy settings [10].

The majority of the hit molecules did not inhibit the two main cytochrome P450 (CYP450) isoforms, CYP1A2 and CYP2C19, indicating a lesser probability of metabolic interference. Nevertheless, LTS0161715 was shown to be a CYP2C9 inhibitor, which may make it more susceptible to drug-drug interactions in individuals who are taking this enzyme’s substrates. Conversely, isoniazid did not exhibit inhibitory action against the studied CYP450 isoforms, demonstrating its comparatively clean metabolic profile and in line with its established pharmacokinetic profile. Except for LTS0064866, this study demonstrated that all of the top compounds were capable of effectively inhibiting CYP2D6 enzymes, which may lead to elevated drug concentrations in the body. Only LTS0161715, and LTS0023082 could successfully inhibit CYP3A4. The therapeutic benefits of CYP3A4 inhibitors can be maximized by increasing the systemic exposure levels of CYP3A4-substrate medications [59].

Molecular dynamic simulations

In this study, we employed a 100-nano second full atomic simulation to understand the dynamic behavior and the interaction of the top hit molecule and InhA (target protein) at the atomic level, in comparison with the co-crystalized ligand-protein interaction. Through this simulation, we will observe the atomic-level movements and interactions of the reference ligand and top hit molecules with the InhA target protein over time. The findings will shed light on the stability of a drug-target complex by indicating any alterations to the binding site and the duration the drug stays bound. Additionally, MD simulations quantify the dynamic nature of proteins and ligands by analyzing fluctuations and identifying important residues involved in interactions. This will improve the likelihood of more accurately predicting drug binding sites and refine and optimize drug candidates for better therapeutic outcomes. In this study, we examined key metrics such as the atomic deviations, fluctuations, radius gyrations and the protein-ligand interactions of the top hit molecule in comparison with the co-crystalized-protein interaction.

Root mean square deviation

In a simulated system, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) is a measure of average deviation that may be used to estimate the stability and denaturation state of a protein [60]. Protein RMSD and ligand RMSD are crucial measurements for assessing the stability, flexibility, and conformational changes of protein-ligand complexes during molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. While the protein RMSD illustrates how much the protein structure deviates from its initial configuration over time, the ligand RMSD reflects the stability of the ligand’s position and orientation inside the protein’s binding site [61, 62]. Figure 5 illustrates the right y-axis, which displays the ligand’s RMSD profile matched with the protein backbone, while the left y-axis of the figure represents the protein’s RMSD. (Fig. 9).

Schematic illustration of 100 ns MD simulation graph of root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of top-performing hit molecules.

For the protein RMSD, the value of the RMSD is determined using the atom selection after all protein frames have been aligned on the reference frame backbone. RMSD analysis can show whether the simulation has stabilized because the simulation’s final fluctuations center on a thermal average structure. For tiny, globular proteins, changes of the order of 1–3 Å are totally acceptable. However, changes that are significantly more than that suggest that the protein is changing its conformation during the simulation [63]. However, for the Ligand RMSD, “Lig Fit Prot” values, which are frequently somewhat higher than the protein RMSD, provide additional information on the ligand’s conformational changes and structural compatibility with the protein (Fig. 5). They also show how flexible or mobile a ligand is with respect to the protein backbone. Significant changes in the ligand’s posture relative to its initial docked position may be indicated by a significantly higher value. Such variations might indicate a less stable association between the ligand and the protein as well as significant conformational changes inside the binding pocket [22].

The RMSD plot illustrates the structural stability of the apo protein and its complexes with four different ligands (LTS0032082, LTS0048566, LTS0078946, and LTS0161715) over a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation (Fig. 9). The apo form (black line) shows a relatively stable trajectory, with RMSD values fluctuating between 1.5 and 2.3 Å, indicating a moderately stable unbound structure. Among the ligand-bound complexes, LTS0032082 (red line) exhibits the most stable behavior, maintaining an RMSD range of approximately 1.5–2.4 Å throughout the simulation, suggesting that it provides conformational stability to the protein upon binding. In contrast, LTS0048566 (green) and LTS0078946 (purple) display moderate fluctuations, reaching up to 3.0 Å and 2.8 Å, respectively, indicating some degree of structural flexibility that may reflect movement in flexible regions or loops. Notably, the LTS0161715 complex (orange) shows the highest level of deviation, with RMSD values exceeding 3.2 Å at several points, suggesting that this ligand may induce significant conformational changes or instability in the protein structure. LTS0032082 demonstrates the highest structural stability and may be the most promising candidate for further investigation.

Root mean square fluctuation

The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) is a metric used to measure the average deviation of each atom’s location from its mean position over many configurations or throughout a simulation [64]. According to Benson & Daggett [65], RMSF elucidates the difference between the particle position and reference location. The metric helps to clarify the stability and mobility of the residues under investigation in the active site. A high RMSF value indicates increased mobility or flexibility. In other words, High RMSF values suggest more flexible binding site residue movements, whereas low RMSF values indicate less flexible binding site residue movements [22].

Figure 10 shows the comparison of the RMSF analysis of the top four hit molecules and the reference ligand. The plot shows the unbound protein, apo (in black), while red, green, orange and purple plots represent the protein bound with different ligands, namely LTS0161715, TS0078946, LTS0023082, and LTS0064866, respectively. The unbound protein shows higher fluctuations at several regions, particularly near residues ∼45, ∼110, and ∼200–210, which are likely loop or flexible regions. This supports the claim made by Chaudhary and Aparoy [66], that apo proteins frequently show higher RMSF in certain locations when compared to their ligand-bound complexes, suggesting more flexibility in the unbound state. Subsequently, the RMSF plot in our study demonstrated a profound decrease in fluctuations when the ligands were bound to the protein during the simulation, indicating stabilization of the protein upon binding (Fig. 10).

Figure 10 revealed that LTS0023 082 (red) had an intense peak at around 110, suggesting localized flexibility in this area. Furthermore, LTS0064866 (green) indicated modest stability with somewhat higher fluctuation at around 200, but lower overall than apo at other places. At residue 200, LTS0064 866 (orange) had the largest peak (∼6 Å), suggesting considerable flexibility (maybe destabilization of a loop/active site area). LTS0064 866 (purple) had fewer variations in the majority of locations, indicating a more stable binding (Fig. 10). Consequently, these findings point to the possibility that ligand binding might change the dynamics of proteins, with certain ligands providing structural stability surrounding functional domains while others may cause flexible loops to become unstable. These variations in dynamic behavior may have an impact on the drugs’ relative binding stability and possible inhibitory efficacy.

Radius of gyration

During a simulation, the radius of gyration (Rg) shows the compactness of a protein. Stable Rg values imply structural integrity, whereas fluctuations may reflect conformational changes spurred on by drug binding [64]. The radius of gyration (Rg) trajectories showed no indication of global unfolding, and all ligand–protein complexes quickly equilibrated and were stable over the 100 ns simulation (Fig. 11).

Radius of gyrations during the 100 ns MD simulation of the top-performing hit molecules.

With the greatest average Rg (∼4.8 Å), the LTS0023 082 complex (red) showed a more extended conformation. In contrast, the orange-labeled complexes and LTS0078 946 (purple) showed better stability and compactness with lower mean Rg values (∼4.1–4.2 Å). LTS0064 866 (green) has an Rg profile in the middle of these two extremes, showing moderate stability. This may indicate more peripheral area flexibility, which is in line with the localized variations shown in RMSF analysis. The orange and purple (LTS0078 946) complexes, on the other hand, produced a more compact fold with smaller variations and a lower average Rg, suggesting that the global tertiary structure was stabilized by ligand (Fig. 11). This stabilization may increase inhibitory efficiency by increasing conformational stiffness around the binding pocket. LTS0064 866 has moderate compactness with relation to apo and partial stabilization, as shown by its intermediate behavior. All of these results point to the possibility that ligand binding might favor a more compact and stable global fold than others, so influencing the protein’s conformational ensemble differently.

Conclusion

This study successfully identified potential InhA inhibitors, particularly from a database of natural compounds, as a strategy to overcome KatG-dependent resistance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Among the lead compounds, LTS0161715 stood out for its superior docking score and stable interactions with key InhA residues, while LTS0032082 demonstrated the highest structural stability during molecular dynamics simulations. The ADMETox evaluation revealed that LTS0161715 and LTS0032082 demonstrated drug-like properties. Due to these findings, subsequent in vitro and in vivo investigations are needed to validate the potential of these compounds as InhA inhibitors targeting Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains exhibiting KatG-dependent resistance. Although our study identified hit molecules through molecular docking and dynamics, we did not progress these hits through systematic hit-to-lead optimization to enhance potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic profiles, nor did we synthesize the candidates or experimentally validate their inhibitory activity. Computational predictions alone, without empirical synthesis and bioactivity testing, remain inherently limited in assessing true biological efficacy and selectivity. Experimental assays, including in vitro inhibition and cell-based studies, are needed to confirm target engagement and functional activity, and subsequent structure-based optimization could refine lead properties. Therefore, future research should integrate experimental validation with iterative computational design to advance these hits toward viable lead compounds.
