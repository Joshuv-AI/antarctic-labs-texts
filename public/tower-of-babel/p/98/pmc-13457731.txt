Author Response: High Flow Nasal Cannula Over Non-invasive Ventilation in Preventing Reintubation: Signal Bias or Both?
Authors: Nimisha S Patil, Madhulika Dubey, Mohd Mustahsin, Krishna P Mall, Supriya Singh
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We sincerely thank the authors for their thoughtful comments and interest in our study, “Comparison of Effects of Postextubation Non-invasive Ventilation with Passive Humidification and High-flow Nasal Cannula in Patients with High Risk of Extubation Failure: A Randomized Comparative Study.” We appreciate the opportunity to address the concerns raised.

Firstly, regarding the use of passive humidification in the non-invasive ventilation (NIV) arm, active heated humidification may offer physiological advantages, including improved secretion clearance and patient comfort. However, as discussed in the manuscript, although physiological parameters may favor active humidification, the superiority of active over passive humidification has been less consistently demonstrated when clinically relevant outcomes are evaluated.1 Therefore, the use of passive humidification in the NIV group remains a valid and clinically relevant comparator.

Secondly, concerning the absence of crossover between high flow nasal cannula (HFNC) and NIV, this was a deliberate methodological decision aimed at preserving the integrity of the randomized study design and minimizing treatment-related bias. Allowing crossover between modalities could have confounded the assessment of the true efficacy of the assigned intervention. As specified in the study protocol, crossover between HFNC and NIV was not permitted, and patients who failed the assigned therapy were managed according to predefined criteria, including reintubation when indicated. While crossover and escalation of respiratory support frequently occur in routine clinical practice, our approach was intended to ensure a clear comparison between the two interventions under investigation.

Thirdly, the authors have highlighted that NIV has traditionally been considered beneficial in certain patient populations, including those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), acute heart failure, obesity, and hypercapnic respiratory failure. We agree that these conditions have historically been viewed as indications where NIV may provide particular benefit. However, emerging evidence has increasingly demonstrated that HFNC is at least non-inferior, and in some settings comparable, to NIV in these clinical scenarios. Recent studies and meta-analyses have reported favorable outcomes with HFNC in these patients.2–9 These findings suggest that HFNC may represent a viable alternative to NIV even in patient populations traditionally considered more suitable for NIV.

Finally, we acknowledge the concern regarding the inclusion of patients with a single high-risk criterion for extubation failure. This design was intentionally chosen to reflect a broader and more representative population of patients encountered in daily clinical practice. While this may differ from studies that included only patients with multiple high-risk factors, it enhances the applicability of the findings to a wider ICU population.

In conclusion, we appreciate the insightful observations raised by the authors. We agree that larger multicenter studies employing standardized respiratory support protocols and extended follow-up periods are needed to further clarify the relative roles of HFNC and NIV in preventing postextubation respiratory failure. The present study contributes valuable evidence to this evolving field and provides additional data supporting the use of HFNC as an effective postextubation respiratory support strategy in patients at high risk of extubation failure.
