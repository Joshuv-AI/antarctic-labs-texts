Cultural, socioeconomic, and demographic factors contributing to child mortality: evidence from Sierra Leone demographic and health survey 2019
Authors: Mohammad Salim Zahangir, Fariha Khanam Lisa, Mahmuda Al Neyma, Md Rasel Hossain, Sawasan Tabassum, Tanvir Faisal Rakin, Mohmmad Omar Faruk, Kabir Hossain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChild mortality, defined as the likelihood of an infant dying before the age of five, is still a significant concern in developing countries with limited healthcare facilities. Sierra Leone, with a child mortality rate of 68.418 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2024—one of the highest globally—necessitates urgent public health interventions to mitigate this critical issue. This study examined the cultural, socioeconomic, and demographic factors associated with under-five mortality in Sierra Leone.MethodsThis investigation used data from the Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey 2019, comprised of 4540 mothers aged 15–49 years who had at least one child who died under the age of five. The significance of explanatory variables was evaluated using a variety of bivariate statistical analyses, including the Mann-Whitney U (MWU) test, Kruskal-Wallis H (KWH) test, and Kendall’s tau_b and Spearman’s rho correlation. Additionally, several multivariate regression models were used to investigate the factors that influence child mortality.ResultThe results showed that increasing maternal age (IRR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.932–0.968), living in the North-Western region (IRR: 0.110, 95% CI: 0.016–0.770), higher maternal education (IRR: 0.563, 95% CI: 0.189–1.676), higher father education (IRR: 0.816, 95% CI: 0.678–0.981), fewer children under five in the family (IRR: 0.853, 95% CI: 0.796–0.913), and being a working mother (IRR: 0.831, 95% CI: 0.705–0.980) reduced the risk of under-five mortality. However, the risk of under-five mortality increased with the total number of children ever born (IRR: 1.411, 95% CI: 1.263–1.577), younger maternal age at first birth (IRR: 1.032, 95% CI: 1.014–1.064), shorter preceding birth intervals (IRR: 1.006, 95% CI: 1.004–1.009), and having twin children (IRR: 9.792, 95% CI: 1.789–53.59).ConclusionThe study highlights the urgent need to strengthen maternal and child healthcare services, particularly for younger mothers, promote family planning programs to ensure optimal birth spacing, and increase healthcare facilities in regions with high child mortality.Keywords: Child mortality, Under-five deaths, Demographic factors, Socioeconomic factors, Cultural factors, Sierra Leone

Introduction

Child mortality, defined as the likelihood of dying before the age of five, is expressed as the number of deaths per 1000 live births. Despite significant progress in child survival over the past three decades, an estimated 4.9 million children still die before their fifth birthday, making it a global concern [1]. Premature birth, birth complications (such as birth asphyxia and trauma), and congenital anomalies account for four out of every ten under-five deaths [2]. Additionally, lower respiratory infections, malaria, and diarrhoea collectively contribute to nearly one-third of these fatalities [3]. According to the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), the under-five mortality (U5M) rate declined from 90 to 43 deaths per 1000 live births between 1990 and 2015 [4]. To confront the persistent issue of high infant mortality rates, the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG), a global indicator, has established a goal of reducing U5M to a minimum of 25 per 1000 live births by 2030 [5]. However, significant disparities persist between developed and low- and middle-income countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where child mortality rates remain high [6]. In 2022, sub-Saharan Africa and Southern Asia reported the highest burden of neonatal and under-five mortality [7]. Collectively accounting for over 57% and 26% of global under-five and neonatal fatalities, respectively [8, 9]. Sierra Leone, among the sub-Saharan countries, had one of the highest neonatal, infant, and under-five mortality rates, with 34, 82, and 111 fatalities per 1,000 live births, respectively [10–12]. In addition, the Ebola outbreak in Sierra Leone from 2014 to 2016 had a devastating impact on infant and child health due to poor access to healthcare and economic hardships. It disrupted healthcare services, reduced immunization rates, and contributed to deaths of numerous pregnant women and children by causing the collapse of routine maternal and child healthcare services [13, 14].

Despite the achievements made during the MDG era (2000–2015), progress in reducing under-five mortality has slowed in the first half of the SDG period (2015–2022). The yearly rate of reduction in under-five mortality fell from 3.8% in 2000–2015 to 2.1% in 2015–2022, on a global scale [3]. Reducing child mortality is not only a public health challenge but also a social and economic issue. Achieving this goal will require comprehensive policies and reforms implemented by non-governmental organizations (NGOs), development partners, and the Government of Sierra Leone [15]. Some key initiatives introduced by the Government of Sierra Leone include the Free Health Care Initiative (FHCI) (2010), the Agenda for Prosperity (AFP) (2013–2018), and the National Health Sector Strategic Plan (NHSSP) (2010–2015). Additionally, the Basic Package of Essential Health Services (BPEHS) (2015–2020) and the Health Sector Recovery Plan (HSRP) (2015–2020) have been implemented to improve healthcare services [16–19].

Lower respiratory infections (19·2%), malaria (18·1%), and diarrhea (16·8%) are the leading causes of under-5 child deaths in Sierra Leone, Nigeria, Central African Republic, Somalia, Chad, and Guinea. These causes account for 0.61 million of the 1.12 million under-5 child deaths in these countries [1, 20–22]. Additionally, the 2018–20 nationally representative mortality survey identified birth asphyxia and trauma as significant contributors to neonatal mortality [23]. Beyond clinical factors, various socio-demographic, anthropometric, and cultural determinants also influence child mortality in middle- and low-income countries [24]. Household-related factors such as the number of children born, the number of children under five in the household, the total number of household members, and household wealth significantly impact child mortality in sub-Saharan Africa [6, 12, 16, 25]. Furthermore, contraceptive use and preceding birth intervals play a crucial role in child death [16, 26–28]. In addition to demographic and household-related determinants, hygiene-related factors also contribute to child mortality [29, 30]. A study based on the Sierra Leona Demographic and Health Survey (2013) found that children in the Eastern region had a higher risk of mortality than those in other regions [31]. Additionally, research on infant and child mortality in developing countries revealed that parental education and occupation significantly affect child mortality [12, 26–28, 32, 33]. Other contributing factors include breastfeeding duration, inadequate micronutrients, and poor maternal healthcare services, which increase child mortality in developing and underdeveloped countries [34–36].

Although numerous studies have explored the determinants of child mortality in Sierra Leone, few have examined the impact of recent social and healthcare interventions on child survival [6, 16, 37]. This study aims to profoundly investigate the socio-economic, demographic, and cultural factors influencing under-five child mortality in Sierra Leone utilizing the most recent data from the Demographic and Health Survey (2019). The findings will provide valuable insights for policymakers and stakeholders to develop effective strategies and interventions to reduce child mortality.

Materials and methods

Study area and population

Sierra Leone is a low-income country, with 60% of the rural population and 20% of the urban population living below the poverty line [36]. Sierra Leone has an area of 72,180.0 square kilometres and is divided into five administrative regions: northern, north-western, eastern, western, and southern provinces [37, 38]. According to a recent World Bank estimate, Sierra Leone had a gross domestic product (GDP) of $480.0 and a Gross National Income (GNI) per capita of $500 in 2021 [39, 40]. The country’s total population in 2021 was 8.42 million, with an annual growth rate of 2.27% [41]. The infant mortality rate is 87.1, and the under-five mortality rate is 120.4 per 1,000 [10].

Study design

This cross-sectional study explores the determinants affecting under-five child mortality in Sierra Leone, a country that exhibits one of the highest rates of child mortality globally. This analysis was conducted utilizing data from the 2019 Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey, which comprised a cohort of 4,540 mothers aged 15 to 49, each having at least one child under the age of five. The principal variables included maternal and paternal level of education, maternal age, occupational status, number of offspring, intervals between preceding births, type of birth, and access to media. Statistical methods identified significant predictors, including bivariate tests and Zero-Inflated Poisson (ZIP) regression. The protective factors encompassed advancing maternal age, elevated levels of maternal and paternal education, maternal employment, and a reduced number of children under the age of five. The risk factors encompassed younger maternal age at the time of the first birth, shorter intervals between births, a more significant total number of children ever born, occurrences of twin births, and access to media. Residing in the North-Western region was correlated with a decreased mortality rate.

The study advocates for enhancing family planning initiatives to promote extended intervals between births and decrease the overall number of births. Enhancing maternal and paternal education, increasing access to healthcare in regions with high mortality rates, and offering specialized care for adolescent mothers and twin births are of paramount importance. Addressing these factors is imperative to mitigate child mortality and enhance public health in Sierra Leone.

Data collection

This study used secondary data from the Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey (SLDHS) conducted in 2019 [42]. The SLDHS 2019 provides comprehensive data on the demographic and health status of the population in Sierra Leone. The sampling frame used for the SLDHS 2019 was based on the 2015 Population and Housing Census of the Republic of Sierra Leone, conducted by Statistics Sierra Leone (SSL). The survey utilized a two-stage stratified sampling method to ensure representativeness at the national level as well as for urban and rural areas and each of the four regions (Eastern, Northern, Southern, and Western). The SLDHS 2019 comprised 40,543 childbearing women aged 15–49 years. First, regardless of the age of the child, we chose 6,109 women who had at least one dead child. We then eliminated 1,569 respondents who had children who had passed away after the ages of five or sixty months because they did not fit the study’s focus on mortality among children under five. The study’s final sample comprised 4,540 women between the ages of 15 and 49 who had at least one child under the age of five. The inclusion criteria were limited to women who were (a) between the ages of 15 and 49 and (b) had at least one child who died under five. The two-stage stratified sampling design minimizes selection bias and makes this sample representative of the national population. In addition, Sub-Saharan African child mortality studies have employed comparable sample sizes [43, 44].

Statistics Sierra Leone has validated DHS data using standardized protocols, which are widely recognized for their methodological rigour. The sampling design guarantees strong national and sub-national representativeness, despite the possibility of selection bias. To preprocess the dataset, electronic files were forwarded to the central office for quality assurance, which included detecting missing data and inconsistencies. To handle missing data, listwise deletion was used. Errors were corrected by cross-referencing paper surveys with electronic records to improve data accuracy. To ensure real-time validation and consistency checks, CSPro software was used for data processing. Pre-selected households were not replaced to maintain randomness and avoid bias [45].

Study variables

A range of variables were considered in this study after carefully reviewing the literature. Here, the dependent variable is the number of child deaths per woman of childbearing age. It is a count variable. The explanatory variables considered in this study are divided into numeric and categorical features. Numeric variables include age, number of household members, number of children ≤ 5, age of the household head, total children ever born, age at first birth, birth order, preceding birth interval, and spousal age difference are numeric variables. Categorical variables include currently breastfeeding, current working status, sex of children, access to mass media, delivery by cesarean section, toilet facility, religion, relationship to household head, type of cooking fuel, residence, current contraceptive use, sex of household head, twin child, source of water, region, maternal education level, socioeconomic status, husband education level, husband occupation, and body mass index.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for quantitative variables were expressed as minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis. The explanatory statistics were described as a frequency distribution table for categorical variables. The variable of interest (or study variable) considered in this study was a count variable and tested for normality in the primary stage. Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were performed to test normality, and the results showed that the study variable is not normally distributed. Hence, non-parametric techniques were applied throughout the study. Kendall’s tau_b and Spearman’s rho test were applied to determine the correlation between numerical predictors and child mortality. In addition, Man-Whitney U (MWU) was used to observe whether the difference in the mean number of children’s deaths between two mutually exclusive categories of exogenous variables is significant. Moreover, the Kruskal-Wallis H (KWH) test was used to determine if there are statistically significant differences in mean differences of child mortality among three or more mutually exclusive categories of explanatory variables.

In this study, six different non-parametric regression models, such as Poisson, Negative Binomial (NB), Hurdle Poisson (HP), Hurdle Negative Binomial (HNB), Zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP), and Zero-inflated Negative Binomial (ZINB) regression were applied to assess the impact of socioeconomic, demographic, and anthropometric features on child mortality in Sierra Leone. The Zero-inflated Poison (ZIP) model was used because the child mortality data have excess zeros, over-dispersion, and heteroscedasticity, which violate the assumptions of standard Poisson regression. The ZIP model effectively accounts for these characteristics by combining a logistic model for the excess zeros and a Poisson count model for non-zero observations, providing a more precise estimate. Log-likelihood, mean square error (MSE), mean absolute error (MAE), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC) were measured to compare the models [45, 46]. The model with the smallest AIC, BIC, MSE, and MAE with the highest Log Likelihood value was considered the best fit. The measurement parameters of influential factors were represented by odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) with their respective p-values. SPSS version 25 and R Programming Language version 4.2.3 were used to analyze the data.

Ethical consideration

This research was granted by the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) program to collect Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey 2019 (SLDHS 2019) data. When dealing with respondents, high confidentiality was maintained. The ethical approval for SLDHS 2019 was obtained from The Sierra Leone Ethics and Scientific Review Committee, and the ICF Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved the survey protocol. When collecting data, informed consent was obtained from the respondents and ensured that the information would be kept confidential [42]. This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki when performing the analysis and preparing the manuscript.

Hence, ZIP was chosen as a parsimonious model for the study.

Factors influencing child mortality in Sierra Leone

The ZIP model provides the incidence rate ratio (IRR) of the determinants of under-five deaths (see Table 6). The findings show that the risk of child U5 mortality decreased with increasing maternal age (IRR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.932–0.968, p < 0.05). For a unit increase in maternal age, the incidence rate of child mortality decreased by 0.95 times. The under-five mortality rate was 89% lower among women in the North-Western region (IRR: 0.110, 95% CI: 0.016–0.770, p < 0.05) than those in the Eastern part. The North-Western region of Sierra Leone has a much lower U5CM rate than the Eastern region, which may be explained by improved maternal education, easier access to healthcare, and better socioeconomic circumstances. Mother education significantly impacts child mortality in Sierra Leone. The risk of child mortality was 0.56 times lower among mothers with secondary and higher education (IRR = 0.563, 95% CI: 0.189–1.676) than the mothers with no education. The number of children under five in a family significantly affects child death (IRR: 0.853, 95% CI: 0.796–0.913, p < 0.05). It is observed from the result that for one child increase in a family, the risk of child death decreased by 14.7%. Table 6 revealed that the total number of children ever born (IRR: 1.411, 95% CI: 1.263–1.577, p < 0.05) and mothers’ age at first birth (IRR: 1.032, 95% CI: 1.014–1.064) substantially influenced the under-five mortality. The child mortality rate was 16.9% lower among working mothers (IRR = 0.831, 95% CI: 0.705–0.980, p < 0.05) than non-working mothers. The result also showed that the preceding birth interval (IRR: 1.006, 95% CI: 1.004–1.009, p < 0.05) significantly impacted under-five child mortality. Considering the paternal education level, the risk of child mortality was considerably lower among the fathers who attained secondary and higher education than the uneducated fathers (IRR: 0.816, 95% CI: 0.678–0.981, p < 0.05). and (IRR: 0.826, 95%CI: 0.569–1.197), respectively. The result also showed that access to mass media substantially affected child mortality (IRR: 1.142, 95% CI: 1.00-1.304, p < 0.05). Additionally, the risk of child mortality is much higher for mothers with twin children than without twins (IRR: 9.792, 95% CI: 1.789–53.59, p < 0.05).

Impact of socioeconomic, demographic, and cultural determinants of child mortality under 5 years in Sierra Leone (Zero-inflated Poisson regression)

Discussion

This research aimed to identify the factors contributing to Under Five Child mortality (U5CM) in Sierra Leone using data from the Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey (SLDHS, 2019). Based on the ZIP regression model results, the region, mother’s education, total children ever born, the number of children under five, mothers’ working status, and husbands’ educational level significantly affect child mortality. In addition, mothers’ age, maternal employment status, paternal education level, the number of children under five, maternal age at first birth, and preceding birth interval, and access to mass media significantly impacted U5CM.

This study revealed that child mortality varied from region to region in Sierra Leone. Children in the southern and western regions had a higher mortality rate than children in other regions. These discrepancies could be caused by socioeconomic inequality, poor healthcare practices, and inadequately furnished, poor healthcare facilities [10]. A prior investigation examining geographical disparities in infant and child mortality in West Africa showed that the probability of under-five mortality is substantially higher in the Southern region of Sierra Leone [47]. Reducing these disparities requires targeted interventions, such as better access to healthcare, nutrition programs, and maternal education. To address the root causes and advance fair child health outcomes, policymakers should concentrate on region-specific approaches [23].

Mother’s education is a contributing factor to child mortality. This study found that mothers with secondary education had a lower rate of child mortality compared to mothers with no education. As the mother with some formal education gathers knowledge about childbearing, the risk of fatality reduces with the increase in education level. Earlier studies have also highlighted that mother with primary or secondary education had lower child mortality rates [27, 48]. According to a study conducted in Nigeria, children born to illiterate mothers were more likely to die before their fifth Birthday [49]. Our findings are consistent with a study conducted in sub-Saharan Africa, which revealed that maternal education reduces child mortality [50]. Additionally, research also discovered that the under-5 fatality rate could be reduced by increasing literacy [51, 52]. According to separate studies conducted in Ethiopia, mothers with higher education had substantially lower child mortality rates [53, 54]. According to Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey Data (SLDHS, 2008), the lack of literacy among 71% of teenage mothers and 66% of women contributes to limited contraceptive use and inadequate health-seeking behaviors. This lack of education and knowledge about sexual and reproductive health causes high rates of maternal and infant mortality, adolescent pregnancy, and serious health problems for both mothers and children. The reduction of under-five mortality may depend heavily on closing this gap by enhancing literacy and educational access, especially for women [55].

This study revealed that breastfeeding had a significant influence on child mortality. A previous study conducted in Nigeria reported that breastfeeding has a protective effect on child survival, with significantly lower under-five mortality among breastfed children [56]. Similarly, a separate study examining Sub-Saharan African countries highlighted Sierra Leone as a high-burden country for infant mortality, but found that early initiation and prolonged breastfeeding significantly improve child survival rates [57, 58]. Furthermore, a study in Kenya found that breastfed children for more than six months had a significantly lower chance of dying young [59].

Twin birth was also found to be a significant factor determining under-five mortality. The result of this study revealed that the risk of child mortality increases significantly for those with twin child compared to those without twins. A previous study showed that the risk of neonatal death in twin births is much higher compared to a single birth, which is approximately 5.22 times greater [60]. Similarly, another study in Ethiopia reported that twin births are a risk factor for child mortality [12]. Research conducted in Southern and Eastern Africa found twins are much more likely to die than singletons, and the excess mortality is greatest during the first year of life. Poor socioeconomic status, male gender, being unwanted, and short birth intervals are some of the factors that contribute to this [61]. They are more vulnerable toward adverse demographic and household socioeconomic influences, which is further exacerbated by biological and gestational complications [62]. Additionally, high infant mortality rates among twins and triplets can also result from being born to young mother, unschooled mothers, after short birth intervals, or as the first live birth, particularly in places with poor access to high-quality maternal and child care services [63]. In order to mitigate these risks and enhance twin survival, under-5 clinics could implement more focused interventions and improved monitoring.

Another influential factor for child mortality in Sierra Leone is the number of children ever born. Our study found that the risk of child mortality decreases as the number of children ever born in a family or household increases. Research conducted in Ethiopia showed a lower prevalence of under-five mortality with more children at home [57]. In Sierra Leone, the protective factor that reduced child mortality was the increased number of under-five children, similar to the previous discovery. However, an earlier study conducted in Sierra Leone yielded conflicting results, suggesting that an increase in the number of children delivered in the household is linked to a higher mortality rate among children under the age of five [6]. In contrast, a study conducted in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) showed households with 3–5 or more children had a significantly higher risk of under-five mortality than those with 1–2 children [64].

Moreover, fewer child deaths were reported among women in higher age groups than women in lower age groups. Mothers below 18 are associated with an increased likelihood of childhood mortality [16]. Other studies have also revealed that younger mothers are more likely to have children who die before the age of five [65, 66]. According to this study, working mothers had a significantly lower child mortality rate than non-working mothers. This finding deviates from a previous study, which noticed that the risk of child mortality among working mothers was 1.24 times higher than among non-working mothers [32]. Additionally, educated fathers have a lower risk of child mortality. Previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [28], Nigeria [49], and Bangladesh [32] also revealed that fathers with at least secondary school education had a lower prevalence of child mortality compared to fathers with no formal education.

Mother’s age at first birth and preceding birth interval are also factors that influence child mortality. Child mortality was observed to increase with increasing age at first birth. According to previous research conducted in Ethiopia, the child mortality rate among children whose mothers were 24 years or older at their first birth was significantly higher than among children whose mothers were less than 15 years old at their first birth [65]. In the same vein, the age of reproductive women at the time of their first birth was associated with child mortality. The results indicated that the risk of child mortality was consistently higher among younger mothers than among elderly mothers, which is consistent with our research [67]. Another study in Brazil found that most of the children were born to mothers aged between 20 and 35 years, and a significantly lower rate of child mortality occurred among those mothers, and the mortality rate of children increased with the increase in mother age [68].

This study also showed that increased preceding birth intervals impacted child mortality. In contrast to these results, two-year or more significant birth intervals were associated with lower child mortality. Another study also showed that the risk of childhood death decreased when the birth interval was extended to 48 months. Additionally, studies conducted in Sierra Leone [16], Ethiopia [12, 26, 28, 60], Bangladesh, and sub-Saharan Africa [27] were consistent with this finding, which showed higher child mortality for longer birth intervals between two preceding births. WHO (World Health Organization) identified that birth interval is a significant risk factor for under-five mortality, and they advised that three to five years’ space of pregnancy reduces the health risks for both children and women.

Policy implications

The results of this investigation demonstrate the urgent need for focused interventions to lower Sierra Leone’s under-five child mortality rate. Child survival rates can be considerably increased by enhancing maternal education, expanding access to healthcare, and encouraging exclusive breastfeeding. Region-specific approaches to addressing healthcare disparities should be given top priority by policymakers, especially in high-mortality areas. Child mortality can be further decreased by expanding family planning programs and providing specialized care for high-risk pregnancies, like twin births. Government and non-governmental organizations must work together to implement long-lasting programs that enhance the health of mothers and children.

Limitations of the study

This study rigorously analyzed the risk factors of child mortality by applying several count data regression models and choosing the best among them through different model evaluation techniques. However, the prime limitation of this study is the use of secondary data for analysis. Although we intended to add some environmental and clinical risk factors, these factors were not available to the used secondary data. Another limitation is that the risk factors identified in this study were from survey data, which may have sampling or non-sampling errors. Experimental data, in this case, may give better results. A significant concern in secondary data analysis is recall bias, where respondents may not accurately remember past events or experiences, particularly over lengthy timeframes. This bias can lead to inaccuracies in the data collected, potentially affecting the study’s outcomes. Additionally, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to draw conclusions about causality, and the analysis does not thoroughly examine the variables affecting regional differences in mortality among children under five. Despite these limitations, the rigorous application of various count data regression models and selection criteria strengthens the reliability of the findings.

Conclusion

Child mortality is an essential indicator of a country’s social development and disparities. According to the findings of this study, factors associated with child mortality are geographical region, level of education, total children ever born, breastfeeding, twin children, mother’s age, mother’s employment status, paternal education, the number of children under five, maternal age at first birth, and birth interval. Based on our analysis, we recommend prioritizing family planning programs and ensuring their effectiveness. The study also suggests providing special reproductive health care to younger mothers. To obtain a comprehensive understanding of child mortality and its risk factors, it is imperative to conduct extensive research in regions with a higher incidence of child mortality. Child mortality and regional disparities can be mitigated through the implementation of a well-organized healthcare infrastructure and the expansion of socioeconomic resources for reproductive women. In addition, specific education campaigns need to highlight the negative consequences of infant mortality brought on by short birth intervals for both the mother and the child. To further enhance results, the significance of parental education should be emphasized, especially with regard to childcare procedures, personal hygiene, and the advantages of breastfeeding.
