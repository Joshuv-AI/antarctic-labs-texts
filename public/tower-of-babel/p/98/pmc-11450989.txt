Vascular complications during appendectomy: severe adhesion of the appendix to the right iliac artery: a case report
Authors: Ahmad Hosseinzadeh, Hoora Rezaeibana, Mohammadreza Khosravi, Armin Sourani, Hadis Yazdanshenas, Reza Shahriarirad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAppendicitis is a common cause of acute abdomen. Rarely, it may form adhesions to nearby structures, complicating surgeries, especially when involving vascular structures such as the internal iliac artery, potentially causing severe intraoperative hemorrhage.Case presentationA 65-year-old Middle Eastern male presented with abdominal pain and anorexia for 5 days. Examination and imaging confirmed acute appendicitis with complications. Additionally, a large pelvic mass was noted. During surgery, severe bleeding was encountered due to an adhesion between the appendix and the right internal iliac artery, managed by ligating the artery. The patient recovered well and was discharged in stable condition. Histopathology confirmed the diagnosis.ConclusionThis case highlights a rare vascular complication of appendectomy due to abnormal adhesions between the appendix and the internal iliac artery, associated with a large pelvic mass. This study emphasizes the need for thorough preoperative evaluation and careful surgical planning in patients with unusual anatomical variations or specific underlying conditions such as neurofibromatosis. Early recognition and strategic management of vascular adhesions are essential to optimize patient outcomes in appendectomies complicated by such rare scenarios.

Introduction

Appendicitis stands as one of the most common etiologies of acute abdomen, a condition characterized by inflammation of the vermiform appendix [1]. Anatomically, the vermiform appendix has a variable position within the abdominal or pelvic cavity [2]. In rare instances, the vermiform appendix may develop uncommon adhesions to neighboring pelvic or abdominal structures, presenting a unique challenge during surgical interventions.

Previous studies have reported cases of such adhesions, including a variety of pelvic and abdominal structures. These adhesions have been observed with structures including ovarian cysts, the gallbladder, a right common iliac artery aneurysm, and even the aorta following prosthetic replacement procedures [3–6]. While these occurrences are infrequent, their clinical significance lies in their tendency to complicate surgical procedures. Of particular concern are adhesions involving arterial structures, such as the internal iliac artery, as manipulation or dissection of these adhesions can precipitate severe intraoperative hemorrhage, leading to life-threatening consequences [7].

Vascular complications arising from adhesions to arterial structures present a unique set of challenges for surgeons [8]. During surgical exploration, the presence of such adhesions can significantly impede dissection and increase the risk of vascular injury, leading to severe intraoperative bleeding. Moreover, these adhesions might remain undetected until encountered intraoperatively, necessitating precise surgical technique and heightened awareness to minimize potential complications [9]. Understanding the anatomical variations and potential consequences of appendiceal adhesions to vascular structures is imperative for surgeons to anticipate and effectively manage such scenarios, thereby optimizing patient outcomes and reducing adverse events during surgical interventions [7, 10]. This study aims to give guidance to the surgeons in anticipating such vascular complications and optimizing the clinical outcomes in patients undergoing appendectomies.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old Middle Eastern male patient was referred to the emergency department with a chief complaint of abdominal pain that began 5 days earlier and subsequently migrated. The patient experienced anorexia but did not report any other concomitant symptoms, such as nausea. Although the patient previously reported being healthy, neurofibromatosis-like lesions were visible on his skin, with no prior diagnosis or treatment for the condition, which he stated had been present since birth and had progressed slowly. Noteworthy medical history includes surgery 20 years prior to resect scrotal and para-anal lesions, and another surgery 8 years later to remove a large mass from his left arm.

Physical examination revealed tenderness in the right lower abdomen, accompanied by rebound tenderness. Sonographic assessment revealed a blind-ended bowel loop with a 8 mm diameter, indicative of acute appendicitis. A spiral computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast revealed an inflamed appendix with an anteroposterior diameter of 7 mm, associated with adjacent fat stranding and a collection measuring 25 × 30 mm in the right lower abdominal quadrant, suggestive of complicated appendicitis. Incidentally, there was evidence of an infiltrative soft tissue density mass in the right ischiorectal area, in close contact with the prostate and perirectal area, extending to the right buttock, with cortical erosion and irregularity of the ramus of the right pubic bone. Several pneumocysts of varying sizes, up to 30 mm, were also observed bilaterally in the visible parts of the chest.

During the surgical procedure, two surgeons—an attending and a resident—began with a McBurney’s incision to perform an appendectomy. However, due to complications, the incision was extended with a low midline approach to improve surgical exposure. The patient experienced significant intraoperative bleeding, estimated at 1.5 L, which was initially managed by packing the pelvic area with gauze, but due to the ongoing bleeding, required the consultation with a vascular surgeon to identify the cause.

Upon removal of the gauze, substantial bleeding was noted, whereupon the right common iliac artery was controlled with a vascular clamp. Subsequent investigation identified adhesion of the internal iliac artery to the tip of the appendix as the primary cause of the hemorrhage (Fig. 1. Intraoperative imaging demonstrating). The artery had been severed from its origin at the common iliac artery by the initial intervention. The complication was managed by ligating the internal iliac artery. The origin of the artery was oversewn with Prolene sutures, successfully controlling the bleeding. The distal portion of the artery was also oversewn. The surgical intervention was deemed adequate, and no further exploration of other anatomical regions was required. The patient was discharged from the hospital after a few days in the intensive care unit (ICU), with stable hemodynamics and no significant complications. Discussion

In this article we presented a rare vascular complication of appendectomy as a result of an abnormal appendix adhesion to the internal iliac artery, manifesting as severe intraoperative bleeding. Although this patient did not have any remarkable history of previous abdominal or pelvic surgery, the appendix had adhesion bands to the internal iliac artery in the presence of a large pelvic mass. This highlights the importance of considering the possibility of unusual vascular adhesions in patients with an abdominal or pelvic mass.

Intraoperative inspection revealed that the appendix was adherent to the internal iliac artery, with the artery severed from its origin at the common iliac by the previous surgical intervention. In such cases, where the internal iliac artery is affected unilaterally, ligation and closure of the artery are recommended. The surgical intervention was deemed adequate, and no further exploration of other anatomical regions was required. Although the possibility of similar adhesions involving arteries in other organs exists, further exploration is only indicated if pathology arises. In such cases, careful exploration is warranted to identify any potential vascular adhesions.

Based on our literature review, seven cases (eight with our case) of adhesion of the appendix to vascular structures of the abdomen and pelvic have been reported in literature and various databases so far (Table 1). Different etiologies for an adhesion between the appendix and a vascular structure are shown in the table, including infection, chronic inflammation, previous surgeries and vascular repairs, and congenital etiology. Most of the cases with an arterial adhesion to the appendix have a previous history of abdominal surgeries, while infection and the congenital formation of such adhesions are less common etiologies. The case of our study had a large pelvic mass, which highlights the hypothesis of the chronic pelvic inflammation to be a possible etiology for this rare and complicated condition. These patients were ranged from 17 to 75 years of age; while most of them (62.5%) were 48 years or older, as in the case in our study, and only three out of eight patients mentioned were younger than 21 years old. Prevalence of such appendiceal adhesions was significantly higher in male patients during our review of the literature, as we could not find a similar condition in a female patient. While facing such arterial adhesions during surgeries could be a challenge for surgeons, there were no intraoperative complications in most cases. However, our 65-year-old patient presented with severe bleeding during operation, but managed to survive the condition unlike the case in the study of Sahlman et al.[11] In most cases, there was no suspicion of such adhesions before the surgery, as in only two studies, CT imaging revealed a possible adhesion before the operation, and the rest of the studies as well as our case were diagnosed to have such adhesions during operation. Considering the fact that in most cases these adhesions were found incidentally, diagnosing these anatomical abnormalities before the surgery could be a challenge. This study suggests a thorough imaging and surgical plan in appendicitis patients who have an underlying etiologic factor for developing such adhesions, such as previous abdomen and pelvic surgeries, infections, and inflammation. Literature review of adhesion of the appendix to vascular structures of the abdomen and pelvis

Intra-abdominal adhesions have several etiologies, but in general they are classified as either congenital or acquired adhesions [17]. The congenital type is present since birth, commonly as a result of an embryological anomaly; however, the acquired type is formed after abdominal and pelvic surgeries, radiation therapies, and inflammatory processes [18]. The adhesion formation in the case of our study seems to be acquired, as the large pelvic mass in this case could be a possible source of chronic inflammation within the pelvic cavity, leading to anatomical disturbance and adhesion formation. An interesting fact about this case is the previous history of having neoplastic lesions of left arm and scrotal and peri-anal areas. The incidence of such neoplastic lesions and a current pelvic mass could be related to the neurofibromatosis. Our patient had the cutaneous manifestations of neurofibromatosis lacking prior diagnosis of the disease. Some studies have described that neurofibromatosis is associated with the incidence of neoplastic lesions including the gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) and adenocarcinomas of different gastrointestinal sites [19]. Thus, the possibility of facing incidental neoplastic lesions and uncommon adhesions in patients with neurofibromatosis should be taken into consideration before planning such surgeries.

Adhesion formation is a common adverse effect of a previous surgery. The incidence of adhesion formation after gastrointestinal surgeries ranges up to 66%, on the basis of a systematic review study [20]. However, observing such adhesions without previous history of surgeries is uncommon, as in our case [21, 22]. Previous studies have documented adhesions and attachments between the appendix and gallbladder, ovarian cyst, herniation sac, and vascular structures and aneurysms [3–6]. Most of these adhesions between the appendix and vascular structures were consequences of previous vascular surgeries, including the aneurysmal repairs or vascular prosthetic replacement surgeries, but in some rare instances, such adhesions has been reported to exist without a previous laparotomy, as in the case report of Sunil et al., wherein an abdominal CT imaging of a 12-year-old patient showed adhesion bands between the vermiform appendix and the calcified wall of the iliac artery aneurysm. The etiologic cause in the mentioned case was revealed to be the consequence of infection and the inflammatory process [6, 16, 23]. In our patient, there was no previous history of vascular surgery or laparotomy. A review of literature revealed that, in many patients with appendiceal adhesions to vascular structures, the involved vessels had a structural abnormality similar to aneurysms; however, there was no evidence of such vascular abnormality in our case report [23–25].

The vermiform appendix is shown to have variable positions within the abdominal or pelvic cavity. It has been mentioned that appendix is the only organ without a definite anatomy, due to its anatomical variations. The most common position of appendix is thought to be retrocecal in some studies [26]; however, an Iranian cadaveric study on 200 bodies revealed the pelvic position to be the most common anatomical position of the vermiform appendix [27]. In our particular case, the appendix was revealed to have a pelvic position that predisposed it to such an abnormal adhesion by neighboring the internal iliac artery. The pelvic position of appendix becomes more important in this case, as we take into consideration the fact that the patient had a large pelvic mass without prior workup and definite diagnosis. Based on the results of the CT imaging, there was possibility of malignant process due to the wide extension of the mass within the pelvic cavity. A highly possible hypothesis in this case scenario is the chronic inflammation within the pelvic cavity as a result of the pelvic mass leading to adhesion formation between the appendix and the internal iliac artery. Moreover, the change in the normal anatomical position of the pelvic structures as a result of the pressure effect of the large mentioned mass could be a key element in setting the involved structures in a closer contact, facilitating the formation of the adhesions.

When planning an appendectomy, particularly in cases with unusual anatomical variations or in the presence of underlying disease such as neurofibromatosis and inflammatory processes of different etiologies such as a large pelvic mass, comprehensive preoperative assessment is crucial. This article suggests a CT angiography imaging as a preoperative evaluation in such cases to precisely determine uncommon vascular adhesions before the operation. Intraoperatively, a cautious approach is essential to prevent vascular damage and manage complications effectively. Surgeons should be prepared for the associated risk of severe bleeding in patients with abnormal vascular adhesions or in patients with a pelvic mass. Additionally, having a vascular surgeon on standby or involved in the procedure can provide critical support in managing vascular complications. As bleeding from major arteries within the pelvic cavity including internal iliac artery could lead to a life-threatening situation during the operation, prophylactic or therapeutic ligation of the internal iliac artery could help surgeons in managing such complicated conditions [28].

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare and severe complication of appendicitis involving adhesion of the appendix to the right internal iliac artery, resulting in significant intraoperative bleeding. The presence of a large pelvic mass likely contributed to the unusual adhesion. This study points the importance of a precise preoperative evaluation of patients with abdominal or pelvic neoplastic lesions and suspected inflammatory processes, as unusual and uncommon adhesions could complicate the surgery. Early recognition and careful surgical planning are crucial in managing such complex cases to optimize patient outcomes.
