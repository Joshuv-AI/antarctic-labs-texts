Immuno-engineered conductive hydrogels: Bridging neural signaling and microenvironmental remodeling for neural repair
Authors: Ying Zhang, Xiaoxuan Han, Zhaowei Li, Jingru Zhou, Shude Yang, Deteng Zhang, Xu Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nervous system injuries and diseases present formidable regenerative challenges, largely due to hostile inflammatory microenvironments that impede endogenous repair mechanisms. Conductive nanocomposite hydrogels have emerged as a transformative class of immuno-engineered biomaterials designed to actively overcome these barriers. Beyond providing electroactive scaffolds to restore neural signaling, these advanced platforms integrate functional nanomaterials (e.g., carbon-based, MXene, and conductive polymers) within biocompatible polymer networks to facilitate targeted immunomodulation. Their core scientific significance lies in their synergistic capacity to simultaneously scavenge pathological reactive oxygen species (ROS) and steer macrophage polarization towards a pro-regenerative phenotype, thereby reprogramming the inhibitory injury niche into a permissive, pro-healing milieu. This review systematically elucidates the design principles spanning crosslinking strategies to nanomaterial selection that underpin these dual functions. We critically summarize recent breakthroughs in applying these multifunctional hydrogels to treat spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury, stroke, and peripheral nerve defects, where they demonstrate enhanced functional recovery. By bridging the fields of conductive biomaterials and immunomodulation, this work not only surveys the state-of-the-art but also provides a unified framework for developing next-generation therapeutic platforms that couple bioelectronic cues with precise immune modulation, offering a novel paradigm for neural regeneration medicine.

Introduction

Despite decades of progress in neuroscience, effective clinical interventions for nerve injury repair remain elusive due to the structural and functional complexity of the nervous system [1]. While the central nervous system (CNS) integrates and processes neuronal information, the peripheral nervous system (PNS) mediates communication between the brain and the rest of the body. Pathologies in either system continue to pose major clinical challenges. For instance, long-segment peripheral nerve defects remain a formidable therapeutic obstacle [2], always leading to severe sensorimotor dysfunction and chronic neuropathic pain. Although autologous nerve grafting remains the gold standard for peripheral nerve repair, its application is hindered by limited donor availability, donor-site morbidity, and secondary infection risk [3]. CNS injuries, such as spinal cord and brain injuries, present even greater challenges. Following a spinal cord injury (SCI), primary and secondary damages drive the loss of local neural tissue, culminating in scar or cavity formation. This creates a highly inhibitory local microenvironment that severely restricts axonal regrowth and nerve regeneration [4]. Similarly, traumatic brain injury (TBI), stroke, tumors, and infections can disrupt neural networks, causing long-term neurological and functional deficits [5]. The resulting insufficient neuronal regeneration profoundly diminishes patients’ quality of life.

A cascade of post-injury pathological events, including neuronal death, disrupted neural communication, chronic inflammation, glial scar formation, and limited intrinsic repair capacity, collectively impedes functional recovery [6]. Since electrical signals form the physical basis of neural impulses and are essential for real-time biological information transmission [7]. Neural tissues are highly responsive to such signals, which regulate neuronal behavior, maintain homeostasis, and promote regeneration [8]. Accordingly, biomaterials and technologies that deliver controlled electrical stimulation Accordingly, biomaterials and technologies that deliver controlled electrical stimulation hold considerable promise. Conductive materials have been shown to enhance intercellular charge transport and signal propagation, thereby promoting neural cell adhesion, proliferation, migration, and neurite outgrowth [9]. Recent study suggests these therapeutic effects stem from the modulation of intracellular Ca2+ concentrations in neurons and glia via voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs), alongside the regulation of CaSR and PLCβ signaling cascades [10]. Furthermore, electroactive materials can drive macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory factors, helping establish a regenerative niche [11]. This immunomodulatory capability positions these materials as highly promising candidates. However, the direct biomedical application of many traditional conductive materials is limited by cytotoxicity, poor solubility, and suboptimal biocompatibility [12]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for conductive scaffolds that can recapitulate the soft, hydrated, and native-like microenvironment of neural tissues [13,14].

Hydrogels are a class of three-dimensional (3D) cross-linked polymeric networks capable of retaining large amounts of water, and have emerged as attractive tissue engineering scaffolds [15]. Their high water content and structural resemblance to the extracellular matrix (ECM) provide essential mechanical support while permitting the diffusion of nutrients, cells, and bioactive molecules [16]. Incorporating conductive materials into hydrogels creates efficient conductive networks [17] that facilitate charge transport across cell membranes and modulate cell-cell or cell-biomaterial interactions [18]. Thus, conductive hydrogels are uniquely suited for neural tissue regeneration, combining electrical conductivity with tissue-mimetic soft mechanics [19]. Nevertheless, many conventional conductive hydrogels suffer from poor tensile strength, low sensitivity, inadequate stability, and limited degradability-drawbacks largely attributable to the reliance on non-degradable synthetic polymers [1].

Conductive nanocomposite hydrogels, engineered by integrating conductive nanoparticles into hydrogel materials, offer a potential strategy to overcome these limitations. Nanoparticles provide high surface-to-volume ratios and abundant functional groups, allowing facile modification to tailor electrical, mechanical, and biological properties [20]. Various nanofillers including carbon-based, metallic and metal oxide-based, and polymer-based conductive nanomaterials, have been incorporated into polymeric networks, significantly enhancing electrical conductivity, elasticity, and bioactivity. This integration enhances electrical conductivity, elasticity, and bioactivity compared to traditional hydrogels. Combining biodegradable polymers with diverse conductive nanofillers has thus become a core trend in regenerative biomedicine [21].

This review systematically explores the potential of conductive nanocomposite hydrogels in neural tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. We focus on synthesis and cross-linking strategies (chemical, physical, and dynamic), and analyze the structural and functional characteristics of various conductive nanomaterials. Furthermore, we highlight recent advances in treating nerve injuries, providing valuable perspectives for future neuroscience and biomaterials research (Fig. 1). Beyond their established role in facilitating electroactive signaling, emerging evidence indicates that many of these conductive nanomaterials inherently possess potent immunomodulatory properties. In this context, conductive nanocomposite hydrogels can function as advanced biomaterial-based adjuvants, offering new insights for seamlessly integrating neural tissue engineering with localized immunotherapy.Fig. 1Overview of therapeutic strategies for neurological diseases and nerve injuries based on conductive nanocomposite hydrogels which act as immunomodulatory platforms.

Crosslinking modes of conductive nanocomposite hydrogels

Conductive nanocomposite hydrogels are typically fabricated by embedding conductive nanomaterials with natural or synthetic polymers [22]. Natural polymers such as chitosan (CS), gelatin, cellulose, alginate, silk fibroin (SF), collagen, and lignin [23], as well as synthetic polymers including polyethylene glycol (PEG), poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and polyacrylic acid (PAA) [24,25], serve as highly tunable matrices. To impart electrical conductivity, various nanomaterials (e.g., carbon nanotubes, graphene, MXenes, and metal oxides) are incorporated as "guests" within the polymeric "host" [26,27]. The specific cross-linking mechanism dictates the 3D network architecture, directly influencing mechanical strength, swelling behavior, stability, and the continuity of the conductive pathways. Generally, these hydrogels rely on two primary modes: physical cross-linking (non-covalent bonds) and chemical cross-linking (covalent or dynamic covalent bonds). By optimizing cross-linking density, polymer type, and nanofiller concentration, the resultant properties can be precisely tailored for neural applications [28].

Physical cross-linking

Physical cross-linking relies on non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interaction, hydrophobic interaction, and crystallization, to form 3D polymer networks. These interactions endow hydrogels with excellent biocompatibility, reversibility, and self-healing capability.

Hydrogen-bond cross-linking constructs reversible networks by hydrogen bonds between polymer chains and the polar groups of conductive nanoparticles, enhancing nanoparticle dispersion and network stability. For example, Yao et al. synthesized an MXene-composite PVA/polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVA/PVP) double-network hydrogel, in which hydroxyl groups (-OH) on PVA form hydrogen bonds with the -OH groups on the MXene sheets. Freeze-thawing induces PVA crystallization, generating additional physical cross-linking points. Incorporating just 1 wt% MXene nanosheets increased the hydrogel's mechanical strength by 2400% while significantly improving ductility and toughness. Electrostatic interactions, which are generally stronger than hydrogen bonds, occur between oppositely charged polymers and nanoparticles, yielding robust networks. For instance, negatively charged PEDOT nanoparticles interact with positively charged chitin nanofibers to form highly conductive, mechanically resilient films (Fig. 2b) [30]. Similarly, Gu et al. incorporated polyaniline nanoparticles (PANI NPs) into a CS-polyacrylic acid (CS-PAA) double-network hydrogel. Electrostatic attraction between protonated amino groups of CS and deprotonated carboxyl groups of PAA enabled uniform nanoparticle dispersion, while synergistic hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions further enhanced toughness and conductivity [32]. Multiple non-covalent forces often act simultaneously, resulting in stronger, more energy-dissipative networks. Jing et al. used MXene as a multifunctional physical cross-linking combined with ultraviolet (UV) light-initiated polymerization of acrylamide, producing an MXene-based hydrogel with low mechanical hysteresis. Owing to abundant polar surface groups and a layered structure, MXene physically entangled with polyacrylamide chains through multiple interactions, relieving stress concentration and improving mechanical resilience [33].

Chemical cross-linking

Chemical cross-linking utilizes covalent or dynamic covalent bonds, typically resulting in higher mechanical strength, lower swelling ratios, and superior long-term stability compared to physically cross-linked systems.

Covalent cross-linking creates irreversible bonds using chemical cross-linkers or reactive functional groups. For example, acid-treated carbon nanotubes (CNTs) can form ester bonds with hydroxyl groups of PVA, resulting in stable hydrogels with improved mechanical properties and swelling resistance [34]. Dynamic covalent cross-linking introduces reversible bonds such as schiff base (C=N), hydrazone, disulfide, boronate ester, and Diels-Alder linkages, enabling self-healing and environmental responsiveness. Tian et al. embedded conductive two-dimensional (2D) MgB2 nanosheets into a PVA hydrogel matrix to serve as highly functional cross-linkers. These nanosheets formed extensive dynamic boronate ester bonds (B-O-C), achieving a tensile strength of 8.58 ± 1.34 MPa and maintaining electrical stability over 1500 stretching cycles (Fig. 2c) [27]. Coordinate cross-linking represents a special case of chemical bonding, where coordinate covalent bonds are formed through metal-ligand complexation. Its defining characteristic is that the bonding electron pair is donated solely by the ligand, while the central metal ion (or atom) acts only as the electron pair acceptor. For example, Xu et al. constructed a calcium alginate (CA)/silver nanoparticle (AgNP) hydrogel, in which carboxyl groups (-COO-) of alginate coordinated with Ca2+ in the CaCO3 solution. Meanwhile, -COO- can combine with positively charged AgNP colloids through electrostatic interaction and electrostatically interacted with AgNPs. This “bridging effect” form a 3D network with enhanced mechanical integrity [35]. Covalently crosslinked hydrogels are generally exhibit better mechanical properties and require higher high energy to degrade, while hydrogels formed through dynamic or coordination bonds offer tunable crosslinking and stimuli-responsiveness to light, pH, temperature, or light [36].

Conductive nanocomposite hydrogels can also be prepared by uniformly dispersing conductive nanoparticles in hydrogel polymer monomers followed by in situ polymerization under controlled temperature or irradiation [[37], [38], [39]]. This method promotes homogeneous dispersion, stable bonding, and maintain conductive nanoparticles properties [40]. For instance, Li et al. incorporated gold nanorods (GNRs) into gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) precursor solution, forming UV cross-linked GelMA-GNR hydrogels. In this hydrogel, GNRs are incorporated into the hydrogel matrix through metal complexation between the positively charged GNRs and negatively charged amine groups. The addition of GNRs enhances both mechanical stiffness (elastic modulus ≈ 1.3 kPa) and electrical conductivity of the hydrogel matrix [41]. Furthermore, dynamically cross-linked hydrogels can also serve as smart drug delivery systems. For instance, conductive PEDOT nanoparticles act as photothermal agents to induce near-infrared (NIR) triggered gel–sol transitions, enabling on-demand drug release from the hydrogel matrix (Fig. 2d) [31].

Crucially, to meet the minimally invasive requirements for treating irregular lesions in brain and spinal cord injuries, hydrogels must exhibit shear-thinning and self-healing properties to avoid secondary surgical trauma. Li et al. developed an injectable, self-healing cod peptide-based hydrogel stabilized by multiple dynamic reversible networks (acylhydrazone, imine, and boronate ester bonds), endowing it with outstanding injectability [42]. Similarly, Liu et al. reported a pH-responsive MoS2-integrated hydrogel where dynamic Schiff-base chemistry combined with photocrosslinking allowed for tunable properties that match native spinal cord tissue [43].

Effects on mechanical, electrical, and biological properties

The crosslinking mode dictates the structural arrangement of conductive nanocomposite hydrogels, directly governing their mechanical, electrical, and biological profiles [44,45]. Incorporating conductive nanomaterials into polymer networks significantly enhances conductivity, elasticity, and bioactivity. Generally, chemically cross-linked hydrogels exhibit robust mechanical strength, physically cross-linked networks offer superior biocompatibility, and dynamically cross-linked systems excel in self-healing capabilities. Recently, researchers have developed multi-crosslinking synergistic strategies to integrate these distinct advantages into multifunctional platforms. For example, Li et al. designed a PVA hydrogel reinforced with MXene nanosheets and polyaniline nanoparticles (PANI NPs). MXene was cross-linked with PVA via hydrogen bonding, while PANI was polymerized in situ within the PVA matrix via chemical cross-linking. The hybridization of these two mechanisms yielded a highly tough and stretchable conductive hydrogel [46]. However, exceeding an optimal threshold of MXene and PANI content induced nanoparticle aggregation, leading to uneven filler distribution and the subsequent deterioration of both electrical and mechanical properties.

To mitigate nanoparticle agglomeration, researchers have actively explored the surface modification of conductive nanomaterials to enhance their interfacial compatibility with hydrogel matrices. Modified nanofillers can form covalent or strong non-covalent bonds with polymer chains, significantly improving dispersion and interfacial adhesion [47,48]. For instance, Luo et al. incorporated polydopamine (PDA)-modified black phosphorus (BP) nanosheets into a GelMA hydrogel matrix to engineer a biodegradable conductive hydrogel. The PDA coating supplied abundant catechol and amine groups, enabling robust hydrogen bonding with GelMA during photopolymerization. This modification strengthened the BP-GelMA interface, resulting in uniform nanoparticle distribution and improved mechanical toughness [49]. Similarly, Lu et al. fabricated a hydrogel reinforced with dopamine-doped polypyrrole (PPy) nanofibers via in situ polymerization. Dopamine improved the hydrophilicity of PPy, promoting the homogeneous dispersion of nanofibers within the hydrophilic polymer network. Benefiting from the synergistic reinforcement of conductive nanofibers and non-covalent matrix interactions, the resulting hydrogel exhibited high electrical conductivity and excellent tensile strength [50]. Furthermore, functionalized carbon nanomaterials can act as both conductive fillers and structural reinforcements. When carboxyl-functionalized multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT-COOH) were integrated into a chitosan (CS) network cross-linked by zinc phthalocyanine tetraaldehyde (ZnPcTa), multiple cross-linking mechanisms occurred simultaneously. These included dynamic Schiff base bonds (ZnPcTa and CS), electrostatic interactions (MWCNT-COOH and CS), and covalent bonding between amino (-NH2) and carboxyl (-COOH) groups. This complex multi-crosslinking network endowed the hydrogel with enhanced mechanical strength, stable electrical conductivity, and remarkable self-healing ability, alongside excellent in vitro biocompatibility (cell viability >94%) [51].

Despite these structural advancements, the aforementioned multi-crosslinked networks often suffer from excessive swelling when immersed in high-water-content physiological environments (e.g., cerebrospinal and interstitial fluids), which can irreversibly disrupt continuous conductive pathways. To address this critical limitation, advanced structural design strategies have been developed to sustain electrical stability under severe swelling conditions [52]. One effective approach is the construction of a triple-crosslinking system that integrates covalent bonds, dynamic coordination bonds, and hydrogen bonds. This configuration restricts excessive water uptake while preserving the tissue-mimetic soft mechanics essential for neural interfaces. For example, Yang's group reported an injectable, conductive, adhesive, and anti-swelling hydrogel (termed ICAA). Tannic acid was introduced to construct a continuous MXene/PEDOT:PSS conductive network, while reinforced covalent cross-links were engineered to suppress fluid-triggered swelling, effectively preventing the separation of conductive fillers upon water infiltration. Following implantation in a peripheral nerve defect model, the hydrogel maintained intact conductive pathways even under prolonged fluid immersion. This sustained low impedance and efficient electrical signal transmission successfully overcame the sharp drop in conductivity typically induced by physiological swelling [53].

Conductive nanomaterials for hydrogel fabrication

In recent years, conductive nanocomposite hydrogels have shown remarkable potential in the treatment of diverse neural injuries. The conductive nanomaterials incorporated into these advanced scaffolds primarily include carbon-based nanomaterials, phosphorus-based nanomaterials, metal and metal oxide nanoparticles, 2D MXene nanosheets, conductive polymers, and their hybrid systems [54].

Carbon-based conductive nanocomposite hydrogel

Carbon-based nanomaterials, particularly graphene and carbon nanotubes (CNTs), are ideal conductive nanofillers owing to their exceptional electrical conductivity, large specific surface area, and rich surface functionalities [55,56].

Graphene, a 2D planar arrangement of carbon atoms in a hexagonal lattice [57], exhibits exceptional optical, mechanical, and thermal properties, alongside an inherent electrical conductivity of104-106 S/cm [[58], [59], [60]]. With a surface area twice that of carbon nanotubes, graphene enables highly efficient molecular adsorption, making it an excellent vehicle for drug delivery [61,62]. Graphene-based hydrogel composites are typically prepared via physical blending, chemical grafting, or in situ polymerization. These approaches successfully integrate the robust physicochemical properties of graphene with the flexibility and biocompatibility of hydrogels. Within the polymer network, 2D graphene sheets function as physical cross-linking nodes and stress-transfer pathways, which dissipate applied stress and prevent network failure from stress concentration [63]. For example, sodium alginate hydrogel doped with graphene quantum dots (GQDs) exhibited a 200% increase in compressive strength and maintained over 90% shape recovery after the cyclic compression test (100 cycles), showing excellent anti-fatigue performance [64]. Furthermore, combining highly conductive graphene with porous hydrogel architectures yields promising platforms for flexible sensors, biosignal monitoring, and neural tissue scaffolds. Their electro-responsiveness to strain, temperature, and humidity facilitates precise environmental sensing and feedback control.

However, the inherent hydrophobicity of pristine graphene often induces aggregation during polymer cross-linking, which severely compromises the composite's electrical conductivity. Consequently, surface modification is essential. Functionalizing the graphene surface, typically via oxidation, not only diminishes cytotoxicity but also strengthens the interfacial cross-linking between graphene and polymer chains [65]. Thus, graphene oxide (GO) and reduced graphene oxide (rGO) serve as widely adopted alternatives alternatives [66]. Integrating these modified graphene derivatives into hydrogel networks combines the distinct advantages of both phases, demonstrating significant potential for neural regeneration [67]. In aqueous physiological environments, exfoliated GO nanosheets display abundant negative surface charges and excellent charge-storage capacity, which significantly promotes the adhesion, proliferation, and neuronal differentiation of PC12 cells [68]. Alternatively, rGO obtained by chemical reduction of GO, possesses bipolar charge characteristics, abundant electron density, and high tunability, providing higher electrical conductivity compared with GO [69]. Park et al. polymerized rGO with GelMA and subsequently chemically reduced it to obtain r(GO/GelMA). The uniform dispersion of rGO within the GelMA matrix resulted in a higher electrical conductivity (8.7 ± 1.6 mS/cm) compared with GelMA alone (1.1 ± 0.1 mS/cm). Graphene-based hydrogels also exhibit robust capabilities in smart drug delivery. Omidi et al. engineered a pH-responsive hydrogel comprising chitosan, aminated graphene, and aminated nanowhiskers, cross-linked via dynamic Schiff base bonds. Under acidic conditions, the weakened dynamic linkages and heightened electrostatic repulsion triggered accelerated drug release [70]. In addition, the remarkable photothermal conversion performance of graphene (over 90% for NIR light) enables remote control of hydrogel swelling and drug release. Liu et al. coated mesoporous polydopamine (MPDA) nanoparticles with GO (MPDA@GO), loaded them with tetracycline hydrochloride (TH), and physically cross-linked them into a cellulose nanofibril (CNF) hydrogel. Upon NIR irradiation, MPDA@GO efficiently converted light into localized heat, accelerating the release of the encapsulated TH [71]. Despite these advances, optimizing graphene-based hydrogels remains challenging. Achieving homogeneous dispersion within the hydrogel matrix is critical, as localized aggregation leads to performance variability. Moreover, the complex interfacial interactions between graphene and polymer backbones require advanced analytical elucidation. Finally, an intrinsic trade-off exists between mechanical ductility and electrical conductivity: increasing the graphene fraction enhances conductivity but severely compromises stretchability. For example, when the rGO content in an rGO/PVA hydrogel exceeds 5 wt%, the tensile strain plummets from 1500% to 300%, markedly increasing material brittlenesss [72].

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are 1D cylindrical carbon allotropes resembling rolled graphene sheets, boasting intrinsic electrical conductivities up to 1.1 × 107 S/cm [73,74]. Compared to graphene, CNTs possess a superior elastic modulus, enabling them to simultaneously enhance both the electrical conductivity and mechanical toughness of hydrogel matrices [[75], [76], [77]]. Based on the number of concentric walls, CNTs are classified into single-walled (SWCNTs) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) [78]. SWCNTs (0.4-2 nm in diameter) exhibits exceptional electronic and mechanical properties due to the absence of interlayer electron scattering. Conversely, MWCNTs (2-100 nm in diameter) offer greater mechanical stability, albeit with slightly lower conductivity [79].

When uniformly dispersed, CNTs overlap to form continuous electron-transport networks within hydrogels, effectively transducing electrical signals, regulating cellular behavior, and providing structural reinforcement. For example, Yao et al. fabricated directionally conductive CNT/GelMA hydrogel fibers using a rotating liquid-bath electrospinning technique to mimic the aligned architecture, conductivity, and stiffness of natural nerve axons. Under electrical stimulation, this biomimetic scaffold significantly enhanced the proliferation, adhesion, and differentiation of neural stem cells (NSCs) and PC12 cells, ultimately improving motor recovery in a rat model. At an optimal CNT concentration (0.5 mg/mL), the composite achieved a physiologically relevant conductivity of 9.23 × 10−5 S/cm [80]. However, higher CNT contents (>5 wt%) often induce aggregation driven by strong van der Waals forces, compromising material uniformity. To overcome this, surface functionalization is routinely employed. Strategies include covalent oxidation (introducing -COOH and -OH groups) or non-covalent modification via π-π stacking. These modifications improve dispersion, mitigate cytotoxicity, and introduce anchoring sites for bioactive molecules [81]. For instance, Ravanbakhsh et al. developed a hydrogel incorporating carboxyl-functionalized MWCNTs (COOH-CNTs). The abundant surface -COOH groups acted as focal adhesion sites, promoting cellular recruitment and migration, thereby serving as an effective bioactive matrix for neuronal growth [82]. Similarly, Ye et al. utilized an ionic liquid medium to achieve homogeneous CNT dispersion without pre-functionalization. The incorporated CNTs increased the nanoscale surface roughness of the hydrogel, enhancing extracellular matrix (ECM) protein adsorption. This structurally modulated microenvironment promoted the maturation of primary hippocampal neurons and the neuronal differentiation of NSCs [83].

Furthermore, CNT-based hydrogels show immense promise as stimuli-responsive drug delivery systems [[84], [85], [86]]. Incorporating CNTs effectively overcomes the limited sensitivity and mechanical fragility of traditional smart hydrogels, improving targeted delivery and drug bioactivity [87]. Lin et al. synthesized a thermo-responsive micro-nano hybrid hydrogel by copolymerizing diethylacrylamide and N-hydroxymethylacrylamide with carboxylated chitosan-modified CNTs (CNT/CCS). Temperature variations triggered site-specific drug release, while tuning the CNT/CCS ratio allowed precise modulation of the release kinetics. Additionally, the CNT/CCS complex significantly reinforced the fracture resistance and compressive strength of the matrix [88].

Despite these advantages, the clinical translation of CNT-based hydrogels faces three primary hurdles: Dispersion limitations. Due to intense van der Waals interactions (especially in SWCNTs), achieving macroscopic homogeneity remains challenging, leading to batch-to-batch performance variability. Prohibitive costs. The fabrication of high-purity SWCNTs is highly expensive. Biosafety concerns. The nanoscale dimensions of CNTs pose penetration risks through respiratory or dermal routes, and their non-biodegradable nature raises concerns regarding long-term cytotoxic accumulation post-implantation.

Phosphorus-based conductive nanocomposite hydrogel

Phosphorus (P) is an essential element and abundant in living organisms [89], offers distinct biomedical advantages. Phosphorus-based nanomaterials degrade naturally in physiological environments, preventing chronic tissue accumulation. While carbon-based fillers primarily rely on electronic conduction, phosphorus-based hydrogels often synergize with the ionic transport mechanisms of the native biological environment (mediated by Na+, K+, and PO43−). Although their absolute conductivity (typically 10−4-1 S/m) is lower than purely electronic systems, this ionic compatibility minimizes risks such as excessive localized currents, making them highly suitable for neural interfacing [90].

Black phosphorus (BP), the most stable allotrope of phosphorus, features a 2D puckered lattice held by van der Waals forces [[91], [92], [93]]. BP exhibits excellent optoelectronic properties, mechanical flexibility, and biocompatibility, finding applications in drug delivery, photothermal therapy, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging [[94], [95], [96], [97]]. Recent studies also highlight BP's ability to promote NSC differentiation [98,99]. Liu et al. integrated tannic acid-encapsulated BP nanosheets into a lipoic acid-modified chitosan matrix, co-delivering a cyclodextrin-conjugated tazarotene drug to form an injectable hydrogel. The controlled degradation of BP and sustained release of phosphate ions promoted vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secretion and angiogenesis. Concurrently, the hydrogel facilitated efficient nerve signal transmission and supported neural cell adhesion [100]. Wu et al. designed a bioelectric coupling system featuring an in situ power-generating CS/gelatin/BP conductive hydrogel as the receiving electrode. The incorporation of biodegradable BP increased the hydrogel's conductivity to 0.289 S/m. In a complete T9 spinal cord transection model, this capacitive coupling system significantly promoted neurotrophic factor secretion, driving motor and urinary function recovery [101].

Importantly, excessive ROS production following SCI induces oxidative stress, cellular apoptosis, and chronic inflammation [102]. Leveraging its high inherent redox activity, BP acts as a potent ROS scavenger [[103], [104], [105]]. Zhang et al. synthesized a self-healing hydrogel by combining BP, glycyrrhizic acid (GA), and methacrylated silk fibroin (SF). Intracellular ROS assays (DCFH-DA staining) confirmed that the SF/BP/GA hydrogel effectively scavenged excessive reactive nitrogen species (RNS) and ROS within the SCI microenvironment, restoring redox homeostasis and shielding neural cells from oxidative damage [106].

Beyond BP, other 2D phosphorus materials like germanium phosphide (GeP) offer superior thermodynamic stability and tunable bandgaps [107]. Xu et al. integrated polydopamine-coated GeP (GeP@PDA) nanosheets into a hyaluronic acid network. The GeP@PDA elevated the hydrogel's conductivity from 0.054 S/m to 0.365 S/m. In vivo evaluations demonstrated accelerated neural recovery and modulated inflammation, predominantly attributed to GeP's robust ROS-scavenging capabilities [108]. Similarly, 2D silicon phosphide (SiP) exhibits excellent biodegradability, with its degradation releasing silicon ions that further stimulate angiogenesis [109]. Xu et al. incorporated polyphenol-modified SiP (SiP@PDA) into a GelMA/ECM network. The uniform dispersion of SiP@PDA provided sustained Si and P ion release, increased conductivity (by ∼0.25 S/m), and activated angiogenic pathways via the upregulation of HIF-1α, VEGF, and bFGF. Metal and metal oxide-based conductive nanocomposite hydrogels

Metal-based nanomaterials, encompassing metal nanoparticles, metal oxide nanoparticles, and metal nanowires (NWs), serve as premier conductive fillers in hydrogels due to their exceptional electrical conductivity, high specific surface area, and robust surface energy [111]. Integrating these rigid metallic structures into hydrogel networks not only enhances conductivity and mechanical strength but also imparts essential flexibility and ductility [112,113]. Beyond electro-mechanical enhancements, metal-based nanocomposites introduce diverse multifunctionality, including antibacterial, magnetic, and photothermal properties. For example, silver nanowires (AgNWs) confer antimicrobial resistance, Fe3O4 nanoparticles enable magneto-actuation, and gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) provide photothermal capabilities. This "conductivity-functionality" integration significantly broadens their biomedical applicability. For instance, an AgNP/chitosan (CS) hydrogel achieved a conductivity of 50 S/m, effectively promoting wound healing via electrical stimulation. Under a mild 0.05-0.1 V stimulus, fibroblast proliferation increased by 50%, while the sustained release of Ag+ provided localized antibacterial efficacy suitable for infected wound management.

AgNPs and AgNWs have emerged as core components in functional biomaterials owing to their remarkable conductivity (up to 6.3 × 107 S/m for AgNWs), broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, and chemical stability. The high aspect ratio (100-1000) of AgNWs enables the formation of a flexible, continuous percolating network that remains stable under extreme deformation. For example, an AgNW/PNIPAM hydrogel endured 1500% tensile strain and 180° bending while maintaining a high conductivity of 103 S/m, with only a 10% conductivity drop after 1000 stretch-recovery cycles [114]. The antibacterial mechanism of silver is dual-modal: biochemically, released Ag+ penetrates bacterial membranes, binds to DNA to halt replication, and generates destructive ROS; physically, the sharp ends of AgNWs puncture bacterial cell membranes. Consequently, AgNW/CS hydrogels demonstrate >99% antibacterial efficacy against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, sustaining this protection for up to 7 days [115].

AuNPs are highly regarded for their biocompatibility, tunable surface plasmon resonance (SPR) optical properties, and facile surface functionalization. Although possessing moderate conductivity (10−2-1 S/m) when dispersed in polymer matrices, AuNP-integrated hydrogels are highly effective as electrically-stimulated scaffolds for nerve and bone regeneration. In an AuNP/gelatin scaffold, electrical stimulation (0.05-0.1 V) facilitated uniform signal propagation, promoting robust axonal outgrowth from NSCs [116]. Furthermore, the physical and electrical cues provided by AuNPs can activate intracellular signaling cascades (e.g., PI3K/Akt), promoting the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells; notably, mineralized nodule formation tripled compared to pure gelatin scaffolds. AuNP-based hydrogels are also widely utilized in flexible biosensors. For instance, an AuNP/glucose oxidase (GOx)/PVA hydrogel sensor monitored glucose concentrations (0.1-20 mmol/L) by detecting conductivity changes (from 0.1 to 0.02 S/m) triggered by H2O2 generated during glucose oxidation [117]. Similarly, an epidermal AuNP/gelatin hydrogel accurately transduced pulse fluctuations into electrical signals with high precision (0.1 mmHg) and rapid response (<100 ms) [118].

Iron, a vital transition metal in the brain, is essential for neurotransmitter synthesis [119]. Iron-based nanomaterials, particularly superparamagnetic Fe3O4, enable unique magnetoelectric coupling within hydrogels. Zhang et al. developed HA/collagen hydrogels embedded with core-shell Fe3O4@BaTiO3 nanoparticles. Under a remote magnetic field, these composites generated localized electrical fields via magnetoelectric effects, successfully stimulating neural activity without invasive electrodes. Enhanced axonal regeneration and myelination were confirmed via MBP and SYN1 immunostaining. This wireless stimulation significantly improved motor recovery in SCI rats, establishing a robust platform for non-invasive physical neuromodulation [7]. Fe3O4 systems also facilitate magnetically guided theranostics. Paclitaxel-loaded Fe3O4/gelatin hydrogels achieved 92% tumor inhibition under magnetic targeting, while the superparamagnetism of Fe3O4 enabled real-time magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) tracking [120]. Additionally, the photothermal effect of Fe3O4 generates localized heat (42-45 °C) under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation. Liu et al. reported an Fe3O4/PVA hydrogel that eradicated 62.7% of tumor cells via NIR-triggered photothermal therapy [121]. In another study, NIR irradiation of Fe3O4-loaded GelMA microspheres triggered the rapid on-demand release of curcumin, effectively suppressing local pro-inflammatory microenvironments [122].

Beyond nanoparticles, metal salt ions (e.g., Na+, K+, Zn2+, Mg2+) endow hydrogels with intrinsic ionic conductivity via ion migration through the polymer network [123]. These ionic hydrogels typically exhibit conductivities of 0.1-10 S/m while preserving high water content, transparency, and flexibility [124]. As trace elements essential to human physiology, these ions exhibit minimal cytotoxicity. For example, a PVA hydrogel containing 0.5 mol/L NaCl maintained >95% skin cell viability after 48 h with no obvious inflammation, whereas Zn2+-containing hydrogels actively promoted cell proliferation, making them ideal for epidermal sensors or implants [125]. Magnesium-based ionic hydrogels are particularly relevant for SCI due to the neuroprotective effects of Mg2+ [126]. SCI often induces acute Mg2+ depletion, triggering tachykinin release, neuronal edema, and apoptosis. Cheng et al. engineered an injectable silk fibroin (SF) hydrogel cross-linked with MgMn-layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanosheets via hydrogen bonding. The incorporated Mn3+ catalyzed the decomposition of ROS into oxygen, alleviating hypoxia and oxidative stress, while the sustained release of Mg2+ restored local ionic homeostasis, facilitating neuronal survival and nerve regeneration (Fig. 5) [127]. Despite these advantages, metal-based nanocomposite hydrogels face translational challenges. Metals and metal oxides are susceptible to oxidation, aggregation, and uncontrolled ion leaching, which can induce dose-dependent cytotoxicity (e.g., fibroblast viability drops below 80% when Ag+ concentrations exceed 10 μg/mL [129]. Mitigation strategies include engineering core-shell nanostructures (e.g., AgNPs@TiO2) to physically isolate the conductive cores from oxidative physiological fluids, incorporating antioxidants into the polymer matrix, restricting nanoparticle sizes (<10 nm) to modulate degradation, and applying biocompatible surface coatings (e.g., CS or PEG) to improve tissue integration.

MXene-based conductive nanocomposite hydrogels

MXenes are an emerging class of 2D transition metal carbides, nitrides, or carbonitrides with the general formula Mn+1XnTx (n = 1-4), where M represents an early transition metal (e.g., Ti, V, Mo), X is carbon and/or nitrogen, and Tx denotes surface termination groups (such as -OH, -O, or -F) [130]. Possessing a graphene-like layered architecture, MXenes boast exceptional electrical conductivity (up to 2.4 × 104 S/cm), vast specific surface areas, and highly tunable surface chemistry [[131], [132], [133]]. Studies have shown that MXene can be completely degraded in rats without adverse effects, indicating excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability [134]. Crucially, unlike graphene, which relies exclusively on covalent bonding, the MXene lattice integrates covalent, metallic, and ionic bonds. This unique structural motif, combined with abundant surface functional groups, provides versatile active sites for chemical modification and the anchoring of therapeutic molecules or nanoparticles [135]. Integrating MXenes into hydrogel networks not only augments electro-mechanical properties but also introduces multifunctional capabilities such as photothermal conversion, biosensing, and bioelectric stimulation [136]. For example, Zhang et al. engineered an MXene/silk fibroin (SF) hydrogel scaffold that effectively induced NSC differentiation, promoted axonal regeneration, and facilitated motor function recovery following brain injury. Compared to carbon-based fillers like graphene or CNTs, MXenes exhibit superior hydrophilicity and dispersibility in aqueous systems due to their abundant polar surface terminations [138]. Cai et al. designed a micro-grooved GelMA/MXene hydrogel to direct aligned neuronal growth. At an optimal concentration of 200 μg/mL, MXene significantly enhanced NSC proliferation and differentiation without inducing cytotoxicity (up to 500 μg/mL). Thanks to MXene's intrinsic metallic conductivity, adding merely 1%-3% filler achieved an impressive hydrogel conductivity of 50-100 S/m-a benchmark that would require >5% loading for graphene. This low percolation threshold preserves the hydrogel's mechanical flexibility and prevents filler-induced brittleness [139]. Furthermore, MXene-based hydrogels excel as biosensors owing to their rapid electrical responsiveness and mechanical tissue-compliance [29,140]. They can sensitively transduce physiological fluctuations into measurable electrical signals, showing great potential for monitoring neurotransmitters and diagnosing neurological disorders [141]. In addition, MXene exhibits excellent photothermal conversion capability [142], and this property has been widely applied in the biomedical applications [143]. Leveraging this, Dong et al. incorporated MXene into a low-melting-point agarose hydrogel, creating a near-infrared (NIR)-responsive platform for on-demand, localized drug delivery [144].

Regarding in vivo safety, MXenes exhibit distinct toxicological, metabolic, and clearance profiles compared to CNTs and graphene. Unmodified carbon nanomaterials degrade sluggishly, and their chronic retention in neural tissues often provokes sustained inflammation. In contrast, MXenes demonstrate tunable degradability and significantly reduced neurotoxicity. Conductive polymer-based conductive nanocomposite hydrogels

Conductive polymers (CPs) are organic semiconductors that merge the electrical and optical properties of metals with the inherent processability and surface functionalization capabilities of polymers [161]. Incorporating CPs into hydrogels offers a compelling alternative to carbon or metallic fillers for enhancing conductivity [165]. CPs are generally cost-effective, biocompatible, and allow for precise tuning of their electrical properties via surface modifications and ionic doping. While bulk CPs often suffer from inherent brittleness that compromises hydrogel mechanics [166], formulating them into nanoparticles (10-100 nm) via emulsion or oxidative polymerization effectively circumvents this limitation. Commonly utilized CP nanoparticles (CP NPs) for neural repair include polypyrrole (PPy NPs), polyaniline (PANI NPs), polythiophene (PTh NPs), and PEDOT NPs [167]. These nano-CPs provide tunable conductivity (10−2-103 S/m), improved environmental stability, and multi-stimuli responsiveness (e.g., pH, thermal, and optical), making them versatile conductive units.

Polypyrrole nanoparticles (PPy NPs)

PPy is extensively explored in biomedicine due to its high conductivity, reversible redox activity, ease of synthesis, and excellent biocompatibility [162]. PPy NPs prepared by chemical oxidative polymerization reach 10−1-102 S/cm [163,168]. Wu et al. fabricated cell-laden collagen microfibers incorporated with PPy NPs using a coaxial microfluidic device, ensuring uniform NP dispersion. At a PPy concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, the hydrogel achieved a tissue-matching conductivity of 0.22 S/m. This conductive network facilitated intracellular electrical communication, significantly enhancing PC12 cell elongation. Furthermore, applied electrical stimulation synergistically upregulated neurogenesis-related proteins and calcium channel expression, accelerating nerve regeneration (Fig. 7) [169]. Beyond scaffolding, PPy-based hydrogels serve as highly sensitive strain sensors for neural monitoring [170]. Liang et al. polymerized pyrrole in situ on microgels to create PPy-modified cross-linkers, resulting in a transparent, stretchable, interpenetrating network hydrogel. As a brain-implantable platform, its optimized optical transparency minimized light scattering for optogenetic applications, while simultaneously enabling precise neuromodulation and signal recording [171].Fig. 7Schematic illustration of (a) the synthesis of PPy NPs in an aqueous dispersion and (b) the construction of cell-laden, PPy-incorporated collagen hydrogel microfibers. Moreover, while traditional hydrogels typically suffer from rapid burst release of hydrophilic drugs [172], CP-based nanocomposites offer precise, stimuli-responsive drug delivery [173]. Conductive nanohydrogels can control drug delivery through electrical stimulation [174]. For instance, Gan et al. developed a tough, conductive PPy-PAM/CS hydrogel loaded with the anionic drug dexamethasone (doped within the PPy chains). Upon the application of a negative electrical potential, the resulting change in the polymer's charge state triggered the controlled, on-demand release of the drug [175].

Polyaniline nanoparticles (PANI NPs)

PANI consists of alternating benzene and quinone rings and, unlike pristine PPy, can be engineered for water solubility. With rational structural design, PANI exhibits tunable electrical conductivities (10−2 to 1 S/cm), providing excellent electroactivity for tissue engineering [176]. Recently, extensive research has focused on nanostructured PANI [177,178], which is typically synthesized via the in situ oxidative polymerization of aniline in an aqueous medium using ammonium persulfate (APS) as the oxidant under acidic conditions [179]. Guarino et al. prepared a macroporous, conductive hydrogel by in situ precipitation of PANI within a polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) solution, followed by UV cross-linking. The incorporation of PANI increased the overall electrical conductivity to (1.1 ± 0.5) × 10−3 mS/cm, while uniformly distributed hydrophilic PANI NP domains boosted localized proton conductivity to (7 ± 0.3) mS/cm. This unique microenvironment successfully stimulated neurite outgrowth and improved the biological responses of PC12 and human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) [180].

To overcome PANI's inherent limitations of poor solubility and non-degradability, it is frequently grafted onto natural polymers [181]. For example, Deng et al. coupled PANI with carboxymethyl chitosan to form a double-layer composite hydrogel nerve conduit, cross-linked via a Schiff base reaction with aldehyde-modified Pluronic F-127 and loaded with 7,8-dihydroxyflavone (DHF). Providing a slow, sustained release of DHF and a maximum conductivity of 0.337 ± 0.036 mS/cm, the conduit effectively promoted peripheral nerve axon regeneration [182]. Beyond electro-activity, PANI NPs offer functional detoxification capabilities. A PANI NP/sodium alginate hydrogel demonstrated high adsorption capacities for heavy metal ions Pb2+ (350 mg/g) and Cr6+ (280 mg/g) via amino coordination, while its intrinsic conductivity further enhanced adsorption efficiency through electro-adsorption mechanisms [183].

Because CP nanoparticles tend to agglomerate due to high surface energy, surface modifications using hydrophilic groups or steric-hindrance layers are essential. For instance, sodium dodecylbenzenesulfonate (DBSA)-modified PANI NPs disperse uniformly within a polyacrylamide (PAM) precursor. At a 5% NP loading, the composite hydrogel exhibited no obvious agglomeration and achieved a conductivity of 15 S/m [184]. Conversely, incorporating >3% unmodified PANI results in severe aggregation, disrupting the conductive network and causing a 50% drop in overall conductivity.

Poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonate nanoparticles (PEDOT: PSS NPs)

PEDOT, polymerized from the 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT) monomer, features a small bandgap and exceptionally high conductivity. It is most commonly processed as PEDOT:PSS [185], where the polyanion polystyrene sulfonate (PSS) stabilizes its dispersion in aqueous solutions [186]. Unlike PPy and PANI, which primarily conduct via electrons/holes, PEDOT: PSS is an archetypal organic mixed ion-electron conductor (OMIEC). This conjugated polymer/polyelectrolyte blend couples both electronic and ionic conductivity, reaching values of 102-103 S/cm [164,187].

Following SCI, the accumulation of microglia, macrophages, and oligodendrocyte precursor cells around the lesion forms a glial scar, creating a persistently inflammatory microenvironment that impedes axonal regeneration [188]. Conductive PEDOT:PSS has been shown to counteract this by promoting cell adhesion, proliferation, and neuronal differentiation [189]. Wang et al. developed a self-assembled, bioactive ECM/PEDOT: PSS/oxidized-starch conductive hydrogel bandage loaded with indole-3-propionic acid (IPA). This hydrogel achieved conformal contact with injured nerves, promoted NSC differentiation and Schwann cell migration, and modulated neutrophil chemotaxis to accelerate peripheral nerve repair [19].

However, the insulating nature of the PSS component caps the ultimate conductivity of the complex. Furthermore, PSS possesses potential cytotoxicity, lacks specific bioactivity, and exhibits suboptimal long-term biocompatibility [190]. To circumvent this, Serafin et al. synthesized pure PEDOT nanoparticles via microemulsion oxidative polymerization, incorporating them into gelatin/HA scaffolds for SCI repair. The uniform dispersion of PEDOT NPs enhanced both the conductivity and self-recovery of the hydrogel. In vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrated non-cytotoxicity, reduced microglial/macrophage infiltration in SCI rat models, and facilitated axonal regeneration [191].

To completely eliminate toxic free PSS, researchers have explored natural degradable polyanions as dopants. Sodium alginate (SA), an algae-derived polyanion, serves as an excellent substitute. Homogeneous, PSS-free PEDOT/SA conductive hydrogels can be fabricated via in situ electrochemical gelation. The abundant hydrophilic carboxyl groups on SA form a dense hydration layer that significantly inhibits non-specific protein adsorption (e.g., from serum or sweat), endowing the hydrogel with outstanding anti-biofouling performance while surpassing conventional PEDOT: PSS in sensing sensitivity. Similarly, Montazerian et al. reported a biodegradable sulfonated alginate-doped PEDOT system (PEDOT: AlgS). The glycosidic bonds within the alginate backbone enable the gradual hydrolytic degradation of the conductive composite. Subcutaneous implantations confirmed that PEDOT: AlgS avoids dense fibrous encapsulation, allowing immune cells to infiltrate and mediate material degradation [192]. Alternatively, in situ polymerization of EDOT onto tannic acid-modified MXene templates offers another low-toxicity, PSS-free route. Tannic acid, a natural polyphenol, modifies 2D MXene nanosheets via coordination bonds, providing abundant adsorption sites for EDOT monomers. This facilitates uniform in situ growth, yielding multi-scale MXene-PEDOT conductive nanosheets with excellent biocompatibility [193].

Despite these advances, CP-based hydrogels face translational challenges. Unreacted monomers, residual oxidants (e.g., APS), and certain CPs (like PANI) can exhibit dose-dependent cytotoxicity; for example, cell viability drops below 80% when APS concentrations exceed 0.1 mmol/L. Additionally, the high specific surface energy of CP nanoparticles drives agglomeration, which not only disrupts the percolating conductive network but also degrades the mechanical stability of the hydrogel.

Others

Single-component conductive nanoparticles often possess inherent trade-offs. For example, metal nanoparticles offer high conductivity but are prone to oxidation; carbon-based nanomaterials provide superior mechanical reinforcement but lack optimal biocompatibility; and CP nanoparticles offer multi-responsiveness but exhibit lower conductivity limits. By exploiting synergistic effects within hybrid systems, researchers can simultaneously optimize conductivity, mechanical toughness, and functional diversity, while preserving the hydrogel's high water content and flexibility.

In metal-carbon hybrids, metal nanoparticles bridge the gaps between carbon sheets/tubes, forming a highly stable, continuous conductive network. In an AgNP/MXene hydrogel, MXene sheets form the macroscopic framework while AgNPs act as conductive nodes, increasing conductivity from 80 S/m to 200 S/m. This hybrid maintained its conductivity with only a 5% attenuation after 1000 bending cycles, vastly outperforming purely AgNP-based hydrogels [183]. Similarly, carbon-CP hybrids synergize structural and functional properties. Rigid carbon nanomaterials act as a structural skeleton, dramatically improving tensile and compressive strength. For instance, via a "rigid-flexible" intertwined hydrogen-bonded network, an MXene/PPy NP hydrogel achieved a tensile strength of 2.8 MPa (9 times that of pure PPy) and a fracture energy of 18 kJ/m2 [194]. Furthermore, the high surface area of carbon nanofillers amplifies the CP's responsiveness to external stimuli. In a graphene/PANI NP hydrogel, pH sensitivity was enhanced by 2.5 times compared to pure PANI, enabling precise pH monitoring (range 2-10) with a detection limit of 0.01 units.

Beyond intrinsically conductive materials, piezoelectric materials which generate electrical surface charges in response to mechanical deformation, offer a self-powered strategy for delivering electro-mechanical cues in neural repair. Piezoelectric polymers and ceramics, such as polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA), zinc oxide (ZnO), and barium titanate (BaTiO3, BTO), are widely investigated [19]. Owing to their high surface-to-volume ratio, significant anisotropy, and excellent biological and mechanical properties, piezoelectric nanomaterials can deliver both mechanical and electrical stimulation to support tissue regeneration [195]. Moreover, they provide mechanical and electrical stimulation for tissue regeneration and can convert various forms of energy, including mechanical, thermal, electrical, and light, into bioelectrically relevant signals. When integrated into hydrogels, piezoelectric nanomaterials can mimic ECM pathways and generate electrical signals to promote tissue regeneration, offering a promising strategy for the non-invasive treatment of neurological diseases. For example, under ultrasound stimulation, they can act as nanosensors. Although ultrasound has limited tissue penetration (tens of centimeters), this combination with piezoelectric nanoparticles can reach deeper [196].

PVDF is highly favored for its strong piezoelectric response, thermal stability, and flexibility [197], however, its non-degradability limits certain clinical applications. To mitigate this, Ma et al. developed a PVDF/poly(L-lactic acid-co-caprolactone) (PLCL) composite film wrapped around an SF/PEDOT aerogel, forming a multi-channel nerve conduit with dual piezoelectric and conductive properties. Exceeding 20 wt% PVDF notably enhanced neurite elongation, a biological effect that was further amplified by applied ultrasonic stimulation [198]. Conversely, BTO offers high piezoelectric coefficients and mechanical strength. Gao et al. incorporated core-shell dopamine-modified BaTiO3 nanoparticles into an anisotropic ovalbumin scaffold. The synergy of topographical cues and piezoelectric signals upregulated axon-growth genes, promoted local Ca2+ enrichment, and enhanced nerve fiber myelination [199].

Piezoelectric materials also enable real-time functional monitoring. Yi et al. engineered a wireless, self-powered sensor using a PLLA/BTO composite. When evaluated in a rat sciatic nerve injury model, the sensor transduced mechanical muscle forces into electrical signals that closely matched conventional electromyography (EMG) recordings, allowing reliable tracking of motor recovery [200]. Furthermore, materials like ZnO not only provide piezoelectric stimulation but also release bioactive Zn2+ ions, which provide neuroprotection and modulate downstream pathways vital for neural development [201].

Ultimately, ensuring the biodegradability of these engineered scaffolds is paramount. Biodegradable conductive and piezoelectric hydrogels gradually break down as tissues heal, preventing chronic foreign-body reactions, mitigating fibrotic encapsulation (which can compress regenerating nerves), and eliminating the need for surgical removal [202]. Conductive hydrogels can gradually undergo biodegradation in vivo due to their water-absorption ability. The degradation behavior of these hydrogels plays an important role in SCI repair, as it not only provides sustained structural support but also modulates the controlled release of therapeutic agents. For example, a biodegradable conductive nerve conduit composed of molybdenum (Mo) particles incorporated into a biodegradable PCL matrix has been developed to accelerate nerve regeneration. This conduit has good biocompatibility and can gradually degrade within a certain period [203]. Tuning the degradation kinetics of these functional hydrogels remains a critical focus for advancing neural tissue engineering（see Table 2).

Applications in neurological diseases and nerve injuries

Nerve injuries, encompassing both the central (CNS) and peripheral nervous systems (PNS), disrupt neural conduction pathways, leading to severe deficits such as motor dysfunction and sensory loss. Effective nerve repair requires reconstructing conductive pathways, guiding directional neural cell migration, and providing an electroactive microenvironment. Conductive nanocomposite hydrogels, with their tunable conductivity, biomimetic mechanics, and excellent biocompatibility, seamlessly integrate "physical support, electrophysiological regulation, and cellular guidance." This makes them premier candidates for neural tissue engineering. The following sections outline the pathological mechanisms of various CNS disorders and review the specific applications of diverse conductive hydrogels in nerve regeneration.

Central nervous system (CNS) repair

Spinal cord injury

SCI arises from direct or indirect trauma to the spinal cord, causing profound sensory, motor, and autonomic dysfunction below the lesion (Fig. 8) [204]. Pathophysiologically, SCI involves a primary mechanical insult followed by a devastating secondary injury cascade characterized by vascular disruption, ischemia, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, neuronal apoptosis, demyelination, and axonal degeneration [205]. Common in vivo SCI models include contusion, compression, and transection. The complete or partial transection models are particularly valuable for evaluating robust axonal regeneration and the therapeutic efficacy of biomaterial scaffolds [206].Fig. 8a) Schematic representation of the CNS injury. b) SCI model and corresponding treatment strategies. Functional spinal cord recovery hinges on reconstructing severed neural circuits. Chen et al. developed a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) hydrogel incorporated with graphene oxide (GO) and molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) for SCI repair. The synergistic MoS2/GO addition enhanced the hydrogel's electro-mechanical properties, while the hydrophilic groups of GO promoted NE-4 neural stem cell differentiation and aligned axonal growth [207]. Similarly, Liu et al. formulated an injectable hydrogel by embedding tannic acid-encapsulated black phosphorus (BP) nanosheets and a tazarotene/β-cyclodextrin inclusion complex into a lipoic acid-modified chitosan (CS) matrix. BP degradation released phosphorus, which, alongside tazarotene, significantly upregulated vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) secretion to accelerate angiogenesis. Furthermore, BP imparted vital conductivity for neural signal transmission while promoting cell adhesion and proliferation [100].

Because endogenous bioelectric activity is fundamental for maintaining neuronal function and driving axonal extension [[208], [209], [210]], combining conductive hydrogels with exogenous electrical stimulation represents a potent strategy for reconnecting severed pathways [211,212]. Kong et al. engineered a patterned, multifunctional hydrogel loaded with MXene-Au nanocomposites and NSCs to direct synaptic extension. MXene-Au integration yielded a conductivity of 0.57 S/m, while Au NPs effectively scavenged over 80% of local ROS, providing a neuroprotective, antibacterial environment. When coupled with electrical stimulation, this platform significantly elongated neuronal synapses, drove neurogenesis, and accelerated motor function recovery in SCI models [213] Furthermore, traumatic SCI frequently results in irregular cystic cavitation and glial scarring. Injectable conductive hydrogels can conformally fill these cavities. For instance, Yu et al. developed a self-healing PPM hydrogel using phytic acid (PA) to cross-link polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and MXene via hydrogen bonding. The incorporated MXene established an electroactive microenvironment that modulated cellular adhesion, promoted angiogenesis (likely via the MEK/ERK pathway), and supported neural regeneration [214].

Traumatic brain injury (TBI)

TBI is defined as brain damage caused by external mechanical forces, leading to temporary or permanent impairments in cognitive, physical, and psychosocial functions [215]. It is the third leading cause of death and neurological dysfunction globally and also results in long-term functional impairments that severely affect patients’ quality of life [216]. The pathophysiology comprises a primary mechanical injury and a secondary cascade unfolding over hours to years, involving blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, cerebral edema, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation [217]. Common animal models include weight-drop, controlled cortical impact (CCI), fluid percussion injury (FPI), and blast injury [218]. The Feeney weight-drop and CCI models are the most prevalent [[219], [220], [221]]. The weight-drop model uses a free-falling weight onto the dura mater, allowing for tunable injury severity [222]. Conversely, the CCI model employs a pneumatic or electromagnetic impactor to induce precise cortical damage, acute subdural hematoma, and BBB dysfunction, making it highly reproducible for studying localized neurological deficits and therapeutic interventions [223,224].

For TBI, injectable hydrogels are highly advantageous as they can bypass the BBB (if applied locally via injection) and conformally fill lesion cavities while matching the rheological properties of delicate brain tissue to minimize irritation [221]. Xu et al. developed an injectable, microporous conductive hydrogel containing microbeads coated with magnetized gold nanoyarn balls (GYBs). The interconnected pores facilitated cellular infiltration and vascularization. Under high-frequency magnetic stimulation, the GYBs generated localized eddy currents, enhancing calcium ion influx and boosting brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) production. Furthermore, combining hydrogel implantation with electrical stimulation significantly boosts post-TBI angiogenesis, delivering essential oxygen and nutrients to support neural circuit reconstruction [226]. However, the long-term biosafety of these implants requires critical scrutiny. As the hydrogel matrix degrades, the release of non-degradable conductive nanofillers can interact with the compromised BBB, risking chronic neuroinflammation. Gao et al. noted that while conductive nanomaterials like graphene offer excellent electrotherapy benefits, their persistent retention in vivo can trigger prolonged M1 microglial polarization. The resulting massive secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) exacerbates BBB leakage, cerebral edema, and glial scarring, potentially forming a vicious pathological cycle [227].

Stroke

Stroke is a leading global cause of death and long-term disability [228]. It is primarily classified into ischemic (arterial occlusion) and hemorrhagic subtypes, with ischemic stroke being the most prevalent [229]. The middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) model is the "gold standard" for ischemic stroke, utilizing an intraluminal filament to temporarily block blood flow, followed by withdrawal to simulate clinical reperfusion injury. Alternatively, the photochemical induction model utilizes an intravenously administered photosensitive dye (e.g., Rose Bengal) activated by targeted laser irradiation. This generates localized ROS, damaging endothelial cells and inducing focal thrombosis [230]. Hemorrhagic stroke encompasses intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) and subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). ICH models replicate hypertensive or aneurysmal ruptures via intra-striatal injection of either bacterial collagenase (which degrades vascular basement membranes) or autologous blood (which more accurately mimics clinical pathophysiological changes). SAH is frequently modeled using endovascular perforation [231].

ROS generation is a major contributor to cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury. Certain metal and metal oxide nanoparticles, such as platinum nanoparticles (Pt NPs) and cerium oxide nanoparticles, possess excellent chemical stability and high ROS-scavenging activity due to their free surface electrons. These nanoparticles are safe and biocompatible, negatively charged, and can be modified to carry therapeutic agents, making them promising candidates for antioxidant neuroprotection after stroke [232].

Stroke survivors frequently suffer from severe motor deficits requiring long-term, patient-driven rehabilitation. Recently, flexible bioelectronics and human-machine interfaces have emerged to monitor real-time muscle signals and enable interactive rehabilitation. Xu et al. designed a visual rehabilitation system utilizing a dome-patterned polyacrylamide-lithium chloride (PAAM-LiCl) ionic hydrogel integrated with conductive fabrics. Inspired by the ultra-sensitive integumentary sensory organs of crocodiles, the dome-shaped geometry amplified the tactile sensitivity of the wearable sensor. Neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs) and other neurological disorders

NDDs encompass a group of disorders characterized by the progressive, often irreversible loss of neuronal structure and function, culminating in neuronal death. These pathologies clinically manifest as cognitive decline, motor impairment, and sensory deficits. Common central NDDs include Alzheimer's Disease (AD), Parkinson's Disease (PD), Huntington's Disease (HD), and Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) [234]. The underlying pathological mechanisms typically involve protein misfolding and aggregation, disrupted signal transduction, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic neuroinflammation [235].

Multifunctional, conductive self-healing hydrogels can effectively mimic the mechanical and electrophysiological properties of neural tissue, facilitating signal transmission crucial for restoring neural activity in neurodegenerative brains [236]. Xu et al. developed an injectable, bioactive hydrogel by employing a tannic acid-modified AuNP cross-linker within a carboxymethyl chitosan network. The incorporated AuNPs provided intrinsic antioxidant and neuroprotective effects, while tannic acid imparted anti-inflammatory properties and enhanced biocompatibility. When injected into a 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA)-induced PD rat model, the hydrogel's dynamic cross-linking provided robust injectability, self-healing, and strong tissue adhesion. Fourteen days post-injection, the PD rats exhibited significant motor recovery, reduced inflammatory infiltration, attenuated astrocyte activation, and the preservation of dopaminergic neurons [237]. Furthermore, conductive nanoparticles can serve as efficient drug-delivery platforms. Liu et al. loaded the antioxidant dye methylene blue (MB) onto BP nanosheets and incorporated them into a thermosensitive injectable hydrogel. Intranasal administration of this nanocomposite in an AD animal model effectively inhibited tau phosphorylation, protected mitochondrial integrity, and alleviated neuroinflammation [238].

The abnormal protein aggregation characteristic of NDDs also serves as a critical diagnostic biomarker [239]. For instance, the Aβ(1-42) peptide is a primary component of senile plaques in AD. Abbasi et al. engineered an electrochemical immunosensor by conjugating anti-β-amyloid antibodies onto graphene-modified screen-printed electrodes (SPGEs). This highly conductive, large-surface-area platform enabled the sensitive detection of Aβ(1-42) in patient plasma, offering a low-cost tool for early AD screening. Beyond NDDs, conductive platforms are vital for managing other severe neurological disorders, such as epilepsy. Chen et al. reported a multifunctional deep-brain probe for integrated neural sensing, modulation, and treatment. Featuring a sodium alginate/MXene conductive core and a PLGA/MgO insulating shell, this hybrid probe allowed simultaneous electrophysiological recording, localized optical stimulation, and controlled drug delivery. Immunomodulatory molecular mechanisms of conductive hydrogels in CNS lesions

Following CNS injury, intricate inflammatory cascades are activated. Resident microglia and astrocytes become reactive, accompanied by a massive influx of peripheral immune cells (T cells, B cells, NK cells), collectively establishing an inhibitory microenvironment that impedes regeneration. Conductive hydrogels can remodel these CNS immune niches by providing endogenous physical regulatory cues via distinct signaling pathways. While ion signal transduction dominates electrical signal-mediated immunomodulation, the complete molecular network remains under active investigation. Bioelectric fields activate voltage-gated ion channels, modulating intra- and intercellular calcium transport to trigger downstream cascades. These bioelectrical cues activate repair-promoting pathways (e.g., TRPM7, STAT6, PI3K/Akt, and TGF-β/SMAD) while suppressing canonical pro-inflammatory cascades (MAPK/JNK, NF-κB), ultimately driving macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [241,242].

Recently, a silk fibroin/PEDOT:PSS (SF/PEDOT) hydrogel was shown to establish a capacitively coupled, wireless electrical stimulation system. In vitro assays demonstrated that this wireless stimulation facilitated the M2 polarization of human blood-derived macrophages (hBDMs) via calcium-dependent PI3K/Akt signaling. This downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and upregulated the M2 marker CD206 and anti-inflammatory IL-10. In overreactive astrocytes, electrical cues upregulated the gap junction protein Cx43, suppressed pro-inflammatory chemokines (CXCL10, GM-CSF), and reduced glial scar-associated inflammation [243]. Electrical stimulation also governs immune cell migration [244]. Under direct-current fields, T cells exhibit enhanced directional movement driven by cathode-directed shifts in membrane potential, calcium influx, and Erk1/2 and Akt pathway activation [245].

Beyond electrical cues, hydrogel topography regulates immune function [246]. Qian et al. demonstrated that spatial confinement dictates macrophage polarization: small pores restrain M1 inflammatory responses, whereas larger pores promote regenerative M2 phenotypes [247]. Additionally, degradable fillers (e.g., P-, Si-, Mg-, and Zn-based nanoparticles) sustainably release bioactive ions that exert profound, electricity-independent immunomodulatory effects. Sequential release of Ca2+ and Zn2+ precisely mediates M1-to-M2 switching via the TRPC1-STAT1/NF-κB and STAT6/PPARγ axes [248], while released Mg2+ specifically regulates CD8+ T cells via the Mg2+–LFA-1 axis [249]. Often, these mechanisms are synergistic. Sun et al. fabricated aligned conductive polyurethane (PUAT) conduits where the dual cues of topological guidance and inherent electroactivity jointly suppressed the M1 marker Nos2 while elevating Arg1 and IL-10, accelerating Schwann cell migration and upregulating myelin-related genes (Pmp22, Ngf) [17].

Peripheral nervous system (PNS) repair

Peripheral Nerve Injury (PNI) involves structural or functional damage to cranial or spinal nerves, resulting in motor, sensory, or autonomic dysfunction [250]. Post-PNI repair involves Wallerian degeneration (the clearance of distal axonal fragments by Schwann cells and macrophages) followed by regeneration. Axons regrow at approximately 0.5-2 mm/day, guided by Büngner bands and supported by Schwann cell-secreted neurotrophic factors (e.g., NGF) and adhesion molecules [251]. While rat sciatic nerve models are the gold standard for evaluating PNI therapies due to their physiological similarities to humans, their smaller nerve sizes pose translational challenges, as clinical human PNIs typically involve much larger gap defects [252].

Clinically, short-gap PNIs are repaired via direct suturing, whereas long-gap defects require autologous nerve grafting. Due to donor site morbidity and limited availability, tissue-engineered nerve conduits present a vital alternative [253]. Hydrogels, with tissue-matching mechanics and tailorable electroactivity, are promising PNS scaffolds [254,255]. Cai et al. developed a multi-channel PVA/GO hydrogel conduit. Through controlled dopamine self-polymerization, GO was selectively reduced to rGO, establishing a vertical conductivity gradient (10.9 to 31.4 mS/m). To overcome the weak endogenous bioelectricity of passive conduits, integrating exogenous electrical stimulation significantly boosts regeneration. Applied low-frequency pulsed electric fields drive the electrophoretic migration of NGF, modulate neuronal membrane potentials, and open voltage-gated calcium channels. The resulting Ca2+ influx upregulates BDNF, maintains high cAMP levels, and drives M2 macrophage polarization [257]. Park et al. incorporated rGO into a gelatin-derivative injectable hydrogel to form a conductive nanocomposite hydrogel, which, when combined with electrical stimulation, promoted the re-connection of injured nerve with their target tissue circuitry [258]. Similarly, Liu et al. constructed a carbon-nanotube-mixed nanofiber hydrogel, where the synergistic application of electrical stimulation promoted neurite growth and myelination, highlighting its potential in nerve regeneration [259].

To avoid the infection risks associated with percutaneous wire implants, wireless stimulation via magnetic or ultrasonic coupling has emerged. Xu et al. engineered a piezoelectric nerve conduit featuring barium titanate nanoparticles (BTNPs) and a thermo-responsive poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (pNIPAM) outer layer. Ultrasonic stimulation wirelessly converted mechanical energy into localized electrical cues, while the mild photothermal effect triggered controlled NGF release from the pNIPAM, resulting in a synergistic therapeutic outcome [260]. Similarly, Wang et al. utilized pulsed electromagnetic fields (PEMF) to irradiate rGO-based hydrogels, inducing localized eddy currents that promoted BDNF release and muscle hypertrophy, achieving repair efficacy comparable to autografts [261].

Preclinical data from various animal models demonstrate that conductive hydrogel scaffolds exert prominent therapeutic effects in the short-term repair of nerve injuries [262]. However, most existing literature lacks systematic assessments of their long-term in vivo performance [263]. Composite hydrogels incorporated with non-degradable fillers such as graphene and carbon nanotubes tend to retain nanoparticle debris around blood vessels 6-8 weeks after implantation. Such persistent residues trigger sustained secondary inflammation and impede long-distance axonal circuit reconstruction [264]. In contrast, degradable nanomaterials like MXene and BP offer superior long-term histocompatibility. Validating these biomaterials in large animal models remains a critical prerequisite for clinical translation.

Conclusion

Neurological disorders and traumatic nerve injuries frequently precipitate a devastating cascade of secondary pathologies, encompassing local ischemia, robust inflammatory responses, glial scar formation, microenvironmental dysregulation, and ultimately, severely impaired axonal regeneration. Over recent years, rapid advancements in biocompatible materials and neural tissue engineering have yielded highly promising strategies for nerve repair. An ideal biomaterial scaffold for such demanding applications must seamlessly integrate excellent biocompatibility, tunable mechanical properties, and appropriate electrical conductivity with a robust capacity to guide neural regeneration. Although traditional polymeric hydrogels exhibit commendable biocompatibility and high water content, their inherent limitations in mechanical toughness and electrical conductivity significantly constrain their clinical efficacy in severe nerve injuries. Consequently, the strategic integration of functional conductive nanomaterials into hydrogel matrices has emerged as a pivotal approach to simultaneously overcoming these electro-mechanical deficits and expanding their utility in advanced neural tissue engineering.

Traditional clinical interventions aimed at suppressing neuroinflammation following central nervous system (CNS) injury predominantly rely on the systemic administration of chemical and biological agents, including small-molecule anti-inflammatory drugs, recombinant cytokines, exosomes, and neutralizing antibodies. However, these exogenous therapeutic modalities share several inherent pharmacokinetic drawbacks: short biological half-lives, initial burst release profiles, off-target systemic toxicities, and the burdensome necessity for repeated administrations. In stark contrast, conductive nanocomposite hydrogels can orchestrate localized, sustained immune remodeling primarily through endogenous physical cues: intrinsic bioelectrical signaling, biomimetic topographical guidance, and the controlled release of bioactive ions. This sophisticated material design enables long-term, in-situ anti-inflammatory effects that are temporally synchronized with the intrinsic neural regeneration timeline, thereby minimizing chemical toxicity and evading severe host immune rejection. Such unparalleled advantages establish conductive nanocomposite hydrogels as an innovative, paradigm-shifting platform for CNS injury repair.

By intricately combining the remarkable electroactive properties of conductive nanocomponents with the soft, tissue-like hydrated architecture of hydrogel networks, these composite biomaterials offer a multitude of distinct functional advantages: (1) Enhanced Electrical Conductivity: They effectively bridge neural gaps, facilitating the seamless transmission of endogenous action potentials along severed nerve fibers and promoting the functional integration of sensory and motor circuits. (2) Optimized Mechanical Integrity: The incorporation of nanomaterials acts as structural reinforcement, yielding superior tensile strength and elasticity that provide vital physical support to the regenerating tissue. (3) Intrinsic Bioactivity: Specific conductive nanofillers undergo gradual degradation to release metabolic ions that actively stimulate angiogenesis and modulate local neuroinflammation, thereby fostering a highly permissive physiological microenvironment. (4) Directed Cellular Regulation: The electro-mechanical cues synergistically enhance the adhesion, proliferation, and targeted differentiation of neural stem cells (NSCs), dramatically accelerating neuronal regeneration. (5) Advanced Drug Delivery: The embedded nanoscale components function as smart delivery vehicles for the spatiotemporally controlled release of therapeutic agents. Furthermore, when deployed locally, these nanocomposites bypass the blood-brain barrier (BBB), while their nanoscale sub-components can deeply penetrate the damaged neural parenchyma for site-specific treatment.

In this comprehensive review, we have delineated the state-of-the-art synthesis strategies and systematic classifications of conductive nanocomposite hydrogels, detailing the incorporation of diverse electroactive nanomaterials spanning carbon-based, phosphorus-based, metal/metal oxide, and conductive polymeric nanoparticles into hydrophilic 3D networks. These hybrid architectures markedly transcend the performance of conventional hydrogels by facilitating robust intercellular electrical communication, reinforcing structural mechanics, enabling programmable drug elution, and actively restoring electrophysiological responses in models of spinal cord and peripheral nerve injury.

Despite these remarkable scientific strides, several formidable challenges must be addressed before widespread clinical translation is realized. Foremost among these are the complex interrelated issues of long-term nanotoxicity, holistic biocompatibility, and controlled biodegradability, all of which are deeply dependent on the concentration, intrinsic properties, and dispersion quality of the conductive nanofillers within the matrix. Exhaustive in vitro and in vivo investigations are imperative to precisely determine the optimal stoichiometric thresholds that maintain structural integrity and maximize electroactivity while minimizing the requisite dosage. Moreover, resolving the intricate spatiotemporal challenge of material degradation remains paramount. For biodegradable systems, the degradation kinetics of the scaffold must meticulously match the biological rate of new neural tissue ingrowth; excessively rapid collapse compromises physical support, whereas delayed degradation physically obstructs axonal elongation and vascularization. Therefore, rigorous longitudinal in vivo evaluations across multiple time points are essential to tailor these degradation profiles accurately.

Crucially, the ultimate in vivo fate of dissociated conductive nanoparticles following bulk hydrogel degradation poses a significant safety concern, particularly within the exquisitely sensitive CNS microenvironment. Non-degradable carbonaceous nanomaterials (e.g., graphene, carbon nanotubes) are notoriously difficult for the body to metabolize and clear in situ. If these residual nanoparticles infiltrate the cerebrospinal fluid and accumulate within neural tissues, they can persistently provoke microglia and astrocytes, inciting chronic secondary neuroinflammation and dense fibrotic encapsulation that irreversibly compromises neural circuit reconstruction. Conversely, degradable nano-architectures, such as MXenes and specific phosphorus- or silicon-based nanofillers, demonstrate progressive oxidative degradation profiles, resulting in negligible cumulative neurotoxicity. Moving forward, the rational design of translational biomaterials must stringently prioritize controllable degradation kinetics, minimized off-target nanoparticle leakage, and validated, biosafe metabolic clearance pathways to completely abrogate the risk of nanoparticle-induced secondary trauma.

Looking ahead, the strategic coupling of these conductive nanocomposite hydrogels with optimized external electrical stimulation holds immense potential for maximizing regenerative outcomes. Although current in vitro paradigms have validated their synergistic therapeutic effects, successful clinical adaptation will necessitate the rigorous standardization of stimulation parameters including modality, precise anatomical positioning, electrode distance, amplitude, and frequency to perfectly harmonize with the unique impedance of the implanted biomaterial. Additionally, engineering multicomponent hybrid systems (e.g., integrating carbon-based skeletons with conductive polymer nodes) may yield next-generation scaffolds boasting unprecedented synergistic conductivity and mechanical resilience. Furthermore, while many current hydrogel networks primarily exert regulatory effects via cell-surface receptor interactions, advancing the design of smart nanomaterials capable of controlled cellular internalization (e.g., via specific endocytic pathways) to directly modulate intracellular neurogenic signaling networks represents a critical, yet largely untapped, frontier.

Finally, while the phenomenological immunomodulatory benefits of these conductive platforms are increasingly evident, the precise molecular mechanisms such as the specific signal transduction cascades governing macrophage polarization and the dynamic intercellular crosstalk between regenerating neurons and local immune populations remain to be fully elucidated. Decoding these complex biological pathways will be unequivocally essential for the rational, targeted engineering of conductive nanocomposite hydrogels, solidifying their future role as precision bioelectronic therapeutics for severe neurological diseases.
