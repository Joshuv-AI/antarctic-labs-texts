Updates on Cardiac MRI and PET Imaging for the Diagnosis and Monitoring of Cardiac Sarcoidosis
Authors: Noriko Oyama-Manabe, Osamu Manabe, Tadao Aikawa, Yoshitaka Sobue, Ryosuke Asakura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to its heterogeneous clinical manifestations and the limitations of conventional diagnostic approaches. Advances in imaging modalities, particularly cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET), have revolutionized the evaluation and management of this complex condition. CMR, with its superior spatial resolution and advanced techniques such as late gadolinium enhancement, T1/T2 mapping, and extracellular volume quantification, offers unparalleled insights into myocardial structure and fibrosis. These techniques not only enhance diagnostic accuracy but also provide critical information on disease activity and treatment response. Among these, T2 mapping has emerged as a valuable marker for active inflammation, with high values reliably indicating acute disease states. FDG-PET serves as a complementary modality by detecting active granulomatous inflammation and guiding immunosuppressive therapy. The synergistic integration of CMR and FDG-PET provides a comprehensive approach to diagnosing and monitoring CS, enabling the identification of subclinical disease and the optimization of therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, the incorporation of quantitative biomarkers, such as strain metrics and T2 values, promises to refine disease assessment and management. These advancements have the potential to transform the paradigm of CS care, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) is a systemic inflammatory disease with significant morbidity and mortality, characterized by granulomatous inflammation primarily involving the myocardium [12]. Despite its relatively low prevalence compared to systemic sarcoidosis (estimated to affect approximately 5% of patients with systemic sarcoidosis), the cardiac manifestation poses unique challenges due to its often subtle, nonspecific symptoms, ranging from asymptomatic cases to sudden cardiac death [123]. The patchy distribution of granulomas and fibrosis in the heart adds to its diagnostic complexity [4], making conventional tools such as echocardiography or endomyocardial biopsy limited in both their sensitivity and specificity [12].

Advanced imaging modalities, particularly cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET), have emerged as key tools for the diagnosis and management of CS. CMR excels at detecting structural abnormalities such as myocardial fibrosis, while FDG-PET provides dynamic insights into active inflammation (Fig. 1) [23]. This article explores the complementary roles of these modalities and their integration to achieve precise diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and therapeutic guidance in CS.

Specific Imaging Methods and Their Utility

CMR and Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE) for Structural Assessment

According to the 2024 guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology [5], the Japanese Circulation Society [6], and the Heart Rhythm Society [7], CMR with late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) is considered the gold standard for detecting myocardial fibrosis, a hallmark of chronic sarcoid involvement (Table 1). This is supported by a pooled sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 84% for detecting CS, as shown in recent studies [8]. LGE-CMR distinguishes between ischemic and non-ischemic cardiomyopathies by identifying specific patterns such as mid-wall or subepicardial enhancement commonly seen in CS (Fig. 2) [12]. These patterns differ from those seen in ischemic cardiomyopathy, where LGE tends to involve subendocardial regions following the distribution of the coronary arteries. The non-ischemic distribution of LGE in the basal ventricular wall, lateral wall, and septum is a typical feature observed in CS. This differentiation is essential for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning [2]. While PET is highly effective in identifying active inflammation, CMR excels in delineating chronic structural damage, such as fibrosis, making it particularly valuable for assessing long-term disease burden (Fig. 1) [3].

T1 Mapping and Extracellular Volume (ECV) for Myocardial Characterization

While LGE on CMR is the cornerstone for identifying myocardial fibrosis, its inability to distinguish between active inflammation and chronic scarring limits its utility in assessing treatment responses. T1 and T2 mapping, along with extracellular volume (ECV) quantification, overcome these limitations and provide robust, non-invasive biomarkers for detecting and monitoring disease activity [29]. T1 and T2 mapping techniques allow for quantitative assessment of myocardial edema and extracellular volume, providing insight into both acute and chronic inflammatory changes. These parameters are particularly valuable in identifying subclinical cases where conventional methods fail to detect abnormalities [23]. Greulich et al. [9] demonstrated that T1 mapping improves the detection of subclinical CS by identifying interstitial fibrosis even in LGE-negative areas. Their results showed weighted mean T1 values of 994 ms in patients with CS using 1.5T CMR, significantly higher than those in healthy controls, emphasizing the role of T1 mapping in the detection of early pathological changes. In patients with CS, elevated native T1 values are associated with active inflammation, and reductions in these values after immunosuppressive therapy indicate a favorable therapeutic response. For example, one study reported that native T1 values decreased significantly in treated patients, correlating with clinical improvement [9]. Importantly, the ability of native T1 mapping to monitor changes in myocardial properties over time highlights its potential role in guiding treatment adjustments and evaluating long-term outcomes.

ECV mapping calculates the fraction of the myocardial volume filled by extracellular matrix components, primarily collagen. This parameter provides a quantitative assessment of myocardial fibrosis and inflammation. Treiber et al. [10] reported that regional ECV within LGE-positive myocardium was significantly higher in patients with CS than in those with acute or chronic myocarditis (0.65 ± 0.12 vs. 0.45 ± 0.13 and 0.47 ± 0.10, respectively, P < 0.001). This suggests that ECV measurement in LGE regions may help to differentiate CS from other myocardial inflammatory conditions. The increased ECV in CS likely reflects fibrosis and granulomatous infiltration, as opposed to the more diffuse involvement seen in myocarditis. They also reported that ECV in LGE-positive regions was significantly higher in CS, with a cutoff of 0.57 achieving an AUC of 0.87 for differentiation from other etiologies [10].

T2 Mapping: A Marker for Active Inflammation

T2 mapping provides quantitative information about myocardial water content, making it an ideal marker of active inflammation. Elevated T2 values have been consistently observed in patients with active CS and correlate closely with histological findings of edema and inflammation (Table 2). These values are particularly useful in differentiating reversible inflammatory processes from irreversible fibrosis, and this distinction is essential for optimizing treatment strategies.

Quantitative T1, T2, and ECV values allow for dynamic monitoring of disease activity and therapeutic responses. For example, reductions in T2 and ECV values following corticosteroid therapy indicate diminished myocardial inflammation and provide non-invasive means of assessing treatment efficacy (Fig. 3) [9]. Furthermore, T2 mapping has been shown to outperform LGE in identifying active lesions in patients with LGE-negative CS, emphasizing its complementary role in CS assessment [911]. The sensitivity of this technique to early inflammatory changes makes it an indispensable tool for evaluating the efficacy of anti-inflammatory therapies.

For example, mean basal T2 values >50.8 ms were independently associated with active myocardial inflammation [11], outperforming traditional diagnostic criteria such as the Japanese Circulation Society CS guideline alone [6]. The CMR protocol to evaluate CS is shown in Figure 4.

CMR Strain Imaging

CMR strain imaging is an advanced technique for the quantitative assessment of myocardial deformation, including longitudinal, circumferential, radial, and rotational strain [12]. Longitudinal strain reflects the basal-to-apical shortening of the myocardium and is primarily governed by subendocardial fibers, which are particularly sensitive to early ischemic or inflammatory injury. Impairment of longitudinal strain is often the earliest sign of subclinical myocardial dysfunction. Circumferential strain, on the other hand, measures the contraction around the short-axis plane and reflects mid-wall fiber function. It tends to be more robust in later stages of the disease and may be preserved longer than longitudinal strain in certain pathologies.

Various CMR-based methods, such as tagging [13], strain-encoded MRI [1415], and feature-tracking imaging [16], have been developed to evaluate myocardial function without the use of contrast agents. Among these, feature-tracking imaging is the most widely adopted in clinical practice due to its applicability to both left ventricular (LV) and right ventricular (RV) function. It facilitates the early detection of subclinical myocardial dysfunction and provides robust quantitative parameters (Fig. 5) [17]. In the multicenter study by Taylor et al. [18], peak regional longitudinal strain values in healthy individuals ranged from -17% to -23%, while circumferential strain ranged from -20% to -27%, depending on myocardial segment and imaging plane. Values less negative than -12% for longitudinal strain and -15% for circumferential strain were suggested to indicate potential dysfunction. These thresholds are supported by consensus recommendations from the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance [19]. These regional strain metrics complement global assessments and may help identify segmental or subtle myocardial involvement in infiltrative or inflammatory cardiomyopathies.

Feature-tracking-derived mechanical dyssynchrony (Fig. 6) is a strong predictor of response to cardiac resynchronization therapy and long-term outcomes in patients with non-ischemic cardiomyopathy [20]. Dyssynchrony is typically assessed by quantifying the temporal dispersion in peak systolic strain timing among the RV free wall, interventricular septum, and LV lateral wall. A pronounced delay in peak strain in one region, especially in the right ventricle or lateral wall relative to the septum, is suggestive of mechanical dyssynchrony.

In the context of CS, patients with RV involvement often exhibit more extensive disease. RV involvement has been associated with a higher number of affected LV segments and a greater likelihood of fulfilling the diagnostic criteria of the Japanese Ministry of Health and Welfare [21]. These findings suggest that biventricular assessment, particularly incorporating RV strain or function, may enhance diagnostic confidence and reflect disease severity. In cases with borderline imaging findings, the presence of RV abnormalities may help justify a diagnosis of CS in accordance with the established criteria. The integration of CMR-derived myocardial strain parameters with conventional imaging techniques such as LGE and T1/T2 mapping enhances diagnostic accuracy. Notably, it enables the detection of early functional impairment before irreversible structural damage occurs, thereby improving risk stratification and treatment monitoring. This complementary approach has been shown to provide incremental prognostic value in various cardiomyopathies and inflammatory myocardial diseases, beyond that offered by conventional imaging alone [22].

Despite its advantages, CMR strain imaging alone cannot reliably distinguish active inflammation from chronic scarring, necessitating complementary assessment with FDG-PET or delayed-enhanced CT [123242526]. This multimodality approach provides a more comprehensive evaluation of disease activity and supports individualized therapeutic decision-making.

FDG-PET for Active Inflammation

FDG-PET plays a crucial role in detecting active myocardial inflammation in CS [23]. By identifying metabolically active granulomas, FDG-PET complements CMR in differentiating inflammatory activity from fibrotic changes. This modality is particularly valuable for guiding immunosuppressive therapy, as serial imaging allows monitoring of treatment efficacy through changes in FDG uptake [2].

To ensure diagnostic accuracy, careful patient preparation is essential to suppress physiological myocardial glucose uptake, which may otherwise mimic pathological findings [23]. Common strategies include prolonged fasting and a high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet. In addition, combining FDG-PET with myocardial perfusion imaging can further improve specificity by distinguishing inflammatory from ischemic myocardial abnormalities [2]. Recent studies have shown that delayed FDG-PET acquisition (approximately 100 minutes after FDG injection) improves detection accuracy compared with early imaging by reducing blood pool activity and enhancing lesion-to-background activity ratio [272829]. In some cases, focal myocardial FDG uptake resembling CS may be observed, and it is sometimes necessary to rule out hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and coronary artery disease (Fig. 8). Focal myocardial FDG uptake in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or coronary artery disease is a well-documented phenomenon that can mimic the PET appearance of CS, leading to potential diagnostic pitfalls [3031].

The integration of CMR and FDG-PET allows for a comprehensive evaluation of CS (Fig. 9), leveraging the strengths of each modality. For example, a positive LGE finding on CMR paired with FDG-PET evidence of active inflammation confirms both structural and functional involvement, aiding in disease staging and treatment planning [23]. Conversely, discordant findings—such as LGE-negative but FDG-positive cases (Fig. 10)—highlight subclinical disease that may benefit from closer monitoring or early intervention [2].

Prognostic Value of LGE and FDG Uptake in CS

Patients with extensive LGE are at increased risk for ventricular arrhythmias, heart failure, and sudden cardiac death. Previous studies showed that the presence of myocardial LGE is associated with increased mortality risk compared to those without LGE even in patients with preserved LV ejection fraction [12]. This underscores the role of CMR in identifying high-risk individuals who may benefit from device implantation or aggressive medical therapy. Patients with significant FDG uptake often require intensified immunosuppressive regimens, and serial imaging can track treatment efficacy [3]. By integrating inflammation and fibrosis data, clinicians can more accurately predict outcomes and tailor interventions.

Future Directions in CS Imaging

Advances in imaging technologies continue to refine the diagnosis and management of CS. Several emerging modalities show promise for improving diagnostic accuracy, risk stratification, and treatment monitoring. Digital PET-CT scanners offer superior spatial resolution and increased sensitivity compared to conventional analog systems [32]. These advances allow the detection of smaller granulomatous lesions and provide an enhanced signal-to-noise ratio, which improves differentiation between active inflammation and background myocardial uptake. In addition, shorter scan times and lower radiation doses contribute to improved patient safety and efficiency in clinical practice.

The integration of PET and MRI into a single hybrid imaging system allows for the simultaneous assessment of inflammation (FDG-PET) and fibrosis (CMR LGE, T1/T2 mapping). This approach has the potential to reduce scan time, improve spatial alignment between modalities, and enhance the characterization of disease activity. Hybrid PET/MR may represent the next frontier in CS imaging, particularly for patients requiring longitudinal monitoring (Table 2) [2].

Artificial intelligence (AI)-driven algorithms, including deep learning and machine learning models, are increasingly being integrated into cardiac imaging workflows. AI has the potential to automate segmentation, reduce interobserver variability, and provide quantitative analysis of LGE, FDG uptake, and myocardial strain. Automated detection of CS-related abnormalities can improve diagnostic confidence, especially in borderline cases, and streamline clinical decision making.

Texture analysis is an advanced computational technique that evaluates the spatial distribution and heterogeneity of imaging signals at the pixel or voxel level [33343536]. In CS, high gray-level run emphasis and long-run emphasis have shown promise in differentiating active inflammation from fibrosis [33]. These parameters may serve as novel quantitative biomarkers, complementing traditional standardized uptake value and LGE assessments. Future studies are needed to validate the role of texture analysis in prognostic stratification and personalized treatment planning (Fig. 11).

CONCLUSION

CS presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to its heterogeneous nature and diverse clinical presentation. Advanced imaging modalities, particularly CMR with T1/T2 mapping and ECV quantification, and FDG-PET, have revolutionized the evaluation of CS. These modalities offer improved diagnostic accuracy, enable the identification of active inflammation, and guide the implementation and monitoring of immunosuppressive therapy. In addition, quantitative analyses, such as texture analysis of FDG uptake, provide a more accurate assessment of disease activity and heterogeneity. The integration of these advanced imaging tools enables comprehensive cardiac assessment, including the detection of the subclinical phase of the disease, and facilitates personalized treatment strategies. Combined with quantitative biomarkers and emerging therapies, these innovations promise to transform the management of CS and improve patient outcomes.
