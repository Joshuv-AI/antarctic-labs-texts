Interpretation drift in explainable AI under label noise
Authors: Alisa Raikovskaia, Nurlan Rakhimzhanov, Oleg S. Pianykh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The comprehensibility and human interpretation of classification models are crucial in many applications, such as decision support systems and knowledge discovery, where explanations drive action. However, the presence of class label noise, widespread in real-life data, can significantly impact the performance and interpretability of data models. This study addresses the problem of interpretability robustness by examining the impact of class label noise on rule-learning models – the models extensively used for discovering transparent, human-readable interpretations of hidden data patterns and decision logic. Our empirical results demonstrate that while model performance may remain stable under increasing label noise, the consistency of explainable model rules suffers significantly. As a result, we uncover a novel and critical phenomenon – interpretation drift – where model explanations change substantially under label noise, even when predictive performance remains stable. This phenomenon can directly impact AI-informed decisions, but is not detectable through conventional performance metrics and therefore poses significant risks in real-world applications reliant on AI explanations. Our findings emphasize the need for standardized, interpretability-aware robustness metrics in the development of trustworthy explainable AI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-37070-4.

Introduction

Modern machine learning (ML) models have achieved remarkable predictive performance across diverse domains. However, their decision-making processesing often relies on opaque “black boxes”, significantly hindering human understanding, validation, and trust ​1. This limitation has fueled a growing interest in explainable and interpretable AI, particularly in high-stakes settings (healthcare2, law3, finance4, where decisions must be fully transparent to be trusted. And although AI interpretability and explainability methods can be extended to post-hoc interpretation of complex black-box models as well, simpler models with intrinsic transparency became increasingly important in critical decision-making. Among those, ruleset-based classifiers, mimicking human reasoning patterns, are particularly valued for their explicit if-then structure and easy-to-understand Boolean logic5,6.

Interpretability, however, remains a domain hard to assess objectively7. To address this challenge, researchers have proposed frameworks that map out conceptual properties and evaluation criteria for explanations8,9. Early experimental studies with users revealed one important factor: as models grow larger, their comprehensibility tends to decline10. Similarly, cognitive research indicates that humans use only up to four variables in their decision making11–13. These insights reinforce a long-standing practice of assessing the interpretability of rule-based models through size-related measures such as the number of model rules, variables, or conditions. Longer or more complex rules may still remain comprehensible and even appear more plausible14,15 yet only short and simple rules can be literally memorized by human decision-makers to be used in their daily work.

This daily work presents another fundamental challenge: noisy, imperfect real-life data. Label noise (mislabeled training examples) is particularly dangerous, as widespread and degrading model performance through less reliable prediction targets7,16–18. Therefore, practical decision-making models must be robust not only by maintaining predictive performance, but also by preserving the consistency of their interpretations. Previous research has shown that many popular ML algorithms are not resilient to label noise, both in terms of predictive accuracy and model complexity17,19,20. This issue is further exacerbated by AI interpretation fragility, in which applying specific adversarial perturbations to the input data does not change the prediction of the model but substantially changes its explanations21,22. Prior research has explored this phenomenon primarily in the context of data selection bias, comparing models trained on different population samples. Stability in interpretation has been measured using metrics such as complexity deviation23, similarity between feature sets23, and similarity between sets of conjunctions24,25.

However, no prior studies have explored how label noise affects the consistency of explainable interpretations – the key aspect of real-life AI. This presents a serious gap, because an interpretable model that substantially alters its reasoning in response to minor data imperfections may undermine user trust, even if its predictive accuracy remains high.

In this study, we propose a methodology for evaluating the robustness of interpretability in rule-based classifiers exposed to label noise. A model is considered robust if its ruleset learned from noisy labels remains consistent with the one learned from clean data, quantified using Jaccard Similarity. To reduce variance from hyperparameter sensitivity, we propose to compare the top \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N$$\end{document} most accurate rulesets. This methodology is particularly well-suited for evaluating ruleset generators, where each rule can be treated as an independent classifier, unlike decision trees or rule lists which depend on the rule order. We apply this framework to both globally optimal rule learners (Human Knowledge Models (HKM), proposed in26 and greedy rule learners (Incremental Reduced Error Pruning (IREP)27 and Repeated Incremental Pruning to Produce Error Reduction (RIPPER)28 – not to benchmark these particular algorithms, but to examine how different optimization strategies may impact interpretability stability.

Through this study, we not only confirm that rule-learning models are vulnerable to interpretation fragility, but we also introduce the new and significant concept of interpretation drift – a phenomenon where model explanations evolve unpredictably with noise, even as accuracy appears deceptively stable. This drift, creating “hallucinating explanations” of the reality, can critically undermine trust in interpretable AI systems, especially in sensitive domains such as medicine and finance. Our findings provide strong evidence for the necessity of robust interpretability metrics and open a new direction for the design of noise-tolerant, trustworthy AI systems.

Methods

This section describes the methodology we used to evaluate the robustness of interpretable rule-learning models in the presence of label noise: our experimental data, models, and the metrics we developed.

Models

Rule learning represents one of the most common and efficient ways to discover human-interpretable inferences from complex and multidimensional data33,34. To account for different approaches in our study, we considered three rule-learning algorithms: (1) Incremental Reduced Error Pruning (IREP27, (2) Repeated Incremental Pruning to Produce Error Reduction (RIPPER, the successor of IREP28, and (3) Human Knowledge Models (HKM26. IREP and RIPPER were chosen as examples of well-established rule learners, while HKM was used as an example of a more recent and globally-optimal learning approach.

All three algorithms yield rulesets as interpretations, but differ in their optimization strategies, complexity constraints, and interpretability objectives. IREP is a widely used ruleset generation algorithm, which constructs rules iteratively by optimizing information gain and refining the rules through pruning27. While computationally efficient, IREP is known to produce overly complex rulesets that are prone to overfitting28. To address these limitations, RIPPER was introduced as an enhancement, extending IREP by incorporating the concept of description length minimization, a principle that avoids unnecessary complexity while maintaining predictive power. However, neither IREP nor RIPPER explicitly prioritize human interpretability, as their optimization primarily focuses on classification performance rather than the cognitive simplicity of the generated rules.

In contrast, the Human Knowledge Models (HKM) algorithm proposes a fundamentally different approach to rule generation by emphasizing human interpretability. HKM learns the class of models that can be evaluated by humans in real time, mentally, without external computational resources26. To achieve this, HKM relies on exhaustive search to find a globally optimal solution using only a few variables/operators. In addition, HKM can be viewed as a metric-driven learner, where one can use any model quality metric (such as F1 used in our study), and the optimal results will be driven by this metric choice. The exhaustive search also enables HKM to find the top N optimal models, rather than only the single best model produced by IREP and RIPPER. Finding multiple optimal models enhances decision-making by providing several explanations of the target label, and helps human interpreters select the most understandable and practically useful explanation.

Another critical distinction between the three rule-learning algorithms lies in their approach to controlling ruleset complexity. This prevents near-duplicates and yields more diverse explanations.

Measuring response to label noise

The main goal of our research was to explore the impact of label noise on the interpretable rule-learning models. Label noise can affect both predictive performance and interpretations, therefore we defined two types of label noise-robustness (LNR):

To evaluate PLNR and ILNR metrics, each dataset from Table 1 was randomly split, with 70/30 ratio, into train and test subsets, and some binary labels in the original train set were randomly changed to the opposite values. Following label noise classification from19, we call it Noisy Completely at Random Model (NCAR). Test datasets (30% of original data) remained noise-free, and were used to assess the quality of the models trained on noisy data.

Following this experimental framework, we varied the amount of noise (noise rate r) introduced into the original labels, from 2.5% to 97.5%, with 2.5% increments. Intermediate noise levels around 50% correspond to the maximum-uncertainty regime (“coin flip”), where the noisy training labels carry minimal or no information about the true labels. At very high noise levels close to 100%, the noisy labels approach the opposite of the original labels (e.g., “has disease” becomes “does not have disease”), thus returning to a systematic learnable pattern of low noise. F1-score was chosen as the model performance metric35, with all model results reported with the test set F1.

Note that frequent 2.5% noise increments, as well as time-consuming exhaustive searches in HKM models required significant processing time: even with code parallelization, a single round of our numerical experiments took around 5 days on a high-performance PC with (4.8 GHz Intel core processor, 24-core CPU). Therefore, we used a single train/test split on each dataset, but were able to process all four datasets from Table 1 to achieve a diverse, robust experimental base.

Interpretation robustness

Unlike model performance, interpretability label noise robustness (ILNR) remains one of the least explored areas in AI research. To address this gap, we applied an intuitive metric to quantify ILNR. As previously noted, multiple rulesets can often provide equally valid explanations for a given outcome – for instance, patients with different phenotypes may demonstrate equally strong response to the same treatment. Accounting for multiple optimal rules, rather than relying on one, generally helps with more stable and robust interpretations of the model’s output, making it less sensitive to minor variations in the data. While the HKM algorithm is inherently capable of generating multiple optimal rulesets, IREP and RIPPER lack this functionality and produce only one single best ruleset. On the other side, HKM does not depend on randomization in seeds or states, while IREP and RIPPER do. Therefore, to generate diverse optimal models supporting more generalizable discovery, we generated multiple optimal outputs for IREP and RIPPER by selecting the top N rulesets across different hyperparameter configurations and random states. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted using the Optuna framework36. Each candidate configuration was evaluated by 5-fold cross-validation on the training split; the best models were selected according to the mean F1-score and then retrained on the full training data. The search space included prune_size ∈ [0.15, 0.6] and max_rules ∈ [1, 40] for both RIPPER and IREP, with an additional k ∈1,10 parameter for RIPPER.

Results

In this section, we study how predictive performance PLNR, and interpretability ILNR of rule-learning algorithms can be impacted by their design and label noise rate. As such, these results do not imply model algorithm benchmarking, but highlight how different rule-learning approaches respond to label noise in both predictive and interpretability robustness. Our findings reveal a divergence between predictive stability and explanation consistency, driven by label noise, algorithmic strategy, and model complexity.

Interpretation complexity and noise-free quality of rule models

The main goal of explainable AI is to produce a highly predictive model with the lowest complexity, to be easily understood by human interpreters. To examine this quality-complexity tradeoff, we first studied the rulesets from the models trained on noise-free data. The predictive performance of these rulesets was evaluated with F1 score for the best model obtained from multiple optimal rulesets. As justified earlier, the complexity of the interpretations was quantified by computing the total number of conjunctions and the total number of conditions within each ruleset.

Figure 2 summarizes our results, while statistical test confirmed that 22 out of 48 pairwise model quality comparisons were statistically significant (p < 0.05, Supplementary Table 1). As one can observe in Fig. 2, the globally-optimal models (HKM) can indeed produce significantly lower rule complexity, while maintaining model quality comparable with that of IREP and RIPPER. This finding highlights the fact that improved model interpretability can often be achieved without compromising the model performance, by leveraging advanced computing power to support exhaustive model optimization. Consequently, IREP and RIPPER were able to outperform the best HKM ruleset only by increasing their interpretation complexity (Fig. 2, bank dataset). RIPPER tended to produce the most complex solutions, suggesting that its description length minimization approach may be more efficiency-oriented, rather than focused on human interpretability.

Label noise and rule model predictive performance

Real-life data is inherently noisy, and ensuring model resistance to label noise becomes as important as maintaining high model quality. Therefore, we tracked model performance label noise robustness (PLNR) by measuring mean F1 score on test data across multiple optimal outputs at the different levels of noise, introduced into the train labels. To study the role of model complexity, we varied rule constraints from 2 to 4 conditions. Unlike using only the very best rule in Fig. 2, we now considered the top N = 30 best rules, which enabled us to compute standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals for mean F1 values.

As r increases, the F1 score for all models declines: as expected, label noise reduces the quality of patterns learned. Importantly, models preserve the same patterns of F1 score reduction across different conjunction complexity parameters. It suggests that the algorithm design choices, such as optimization method and loss function, rather than the model’s regularization settings, are critical determinants of PLNR. Figure 4 provides additional context by showing the evolution of the proportion of positive predictions under noise. At the intermediate maximum-uncertainty noise levels (30–70%), F1-driven HKM models degrade into overproducing positive predictions (trying to achieve high recall in F1 as the data signal becomes weaker). Therefore, high robustness of HKM at 30–70% noise is partly an artifact of the F1 metric’s asymmetry, especially in datasets with strong class imbalance (such as hepatitis), where positive-only predictions produce very high F1 score by definition. By contrast, at the most prediction-uncertain 30–70% noise, greedy IREP and RIPPER tend to drift toward overpredicting the negative class. This behavior is explained by their information gain–based optimization: as noise reduces useful signal, fewer rules are added, leading to a growing prevalence of false predictions.

At very high noise, all three algorithms converge to predicting the inverse of the noise-free class proportion, as the model fits the predominantly inverted training labels, as mentioned earlier. The exception is HKM on the bank dataset, where limited model complexity fails to capture the underlying signal (see Fig. 2).

As a result, Figs. 3 and 4 demonstrate that while predictive performance may appear stable, the distribution of predicted classes reveals deeper drifts in the learned patterns. This raises the critical question of whether interpretability itself remains consistent when performance metrics appear unchanged, and we address this question below.

Label noise and loss of interpretability quality

In contemporary AI, models are evaluated predominantly based on their performance, and models with higher quality are assumed to be superior. This can make one assume that, as label noise increases, the model with more resilient performance (PLNR) will also remain more resilient in interpretability (ILNR). However, our results in Fig. 4 begin to suggest that the rules learned by explainable AI can change, while the overall quality of their interpretation remains the same.

Undoubtedly, this “invisible” drift in the interpretation rules can have serious consequences. For instance, a healthcare model might continue to predict patient outcomes with high accuracy, while significantly drifting in its interpretation of the underlying patient symptoms. This can lead to diverging clinical decisions, extracted from a seemingly robust model.

To study this phenomenon of “interpretation drift”, we used our Jaccard Similarity measure of interpretation robustness (ILNR). For each noise rate we computed the 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the JS across the top best N rules, with N ranging from 1 to 30. Similarly to PLNR analysis, we investigated each algorithm with different conjunction complexity parameter settings. The evolution of these confidence intervals is presented for each model in Fig. 5.

ILNR: Jaccard Similarity (with 95% confidence intervals) across different models and noise rates. All models suffer from significant reduction in interpretation consistency with noise rates as low as 30%; more complex models exhibiting slightly faster drop.

Figure 5 confirms that good model performance does not necessarily warrant interpretation stability: PLNR does not lead to ILNR. As one can see, Jaccard Similarity between the original noise-free and the following noisy interpretation rules drops much faster than F1 scores in Fig. 3, highlighting the models’ struggle to maintain stable rules as the label noise increases, even when predictive performance remains acceptable. The discrepancy is particularly striking in RIPPER: while Fig. 3 classification accuracy remains stable up to 30% noise in several datasets, its rules already diverge heavily, with maximum JS values not exceeding 0.5 at low noise. These findings align with interpretation fragility phenomenon described in terms of data selection bias in previous research22.

Showing greater interpretability robustness at noise rates up to 15%, HKM interpretations begin to diverge even faster than those produced by IREP and RIPPER. We attribute this to our observation made earlier: unlike increasing F1 performance by overproducing positive predictions in Fig. 4, interpretability robustness cannot be “artificially inflated” by optimizing F1 recall.

Once noise exceeds 30%, Jaccard Similarity approaches zero across nearly all models and datasets, indicating a complete mismatch between rules extracted from noisy and noise-free data. Moreover, for all algorithms, increasing conjunction complexity leads to lower interpretability robustness, which contrasts with similar PLNR patterns for different model complexity parameters (Fig. 3). This complexity-driven interpretation fragility can be explained by the fact that increasing conjunction complexity creates longer rules, more likely to be altered with noise.

Label noise and interpretation drift

Does label noise only reduce the interpretability quality of the data, or can it also change the interpretations themselves?

We would like to approach this question with classical Kaplan-Meier survivability analysis, considering the probability of each rule survival under the increasing level of label noise. Classical survival analysis studies “time-to-event” outcomes by estimating survival probabilities37,38. In our study, we are essentially evaluating interpretation rule survival (consistency of explainable AI) in response to the increasing label noise. Therefore, “time” corresponds to the increasing level of label noise, and “event” – to the disappearance of a rule from the optimal ruleset. This analogy allows us to understand how rules fluctuate or vanish under progressing noise, even if the absolute number of rules per dataset is small.

Figure 6 provides three examples of this analysis, considering the survival probabilities of five most persistent rules (the rules with the highest average occurrence across noise levels). Although one might expect a smooth gradual decay of rule strength, with rules prevailing at low noise levels gradually disappearing, replaced by their logical opposites as high noise levels, our results uncover a more complex, and more interesting pattern. As Fig. 6 demonstrates, the survival probabilities of rules can fluctuate, often sharply, peaking at different noise levels.

As a result, varying levels of noise can lead to different interpretations of the same data. This is similar to looking at a distant object on a street as night falls: at first, you might see a man, then a tree, and later a lantern. The darker it becomes, the more your interpretation drifts – yet each version seems equally valid under the given conditions. Likewise, in the presence of varying label noise, multiple interpretations may emerge, each appearing plausible within its own level of distortion. As our experiment demonstrates, explainable AI can fall into the same “interpretation drift” trap. For example, in the division dataset with RIPPER, the rule DivBy6 = 0 & DivBy8 = 0 appears at low noise levels and correctly captures divisibility by 24. As noise increases, it gives way to broader but still partially meaningful patterns like DivBy6 = 0. At higher noise levels, logically unrelated rules such as DivBy3 = 1 or DivBy3 = 2 begin to emerge. Discussion

The principal aim of this work was to investigate the robustness of interpretations in rule-based explainable AI models subjected to increasing label noise. Our findings offer several critical insights for the applied AI community.

First, we demonstrated that while model performance can be artificially inflated with nearly positive or nearly negative rules, model interpretability degrades significantly as the noise increases. Interpretability robustness drops sharply as early as at 20–30% label noise, exposing substantial divergence in rule-based explanations even when F1 remains high. As a result, strong predictive performance of explainable AI models does not guarantee reliable inference.

Second, this work introduces a novel and significant concept: interpretation drift – the phenomenon where an AI model maintains stable performance while changing its internal logic. Interpretation drift differs from model fragility, since it does not require specific adversarial data perturbations; instead, it is observed even under moderate, random noise, which may exist in routinely acquired data. Our rule survival analysis shows that explainable AI rules may emerge, transform, and vanish across different data noise levels, trying to rationalize distorted labels in the ways undetected by the performance metrics alone.

Third, our experiments indicate that model complexity tends to exacerbate the interpretation drift. As conjunction size increases, rules become more susceptible to noise-induced changes. Interestingly, both greedy (IREP, RIPPER) and exhaustive (HKM) rule learners suffer from this phenomenon, indicating that interpretation drift is a systemic issue, independent of specific optimization strategy. Note that our study was aimed at identifying the key interpretability risks posed by noisy labels, which was done with a limited set of data and the rule-learning models we considered. However, the recognition of the interpretation drift phenomenon, demonstrated in our work, opens new opportunities for its more complete characterization, particularly with respect to broader ranges of application fields, datasets, and AI models.

Our insights call into question the current state of trust in explainable AI. Most recent research and regulatory attention remain focused on ensuring accuracy, fairness, or transparency – but not consistency of explanations, so essential for explainable AI. As our results demonstrate, in safety-critical settings such as healthcare or finance, even subtle interpretation drift can seriously distort AI-driven decision making, despite a model’s seemingly stable predictive behavior.

We propose that interpretation robustness - and interpretation drift detection - should be established as first-class evaluation metrics for explainable AI models. Without such safeguards, the “explanation” provided by an interpretable AI may be nothing more than a narrative shaped by noise.
