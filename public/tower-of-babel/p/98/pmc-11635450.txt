Steroid-resistant immunoglobulin G4–related coronary arteritis: a case report
Authors: Tomoaki Okada, Wataru Takagi, Kazumasa Nosaka, Masayuki Doi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunoglobulin G4 (IgG4)–related diseases are systemic fibroinflammatory disease characterized by extensive infiltration of IgG4-positive plasma cells in the affected tissue(s), with high plasma levels of IgG4. However, coronary involvement is rare.Case summaryA 70-year-old man was diagnosed with IgG4-related coronary arteritis, pancreatitis, and cholangitis during full-body contrast computed tomography (CT) examination prior to surgery for an iliac artery aneurysm. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography/CT showed increased uptake of 18F-FDG in the pancreas, extrahepatic bile ducts, and proximal right coronary artery (RCA). Despite the patient being asymptomatic, the RCA showed severe stenosis. The patient was administered a conservative treatment with prednisolone, 30 mg/day, gradually tapered to 5 mg/day, for 6 months. Two years later, contrast CT showed improvement of the pancreatic and bile duct lesions; however, the steroid therapy had not improved the coronary artery lesions, and gradual progression of the lesions was observed. Percutaneous coronary intervention was performed with a cutting balloon in the RCA, and good patency was maintained for 1 year after the procedure.DiscussionSteroid therapy is the first-line treatment for IgG4-related diseases; however, there may be some refractory cases. The stenotic and aneurysmal types of IgG4-related coronary arteritis are life-threatening; therefore, we performed revascularization using balloon angioplasty. Determining the optimal revascularization technique for drug-refractory cases requires further investigation.

Introduction

Immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4)–related diseases are systemic inflammatory diseases characterized by elevated serum IgG4 levels and tissue infiltration by IgG4-positive plasma cells.1 The most common organs affected include the pancreas, bile ducts, kidneys, and retroperitoneum. Rarely, cardiovascular lesions have also been reported.2 Steroids are effective in treating cardiovascular lesions3; however, we encountered a case of steroid-resistant IgG4-related coronary arteritis treated with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).2

Case presentation

An asymptomatic, 70-year-old man with a history of hypertension and dyslipidaemia was diagnosed with external iliac artery aneurysm. He was referred to our institution’s Cardiovascular Surgery Department. Preoperative full-body contrast computed tomography (CT) revealed diffuse pancreatic enlargement, abdominal wall thickening, and extrahepatic bile duct stenosis. He was referred to the Department of Gastroenterology for consultation (Figure 1A and B). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography revealed diffuse pancreatic enlargement, dilation and stenosis of the main pancreatic duct, and extrahepatic bile duct wall thickening. Laboratory data (normal range) were as follows: C-reactive protein, 0.48 (0–0.14) mg/dL; amylase, 35 (44–132) U/L; IgG, 2472 (861–1747) mg/dL; IgA, 258 (93–393) mg/dL; IgM, 95 (33–183) mg/dL; and IgG4, 1380 (11–121) mg/dL. Consequently, the patient was diagnosed with IgG4-related pancreatitis and sclerosing cholangitis. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography/CT (PET/CT) showed increased uptake of 18F-FDG in the pancreas, extrahepatic bile ducts, and proximal right coronary artery (RCA) (Figure 1C and D). Positron emission tomography/CT imaging did not reveal any evidence of IgG4-related lesions in the salivary glands, lungs, lymph nodes, or retroperitoneum.

Abdominal contrast computed tomography. Diffuse pancreatic enlargement and wall thickening and stenosis of the extrahepatic bile ducts observed (A and B, arrows). 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography shows increased uptake of 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose in the pancreas, extrahepatic bile ducts (C, arrowheads), and proximal to the right coronary artery (D, arrowheads). Coronary computed tomography angiography shows right coronary artery aneurysm and diffuse wall thickening in the right coronary artery and left circumflex artery (E and F, arrows).

Contrast-enhanced coronary CT angiography (CCTA) revealed RCA aneurysm and diffuse wall thickening of the RCA and left circumflex artery (LCX) (Figure 1E and F). Coronary angiography (CAG) showed stenosis and wall irregularities in the proximal RCA without blood flow limitation [thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) grade 3] (Figure 2A; Video 1). Moderate stenosis was observed in the LCX (Figure 2B). Intravascular ultrasonography (IVUS) revealed thickening of the adventitia surrounding the RCA, intimal surface irregularity, and calcification (Figure 2C; Video 2). Based on these findings, IgG4-related coronary arteritis was confirmed.4 We opted for conservative treatment without PCI because the patient was asymptomatic.

Coronary angiography shows severe stenosis and wall irregularities in the proximal right coronary artery (A, arrows). Moderate stenosis observed in the left circumflex artery (B, arrows). Intravascular ultrasound at the site of red arrow shows thickening of the adventitia surrounding the right coronary artery (C, arrows) and intimal surface irregularity and calcification (C, arrowheads).

Steroid therapy was initiated with prednisolone, 30 mg/day, which was tapered to 5 mg/day for 6 months. Aspirin and lipid-lowering therapy were also administered, and LDL levels were maintained at <70 mg/dL. Following 2 years of steroid therapy, serum IgG4 levels decreased from 1380 to 200 mg/dL. Contrast-enhanced CT showed improvement in the pancreatic and bile duct lesions, including pancreatic enlargement and wall thickening of the extrahepatic bile duct (Figure 3). Steroid therapy, however, did not improve coronary artery lesions, and gradual lesion progression was observed. Contrast-enhanced coronary CT angiography showed increased wall thickening, progressive stenosis around the proximal RCA, and increased calcification (Figure 3). The fractional flow reserve derived from coronary CT angiography (FFRCT) indicated the possibility of myocardial ischaemia in the RCA and LCX; therefore, CAG was performed.

Treatment course. Steroid therapy started with prednisolone at 30 mg/day, which was tapered to 5 mg/day. The serum immunoglobulin G4 levels decreased to 200 mg/dL. Contrast computed tomography showed the improvement of pancreatic and bile duct lesions. However, coronary computed tomography angiography showed increased wall thickening and progressive stenosis around the proximal right coronary artery.

Coronary angiography showed progression of stenosis and wall irregularities in the proximal RCA, with limited blood flow (TIMI 2) (Figure 4; Video 3). The LCX showed only mild stenosis progression, and additional physiological evaluation using FFR did not show myocardial ischaemia. Consequently, we performed PCI for the RCA. Intravascular ultrasonography showed progression of the adventitial thickness surrounding the RCA and no improvement in intimal surface irregularity (Figure 4; Supplementary material online, Video S1). Immediately after balloon angioplasty using a cutting balloon (Wolverine, Boston, MA, USA), an improvement in the RCA flow was observed (TIMI 3). Physiological evaluation using FFR performed after PCI did not reveal any myocardial ischaemia. Coronary angiography, performed in another department as a preoperative evaluation 4 months post-PCI, showed resolved lesion without restenosis (Supplementary material online, Video S2). Contrast-enhanced coronary CT angiography performed 1 year later confirmed focal restenosis, and an additional myocardial ischaemia evaluation performed with FFRCT was negative (Figure 5).

Discussion

We report a rare case of a patient with IgG4-related coronary arteritis who underwent PCI for progressive coronary artery lesions despite steroid therapy. Immunoglobulin G4–related disease has diverse clinical manifestations.2 Although cardiovascular involvement associated with IgG4-related disease has been reported, medium-to-large arteries, such as the abdominal aorta, are commonly affected, whereas coronary artery involvement is rare.5 In a study with 248 cases of IgG4-related periaortitis/periarteritis, only 27 cases were associated with coronary arteritis.6 Though cardiac PET is a useful diagnostic tool, assessment of pericoronary inflammation becomes challenging if FDG uptake in the myocardium is not adequately suppressed. However, in our case, the proximal RCA, which is distant from the myocardium, showed increased uptake, confirming the diagnosis of coronary artery inflammation. The final diagnosis for our patient was coronary arteritis, pancreatitis, and sclerosing cholangitis associated with IgG4-related disease. The pancreatic and bile duct lesions improved with steroids; however, the coronary artery lesions remained resistant and worsened. The patient was an elderly man with dyslipidaemia and hypertension, two coronary risk factors. The inflammatory process of atherosclerosis obviously contributed to the patient’s worsening of coronary artery lesions. However, IVUS imaging showed findings different from intimal thickening due to normal atherosclerosis. Therefore, we speculate that the progression of coronary artery lesions in our case was largely due to immunological processes related to IgG4. It is unclear why the effect of steroids varied across local organs in this case. It has been reported that IgG4-related diseases may have different prognoses depending on the clinical phenotype.7 Furthermore, the existence of sustained IgG4-related lesions can lead to the accumulation of organ damage with fibrosis, as well as irreversible changes, which may affect the effectiveness of steroids.8

Coronary artery lesions associated with IgG4-related diseases can be classified into three types: stenotic, aneurysmal, and diffuse wall thickening.6 The stenotic and aneurysmal types are life-threatening; in our case, despite steroid therapy, the stenosis progressed. Although there is no clearly established optimal therapeutic strategy for IgG4-related vascular diseases, steroid therapy is considered the first choice.2,3 While steroids are typically effective, cases refractory to steroid therapy have been reported.9 In IgG4-related periaortitis/periarteritis, the main risk factor for worsening of luminal dilation after steroid therapy is the prior presence of luminal dilation.10

No established PCI strategies for coronary artery stenosis caused by IgG4-related coronary arteritis are known. Intravascular imaging techniques, such as IVUS and optical coherence tomography (OCT), can provide important information. The adventitia is most susceptible to IgG4-related inflammatory processes, which can disrupt elastic fibres in the media leading to aneurysmal transformation.2 Therefore, IVUS can show tumour-like lesion with a low-echoic pattern extending between the media and adventitia.11 Furthermore, infiltration of IgG4-positive plasmacytes sometimes involves the intima.12 Serum IgG4 levels are associated with low-density plaque, and IgG4-related immune inflammation may contribute to the vulnerability of coronary plaques.13 Intravascular ultrasonography and OCT may reveal plaque rupture, calcification, and intimal irregularities.14 Regarding revascularization, occlusion due to stent migration within coronary aneurysms after stenting has been reported.15 The efficacy of a drug-coated balloon is also unclear; therefore, we selected balloon angioplasty with a cutting balloon and achieved good clinical outcome. The optimal revascularization of coronary artery lesions associated with IgG4-related coronary arteritis has not been fully investigated and will require further discussion.

This report describes a case of steroid-resistant IgG4-related coronary arteritis that was treated with PCI. Steroid therapy is the first-line treatment for IgG4-related diseases; however, cases of steroid ineffectiveness exist. In such cases, PCI may be a promising option.
