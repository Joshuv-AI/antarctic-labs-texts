Myosteatosis is associated with advanced TNM stage and tumor features in young females with breast cancer
Authors: Jarson P Costa-Pereira, Ingryd F Macêdo, Giselle A Barbosa, Glauber M Leitão, Galtieri O C Medeiros, Ana L Miranda, Gláucia M C Ferreira, Agnes D L Bezerra, Alcides S Diniz, Ana Paula Trussardi Fayh, Sara M M L Verde
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the associations between body composition and markers related to disease severity in young females newly diagnosed with breast cancer.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted across two Oncology units. Inclusion criteria were females aged 20 to 40 years, newly diagnosed with ductal invasive breast cancer (stages I to IV), who had undergone abdominal computed tomography (CT) scans. CT images at the third lumbar vertebra (L3) level were used to assess body composition, including the cross-sectional area of skeletal muscle (cm²), skeletal muscle radiodensity (SMD, HU), intermuscular adipose tissue (IMAT, cm²), and total adipose tissue with its visceral and subcutaneous compartments.ResultsA total of 190 females were included in this analysis (median age: 35 years, quartile range: 31 to 37). 69.5% were diagnosed at stages III to IV. Patients at stages III to IV exhibited a significantly higher frequency of myosteatosis, assessed by high IMAT (P = 0.003). A higher IMAT was independently associated with higher odds of presenting with stages III to IV (OR adjusted: 2.5, 95% CI 1.1 to 5.6). Similar results were found when evaluating molecular subtypes markers of disease severity (i.e., triple negative, luminal B, and overexpression of ER2-HER2+). Higher SMD values were inversely associated with luminal B, and overexpression of ER2-HER2+.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate the potential significance of evaluating myosteatosis in young females newly diagnosed with breast cancer. The infiltration of adipose tissue and fat within skeletal muscle (i.e., myosteatosis) may hold implications for understanding disease severity, even when overall muscle mass quantity remain unchanged.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15047-2.Keywords: Breast cancer, Body composition, Computed tomography, Muscle quality, Myosteatosis

Introduction

Breast cancer is a significant public health concern, with its incidence rising worldwide, particularly in low- to middle-upper-income countries, such as Brazil [1–5]. This disease predominantly affects females at more advanced ages, and despite improvements in treatments, mortality rates continue to grow [1–5]. Despite being highly correlated with older age [2, 3], breast cancer is increasingly diagnosed in young females who also experience disease severity and poor prognosis [6]. These metrics reinforce the need to investigate factors related to disease severity, as they may impact functional capacity, quality of life, and overall prognosis [7, 8].

Growing evidence suggests that advanced age, nutritional status, and dietetic consumption (e.g., obesity, weight gain, ultra-processed food intake) correlate with an increased risk of breast cancer development and poor prognosis, including disease severity, cancer recurrence, and poor survival rates [9, 10]. Despite the relevance of nutrition in breast cancer, many studies focus on assessing the impact of body mass index (BMI), thereby overlooking subtle variations in body composition [11]. This constraint is relevant for young females newly diagnosed with breast cancer, as they may not exhibit substantial wasting in body composition, especially muscle quantity. Therefore, it is important to explore muscle composition more thoroughly, as it reflects the morphological “quality” of muscle. Muscle “quality” can change before alterations in muscle quantity [12] and may provide more detailed insights into the connection between nutritional status and breast cancer outcomes.

Computed tomography (CT) scans can be conveniently used to evaluate body composition in patients with cancer [13]. Beyond quantifying adipose and muscle compartments, CT also provides insights into morphological muscle “quality” through parameters such as skeletal muscle radiodensity (SMD) and intermuscular adipose tissue (IMAT), both markers of myosteatosis [13]. Myosteatosis is characterized by impaired muscle composition, including the infiltration of fat and/or adipose tissue within or between muscle fibers [14], and may occur irrespective of changes in body mass [15]. Although “myosteatosis” is an umbrella term, we use it here for simplicity. This condition has been associated with poor prognosis in cancer [16]. IMAT, in particular, reflects adipose deposition in and around skeletal muscle (SM) that can alter muscle metabolism, impair insulin signaling, and promote local pro-inflammatory cytokine release [11–16], mechanisms that may influence tumor biology through metabolic and endocrine crosstalk.

Based on this rationale, it is relevant to investigate the relationship between body composition, encompassing distinct phenotypes of muscle abnormalities, and disease severity in young females with breast cancer. Accordingly, this study aimed to examine the associations and distinctions between CT-derived body composition parameters and markers of disease severity in this patient population.

Methods

Study design and subjects

This is a cross-sectional study conducted at two oncology units in Northeast Brazil: Instituto do Câncer do Ceará, CE, and Centro Avançado de Oncologia – Liga Norte Rio Grandense Contra o Câncer, RN. Consecutive young females, aged between 20 and 40 years old [17], who were newly diagnosed with ductal invasive breast cancer (clinical stages I to IV) were sampled. Additional inclusion criteria were those who had undergone abdominal CT scans for diagnostic purposes and did not have a previous history of any other type of cancer. These patients were attending mastology consultations. Exclusion criteria encompassed patients with conditions that could potentially interfere with CT evaluation, such as subcutaneous edema, ascites, or anatomical variations. The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the institutions (CAAE: 43488621.4.0000.5528). Data collection was conducted via electronic medical records, and informed consent was waived. Data from patients who attended the clinics between January 2015 and July 2021 were included in this analysis.

Clinical and covariates

Sociodemographic, clinical, and anthropometric information was obtained. Data on age, ethnicity, level of education, marital status, age of menarche, nulliparity, and breastfeeding were collected. For clinical assessment, information related to the disease was recorded, including biological features of breast tumors (e.g., molecular markers: estrogen [ER], progesterone [PR], human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 - HER2), and Ki-67 expression levels determined immunohistochemically. Tumor, Node, Metastasis (TNM) stage (I to IV) was also evaluated. For initial analytical purposes about the disease’s severity, patients were grouped into stages I to II and stages III to IV. Body weight (kg) and height (m) were utilized to calculate BMI (kg/m2) and to classify body mass, according to the World Health Organization criteria [18].

Body composition from CT scans

Body composition was evaluated at the third lumbar vertebra (L3) level on CT scans obtained during disease staging, before chemotherapy initiation. Images were analyzed using Slice-O-Matic software (v.5.0, Tomovision®, Montreal, Quebec, Canada), applying standardized Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholds for tissue segmentation.

The muscles of interest at L3 were psoas, erector spinae, quadratus lumborum, transversus abdominis, internal and external obliques, and rectus abdominis. The area of SM (in cm2) and SMD (in HU) were delineated using a − 29 to + 150 HU range. Adipose tissue compartments were segmented using − 190 to − 30 HU for subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) and intermuscular adipose tissue (IMAT), and − 150 to − 50 HU for visceral adipose tissue (VAT). The distinction between SAT and IMAT was based on anatomical location: SAT was defined as adipose tissue located superficial to the outer muscle fascia (external to the rectus abdominis, obliques, and erector spinae) and enclosed beneath the skin contour. IMAT was defined as adipose tissue located beneath the outer muscle fascia, interspersed between or within muscle groups, without contact with the skin surface. Tissue boundaries were visually confirmed on each slice, ensuring that fascial planes were used as demarcation lines. Cross-sectional areas (cm²) were calculated by summing pixel counts multiplied by pixel surface area. SM was normalized by height² to calculate SMI [19]. All analyses were performed by a single trained radiologist with anatomical expertise (G.O.C.M.). Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated from 15 randomly selected images, each re-analyzed at six-month intervals; ICC values for all body composition parameters were 1.0 (95% CI: 1.0–1.0).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using MedCalc version 22.0.0.9 (MedCalc, Mariakerke, Belgium) and SPSS version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed variables are expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as absolute (N) and relative (%) frequencies, compared using Pearson’s χ2 or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Body composition cut-offs for muscle and adiposity parameters were defined using cohort-specific tertiles (Supplementary Table 1). Values below the lowest tertile (< P33) were classified as “low” for SM and SMD. Values above the highest tertile (> P66) were classified as “high” for adipose tissue compartments (i.e., IMAT, SAT, VAT, and TAT). These cohort-specific thresholds were adopted because no validated reference values exist for our specific population. Given known geographic and ethnic variability in body composition, region-specific cut-offs may provide more accurate classification than extrapolating from other populations.

Binary logistic regression (Wald test) and multinomial logistic regression were used to examine associations between body composition variables and disease severity, providing both crude and adjusted odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Exploratory correlations between CT-derived body composition parameters and tumor proliferation index (Ki-67) were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation.

Treatment modality data were available for most patients; however, due to the predominance of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (> 80%) and small numbers in adjuvant or palliative subgroups, formal stratified analyses by treatment type were not feasible. Furthermore, given the limited number of stage IV cases (n = 19), stages III and IV were grouped for analyses to preserve statistical power. We recognize that this grouping combines heterogeneous disease categories; however, stage-specific comparisons were not feasible due to small subgroup sizes. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

A total of 190 females newly diagnosed with breast cancer and not yet treated were included in the analysis. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 35 years (interquartile range 31–37), with no variation across TNM stage groups. Most participants self-identified as non-white, had completed elementary or secondary education, and lived with a partner. Nearly one-third of the cohort (≈ 30%) had triple-negative breast cancer. More than half of the sample presented with positive progesterone (57.9%) and estrogen (60.3%) receptor status, while 71.3% had a Ki-67 index > 20%. No significant clinical differences were observed between stage groups. Overall, 61% of participants had excess weight (overweight or obesity) by BMI, also with no differences between TNM stages.

Discussion

Our study evaluated CT-based body composition concerning TNM stage and molecular markers of disease severity in a cohort of young females newly diagnosed with breast cancer. We found that myosteatosis, reflected by higher IMAT and/or lower SMD, was independently associated with advanced disease stage (III-IV) and specific molecular subtypes (Luminal B, triple negative, and ER2-HER2 + overexpression). In contrast, other adipose compartments such as VAT, SAT, and TAT showed no significant associations. Our findings potentially emphasize the distinct metabolic and endocrine role of myosteatosis in breast cancer severity.

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to apply CT-defined body composition metrics, including IMAT and SMD, specifically to a younger breast cancer population. Most imaging-based body composition research in oncology has focused on older or mixed-age cohorts, where conventional anthropometric measures (e.g., BMI) may detect overt sarcopenia. In younger females, who often present without visible muscle wasting, CT-based identification of myosteatosis may offer prognostic and interventional opportunities not captured by standard assessments. This aligns with evidence that CT scanning provides superior anatomical detail compared to alternative methods, such as bioelectrical impedance analysis and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which can obscure adipose infiltration within muscle fibers and overestimate muscle mass [20–22].

“Myosteatosis” measured as high IMAT, has been associated with frailty [23], perioperative complications [24], poor quality of life [25], and decreased survival [26, 27] across multiple clinical contexts, including breast cancer. In our cohort, categorical SMD did not differ between TNM stage groups. This may reflect early-stage muscle alterations in which adipose infiltration is present but insufficient to significantly alter muscle density. The heterogeneous distribution of intermuscular adiposity and variation in CT slice selection may also influence SMD measurements [24]. Previous studies suggest that evaluating the total abdominal muscle area at L3 may fail to capture detailed anatomical variation in muscle adiposity [28, 29], whereas IMAT segmentation more precisely reflects inter- and perimuscular adipose content [28], partially explaining our findings. The absence of significant categorical SMD associations may also relate to the use of cohort-specific cut-offs.

When examined as a continuous variable, SMD showed an inverse association with molecular biomarkers (Luminal B and ER2-HER2+), suggesting that higher muscle quality could be linked to more favorable disease status [16]. This reinforces the sensitivity of continuous metrics compared with categorical classifications, particularly in small-sample analyses where variability may be masked by cut-off thresholds.

Mechanistically, myosteatosis has been linked to endocrine disorders such as insulin resistance and muscle dysfunction in both young and older adults [30]. IMAT can activate inflammatory pathways by stimulating macrophage cytokine secretion, which may impair insulin sensitivity [30]. Higher insulin resistance has been associated with increased breast cancer incidence [31], and given insulin’s role in muscle health [32], even young, functionally intact women with normal SMI and TAT could harbor higher risk factors for adverse prognosis due to the combined effects of myosteatosis, inflammation, and muscle impairment.

Myosteatosis may also develop alongside muscle atrophy [33], contributing to reduced functional capacity. Evidence from muscle biopsy studies in patients with breast cancer shows that IMAT levels can worsen after chemotherapy [34]. Accordingly, young females with poor baseline myosteatosis status (high IMAT, low SMD) may represent a high-risk subgroup for future adverse outcomes. Early detection could guide targeted nutritional and rehabilitative interventions aimed at preventing premature declines in function and quality of life [35, 36].

Our study is not without limitations. The observational cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships. Other unassessed pertinent factors may skew our findings, including physical activity status and dietary intake. The reliance on medical records may also introduce bias. Furthermore, due to logistical constraints, the contrast-enhanced phases in CT images were not standardized. It is currently discussed that variations in CT contrast phases can influence HU [37], affecting SMD and potentially impacting functional results [38] and myosteatosis evaluation. Furthermore, detailed treatment modality data were unavailable for a small proportion of patients (n = 4), and among those with available information, the majority (>80%) received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The small numbers in the adjuvant and palliative subgroups precluded adequately powered stratified analyses by treatment modality. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution regarding potential treatment-related effects on body composition.

Another limitation is that disease stages III and IV were analyzed together. This approach, although not ideal due to biological and prognostic differences between stages, was necessary because only 19 patients were classified as stage IV, precluding adequately powered TNM-specific comparisons. To surmount these limitations, future studies should aim to include larger and more representative samples, employ longitudinal models, and utilize CT images in non-contrast phases to validate our hypotheses.

Conclusion

Our study suggests the potential importance of assessing myosteatosis in young females newly diagnosed with breast cancer. We found that myosteatosis (using its CT-based proxies, IMAT and SMD) was independently associated with advanced disease staging and molecular biomarkers of breast cancer. Our results emphasize the importance of evaluating markers of morphological muscle “quality”, as it may precede changes in muscle quantity, and may prospectively correlate with adverse outcomes.
