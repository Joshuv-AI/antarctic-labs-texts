Construction and validation of risk prediction model for glioblastoma associated with cancer stem cells and disulfidptosis
Authors: Dang Tang, Zhongkun Ren, Bibo Gao, Jiang Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) is the most common malignant brain tumor, and effective therapeutic strategies remain scarce. Therefore, the study aims to screen biomarkers to reveal the molecular mechanisms of cancer stem cells (CSCs) and disulfidptosis in GBM therapy.MethodsThe transcriptome data and clinical data for GBM were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. The GSE74187 was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Firstly, the messenger RNA (mRNA)-based stemness index (mRNAsi) and disulfidptosis scores were computed, and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed in TCGA-GBM using mRNAsi and disulfidptosis scores as traits to obtain the key module genes. Secondly, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of the disease and normal groups in TCGA-GBM were screened, and DEGs were used to cross the key module gene to obtain intersection genes. Thirdly, univariate Cox, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed on intersection genes in TCGA-GBM to screen for biomarkers, and the biomarkers were used to construct survival risk score model. Meanwhile, clinical characteristics, immune infiltration, and drug sensitivity prediction of biomarkers were studied. To clinically validate the identified biomarkers, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis was conducted on 20 samples.ResultsLOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6 were obtained by three regression analyses to build survival risk score model, and they were verified in GSE74187. Clinical pathological features analysis found that risk score and isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) status were independent prognostic factors for GBM. Immune-related analysis showed that the risk score had positive correlation with PDCD1, NRP1, TGFB1, and PVRL2. In silico drug sensitivity prediction suggested differential responses to 88 compounds between risk groups. Furthermore, preliminary molecular docking analysis indicated potential binding affinity of A_443654 and A_770041 with the biomarker proteins, highlighting these compounds as candidates for further experimental investigation. The RT-qPCR results revealed significant overexpression of LOXL1 and SP6 in the tumor group compared to the control group, while LOXL4 expression remained with no significant difference between the groups.ConclusionsA risk prediction model based on LOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6 was constructed and validated. The model, rather than individual genes, provides valuable insights, providing valuable insights for the diagnosis of GBM in the field of CSC and disulfidptosis.Keywords: Glioblastoma (GBM), cancer stem cells (CSCs), disulfidptosis, bioinformatics analysis, prognosis

Highlight box

Key findings

• A three-gene risk model consisting of LOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6 was constructed and validated to effectively predict the prognosis of glioblastoma (GBM).

What is known and what is new?

• GBM is a common malignant primary brain tumor with limited therapies, and cancer stem cells and disulfidptosis are critical for GBM progression.

• The risk score is an independent prognostic factor, correlates with immune checkpoints, and distinguishes drug sensitivity; LOXL1/SP6 are overexpressed in GBM, providing novel biomarkers for diagnosis and individualized therapy.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The LOXL1/LOXL4/SP6 signature provides a basis for personalized GBM management, and further studies are needed to verify the functions of these genes, validate the model in larger cohorts, and explore targeted combination therapies to improve GBM treatment.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common primary malignant brain tumor caused by glial cell canceration in the brain and spinal cord, with an annual average incidence of 3.44/100,000, high degree of malignancy, high aggressiveness, and low 5-year survival rate (1). So far, there are many factors affecting its progression, including patient age, tumor grade, genetic mutations, scope of surgical resection, and radiotherapy and chemotherapy. As the disease progresses, about 90% of patients with GBM eventually die from tumor recurrence and metastasis. Current clinical treatment options for GBM include surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy, but the prognosis is still poor (2). Considering the current situation of poor prognosis and the lack of widely accepted prognostic prediction models, the construction of new prognostic models, the identification of high-risk patients, and accurate treatment can effectively improve the clinical prognosis of GBM.

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are defined as cells that have stem cell properties, the ability to self-renew, and promote the invasion and growth of tumor cells in tumors (3). CSCs show a high degree of plasticity, resulting in different cell phenotypes, functions, and metabolic characteristics. Cancer stemness (CS) assumes that tumor growth is driven by a limited number of specialized stem cells capable of self-renewal, representing the degree of tumorigenic dedifferentiation (4). Many researches have revealed the molecular mechanism of CS, for example, oxidation-reduction and JAK/STAT3 signaling pathways affect the status of breast CSCs; prostaglandin E2 can promote the expansion of colorectal CSCs; epithelial-mesenchymal transformation induces mitochondrial fusion by regulating the miR200c-PGC1α-MFN1 pathway and directs asymmetric division of mammary stem cells (5). There is growing evidence of the importance of CS biomarkers or CS genes in tumors, but their role in prognostic risk of GBM has less been evaluated.

Cell death is a physiological process that maintains biological development and homeostasis of internal environment. Recently, researchers have discovered and identified a completely new form of regulated cell death—disulfidptosis, opening up new possibilities for cancer treatment (6). Disulfidptosis is a rapid mode of death caused by disulfide stress due to excessive cystine accumulation in cells. Disulfidptosis is characterized by the rapid depletion of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) and high expression of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) during glucose starvation in cells, leading to abnormal cystine accumulation, which induces abnormal disulfide bond formation in actin cytoskeleton proteins, and this eventually leads to the breakdown of the actin network and disulfidptosis (7). Recent researches have found that disulfidptosis plays a significant role in the progression of cancer, suggesting novel approaches for prevention and treatment. However, disulfidptosis related prognostic model has less been reported in GBM.

Disulfidptosis is a novel form of programmed cell death triggered by abnormal intracellular cystine accumulation, which induces disulfide bond stress and ultimately leads to the collapse of the cytoskeletal protein network (8). Studies have demonstrated that both disulfidptosis and CSCs are key factors contributing to therapeutic resistance in GBM (9,10). Specifically, the survival and functional status of CSCs are closely associated with the specific intracellular redox microenvironment (11), while disulfidptosis also modulates cellular redox homeostasis (12). These findings suggest that they may exert synergistic or complementary effects in the progression of GBM. Therefore, a systematic investigation into the crosstalk between CSCs and disulfidptosis in GBM may provide new insights into understanding the mechanisms underlying therapeutic resistance and developing novel treatment strategies. However, systematic research on the interaction between these two factors and their synergistic prognostic value in GBM remains lacking. Hence, identifying key gene modules co-regulated by CSCs properties and disulfidptosis in GBM, and constructing more accurate prognostic models based on these modules, possesses important clinical significance for exploring new therapeutic strategies against GBM.

This study aims to screen and identify key genes of GBM related to disulfidptosis score and messenger RNA (mRNA)-based stemness index (mRNAsi) modules through bioinformatics methods, and explore their biological functions, so as to reveal the possible pathogenesis of GBM (including whether it is related to disulfidptosis score and mRNAsi respectively, or whether both occur at the same time to affect disease development, etc.). Furthermore, a risk prognostic prediction model is constructed to obtain biomarkers and evaluate their relationship with clinical features, immune correlation, and drug sensitivity, so as to better understand the pathogenesis of GBM associated with disulfidptosis and CS. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1-2754/rc).

Methods

Data collection

The transcriptome data and clinical data for The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-GBM were acquired from the TCGA database. The RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data for TCGA-GBM included 168 tumor samples and 5 normal samples for the following study. Subsequently, the GBM-related dataset (GSE74187) (13) was sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, including 60 tumor samples to verify for GBM prognostic model. This dataset was generated using the Agilent microarray platform (GPL6480) and constitutes gene expression profile data. The 16 genes (14) were associated with disulfidptosis and known as disulfidptosis-related genes (DRGs) (Table S1) (15-17).

Calculation and subsistence analysis of mRNAsi and disulfidptosis scores

In TCGA-GBM, the differences in mRNAsi were analyzed using “Wilcoxon” test. The disulfidptosis scores were counted using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) of the “gene set variation analysis (GSVA)” (version 1.42.0) (18). Then, based on the optimal cut-off for mRNAsi and the disulfidptosis scores separately, all samples were divided into the high- and low-score groups. The DESeq2 package was used to screen for differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Firstly, DESeq2 will preprocess and standardize the original sequencing data. This included steps such as removing low-quality reads and calculating the number of reads (i.e., count number) for each gene. Standardization was carried out to address the issue of differences in gene expression levels caused by variations in library size (i.e., sequencing depth) and library composition. DESeq2 calculated the size factor for each sample and divided the number of reads of each gene by the corresponding size factor to obtain the standardized expression level. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was evaluated the overall survival (OS) between high-/low-score groups by using the “survminer” (version 0.4.9) (19).

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) analysis

The associations between modules and biological traits were analyzed by “WGCNA” (version 1.71) (20). Firstly, sample clustering was performed to remove outlier samples. Secondly, in order to maximize compliance with the scale-free distribution in the interactions between genes, soft thresholds were performed on the data. Next, the similarity was calculated based on the neighbor-joining among genes, and a systematic clustering tree was acquired. Similar modules analysis was performed by dynamic tree cutting algorithm. In order to find modules related to traits, the correlation of each module and traits was analyzed, and the modules with the highest correlation with the trait were selected as the key modules. The genes in key modules were defined as key module genes.

Identification of intersecting genes

The genome expression data of samples treated with tumor samples and normal samples (GSE74187) in each data set were analyzed by “DESeq2” (version 3.50.3) (21). DEGs were filtered by the following terms: |log2fold change (FC)| >2 and P value <0.05. Subsequently, use the “ggVenn” (version 1.2.2) (22) to intersect the DEGs and key module genes to obtain the intersecting genes, and the intersecting genes were visualized using the “ggVolcano” (version 0.02) (23) and “ComplexHeatmap” (version 2.14.0) (24).

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction and enrichment analysis

The intersecting genes were subjected to PPI network construction and enrichment analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were executed by “clusterProfiler” (version 4.6.2) (25), with P<0.05 as threshold. The results were visualized by “treemap” (version 2.4-3) (26). In addition, in order to explore the interaction relationship between intersection genes, the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database (https://string-db.org/) was used for PPI network analysis of intersecting genes.

Establishment of prognostic intersecting genes

The intersecting genes were subjected to univariate Cox regression analysis (P value <0.01). The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis was performed to screen characteristic genes by “glmnet” (version 4.1-4) (27), and parameter settings: family = “cox”, nfold =10. The multivariate Cox analysis was performed on the characteristic genes to obtain biomarkers, and set the parameter direction to “both”. The subsequent analysis utilized the “psych” (version 2.5.3) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=psych) to perform Spearman correlation analysis between the obtained biomarkers and classical glioma stem cell markers, with thresholds set at |cor| >0.3 and P<0.05. The multifactor coefficient of each biomarker was calculated and constructed survival risk model. Then, survival risk model evaluation was performed in TCGA-GBM, and the formula utilized was used. By the biomarkers risk-score, all GBM disease samples were stratified into the high- and low-risk groups, with the median risk value (0.955) as the cut-off. Additionally, for more granular stratification, the samples were further subdivided into four risk tiers (Q1 to Q4) based on their quartiles to perform KM survival analysis, which was evaluated using the “survminer” (version 0.4.9) (28). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized to assess the accuracy in prognosis prediction of risk model, and the area under the curve (AUC) for 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS was measured using the “survival” (version 3.2-13) (29). Furthermore, to validate the suitability of risk model, the validation was performed in GSE74187.

Correlation of clinical pathological features with TCGA-GBM samples

The clinical pathological features [age, gender, radiation therapy, isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) status, transcriptome subtype, and risk score] of disease in TCGA-GBM were covered by the univariate Cox analysis to explore risk model and independent prognosis of clinical pathological features, with P value <0.05 as threshold. The “survival” (version 3.2-13) (29) was conducted PH hypothesis test on the above clinical pathological features to determine whether satisfied the multifactor Cox regression model. Subsequently, the independent prognostic factors were scored separately, and the total scores of each factor were added to the corresponding total scoring. The survival rates of 1-, 2-, and 3-year were predicted and drawn the ROC curve and calibration curve.

Assessment of immune relevance

The “ssGSEA” algorithm of the “GSVA” (version 1.42.0) (18) was analyzed the infiltration of 28 immune cells in the high- and low-risk groups, and the score of each immune cell was obtained. The Spearman method was analyzed for the correlation between risk scores and immune-related markers. Differences in Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) and tumor mutational burden (TMB) scores between the high- and low-risk groups were analyzed by “Wilcoxon” and visualized using the “ggplot2” (version 3.3.6) (30). The infiltration of immune cells was further analyzed using the “Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER)” algorithm from the “Immuno-Oncology Biological Research (IOBR)” (version 0.99.9), with significant differences assessed by the Wilcoxon test. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis (|cor| >0.3, P<0.05) between biomarkers and immune cells, as well as immune checkpoints, was performed using the “psych” (version 2.5.3).

Molecular regulation network analysis

To better understand the regulatory role of upstream regulators in the biomarkers, the miRNet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/) was used to predict transcription factors (TFs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) associated with these biomarkers. The molecular regulatory networks between non-coding RNAs, TFs, and biomarkers were explored, and the TF-mRNA-miRNA regulatory network was visualized.

In silico prediction of drug sensitivity and molecular docking

According to the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value of the drug was calculated through the “pRRophetic” (version 0.5) (31), and the “Wilcox” test was compared the IC50 values of drugs in the high- and low-risk groups. Finally, the “ggplot2” (version 3.3.6) (30) was used for visualization. To verify the binding ability between sensitive drugs and biomarker proteins, this study further explored the molecular docking patterns of drug-biomarker. The specific steps were as follows: (I) the three-dimensional (3D) structures of biomarkers (PDB files) were obtained from the Alphafold database (https://alphafold.com/); (II) the 3D structure files of drugs (SDF files) were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/); (III) the PDB files of biomarkers and SDF files of drugs were imported into the cb-dock2 website (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php) to calculate charges and affinities, and the docking results were exported; and (IV) the docking results were visualized using Pymol (2.4.0) software. The results were ranked by analyzing the binding sites between biomarkers and drugs, and a docking score of ≤−5.0 kcal·mol−1 was determined as indicating good binding affinity of the drug to the target.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

In this study, a total of 20 tissue samples, including 10 GBM samples and 10 control samples, were collected from The First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Human Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (No. 2024L135). Informed consent was taken from all the patients. Total RNA was subsequently extracted from the tissues using chloroform and TRIzol reagent. Following RNA extraction, reverse transcription into cDNA was performed using the SweScript First Strand Complementary DNA (cDNA) Synthesis Kit from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). For the RT-qPCR reactions, cDNA was mixed with 2× Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix, forward primer (10 µM), and reverse primer (10 µM) (Table 1), and the reactions were carried out on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System. Finally, the 2−ΔΔCT values were calculated, and the P values were determined using GraphPad Prism 5.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses for the bioinformatics section were performed in R (v 4.1.0), with Student’s t-test used for between-group comparisons. For RT-qPCR analysis, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for between-group comparisons, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Totally 3,132 DEGs between disease and normal samples were mined in the TCGA-GBM, including 1,448 up-regulated and 1,684 down-regulated genes. WGCNA was accomplished to screen key module genes. Firstly, the result of the sample clustering outliers showed that three outlier samples were obtained, including TCGA-28-5218-01A, TCGA-14-0871-01A, and TCGA-06-2569-01A, and the 170 samples were retained for subsequent analysis (Figure 2A). Secondly, the optimal soft threshold was set to 10 to construct the co-expression network (Figure 2B), and 17 modules except for MEgrey were identified (Figure 2C). The MEdarkturquoise was negatively associated in mRNAsi score (cor =−0.73, P=1e−29), and it was positively associated in DRGs score (cor =0.68, P=7e−25). A total of 460 genes were obtained as key module genes (Figure 2D). Finally, the Venn diagrams showed that the DEGs and key module genes were taken to intersect to obtain 133 intersecting genes, and they were also showed using volcano and heat maps (Figure 2E-2G).

Construction and verification of survival risk score model

The univariate Cox, LASSO, and multivariate Cox analyses were performed in TCGA-GBM to obtain survival risk score model. Firstly, univariate Cox analysis found that 15 characteristic genes were significantly related to prognosis (P<0.01). However, the HR of MSTN was <1, which was a protective factor for prognosis. And the remaining 14 characteristic genes were risk factors for prognosis (Figure 4A). Then, the 15 genes in the univariate Cox were used to screen characteristic genes using the LASSO analysis, as shown that eight characteristic genes were obtained, including MDK, IGFBP2, LOXL1, LOXL4, MSTN, IGFBP4, PDIA4, and SP6 (Figure 4B,4C). Finally, eight characteristic genes were subjected to stepwise multivariate Cox analysis to obtain three biomarkers (LOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6) (Figure 4D). Subsequently, correlation analysis was conducted between the three biomarkers and classical glioma stem cell markers (CD133, SOX2, OLIG2). The results showed that all three biomarkers were significantly negatively correlated with SOX2 (P<0.05), while LOXL1 and LOXL4 were significantly negatively correlated with OLIG2 (P<0.001) (Figure 4E), demonstrating the reliability of the obtained biomarkers. The multivariate coefficient of each biomarker was counted to construct survival risk model. Then, the GBM disease samples were stratified into low-/high-risk groups. KM survival curves found that the high-risk samples were lower survival rate (Figure 4F). Further dividing the samples into four risk tiers (Q1 to Q4) based on their quartiles to draw KM survival curves, the results showed that the high-risk group had the lowest survival rate while the low-risk group had the highest (P<0.001), indicating the robust prognostic stratification ability of this scoring model (Figure 4G). The risk curves and heat map found that the higher the expression of LOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6, the higher the risk of the patient (Figure 4H-4J). The time-dependent ROC analysis showed that AUC with 1-, 2-, and 3-year were 0.658, 0.648, and 0.786 (Figure 4K). The above results were validated in GSE74187, and the result was consistence with the outcome in TCGA-GBM, and all the high-risk samples were lower survival rate (Figure 4L-4O). AUC with 1-, 2-, and 3-year were 0.644, 0.721, and 0.700, respectively (Figure 4P).

Risk score and IDH status were independent prognostic factors

To figure out the relationship between clinical characteristics and patient survival, univariate Cox analysis was explored the clinical pathological features and independent prognosis factors. The results showed that age, radiation therapy, IDH status, and risk score results were significant (P<0.05) (Figure 5A). The multivariate Cox model showed that age and radiation therapy were excluded (P<0.05). The remaining four variables were allowed by multivariate Cox analysis, as shown that the risk score and IDH status were independent prognostic factors for GBM (Figure 5B,5C). Then, the line diagram was constructed for the independent prognostic factors. The ROC curves showed that AUC with 1-, 2-, and 3-year were 0.655, 0.643, and 0.855, respectively (Figure 5D-5F). Finally, the box plots showed that age, IDH status, transcriptome subtype, and risk score were related (P<0.05). The heat map showed that age, IDH status, and transcriptome subtype were different between the high- and low-risk groups (P<0.05) (Figure 5G,5H).

The landscape of tumor microenvironment (TME) immune-related analysis in GBM

The “ssGSEA” algorithm was used to analyze infiltration of 28 immune cells in the two risk groups. The results showed that 26 immune cells were significantly higher in the high-risk group (P<0.05) (Figure 6A). The Spearman method showed that the risk score and PDCD1, NRP1, TGFB1, and PVRL2 were positive correlation (P<0.05) (Figure 6B). According to reference, a total of 33 immune checkpoints were obtained, as shown that the significant differences of 23 immune checkpoints were higher in the high-risk group (Figure 6C). Finally, the TIDE score between the high- and low-risk groups was executed, as shown that the TIDE score in the high-risk groups was significantly higher (P<0.05) (Figure 6D). But the TMB score between the high- and low-risk groups was no difference (Figure 6E). Further analysis of the six immune cell types using the “TIMER” algorithm revealed that the infiltration levels of all six were significantly different between the two groups. Among these, five were significantly higher in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (P<0.05) (Figure 6F). The Spearman method indicated that the biomarkers LOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6 showed significant correlations with these immune cells (P<0.05) (Figure 6G). Drug sensitivity analysis and molecular docking

The sensitivity of 138 commonly used drugs was analyzed, and drug sensitivity was represented by IC50 value. As shown, the drug sensitivity was compared with high-/low-expression groups, and the IC50 of 88 drugs were significant differences between the two risk groups (P<0.05), including A.443654, A.770041, ABT.263, AS601245, ATRA, AUY922, etc. (Figure 7B). Furthermore, in this study, molecular docking was performed between the LOXL1 (Figure 7C,7D), LOXL4 (Figure 7E,7F), and SP6 (Figure 7G,7H) genes and the drugs A_443654 and A_770041. The results showed that LOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6 all had strong binding affinities with A_443654 and A_770041. These exploratory in silico results suggest that A_443654 and A_770041 may possess binding potential to the identified biomarker proteins, warranting further experimental validation to assess their actual therapeutic efficacy against GBM.

Discussion

Although enormous efforts have been taken in the development of therapies for GBM, there is a poor prognosis attributed to ineffective anti-tumor treatments with significant challenges due to its immunosuppressive features, aberrant tumor stroma, and spatiotemporal heterogeneity (32). Early detection and diagnosis methods are crucial for improving the OS rate and prognosis of GBM patients. Therefore, new insights into prognostic prediction and treatment approaches for GBM patients are necessary. Recently, many prognostic models through bioinformatic analysis methods have been constructed in GBM, including the expression profiles of autophagy, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis-related genes, which provide new theoretical support for developing an effective treatment strategy and predicting the prognosis of GBM patients (33). CSCs charactered with stem cell properties promote the invasion and growth of tumor cells in cancer. Disulfidptosis as a distinct form of regulated cell death results from excessive disulfide stress. Further exploration and validation of disulfidptosis-targeting strategies in preclinical and clinical settings could open up a new era of precision medicine for SLC7A11-high cancers (34). Accordingly, this study mainly applied bioinformatics to identify prognostic biomarkers associated with disulfidptosis and CSC, and constructed risk model and nomogram to predict the survival status of GBM patients. The relationship between risk model, immunotherapy, and drug sensitivity was further analyzed to provide reference for effectively improving the clinical prognosis of GBM and formulating immunotherapy strategies.

The CSCs bring cellular heterogeneity, innate antibiotic resistance, and invasive capacity in malignancy, which promote cancer progression and metastasis (35). High stemness index scores are associated with poor survival in cancer patients, and the mRNAsi is typically conducted in tumor tissues (36). Several conventional CSCs markers, including CD44, CD90, and SOX2, have been identified in lung cancer. Li et al. have revealed that lung cancer tissues exhibit higher mRNAsi values than normal tissues, and suggest that high mRNAsi values are associated with poor survival in lung adenocarcinoma patients (37). Su et al. have reported that the mRNAsi value of metastatic tumors is higher than primary tumors, particularly in prostate and pancreatic cancers (38). High mRNAsi values were associated with a worse prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma (39). mRNAsi was significantly related to multi-omics parameters, and CHI3L2, FSTL3, RPA3, RRM2, and YTHDF2 were obtained to be the targets of GBM stem cell therapy (40). Zhang et al. evaluated GBM stemness indices based on large cohorts and established a corrected mRNAsi-based classifier for prognosis prediction (41). Disulfidptosis is a novel form of programmed cell death involving crucial physiological processes, which serves as a mediator for the therapeutic efficacy of glucose transporter inhibitors in the treatment of SLC7A11-high cancers (42). Zhang et al. proposed prognostic model consisted of four disulfidptosis-related gene signatures (CLCNKA, AC068987.2, AC097641.2, and AL031432.5), which may have a role in regulating the development and progression of GBM by modulating disulfidptosis (43). An eight-gene disulfidptosis-based subtype predictor was developed, and disulfidptosis-based classification offers a prognostic predictor for GBM (44). Twenty-three disulfidptosis-related genes were successfully identified to demonstrate substantial potential for establishing a prognostic model for GBM (45). Furthermore, a comprehensive assessment of stemness indices and disulfidptosis in GBM remains required for precise prognosis prediction and targeted treatment.

Consequently, we acquired RNA-seq data and clinical characteristics of GBM from the TCGA database. Firstly, to calculate the mRNAsi and DRGs scores of each sample of TCGA-GBM, and analyze the differences of mRNAsi and DRGs scores between tumor and normal samples in TCGA-GBM by the Wilcoxon. Then, to divide the samples into high- and low-score groups according to the optimal threshold of mRNAsi and DRGs scores, respectively, and analyze the survival differences of high- and low-score groups combined with TCGA-GBM survival information. To take mRNAsi and DRGs scores as traits to analyze the TCGA-GBM gene by WGCNA, and a total of 460 modular genes related to traits were screened. To screen DEGs between tumor and normal samples through TCGA-GBM, a total of 3,132 DEGs were obtained, of which 1,448 were up-regulated and 1,684 were down-regulated. Then, the intersecting genes were obtained by intersecting module genes and DEGs. To construct the PPI network for intersecting genes through the STRING database to show the interaction between the intersecting genes, and make the GO and KEGG functional enrichment analysis. Based on intersecting genes of TCGA-GBM, univariate Cox regression analysis, LASSO analysis, and multivariate Cox analysis were performed for a survival risk model construction to obtain three biomarkers (LOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6), and the validation was performed in GSE74187. Subsequent validation results from RT-qPCR showed that LOXL1 and SP6 were significantly upregulated in GBM, whereas the expression level of LOXL4 showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups. These findings indicate that LOXL1 and SP6 have potential diagnostic or prognostic value, while whether LOXL4 exerts a functional role in GBM remains to be further investigated. Studies have reported that LOXL1, part of the LOX family, is implicated in various cancers, influencing cell proliferation and metastasis through ECM remodeling (46,47). Studies have shown that LOXL4 expression levels and tumor proliferation, migration, invasion and patients’ prognosis and OS in different types of tumors (48). Recent study has found that LOXL1 and LOXL4 are key risk genes that promote GBM progression and may act through complex intercellular communication, which is consistent with our results (49). SP6 is a transcription factor gene which belongs to a family of transcription factors that contain three classical zinc finger DNA-binding domains consisting of a zinc atom tetrahedrally coordinated by two cysteines and two histidines. SP6 is a candidate gene that may be involved in the pathogenesis of prostate cancer (50). SP6 has also been implicated in breast cancer therapy resistance and linked to the regulation of the Wnt-BMP signaling pathway (51).

The risk prognostic model incorporating LOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6, which was constructed in the context of GBM, holds important biological significance, especially when considering its potential association with CSCs and the disulfidptosis mechanism. LOXL1 catalyzes the cross-linking of collagen and elastin fibers in the ECM, which can create a more stable microenvironment for CSCs (52). This stable ECM can enhance the adhesion of CSCs, protecting them from detachment-induced cell death and promoting their self-renewal properties. By modifying the ECM structure, LOXL1 may affect the diffusion of ROS and antioxidants, which in turn can impact the sensitivity of GBM cells to disulfidptosis (53). Moreover, LOXL1 may interact with proteins involved in the cellular antioxidant defense system. In CSCs, LOXL4 can contribute to the formation of a permissive niche for these cells and may also be involved in the regulation of cell adhesion molecules (54). LOXL4 is associated with the regulation of cell metabolism of ECM components (55). Additionally, it could modulate the activity of enzymes involved in the synthesis or degradation of disulfide bonds, which are central to the process of disulfidptosis. In CSCs of GBM, SP6 may play a role in maintaining the undifferentiated state of these cells. It could potentially regulate the expression of genes that are essential for CSC self-renewal, such as pluripotency factors (56). The importance of SP6 in the proliferation and differentiation of enamel epithelial cells provides a basis for understanding the role of SP6 in regulating gene expression in cell development (57). It may directly or indirectly control the expression of genes encoding proteins involved in the disulfidptosis pathway. In the correlation analysis, the biomarkers exhibited a significant negative correlation with OLIG2 and SOX2, two classic markers of GSCs. These results indicate that these genes may affect patient prognosis through mechanisms independent of the canonical stem cell core pathways. For instance, members of the LOXL protein family are primarily involved in the remodeling and crosslinking of the ECM (58). Specifically, LOXL1 and LOXL4 may promote tumor invasion and impede the penetration of chemotherapeutic agents by remodeling the ECM to form a dense tissue barrier (59), while also creating a microenvironment that protects stem cells from immune attack and anoikis (60). In addition, SP6 is likely involved in the regulation of ECM remodeling processes (61). Ultimately, this pro-survival and pro-invasion microenvironment, synergistically shaped by LOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6, may essentially represent a distinctive state of tumor dedifferentiation mediated by the ECM (62). This dedifferentiated state is quantified by the mRNAsi (63) and is simultaneously associated with the intratumoral stress microenvironment that predisposes cells to disulfidptosis (64,65).

Correlation between clinical features and patient survival were covered by the Cox analysis to explore independent prognosis. The result showed risk score and IDH status were independent prognostic factors in GBM. To understand the correlation between risk scores and clinical features, we included 167 samples from the TCGA database. The result showed risk scores were correlated with age, IDH status, transcriptome subtype, and indicated the differences between high- and low-risk groups. TME has a significant effect on the development of tumors, and the Spearman method showed that the risk score and immune-related markers (PDCD1, NRP1, TGFB1, and PVRL2) were positive correlation in our study. Immune checkpoints are a class of immunosuppressive molecules to keep the level of immune system activation within the normal range and avoid overactivation of the immune system. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to determine the difference between high- and low-risk groups, and the results showed that many immune checkpoints, including CD44, TNFRSF9, NRP1, CD40, CD40LG, CD276, TNFSF4, CD274, CD80, TNFSF14, IDO1, VTCN1, CD27, TNFRSF14, CTLA4, ICOS, CD200R1, KIR3DL1, CD28, TIGIT, TNFRSF18, PDCD1, and IDO2, were significant differences. TIDE is another biomarker for evaluating immune escape mechanisms for predicting immunotherapy response. A higher TIDE score means that tumor cells are more likely to evade immune surveillance, indicating a lower response rate to immunotherapy. We also analyzed clinical responses to anti-PD1 and anti-CTLA4 therapy in the high- and low-risk groups, and showed that the TIDE score in the high-risk group was significantly higher, which demonstrated the high-risk group had a higher probability of immune dysfunction. Furthermore, the sensitivity analysis of 138 common drugs was carried out, and showed that 88 drugs were significant differences between high- and low-risk groups, including A.443654, A.770041, ABT.263, AS601245, ATRA, AUY922, etc.

The study successfully identified three mRNAsi and disulfidptosis-associated genes in predicting prognosis, immune infiltration, immunotherapy response, and drug sensitivity in GBM. Previous research has found that different tumor types express dysregulated levels of disulfidptosis regulators, and most affecting tumor prognosis. Also, disulfidptosis activity correlates with classic BPs and pathways in various cancers, and is linked to tumor immune characteristics and could predict immunotherapy outcomes (66). Furthermore, preclinical studies show that glucose transporter inhibitor-based metabolic therapy can trigger disulfidptosis and suppress cancer growth (67). These findings provide a novel avenue for GBM treatment strategies and predicting survival prognosis. The development of the risk model may facilitate personalized treatment approaches for GBM to improve their overall prognosis and quality of life. However, there are certain limitations in this study that should be acknowledged. First, the number of normal control samples used in the analysis of DEGs was limited, and the entire study was based on a restricted set of databases. Future research should incorporate more diverse and large-scale cohorts to improve the generalizability of the model. Second, both CSCs and disulfidptosis represent emerging research fields, and the specific functional links between the identified biomarkers and these two BPs require further in-depth experimental validation. Furthermore, although the model exhibited prognostic stratification ability in independent datasets, its application value in broader clinical settings still needs to be further evaluated. Meanwhile, the sample size for the preliminary RT-qPCR validation was relatively small, which warrants expansion for confirmation. Finally, the computationally derived results of drug sensitivity prediction and molecular docking are exploratory findings, which need to be verified using experimentally resolved structures and rigorous preclinical studies. In summary, future research should focus on expanding sample sizes, conducting in-depth in vitro and in vivo mechanistic experiments, and performing stringent preclinical efficacy validation, thereby facilitating the translation of these findings into clinical applications.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identified LOXL1, LOXL4, and SP6 as reliable biomarkers for GBM by integrating bioinformatics analysis and clinical validation. These biomarkers are closely related to CSCs and disulfidptosis, and the constructed risk score model can effectively predict the prognosis of GBM patients, provide insights into immune regulation and drug selection, and ultimately offer important reference value for the clinical diagnosis, prognosis evaluation, and targeted therapy of GBM in the field of CSC and disulfidptosis research.
