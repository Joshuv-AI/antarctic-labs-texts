Harnessing survey data across multiple demographic groups in Illinois to assess survey questions and reveal insights about ticks and tick-borne disease risk: a meta-comparison study
Authors: Sulagna Chakraborty, Abrar Hussain, Nohra Mateus-Pinilla, Rebecca L Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTicks and tick-borne diseases (TTBDs) are major public and veterinary health issues, existing at the intersection of behavioral, environmental, ecological, climatic, and entomological factors. Knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) surveys have become an insightful tool to yield information regarding the behavioral factors that can predispose individuals to TTBDs. Here we present a unique meta-comparison study of six KAP surveys on TTBDs conducted in Illinois among various demographics: farmers, Extension workers, general public, veterinary, human medicine, and public health professionals. Our goals were multi-fold: (a) to develop an approach to compare KAP surveys used across several stakeholders on a subject of public health concern (i.e., TTBD risk), (b) to analyze the usefulness of questions for revealing information on TTBDs across varied demographic groups in Illinois, and (c) to reveal qualitative insights from the open text survey responses across these surveyed groups.MethodsWe conducted comparative and item response theory (IRT) analysis of all the surveyed questions, which yielded a final set of 39 questions on knowledge and practices, most of them with discriminatory power. Independent textual analysis of the survey responses led to the development of eight separate sub-categories across three main super categories: ticks and tick-borne disease (TBD) risk, TBD treatment and management, and TBD prevention.ResultsThis study not only introduces a novel approach for comparing multiple KAP surveys on the topic of TTBDs, but also highlights gaps regarding TTBD knowledge, perceptions, and prevention measures among a variety of stakeholders and calls for tailored training and awareness campaigns to reduce the burden of TBDs in the Midwestern US. This meta-comparison approach unveils additional insights from the combined analyses of the KAP surveys. We also discuss perspectives of the surveyed demographics, their experiences with TTBDs, and provide recommendations for future public health efforts.ConclusionThese results can guide public health campaigns around TBDs in the United States and help reduce the increasing burden of TBDs on humans and animals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28618-0.Keywords: Tick-borne diseases, Public health, Tick exposure, Methodology, KAP surveys, IRT, Textual analysis

Background

Over the past several decades, the incidence of notifiable tick-borne diseases (TBDs) has steadily increased, comprising over 75% of vector-borne infections reported annually in the United States and posing ongoing and emerging challenges to public health [1, 2]. Ticks of medical and veterinary significance, including the lone star tick (Amblyomma americanum) and black-legged ticks (Ixodes scapularis and Ixodes pacificus), have become increasingly a hazard across North America in recent years [3–6]. The annual economic burden of Lyme disease, a single tick-borne illness, in the United States, is estimated to range from $345 million to $968 million [7]. However, this figure is under-estimated due to the underreporting of cases [8]. In Illinois and the surrounding North Central Region, the incidence of Lyme disease has risen significantly, with the number of counties classified as high incidence increasing by approximately 250% between 1993 and 2012 [9]. Reported cases of TBDs such as Lyme disease, Rocky Mountain spotted fever (RMSF), ehrlichiosis, and anaplasmosis in Illinois saw a tenfold increase between 2000 and 2020 [10, 11]. Additionally, A. americanum has been linked to Alpha-gal syndrome, a condition in which an allergic reaction to the carbohydrate galactose-α-1,3-galactose occurs after exposure through tick bites [12]; as Alpha-gal is not a reportable disease, there is limited understanding of its incidence and geographical spread. Over the past two decades, the primary tick species responsible for spreading these diseases (I. scapularis, A. americanum, Amblyomma maculatum, and Dermacentor variabilis) have expanded their geographical range [13, 14], and are now established throughout Illinois [15, 16]. Effectively addressing ticks and tick-borne diseases (TTBDs) requires a holistic approach integrating expertise and tools from multiple disciplines such as epidemiology, public health, social science, and a One Health lens [5].

One valuable tool within this integrated approach is the Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) survey, which is a type of survey questionnaire with specific sections to determine targeted demographics’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices on a topic of interest. These KAP surveys can help identify gaps in people’s understanding and behavior, as well as barriers to change, insights that are essential for designing targeted public health strategies. These surveys have been used to uncover community perceptions about TTBDs while identifying deficiencies in preventative practices specific to the surveyed population. Across the United States, KAP findings related to TBDs differ between communities, reflecting varying levels of engagement with tick prevention measures [17–19]. This diversity highlights the need for tailored public health messaging to enhance the effectiveness of TBD prevention efforts. Such messaging should consider the complexity of behavior change and the psychological and demographic characteristics of the different target audiences [20–21].

Target audiences for KAP surveys on TBDs may include the general public, high-risk groups, or professionals dealing with TBDs in health settings. Conducting surveys among different professionals within a specific region about a particular issue or disease is instrumental in identifying knowledge gaps, attitudes, and barriers to effective practice. For instance, a recent study emphasized the importance of community-based participatory research (CBPR) in designing, conducting, and evaluating health interventions within American Indian communities [22]. The research highlights how engaging local professionals and community members through surveys and collaborative methods can uncover specific health challenges and cultural factors, leading to more effective and culturally appropriate health strategies. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [23] emphasize the value of surveying healthcare professionals and stakeholders within a region and how this information is essential for developing evidence-based interventions and policies tailored to the specific health needs of the population. These examples underscore the significance of regional surveys among different professional groups as critical tools for obtaining detailed insights into local health challenges. The data gathered through such surveys facilitates the development of targeted and effective public health strategies that addresses identified gaps and barriers, ultimately improving health outcomes within the community.

Due to the established presence of medically important tick species in Illinois [15, 16–24], and rising incidence in cases of TBDs across various counties in Illinois [11], we wanted to synthesize information from KAP surveys pertaining to TTBDs conducted among a variety of demographic groups through a unique meta-comparison study. We had three objectives guiding this study: (a) to develop and present a new approach to compare KAP surveys used across several stakeholders on a subject of public health concern i.e., TTBD risk, (b) to assess the discriminatory value of KAP questions on TTBDs for assessment of individuals’ knowledge and attitudes on TTBDs in the Midwestern region, and (c) to synthesize the previously unanalyzed qualitative information from the free-text survey responses to yield additional insights related to TTBDs in these demographic groups. The extensive information derived from KAP surveys on the shared topic of TTBDs offers valuable perspectives that can inform and enhance future interventions and policies to reduce the TBD burden. Additionally, this new approach could be applied to analyze other collections of KAP studies within a given region.

Methods

a) KAP meta-comparison approach

To compare the aforementioned KAP surveys in Illinois, we present a novel approach to synthesize and present valuable information from multiple surveys using quantitative and qualitative methods. We compiled data from six KAP survey studies in Illinois among public health professionals [25], medical health professionals [26], veterinary health professionals [27], farmers [28], Extension workers [29], and dog owners [manuscript under review] in one Excel spreadsheet. Public health professionals are personnel working in public health agencies or departments. Extension workers are individuals affiliated with land-grant institutions in the state who work closely with citizens, act as liaisons between researchers and the public and help spread awareness on multiple topics. Each of the surveys had questions divided into sections on demographics, knowledge, personal exposure, attitudes, and practices. The number of responses received from each of the surveys are as follows: public health professionals (n = 60), medical health professionals (n = 356), veterinary health professionals (n = 72), farmers (n = 50), Extension workers (n = 233), and dog owners (n = 605). Each individual study received individual Institutional Review Board (IRB) approvals from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, all data were de-identified prior to re-use, and no new data were collected; thus, IRB approval for this meta-comparison study was not required. Two authors (SC and AH) independently selected a set of key questions from each of the knowledge, attitudes, and practices sections of each KAP survey. Items were chosen based on their conceptual relevance to assessing awareness, perceived risk, and tick-related behaviors, and priority was given to questions that appeared across multiple surveys or were adaptable for cross-group comparison. Disagreements in item inclusion were resolved through consensus after discussion, and a third author was consulted (if needed) to finalize the selection. The total set of 53 questions (S1) comprising seven demographic items, 15 Knowledge items, 16 Attitude items, 13 practice items, and two personal exposure items were chosen based on relevance to assessing an individual’s awareness, knowledge, and practices with respect to TTBDs.

Due to the lack of uniformity across survey instruments, some surveys included only a subset of the items considered in this analysis, limiting the ability to make direct comparisons across all demographic groups. We addressed this using the approach described below, which allowed us to develop a refined pool of items that can serve as a valuable resource for designing standardized and more comparable future KAP surveys in Illinois.

b) Item response theory (IRT) analysis

The second objective was to assess the value of individual questions for use in future KAP surveys on TTBDs in the Midwestern region. To do so, we first wanted to understand which questions were asked across all the surveyed groups and then to assess the relative value of each of those questions for differentiating participants within a subgroup with regard to knowledge, attitudes, and practices using Item Response Theory (IRT) analysis [30]. Item Response Theory (IRT) is a group of statistical methods used to understand how individuals with different levels of ability or traits respond to test items. It models the relationship between a person’s underlying characteristics, such as knowledge or practices, and the likelihood of answering each question correctly [31–32]. IRT helps identify which items are most effective at measuring the intended trait and evaluates the overall quality of a survey instrument or set of questions. It also plays a key role in standardizing survey instruments by determining how well each item performs across diverse populations and ensuring that scores are comparable [33].

We excluded all demographic questions, two personal exposure items, and seven opinion-based questions before performing IRT. From the original set of 53 survey questions, 39 questions were retained for IRT. The opinion-based questions were removed from the attitude section, as they were subjective and could not be scored as correct or incorrect. Items were included only if they could be evaluated against an objective or evidence-based framework and consistently scored as correct or incorrect. Examples of some opinion-based attitude questions are “What is your source of information about ticks and tick-borne diseases?” “Do you think local public health departments are doing enough work to reduce TBDs in your area?” In contrast, items reflecting knowledge, practices, or risk awareness were retained if responses could be categorized as appropriate vs. inappropriate based on established evidence or guidelines. The remaining attitude section questions measured individuals’ perceptions and additional areas of knowledge, so these questions were ultimately merged with the knowledge section, and the ensuing set of questions were categorized into two domains, Knowledge and Practices, based on their content. Additionally, certain items that appeared perception-based were kept if they could be anchored to objective epidemiological evidence or categorized into meaningful levels reflecting exposure risk. Of these 39 selected items, 10 were present in the medical professionals’ survey, 11 in the veterinary professionals’ survey, 4 in the public health professionals’ survey, 28 in the farmers’ survey, and 24 in the Extension workers’ survey (S2). Detailed justification for the inclusion or exclusion of each item, along with the corresponding scoring criteria, have been provided in Supplementary File (S3) for transparency. The dog owner survey was not used in the IRT analysis, as it was not designed using the standard KAP survey format; however, this survey was used in the textual analysis portion of the manuscript.

The IRT analysis was conducted independently for each respondent group. The selected questions available on each survey were subjected to an IRT analysis [30] using the ‘Itm’ package [34] in R Studio (version 4.5.1) [35]. Responses to questions were scored in R into a binary format (1 = correct/appropriate, 0 = incorrect/inappropriate) using keyword-based string matching and manual validation to ensure consistency across groups. For each of the included questions, we developed scoring charts to determine which responses should be scored as correct and the responses to be scored as incorrect. The scoring was done based on discussions between the authors and based on information from the published literature; these scoring charts have been added in the supplementary section (S3). Classical item discrimination was measured using the Upper–Lower Index (ULI) to provide a preliminary measure of item discrimination prior to IRT modeling. The ULI was used as a descriptive indicator of item performance from classical test theory, while the IRT models estimated item difficulty (β). Thus, ULI and IRT parameters were interpreted as complementary rather than mutually exclusive metrics. For each question, ULI was calculated as the difference in the proportion of correct responses between the upper and lower quartiles (where values closer to 1 indicated highly discriminative items and values near 0 suggested poor discrimination). We fitted three IRT models to the binary response data:

Rasch Model (1PL): Assumed equal item discrimination, estimating item difficulty alone.

Rasch Model (1PL): Assumed equal item discrimination, estimating item difficulty alone.

2PL Model: Estimated both item difficulty and discrimination parameters.

3PL Model: Included a pseudo-guessing parameter for random guessing behavior.

3PL Model: Included a pseudo-guessing parameter for random guessing behavior.

Model fit was assessed using log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The model with the lowest AIC and BIC values was selected as the best-fitting model for each respondent group (See Supplementary files). Item parameters were estimated using marginal maximum likelihood implemented in the Itm package in R, which uses an expectation-maximization algorithm. Item response functions were examined through model fit statistics and graphical inspection of item characteristic curves generated by the IRT models. Because the focus of this study was to compare the relative difficulty of survey items across multiple datasets rather than to interpret raw IRT scale values, the estimated difficulty parameters (β) were normalized to a 0–1 scale. This normalization facilitated interpretation and visualization of item difficulty across the different respondent groups, where 0 represents the easiest item (question) and 1 the hardest. The IRT models were fitted assuming a unidimensional latent trait representing knowledge and practices related to TTBDs. As part of the IRT modeling framework, local independence of items was assumed, meaning that item responses were considered independent after conditioning on the latent trait. Variation in item responses across groups reflects differences in knowledge and practices related to TTBDs among the surveyed populations. Visualization was used to assess item difficulty (β-values) derived from the IRT model and item discrimination, as indicated by the Upper-Lower Index (ULI) from classical item analysis, across all groups.

c) Textual analysis

For the third aim of the study, we focused exclusively on the analysis of open-text survey responses (presented in text form), as a majority of these responses had not been incorporated into prior analyses and could identify topics not covered in the existing survey questions. Open-text or open-ended survey responses can be defined as responses that are in an unstructured format without defined pre-set categories, and are often a successful way to solicit authentic and unexpected feedback from survey respondents [36]. Textual analysis is a research method that involves closely and critically examining written, spoken, or visual messages to gain information regarding how people make sense of and communicate life and life experiences [37]. It refers to the process of deconstructing text and its underlying themes, messages, and symbols - to understand the intentions, motivations, and perspectives of its creators [38]. From each of the six surveys, we used all answers to open-text questions for analysis; the number of individuals responding to these questions varied, i.e., farmer survey (n = 13), Extension survey (n = 4), public health professional survey (n = 2), veterinary professional survey (n = 18), medical professional survey (n = 20), and dog owners/general public survey (n = 7). The compiled list of questions from the six surveys were entered into a new spreadsheet to find commonalities, differences, and to identify questions/prompts that would have provided vital information but were not included. To evaluate qualitatively these responses, we used principles from the thematic analysis approach described by Kiger and Varpio [39]. The authors of the study independently coded the free-text responses received from the surveys. These codes represented the various concepts expressed by the survey respondents. Following this, the coders met to discuss these codes and generated a pool of main themes. These themes were then independently grouped into domains that would capture all the ideas and viewpoints expressed by the respondents, which in turn were finally coalesced into overarching super categories that came out of this iterative textual analysis approach (Fig. 1) (S4).

Results

a) Demographic summary

Barring veterinary and medical professionals, most participants of the Illinois KAP surveys were 50 years or older (207/283); notably, there was low representation from the 18–25 years age group overall (29/711). The majority of the participants across the farmers and Extension surveys identified as white (255/283). Education data was available for farmers, Extension workers, medical professionals, and veterinary professionals; most of the individuals who completed these surveys had at the very least a Bachelor’s degree (496/771). Gender data was available for all surveys except public health professionals; most respondents to the surveys were female (464/661), with the exception of the farmer survey (33/50). The level of knowledge regarding TTBDs tended to vary by the surveyed group; most participants had moderate baseline information but lacked information on several aspects, which also varied by occupation. Levels of concern regarding TTBD risk also tended to vary between the surveyed groups, with the only commonality being that most of the participants exhibited lower concern for TTBD risk in animals. Among farmers, the level of TBD concern was higher if they had a prior history of a TBD diagnosis; among Extension workers, the level of concern was directly correlated with knowledge scores. For veterinary professionals, prior experience with diagnosing a TBD, having received TTBD training in the last 5 years, and being in practice for more than 10 years led to higher concern for TBDs. Among medical professionals, although 72% were concerned, only 34% of providers who were concerned would routinely ask patients about tick exposure. Finally, participants generally agreed on the need to increase public education and awareness on TTBDs (with the exception of public health professionals, as this question was not asked of them). Only veterinary and medical professionals were asked if they had received past training on TTBDs. As all these demographic questions were missing from the dog owner survey, we opted to exclude that survey from Table 1, though this group was included for the textual analysis.

In terms of the coverage of these questions in each of the surveyed groups, we found that the farmer survey (28/39, 71.8%) had the greatest number of relevant questions. This was followed by the Extension worker survey (24/39, 61.5%), the veterinary professionals (11/39, 28.2%), the medical professionals (10/39, 25.6%), and lastly the public health professionals survey (4/39, 10.3%).

The Rasch model (1PL) fitted best for public health professionals and farmers, while the 2PL model fitted best for medical professionals, veterinary professionals, and Extension workers, based on model fit statistics (AIC and BIC) and the response patterns observed in each study population (Table S2) (Supplementary Figures S1 & S2).

Across the 39 retained items, β values ranged from 0.0 to 1.00 and ULI values from 0.0 to 1.00, indicating a wide spectrum of item difficulty and discrimination, respectively. In the Extension workers survey (n = 24 items), 14/24 items had β ≤ 0.5 (low-to-moderate difficulty), 6/24 items ranged between 0.5 and 0.8, and 4/24 items were ≥ 0.8. For ULI values, 18/24 items ≤ 0.4, with 6/24 items > 0.4, indicating generally low-to-moderate discrimination with a subset of well-performing items.

In the farmer survey (n = 28 items), 16/28 items had β ≤ 0.5 (low-to-moderate difficulty), 8/28 items ranged between 0.5 and 0.8, and 4/28 items ≥ 0.8 (higher difficulty). ULI values showed 20/28 items ≤ 0.4, and 8/28 items > 0.4, reflecting predominantly low discrimination with several moderately discriminative items. Among veterinary professionals (n = 11 items), β values ranged from 0.05 to 1.00, with 5/11 items ≥ 0.5, and 4/11 items ≥ 0.8, indicating a spread toward higher difficulty. ULI values were comparatively strong, with 7/11 items ≥ 0.7 and 3 items approaching 1.00, demonstrating high discriminatory capacity. In the medical professionals’ survey (n = 10 items), β values ranged from 0.16 to 1.00, with 7/10 items ≥ 0.6, indicating a predominance of difficult items. ULI values ranged from 0.04 to 0.69, with 6/10 items between 0.4 and 0.7, suggesting moderate discrimination. For public health professionals (n = 4 items), β values were 0.0, 0.10, 0.29, and 0.99, indicating both very easy and very difficult items. ULI values ranged from 0.0 to 0.69, with 2/4 items ≥ 0.5, reflecting variable discrimination. Direct comparisons across stakeholder groups were limited to items shared across surveys (Figs. 2 and 3).

c) Textual analysis of the open-text survey responses

Textual analysis of the open-text survey responses led us to develop three super categories and eight sub-categories of comments from the six surveyed groups in Illinois. Super category is a high-level grouping that encompasses several related categories or themes. The eight sub-categories were “Exposure sites,” “TBDs of concern and TBDs diagnosed,” “TBD testing,” “TBD symptoms,” “TBD treatment,” “Tick prevention,” “TBD training,” and “TBD resources.” We coalesced these sub-categories into three super categories, i.e., “Ticks and TBD risk,” “TBD treatment and management,” and “TBD prevention”. We must note that not all participants provided responses to all open-text questions, so the results should not be generalized to the whole survey population as described in the demographics above.

Super category 1: ticks and TBD risk

To better characterize the considerable impact that TTBDs pose on humans and animals, we have to understand the connections between tick exposure, symptoms, disease concern, and vulnerability to TBDs.

i) Exposure sites

Farmers, veterinary, and medical professionals were asked to list sites where people could be exposed to TTBDs. All three of these groups had some level of knowledge regarding sites where tick exposure could occur. In terms of actual areas of risk, farmers reported the southern counties (51.1%, 24/47), followed by the west-central counties (25.5%, 12/47) to be the main sites in Illinois where people might be exposed to ticks.

Medical professionals had variable knowledge; 42% (65/155) said that they did not know about travel sites in their county associated with TBD risk, and about 4% (6/155) said that they had no knowledge about where tick exposure could occur locally. About 34% (52/155) mentioned natural areas as sites of risk, such as:

“Any area that is forested throughout the Midwest and most of the central USA in general. Texas being known for the lone star ticks.”

“All those in/around state parks, woodlands and hunting areas.”

While others provided specific locations but indicated that the incidence in these areas was low:

“Monticello- RMSF. Tularemia - Savoy. Many of these are single cases, not outbreaks.“

For veterinary professionals, although most participants did not respond to this question (52/72), of those providing a response the majority (95%, 19/20) had some knowledge about where tick exposure could occur, such as:

“Locally, as well as the southwest, east coast and upper Midwest.”

“History of travel to northern woods, Ozarks and points south and west.”

As TTBDs are a risk throughout the state of Illinois, these responses may indicate a limited awareness of the extent of the risk.

ii) TBDs of concern and TBDs diagnosed

Open-text responses to the question regarding which TBDs were concerning people or TBDs that have been diagnosed in their areas were available from the medical, veterinary, farmers, and dog owner surveys. Not surprisingly, Lyme disease was the most common TBD that both medical and veterinary professionals were most aware of (medical: 78%, 187/239; veterinary: 74%, 32/43), followed by RMSF (medical: 10%, 24/239; veterinary: 12%, 5/43). Cases of other TBDs diagnosed such as anaplasmosis, ehrlichiosis, and tularemia, were reported predominantly by veterinary professionals, farmers, and dog owners, and the least by medical professionals. Some farmers (3/13) mentioned knowing people in their community who had been diagnosed with alpha-gal syndrome. Only two out of fourteen veterinary professionals mentioned alpha-gal syndrome as a TBD of concern while none of the medical professionals were concerned about alpha-gal, barring three medical participants who listed it as part of other TBDs endemic in the area. Overall, most people seemed primarily aware about Lyme disease, and in comparison with state-level TBD reporting data, people lacked awareness about other TBDs (which may be fatal) that are already present in their counties. The majority of medical professionals reported being unsure about actual TBDs diagnosed in their clinics (85%, 35/41), indicating a major gap. Farmers and dog owners reporting diagnosed TBDs were more likely to report a single TBD diagnosis (72%, 18/25 and 81%, 17/21, respectively) rather than multiple TBD diagnoses, while veterinary professionals indicated commonly diagnosing multiple TBDs in animals (70%, 30/43). Veterinary professionals were the only group to be asked about their knowledge on tick species present in their areas; 89% (41/46) reported knowledge about the various tick species present. These results show a greater awareness of TTBDs among those with regular animal contact (farmers, veterinary professionals, and dog owners) compared to medical professionals.

Super category 2: TBD treatment and management

i) TBD testing

Slightly more than half of medical professionals (56%, 10/18) said that apart from circumstances when patients would show TBD symptoms, they did not test for TBDs in their clinics. Factors that would usually lead to TBD testing in humans were patient concerns, unknown sources of infection, and discretion of the healthcare provider. Some medical professionals even said that they did not know about TBD testing and were unsure when to order for tests.

“Typically, I have just empirically treated when suspicious rather than tested.”

“We don’t test in our area.”

Another factor that might play a role in the decision to test for TBDs is insurance status of the patient in question. Most medical professionals (78%, 56/72) responded that they would provide care no matter what the insurance status of the patient:

“I work in an ED, so insurance status cannot impact my treatment under EMTALA.”

“Not a factor in clinical decision-making.”

However, some said that they would have to consider cost or insurance before moving forward with testing (8%, 6/72):

“I wouldn’t order the test unless I felt it was absolutely necessary.”

“We test regardless - we may have to prior auth, and in that case, if denied, the patient has to decide.”

Veterinary professionals were asked a slightly different question regarding their preference for TBD testing in animals. Most veterinary professionals had prior experience with TBD testing (98%, 43/44). The IDEXX 4Dx SNAP test (this test enables the evaluation of antibodies for Anaplasma spp., Ehrlichia spp., and C6 antibodies from B. burgdorferi, and detecting D. immitis antigens) was the most commonly ordered test for veterinarians (61%, 27/44). However, other tests such as PCR, blood tests, and a combination of SNAP with other molecular based tests were also used:

“We start with the 4Dx then evaluate any positives with a CBC or a Lyme C6 test through IDEXX. If we suspect a disease not found on the 4DX then we send blood out for a panel.”

When asked what other reasons could make clients decline testing for veterinary patients, most stated that clients do not usually decline testing, but one person mentioned that cost could be a limiting factor.

ii) TBD symptoms

Farmers and Extension workers provided responses about experiences with TBD symptoms. Out of the seven responses from farmers, neurological symptoms (57%, 4/7) such as light sensitivity, rash, and meningitis were the most commonly reported symptoms, followed by an inability to eat red meat (29%, 2/7) and fatigue (14%, 1/7). Comparable results were observed among Extension workers, who also reported neurological (44%, 4/9) and physical symptoms (22%, 2/9) but also mental health impacts, such as anxiety and fear of outdoors (22%, 2/9), and chronic health issues (11%, 1/9).

iii) TBD treatment

Both medical and veterinary professionals preferred a single-treatment approach (medical: 67%, 154/229; veterinary: 64%, 30/47) followed by a combination-treatment approach (medical: 16%, 37/229; veterinary: 30%, 14/47). Doxycycline was the most common antibiotic used by both medical and veterinary professionals to treat TBDs. Depending on the disease and the symptoms displayed by the patient, other therapeutics such as painkillers, NSAIDs, steroids, anti-inflammatories, and referrals to infectious disease specialists (for human patients) would be prescribed.

Super category 3: TBD prevention

i) Tick prevention

Open-ended responses regarding TBD prevention were collected from farmers, dog owners, and public health professionals, revealing distinct approaches and gaps in tick control measures across these groups. Among farmers, nearly half (45%, 5/11) reported not using any chemical treatments on their farms for tick control. Additionally, 88% (8/9) did not apply tick repellents on themselves, while 47% (7/15) resorted to crushing ticks when they encountered them.

“Pop its head off with a fingernail.”

“Kill it by stepping on it until it is known dead.”

Among dog owners, 58% (31/53) used chemical sprays in outdoor areas to mitigate tick exposure.

“My husband works in pest control, so he is able to spray our outdoor space for mosquitos and ticks.”

“Our city sprays for mosquitoes city-wide, and we also have a service spray for insects 4 times from spring-fall.”

“UV Light source with trap, spray when have yard activities.”

For their pets, 56% (132/233) relied on oral medications, while 25% (58/233) used topical treatments to protect their dogs from ticks.

Among public health professionals, the majority (59%, 27/46) reported they did not conduct any tick surveillance, while 15% (7/46) were engaged in tick identification. Looking ahead, 29% (8/27) expressed intentions to implement tick drags as part of their future tick surveillance efforts; as this survey was completed prior to a tick surveillance training session, this type of response would be expected.

“Hoping to assist with other departments tick surveillance, no current plans to lead any ourselves in our jurisdiction.”

“Have not done any, interested in learning how to do tick surveillance.”

ii) TBD training

Medical and veterinary professionals were asked to describe any prior training they had received on TBDs. Among medical professionals, the majority, 76% (54/71), reported utilizing training methods categorized as “other,” while a smaller proportion, 18% (13/71), indicated that they relied on Continuing Medical Education (CME) for training. Descriptions focused on basic or general information, rather than specifics.

“We learned about the basics of it in nursing school.”

“Basic knowledge of symptoms. Educated myself after all family members were bitten by ticks on a camping trip.”

“General information of organisms then known.”

In contrast, veterinary professionals mostly (58%, 25/43) reported participating in Continuing Education (CE) programs as their primary training method, whereas 16% (7/43) opted for “other” training modes.

“Attended a tick-borne disease CE.”

“Parasitology and medicine in school, some CE since then.”

“Training in parasitology to identify different tick species. General knowledge of tick-borne diseases as presented in college. Have gone to CE classes on tick-borne diseases.”

Both medical (69/71, 97%) and veterinary professionals (42/43, 98%) reported more didactic training rather than clinical training or personal experiential training on TTBDs.

iii) TBD resources

Many of the surveyed groups provided information on resources that they felt would help their communities to protect themselves from TTBDs. Extension workers best described the various resources that could be shared with the community (Fig. 4). Resources grouped under ‘other resources’ include tick merchandise; opportunity to work in Extension offices; tick stickers/magnets; tick species comparison resources; information on the I-TICK program, and others.

Resources on TBDs mentioned by Extension workers that would be useful. (Data shown are based on Extension workers who responded to this question (n = 156) out of the total respondents (n = 233)

Farmers were also interested in seeing more information on TTBDs, but also stressed that education of the medical community was the most crucial.

“Lot of assuming about tick-borne illness. Very little diagnostic testing done because it is assumed and treated with antibiotics. Regularly observe rashes or bumps on skin in the summertime and monitor for any further development.”

“Just a note, mushroom hunting season (early May) is usually the worst time for ticks around here - mostly because of the increased amount of time in the woods, I think. My mom has had over forty already this year, and while she has tested negative for Lyme, she is having similar symptoms. I feel strongly that our doctors in the area don’t give enough credence to tick borne diseases unless you have a rash.”

Some similar responses were seen in the medical survey, indicating a need for more medical education around TBDs.

“As I neuro nurse I have a few times requested MDs order tests for tick-borne illnesses after assessing personal histories. The MDs have been reluctant, and I don’t know why.”

“I am a therapist and treat a client with chronic Lyme. More information, training, and help are needed to treat the psychological effects of this disease.”

“Need to know geographic areas around the country where tick-borne disease is a concern as many patients travel.”

“I think there is a lot of misinformation out there. I have seen a few people on chronic antibiotics for ‘chronic Lyme’ then they get GI disorders and end up in my world.”

Veterinary and medical professionals requested resources on treatment, testing, community awareness, tick identification, reliable sources of information on TTBDs that they can refer their patients to, information on areas where people could be exposed to ticks, and local estimates of TBD cases in humans and animals. Medical professionals in particular reported a need for more information.

“I just hope tick disease awareness can be widespread, undiagnosed patients can get better treatment, there could be a point person/provider in the system who is Lyme literate.”

“I’d be interested to see real numbers of Lyme in my county. Our health department does a poor job of updating their annual numbers.”

“Application of this for inpatient settings.”

Medical professionals were the only group to be asked about what TBD materials they shared with their clients, and most said they gave an educational handout (53%, 26/49), discharge information (37%, 18/49), or clinical information (4%, 2/49).

Discussion

Novel approach

In this paper, we have demonstrated a novel approach to comparing multiple KAP surveys on TTBDs disseminated to six distinct demographic groups within the state of Illinois. We performed a combination of quantitative (IRT analysis) and qualitative (textual analysis), identified key questions from each of these surveys that are important for a KAP questionnaire on TTBDs, and synthesized common perceptions, gaps, and views of the surveyed groups with regard to TTBDs. Utilization of systematic, scoping, narrative, or comparative reviews and meta-analyses of a large number of studies leads to useful insights on the specific topic and unearths information that may have been previously unpublished or under-discussed in scientific literature. Review studies can add evidence to support existing or new theories or treatment methods/protocols; they can provide insights on patterns observed from a variety of studies and provide a synthesis of a large body of literature. However, such a method of aggregating or comparing information from multiple KAP surveys distributed to varied demographic groups on the topic of TTBDs has not been done before. We have seen comparisons of tick sampling methods [40], species distribution models [41], and surveillance methods [42]. A narrative literature review of KAP surveys on mosquitoes and mosquito-borne diseases has also been published previously [43]. However, there are no studies in the literature that compare information across behavioral surveys of different groups of stakeholders all of who are affected by TTBDs. In areas with multiple KAP surveys on a similar topic of public health concern, this approach can be used to compare the responses received from the various groups to unveil helpful insights.

Observations from IRT analysis

To evaluate the performance of survey questions related to knowledge and practices of TBDs, we applied IRT analysis to a subset of questions. The IRT analysis demonstrated variability in item difficulty (β), and classical item analysis showed variability in discriminative capacity (ULI) within and across the surveyed groups. Generally, the distribution of item difficulty varied across respondent groups; however, direct comparisons between groups should be interpreted cautiously because the surveys used different question sets for each group. This limitation is common in vector-borne disease research because KAP surveys often differ in structure, wording, and theoretical frameworks, which can limit direct comparisons across populations and studies [44, 45]. Therefore, the reported β ranges primarily reflect the difficulty of items within each survey rather than strict cross-group comparisons.

These differences may partly reflect variations in roles among respondents, such as the differences in the veterinary and medical survey participants. Veterinary participants were primarily veterinarians and veterinary technicians working in small-animal practices, whereas medical respondents were primarily nurses, with only about half working in first-line clinical practice. Thus, the participants in the veterinary survey were more likely than participants in the medical survey to encounter TTBDs regularly in their respective practices. Similarly, analysis of the surveys for farmers and Extension workers highlighted different questions as having strong discriminatory power. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring survey instruments, as items that perform well in one group may have limited utility in another.

The results of the IRT analysis also provide insights into the design of future KAP surveys addressing tick-borne diseases. Questions that demonstrate moderate difficulty and strong discriminatory capacity are particularly valuable for assessing differences in knowledge levels among those groups’ respondents. Items related to tick attachment sites, tick habitats, and exposure risks consistently demonstrated strong discriminatory performance across multiple stakeholder groups. Previous studies have also shown that greater awareness and knowledge of tick-borne diseases is associated with increased adoption of preventive behaviors, highlighting the importance of educational interventions in reducing exposure risk [46]. Incorporating such questions into future KAP surveys could help standardize assessments of TBD knowledge and allow better comparison across studies and stakeholder populations.

Because the surveys included different sample sizes and recruitment strategies, some respondent groups were under-represented. To complement the quantitative findings, we also conducted textual analysis of open-ended responses to capture perceptions, beliefs, and perceived risks related to TTBDs across the surveyed groups.

Observations from textual analysis

Based on how the respondents tended to differ as they answered the survey questions and the differences in knowledge and practices observed with respect to TTBDs, we find that there is considerable variability of information among the surveyed groups. This variability in knowledge among the healthcare groups is especially concerning. In comparison with veterinary professionals, medical professionals in Illinois were less able to list where people could be exposed to ticks in their counties or which TBDs were diagnosed in their clinics, expressed more uncertainty about diagnosis of multiple TBDs in humans, and mentioned TBDs other than Lyme disease and RMSF less. Other researchers have also noted that physicians and primary care doctors have difficulty in testing, identifying, and diagnosing TBDs [47–50]. Variability in the level of healthcare provider knowledge has also been documented between areas with high and low TBD incidence [48, 51–52]. Veterinary professionals in Illinois were more able to provide knowledge about TBDs in their practice area than their physician counterparts, but still lacked information in some areas. Factors such as the availability of the SNAP test to quickly detect TBDs, and training on tick prevention measures for animals, such as repellants and various acaricide formulations, could potentially give veterinary professionals an advantage over medical professionals [6, 27, 53]. Likewise, as mentioned above, the veterinary participants were 61% veterinarians, while the medical participants were 61% RNs (Registered Nurses); these positions have different roles within their respective practices, which may explain their different ability to comment on topics such as diagnostics. It would be of interest to repeat this analysis using comparable subgroups that differ in their areas of medical/veterinary practice, such as frontline and primary care as opposed to specialists, but the sample sizes of the available survey respondents are too small to make this comparison possible at this time.

Symptoms of TBDs are an area where awareness should be increased across all medical groups. Even though erythema migrans is the classic symptom associated with Lyme disease in humans, there is a whole suite of various symptoms (differing from person to person) that can occur after infection with Lyme disease and other TBDs [54]. Besides the physical symptoms, farmers and Extension professionals reported some of the less talked about symptoms, i.e., neurological symptoms and negative impacts on mental health. Maxwell et al. [55] indicated that current public health guidance does not include a variety of neuropsychiatric presentations of patients affected with TBDs, with a need to create awareness among medical providers to recognize these symptoms and communicate effectively with patients. In another landmark study, researchers found that patients who had received a diagnosis of a TBD had a 28% higher rate of mental disorders and higher rates of suicide, highlighting the serious and debilitating effects that TBDs can have on patients [56]. Therefore, the need for regular training, updated information on local tick species and TBDs, and appropriate diagnostic and treatment protocols should be disseminated to all clinical and healthcare professionals.

Much of the outreach work around TTBDs has focused on education and knowledge distribution [57]. Even though didactic trainings during medical and veterinary school was indicated as the most common source of training on TTBDs by healthcare providers, other avenues such as conferences, Continuing Education (CE) training credits, on-the-job training, and seminars should be explored to increase clinicians’ awareness of this critical subject. Farmers also emphasized the critical need for training healthcare providers to be aware of tick species and TBDs that can be present in their region. Extension workers suggested that information on TTBDs should be provided through handouts, reliable online websites, infographics, and multi-media formats so that it is easily accessible by clinicians, educators, and the public. Clinicians also requested sources of information that would be easy to share with patients, a request echoed in a national survey of veterinarians throughout the US [58].

Considered together, the IRT and textual analysis results offer complementary perspectives on the same underlying knowledge domain. Items related to tick attachment sites, tick habitats, and exposure risk were among the strongest discriminators across stakeholder groups, indicating that these topics meaningfully separate respondents with higher knowledge from those with lower knowledge. This pattern is echoed in our textual analysis, where medical professionals showed clear gaps in exposure-related knowledge: 42% were unable to identify travel-related risk sites in their county, in contrast to farmers and veterinary professionals, who were generally able to describe specific locations or regions of risk. This convergence between the quantitative and qualitative findings suggests that exposure-related knowledge, rather than general awareness of ticks or TBDs, is an area where targeted educational interventions could be especially valuable, particularly for medical professionals.

Limitations

We acknowledge that recruitment strategies used for individual surveys were different, with some demographic groups being more difficult to recruit than others are, resulting in surveys with low sample sizes, which is reflected in this comparative analysis. An important limitation to this analysis was the removal of opinion-based attitude questions, which can often offer important insights into an individual’s behavior. The literature also indicates that knowledge on TTBDs alone is insufficient to drive behavior change in people and needs to be accompanied with change in attitudes and perceptions [19, 59]. Mader et al. [60] discussed the lack of attitude-related questions in KAP surveys on TTBDs, indicating a vacuum in this area. Future studies should focus on developing effective questions that can elicit important information with respect to individual’s attitudes that lead to behavior change.

A limitation of the IRT analysis is the relatively small sample sizes in some respondent groups compared with the number of survey items. Although some guidelines recommend larger sample sizes for stable parameter estimation, the purpose of the IRT analysis in this study was primarily exploration to evaluate relative item difficulty and discrimination patterns across surveys rather than to construct a precise psychometric scale. Therefore, the IRT results should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies using larger and more standardized survey datasets would allow more robust model estimation and stronger cross-group comparisons.

Additional limitation to this study is that KAP surveys can be impacted by social desirability bias and recall bias, which may have affected some of the open-text answers. For the textual analysis section, response rates differed by group and due to the low sample sizes, the interpretations should not be generalized beyond the groups studied here. Despite these biases, our approach to compare the responses between the surveyed demographic groups in Illinois on TTBDs provides valuable information in the fight against tick-borne diseases and in the design of KAP surveys for TTBDs.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated a novel approach to compare several knowledge, attitudes, and practices surveys that were disseminated to a variety of stakeholders on a topic of public health concern viz., ticks and tick-borne diseases. We used a combination of quantitative (IRT) and qualitative (textual analysis) approaches to convey critical insights across the six surveyed groups on TTBDs in the Midwestern United States. The use of item-response theory helped us estimate the discriminatory value of a set of survey items to assess tick-related knowledge and practices among key demographic groups in Illinois. Despite some limitations in uniformity of item administration, the results offer a guide to develop robust tools for future targeted education, assessment, and surveillance initiatives addressing TBDs in Illinois. The use of textual analysis uncovered important information such as considerable gaps in knowledge regarding TBD exposure, treatment, and diagnosis among healthcare groups, barriers to treatment and diagnosis faced by the general public and healthcare groups, lack of concern about the impact of TBDs, along with behaviors and knowledge gaps that can affect people’s risk of exposure to TTBDs.

Based on these findings, we support the following recommendations across all surveyed groups:

i)the dissemination of information campaigns that emphasize the importance of following tick prevention measures throughout the year;

ii)the distribution of tick prevention information to at-risk groups to adhere to tick prevention measures such as regular tick checks with use of proper tick repellants, and treating animals with safe and recommended tick prevention products;

iii)providing regularly updated information on TTBDs to clinically trained healthcare professionals on symptom identification, diagnosis, disease management, and treatment via Continuing Education credits, workshops, and on-the-job trainings;

providing regularly updated information on TTBDs to clinically trained healthcare professionals on symptom identification, diagnosis, disease management, and treatment via Continuing Education credits, workshops, and on-the-job trainings;

iv)foster strategic partnerships between researchers, health professionals, and community organizations to produce audience-specific TTBD materials with clear dissemination plans to increase overall awareness, guide beliefs with scientific evidence, and encourage the use of tick preventive measures across the state.
