Weight loss with atogepant in the long‐term treatment of migraine: An interim analysis of a safety endpoint from a phase 3, multicenter, open‐label, 156‐week extension study
Authors: Dale S. Bond, Jonathan H. Smith, Alexandra Sinclair, Peter J. Goadsby, Jessica Ailani, Yingyi Liu, Rosa De Abreu Ferreira, Brett Dabruzzo, Joel M. Trugman, B. Lee Peterlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess weight loss with atogepant 60 mg once daily during long‐term treatment of chronic migraine (CM) and episodic migraine (EM) among participants previously failed by two to four classes of conventional oral preventive medications.BackgroundThe risk of migraine, including CM, in individuals with obesity is higher than in those without obesity. Calcitonin gene–related peptide has been linked to the pathophysiology of both obesity and migraine. Weight loss with atogepant, a calcitonin gene–related peptide receptor antagonist indicated for the preventive treatment of migraine, has been observed during the 12‐week EM and CM trials, as well as in long‐term (40‐ or 52‐week) EM trials. Long‐term effects of atogepant on body weight in participants with increased migraine burden (i.e., EM or CM) have not been reported.MethodsIn this interim analysis of a safety endpoint from study 312, a phase 3, open‐label, extension study, weight loss was assessed in the overall population and by prior participation in each lead‐in study (12‐week double‐blind treatment period): PROGRESS (phase 3, multicenter, randomized, controlled study in CM) or ELEVATE (phase 3, multicenter, randomized, controlled study in EM treatment failure). Change from baseline in body weight at each study visit through end of treatment (PROGRESS and ELEVATE) or up to week 52 (study 312) was assessed (safety endpoint). Proportions of participants with ≥5% weight loss at any time, at the end of the lead‐in study, or at week 52 in study 312 were evaluated.ResultsParticipants from PROGRESS (n = 325) and ELEVATE (n = 270) rolled over to study 312 (n = 595) and were treated with atogepant 60 mg once daily. In study 312, mean (standard deviation) body weight decreased over time (−2.16 kg [5.89 kg; −4.76 lb] at week 52), with 44.8% (265/592) of participants experiencing a ≥5% weight loss at any time during the study and 29.5% (140/474) experiencing a ≥5% weight loss at week 52. In study 312, weight loss from lead‐in study baseline was evident as early as week 4 and appeared to plateau around weeks 28 to 36, reaching a maximum numerical weight loss at week 44. Higher baseline body mass index was associated with greater odds of achieving ≥5% weight loss both at any time and at week 52. No significant effect of sex, race, adverse drug reactions, or therapeutic response to treatment was observed.ConclusionsParticipants receiving atogepant 60 mg once daily for long‐term preventive treatment of migraine were observed to have a decrease in mean body weight after 1 year of open‐label treatment. Approximately 30% of participants experienced a clinically meaningful (≥5%) weight loss threshold after 1 year of open‐label treatment. Future studies are needed to further characterize the mechanisms of weight loss associated with atogepant treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Migraine is a common, disabling neurologic disease associated with substantial burden. 1 , 2 Calcitonin gene–related peptide (CGRP), a multifunctional neuropeptide known to play a key role in migraine pathogenesis, also has potential for involvement in the pathophysiology of obesity and weight loss based on the findings of preclinical studies. 3 , 4 , 5 Although the potential mechanistic link between migraine and obesity is not fully understood, the risk of migraine is increased by 27% to 28% in those with a body mass index (BMI) over 30, and this risk rises with increasing BMI. 6 , 7 , 8 Women with a diagnosed history of migraine have been shown to have a 67% greater risk of gaining at least 10 kg in adulthood than those without a history of migraine, further suggesting a link between migraine and obesity. 9 Conversely, people with migraine and obesity who lose weight have been shown to experience decreased frequency and severity of migraine attacks, suggesting weight management could play a role in mitigating migraine symptoms. 10 , 11 There is also evidence to suggest that those with migraine exhibit differences in weight‐related behaviors (e.g., decrease in physical activity) and greater weight gain and obesity. 9 , 12 , 13

There is a need for safe and effective preventive migraine treatments that have a favorable effect on body weight. Published reports from clinical trials of atogepant, a CGRP receptor (CGRP‐R) antagonist demonstrated to be effective for the preventive treatment of migraine, have noted loss of body weight among participants who received atogepant versus placebo. 14 , 15 , 16 Interestingly, weight loss has not been reported with any of the other CGRP/CGRP‐R antagonists. Recently, a post hoc analysis of data from five atogepant clinical trials conducted among participants with episodic migraine (EM) or chronic migraine (CM) evaluated the extent and time course of weight loss among participants receiving atogepant for the preventive treatment of migraine. 17 This analysis found that atogepant was consistently associated with a modest, dose‐ and duration‐dependent loss of body weight during the 12‐week double‐blind treatment period of placebo‐controlled episodic and chronic migraine trials, as well as in long‐term episodic migraine studies with 40‐ or 52‐week treatment periods. 17 Weight loss associated with atogepant was also reported in a 12‐week, real‐world, prospective study of 106 participants with migraine. 18 However, the long‐term effects of atogepant on body weight in participants with increased baseline burden of migraine have not been previously reported.

Study 312 is a 156‐week, open‐label extension study that includes individuals who previously participated in the PROGRESS or ELEVATE atogepant clinical trials. Interim 1‐year safety and efficacy data from study 312 have been published previously. 19 Here, we report data from a 1‐year interim analysis of body weight change with atogepant 60 mg once daily in participants with CM and those with EM who were previously failed by two to four classes of conventional oral preventive medication. Based on the findings described above, we hypothesized that long‐term treatment with atogepant is associated with weight loss in these populations.

METHODS

Study design

Study 312 (3101–312‐002 [NCT04686136]) is a phase 3, multicenter, open‐label, 156‐week extension study being conducted among participants who completed one of two phase 3, multicenter, double‐blind, randomized controlled trials evaluating the safety and efficacy of atogepant for the preventive treatment of migraine among participants with CM (PROGRESS) or with EM and previously failed by two to four classes of conventional oral medication (ELEVATE). This interim safety analysis was completed in November 2023 (data cutoff September 14, 2023) after all study participants had completed the efficacy data collection portion of the study at visit 9 (week 52) or terminated early. Detailed methods for each lead‐in study have been published previously. 16 , 20 PROGRESS (conducted in North America, Europe, and Asia [3/11/2019–1/20/2022]) randomized adult participants with ≥15 monthly headache days in the past 3 months and ≥15 headache days (with ≥8 qualifying as migraine days) during the 28‐day baseline period to atogepant 30 mg twice daily, atogepant 60 mg once daily, or placebo for 12 weeks. 16 ELEVATE (conducted in North America and Europe [3/5/2021–8/4/2022]) randomized adult participants with 4 to 14 monthly migraine days in the 3 months prior to visit 1 and 28‐day baseline period, and previously failed by two to four classes of conventional oral preventive migraine treatments to atogepant 60 mg once daily or placebo for 12 weeks. 20 This study was approved by an institutional review board and conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonization guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants provided written informed consent prior to initiation of study activities.

Participants

Those participants who completed visit 7 (i.e., week 12 visit) or visit 8 (i.e., safety follow‐up visit) of PROGRESS or ELEVATE with no significant protocol violations and did not experience an adverse event indicative of unacceptable safety risk were eligible for study 312. Participants were excluded if they had clinically significant disease (e.g., hematologic, endocrine, cardiovascular, pulmonary, renal, hepatic, gastrointestinal, neurologic) or exhibited significant risk of self‐harm, harm to others, or suicidal ideation. Participants also were excluded if they were taking a study‐prohibited medication that could not be discontinued 30 days prior to visit 1 of the extension study (strong cytochrome P450 3A4 [CYP3A4] inhibitors, strong/moderate CYP3A4 inducers, strong organic anion transporting polypeptide 1B1/1B3 [OATP1B1/OATP1B3] inhibitors, drugs with narrow therapeutic margins and theoretical potential for CYP drug interactions, herbal medications with efficacy for the prevention of migraine, and cannabidiol oil or cannabis). Participants who were taking only one migraine preventive medication at a stable, well‐tolerated dose during the PROGRESS lead‐in study were allowed to continue that medication at the same dose during the extension study. Use of rimegepant, CGRP‐targeted monoclonal antibodies, and botulinum toxin injections into areas of the head, face, or neck was prohibited within 6 months prior to visit 1 and throughout the study period.

Per protocol, participants from PROGRESS and ELEVATE could enroll in study 312 via three pathways (Figure 1). Participants who completed the double‐blind treatment period of PROGRESS or ELEVATE were eligible for direct enrollment in study 312 after visit 7. Participants who completed the double‐blind treatment period and 4‐week safety follow‐up period of PROGRESS or ELEVATE were eligible for direct enrollment into study 312 after visit 8. Finally, participants who fully completed PROGRESS or ELEVATE were allowed to participate in study 312 if they wished to at a later date. At the time of this interim analysis, all enrolled participants had either completed at least 52 weeks of study 312 or terminated early.

Study outcomes

Baseline body weight from the lead‐in PROGRESS and ELEVATE trials was used as the baseline for study 312. Changes from baseline in body weight (kg) were assessed during PROGRESS and ELEVATE and among study 312 participants at each study visit during the first year of open‐label treatment. Weight changes were additionally assessed among study 312 participants who experienced an off‐treatment gap following their last dose of study drug in their respective lead‐in study.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and outcomes were analyzed descriptively, unless otherwise noted, among all participants who received at least one dose of atogepant during the extension study (safety population). Because this was a post hoc safety analysis, no sample size calculation or statistical power justification was performed. A subanalysis by race and sex was conducted for proportions of participants with ≥5% weight loss at any time or at the end of each study. Weight loss of ≥5% is a common efficacy endpoint in overweight and obesity trials and has been considered clinically meaningful due to a significant reduction in the risk for various comorbid conditions. 21 , 22

To examine factors associated with weight loss, dependent outcomes of weight loss ≥5% in study 312 at any time or at week 52 were analyzed using a multivariable logistic regression model adjusted for age, sex, race, baseline BMI (continuous), lead‐in study, lead‐in study treatment, completer category (visit 8/study completer or not vs. visit 7 completer), adverse drug reaction status (i.e., any of constipation, nausea, or decreased appetite; yes or no), treatment response to atogepant during study 312 (i.e., ≥50% reduction in monthly migraine days at weeks 45 to 58 [yes or no], and change in Migraine‐Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire version 2.1 Role Function–Restrictive domain at week 52).

Cumulative distribution function plots were generated to represent graphically the cumulative percentage of participants experiencing different percent changes in body weight from baseline to the end of treatment (PROGRESS and ELEVATE) or week 52 (study 312). Cumulative distribution function plots are presented with thresholds of 5% for weight loss and 7% for weight increase.

Means with standard deviations (SDs) for normally distributed continuous data and counts with percentages for categorical variables are presented. The last observation carried forward (LOCF) method was used to account for missing data for cumulative distribution function plots. The LOCF method was used only for end‐of‐treatment analyses; the last available weight assessment during the open‐label treatment period was used as the value for the end of treatment. For the analysis of change in body weight by study visit during study 312, a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) analysis was conducted to calculate LS mean change and 95% confidence intervals at each visit; covariates for the MMRM model included baseline weight and a baseline‐by‐visit interaction. An unstructured covariance matrix was used to model the covariance. The Kenward–Roger approximation was used to estimate denominator degrees of freedom. All other analyses and visits used observed data without imputation of missing data. Statistical significance for regression analysis was defined as p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Participants

A total of 595 participants were treated with ≥1 dose of atogepant 60 mg once daily in study 312 (safety population: rollovers from PROGRESS, n = 325; rollovers from ELEVATE, n = 270) (Figure S1). The mean (SD) overall extent of exposure for the interim analysis was 496.5 (224.7) days. The majority of study 312 participants who rolled over from PROGRESS were categorized as lead‐in study completers (66.2%; 215/325), with a mean (SD) treatment gap of 227 (132.5) days. The majority of study 312 participants who rolled over from ELEVATE were categorized as visit 7 completers (83.3%; 225/270) with a mean (SD) treatment gap of 0.2 (2.0) days (Table S1).

In the study 312 safety population, mean (SD) age was 42.2 (11.8) years, mean (SD) weight was 72.0 (17.3) kg, and mean (SD) BMI was 25.9 (5.5) kg/m2 (Table 1); overall, 87.7% of participants were female, and 84.0% were White. Baseline characteristics were similar between participants who rolled over from PROGRESS and ELEVATE, except that there was a higher proportion of Asian participants among those from PROGRESS (21.8% vs. 1.5%), which included study sites in East Asia. Topiramate use was reported by six (1.0%) participants during study 312 and by eight (1.3%) participants during the time between the lead‐in and extension studies.

Body weight change during and after PROGRESS and ELEVATE

Body weight change during and after the lead‐in studies is shown in Figure 2 and Table S2 for all participants who subsequently participated in study 312. The magnitude of weight loss among atogepant‐treated participants was similar for the two lead‐in studies. Among all prior PROGRESS participants, the mean body weight change from baseline at the end of the double‐blind treatment period was −0.92 kg (−2.03 lb) for atogepant 60 mg once daily and +0.19 kg (+0.42 lb) for placebo (Figure 2A). Among all prior ELEVATE participants, these values were −1.17 kg (−2.58 lb) for atogepant 60 mg once daily and +0.28 kg (+0.62 lb) for placebo (Figure 2B). Interestingly, participants treated with atogepant in PROGRESS were observed to experience weight gain during the off‐treatment gap prior to starting study 312 (Figure 2A). Among PROGRESS study completers, a mean increase of 0.92 kg (2.03 lb) for atogepant 30 mg twice daily and 0.81 kg (1.79 lb) for atogepant 60 mg once daily compared with −0.29 kg (−0.64 lb) for placebo was observed during the off‐treatment gap (mean [SD] treatment gap of 227.0 [132.5] days). The majority of participants who rolled over from ELEVATE were visit 7 completers (mean [SD] treatment gap of 0.2 [2.0] days) and therefore had little to no gap in treatment (Figure 2B).

Body weight change during study 312

During study 312, atogepant‐treated participants (60 mg once daily) experienced weight loss over 1 year of treatment (Figure 3; Table S3). At week 52, the mean change from baseline in body weight was −2.15 kg (−4.74 lb) for participants rolled over from PROGRESS, −2.17 kg (−4.78 lb) for those rolled over from ELEVATE, and −2.16 kg (−4.76 lb) for the total population. Weight loss from lead‐in study baseline in study 312 was evident as early as week 4 and appeared to plateau around weeks 28 to 36, reaching a maximum numerical weight loss at week 44. An MMRM analysis found similar results for LS mean change in body weight over 52 weeks with upper bounds of the 95% confidence intervals below 0 at all timepoints assessed (Table S4).

Body weight loss of ≥5%

Among participants who entered study 312 from the lead‐in studies, the proportions of participants with ≥5% weight loss at any point were numerically higher for those treated with atogepant versus placebo during the lead‐in studies (Figure 4A). In PROGRESS, 3.9% of participants in the placebo group showed ≥5% weight loss compared with 12.5% of participants with atogepant 30 mg twice daily and 20.4% of participants with atogepant 60 mg once daily. In ELEVATE, 3.0% of participants in the placebo group showed ≥5% weight loss compared with 10.1% of participants with atogepant 60 mg once daily. During study 312, 44.8% of participants experienced ≥5% weight loss at any timepoint (Figure 4B). Among this subgroup of participants, the mean weight loss at week 52 was −5.32 (6.68) kg (−11.73 [14.73] lb) (n = 231). Similar patterns were observed for proportions of participants with ≥5% weight loss at the end of treatment (PROGRESS and ELEVATE) or at week 52 of study 312 (Figure 5). At the end of treatment in PROGRESS, 3.9% of participants in the placebo group showed ≥5% weight loss compared with 7.5% of participants with atogepant 30 mg twice daily and 14.6% of participants with atogepant 60 mg once daily (Figure 5A). At the end of treatment in ELEVATE, 1.5% of participants in the placebo group showed ≥5% weight loss compared with 9.3% of participants with atogepant 60 mg once daily (Figure 5A), and 29.5% of participants showed ≥5% weight loss at week 52 of study 312 (Figure 5B). Among these study 312 participants, the mean change from baseline at week 52 was −8.07 (6.60) kg (−17.79 [14.55] lb).

Body weight change over time in study 312

Cumulative distribution function plots depicting the cumulative percentage of study 312 participants experiencing different percent changes in body weight from baseline to the end of treatment in study 312 are shown in Figure S2. The time course of weight loss in study 312 was similar regardless of which treatment participants received during the lead‐in study. Stratification of data from study 312 by race (including males and females or females only) showed that proportions of participants with ≥5% weight loss at any point and at week 52 were similar across most race subgroups, although some subgroups were too small for meaningful analysis (Table S5).

Weight decrease was not related to the occurrence of adverse drug reactions (ADR) (e.g., constipation, decreased appetite, nausea). The proportion of participants with (n = 99) or without (n = 496) ≥1 ADR experiencing ≥5% weight loss at week 52 was 33.8% versus 28.8%, respectively. The cumulative distribution function plot of percentage change from baseline in body weight (kg) at week 52 was overlapping between those with or without ≥1 ADR (Figure S3).

Effect of BMI on weight loss of ≥5% in study 312

In multivariable regression models, higher baseline BMI was associated with greater odds of achieving ≥5% weight loss both at any time and at week 52 in study 312 (Tables S6 and S7), while controlling for other baseline characteristics. Analysis of ≥5% weight loss at any time: Coefficient for baseline BMI was 0.066 (p = 0.004), equivalent to an odds ratio of 1.07 (i.e., each one‐unit increase in baseline BMI corresponds to a 7.0% increase in the odds of having ≥5% weight loss at any time) (Table S8). Analysis of ≥5% weight loss at week 52: Coefficient for baseline BMI was 0.050 (p = 0.028), equivalent to an odds ratio of 1.05 (i.e., each one‐unit increase in baseline BMI corresponds to a 5.0% increase in the odds of having ≥5% weight loss at week 52) (Table S9). Notably, no significant effect of sex, race, adverse drug reaction, or response to treatment was observed (p > 0.05) (Tables S6–S9).

Treatment‐emergent adverse events related to weight loss

During study 312, 1.3% (8/595) of participants reported treatment‐emergent adverse events related to weight loss (weight decreased 1.2% [7/595] and abnormal weight loss 0.2% [1/595]), whereas 0.2% (1/595) reported abnormal weight gain (Table S8). All weight‐related treatment‐emergent adverse events were nonserious, mild to moderate in intensity, and did not lead to discontinuation of atogepant.

DISCUSSION

A previous post hoc analysis of five clinical trials conducted in participants with EM or CM reported modest weight loss in participants treated with atogepant. 17 This current analysis extends these observations to include long‐term clinical trial participants with either baseline CM or EM with prior treatment failures. In addition, these data support prior observations that longer‐term, potentially clinically meaningful weight loss (i.e., ≥5%) with atogepant is associated with higher baseline BMI but not with treatment efficacy or adverse drug reactions (e.g., nausea, constipation, or decreased appetite). 17

Results from this interim analysis of people with migraine who participated in an open‐label extension study demonstrated modest but consistent weight loss in participants treated with a‐to‐ge‐pant 60 mg once daily. Moderate weight loss (approximately −1.0 kg [−2.2 lb]) was evident during the 12‐week lead‐in studies for participants with CM (PROGRESS) and participants with EM who were previously failed by two to four classes of conventional oral preventive medication (ELEVATE). Whereas the majority of study 312 participants from ELEVATE were direct rollovers (mean treatment gap of 0.2 days), 66.2% of participants from PROGRESS enrolled in study 312 after a sizable gap in treatment (mean 227.0 days), during which a weight regain of ≈0.9 kg (≈2.0 lb) was observed for participants treated with atogepant. This increase in weight during the treatment gap provides additional support for the possibility of atogepant‐associated weight loss. Finally, the analysis of change in body weight during study 312 showed a mean body weight loss of approximately 2 kg (4.4 lb) at 52 weeks of treatment, with 44.8% of participants achieving a weight loss of ≥5% from baseline at any time during study 312. This weight loss was consistent between participants with CM and those with EM who were previously failed by two to four classes of conventional oral preventive medication.

The magnitude of weight loss observed was modest on the population level but may be considered clinically meaningful for individual participants. In the current analysis, 44.8% of participants in study 312 experienced ≥5% weight loss at any time. Weight loss of ≥5% is a common efficacy endpoint in overweight and obesity trials because it has been shown to significantly improve glycemic and lipid profiles, lower blood pressure, and reduce diabetes risk. 21 , 22 Thus, our current results suggest that a substantial number of atogepant‐treated individuals experienced weight loss at a level that has been associated with health benefits. These findings align with those from a previous post hoc analysis of data from five atogepant trials. 17

Multiple potential contributing factors have been proposed for putative mechanistic links between migraine and obesity. Neuropathways implicated in migraine pathophysiology overlap with those that regulate feeding behavior and adipose tissue function. 23 These pathways may be perturbed by systemic low‐grade inflammation associated with obesity, contributing to elevated CGRP levels. 4 , 23 Preclinical studies and animal models show impairment of insulin secretion and action by CGRP, contributing to insulin resistance and altered glucose metabolism. 24 Neuroendocrine dysregulation is also suggested by animal models of obesity that show CGRP‐related trigeminal sensitization. 24 Further results from preclinical studies using cell models indicate that atogepant is a potent antagonist of the CGRP receptors found in human adipocytes. 25 , 26 Although the underlying mechanism for atogepant‐mediated weight loss is not known, a dose‐dependent effect was suggested in the previous post hoc analysis (i.e., the atogepant 30 mg and 60 mg once‐daily groups experienced greater weight loss than the atogepant 10 mg once‐daily group), suggesting a pharmacologic mechanism. 17 More research is needed to understand whether weight loss observed with atogepant treatment is mediated by CGRP antagonism directly or is a result of downstream effects on other obesity‐related proteins.

Changes in weight have been reported as a side effect of several non–CGRP‐targeted oral medications commonly used for the treatment of migraine, including tricyclic antidepressants, valproate, flunarizine, and topiramate. 27 Treatment‐emergent weight gain and related distress have been associated with discontinuation and/or poor adherence for other treatments known to have this side effect, such as antipsychotic drugs. 28 Weight gain due to migraine preventive treatment could also reduce compliance and contribute to preventive treatment failure, which is associated with increased migraine severity, greater functional and emotional impact, and poorer quality of life. 29 , 30 Although not evaluated in this analysis, individuals with migraine receiving preventive treatment with atogepant may be less likely to discontinue treatment due to weight gain. Conversely, atogepant‐related migraine improvements could allow participants to more fully engage in healthy behaviors associated with weight loss (e.g., spending more time in physical activity). Patients with migraine and comorbid obesity may particularly stand to benefit because higher BMI was associated with increased likelihood of weight loss with atogepant treatment in the present analysis.

Whereas obesity has been shown to be negatively associated with response to some CGRP‐targeted monoclonal antibodies, our findings demonstrate that EM and CM populations treated with atogepant had similar efficacy across BMI categories. 17 , 31 , 32 , 33 No significant effect of sex, race, adverse drug reaction, or response to treatment was observed in our analysis despite differences in obesity between sex and race subgroups. 34 In particular, different populations may have different BMI settings or body fat distribution; for example, studies have suggested that Asian populations have more body fat relative to weight than White populations. 35 Our regression analysis results indicate that long‐term weight loss was observed across racial subgroups.

Study limitations

There are several limitations to this analysis related to the study design. Most critically, study 312, PROGRESS, and ELEVATE were not specifically designed to examine the impact of atogepant treatment on body weight. Consequently, physical activity, caloric intake, and other lifestyle confounders were not assessed; there was no standardized protocol for body weight measurement; and procedures may have differed between study sites, which may have limited causal inference. The use of an open‐label, extension study design for these analyses introduces risk of selection bias because the study population was enriched with participants who likely tolerated and responded well to the migraine preventive treatment, potentially overestimating the beneficial effects of atogepant. Additionally, this open‐label trial included participants from two different lead‐in trials with different average length of treatment gaps (PROGRESS: Most participants reinitiated treatment after a long gap; ELEVATE: Most participants received continuous treatment). Notably, treatment gap was included as a covariate in the regression model used in this analysis and was not associated with weight loss. The high proportion of women (88%) and lack of racial diversity (84% White) may also affect the generalizability of study findings, although this demographic is typical of the migraine population. 2

Additional limitations related to the analysis are also present. Socioeconomic status and treatment adherence were not included as covariates in the regression model used in this analysis. However, treatment adherence was high in study 312: Mean (SD) adherence was 98.6% (6.5%) at week 52. Another potential confounding factor was the use of topiramate (an oral migraine preventive treatment that has previously been associated with weight loss 27 ), which was reported by some participants. Although our analysis did not subdivide individuals by topiramate use, this proportion was small (approximately 1%) and thus was unlikely to substantially affect study outcomes. The potential placebo effect, regression to the mean, and Hawthorne effect were not addressed in this analysis, although the effect of atogepant on body weight was compared with that of placebo and standard of care in the analysis of previous trials. 17 Additionally, although a regression analysis found a significant impact of baseline BMI on weight loss of ≥5% at week 52, further analyses are needed to fully characterize weight loss within BMI subgroups.

As noted above, our analysis evaluated change in body weight (safety endpoint) using data from an open‐label clinical trial designed to evaluate atogepant as a preventive treatment for migraine. To strengthen the validity and clinical applicability of the current observations, data are needed from a rigorously designed clinical trial focused on the impact of atogepant on body weight. Additional research should incorporate randomized controlled comparisons, prespecified weight‐related endpoints, and rigorous methods to account for potential biases and confounding factors.

CONCLUSION

Participants receiving atogepant 60 mg once daily for the long‐term preventive treatment of migraine reported weight loss that was consistent in those with CM and in those with EM who were previously failed by two to four classes of conventional oral preventive medication. Approximately 45% of atogepant‐treated participants had a ≥5% weight loss at any time during the present study, and approximately 30% had a ≥5% weight loss at week 52, suggesting that a substantial number of individuals achieved a clinical weight loss criterion (≥5%) that may be associated with health benefits. Future randomized, controlled studies are needed to confirm these findings and to further characterize weight loss associated with atogepant treatment.
