Multidisciplinary management of persistent severe cancer pain: a collaborative European approach
Authors: Denis Dupoiron, María Luz Padilla, Monica Iorfida, Alejandro Falcon, Matthew R D Brown, Christophe Perruchoud, Xander Zuidema, Vittorio Guardamagna, Carlos Tornero, Christoph Hoefing, Somnath Bagchi, Debora Van Keulen, Rita Kortbaoui, Teresa Macarulla, Mario Di Palma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCancer pain is often undertreated, with patients frequently referred late to specialist services. This study aimed to develop expert-led recommendations to address unmet needs in the management of persistent severe cancer pain in Europe.MethodsAn online questionnaire on cancer pain management was used to identify controversies in the clinical practice of European oncologists. A series of recommendations to address these controversies was then proposed and validated, using a Delphi-like methodology, by a joint expert panel of pain specialists and oncologists.ResultsConsensus was reached on 13 recommendations. Key agreements focused on the following: (i) collaboration: establishing cancer pain committees (87% agreement) and shared protocols (100%), (i) education: implementing telematic consultations (100%) and training sessions for oncologists (100%) and (iii) referral: establishing clear referral criteria (100%) for patients with poor quality of life (QoL) (100%) or persistent pain, regardless of aetiology (87–100%), to ensure timely access to specialist care.ConclusionEffective cancer pain management necessitates enhanced collaboration between specialties, targeted education for oncologists, and locally co-created protocols with clear referral criteria. These measures are essential to ensure that patients are referred to pain specialists in a timely and appropriate manner, which is crucial for improving their QoL.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00520-025-09997-3.Keywords: Cancer pain, Persistent severe pain, Pain specialist, Delphi-like methodology, Clinical practice, Oncology expert

Introduction

The global burden of cancer is both substantial and growing. In 2022, there were nearly 20 million new cases and approximately 10 million deaths. Projections suggest this could rise to 35 million new cases annually by 2050, representing a 77% increase from 2022 [1]. Pain is a pervasive and distressing symptom among cancer patients [2, 3], often presenting as the most common manifestation at diagnosis [4]. A comprehensive systematic review reports a significant proportion of patients experience pain, ranging from 39% in patients after curative treatment, to 55% in patients on anticancer treatment and to 66% in patients with metastatic, advanced, or terminal disease [5]. Over a third of these patients rate their pain as moderate to severe [5, 6], which significantly impairs their quality of life (QoL) [2, 3, 7]. A pan-European survey from 2006 to 2007 revealed that 56% of patients with cancer experienced moderate-to-severe pain on at least a monthly basis [8]. Critically, despite its prevalence and the existence of effective guidelines, an estimated 51% of cancer patients experience undertreatment of their pain [9]. Furthermore, up to one-third of patients receive inadequate analgesia [10, 11], with 26% of patients failing to achieve sufficient pain relief with systemic analgesics or experience intolerable adverse effects [12], leading to increased suffering and a significantly reduced QoL [4].

This situation presents a clinical paradox. Although the management of cancer pain remains a major clinical challenge [13], the therapeutic arsenal for managing severe cancer pain has improved in the last decades [5]. The pharmacological landscape is evolving beyond traditional algorithms towards more sophisticated, multimodal strategies [13], and the evidence for advanced interventional therapies is robust [6]. For instance, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that intrathecal drug delivery can reduce pain scores by over 50% while substantially decreasing the need for systemic opioids and their associated toxicities [7]. The persistent gap between the availability of these effective treatments and their suboptimal use in practice suggests that the primary obstacles are not therapeutic, but rather systemic and organisational.

In clinical practice, referrals to specialised pain or palliative care services are often delayed [8], preventing patients from accessing the very expertise required to navigate this complex therapeutic landscape [7]. This disconnect between oncology and specialist pain services represents a critical failure in the delivery of integrated supportive care, a standard championed by leading bodies such as Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer (MASCC) [14]. The ‘missing link’ appears to be the organisational infrastructure that enables timely communication, appropriate referral, and collaborative decision-making [15].

To address these deficiencies, this study brought together a panel of European pain management and oncology experts. The objective was to develop practical recommendations to improve the management of severe cancer pain, focusing on establishing clear and effective referral pathways to ensure patients receive timely and appropriate specialist care. Using a Delphi-like methodology informed by a preliminary online survey, the expert panel identified key controversies and reached a consensus on actionable recommendations. This paper reports the results of the expert consensus study.

Materials and methods

Study design

The project was guided by two scientific committees: a Pain Scientific Committee (PSC, n = 11) and an Oncology Scientific Committee (OSC, n = 4). The study proceeded in two phases: a diagnostic survey to identify controversies in clinical practice (Phase I), followed by a consensus-building process to establish expert recommendations (Phase II).

In Phase I, the PSC developed a 19-item online questionnaire to assess the management of persistent severe cancer pain in the routine clinical practice of European oncologists. The questionnaire, validated by the OSC, covered six topics: (1) perceptions and clinical relevance, (2) diagnosis and assessment, (3) conventional pain management, (4) interventional pain management, (5) patient follow-up, and (6) referral practices. The survey targeted European Medical Oncology specialists with a minimum of five years of post-residency experience. The members of the PSC and OSC shared the questionnaire link to 10 relevant contacts who met the inclusion criteria. The questionnaire could be further distributed using a snowball sampling method. The survey was conducted between July 3 and October 18, 2023.

In Phase II, the PSC analysed the survey results to identify five main areas of controversy in clinical practice (Supplementary Table S1). Based on these findings, the committees jointly proposed 15 initial recommendations to address the identified gaps. These recommendations were subjected to a two-round, face-to-face Delphi-like validation process at the European Meeting on Severe Cancer Pain Management in Barcelona on 17th November 2023. The expert panel comprised the 15 members from both scientific committees. In each round, participants anonymously registered their level of agreement with each recommendation using a 9-point Likert scale (1 = ‘completely disagree’ to 9 = ‘completely agree’) via a digital application. Responses were divided into three tertiles: disagreement (1–3), indeterminacy (4–6), and agreement (7–9). A recommendation achieved consensus if ≥ 75% of responses fell within the same tertile. Recommendations without consensus in the first round were discussed, reformulated if necessary or deleted, and voted on in a second round.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (median, interquartile range [p25–p75]) were calculated for all items. The internal consistency of the entire questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α of C), ranging from 0 to 1, with lower values indicating lower reliability (acceptable values: > 0.7; high reliability: 0.7–0.9; very high reliability: > 0.9). The correlation between the two rounds of the questionnaire was measured using the Spearman coefficient (rs) according to Ortega et al. 2009 [16] criteria (poor or none: rs = 0–0.25; weak: rs = 0.26–0.50; moderate to strong: rs = 0.51–0.75; strong to very strong: rs = 0.76–1; perfect: rs = 1). Concordance was qualitatively assessed using the kappa index (k) and considering response tertiles (no agreement: k < 0; poor or none: k = 0–0.20; weak: k = 0.21–0.40; moderate: k = 0.41–0.60; good: k = 0.61–0.80; very good: k = 0.81–1) [17]. The coefficient of variation (CV) of the questionnaire, which determined the need for further rounds, was calculated for each round, and the relative change (Δ) in the second round over the first (CV second – CV first/CV first) was calculated. A ΔCV < 10% was considered indicative that further rounds would not yield substantive changes. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

Phase I: baseline characteristic of respondents

A total of 77 oncologists completed the survey. The median age was 43 years (range: 32 to 66 years), and 66% were female. Most (57%) had practiced for 5 to 14 years since completing residency, and 58% were based in specialised oncology hospitals. Key specialties included breast (22%), gastrointestinal (14%), genitourinary (12%), and thoracic (12%) oncology.

Phase I: survey findings and identified controversies

The survey (Supplementary Table S1) identified five main areas of controversy in current clinical practice:

Communication and collaboration: a lack of communication between oncologists and pain specialists was noted by 31% of respondents, and the role of pain specialists was not always clearly defined in cancer pain management, with 51% of respondents considering that pain specialists should attend to other multidisciplinary meetings.

Phase II: Delphi-like consensus process

The 15-member expert panel reached a consensus on 13 of the 15 proposed recommendations. In the first round, 11 items achieved consensus (73.4% agreement rate), with a high overall internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.780). Three items without consensus were carried to the second round after being rephrased, along with one newly added recommendation. Of these four, three achieved consensus. The final recommendations (13 of the 16 final recommendations, 81.25% agreement rate, Table 1) are grouped into three domains: collaboration and communication, education and training, and referral.

Collaboration and communication

Regarding collaboration and communication (Table 1), experts reached a strong consensus on the need for improved collaboration. They recommended the creation of specific committees for cancer pain treatment in centres where a tumour board is not available, as well as a shared common protocol for treating and referring patients. Early communication on pain management and the role of pain specialists were identified as key drivers of patient QoL.

Education and training

In the context of Education and Training (Table 1), the panel recommended that pain specialists should organize regular update sessions for oncologists on advances in pain management, including guidelines, case studies, interventional techniques and appropriate patient selection. Oncologist and pain specialist should implement digital communication for cancer pain discussions.

Referral

Regarding Referral (Table 1), to improve cancer pain care and prevent delays in treatment, clear-cut referral criteria are needed. Cancer patients should be referred if pain is an initial symptom, especially in cancers that are frequently associated with pain, but also, when they have poor QoL due to persistent severe cancer pain, regardless of pain localisation, chemotherapy-related adverse events or conventional pain treatment side effects.

The robustness of the consensus process was confirmed by statistical analysis. The overall internal consistency of the questionnaire was high across both rounds (Cronbach’s α = 0.780 and 0.776, respectively). A strong quantitative correlation between rounds (Spearman’s r = 0.868, P < 0.001) and a moderate qualitative concordance (Kappa k = 0.500, P = 0.006) demonstrated the stability of the expert opinions. The low to moderate Spearman correlation coefficients, combined with Kappa values indicating only weak or no agreement, demonstrate that rephrasing those three recommendations in the second round led to a significant and meaningful shift in expert opinion. The minimal change in the coefficient of variation (relative ΔCV = 5.63%) confirmed that a third round was unnecessary.

Discussion

This study, through a formal consensus process, has produced 13 clear recommendations to address critical gaps in the management of persistent severe cancer pain. The consensus highlights three core pillars for improvement: enhancing collaboration and communication, standardising referral pathways, and promoting targeted education.

Chronic pain is frequently associated with cancer, yet it remains poorly controlled in one-third of cancer patients [4, 10]. This suboptimal management can lead to disability and reduced QoL [4, 18]. An estimated 51% of cancer patients experience undertreatment of their pain [9]. Despite significant progress has been made in chronic cancer pain management in the last few years with the novel taxonomy of the International Classification of Disease (ICD-11) and the updated World Health Organisation (WHO) analgesic ladder and The Polyanalgesic Consensus Conference (PACC) guidelines [6, 19, 20], the translation of these theoretical frameworks into clinical practice remains a critical challenge. The findings of this study reveal fundamental shortcomings in the management of cancer-related pain among oncologists in Western Europe, underscoring the need for systematic improvements.

A central theme emerging from our data is the disconnect between specialties. The 100% consensus on the need for shared protocols for treatment and referral, and the strong support (87%) for establishing dedicated cancer pain committees, highlight a clear mandate for structural integration. This resonates with the concept that the ideal of holistic, multidisciplinary supportive care [11, 14] has been ‘eroded’ into fragmented services [21]. In this regard, supportive care should be viewed not as a series of additive services, but as a framework of cancer supportive care [21]. The validity of these findings is powerfully underscored by independent research. A Spanish Delphi study by Escobar et al. identifies an identical set of barriers and, remarkably, also reached a 100% consensus on the need for shared protocols [15]. The lack of communication and inconsistent referral pathways identified in our study directly reflect this fragmented approach, a reality that remains prevalent in clinical practice.

The necessity of this integration is amplified by the increasing complexity of the clinical landscape. As the management of cancer pain is proposed to evolve beyond the traditional WHO ladder towards a more personalised, multimodal, crossroad strategies [2, 13], the demand for specialised expertise grows. Our survey reveals that 55% of oncologists were unfamiliar with the full therapeutic portfolio of their local pain unit, a critical knowledge gap. This finding provides the rationale for our panel’s unanimous recommendation for pain units to organise regular update sessions for oncologists. Likewise, Escobar et al. pointed to the need for continuing education and training of oncologists [15]. Such educational initiatives are not merely beneficial; they are a prerequisite for aligning clinical practice with the evidence on advanced therapies [6, 7] and for navigating the complex risk–benefit calculus of modern analgesia [13].

Furthermore, our study highlights the urgent need for clear and timely referral pathways. The consensus that patients should be referred when experiencing poor QoL (100%) or persistent pain regardless of its aetiology (87–100%) reflects a shift towards a more proactive, patient-centred approach. The clinical cost of current, delayed referral patterns is substantial. A key meta-analysis by Perruchoud et al. quantifies the significant benefit of advanced interventional therapies like intrathecal drug delivery (IDD), which can reduce pain by over 50% and drastically cut systemic opioid use [7]. The fact that patients often access these therapies late, if at all, is a direct consequence of the referral failures our study aims to correct. The existence of such systemic barriers is not an isolated finding. A specific consensus on intrathecal therapy candidates also documented these limitations, which prevent patients from fully benefiting from proven and effective advanced therapies [3]. This context explains the recent evolution of the PACC guidelines, which now advocate for an earlier consideration of IDD in the treatment algorithm [6]. Therefore, our results suggest that the persistent problem of undertreated cancer pain is not due to lack of effective therapies, but rather to systemic and organisational failures that prevent their optimal implementation.

Interestingly, the items that failed to reach consensus in our study are equally insightful, as they reveal the tension between the ideal and the practical. The proposal for a pain specialist to attend every tumour board, for instance, was not endorsed, likely due to perceived resource and logistical constraints rather than a lack of perceived value. Similarly, the idea that all patients with cancer pain should be seen by a pain unit was not supported, suggesting the panel favoured a more targeted, criteria-based approach over a blanket policy that could overwhelm services. These ‘negative’ results strengthen the importance of our successful recommendations, which offer a more pragmatic and feasible pathway towards integration.

Although this study has strengths including its pan-European, multidisciplinary panel and robust methodology, we acknowledge several limitations. Our expert panel comprised 15 specialists, which might be considered a modest sample size, it represents a novel approach to this important question. Additionally, the panel consisted of highly experienced oncologists and pain specialists representing diverse European healthcare systems, providing valuable cross-cultural perspectives. Responses inevitably reflect individual clinical experiences that may be influenced by several factors including country-specific healthcare systems, hospital protocols and local resource availability. Nevertheless, the panellists’ broad expertise in cancer and pain fields strengthens the validity of their contributions, and, although we employed a modified version of the traditional Delphi methodology, the statistical analyses support the robustness of our methodology. The high internal consistency and strong quantitative correlation between rounds demonstrate the reliability of the process. Despite these limitations, the high degree of consensus achieved, and its strong alignment with independent national studies [3, 15], suggests our findings are both valid and widely applicable irrespective of the geographic location.

Conclusion

This consensus provides a clear and actionable roadmap for addressing the identified gap and improving the management of severe cancer pain. Multidisciplinary collaboration, enhanced communication, continuing training on pain management, and clearer referral pathways are essential for an improved and effective cancer patient care. This represents a fundamental step towards ensuring all cancer patients receive timely, effective, and comprehensive pain management, thereby improving their QoL and potentially enhancing oncology outcomes.

Declarations

Ethics approval

Ethics approval was not required for this study. This study is based on clinical practice reported by Pain and Oncology specialists through an online questionnaire and does not present data from any study involving human or animal participants conducted by any of the authors.

Consent to participate

Participation in this study was voluntary. All physicians who completed the questionnaire provided consent for the use of their responses in the subsequent analysis prior to completing the questionnaire. All participants involved in the Delphi-like methodology provided verbal informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study. Confidentiality and anonymity of participant responses were strictly maintained throughout the study to protect their privacy. No personal identifying information was collected or shared during the research process.
