Stress-mediated multi-fault rupture dynamics of the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquake sequence, Türkiye
Authors: Süleyman S. Nalbant, Fatih Uzunca, Ian G. Main, Murat Utkucu, Hatice Durmuş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The 2023 Kahramanmaraş (M7.8) and Elbistan (M7.6) earthquakes in Türkiye provide a profound case study in multi-fault rupture dynamics. We update a pre-existing Coulomb stress model¹ to analyse the historical and immediatestress conditions that caused this doublet. Our fi ndings indicate the Pazarcık segment of the Kahramanmaraşearthquake source rupture failed under high historical loading (12 bars since 1822) and immediate Coulomb stresstransfer (~2.5 bars) from the initial Narlı rupture. The pattern of stress changes is consistent with the observations ofsuper-shear rupture to the Northeast of the Pazarcık segment and sub-shear rupture speeds to the south-west.Conversely, the Elbistan earthquake was primarily triggered by signifi cant normal stress reduction (unclamping; ~11bars) from the mainshock, which overcame an initially negative stress state, and creating favourable conditions forthe observed super-shear rupture. This sequence underscores the critical role of combined coseismic and long-termstress transfer in driving complex, cascading fault ruptures.

Introduction

On 6 February 2023, two major earthquakes near Kahramanmaraş (KM) (M7.8) and Elbistan (M7.6) in southeastern Türkiye caused unprecedented devastation and loss of life across 11 provinces (Fig. 1). The events claimed over 53,000 lives in Türkiye and 8,000 in Syria, caused over 107,000 injuries, and incurred massive financial losses2–4.

The M7.8 Kahramanmaraş (KM) earthquake ruptured approximately 375 km along the East Anatolian Fault Zone (EAFZ), involving three segments (Fig. 2b): The Narlı segment of the Dead Sea Fault, followed by the Pazarcık and Amanos segments of the EAFZ5,6. For simplicity, we refer to the contiguous Pazarcık and Erkenek segments (which ruptured as a single sub-event) as the ‘Pazarcık’ segment. Nine hours later, the M7.6 Elbistan earthquake occurred along the east-west trending Çardak fault (hereafter referred to as the Elbistan fault to avoid confusion with the event name), producing ~ 165 km of surface rupture and involving a sequence of 3 segments that began on its central section before propagating to the western and eastern segments7. This rare occurrence of two M > 7.5 earthquakes in one day significantly amplified losses, an effect further exacerbated by local site amplification and ground failure along coseismic rupture zones8,9.

The KM earthquake was tragic but unsurprising. Stress evolution analysis along the EAFZ in 2002 had already identified the Kahramanmaraş-Malatya region, highly loaded by historical events but seismically quiescent since 1513, as a likely location for the next large earthquake in southeastern Türkiye1. The M7.8 KM earthquake nucleated some 40 km south of the EAFZ on the Narlı Segment. Near-field seismic data confirmed that the rupture on the Narlı fault transitioned from sub-Rayleigh to super-shear within approximately 19.5 km10. This small rupture might have arrested with limited damage, but static and dynamic stresses11,12 from this event brought the relatively highly stressed Pazarcık segment even closer to failure. This segment then ruptured bilaterally about 10 s after nucleation on the Narlı Segment, causing peak moment release and triggering successive failures of the Pazarcık and Amanos segments8,13–15 (Fig. 2c). Previous studies of stress transfer during this sequence reveal how complex nonlinear effects within the sequence transformed a modest initial rupture into a cascading sequence culminating in the Elbistan mainshock 9 h later8,13,15, but neglected the long-term stress evolution prior to its nucleation. Moreover, previous coseismic Coulomb stress modelling studies of the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquake sequence (e.g., refs. 13, 15, 19) report stress changes that differ in magnitude from one another and from those presented here. These differences arise from variations in adopted slip models, model parameters, and fault geometries, as well as from the fact that most studies focus on coseismic stress changes associated with individual rupture segments. As a result, absolute stress amplitudes are not directly comparable, although the first-order spatial patterns of stress increase and decrease along the EAFZ are broadly consistent. This highlights the need for approaches that place the 2023 sequence within a longer-term stress evolution framework.

Here, we update the Coulomb stress model of ref. 1 using revised historical and paleoseismic constraints by recalculating the pre-2023 stress state resulting from cumulative Coulomb stress transfer and viscoelastic relaxation associated with earthquakes since 1822. While previous studies13,15 primarily focused on coseismic stress changes, our approach integrates long-term stress loading with viscoelastic and pore-pressure–related effects, allowing us to show how inherited stress conditions facilitated sub-event triggering during the Kahramanmaraş earthquake and subsequently promoted failure of the Elbistan fault, providing a physically grounded explanation for the observed super-shear and sub-shear rupture behaviour.

Coseismic and postseismic stress change between 1822 and 2023 (pre-2023 sequence)

Reliable records of large earthquakes in the area, complete with location and magnitude, are available from the 19th century onward and correlate with mapped active faults16-18 (Fig. 2). Due to the large uncertainty surrounding earlier events, our Coulomb modelling focuses on a series of consecutive earthquakes from 1822 onward, consistent with the selection criteria of ref. 1 (‘Modelled Earthquakes’ in Methods).

In the first part of the paper, we concentrate on the effects of co-seismic and subsequent viscoelastic Coulomb stresses to isolate their role on the triggering of subsequent events, including during the sequence (‘Calculations of Coulomb Stresses’ in Methods).

Figure 3 shows cumulative co-seismic, postseismic (viscoelastic or VE), and total (coseismic + VE) Coulomb stresses before the 2023 sequence at 10 km depth. The Narlı fault received cumulative coseismic Coulomb stress load ranging from 17.63 bars at its SW end to 1.42 bars at its NE end (Fig. 3a). Additionally, it was subsequently loaded with VE Coulomb stresses between 7.05 and 0.55 bars from SW to NE tips (Fig. 3b). It is clear that the Narlı fault was highly stressed, primarily due to stress transfer from the 1822 event, now assigned to the nearby Yesemek fault (Fig. 2b) as explained in ‘Modelled Earthquakes’ in Methods. The Pazarcık segment was also positively stressed, though the Amanos segment shows localized high-stress patches but lies predominantly in a stress shadow from the 1822 event (Figs. 3a and c).

Many faults show positive total stress changes on Fig. 3c. However, a large part of the Elbistan fault exhibits consistently negative coseismic (ranging from − 0.62 to − 0.25 bars from E to W) and postseismic (ranging from − 0.5 to − 0.05 bars from E to W) stress changes prior to 2023 (green/blue on maps; Fig. 3a, b and c). This raises the question of whether the Elbistan rupture was primarily triggered by stress changes from the earlier KM event. Having established this baseline, we next examine the impacts of the 2023 events.

Coulomb stress changes during the 2023 Kahramanmaraş Earthquake

Ref. 1 reported co-seismic and secular Coulomb stress changes along the EAFZ from 1822 to 2002. Their findings indicated that stress levels had reached particularly high values along the Pazarcık segment by 2002, ranging from 12.4 bar at the segment’s centre to over 20 bar at its northeastern end. As previously indicated, the 2023 M7.8 rupture initiated on the NNE-striking Narlı fault (Figs. 1 and 2) before jumping onto the NE-striking Pazarcık segment (Fig. 4a, b). This immediate triggering was likely promoted by a combination of the long-term historical loading by stress transfer described above and the additional static stress transfer from the initial rupture on the Narlı rupture. Coseismic Coulomb stress changes on rupture planes Pazarcık (sub event 2) and Amanos (sub event 3) from Narlı (sub event 1) alone are shown in Fig. 4b and c respectively. Figure 4b shows Pazarcık rupture experienced up to 20 bars of positive Coulomb stress change (though locally 2 bars of negative stress change). The nucleation point received approximately 2.5 bars of coseismic Coulomb stress, and was followed by bilateral rupture propagation at the speed of super-shear (4.0–4.5 km/s) in northeast direction19, where our coseismic Coulomb stress change was uniformly positive (Fig. 4b), while at the speed of sub-shear (~ 2 km/s) in southwest direction12,15,19,20 where our coseismic Coulomb stress change is more variable (Fig. 4b). This observed correlation between a zone of uniformly high cumulative Coulomb stress and sustained super-shear rupture is consistent with numerical models that identify high, homogeneous initial stress as a primary condition enabling the transition to and maintenance of super-shear speeds21.

Ref (19) also demonstrated that rupture propagation to the south-west along the Amanos segment occurred at sub-shear speeds. As shown in Fig. 4c, the segment initially exhibited negative coseismic Coulomb stress change (reaching − 1.9 bars) in its northeastern portion, with approximately − 0.5 bars at the nucleation point. However, the cumulative coseismic stress from subevents 1 (Narlı) and 2 (Pazarcık) segments shown in Fig. 4d produced over 40 bars of positive Coulomb stress change across most of the subevent 3 (Amonos) fault plane, comparable to the typical stress drop for earthquakes. While a narrow band of negative stress persisted at the segment’s NE tip, this barrier proved insufficient to arrest the sub-shear rupture propagation.

Triggering of the Elbistan Earthquake

Figure 5a displays the cumulative coseismic Coulomb stress changes on the fault system from 1822 until immediately before the Elbistan earthquake, i.e. including stress transfer from the KM event. The hypocenter nucleated within a ~ 1 bar positive Coulomb stress zone on the Elbistan fault (red) but subsequently propagated into regions of negative Coulomb stress change (green/blue). Figures 5b-d illustrate the individual stress components (i.e., shear and normal stresses) that constitute the Coulomb stress changes on a cross-section of the Elbistan fault. We begin with the stress effects of all events that occurred between 1822 and the KM event (Fig. 5b). The normal stress change ranges from − 0.44 to -1.78 bars, while the shear stress varies between 0.22 and 0.50 bars along the cross-section (Fig. 5b). Consequently, the resulting cumulative Coulomb stress change ranges from − 0.63 to 0.31 bars, with the hypocenter located in an area of negative Coulomb stress change prior to the KM event. As underlined in the previous section, the VE Coulomb stress change at the hypocentral area is also negative (-0.25 bars). This indicates that the pre-2023 stress state prior to the KM event was not especially conducive to rupture on the Elbistan earthquake source. However, the KM earthquake dramatically modified the stress state of the Elbistan source fault complex (Fig. 5c), reducing normal stress (unclamping) by over 11 bars (red) while inducing a small amount of positive shear stress change (< 1 bar). This stress modification changed the cumulative coseismic Coulomb stress change from negative before the event (Figs. 4a-b and 6b and b) to positive afterwards (Fig. 5d). When incorporating all previous events (Fig. 5d), the model shows slightly reduced unclamping but increased shear stress (~ 1 bar), ultimately generating positive cumulative coseismic Coulomb stress changes up to 6 bars over half of the cross-section, creating conditions favourable for a large source rupture at super-shear speeds, as observed¹³,¹⁵,¹². The hypocenter was located in an area with a Coulomb stress change of ~ 1.32 bars, which may have contributed the rupture initiation of the Elbistan earthquake significantly.

Interpretation and discussion of results

Our work on both events confirms the hypothesis that uniform positive (or more variable) cumulative Coulomb stresses help promote (or inhibit) super-shear rupture. Our stress modelling (Fig. 4b) also indicates that the − 9 bars Coulomb stress change located near the southwest of the nucleation point (marked by a star) on the Pazarcık segment may have acted as a barrier22, causing rupture deceleration. This observation is consistent with models demonstrating how pre-stress influences rupture geometry and velocity (e.g22,23.,).

Ref (22). emphasized the critical role of stress barriers (regions of negative Coulomb stress change) in controlling rupture propagation along the Mentawai segment of the Sunda megathrust. Three large earthquakes partially ruptured 700 km long section of the segment in 2007 and 201024. On September 12, 2007, M 8.4 and M 7.9 earthquakes struck 12 h apart. In 2010, an M 7.8 earthquake initiated seaward of the Pagai Islands, precisely where the 2007 M 8.4 rupture had ended, and ruptured the shallow portion of the megathrust25. The research of ref (22). showed that the 1797 (M 8.7) and 1833 (M 8.9) earthquakes created long-lasting stress shadows that persisted into the 21st century, and concluded that these barriers likely either stopped or redirected ruptures during the 2007 (M8.4, M7.9) and 2010 (M7.8) earthquakes, preventing larger thoroughgoing ruptures. Notably, the 2007 M 8.4 rupture terminated abruptly when it encountered the 1833 stress shadow. Similarly, ref (23). documented how the 2007 M 7.9 earthquake in the same sequence exhibited a two-stage rupture process; initial propagation followed by an approximately 10-second stall upon encountering the 1833 stress barrier before resuming. This demonstrates how stress barriers can at least temporarily arrest rupture propagation, affecting both rupture speed and slip distribution.

Figure 4c shows the Amanos segment initially exhibited negative Coulomb stress (peaking at -1.9 bars) in its northeastern portion, with approximately − 0.5 bars at the nucleation point. However, the combined stress from subevents 1 and 2 (Fig. 4d) generated over 40 bars of positive stress change across most of the fault plane. Although a narrow zone of negative cumulative Coulomb stress change persisted at the northeastern tip, this barrier proved insufficient to arrest the observed sub-shear rupture propagation19.

The Mw7.8 KM mainshock induced a significant normal stress reduction (up to 11 bars) on the Elbistan rupture, while shear stress changes remained relatively small (< 1 bar) (Fig. 5c). This unclamping effect increased Coulomb stress to over 5 bars, overcoming the fault’s prior stress deficit (Fig. 5c). As shown in Fig. 3b, VE loading onto the Elbistan fault from preceding events since 1822 is negligible (~ − 0.3 bars). Consequently, the KM earthquake alone accounted for > 95% of the stress change on the Elbistan Fault in the studied period. This stress change appears to have been a crucial element leading to failure, creating high and uniform stress change conditions that are conducive to the super-shear rupture speeds also inferred for components of the Elbistan rupture13,15,19 (Fig. 5d).

Unclamping can occur by reducing the normal stress and/or increasing the pore pressure²⁶⁻²⁸. The 1987 Elmore Ranch-Superstition Hills sequence exemplifies this mechanism: The M6.2 Elmore Ranch event unclamped the Superstition Hills fault by a normal stress change of ~ 30 bars, triggering its M6.6 rupture 11.4 h later despite initially negative shear stress changes29. Similarly, the 1988–1989 Lake Elsman earthquakes unclamped the future Loma Prieta (M6.9, 1989) rupture plane, influencing its slip distribution30. In our case, both the hypocenter and peak slip zone of the Elbistan event occurred within zones of lowered normal stress (Figs. 5c, d). This naturally raises the question of whether a quantitative relationship exists between normal stress change and rupture speed. As shown in Fig. 5c and d, however, super-shear rupture occurred across both clamped and unclamped regions of the Elbistan fault plane, indicating that rupture speed is not governed by a simple or linear dependence on either normal stress or Coulomb stress changes alone. Instead, rupture velocity likely reflects the combined effects of stress heterogeneity, initial shear stress, fault geometry, frictional properties, and dynamic rupture processes.

While Coulomb stress change (Eq. 1 in ‘Calculations of Coulomb Stresses’ in Methods) provides a vital first-order estimate of seismic potential, incorporating mean stress changes (Eq. 2 in ‘Calculations of Coulomb Stresses’ in Methods) offers a more physically complete picture of how the crust is primed for failure, because mean stress change is directly proportional to a change in pore pressure through the Skempton coefficient. Here, mean stress change is defined as the first invariant of the stress tensor and is interpreted as a proxy for pore pressure variations, with reductions in mean stress indicating conditions favourable for fluid-assisted fault weakening. This approach explains not only where earthquakes are more likely to occur but also how the preparatory weakening processes operate31,32. Although normal stress is related to mean stress, it is also affected by fault orientation, whereas mean stress is an invariant of the stress tensor. Over time, pore fluids flow in response to these initial stress changes, and the crust relaxes toward an equilibrium state33,34. Consequently, pore fluid flow must be considered an important factor that modifies both the regional stress field and the frictional strength of faults³⁵.

We therefore examine how mean stress changed immediately following the KM earthquake. Figure 6 shows a map of the mean stress change immediately after the KM event, where negative values represent compressed areas and positive values represent dilatational areas. For comparison, Figs. 6b and c show the mean and normal stress changes, respectively, on the rupture plane of the subsequent Elbistan event. The arrows in Fig. 6b indicate the possible direction of pore fluid diffusion from compressed to dilatational areas. A region of unclamping (positive normal stress change) in Fig. 6c correlates well with the dilatational area in Fig. 6b and is larger both in spatial extent and magnitude of stress change. Pore fluid diffusion into the region of fault unclamping on the Elbistan fault may have exacerbated the unclamping to some extent, although the relatively short time interval implies this time-dependent effect may be secondary. In any case, the initial unclamping, followed by a degree of lubrication, is consistent with the observation of two large earthquakes (M ≥ 7.5) occurring within a single day and in close proximity.

Calculations of coulomb stresses

The Coulomb stress change (Δσf) is defined by.

Laboratory experiments on rocks at low sliding velocities yield friction coefficients between 0.6 and 0.85 (e.g38,39.,), and values of Skempton’s coefficient vary between 0.55 and 0.85 for saturated rocks under undrained conditions³⁶,⁴⁰. Hence, we use µ = 0.7 and B = 0.7 as representative values in the middle of the ranges of published values. These values are also consistent with those used in ref (1), so we can make a direct comparison with the results of that work as a suitable benchmark. This formulation allows us to incorporate and infer pore fluid pressure changes through the mean stress change.

Postseismic viscoelastic (VE) relaxation in ductile layers following large earthquakes (M > 7.0) can persist for decades, altering regional fault stresses⁴²,⁴³. VE stresses often exceed coseismic stresses on distant faults and can play a significant role in accelerating seismic cycles41,43,44.

VE Coulomb stress changes were computed using the VISCO1D code of ref45., which simulates earthquakes as rectangular dislocations embedded in an elastic upper crust overlying a layered viscoelastic lower crust and upper mantle. The rheological structure and material parameters adopted in the modelling are summarized in Extended Data Fig. 1, compiled from refs46–48. The calculations include only coseismic and postseismic (viscoelastic) stress evolution and explicitly exclude secular stress accumulation associated with steady plate motions. Stresses were evaluated on either future rupture planes or digitized active faults using the corresponding strike, dip, and rake angles at a depth of 10 km, approximately half the thickness of the seismogenic layer in the region and close to the reported hypocentral depths of large earthquakes49. Modelled Earthquakes

Since 2002, new paleoseismic data and fault maps have emerged, which necessitate a revision of the input earthquake catalog used in ref1. A number of authors50–52 independently conclude that the 1874 rupture extended farther NE along the EAFZ than previously assumed. Consequently, the EB segment in ref1, extending between Elazığ and Bingöl and identified as one of the two segments posing a particularly high seismic risk, was shorter than modelled in ref1. The forecast made by ref1. was fulfilled in 2010 by an M6.1 event (Fig. 253;).

The modelled earthquake rupture planes are shown in Fig. 2, with their parameters listed in Extended Data Table 1. Our model now includes significant events from adjacent fault zones due to their stress interactions with the EAFZ: the 1939 M7.9 and 1992 M6.8 Erzincan earthquakes (NAFZ) and the 1975 M6.8 Lice event (Bitlis Thrust and Folding Zone, BTFZ; Fig. 2). We also added the 2020 M6.8 EAFZ earthquake, which ruptured a segment between the 1875 and 1905 rupture zones.

The source fault of the 1822 earthquake is now better defined. Early intensity data16,17 place the central part of the damage isoseismal distribution over the northern part of the Yesemek Fault. Conversely, ref18. later identified the Amanos Fault as the source. This interpretation was incorporated into subsequent studies (e.g., ref. 1). However, a field study by ref52. found no preserved geomorphic evidence for a recent surface rupture on the Amanos segment. They concluded that the Yesemek Fault is the most likely source, citing its fresh fault-related topography. This conclusion supports the earlier intensity-based epicentral location of ref16. The geodetic and intensity data are consistent with the low accumulated slip deficit of only 1.06 m since 1822 on the Amanos segment prior to 2023 event, calculated by integrating the observed slip rate of 4.5–6 mm/yr54,55 over 200 years (200 year × 5.3 mm/yr = 1.06 m). This slip deficit is insufficient on its own to explain peak slips of 4.5–6.5 m and an average ~ 2.5 m during the event6,7,13,15,57 with a simple slip-predictable model. Ref (57), using contemporary accounts and the damage distribution, identified the Yesemek and Afrin faults as potential sources (Fig. 2b). On the weight of evidence, as explained above, we assigned the 1822 event to the Yesemek fault (Fig. 2a).

Similarly, ref1. attributed the 1893 Malatya earthquake to a part of the NE Pazarcık segment. Post-2023 observations show up to 6 m of co-seismic displacements here56, corroborated by finite-fault inversions7,8,13,15. This observed slip is much larger than the slip deficit of ~ 69 cm calculated from the geodetic slip rate (4.5–6 mm/yr) integrated over 130 years (130 year × 5.3 mm/yr = 69 cm), but it is consistent with the pattern of intensity data¹⁶ which suggest the BTFZ is the most probable place for this rupture. Accordingly, we use the attribution of ref16. here (Fig. 2).

Conclusions

Our analysis yields the following principal findings:

The 2023 KM earthquake sequence has provided critical insights into multi-fault rupture dynamics. The cascading rupture behaviour, initiating on the off-fault Narlı segment before propagating bilaterally along the Pazarcık segment at super-shear speeds, was strongly controlled by pre-existing stress conditions. Historical stress transfer from the 1822 earthquake created critically loaded conditions, while the Amanos segment’s sub-shear rupture despite initial negative Coulomb stress demonstrates how cumulative stress from multiple subevents can overcome local barriers.

The M7.6 Elbistan earthquake was dominantly triggered through an unclamping mechanism (≤ 11 bars normal stress reduction) from the KM mainshock along a large part of the rupture, also creating conditions favourable to the observed super-shear rupture. This unclamping effect was sufficient to overcome initially unfavourable Coulomb stress conditions. In addition, reductions in mean stress, which are directly related to increases in pore pressure, indicate a broader state of crustal weakening that may have further facilitated rupture initiation. Pore fluid diffusion following the KM earthquake may therefore have enhanced this triggering process by increasing pore fluid pressure on the fault.

Our integrated coseismic and postseismic stress modelling from 1822 provides a detailed account of the stress evolution that preconditioned the crust for this cascading failure. The study demonstrates that conventional seismic gap analysis may fail to account for complex fault interaction potentials of the type investigated here, where nearby events can dramatically alter the stress state of a fault through mechanisms like unclamping.
