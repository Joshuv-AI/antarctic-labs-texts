Point-Spread Function Deformations Unlock 3D Localization Microscopy on Spherical Nanoparticles
Authors: Teun APM Huijben, Sarojini Mahajan, Masih Fahim, Peter Zijlstra, Rodolphe Marie, Kim I Mortensen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanoparticles (NPs) have proven their applicability in biosensing, drug delivery, and photothermal therapy, but their performance depends critically on the distribution and number of functional groups on their surface. When studying surface functionalization using super-resolution microscopy, the NP modifies the fluorophore’s point-spread function (PSF). This leads to systematic mislocalizations in conventional analyses employing Gaussian PSFs. Here, we address this shortcoming by deriving the analytical PSF model for a fluorophore near a spherical NP. Its calculation is four orders of magnitude faster than numerical approaches and thus feasible for direct use in localization algorithms. We fit this model to individual 2D images from DNA-PAINT experiments on DNA-coated gold NPs and demonstrate extraction of the 3D positions of functional groups with <5 nm precision, revealing inhomogeneous surface coverage. Our method is exact, fast, accessible, and poised to become the standard in super-resolution imaging of NPs for biosensing and drug delivery applications.Keywords: point-spread function, single-molecule localization microscopy, DNA-paint, nanoparticles, plasmonics, surface functionalization

Functionalized nanoparticles (NPs) have made a rapid and substantial entrée in the modern healthcare industry. The large surface-to-volume ratio and versatile chemical functionalization leading to biocompatibility and functionality make these colloidal particles invaluable for targeted nanomedicine and diagnostics. These nano- to micrometer-sized particles of various materials, sizes, shapes, and compositions have great potential in the biomedical fields of prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of diseases. For example, lipid NPs are used as carriers of mRNA in vaccines against Covid-19. Dielectric NPs (e.g., hydrogel, polymer, or silica) are promising candidates for vehicles of targeted drug delivery.1−3 Metallic (e.g., gold) NPs have optical and plasmonic properties that make them suited as biosensors in diagnostics4−6 and photothermal agents in cancer therapy.7−9

However, the functionalization of the NPs, i.e., the chemical conjugation of molecules such as DNA, peptides, aptamers, drugs, or proteins to the NP surface is crucial for their function. The density and distribution of the functional groups on the NP surface influence the performance of these biofunctionalized particles in their respective applications.10,11 For therapeutic use, multiple studies have shown that the density and distribution of moieties on the surface strongly affect the internalization of gold,12−14 hydrogel,14 quantum dot,15 micelle,16 or polymer17,18 particles into tumor cells. Having a heterogeneous collection of particles impacts the overall performance of those particles.19,20 Not only the variation between particles but even the heterogeneity on the surface of a single NP21 has been shown to affect the ability of biosensing19 and particle assembly,22−25 a topic of great promise for advanced optical materials. Hence, it is crucial to know and control both parameters, density and distribution, to enable rational design of the NPs for the desired application. Conventionally, ensemble-based methods such as spectroscopy or electrophoresis are used to quantify the density of ligands. However, such ensemble averaging does not reveal particle-to-particle heterogeneity let alone the distribution of ligands on each NP.

Single-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM), on the other hand, is ideally suited to study the surface functionalization of individual NPs and to characterize inter- and intraparticle functionalization heterogeneity of the NP in their native, typically aqueous, environment.26−28 To circumvent the diffraction limit, one repeatedly images sparse (random) subsets of fluorophore-labeled functional sites in a sample, as schematically depicted in Figure 1a. Various SMLM techniques exist, with different mechanisms to ensure a sparse subset of fluorophores, e.g., blinking of the fluorophores tuned by the imaging buffer (dSTORM29), the photophysics of the fluorophore itself (PALM30), or sequential binding and unbinding of the fluorophore to the target molecule (PAINT31). The sparsity of emitting fluorophores at each time point ensures that each fluorophore images as a diffraction-limited spot. The position of every fluorophore can be determined by fitting a model for the spatial distribution of photons in the image plane, i.e., the point-spread function (PSF), to the spot in an image. When all such positions of fluorophores on the NP surface are combined, one obtains a super-resolved mapping of the functional groups on the NP26−28 (Figure 1a).

In conventional SMLM, a 2D Gaussian PSF model is used for the analysis of images. For most practical applications, the 2D Gaussian PSF model approximates well the distribution of photons in a spot.32 However, when employing SMLM close to NPs, the presence of the NP in the vicinity of the fluorophore influences its radiation pattern,33−35 which results in a complex distortion of the PSF (Figure 1a,b). Furthermore, the PSF shape strongly depends on the position of the fluorophore relative to the NP (Figure 1b). Consequently, fitting such spots with a 2D Gaussian PSF leads to systematic localization biases, commonly referred to as mislocalizations (Figure 1a). If imaging is done with large pixels, such PSF deformations are often visually concealed, but the mislocalizations still impact the results. Also, techniques like MINFLUX36 and recent variants,37 which allow localization with high precision, would be affected by an optically dense particle. The NP may distort the well-calibrated donut-shaped illumination profile, crucial for obtaining subnanometer precision.

This deformation of the PSF for a fluorophore near a particle is expected for any particle material, dielectric or metallic alike, albeit the mechanism and degree of deformation are different. For dielectric NPs, the refractive index contrast between the particle and the medium results in refraction of emitted photons, where the particle essentially acts as a spherical lens. For metallic particles, on the other hand, coupling of the fluorophore to plasmon resonances has two main effects. First of all, the plasmonic coupling modifies the total fluorescence intensity where enhancements of multiple orders of magnitude have been observed.38−44 More importantly here, also the radiation pattern is modified resulting in strong PSF deformation that depends on the degree of plasmonic coupling between the particle and fluorophore.45−48

Nevertheless, SMLM on NPs is employed in biosensing applications,6,49,50 to study the NP geometry,48,51 to identify the position and intensity of near-field plasmonic hotspots,47,52−55 and to follow chemical reactions catalyzed at the NP surface.56 In these examples, the results are based on the localization of emitters. However, the increased localization precision provided by the fluorescence enhancement (uncertainty scales as , with N the number of detected photons32) is rendered useless since it is accompanied by a dramatic decrease in localization accuracy.57−59

Despite the fact that dielectric particles also induce PSF deformation, the phenomenon has mainly been investigated for metallic particles for which the physical mechanism and severity of mislocalization were quantified using a Gaussian PSF model.33,34,45−47,51−54,57−66 The degree of mislocalization was determined either directly, based on a known position of the emitter relative to the NP,57,59 or indirectly, based on the reconstructed shape of the NP compared to the known shape.33,45,46,51−53,58,61−64 For these plasmonic structures, it was found that the degree of mislocalization is affected by the spectral overlap between the emitter and the NP46−48 emission, and by the position of the emitter with respect to the NP.45,46

These studies employed numerical approaches33,34,45,52−54,57,58,60,62−64 (Figure 2a) to compute the photon distributions in each spot, which in turn enabled the study of mislocalizations. The computational time of each numerically calculated PSF (in the order or minutes) is prohibitive for use in conjunction with localization microscopy image analysis, since a single PSF fit requires many evaluations of the PSF and a typical SMLM experiment consists of several thousand localizations.

Furthermore, the focus has mostly been on the magnitude of the mislocalizations, but never on the origin of the problem, the deformation of the PSF. Previously, mislocalizations induced by the NP have been considered a disadvantage, and the aim has been to minimize the mislocalizations by tuning experimental parameters like the NP material, fluorophore emission wavelength, or refractive index of the medium. Instead, we here take advantage of the fact that the particle-emitter coupling encodes the fluorophore’s position relative to the NP in images to unlock 3D super-resolution microscopy on NPs from 2D images without explicit optical engineering of the PSF. In order to do so, we derive the analytical PSF model for a fluorophore near a spherical NP, based on the Mie67−69 and vector diffraction theories.70,71 Our analytical model, with appropriate approximations, is exact and consistent with numerical calculations, yet up to four orders of magnitude faster than numerical approaches, enabling direct fitting to localization microscopy data sets.

Our analytical PSF model can be used to rapidly identify parameter regimes where PSF deformations are detectable, while at the same time enabling 3D localization microscopy by exploiting these deformations. We employ the PSF model in a fitting procedure to study the surface distribution of single-stranded DNA on spherical dielectric and metallic NPs in 3D. The rapid and precise localization enables mapping of the full surface functionalization in 3D of individual nanometric particles, hence making it possible to visualize interfacial surface chemistry and its heterogeneity. For the gold particles, our analysis reveals an inhomogeneous surface coverage of the accessible DNA docking strands on their surface with a strong heterogeneity between particles.

Results and Discussion

Exact PSF Model for Fluorescent Molecules Near Spherical NPs

The PSF is the probability density function for photons to arrive on the camera. In order to derive it, we calculate the propagation of the emitted light through the microscope. Figure S1a depicts the microscopy system we consider. In this system, the spherical NP and a fluorophore are present in water, just above a glass coverslip, and imaged using an objective lens.

The process of deriving the PSF comprises multiple steps (Figure 2b and Figures S1c and S2). Initially, we express the electromagnetic (EM) field radiated by the fluorophore, modeled as a dipole emitter, in a linear combination of vector-spherical harmonics (VSHs). Expressing the field in spherical basis functions is required to account for interactions with a spherical object, the NP, in contrast to previous PSF models for emitters in water32,72 for which a basis of plane waves was sufficient. Next, we use Mie scattering theory67−69 to determine how the spherical NP scatters the light emitted by the dipole. Both contributions, the initial dipole field and the NP scattered field, are subsequently decomposed into plane waves,73,74 refracted at the water–glass interface, projected into the far-field (i.e., back focal plane), and focused onto the camera by the objective. Note that for simplicity we do not include multiple scattering, since it has a negligible effect on the PSF (Figure S3). The PSF is represented by the intensity of the light at the camera plane, given by the z-component of the Poynting75 vector E⃗ × B⃗*. Concatenating all these steps results in a single analytical expression for the PSF at any position on the camera, which we will refer to as our PSF model (see Methods for more details, Note S2 for the full mathematical derivation, and the GitHub repository for the Python implementation). We evaluate the required integrals in this expression numerically, since no closed-form solution exists.32 This is common practice and does not mean that the propagation of light from the fluorophore to the camera is deteriorated by discretization, as is the case for numerical methods. Implemented in Python (version 3.12), and computed on a standard CPU (Intel Xeon), the evaluation of a single PSF takes approximately 100 ms for 10 × 10 pixels in the PSF and 10 truncated Mie orders (for the dependency of the computational time and the PSF accuracy on the number of pixels in the PSF and the number of considered truncated Mie orders, see Figures S4 and S5).

The PSF Model’s Accuracy and Speed Ensure Its Feasibility for Direct Use in Image Analysis

The accuracy and computational efficiency of our analytical PSF model are quantified by comparing it to numerical calculations of the same NP-dipole system. In order to validate the analytical PSF and highlight the shortcomings of numerical calculations, we additionally developed and optimized a numerical procedure for the calculation of the PSF based on an existing implementation.33 The numerical approach is by construction and approximation, due to the discretization of the system (Figure 2a). Therefore, the results depend on numerical parameters such as the size of the simulated domain and the resolution of the discretization, referred to as mesh size. We investigated the influence of these two parameters on the numerical PSF (Figure S6) and selected parameters (Methods) that result in high precision, i.e., <1% difference with the analytical PSF, with a reasonable computational time in the order of tens of minutes. We quantitatively compare the difference between the analytical and numerical PSF for multiple NP materials, sizes, and compositions, starting with the arbitrary system of a horizontally oriented dipole located 10 nm above the surface of a 200 nm gold spherical NP (Figure 2c). The accuracy of the numerical calculation improves with decreasing mesh size, since the discretized system better resembles reality in that case. By decreasing the mesh size in the numerical approach, the numerical PSF converges toward the analytical PSF (Figure 2c, left y-axis), indicating that the analytical PSF is indeed the exact solution. The associated drastic increase in the numerical computational time (Figure 2c, right y-axis) demonstrates the impracticality of the numerical method for direct use in PSF fitting in localization microscopy. The evaluation of the analytical PSF is four orders of magnitude faster compared to the near-exact numerical PSF.

For completeness, we compare both the electric far-field and the PSF between the analytical and numerical approaches for NPs with other materials, sizes, and compositions such as gold spheres, silica spheres, and gold shells (Figure 2d). Advantageously, the Mie scattering theory allows us to extend our model to other spherical particle geometries such as core–shell particles consisting of a spherical core with concentric shells with arbitrary thicknesses and materials. In particular, we calculate PSFs for particles with a silica core and a gold shell (Figure 2d) that have their plasmon resonance in the near-infrared, making them interesting candidates for targeted photothermal cancer therapy.76 In all examples, the fluorophore positions and orientations lead to exotic PSFs, which exemplify concrete cases that would challenge the applicability of conventional SMLM. We can see that the complex electric vector field measured on an arc of the far-field hemisphere is identical between the analytical and numerical approaches, for different NP compositions, and dipole orientations and positions (Figure 2d). Consequently, the numerical PSFs are almost identical to the analytical PSFs with maximal relative pixel intensity differences around only 1%, explained by the limited discretization resolution of the numerical approach. The full comparison, including all electric field components in the far-field and differences with the analytical PSF, can be found in Figure S7.

In summary, our analytical PSF is correct, compared to numerical approaches that suffer from meshing. Additionally, we have demonstrated a drastic improvement in computational speed of up to four orders of magnitude, compared to the numerical approach using the finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method33 (Figure 2c). This makes our PSF model reliable and practical as an analysis tool for super-resolution imaging of functional groups on spherical NPs.

The Distribution of Functional Sites on Gold NPs Is Patchy

We showcase this analytical approach by applying it to 2D localization microscopy images of emitters on a spherical gold NP to obtain a 3D super-resolution reconstruction. Standard SMLM approaches would fail in this case, since the PSF for an emitter on a spherical NP resembles the PSF for an isolated fixed emitter, due to the strong plasmonic coupling between the emitter and the NP (Figure S8). This results in biased Gaussian fitting.32,72,77 To demonstrate our approach, we perform SMLM on DNA-coated 100 nm gold nanospheres (GNS) by using DNA-based point-accumulation for imaging in nanoscale topography (DNA-PAINT78). We conjugate single-stranded 30-nucleotide (nt) DNA docking strands to the NP surface with a thiol bond (see Methods) and image the sample on an inverted total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscope. Freely diffusing 9-nt complementary single-stranded DNA imager strands, labeled with ATTO655, transiently bind to the NP-bound docking strands (Figure 3a). Each binding event creates a fluorescent burst on the camera (Figure 3b), whose PSF is fitted using our analytical PSF model. Acquisitions of 30 min accumulate hundreds of binding events per individual particle (Figure S9). We perform necessary controls with noncomplementary imager strands (Figure S10) to verify that the observed binding events are indeed specific (i.e., due to DNA hybridization rather than nonspecific sticking), and we perform DNA-PAINT on the glass substrate without NPs to demonstrate that the fluorophores are freely rotating in orientation and that the presence of exotically shaped PSFs (Figure S9) are solely explained by presence of the NP (Figure S11).

The high similarity between the experimental PSFs and the fitted theoretical PSFs shows that the analytical model perfectly describes the experimental data. The fit has four free parameters: the position of the emitter relative to the NP (expressed in spherical coordinates ϕ and θ), the number of photons, and the background intensity. By PSF fitting, the position of each emitter on the 100 nm spherical NP surface is obtained, expressed in spherical coordinates. We detect binding events by thresholding the intensity time trace of each individual GNS and subsequently average the frames within each individual event to obtain the PSF with a higher signal-to-noise ratio (Figure 3b,c). The measured intensity contains not only fluorescence originating from the bound imager strand but also background (BG) photons and one-photon photoluminescence (PL) from the gold. Note that these contributions do not modify the shape of the PSF due to a lack of coherence with the emission of the NP–dye conjugate. To remove these two components, the true fluorescence PSF is obtained by subtracting the average of a set of frames around each event (Methods and Figure S12). The added value of using gold, in addition to the high signal-to-noise ratio due to plasmon-enhanced fluorescence (Figure S13), is that the PL is very photostable and can be used to localize the GNS. This provides us with the GNS position at the time of every measured PSF, which (i) aids the fitting, since only the relative position of the emitter to the NP needs to be determined, and (ii) substitutes the need for in-plane sample drift correction (Figure S14). In the case of dielectric NPs, which lack PL, the NP position can be inferred from either the (auto)fluorescence of the dielectric material itself or the distribution of all localizations on a single NP (see Methods).

We fit all the average PSFs obtained from a single GNS with our analytical PSF model, resulting in the positions of the binding events on the GNS surface (Figure 3d). The analytical PSF model is corrected for field-dependent aberrations quantified on unspecific binding events away from the particles (Methods). We know the position of the GNS from a Gaussian fit of the PL; the only remaining free fit parameters are the position of the emitter on the GNS surface, expressed in spherical coordinates θ and φ, the number of photons in the PSF, and the background generated by camera offset and background photons. These four parameters we extract by a least-squares fitting approach of our analytical PSF model to the experimental PSF (see Methods for details). We project the obtained emitter positions onto a spherical surface to visualize the location of the binding event on the GNS surface (Figure 3d). In principle, every emitter position on the NP surface results in a unique PSF, and with enough photons, the fit would be able to discriminate between them. However, for practical signal-to-noise levels, this is not possible due to the spherical symmetries of the GNS that make multiple emitter positions on the surface result in PSFs with a similar shape. We solve this ambiguity by additionally using the position of the GNS from the PL signal (Figure S15).

The fit assumes a spherical NP with a known diameter. To confirm that the analyzed GNSs are indeed single gold NPs, we performed two additional correlated measurements on the same sample, namely, single particle spectroscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The parameters of the Lorentzian fit to the single particle scattering spectrum in addition to the visual information from the high-resolution SEM image confirm that the GNSs are not clustered and have a size and shape as expected (Figure S16). Interestingly, we note that the PSFs originating from clusters of GNSs (dimers, trimers, heptamers, etc.) show a different set of intriguing shapes (Figure S17), but we exclude them from the analysis here.

We collect hundreds of PSFs per GNS, providing us with good statistics on the surface functionalization. Figure 4a shows the fitting result of all 828 localizations on a single GNS (see Figure S16 for the scattering and SEM results). To better visualize the entire GNS, we perform a 2D unfolding of the spherical surface (Figure 4b), which visualizes all sides of the GNS. We also compute a localization density map (Figure 4c) by rendering each localization as a normalized 2D asymmetric Gaussian, where the two Gaussian axes represent the localization uncertainty (Figure 4d) in the polar (Δθ) and azimuthal direction (Δϕ), respectively. The angular localization uncertainties in the polar and azimuthal directions are obtained directly from the PSF fitting procedure, and we convert them to distances on the surface by multiplying with the radius of the NP. The histogram of the combined uncertainty shows that we can localize the binding events with an uncertainty of 4 ± 1 nm on the surface (Figure 4d), which is consistent with the spread of localizations when fitting individual frames of long-lasting events (Figure S18). The dependency of the localization uncertainty with respect to the fluorophore positions and the number of photons (Figure S32) can be found in Figure S19, together with a comparison to the Cramér–Rao lower bound of the PSF model for these gold NPs.

We clearly see that the localizations are not homogeneously distributed over the GNS. Due to the statistical nature of DNA-PAINT, we do not necessarily probe every available docking strand, and every localization does not necessarily represent a unique docking strand. To draw solid conclusions about the inhomogeneous surface coverage of accessible docking strands, we consider four different analyses. First of all, we apply the same PSF fitting procedure to multiple GNSs, resulting in surface reconstructions that all show an inhomogeneous and different distribution of localizations (Figure 4e). Second, we employ a strand-displacement assay79 (Note S4) to independently quantify the total number of docking strands and the number of accessible docking strands on the NP surface. We obtain a number of (7.0 ± 0.7) × 102 (mean ± std) accessible docking strands per NP, which is approximately the same as the number of detected PSFs per NP. From this, we conclude that every accessible docking strand creates on average a single localization under our experimental conditions. Since the number of localizations per accessible strand follows the Poisson distribution, 63% of the accessible docking strands result in at least one binding event, which makes it very unlikely for an area on the NP that has accessible strands to not have bound any imager strands during the measurement (30 min). Following this logic, a patch of NP surface with only a few localizations is the result of locally having less accessible docking strands. Third, to illustrate that the empty patches are not the effect of undercounting, we show that the surface pattern of localizations is constant over time and is already established after the collection of a fraction of the total number of localizations (Figure S20). If the patchiness was an effect of undercounting, the pattern would have evolved over time, which is indeed not what we observe. Fourth, the localization uncertainty directly provided by the PSF fit is 4 ± 1 nm on the surface (Figure 4d, see Methods), which is small compared to the NP’s circumference. From this combined analysis, we conclude that consistent inhomogeneous surface coverage is not a side effect of having a large localization uncertainty or undersampling but is rather caused by a patchy particle functionalization.

To show that the principle of PSF fitting of deformed PSFs is not limited to metal particles, we performed the same experiment and analysis (see Methods) on 1 μm polystyrene NPs. The neutravidin-functionalized polystyrene NPs are immobilized on a biotin-BSA passivated surface. Afterward, biotin-conjugated docking strands are bound to the NP surface to which ATTO655-labeled 9-nt imager strands bind in a DNA-PAINT experiment imaged in epifluorescence (Figure 5a). The absence of plasmonic coupling between the dielectric material and the emitter results in PSFs of lower intensity, but the scattering of light by the spherical NP is sufficient to significantly deform the PSF, even into shapes not observed for gold (Figure 5b). By PSF fitting, we infer the positions of the binding events on the NP surface. Since we focus the microscope on the center of the NP and the NP is bigger than the depth of focus, the PSFs originating from the bottom part of the NP are too far out of focus for the signal-to-noise to be sufficient for PSF fitting (Figure 5c). Surprisingly, the PSFs originating from the top of the NP, albeit being equally far out of focus, have high intensity and can be fitted. The difference between top and bottom is explained by the fact that the dielectric NP acts as a lens and focuses the excitation light on the top part of the NP (Figure S32b,c). With the obtained number of localizations and the localization precision of 15 ± 10 nm (Figure 5d,e), we can conclude that the surface functionalization for the polystyrene NP is more homogeneous than for the gold NP (Figure 4e). However, taking into account the size difference between the particles, to detect local density variations at the 100 nm scale (as for gold), a larger number of localizations is necessary.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have introduced an analytical PSF model for a fluorophore in close proximity to a spherical NP. The model is up to four orders of magnitude faster compared to state-of-the-art numerical approaches, it is exact, and it is open source: It should become a standard for analysis of SMLM data obtained from spherical particles with compositions and sizes that give rise to optical distortions.

First and foremost, we show an effective way to exploit PSF deformations for 3D localization microscopy on spherical NPs. The computational speed of our model enables fast identification of the degree of PSF deformations, which depends on NP size, NP material, emission wavelength of the fluorophore (Figure S21), and distance between the fluorophore and the NP (Figure S22). In the unlikely case that PSF distortions are negligible, one can make use of conventional localization algorithms that assume a 2D Gaussian PSF. With our method, we can map out the complete surface functionalization on NPs. We demonstrate this by applying our approach to experimental data obtained through DNA-PAINT experiments on DNA-coated spherical 100 nm gold and 1 μm polystyrene NPs. Surprisingly, we find that the functionalization with DNA via thiol chemistry to gold NPs is inhomogeneous over the NP surface.

The patchy nature of the functionalization on gold NPs has never been visualized before and could originate from the presence of facets and an inhomogeneous surface charge,80 leading to an inhomogeneous surface coverage of DNA. It is also noteworthy that areas with a higher number of localizations do not necessarily contain a higher density of DNA strands. The frequency of observed binding events does not only depend on the number of docking strands N but also on the accessibility of the strands, since a high density of DNA may cause steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion. Indeed, the accessibility of the docking strands can modulate the binding kinetics of incoming imager strands and is known to depend strongly on DNA density.81 Our method thereby reveals the presence of accessible docking strands (rather than total docking strands), which is valuable information in the context of biomedical applications where functionality arises from accessible surface groups.82 Moving forward, our method enables researchers to relate inhomogeneous surface coverage to the employed chemical functionalization protocols, in order to better understand and improve desired NP conjugates for a multitude of purposes.

The current model describes the PSF for fluorophores close to spherical NPs. The spherical symmetry of the NP is crucial for being able to employ the Mie scattering theory.

Where our method solves the problem of mislocalizations for spherical NPs, it is important to note that PSF deformation and subsequent mislocalization are general concepts for NPs of any shape. Using our numerical approach, we show that a wide variety of gold NP shapes (cubes, rods, and cones) give rise to deformed PSFs where the fluorophore position is encoded in the shape of the PSF (Figure S23). Even though the analytical solution for other NP shapes is not (yet) derived, having an exact solution for the spherical case allows finetuning of the parameters for the numerical calculations. In this way, PSFs for nonspherical NPs can be computed reliably. For subsequent fitting to experimental data, one will have to resort to data-driven PSF fitting approaches like decomposition into a set of basis functions34 or (convolutional) deep neural networks.83

In the current work, we validated our PSF model by comparison to numerical approaches and achieved consistency with experimental data. Through fitting, we obtain localization precision down to 4 nm for 30,000 photons. Still, exciting future work is necessary to assess the localization accuracy by obtaining the ground-truth position of the fluorophore and comparing this to the inferred position by the PSF fit. To acquire the ground-truth location, one can think of approaches to deterministically place the fluorophore on a designed position relative to the NP, for example with DNA-origami,59 or by correlating with SEM measurements after attaching quantum dots to the DNA docking strands.60

Our approach can visualize functional groups on the molecular level. We anticipate that our method will be instrumental for the future characterization of NP heterogeneity and that the combination with functional assays will provide much-needed information on the correlation between nanoparticle coating (both density and distribution) and particle behavior in biomedical applications.

Methods

Analytical PSF Model

In order to calculate the PSF for a dipole emitter fixed in orientation near a spherical NP, we start from the light emitted by the excited dipole emitter and trace these EM fields through the microscope onto the camera. The calculation consists of six steps, which are schematically depicted in Figure S2. Each of the six steps is explained in Notes S1 and S2, where Note S1 gives a concise textual overview and Note S2 the full in-depth mathematical derivations.

The PSF for a freely rotating dipole is obtained by averaging the PSF for three orthogonal dipole orientations (Figure S24). We mathematically prove that the average over three orthogonal orientations is identical to integrating over all possible orientations (Note S3), in line with previous works.84,85

Numerical PSF Calculations

The numerical approach for calculating the PSFs is based on an existing implementation33 and comprises three steps: (1) numerically calculating the EM near-field and recording them just below the coverslip, (2) far-field projection, and (3) focusing (Figure S1b). In the first step, the EM fields are numerically calculated using FDTD86,87 calculations performed in Ansys Lumerical FDTD software (Lumerical Inc., 3D Electromagnetic Simulator, version 2021 release 1.3) on the high-performance cluster of the Technical University of Denmark.88 In the FDTD algorithm, the electric and magnetic components of the EM-field are propagated over a three-dimensional Cartesian grid in a time-coordinated fashion. As depicted in Figure S1b, the simulated domain encloses the water medium (containing NP and dipole emitter) and the top part of the glass coverslip. Discretizing the entire microscopy setup, from sample to camera, would result in impractical computational times and memory requirements, since these factors scale drastically with the size of the simulation domain. Since the immersion oil and the coverslip have the same refractive index as glass, they are treated as one medium. The complete simulation domain has dimensions 8 μm × 8 μm × 220 nm, with a mesh accuracy parameter of 6 (which Lumerical converts in a mesh size of 17 nm in case of a 200 nm NP). These parameters are determined by a parameter sweep and comparison with the analytical PSF (Figure S6). The mesh is locally refined to a mesh size of 3 nm in a region of 200 nm × 200 nm × 180 nm around the NP, based on the rule of thumb to have a mesh size at least 10 times smaller than the radius of the NP. The simulation time is set to 40 fs, based on the speed of light and the dimensions of the simulation domain. The time it takes the light to travel from the dipole to the edge of the detector is approximately t = s × n/c = 8 μm × 1.33/(3 × 108 m/s) = 35 fs, with s the distance, n the refractive index, and c the speed of light. The EM fields are detected with a near-field detector that is positioned 20 nm below the water–glass boundary with a width extending the entire simulation domain. After running the numerical simulation, the detected EM near-fields are projected into the far-field87 using the build-in function farfieldexact. Note that at this stage, the far-field represents the angular distribution of the collected light and is equivalent to the information found in the back focal plane of the objective. The far-field is evaluated on a hemisphere with a radius of 1 m, centered around the NP center, and is discretized in 100 × 100 points in, respectively, the polar and azimuthal directions (Figure S25). The calculated electric and magnetic complex vector fields on the hemisphere are saved and imported into MATLAB for further analysis. In the third step, the fields are focused onto the camera plane forming the PSF using the Debye–Wolf diffraction integral, in the same way as for the analytical approach (Note S2 chapter 6). As an addition to the field, the code of our validated numerical approach, presented here, and the instructions for the FDTD simulations in Ansys Lumerical FDTD are both publicly available (see Methods).

All necessary scripts to construct the simulation elements, perform the far-field transformation, and calculate the focusing are available on the GitHub repository connected to this publication.

Sample Preparation (DNA-PAINT on Gold NP)

Glass coverslips (22 mm × 22 mm; thickness #1.5) are sonicated in a methanol bath for 20 min and dried with nitrogen gas. The coverslips are then hydrophilized by ozone cleaning for 60 min. For thiolation, the coverslips are dip-coated in a solution of MPTMS (5% v/v) in ethanol (purity >99.9%) for 3 min followed by ethanol rinsing and nitrogen drying.

For immobilizing gold NPs (CTAB-coated 100 nm nanospheres obtained from NanoSeedz, product NS-100-50), 500 μL of stock solution was centrifuged at 1500 rcf for 3 min and the pellet was redispersed in 250 μL of 1 mM CTAB in Milli-Q water to obtain 2× concentrated solution. Right before spin-coating the particles on the thiolated coverslips, the nanospheres are heated to 30 °C for 1 min and sonicated for 30 s to minimize agglomeration. The nanospheres are then immobilized by spin-coating (at 2000 rpm for 1 min) on the thiolated coverslip. The coverslips with GNS are generously rinsed with methanol, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and water, to get rid of the residual CTAB and weakly bound particles.

The GNS were then incubated with a mixture of 5 μM of single-stranded DNA docking strands (5′ SH-CAT CAT CAT ACG CTT CCA ATA ATA CAT CTA-3′, from Integrated DNA Technologies) and 1 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 3, 1 M NaCl) for surface functionalization. After 2 h, slides were rinsed with PBS and buffer B (5 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) to wash away free DNA strands. The slides were stored in buffer B in a humidity chamber, until the optical measurement.

A negative control experiment was performed to confirm that the observed fluorescent events represent the specific binding of the imager strands to the docking strands. The sample with complementary imager strands was washed with buffer B two times. Then, 50 μL of 200 pM (in buffer B) noncomplementary imager strands (5′-TTC TTC TCC C-ATTO655-3′, from Integrated DNA Technologies) was added to the sample slide, and imaging was performed.

Sample Preparation (DNA-PAINT on Polystyrene NP)

Neutravidin-coated 1 μm polystyrene particles (Thermo Fisher Scientific F8777) are immobilized on a clean gridded glass coverslip (Ibidi 10816). To this end, the coverslip is first cleaned using 15 min sonication steps in Triton X-100 and KOH interleaved with sonication in Milli-Q water for rinsing. The coverslip is then dried with nitrogen. A silicone gasket (Grace Bio-Laboratories SKU 103250) cut to the size of the coverslip is deposited to create a chamber, and the assembly is cleaned with oxygen plasma. The cleaned coverslip is incubated with 100 μL of biotinylated BSA (Sigma-Aldrich A8549) at 0.1 mg/mL for 30 min and then rinsed with 1× PBS. Subsequently, the coverslip was incubated for 1 h with 100 μL of 100:1 diluted stock concentration of the particles and then rinsed three times with 200 μL 1× PBS. Thereafter, the sample was incubated with 100 μL of 20 μM biotinylated DNA docking strands for 1 h, washed twice in 1× PBS, and once with the imaging buffer. The docking strand sequence is 5′-biotin-TTATACATCTA-3′ (IDT). The imager strand sequence is 3′-atto655-TATGTAGAT-5′ (IDT). The imager solution is prepared by diluting the imager strand DNA to 50 pM in imaging buffer consisting of buffer B with 10 mM MgC2. Both concentrations are adjusted to obtain single events on beads and minimize the background in epifluorescence imaging.

Fluorescence Microscopy (on Gold NP)

An inverted TIRF microscope with a 100×/NA 1.49 objective (Nikon) was used for the DNA-PAINT experiment. Once the glass slide with GNS was mounted on the sample stage, buffer B was carefully removed, and then 50 μL of 200 pM of complementary imager strands (3′ TAT GTA GAT C-5′, from Integrated DNA Technologies) with ATTO655 diluted in buffer B was drop-casted with a pipet onto the slide. The sample was illuminated with a 637 nm laser (Coherent OBIS FP 1193837 | 637 nm LX 100 mW) with a power density of 3.98 kW/cm2, the intensity time traces were recorded with a CMOS camera (Prime BSI Scientific CMOS, physical pixel size 6.5 μm). The dichroic mirror (ZT640rdc-UF2, Chroma Technology) and several filters such as a notch filter (ZET635), a long-pass filter of 650 nm (Thorlabs), and a bandpass filter (center wavelength 700 nm, bandwidth of 75 nm, Thorlabs) are used in the detection path to get a high signal-to-noise ratio. Hundreds of PSFs were obtained from single GNSs from 18,000 frames recorded at 10 fps.

Scattering Spectroscopy and SEM

Two additional measurements were performed to confirm that the NPs analyzed in the DNA-PAINT experiment were single, spherical GNS with a size of around 100 nm. After the fluorescent measurements, the same setup was used to execute single-particle scattering spectroscopy by replacing the red laser with a supercontinuum white light source (SuperK COMPACT from NKT Photonics), and all the wavelength filters in the detection path were removed. The microscope was still aligned in TIRF, and a beam block was placed right below the objective to block the direct source light. The scattering signals were collected from 500 to 640 nm with a bandwidth of 10 nm for the same sample area as DNA-PAINT (Figure S16a,b). The optical properties of plasmonic particles are dependent on the shape and size of the NP. Thus, the resonance peak and line width obtained from the single particle scattering spectroscopy by fitting a Lorentzian curve gives an insight into whether the spectrum is collected from a single particle or a cluster of particles. Particles 1 and 2 are highlighted in the images (Figure S16a–c) to show the correlation. The single particle spectra from GNSs 1 and 2 are shown in Figure S16e and h, respectively. The single-particle spectra also depend on the environment of the particle, so deducing the size of the nanosphere is complex. Many bright spots were observed in the fluorescent measurements (Figure S16a), and the particles that we chose for further PSF analysis were dimmer, mainly because the filter in the detection path was filtering out the PL signal from a single GNS. The brighter spots were thought to be clusters as the absorption cross-section scales with the size of the particle.

To be certain, SEM imaging was performed on the sample and the particles of interest were imaged at a higher magnification (Figure S16c,f,i). For correlative SEM imaging, the glass slides were marked with an SEM pen marker (Agar Scientific) before functionalizing the GNS with docking strands. After the DNA-PAINT and scattering measurements at the optical microscope, the sample slide was dried in air and treated with ozone for 10 min to get rid of organic layers from the particle surface. The glass slides were then sputtered with a thin (approximately 8 nm) layer of gold to make the substrate conduct for SEM imaging. The imaging was performed with a 10 kV electron beam at 250k magnification in SE mode (SEM Quanta 3D FEG). The SEM images of the same field of view as the optical microscope show that the brighter spots in the fluorescence measurements were indeed from dimers, trimers, or clusters of nanospheres while the dimmer spots were single GNS of 100 nm on average (Figure S16a,c). The particles that have been used for further analysis and fitting were confirmed as single nanospheres from scattering measurements and SEM images.

Fluorescence Microscopy (on Polystyrene NP)

Imaging is performed at 150× (Nikon 100×/1.49) on a motorized epifluorescence microscope (Nikon Ti2-E) equipped with an sCMOS camera (Teledyne Prime 95B, 11 μm pixels, resulting in an effective pixel size of 73 nm) and a home-built laser illumination. Bright-field images are obtained in transmission mode. Fluorescence images are recorded with the 638 nm laser (Cobolt 06-MLD, 180 mW) illumination in epi with a Quad band filter cube (AHF, F74-866, F73-478_T3, F72-866). The well is filled with 100 μL of imaging solution and covered with a glass lid with humidified paper to minimize evaporation. The focal plane is set manually to the glass surface using unspecific blinking spots on the glass surface. The focus position is maintained using the perfect focus system of the microscope to which an offset is applied to set the focus to the center of the NP. Each FOV is first bleached for 1 min at maximum laser intensity. The laser power is then set to 30 mW (measured on the back focal plane of the objective) and the measurement entails 20,000 frames with an exposure time of 100 ms.

Data Processing and PSF Fitting (Gold NP)

The data processing from a 3D (x–y-time) stack of raw microscopy data to PSFs is implemented in Python and performed as follows (Figure S12). Spots of interest in the FOV are found by employing an algorithm to find local maxima (github.com/dwaithe/MaximaFinder) to the time-averaged stack of data, and maxima near the edge are removed. Intensity traces are generated by averaging the 10 × 10 pixel values around the found maximum in every time frame. Manually inspected spots (stable intensity background, many events, single spot in 10 × 10 neighborhood) are selected. For every selected spot, the events are found by detecting the time frames where the intensity is 100 values (58 photons) above the median of the entire trace. Consecutive time frames above the threshold are grouped, and every group is considered an event. Per considered event, the 10 × 10 images are averaged over time, creating the raw PSFs. The one-photon PL signal and camera BG noise are averaged over 10 consecutive frames before or after each event (from now on referred to as the PL+BG image), and subtracted from the raw PSF, resulting in the final experimental fluorescence PSF, which will be used for PSF fitting.

We fit all the PSFs obtained from a single GNS with our analytical PSF model, to localize the binding events on the GNS surface. The PSF model has several microscope-, NP-, and fluorophore-specific parameters that we do not have to fit since they are known, like the magnification, pixel size, NA, NP radius, NP refractive index, and focus level (see Table S1 for the full list). Assuming the wrong NP radius has a minor effect on the obtained localizations (Figure S26), we assume the distance between the fluorophore and the NP surface to be 5 nm in the PSF fitting. The physical distance between the fluorophore and NP surface is maximally 21 nt (∼7 nm) but is most likely shorter due to the bending of the ssDNA. We show that varying the distance in this range has a negligible effect on the PSF shape (Figure S22). The six free parameters we are left with are the position of the emitter on the GNS (expressed in spherical coordinates θ and φ), the 2D position of the GNS in the field of view (x, y), the number of photons N, and the background intensity b. The position of the GNS in the image (x, y) is determined by performing a Gaussian fit using MLE32 to the PL+BG image (Figure S12), which means we only have four free parameters left to fit with our analytical PSF (φ, θ, N, and b). We find these four parameters by a multistep least-squares fitting approach of our analytical PSF model to the experimental PSFs.

In order to obtain a good starting guess for the fit, in the first step, we fit the PSF model of a fixed dipole emitter without the presence of an NP.32 This is done for two reasons: (1) this fit is significantly faster and therefore an easy way to get a good initial guess, and (2) the PSF for an emitter on a gold NP resembles the PSF for an isolated fixed emitter quite well, since the strong plasmonic coupling between the emitter and the NP basically makes the collective emission resemble a fixed dipole emitter (Figure S8). This first fit is given the freedom to fit all six parameters (φ, θ, x, y, N, and b). The fit is initiated with the starting guess of (0, π/4, 0, 0, N̂, and b̂), where the center of the image has coordinates (0,0) and N̂ and b̂ represent estimated values from the PSF. The background b̂ is estimated as the average intensity on the border of the image of the PSF, and the number of photons N̂ is estimated as the sum intensity of the image minus the background estimate b̂. In the second step, we fit our analytical PSF model where we only fit the four necessary parameters (φ, θ, N, and b). Fitting x and y is not necessary, since they are known from the Gaussian fit to the PL+BG. The fit is initialized with the parameters found by the first fit of Mortensen et al.32 Fitting our full analytical PSF model in the second step is computationally feasible, since the initial parameters are already estimated approximately in the first step. Due to the strong plasmonic coupling, it is sufficient (for emitters close to the metal NPs, see Figure S22) to reduce the analytical PSF model to only the dipole orientation that is perpendicular to the NP surface (Figure S8), even though the fluorophore is freely rotating (Figure S11) and the PSF should theoretically be averaged over three orthogonal dipole orientations (Figure S24 and Note S3).

Given the ambiguity problem (see main text and Figure S15), multiple emitter locations on the NP surface result in a PSF with the same shape. More precisely, the two locations on opposite sides of the NP (e.g., north vs south, or left vs right) result in PSFs with a near-identical shape. Although these two PSFs are similar in shape, they have different positions in the image, allowing the fit to differentiate between the two ambiguous emitter locations by knowing the position of the NP from the PL. However, the fit may be biased toward finding the emitter location closest to the initial guess and thus not finding it on the other side of the NP. As a solution, we initialize the fit four times with four opposite initial guesses, to force the fit to sample the four ambiguous emitter locations on all sides of the NP. All initializations result in a set of four parameters, of which we choose the optimal one by evaluating the mean-squared error between the fitted and the experimental PSF. All fitting is performed using nonlinear least-squares (scipy.optimize.curve_fit in Python), since the PSFs contain a sufficient amount of photons32 (Figure S13).

Aberration Correction

For the gold NP data, we correct the analytical PSF model for field-dependent aberrations before fitting to experimental data. We quantify the spherical aberrations over the FOV by looking at unspecific binding events away from the particles (Figure S27). We first crop an area of approximately 5 × 7 μm around the NP of interest. We detect all spots in all frames using the ThunderStorm89 plugin in Fiji and remove the localizations that are closer than 150 nm to an NP. We fit a 2D Gaussian function to each spot using the maximum-likelihood fitting approach of Mortensen et al.(32) For the example given in Figure S27, we obtain σ values (widths of the fitted Gaussians) of around 138 nm, which is bigger than the 118 nm expected from theory (Figure S27c). The σ values vary slowly over the FOV (Figure S27b). We assume that the optical aberrations can be approximated by Gaussian blurring. We calculate that a convolution of the theoretical PSF with a Gaussian kernel with a σ of 73.5 nm gives the experimentally obtained PSF width. For each analyzed NP, we obtain the width of the Gaussian blurring kernel individually since the severity of the aberrations depends on where the NP is located in the FOV. In the PSF fit, we correct for these spherical aberrations by blurring the analytical PSF model with the obtained Gaussian kernel. Correcting the PSF model with the wrong aberrations gives a bias in the obtained localizations (Figure S26). We investigate whether a multivariate Gaussian is a better model for the aberrations, since it allows for elliptical aberrations (Figure S28). However, we find that the ellipticities of the unspecific binding events are negligible and that fitting with a PSF model that is corrected for elliptical aberrations does not significantly change the obtained emitter locations.

Data Processing and PSF Fitting (Polystyrene NP)

For each detected localization, a 26 × 26 pixel cropped image of the fluorescence image, averaged over all frames of the events (the experimental PSF), is fitted with the analytical PSF model. The fitting is performed in the same way as for the gold NPs, with four exceptions. First, the position of the NP is found by localizing all frames using the Picasso90 software. The position of the NP is extracted as the drift-corrected median position of all localizations near the NP of interest. The drift is identified by tracking unspecific clusters of imager strands on the glass surface. In the fit, we use the interpolated position of the NP at the time of the event. Second, in contrast to fitting the PSFs on the gold NP, here we fit the full rotationally free fluorophore model (Figure S8). Third, to account for ambiguity, only two initializations are needed, since for the polystyrene NP the top-down symmetry in the PSF shape is absent. The fourth difference between fitting the PSFs from polystyrene NPs and the gold NPs is the sequence of fitting steps. For the gold NPs, the fitting is performed in three steps to obtain a good starting guess, starting with the PSF model for a fixed dipole emitter. The PSFs for polystyrene NPs do not resemble a fixed dipole emitter, so we directly fit the analytical PSF model for an emitter on an NP, where we use as guess for the azimuthal position the angle between the brightest pixel in the image and the center of the NP. The NP radius and focus position are obtained in an iterative process. In the first iteration, analysis of a subset of localizations revealed artifacts in the reconstruction due to wrongly assumed NP radius and the focus position. In the second iteration, the radius of the NP was adjusted to 508 nm and the focus position to 640 nm above the glass, after which all localizations were fitted with these values. Localizations corresponding to frames with more than one emitter on the NP are detected and discarded.

Localization Uncertainty

The localization uncertainty as presented in Figures 4d and 5e of the main text is defined as the uncertainty on the NP surface. The least-squares fitting procedure provides us with the uncertainty (standard deviation) on each of the estimated parameters. The uncertainty on the emitter position on the NP surface (expressed in spherical coordinates θ and φ) is called angular uncertainties, which we convert to positional uncertainties by multiplying with the radius of the NP. The overall uncertainty on the position is calculated as . For particles in the same size range, the angular uncertainty is relatively constant (Figure S19d); therefore, the positional uncertainty scales with the radius. For other particle sizes or materials, the dependency of the theoretical localization uncertainty with respect to any system parameter can be easily calculated using an example script in our online code repository. For the 100 nm gold NPs, we obtain a localization precision of 4 ± 1 nm (Figure 4d) and for the 1 μm polystyrene NPs 15 ± 10 nm (Figure 5e). For example, a reported localization uncertainty of 4 nm means that the found localization could be off by a distance of 4 nm moved over the spherical surface. The localization precision is consistent with fitting the PSF model to individual frames of a single binding event (Figure S18). Note that since the experimental PSFs are obtained by averaging multiple frames belonging to the same binding event, the localization uncertainty is in principle dependent on the length of the event, since averaging will improve the signal to noise. However, in practice, the signal-to-noise ratio of single frames is already high enough for single-frame fitting. In Figure S19, we show the theoretical Cramér–Rao lower bounds (CRLB) of the localization uncertainty in the polar and azimuthal directions, as a function of the number of photons and NP size. The CRLB shows qualitatively the same trend as the uncertainties obtained by PSF fitting. The experimental uncertainties are higher than the CRLB. This is explained by the additional uncertainty in the NP position, which propagates into the positional localization uncertainty of the emitters on the NP surface.
