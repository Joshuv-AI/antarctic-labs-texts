Zinc-polydopamine nanozyme promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and alleviates inflammation and muscle atrophy during the perioperative period of surgery
Authors: Haoran Guo, Ruixing Wan, Qingqing Qin, Wenqiang Liang, Huaibin Zhang, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCisplatin is a commonly used chemotherapeutic agent for the treatment of diverse malignancies; however, its clinical use often results in skeletal muscle atrophy (SMA). Excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and persistent unsettled inflammation are significant contributors to cisplatin (CPT)-induced skeletal muscle atrophy (CiSMA). Nanoparticles capable of scavenging ROS and alleviating inflammation may effectively address CiSMA. We developed a straightforward, fast one-step method for fabricating tailored zinc-polydopamine (Zn-PD) nanozymes and confirmed their promising therapeutic agents for CiSMA. Zn-PD, which exhibits diverse enzyme-mimicking capabilities, Zn-PD effectively inhibits ROS-triggered myotube apoptosis, rectifies mitochondrial dysfunction, and enhances mitochondrial biogenesis, demonstrating significant anti-inflammatory effects by obstructing the M1 macrophage infiltration into the muscle milieu, thereby mitigating CiSMA in mice. Collectively, this study proposes a treatment approach for CiSMA and underscores the potential of Zn-PD-based therapies for treating muscle atrophy during the perioperative period of surgery.

Introduction

Chemotherapy is widely used in oncological treatment, involving the systematic administration of antineoplastic agents according to prescribed protocols [1]. Among the most widely used broad-spectrum anticancer agents are those that act by damaging deoxyribonucleic acid. This category encompasses a group of platinum-based compounds, including cisplatin, carboplatin, and oxaliplatin, each varying in effectiveness and toxicity. Cis-diammine-dichloroplatinum (II), also known as cisplatin or CPT, is the most widely used medication in this category and serves as the standard treatment for various human malignancies [2–4]. This medicine is notably effective against cancer, having considerable clinical relevance for individuals diagnosed with head and neck, lung, bladder, ovarian, and testicular malignancies [5]. CPT activation occurs intracellularly by aquating one of its two chloride leaving groups, resulting in covalent attachment to DNA. Although successful, CPT-based chemotherapy is associated with significant adverse effects, including neurotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, and muscular atrophy [6]. Cancer patients receiving CPT treatment may encounter substantial weight loss, predominantly because of muscular atrophy. Cachexia, a metabolic milieu marked by the reduction of skeletal muscle mass, body fat, and inflammation, is responsible for almost 20% of all cancer-related fatalities [7]. Some cancer therapies, including chemotherapy, may facilitate the onset of cachexia. This syndrome is a critical prognostic indicator for numerous cancer patients, and maintaining body weight and preventing muscle wasting are essential for increasing survival prospects and enhancing overall quality of life [8]. Therefore, it is imperative to design more efficient and safer chemotherapeutic adjuvants or nutritional supplements to mitigate CiSMA. Comprehending the molecular processes implicated in CiSMA is crucial for pinpointing very promising treatment targets [9]. The underlying underpinnings of CiSMA identify novel treatment options to alleviate muscle wasting and enhance outcomes for cancer patients.

To date, many nanomaterials have been engineered to replicate the catalytic activity of natural enzymes, including metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), metals, metal oxides, and carbon. The superoxide anion (O2 − ) is a principal ROS [10]. Superoxide dismutase converts it to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which can subsequently be turned into the reactive hydroxyl radical (OH) by the metal-mediated Fenton reaction [11]. Furthermore, O2− interacts with nitrogen oxide (NO) to generate peroxynitrite (ONOO − ), a reactive nitrogen species (RNS). Excessive formation of ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) in muscles induces oxidative stress, resulting in the impairment of cellular constituents, including DNA, proteins, and lipids, thus exacerbating cellular and tissue damage [12]. Muscle contractions in the diaphragm and hind limbs of mice elevated ROS levels; thus, ROS are clearly produced during exercise. Muscle contraction requires substantial ATP, predominantly produced by mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), leading muscle mitochondria to consume oxygen at a rate 100 times greater during intensive exercise than at rest [13]. Nanozymes exhibiting antioxidant-like activity are crucial in protecting cells from the detrimental effects of ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which are essential for the management of numerous disorders [14]. In addition to exhibiting antioxidant action, nanozymes with prooxidant properties that generate ROS possess therapeutic potential due to their ability to induce oxidative damage, effectively eradicating bacteria or cancer cells [15]. A basal level of ROS can act as a second messenger, essential for fundamental biological processes such as cellular proliferation and differentiation. Conversely, excessive ROS induces oxidative stress, which is associated with numerous diseases, including cancer, neurological disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and inflammation [16]. Therefore, mitigating ROS with antioxidant interventions or enhancing ROS generation via endogenous prooxidants is essential for maintaining normal physiological function [17]. Conventional strategies for ROS management primarily rely on redox enzymes or small-molecule pharmaceuticals; however, these approaches often do not fully meet clinical needs [18]. Conversely, nanozymes offer a unique advantage in ROS owing to their elevated, sustained catalytic activity, which stems from their stable architecture and multiple catalytic sites [19].

The human body contains ≈2.5 grams of zinc (Zn), with the majority residing in the musculoskeletal system. Zn is a component of more than 200 different enzymes and, as an essential trace element, serves as a second messenger in immune cells, playing crucial roles in various biological processes, including antioxidant stress, DNA repair, cell division, and differentiation [20–22]. In addition, Zn is a key structural element of zinc finger proteins. Zhou et al. proposed that zinc facilitates myoblast proliferation, differentiation, and myofiber maturation [23]. Consequently, it is rational to speculate that nanozyme may ameliorate chemotherapy-induced muscle atrophy [24]. In the study, we developed Zinc-based nanozymes (Zn-PD) with benign cytocompatibility and superior enzyme activity, enabling relief of inflammation and concurrent ROS scavenging (Scheme 1). Hence, Zn-PD may suppress oxidative stress-induced apoptosis in skeletal muscle cells, enhance mitochondrial function, and modify the local pro-inflammatory milieu, thus mitigating SMA caused by CPT. These outcomes not only illustrated the potential of nanozymes with ROS-regulating capabilities for CiSMA during the perioperative period of surgery.

Experimental section

Preparation of Zn-PD nanozymes

Zinc-based nanozymes (Zn-PD) were prepared via a simple self-assembly technique [25–27]. PD (1 mg/mL) solution, developed by immersing PD hydrochloride (5 mg) in 5 mL of DMSO solution. ZnCl2 (18 mg) and PVP (50 mg) in 10 mL of DMSO solution. Following the dissolution of the Zn and PD solutions via ultrasonication, 1 M NaOH (80 μL) solution was introduced. After 5 h, the reaction products were collected by ultracentrifugation (5000 rpm for 5 min) and subsequently rinsed twice with UPW. The nanoparticles were subsequently rinsed with an ethanolic solution and collected via ultracentrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The nanoparticles were further lyophilized to provide the Zn-PD. Zn-PD was solubilized in UPW to a suitable concentration before use.

Characterization of Zn-PD of Zn-PD

The particle diameter and zeta potential of Zn-PD were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano-Zs (Malvern Instruments, UK). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images and Zn-PD mapping were obtained using a Talos F200x TEM (FEI Ltd., USA) operated at 200 kV. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were performed on a Bruker EPR EMX Plus spectrometer (Bruker Beijing Science and Technology Ltd, USA) at 9.8 GHz (microwave power: 1 mW). The stability of Zn-PD in vivo was evaluated by measuring size changes in a FBS over five days. The specimens were preserved at 4 °C, and DLS measurements were conducted 5 days later.

Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy of Zn-PD

DMPO was used to detect •O2–. 10 μL of Zn-PD solutions (10 mg/mL), 10 μL of DMPO, and 10 μL of 10 M H2O2 were added to 500 μL of DMSO. EPR measurements were carried out via the Bruker EPR EMX Plus (Bruker Beijing Science and Technology Ltd, USA) at a frequency of 9.8 GHz (microwave power: 1 mW).

Hemolysis assay of Zn-PD

10 mL of fresh blood; centrifuge at 3000 rpm for 5 min; remove the supernatant; and wash the precipitated red blood cells at least 3 times with PBS, as described above. The red blood cells were prepared into a 2% suspension in PBS. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of Zn-PD solutions of different concentrations were mixed with 0.5 mL of attenuated red blood cell suspension. The positive and negative controls were mixed with 0.5 mL of deionized water or PBS and 0.5 mL of the attenuated red blood cell suspension. All mixtures were incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 2.5 h and then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Aspirate the supernatant into a 96-well plate. Use a microplate reader to measure the sample’s OD at 540 nm. The calculation formula for the hemolysis rate:

Hemolysis rate (%) = [(ODsample − ODnegative)/(ODpositive − ODnegative)] × 100%

ODsample, ODpositive, and ODnegative represent the absorption values of the sample, positive control, and negative control, respectively [20].

In cell culture and cell viability assay by Zn-PD

The mouse macrophage RAW264.7 cells and mouse myoblast L6 cells were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) in Shanghai, China. The cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS. Cells were cultured in DMEM containing 80 U/mL penicillin, 80 μg/mL streptomycin, and 10% FBS. The cells were incubated in a moist atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Cell viability of Zn-PD was quantitatively determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. The C2 and RAW264.7 cell lines were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well in 100 μL of DMEM or RPMI-1640, which contained 10% FBS 1% antibiotics (penicillin/streptomycin) under 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After the cells grew for 12 h, the medium was replaced with a new medium (100 μL/well) containing Zn-PD at different concentrations. Then, the C2 cells were co-cultured with H2O2 for 12 h. The cells were simultaneously replaced with a 100 μL culture medium containing 10% CCK-8. After incubation for 1 h, the absorbance at 450 nm in each well was determined using a microplate reader. Relative cell viability was calculated to quantify cytotoxicity [24].

Finding of ROS in C2 cells and skeletal muscles by Zn-PD

This experiment was conducted on C2 cells. Cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 40,000 cells per well. After overnight incubation, treatments were initiated. To assess residual intracellular ROS levels, cells were incubated with 10 µM 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; HY-D0940, MCE, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol [28]. Briefly, treatments consisted of sequential administration of different concentrations of Zn-PD and 150 µM H2O2. After a 12-h incubation, the medium was replaced with serum-free medium, and the DCFH-DA working solution was applied for 30 min to load the fluorescent probe. The cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and intracellular ROS levels were quantified by measuring fluorescence intensity using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX73, Olympus, Japan).

Gene expression by Zn-PD

Total RNA from gastrocnemius muscles or RAW264.7 cells was extracted using Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme), and cDNAs were reverse-transcribed using HiScript II Q Select RT SuperMix (Vazyme) or the miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (by stem-loop) (Vazyme) for miRNA following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) of the mRNAs and miRNAs in this study was performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR PCR Kit (Vazyme, #Q711) on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) by Zn-PD

Freshly harvested muscle tissues were homogenized and analyzed using ELISA kits (AiFang Biological) according to the manufacturer’s protocols: CXCL10, iNOS, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α. Briefly, standards and samples were loaded in triplicate onto pre-coated plates alongside blank controls, followed by 37 °C incubation (30 min), repeated incubation/washing, 10-min chromogenic reaction (37 °C protected from light), and termination. Optical density was measured at 450 nm within 15 min post-termination using a microplate reader, with blank-adjusted values used for calculations [29].

Animal care and experimental protocol

The skeletal muscle acceleration effects of Zn-PD were evaluated using the mice tibialis anterior muscle defect according to previous studies [30]. The experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of the First Hospital, Lanzhou University, Lanzhou (approval number: LDYYLL2025-484), and all institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals were followed. All surgery procedures were performed under aseptic conditions. Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility with a 12-h light/dark cycle and relative humidity of 40–70%. At 8 weeks of age, the skin on the lateral right hind limb was depilated (1.5 cm diameter). Next, the rats were anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of chloral hydrate (0.3 g/kg body weight). An anterolateral skin incision was made to expose the tibialis anterior muscle (n = 6). The mice were separated to receive i.p. injections of PBS, CPT, CPT + Zn-PD, or CPT + Celecoxib (CEL). For the CPT group, CPT (3 mg/kg) was injected day-to-day on days 0–3. To distinguish the therapeutic effects of Zn-PD or CEL on CiSMA, the mice were administered CPT, then CEL (5 mg/kg) or Zn-PD (10 mg/kg). All injured skeletal muscle tissues were collected and stored at −80 °C before analysis. On day 7, the grip strength and the rotarod tests were assessed.

Haematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

The gastrocnemius muscle and organs (Heart, Liver, Kidney, Spleen, and Lung) were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, then processed through paraffin embedding, sectioning at 4–5 μm thickness, deparaffinization with xylene, standard H&E staining, graded ethanol dehydration, and finally whole-slide digital scanning using a high-resolution slide scanner (KF-FL-020, KFBIO) to obtain H&E-stained tissue sections. For Tunnel immunofluorescence, deparaffinized gastrocnemius muscle sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, then incubated with Myf5 and MyoD1 reaction mixture (C1088, Beyotime) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark, counterstained with DAPI.

Analysis of oxidative stress markers

Freshly dissected gastrocnemius muscle tissue was homogenized in ice-cold PBS. GPx, SOD, CAT, and MDA assay kits were used to investigate oxidative stress levels of GPx, SOD, CAT, and MDA [31].

In vivo biosafety assessment

The blood was drawn from the C57BL/6 mice. PBS (negative control) or Zn-PD (20 mg/kg) was i.p. administered as an injection (for acute toxicity) or every day for 12 injections (for chronic toxicity). Key serum biochemical indicators of liver and kidney function, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA), were measured using an automatic hematology analyzer (Hitachi, Model HA3100) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical examination

The experimental data were expressed as mean ± SD. A one-way ANOVA with a Tukey post hoc test or a two-tailed Student’s t test was used to analyze experimental data across multiple groups. A two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t test was used to analyze statistical differences between two groups. Significant differences are indicated by asterisks (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001). All statistical results of the experiments were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterizations of Zn-PD

Zn-PD were developed via a simple self-assembly technique. Zn-PD, secured by PVP, was synthesized by combining ZnCl2, PVP, and PD in DMSO, followed by the addition of a NaOH solution to facilitate the reaction. The size of Zn-PD was 149.85 ± 6.78 nm, as measured by DLS (Fig. 1A). The surface charge of Zn-PD was approximately –21.87 ± 1.6 mV. The structure of Zn-PD was studied by TEM, revealing a near-spherical morphology (Fig. 1B). Elemental mapping was conducted to ascertain the Zn-PD composition. Uniform distributions of Zinc components throughout the structure of Zn-PD were detected, signifying the successful integration of Zn2+ ions (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, to assess the stability of Zn-PD, we used an FBS solution to simulate the blood circulation milieu [32]. The DLS findings indicated that the diameter of Zn-PD did not change significantly over 5 days under a blood-mimicking milieu, suggesting that Zn-PD may exhibit remarkable stability in vivo.

Considering the pivotal role of ROS in the pathogenic mechanism of CiSMA, we subsequently assessed the ROS-scavenging capacity of Zn-PD against hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide (O2•-), and hydroxyl radicals (•OH) [33]. Zn-PD exhibited significant SOD-like activity of 25 μg/mL (Fig. 1C), facilitating substantial ROS scavenging. The findings indicated that Zn-PD exhibited strong ABTS scavenging ability (Fig. 1D). The radical scavenging capacity of Zn-PD was further assessed using the DPPH• free radical scavenging assay. As illustrated in Fig. 1H, Zn-PD significantly neutralized DPPH• free radicals, demonstrating their potential antioxidative properties. The findings indicated that the scavenging rate of H2O2 increased markedly with higher Zn-PD concentrations, surpassing 85% at 200 μg/mL (Fig. 1E). The scavenging efficacy of O2•- and •OH exhibited comparable outcomes (Fig. 1F-1G). The overall antioxidant activity of Zn-PD was evaluated utilizing an ABTS test. The ability to scavenge •OH, O2•-, and DPPH• was further confirmed using ESR (Fig. 1I, K). The results collectively suggest that Zn-PD may serve as a powerful antioxidant to eliminate excess free radicals, underscoring its potential to address oxidative stress-related milieu, including CiSMA.

In vitro ROS and anti-inflammatory properties of Zn-PD

We employed H2O2-subjected to C2 cells to assess the ROS-scavenging efficacy of Zn-PD. Initially, we assessed the cytocompatibility and cytotoxicity of Zn-PD. The hemolytic assay was completed by adding several doses of Zn-PD to an erythrocyte and incubating for 3 h at 37 °C. The findings indicated that Zn-PD did not cause a substantial increase in the hemolytic ratio until 800 μg/mL (Fig. S1). We assessed the ROS-scavenging capacity of Zn-PD in H2O2-subjected C2 cells. The CLSM images indicated that Zn-PD pretreatment markedly reduced ROS generation in H2O2-treated C2 cells (Fig. 2A), and the inhibitory effect was concentration-dependent (Fig. 2B). The CCK8 assay showed that Zn-PD did not elicit toxicity in RAW264.7 and C2 cells at 400 μg/mL (Fig. 2C, D). The results demonstrated the favorable biocompatibility of Zn-PD for in vivo therapeutic effects. Moreover, H2O2-induced C2 cells pretreated with Zn-PD exhibited enhanced cell viability, indicating that Zn-PD may mitigate oxidative stress-induced cellular damage, with the protective effect intensifying at higher Zn-PD doses (Fig. 2E). The results indicated that Zn-PD could efficiently eliminate ROS and safeguard C2 myoblasts from cytotoxicity generated by oxidative stress.

Excessive inflammatory activation is considered a significant factor in CPT-triggered cachectic muscle loss. Consequently, the anti-inflammatory efficacy of Zn-PD was later assessed in LPS-subjected RAW264.7 cells. The qRT-PCR findings indicated that Zn-PD significantly inhibited the pro-inflammatory markers mRNA expression in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 2F–J). The findings indicated that the inflammatory cytokine concentrations in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells were significantly increased. The LPS-induced elevation of these cytokines could be markedly reduced by Zn-PD (Fig. 2K–O). Collectively, our outcomes indicate that Zn-PD possesses remarkable ROS-scavenging potential and exceptional anti-inflammatory activity.

Zn-PD increased in vitro myogenic differentiation and myoblast fusion

Subsequently, we assessed the impact of Zn-PD on myoblast fusion and myogenic differentiation. Typical and ordered myotubes were observed in C2 myoblasts treated with Zn-PD. Conversely, control C2 myoblasts cultured in PBS showed a marked decrease in the number of intact, ordered myotubes. The size of myotubes in the Zn-PD was 1.68 times greater than in the control (Fig. S2A). The myotube marker MyH was identified by immunofluorescence analysis. The findings indicated that Zn-PD markedly increased the MFI of MyH in C2 myoblasts. The fusion index and myogenic index in C2 myoblasts subjected to Zn-PD were 2.06-fold and 1.75-fold higher than in the control (Fig. S2B). Collectively, our findings indicated that Zn-PD therapy enhanced C2 myoblast fusion and myogenic differentiation.

In vivo biosafety assessment

A biosafety assessment was initially conducted on healthy mice to examine the potential for Zn-PD. The acute toxicity was evaluated 24 h after a single intraperitoneal injection of Zn-PD at a dosage of 20 mg/kg [34]. The alteration in body weight showed minimal differences between the control and Zn-PD treatment groups. A homological chemistry analysis was performed to elucidate the in vivo toxicity. No substantial change was observed in the examination of liver markers (AST and ALT), cardiac function markers (CK and ALP), and renal function markers (UREA) (Fig. 3A). The chronic toxicity was assessed 48 h following 12 intraperitoneal injections of Zn-PD (20 mg/kg). Figure 3B shows that alterations in body weight and blood chemistry were comparable to those observed in acute toxicity evaluation. Additionally, H&E staining was performed on primary organs. Compared with the control, Zn-PD administration resulted in negligible differences, indicating minimal impact on organ integrity (Fig. 3C). The results demonstrated that Zn-PD exhibited superior biosafety.

Zn-PD improved in vivo CPT-induced cachexia

Subsequently, we developed a mouse model of CiSMA by administering intraperitoneal CPT injections. To investigate the impact of Zn-PD on CPT-induced cachexia, C57BL/6 mice were administered CPT and Zn-PD, or CEL, via intraperitoneal injection (Fig. 4A). CEL is a selective cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitor utilized as a conventional non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medication. Prior research has indicated that CEL may mitigate muscle degradation in denervation- or disuse-induced diabetic sarcopenia and SMA. Consequently, CEL was utilized as the positive control. Our analysis revealed no substantial differences in food intake across treatments (Fig. 4B), so we excluded the catabolic effects of Zn-PD. The gross morphological images indicated that Zn-PD, in conjunction with CPT, significantly mitigated chemotherapy-induced reductions in epididymal fat mass and hind limb muscle (Fig. 4C), a finding corroborated by isolated tibialis anterior and gastrocnemius muscles and hind limbs (Fig. 4D, E). The quantitative analysis corroborated the visual findings, revealing that Zn-PD mitigated the CPT-induced decreases in the weights of the tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius, and epididymal fat (Fig. 4F–I). Subsequently, we investigated the impact of Zn-PD on muscular performance and physical functionality. As illustrated in Fig. S3A–S3C, mice administered CPT + Zn-PD exhibited markedly greater grip strength than those treated solely with CPT. The rotarod test findings indicated that Zn-PD could markedly prolong the duration of mouse locomotion [9]. These results were likely due to an effect facilitated by SMA preservation. Unlike Zn-PD, CEL did not alleviate CiSMA, contradicting its previously reported benefits in other SMA models. Variation in treatment outcomes may stem from differences in pathophysiological pathways across various SMA models. Conversely, the method of administration and the dosage provided may result in varying therapeutic outcomes. Zn-PD administered alongside CPT treatment markedly mitigated CPT-induced weight loss in the kidney, spleen, and heart; however, this combination did not prevent the CPT-induced weight loss in the lungs and liver (Fig. S3D–S3H). The data indicated that Zn-PD significantly mitigated CPT-induced cachexia in vivo.

In vivo Zn-PD ameliorated CiSMA

Zn-PD may alleviate CPT-induced muscle weight loss [35]. We evaluated muscle atrophy at the tissue and cellular levels. The protein expression and ultrastructure associated with myogenesis were examined. The H&E staining of the muscle fibers’ cross-sectional area (CSA). CPT significantly decreased muscle fiber cross-sectional area in gastrocnemius tissues; this effect may be mitigated by Zn-PD treatment (Fig. 5iA). Nonetheless, this impact may be significantly attenuated by Zn-PD (Fig. 5B), suggesting that Zn-PD could mitigate CPT-induced cellular damage in muscle tissue. Subsequently, we identified the expression of Myf5 and MyoD1, which serve as indicators of myogenesis [8]. The findings indicated that the reduced MFI of MyoD1 and Myf5, caused by CPT, was markedly augmented by Zn-PD (Fig. 5C–F). The data suggested that Zn-PD may enhance SMA by facilitating myogenesis during the perioperative period of surgery.

Mitochondria are crucial for sustaining skeletal muscle activity via ATP synthesis and ROS production. Mitochondrial biogenesis denotes the process by which cells increase mitochondrial number and mass, whereas mitochondrial dysfunction may arise from compromised mitochondrial biogenesis and diminished mitochondrial numbers due to ROS. Recent investigations indicated that chemotherapy agents, including doxorubicin and CPT, caused decreased mitochondrial biogenesis and malfunction, leading to SMA [36]. Consequently, to assess whether Zn-PD may mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction and augment mitochondrial biogenesis induced by CPT, we examined the ultrastructure of gastrocnemius mitochondria in Zn-PD using the JC-1 probe and TEM. The JC-1 probe determined the effect of Zn-PD on mitochondria in C2 cells. As shown in Fig. S4, Zn-PD decreased the red-to-green fluorescence ratio, indicating mitochondrial depolarization. No notable structural alterations in mitochondria were seen in the control. Nonetheless, a significant quantity of enlarged mitochondria was noted in the gastrocnemius of CPT. Conversely, Zn-PD mitigated the CPT-induced mitochondrial defects (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, quantitative measurements showed that Zn-PD significantly increased mitochondrial density per unit area compared to CPT, but Zn-PD markedly counteracted this effect. The ratio of impaired mitochondria caused by CPT may be significantly reduced by Zn-PD (Fig. 5H–L). The results revealed that Zn-PD could augment mitochondrial biogenesis and mitigate CPT-induced mitochondrial impairments.

Zn-PD showed in vivo antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties

An overabundance of ROS generated by chemotherapy might provoke the synthesis of inflammatory mediators, leading to a disruption of catabolic and anabolic processes and ultimately resulting in SMA [3]. We have shown that Zn-PD can scavenge ROS, suppress inflammation, and prevent CiSMA. ROS induces lipid peroxidation, resulting in the formation of malondialdehyde (MDA). Consequently, MDA may function as a marker for oxidative stress. We measured MDA levels in the gastrocnemius and found that CPT led to a significant increase, whereas Zn-PD treatment effectively reversed this trend (Fig. 6A–D). We also measured the activities of GPx, SOD, and CAT, the principal antioxidant enzymes that protect against ROS. The CPT treatment cohort showed markedly reduced activity of these GPx, SOD, and CAT. Nonetheless, Zn-PD fully restored enzyme activity compared with the CPT (Fig. 6A–D). The GPx, SOD, and CAT can efficiently remove ROS; we subsequently assessed ROS levels in the gastrocnemius muscle. The CPT exhibited the most intense ROS fluorescence signal, whereas Zn-PD therapy nearly eliminated ROS induced by CPT (Fig. 6E, F).

Subsequently, we assessed the concentrations of anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory markers in the gastrocnemius muscle. As anticipated, Zn-PD treatment led to a substantial elevation in the anti-inflammatory cytokines’ mRNA expression (such as IL-10 and TGF-β) (Fig. 6G, H), alongside a pronounced decrease in the pro-inflammatory markers’ mRNA expression (including IFN-γ, CXCL10, iNOS, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) that CPT upregulated in the gastrocnemius (Fig. 6I–N). Mice treated with CPT exhibited elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (including CXCL10, iNOS, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α) in the gastrocnemius, but Zn-PD therapy mitigated this increase (Fig. 7A–F). Figure 7G–J shows that CPT led to a notable increase in the percentage of iNOS+ (M1 macrophages) and a reduction in CD206+ (M2 macrophages). Nonetheless, these effects were significantly diminished by Zn-PD. The results aligned with our studies and demonstrated that Zn-PD mitigated the local inflammatory milieu in skeletal muscles. Overall, the data suggest that the therapeutic efficiency of Zn-PD may stem from the removal of ROS and the reduction of the inflammatory milieu.

Discussions

Reduced muscular strength is a principal factor leading to ambulation challenges in the elderly, and there are yet no highly effective treatment interventions for age-related deterioration in muscle function. Our results revealed that the bulk of skeletal muscle in mice treated with Zn-PD was markedly reduced. Additionally, lipid accumulation, inflammation, oxidative stress, and diminished motor performance were noted in the skeletal muscle of aging mice. Research indicates that excessive inflammation exacerbates proteolysis, diminishes protein synthesis, hinders muscle regeneration, and ultimately results in skeletal muscle atrophy; disrupts metabolic processes, leading to ectopic lipid deposition that impacts the oxidative stress response in skeletal muscle.

Cachexia, a multifaceted disease prevalent among cancer patients, is defined by progressive and permanent loss of skeletal muscle, significantly diminishing quality of life and elevating mortality risk. Therefore, preserving skeletal muscle is crucial for improving clinical outcomes in these individuals. At present, a limited number of sanctioned medications exist for cachexia, and clinical management frequently depends on multimodal strategies including exercise, nutritional therapy, and appetite enhancers. Nevertheless, exercise is sometimes impractical for cancer patients owing to weariness and physical constraints, and certain appetite stimulants have been linked to significant side effects. While numerous pharmacological agents are being evaluated in clinical trials, only a limited number have shown significant results to date.

Inflammation is a significant contributor to skeletal muscle atrophy. Reducing inflammation can improve myogenic differentiation. Research has shown a substantial negative association between MyH synthesis rates and the circulating levels of several immune-activating markers, including IL-6 and TNF-α. MyH and MyoD are essential regulators of muscle differentiation efficacy. To determine whether the muscular phenotype induced by chemotherapy was associated with alterations in muscle-specific signaling pathways, we measured levels of several proteins known to regulate skeletal muscle growth. We found alterations in many mediators of muscle atrophy and mitochondrial biogenesis using TEM.

Challenges

While many studies have examined the effects of cisplatin and exercise therapies in animal models, these results may not fully apply to human physiology. Animal trials provide meticulous control over dosage, environment, and genetic background; yet they fail to fully replicate the intricacies of human responses, including interindividual variability, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors. For instance, certain protective mechanisms of exercise identified in rodents—such as increased mitochondrial biogenesis and antioxidative enzyme activity—may vary in amount or even direction in humans. Consequently, results from animal studies, such as histological recovery patterns or molecular biomarkers, must be interpreted with caution when extrapolated to potential human benefits.

Subsequent research should prioritize translational methodologies, encompassing rigorously controlled clinical trials, to corroborate these results and elucidate species-specific reactions. Identifying these translational constraints facilitates the delineation of further stages for future research. Despite significant advances in understanding the mechanisms of cisplatin-induced muscle atrophy and the preventive benefits of exercise, some gaps remain that require attention.

Conclusions

We have developed Zn-PD for the remediation of CiSMA. The Zn-PD, with superior enzymatic activity, can efficiently eliminate excess ROS, thereby mitigating oxidative stress-induced mitochondrial malfunction and enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis and cytotoxicity in muscle tissues. Zn-PD can diminish M1 macrophage infiltration while enhancing M2 macrophage infiltration, thereby inhibiting cytokine release and retreating the skeletal muscle inflammatory microenvironment. The enhanced ROS ability and remarkable anti-inflammatory effectiveness of Zn-PD contributed to the mitigation of CiSMA. Significantly, our study demonstrated that Zn-PD did not cause negative immunological or in vivo toxicity, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic. This discovery offers a promising treatment for CiSMA during the perioperative period of surgery and illuminates the advances of novel techniques to address oxidative stress-associated disorders.
