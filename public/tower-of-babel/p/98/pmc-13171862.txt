A Dataset of University Students' Stress and Anxiety Levels based on Questionnaires and Wearable Sensors
Authors: Enrique Garcia-Ceja, Joanna Alvarado-Uribe, Ponciano Jorge Escamilla-Ambrosio, Adriana Lara, Alma Mena-Martinez, Gina Gallegos-Garcia, Miguel Gonzalez-Mendoza, Raul Monroy, Gilberto Martinez Luna, Juan Manuel Fernández-Cárdenas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mental health issues such as stress and anxiety are highly prevalent among university students, often affecting their academic performance and overall well-being. Understanding these conditions through objective, real-world data is essential for developing effective monitoring and intervention strategies. We present a multimodal dataset that captures students’ daily stress and anxiety levels through self-reports and wearable sensor data. The dataset was collected during one academic semester (February-July 2025) from undergraduate volunteers at two Mexican universities. Participants provided daily ratings of stress and anxiety using a mobile application, while Fitbit Inspire 3 devices continuously recorded physiological and behavioral data including heart rate variability, sleep quality, oxygen saturation, stress score, physical activity, and step count. The dataset features over 80% questionnaire compliance and validated Fitbit measurements. This dataset addresses the scarcity of public, ecologically valid datasets on student mental health and enables reproducible research and analyses in affective computing, wearable sensing, and machine learning for stress and anxiety monitoring.

Background & Summary

University life is often described as a critical developmental period; yet, it is also a time when students are highly vulnerable to stress and anxiety1–3. Both undergraduate and graduate students face academic demands, financial pressures, social transitions, and uncertainties about their future careers, all of which can negatively affect their mental health. Studies have consistently reported elevated rates of psychological distress in this population, with stress and anxiety strongly linked to test performance, workload, and socioeconomic status4–7. For instance, cross-sectional surveys show that a significant proportion of undergraduates report moderate to severe levels of stress and anxiety, often exacerbated by a lack of coping mechanisms, poor physical activity, and increased screen time8. The consequences of untreated stress and anxiety go beyond immediate psychological discomfort; they significantly affect academic performance and student retention. Research shows that psychological distress is linked to lower grade point averages, reduced class attendance, and a higher likelihood of academic probation or dropout9. First-year students seem particularly vulnerable, with mental health issues during their freshman year predicting both current and future academic challenges10. These findings highlight that mental health challenges in university settings are not only a public health concern but also a significant barrier to educational achievement and career development.

Graduate students, while somewhat less frequently studied in large-scale reviews, also show substantial levels of stress and anxiety, often with distinct contributing factors. In medical and healthcare graduate programs, for example, the combination of clinical responsibilities, research obligations, future job market uncertainty, and extended study duration contributes to a high psychological load. A recent meta-analysis focusing on medical students during the COVID-19 pandemic reported a pooled anxiety prevalence of 45% and depression of 48% among these students11. These findings highlight that stress and anxiety are not isolated to undergraduate populations, but extend into graduate levels, particularly where academic stakes and performance pressure are high.

Traditional methods for assessing mental health in university populations often depend heavily on retrospective self-report questionnaires and periodic clinical screenings. However, these approaches have significant limitations. Self-report measures can be influenced by recall bias effects, and they only provide snapshots of mental states that may vary throughout the day12. Additionally, students in significant distress may be less likely to complete surveys or seek clinical evaluations, which can introduce selection bias into prevalence estimates. These methodological shortcomings emphasize the need for passive, continuous monitoring approaches that can capture real-world fluctuations in mental health states without relying solely on active self-reporting.

New wearable devices are increasingly leveraging advanced sensors and physiological data to detect mental health states passively and in real time. Common sensors include those measuring heart rate variability (HRV), electrodermal activity (EDA), accelerometers (for movement / physical activity), sleep tracking, and resting heart rate. Wearables are shrinking in size, increasing battery capacity, and improving sensor quality, which enables more continuous and ecologically valid monitoring of users in daily life13,14. For example, in a systematic scoping review of wearable biosensors with artificial intelligence (AI) for mental health monitoring15, the authors mentioned works that use devices collecting HRV, EDA, accelerometry, sleep, etc., to predict conditions like depression16 and anxiety17. Collectively, these trends underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions, such as stress management and monitoring programs, to mitigate the burden of stress and anxiety in higher education settings.

Despite the rapid growth of wearable technologies and machine learning applications for mental health, a major limitation is the scarcity of publicly available datasets focused on stress and anxiety, particularly in university contexts. Most existing studies rely on small, proprietary, or institution-specific datasets, often collected in controlled laboratory environments rather than real-world settings, which restricts the reproducibility and generalizability of findings. For example, This lack of large, longitudinal, and ecologically valid public datasets hinders the development and benchmarking of analyzes, models, and technologies, ultimately slowing progress in translating wearable sensing into scalable mental health applications.

To address those limitations, we are publishing a database of University Students’ Stress and Anxiety Levels based on Questionnaires and Wearable Sensors which we will refer to as the SSAQS dataset. The SSAQS dataset was collected from a cohort of university students, designed to provide both ecological validity and richer multimodal information. Unlike many existing public datasets on stress detection, which are often limited to short-term laboratory protocols or low-frequency self-reports, SSAQS provides semester-long longitudinal monitoring in a real-world academic context. The dataset combines self-reported daily measures of stress and anxiety with continuous physiological and behavioral data recorded from Fitbit Inspire 3 devices, including heart rate variability, physical activity, sleep patterns, oxygen saturation levels, and so on. By integrating subjective assessments with objective sensor data in a naturalistic university setting, this resource provides an opportunity to study fluctuations in stress and anxiety across academic contexts. Furthermore, although several datasets have been collected from university students18,19, the majority originate from institutions in the United States and are typically limited to a single university cohort. To the best of our knowledge, SSAQS represents the first publicly available dataset featuring participants from Latin America, and uniquely includes data collected from students enrolled at two distinct universities.

We anticipate that this dataset will serve as a valuable benchmark for researchers developing and validating new data analysis and machine learning models for mental health monitoring, while also encouraging more reproducible and generalizable approaches in the field.

Methods

The SSAQS dataset can be downloaded from the zenodo repository20 (10.5281/zenodo.18706837). The SSAQS dataset was collected as part of the government funded SMIEAE project: Intelligent Stress and Anxiety Monitoring System for Students. One of the main objectives of the project was to design and implement an innovative and secure stress and anxiety monitoring system for students using Internet of Things (IoT), artificial intelligence, and data science technologies that allow for the proactive detection and management of stress and anxiety in students. One of the first phases of the project was data collection. We invited students from two Universities in Mexico to participate in the study as volunteers during one semester (February-July 2025). The students were enrolled from a computer science program and a mathematics engineering program.

The students were invited to participate during the first weeks of the semester. We invited students from two undergraduate courses and one graduate course. For this initial phase, we include data only from the undergraduate students, as the data from the graduate group is still being processed.

All participants signed an informed consent form (approved by the ethics committee) for participation in the study and data sharing. Personal information about the participants was anonymized. Furthermore, to avoid re-identification, information about the universities’ names and course’ codes was anonymized as well. The Tecnologico de Monterrey University ethics committee approved the study protocol with the number P-EIC-202510-001 and all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

We collected data from daily self-reported stress levels, Fitbit devices, and smartphone sensors. Data from their smartphones’ inertial sensors was intended to be collected at the beginning and end of each class. The students used the Sensor Logger application21 for this purpose; however, we did not include this data because it was scarce. One of the reasons is that in some smartphone models, the background data collection process is disabled by the operating system to save battery; thus, there are many missing values. Another reason is that most of the students place their phones on their desks, and it was also common for them to forget to activate the application at the beginning of each class.

Participants

Between 10 and 15 volunteer students were selected for each class group based on the available number of Fitbit devices. If more volunteers were interested in participating, they were be chosen at random. The inclusion criterion was that participants had to be at least 18 years old, which is the minimum adulthood age in Mexico. After the random selection, there were two groups with 10 participants each and one group with 15 participants. The participants answered an initial questionnaire containing demographic information. Daily self-questionnaires

The students were asked to answer daily questions about their overall stress and anxiety levels throughout the day. Every day at a random time between 20:00 and 22:00, they received a questionnaire on their phones. Remainders were set to trigger at 30,60,90, and 120 minutes. If the questionnaire was not answered within 60 minutes after the last reminder, it was discarded. The m-Path application22 was used to manage and send the questionnaires. The stress and anxiety levels were recorded on a scale of 0-100. The two questions were the following: 1) What was your average stress level during the day? 2) What was your average anxiety level during the day? A scale of 0-100 was chosen to make it intuitive to the participants, 0 meaning no stress/anxiety and 100 meaning maximum stress/anxiety. The questionnaire was sent only once per day to minimize interruptions. Figure 2 shows a screenshot of the questionnaire (in Spanish). The answers are sent automatically to the server. The users do not need to enter any personal information in the application. They enroll in the questionnaire application by typing a unique private code provided to each participant, which only the principal investigator has access to, so each person’s answers can be associated with the rest of the data.Fig. 2First daily question example.

First daily question example.

Fitbit data

The students were provided with a Fitbit Inspire 3 device and instructed to wear it 24 hours a day. Before deciding on the final device, we conducted a benchmark of different wearable sport and watch products23. We evaluated different criteria, including battery life, sensors, price, accessibility to raw data, and compatibility with operating systems. The participants were asked once a month to download the raw data from their accounts and share it with the principal investigators. A script written in the R programming language was used to anonymize the data and extract the relevant sensors’ information. In the ‘Data Records’ section, we describe in detail all the extracted Fitbit’s data.

Data Records

The dataset is available at the zenodo repository20 (10.5281/zenodo.18706837).

The root directory of the SSAQS dataset consists of two .csv files (users-courses.csv, course-details.csv) and a subdirectory for each of the participants. The subdirectory name corresponds to the id of the participant. The specific data for each participant are contained in their corresponding subdirectory. Each subdirectory has seven .csv files (activity_level.csv, daily_questions.csv, hrv.csv, oxygen.csv, sleep.csv, steps.csv, stress.csv), except for the three users who did not provide Fitbit data. In those cases, only the daily_questions.csv is present. Every file (except users-courses.csv and course-details.csv) includes timestamps which can be used to synchronize the data across sensors. Since the granularity of each sensor varies, averages over fixed-length windows can be computed (depending on the use case). The daily_questions.csv file contains additional timestamps indicating the time at which the questionnaire was sent, answered, etc. which can be used for further analysis (see Table 4).

Technical Validation

To evaluate the technical quality of the data, we performed an exploratory data analysis (EDA) of both, questionnaires and Fitbit data, which are described in the following subsections, respectively.

Questionnaires

The m-Path application was designed so that it does not require an active internet connection. If the device is offline, the responses are saved locally and uploaded once an internet connection is detected. This guaranties that if the user answers the questionnaire, their responses will be stored on the server at some point. The data is encrypted in transit and at rest and is transferred over a secure connection. Personal information, such as name, email, location, etc., is not collected with the application. On average, the students answered 80.2% of the questionnaires. The average number of sent questionnaires per user was 89.5. All the reported levels of stress and anxiety were between the expected values of 0 and 100. The overall mean stress level was 33.2, and the mean reported anxiety level was 34.3. Fitbit

Since the students manually shared their data once a month, it was possible to have some duplicate records due to an overlap between the last records from the previous month and the first records of the current month. Thus, all monthly data from each user were concatenated, and duplicate rows were removed. Another aspect to take into account when validating the quality of the data is in the stress.csv files. The column CALCULATION_FAILED: indicates if the calculated stress value is valid or not. A value of TRUE indicates that the calculation has failed; thus, those rows should be discarded. Figure 6 shows histograms for all of Fitbit’s numeric data and a bar plot for the number of steps. Here, we can see that the oxygen saturation histogram has a high number of values equal to 50. This may be due to users removing the device and insufficient data to perform the computation. Thus, this should be taken into account when analyzing these data. For example, by removing registers with values outside the range 95-100 which is considered a normal one. On the other hand, the stress score histogram presents a high frequency of zero-valued registers. As previously mentioned, the calculation may have failed, as indicated by the CALCULATION_FAILED column. Limitations

One of the limitations of the data is that some of the measures registered by the Fitbit device such as sleep and stress scores are computed using proprietary algorithms, thus, limiting their detailed interpretability. For future work, we will explore the possibility of collecting raw data as well. Another consideration is that participants were recruited primarily from technical degree programs. For future work we will include participants from other programs as well. Another limitation is the self-reported nature of the questionnaires and granularity. For instance, participants were asked at the end of the day about their stress and anxiety overall levels. If they went through several levels of stress and anxiety during the day, they only reported their overall perception. This can introduce some recall bias and loss of granularity. To reduce those effects, in the upcoming semesters we will update the application such that the questionnaires are sent to the participants at different time periods during the day. Another approach would be to include Ecologically Momentary Assessments and ask the participants about their stress level at particular points in time based on the Fitbit’s provided stress score. Furthermore, we expect to increase the number of participants in the study, the number of groups, and add more sensors.
