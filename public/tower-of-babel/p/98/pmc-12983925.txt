Introduction

Total hip replacement (THR) remains the most effective treatment for patients with disabling hip arthritis.1 The vast majority are satisfied with the outcome of their THR, but 11% of patients report they are dissatisfied 1 year after their operation.2 This cohort of dissatisfied patients and those who have worn out their artificial joints over time may end up requiring a revision hip replacement. The identification, work-up3 and management4 of these patients can be slow, complex and costly.5 Appropriate selection and a multidisciplinary approach to management6 is essential for the over 7200 revision hip replacements performed in the UK annually.7 There is a paucity of high-quality evidence regarding the assessment, management and rehabilitation of this group of patients. This evidence gap leaves patients, carers and clinicians faced with uncertainties regarding the best way to move forward. The James Lind Alliance (JLA) is a non-profit making initiative established in 2004 and funded by the National Institute of Health Research (NIHR). It aims to help patients, carers and clinicians work together and form Priority Setting Partnerships (PSPs) in order to reach a consensus on the most important treatment uncertainties in their particular area of interest.8 In June 2021, the BHS commenced the ‘Problematic Hip Replacement’ PSP in collaboration with the JLA. The aim of this PSP was to identify, from a patient and clinician perspective, the unanswered questions about the assessment, management and rehabilitation of patients with problematic hip replacements. These ‘known unknowns’ were then ranked by consensus into the top 10 such that future research and its funding can be designed to answer these uncertainties and prioritised to answer them.

Methods

Steering group formation

The Steering Group (SG) was formed in September 2021 and the final workshop took place in September 2023. The methodology by which priorities were determined was according to clear guidelines established by the JLA. An overview of this process can be found in figure 1. This methodology has been employed previously in several published PSPs in orthopaedics, including topics such as early osteoarthritis, primary hip and primary/revision knee replacements, trauma and paediatrics.9,11

Flow diagram showing stages of the JLA-led PSP process. AHP, Allied Health Professional; HCP, healthcare professionals; JLA, James Lind Alliance; PSP, Priority Setting Partnership.

The SG included patients and their representatives, allied health professionals and orthopaedic surgeons. The SG was formed of 15 individuals with diverse backgrounds, representing various stakeholder groups throughout the UK. The SG was led by the senior author (TNB) and coordinated by AK. SG members supervised the entire process, disclosed their areas of interest and any potential conflicts of interest at the initiation of the PSP. They collaborated with partner organisations (online supplemental table S1) who committed to supporting the PSP by disseminating surveys and helping to gather uncertainties of practical importance to their members. These partner organisations included charitable institutions and specialty societies which could reach and advocate for all stakeholders. An adviser from the JLA oversaw the process and guided the SG, serving as an independent and neutral facilitator to ensure that the established protocols were adhered to and that all stakeholders had an equal opportunity to contribute. Furthermore, an independent information specialist analysed the initial survey questions into “collated indicative questions” which are clear, addressable by research and understandable to all.

Scope of the project

The scope of this PSP encompassed individuals who encounter problems following hip replacement surgery. This includes the period starting from when they first notice problems with their hip replacement, progressing through the stages of assessment and management, which involve investigations, decision-making, consideration of both surgical and non-surgical treatment options and the subsequent rehabilitation phase. We excluded uncertainties relating to the prevention of problems with primary hip replacements.

Survey distribution and data collection

An initial survey was distributed openly through invitations to patients, caregivers and HCPs in both online and paper format (online supplemental first survey). Partner organisations helped with survey distribution to their membership. Survey respondents were asked to submit their questions as free text. Additional data collected included basic demographics, occupation, work setting, age, gender, ethnicity and respondents’ region of residence. The survey was available for a period of 6 months. The survey started with an introduction to what problematic hip replacements are and posed the question ‘If you have had experience of problematic hip replacements either as a patient, carer or health professional, please tell us the questions you would like to see answered by future research’. The number of responses was actively monitored and efforts were made to improve this, including provision of hard copy questionnaires for distribution in clinics, leafleting and posters in clinic settings and regular email contact and encouragement to members of the steering group to gather local responses. Respondents to the first survey were asked to provide an email contact if they wished to be involved in the next phase of the project.

Data categorisation and question refinement

Survey responses were categorised by an information specialist (KS), who grouped the raw questions into common topics and summarised each group of questions into a single ‘indicative question’ designed to be easily understood by all participants. The wording of these indicative questions was carefully reviewed and adjusted based on feedback from patients, carers and clinicians on the steering group, with a focus on maintaining the meaning of the original submissions. The following categories of research questions were identified as falling outside the scope of this initiative: those that could be addressed through an audit; those that were overly broad, unrelated to the PSP or difficult to interpret; questions seeking clinical information or advice and those related to service accessibility, service quality, professional training and guideline development.

Literature review and evidence checking

A literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane and Allied Health databases to identify high-quality studies (systematic reviews and meta-analyses of level 1 and 2 studies and randomised control trials) which directly answered each indicative question. These searches utilised strategies developed by a member of the team and covered the previous 10 years. After removing duplicate entries, abstracts were reviewed by at least two evaluators, and full texts were examined when necessary. Studies were excluded if they were not systematic reviews or RCTs, if they were unrelated to the questions, or if they were not in English. Any discrepancies in evaluation were resolved by a third reviewer. The selected papers were assessed for their quality, the extent to which they resolved remaining uncertainty, and their ability to provide a comprehensive answer to a question. If it was determined that evidence exists that has previously answered the question such that there was no significant uncertainty, then the question was removed as it was deemed answered. The remaining unanswered questions formed the basis of a ‘long list’ of questions for further evaluation.

Interim ranking and workshop preparation

An interim ranking was established through a second survey, where participants were asked to select their ten most important questions from the long list of questions. This survey, as with the initial one, was conducted openly, inviting responses over an 8-week period and was conducted online. A predefined scoring system was applied: a question ranked first received 10 points, the second-ranked question received 9 points, and so on, with the tenth-ranked question receiving 1 point. Questions not included in a respondent’s top 10 received zero points. The points from all respondents were collated to create an interim ranking, which was then presented to the steering group to assist in generating a shortlist of questions for discussion at the final online workshop.

Workshop participant selection and process

Participants for the workshop were selected from volunteers who had been identified in earlier phases of the PSP or had responded to posters, adverts and direct clinical approach to ensure a diverse representation of clinicians, allied health professionals and patients or their representatives. Before the workshop convened, participants were tasked with ranking all the questions that had been shortlisted. The workshop consisted of three main phases, adhering to the established JLA methodology:

Introductory Session: Following an initial introduction, the shortlisted questions were thoroughly discussed within three small groups, each comprising an equal number of participants. These groups were deliberately composed to ensure that each contained a balance of both professional expertise and personal patient experiences. In this phase, each participant took turns expressing their opinions. Facilitators documented the reasons behind prevailing agreements and disagreements. Subsequently, each small group ranked the shortlisted questions based on their discussions.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and carer representatives were involved throughout the process. They helped to define the scope of the project and actively participated in reviewing all patient-facing media. They were involved in all steering group meetings and decisions. Collaborating with patient organisations, they played a key role in ensuring a diverse range of patient and carer groups were reached for the surveys and the final workshop. A lead patient representative is a co-author on this paper.

Results

Survey demographics and participation

There were a total of 201 uncertainties (questions) submitted by 84 respondents through the initial survey. 45 (54%) of the respondents were identified as male. Respondents included 33 patients, 3 carers and 48 HCPs. There was a wide geographical spread of respondents (online supplemental table 2). The age groupings of the respondents are shown in online supplemental table 3.

The majority of respondents (74; 88%) identified their ethnic group as White (Caucasian), sseven (8.3%) as Asian/Asian British, one (1.2%) as Mixed Arabic and two (2.4%) preferred not to say.

Of the HCPs that responded, the majority (%) were surgeons, nine (19%) were physiotherapists, three (6%) were nurses, three (6%) were general practitioners (GPs) and 1 was (2%) an occupational therapist. Six (12.5%) of HCPs also identified themselves as patients or carers. 35 (73%) of HCPs worked out of a hospital setting, and five (11%) were based in the community. 28 (78%) of patients and carers that responded reported that either they or the person they cared for had reported problems after hip replacement surgery.

Question categorisation and evidence review

Eight broad categories of question were identified (assessment, health services, implants, prognosis, rehabilitation/prehab/self-management, surgery, treatment and psychological/social impacts). Within each of these categories, survey responses that were about a similar research topic were grouped together. These groups were summarised by one summary question that remained true to the initial submissions. 32 summary questions emerged in an initial long-list of questions to be evidence-checked.

The initial evidence checking literature search produced 3948 abstracts, which were screened for inclusion. After assessment of relevant full text articles, it was agreed by the steering group that only 1 of the 32 long list questions had sufficiently been answered by previous research, which was ‘is revision hip replacement best done in one or two stages’? It was agreed that the results of the NIHR-funded INFORM study had answered this question.12,15 Therefore, 31 questions progressed to the interim ranking survey.

Interim survey results and workshop selection

A total of 191 responses were received for the interim ranking survey. 80% of the respondents were HCPs, 17% were patients and 3% were carers. 63% of the respondents identified as male. The demographics, work settings and distribution of HCPs are detailed in the online supplemental interim ranking survey. Discussion

This national PSP has identified the top 10 research priorities for problematic hip replacements. The application of the JLA methodology has been key in identifying priorities that are important to a broad scope of stakeholders including patients, carers and HCPs.9 10 The top three priorities focused on expeditious/accurate diagnosis of causes of problematic hip replacements, understanding/managing pain and improving outcomes from revision through interventions such as prehabilitation. The next tier of priorities focused on managing periprosthetic fractures, identifying patients who will do best from revision surgery and enhancing post-operative care to improve outcomes after revision surgery.

Regarding the top priorities identified, diagnosis of problematic hip replacements can be challenging, and prior to revision, the cause of symptomatology can be multifactorial and requires a systematic approach.16 Addressing this priority will require multidisciplinary research and involvement between clinicians and hospital management. Ongoing national delays in overall diagnostic waiting times and a shortage of radiologists are barriers to be overcome.17 Furthermore, at a regional and local level, service organisation is crucial, as is an increased awareness of the seriousness of problematic hip replacement.

Pain is a complex problem, posing both a diagnostic and therapeutic conundrum to the MDT managing problematic hip replacements. Like prosthetic joint infection, it requires a team-based approach with consideration of potential causes and treatments, with revision surgery reserved for patients who have a cause identified and who have failed conservative treatment.18 Less commonly considered, but eminently treatable diagnoses such as psoas impingement19 should form part of any diagnostic pathway. New imaging methods such as Image Movement Analysis (IMA)20 may also form part of improved pathways. The optimal non-operative management of painful hip replacements has not been well researched and is likely to be dependent on the correct diagnosis, which, as outlined above, relies on timely and appropriate radiological and other investigations. Thus, there is an intersection between diagnosing the cause of a problematic hip replacement and treating the pain associated with hip pathology following THR.

Optimising outcomes of revision surgery was a key outcome from the PSP. There is extensive literature on the role of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS), physical21 and nutritional22 prehabilitation, and the combination of these approaches has been shown to be complementary and beneficial in improving outcomes.23 Prehabilitation has been shown to be effective in orthopaedic24 and major non-orthopaedic surgeries.25 Therefore, we hypothesise that while prehabilitation could improve outcomes of elective hip revision, ERAS could improve outcome in both elective and emergency cases. There is, however, a gap in the knowledge base, and as such, a high-quality randomised study investigating the role of such interventions around revision hip surgery would be warranted. With the caveat that there may be some challenges due to the associated morbidity and pain associated with problematic hip replacement, which may preclude participants from certain physical interventions, that an individually tailored approach could overcome.

One of the emerging changes in the management of problematic hip replacements over the last few years has been the sub-specialisation of revision hip surgery. This potentially complex surgery is now often dealt with in secondary or tertiary centres by higher volume surgeons.7 There has been a drive to increase the number of local and regional MDT meetings to aid clinical decision making. This new approach should help to address some of the top 10 priorities. Furthermore, a ‘specific painful hip replacement’ MDT or clinic could, in theory, bring together experienced surgeons, anaesthetists, radiologists, microbiologists, pain specialists and allied HCPs and could expedite diagnosis, guide evidence-based management and triage the highest-risk patients for surgical intervention. There is, however, an evidence gap here, and future studies may seek to quantify what, if any, benefit MDT meetings have in improving longer term outcomes from problematic hip replacements given the resource implications of providing them. Revision hip arthroplasty continues to have a global and national burden, with over 7200 hips revised in the UK in 2024.7 Each of these cases represents a suffering patient and an extensive multi-disciplinary team who are also affected, as well as a socioeconomic burden on the NHS to treat and manage each patient. For each patient undergoing revision, there are many others with complex problems, including the 13% of patients who suffer from chronic pain,26 who are suffering but may be deemed to be either unfit or unsuitable for further surgery. Thus, looking at the number of revisions alone underestimates the burden of problematic hip replacements.

This study had many strengths. It is the first PSP reporting the national research priorities in problematic hip replacements, and we report a comparable number of respondents to other similar studies.9 Our data include extensive engagement from a broad scope of respondents in terms of age, relationship to problematic hip replacements (HCPs, patients and carers) and geographical location. This PSP included the perspectives of respondents aged <40 to over 80, as well as respondents from the majority of England and Scotland. Our approach used the previously extensively validated JLA approach, which has previously been applied to orthopaedic9 10 and non-orthopaedic25 conditions. Within the HCPs, the views of a broad scope of the MDT managing these conditions were represented, including medical and non-medical staff. Our study had some limitations; it was national and therefore was comparable to other JLA PSPs,9 but did not include the views of any international respondents, which has been previously reported in major trauma.10 Although this work was done in collaboration with patients/carers, only 17% of our respondents fell into this group, when compared with 31%10 and even >50%9 in other PSPs. We did report data from a majority of the UK, however, there was a bias towards responses from the North West (43.6%), this is likely due to the PSP being based out of the region, with no responses received from Wales. Consequently, this bias also meant that the majority of respondents (88%) were white Caucasian, and 63% were male, which may indicate that the top 10 priorities identified may under-represent the views of patients, carers and staff who were female or from ethnic minorities. Future PSPs may seek to include more views from under-represented cohorts.27 Finally, the final top 10 questions may seem quite broad in subject, which may make individual studies difficult to design. However, we feel this reflects the large scope of remaining issues in this particular field, and any research design could focus on a small element of an individual question.

Progress has already been made with some of the uncertainties raised. Even before the completion of this PSP, it was identified that for many studies on revision hip replacement, a comprehensive classification tool was required and was therefore developed by the British Hip Society.6 Studies are also complete investigating who should be performing revision surgery.28 29

In conclusion, we have applied a well-validated method in collaboration with multiple stakeholders to identify the top 10 key research questions in problematic hip replacements. Future research should focus on developing the evidence base to answer these key questions.

Take home message

In this publication, the perspectives of patients, caregivers and healthcare experts who have assessed and ranked their primary research priorities regarding problematic hip replacements are featured. These priorities were established and ranked by a clearly defined, transparent and structured method directed by the JLA.
