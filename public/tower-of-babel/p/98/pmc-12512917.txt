Drug-induced insulin autoimmune syndrome: a clinico-epidemiological analysis integrating pharmacovigilance data and case series
Authors: Xiuheng Yu, Min Li, Yi Liao, Yuqing Xie, Jing Xu, Youqi Long, Shixin Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsulin autoimmune syndrome (IAS) is a rare immune-mediated hypoglycemic disorder predominantly triggered by pharmacological agents. Despite the established links to thiol-containing drugs, emerging non-thiol triggers and significant geographical reporting biases limit comprehensive risk profiling. This study integrated pharmacovigilance data and published evidence to establish the first systematic epidemiological and pharmacological profile of drug-induced IAS.MethodsWe analysed 228 IAS cases from the FDA AE Reporting System (FAERS; 2004–Q2 2024) and a systematic review of 263 published cases (1980–2024). Multimodal disproportionality analysis—including Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR), Proportional Reporting Ratio (PRR), Multi-item Gamma Poisson Shrinker (MGPS), and Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN)—identified drug-induced adverse event signals. Associations were stratified into a three-tier evidence framework (Levels 1–3) based on methodological concordance, case counts, and strength of the supporting literature.ResultsFifty-eight agents showed potential IAS associations, including 12 novel pharmacovigilance signals (e.g., bevacizumab, sitagliptin, amlodipine, and olmesartan). Thiol-containing drugs exhibit the strongest signals (PRR > 200; e.g., captopril, methimazole, and clopidogrel). Level 1 evidence (highest confidence) implicated the use of clopidogrel, captopril, omeprazole, and methimazole. IAS was predominantly affected in older patients (median age, 66 years; IQR, 58–77 years), with a male predominance (59.21%), reflecting sex-specific prescription patterns. Geographical disparities persisted, with 85.17% of the cases reported in the literature in Asia. Reports of hypoglycemia exceeded IAS cases for most agents, suggesting an underdiagnosis.ConclusionsThis study established the first evidence-based hierarchy for drug-induced IAS, identifying 58 agents warranting clinical vigilance. Thiol-containing drugs dominate the high-risk profile; however, novel associations (e.g., proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors) reveal broader immunological mechanisms. Clinicians should prioritize IAS in older patients with unexplained hypoglycemia exposed to antiplatelet agents or PPIs. Introduction

Insulin autoimmune syndrome (IAS), also known as Hirata’s disease, is a rare condition characterized by recurrent spontaneous hypoglycemia, elevated serum insulin levels, and high insulin antibody titers. First described by Hirata in Japan in 1970, IAS was identified in individuals without prior exposure to exogenous insulin [1]. Pharmacological triggers remain the predominant etiological factor, accounting for over 80% of reported cases [2]. Among them, methimazole is the most frequently used, followed by α-lipoic acid, clopidogrel, and tiopronin. Over the past five decades, more than 40 drugs have been associated with IAS [3–6]. Notably, over half of IAS patients report exposure to drugs containing thiol groups, which can chemically and immunologically interact with insulin molecules, triggering the production of insulin autoantibodies [7, 8]. However, emerging reports of IAS induced by non-thiol drugs suggest alternative immunological pathways [9, 10].

The current research exhibited significant geographical bias, with most studies focusing on East Asian populations. This regional predominance may reflect both the high prevalence of human leukocyte antigen DRB1 (HLA-DRB1) *04:06 alleles [11] and the widespread use of thiol-containing drugs (e.g., methimazole for Graves’ disease) in these populations [8]. This geographic bias limits generalizability, especially for non-thiol drugs such as proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), for which the reported associations lack population-specific validation despite the growing number of case reports [12–14].

Our recent clinical encounter with a methimazole-associated IAS case highlighted a critical knowledge gap: when transitioning to alternative therapies, such as propylthiouracil, the literature suggests potential recurrence of IAS or hypoglycemia [2, 4, 15], yet comparative risk stratification between antithyroid agents remains undefined.

The true incidence of IAS remains elusive, owing to its rarity. Existing research is primarily confined to sporadic case reports and small case series studies, posing the risk of underreporting mild cases deemed lacking in publication merit and misdiagnosing IAS as uncomplicated hypoglycemia [3, 16]. Diagnostic challenges, requiring concurrent measurement of serum insulin levels during hypoglycemic episodes and insulin autoantibody titers, are compounded by limited access to specialized assays [17]. A 2022 systematic review of 294 IAS cases revealed that few reports provided definitive evidence of rechallenge or dechallenge [18], while mechanistic studies remain scarce for novel signals, such as TNF-α inhibitors [19, 20].

Although prior studies have utilized the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) to investigate drug-associated hypoglycemia and clopidogrel-associated IAS [21, 22], comprehensive analyses of IAS cases and disproportionality signal detection remain unexplored. This study integrates FAERS data (2004–Q2 2024) with systematic evidence synthesis to identify emerging drug-iatrogenic AE (IAS) associations through multi-method disproportionality analytics. It establishes a risk stratification framework that integrates epidemiological and mechanistic evidence to characterize demographic and geographic patterns.

Materials and methods

Case review

Patient history, diagnostic, and treatment process

A 23-year-old female presented to our endocrinology department on March 26, 2024, complaining of episodic chest tightness, generalized Limb weakness, and other associated symptoms. These manifestations began on March 20, 2024, without apparent cause, and were accompanied by paroxysmal fever and dizziness, with reported symptom relief following food intake. The symptoms occurred more frequently in the afternoon (approximately twice daily). On March 25, 2024, she experienced two severe episodes of chest tightness, fever, sweating, and limb weakness, which occurred between 4:00 PM, 5:00 PM, and 10:00 PM.

One month previously, the patient was diagnosed with hyperthyroidism, as evidenced by hand tremors and abnormal thyroid function test results. The patient was administered methimazole (30 mg daily). Approximately one week before admission, the dosage was independently reduced to 15 mg daily.

Laboratory evaluation confirmed hyperthyroidism with thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels < 0.014 µIU/mL and free thyroxine (FT4) levels of 6.79 pmol/L. Insulin levels were markedly elevated to 1151.11 µU/mL. Testing for insulin autoimmune antibodies revealed reactive insulin autoantibodies at 187.00 C.O.I. (cut-off index), whereas islet cell antibodies were non-reactive at a concentration of 0.05 C.O.I. Glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies measured 0.72 IU/mL; the C-peptide level was 6.15 ng/mL. The blood glucose level was 5.95 mmol/L. An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) performed one month before admission revealed no significant abnormalities in insulin secretion. Enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the upper abdomen revealed no pancreatic abnormalities.

Propylthiouracil was initiated as an alternative antithyroid medication for hyperthyroidism management. The patient underwent continuous glucose monitoring to track the blood glucose levels in real time. During the hypoglycemic episodes, glucose supplements were promptly administered. Additionally, nutritional support was provided, including dietary recommendations to prevent hypoglycemia and enhance overall nutritional intake. Following treatment, the frequency and severity of the patients’ symptoms significantly decreased, and insulin levels returned to normal ranges. At the one-month follow-up, there was no recurrence of chest tightness, limb weakness, dizziness, or other symptoms.

Causality assessment

To evaluate the causality between drugs and IAS, this study employed a modified version of the Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale [23]. In this case, the Naranjo scale assigned a “probable” causality score (7/13) to methimazole (Table 1). While temporality and dechallenge positivity support an association, definitive causal attribution requires rechallenging evidence. Rechallenge was not performed due to the risk of severe hypoglycemia recurrence and the availability of effective alternative therapy (propylthiouracil). This limitation precludes a “definite” Naranjo causality rating but is consistent with clinical practice, where rechallenge is often avoided in severe reactions.

Literature review

Literature searching strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted across three major databases, PubMed, Embase, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), encompassing records up to October 2024. This investigation targeted review articles and case reports on IAS using a comprehensive search strategy. The search parameters included title, abstract, keywords, and full-text fields using the following search string for PubMed: (((“Insulin Autoimmune Syndrome” [MeSH Terms]) OR “Insulin Autoimmune Syndrome” [tiab] OR (“Hirata Disease” [tiab] OR “Hirata’s Disease” [tiab] OR “Hirata Syndrome” [tiab]) OR “insulin autoimmune” [tiab])) AND (“Review” [pt] OR “Case Reports” [pt] OR “case series” [tiab]). Comparable search strategies were implemented for Embase and CNKI with appropriate modifications to accommodate database-specific syntax requirements. No linguistic or temporal restrictions were imposed during the searches.

Literature screening criteria

The selection process strictly adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [24], ensuring methodological rigor in the screening and selection procedures. Studies were included if they were case reports, case series, or systematic reviews that explicitly linked medications to IAS, provided drug-IAS causality assessments, and were accessible in full text. The exclusion criteria were duplicate publications, insufficient diagnostic evidence, non-original reviews, and cases that attributed IAS to non-pharmacological causes.

Open-source analysis

Data source

Data were obtained from the FDA portal (https://fis.fda.gov/extensions/FPD-QDE-FAERS/FPD-QDE-FAERS.html). We retrieved the FAERS database from Q1 2004 to Q2 2024 and used SAS 9.4 to merge the datasets. Cases where the target drug was listed as “secondary suspicion,” “concurrent medication,” or “interaction” were excluded, and only instances in which the target was listed as a “primary suspicion” of an AE were included. Each patient has a unique medical record number continuously updated, and duplicate submissions were removed based on the FDA recommendations. This validated workflow was applied uniformly to all analyses.

Definitions of drugs and AEs

In FAERS, drugs are identified using their generic and brand names as listed in the Drugs@FDA database (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cder/daf/index.cfm). Drugs with identical generic names were used for the analysis. Drug classification was performed using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system (https://atcddd.fhi.no/atc/structure_and_principles/). Adverse Events (AEs) were queried using the preferred term (PT) from the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) version 27.0. A single PT term was used for autoimmune insulin syndrome. For anti-insulin antibody (IAA)-related events, relevant PTs included “anti-insulin antibody,” “anti-insulin antibody positive,” and “anti-insulin antibody increased.” anti-insulin antibody increased. Hyperinsulinemia-related PTs include “hyperinsulinemia (HI)” and “hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia (HH).” The term “hypoglycaemic” was used to describe hypoglycemia-related events.

Process of data mining

To evaluate the potential associations between suspected drugs and AEs, such as IAS and hypoglycemia, four distinct algorithms based on Bayesian analysis and disproportionality assessment were applied. These algorithms included the Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR), the Proportional Reporting Ratio (PRR), the Multi-item Gamma Poisson Shrinker (MGPS), and the Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN) [25, 26]. The equations and criteria for these algorithms are listed in Table 2. The primary objective of these methods is to quantify the strength of association between specific drugs and AEs. A drug was deemed to exhibit a positive signal of association with an AE if any of the four algorithms produced results that met the predefined criteria.

Sensitivity analysis for reporting duplication

Patient-level validation was performed to evaluate the potential inflation of signals from duplicate reporting. Reports were consolidated into distinct patients using a composite matching key comprising exact age, sex, reporting country, and reporting date of ± 7 days. For these agents, all disproportionality metrics (PRR, ROR, MGPS, and BCPNN) were recalculated using patient counts.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel 2016 and Origin Pro 2021 software.

Results

Systematic literature review findings

Identified drug associations and demographic patterns

A systematic literature review was conducted, which included 48 review articles and 225 case reports (Fig. 1; a complete list of 263 cases is provided in Supplementary Table S1). Through the case report data analysis, 36 medications were identified as potentially associated with IAS. This list was supplemented with 10 additional agents identified in the review articles, resulting in 46 drugs associated with IAS development (Supplementary Table S2).

PRISMA flow diagram of the literature screening process for IAS (1980–2024.10). The diagram shows database search results, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and the process of identifying the final 263 included studies

Antithyroid drugs (n = 105, 39.92%), insulin and analogs (n = 56, 21.29%), and α-lipoic acid (n = 38, 14.45%) were the most frequently reported categories of drugs associated with AEs. Methimazole (n = 95), α-lipoic acid (n = 38), clopidogrel (n = 13), and tiopronin (n = 10) were each associated with over 10 documented cases, whereas carbimazole (n = 6) and omeprazole (n = 6) were associated with more than five cases.

Temporal and geographic distribution trends

A striking temporal trend was observed, with 77.57% (n = 204) of the cases reported since 2010. Affected individuals had a mean age of 53.24 ± 16.59 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 37–68 years). Sex-specific patterns were observed: females comprised 63.81% of antithyroid drug-induced IAS cases, whereas males accounted for 58.06% of IAS cases induced by other drugs. Geographically, the reported cases were predominantly concentrated in Asia (85.17% of all cases), particularly in China and Japan.

FAERS database analysis

In this study, 21,558,936 patient-reported cases were retrieved from the FAERS database. Following deduplication according to the FDA reporting guidelines, 3,611,216 duplicate entries from the same patients were removed, leaving 17,947,720 unique patients associated with 53,352,754 AE reports (noting that a single patient submission may contain multiple distinct AE records), resulting in the identification of 228 reports with target adverse events.

Case characteristics and temporal trends

We analysed the demographics and clinical outcomes of IAS cases reported in the FAERS database (Table 3), with geographic distributions detailed separately (Supplementary Table S3). The median age of patients with IAS-related events was 66 years (IQR: 58–77), indicating a predominantly older patient population. Only one case has been reported in individuals under 18 years of age. Regarding sex distribution, there was a significant predominance of male patients compared to females. Reported cases of IAS adverse reactions were predominantly from Asia (135 cases, 59.21%), followed by North America (37 cases, 16.23%), and Europe(18 cases, 7.89%). China (n = 62, 27.19%), Japan (n = 58, 25.44%), and the United States (n = 35, 15.35%) were the top reporting countries. Other countries that reported five or more cases included Australia, Brazil, India, South Korea, Poland, and Germany. Continence was not specified in 5 patients (2.19%).

Primary signal detection for drug-ias associations

Bayesian and disproportionality analyses identified 43 distinct drugs associated with IAS in the FAERS database; of these, 29 met the predefined positive signal detection criteria (signal patterns summarized in Table 4; full algorithm results in Supplementary Table S4). Twelve agents demonstrated concordant positive signals across all four methodologies (denoted as “YYYY” in Table 4), including clopidogrel (74 cases), captopril (15 cases), omeprazole (12 cases), sildenafil (10 cases), esomeprazole (9 cases), methimazole (7 cases), alongside bevacizumab, sitagliptin (5 cases each), and pantoprazole, candesartan, isoniazid, diltiazem (4 cases each). Notably, 23 signals involved non-insulin drugs, with 12 representing novel pharmacovigilance signals that have not been reported in the literature. Integration with literature-derived agents yielded a consolidated profile of 58 therapeutic compounds potentially associated with IAS.

Sensitivity analysis: impact of deduplication

Patient-level deduplication using exact age, sex, reporting country, and event onset date within a ± 7-day window consolidated sildenafil reports from 10 to 1 distinct patient and diltiazem reports from 4 to 1. This rendered the PRR and ROR signals non-assessable for both agents owing to insufficient case numbers (n < 3). For sildenafil, the MGPS signal shifted from positive (EB05 = 12.28) to negative (EB05 = 0.87), whereas the BCPNN signal remained persistently positive (IC025 = 2.14) (EB05 and IC025 defined in Table 1 footnotes). Diltiazem maintained a positive MGPS signal despite attenuation (EB05 = 9.70 to 3.62) and consistently demonstrated positive BCPNN results (IC025 = 1.72). For candesartan and isoniazid, all reports were consolidated to two distinct patients, resulting in negative PRR and ROR signals; however, both agents maintained positive MGPS and BCPNN signals. No other agents exhibited signal polarity changes following deduplication validation.

Evidence hierarchy classification

Using disproportionality analysis of FAERS data for drug-associated IAS signals, the identified associations were categorized into a three-tiered evidence hierarchy integrating methodological concordance, case count, and literature support. Level 1 (strong evidence) comprised agents demonstrating positive signals across all four methodologies (PRR, ROR, MGPS, BCPNN, and YYYY) with ≥ 3 distinct patient cases and prior Literature corroboration, indicating high statistical confidence and biological plausibility. Representative Level 1 agents included clopidogrel, captopril, omeprazole, esomeprazole, methimazole, and pantoprazole.

Level 2 (moderate evidence) encompassed agents warranting significant attention under three scenarios: a) complete methodological concordance (YYYY) without existing literature reports (e.g., bevacizumab and sitagliptin); b) a positive MGPS signal (EB05 ≥ 2) OR a positive BCPNN signal (IC025 ≥ 0) with ≥ 2 cases, irrespective of literature status (e.g., amlodipine, olmesartan, glimepiride, candesartan, isoniazid, meropenem, and albumin human); and c) literature-supported single-patient agents (n = 1) with positive BCPNN signals (IC025 ≥ 0) reflecting biologically plausible associations requiring surveillance (e.g., sildenafil and diltiazem). Level 3 (weakevidence) included agents with signals in only one methodology, no literature support, and minimal cases (n = 1) representing preliminary hypotheses requiring substantial validation, which typically show borderline or negative signal metrics, exemplified by cilostazol, famotidine, and rabeprazole (all n = 1 with single-method signals unreported in the literature).

Supplementary signal analysis: IAA/HI/hypoglycemia events

To investigate potential undiagnosed IAS, FAERS reports of anti-insulin antibodies (IAA), hyperinsulinemia/hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia (HI/HH), and hypoglycemia were analyzed (Fig. 5). In addition to interferon alfa, the sole agent exclusively associated with IAA without concurrent IAS reports, four drugs (sertraline, rifampin, hydralazine, and atenolol) exhibited weak BCPNN hyperinsulinemia signals (IC025 > 0) but lacked corroborating IAS evidence. Notably, hypoglycemia reports substantially exceeded IAS cases for most agents (≥ 10 documented episodes), suggesting widespread underdiagnosis, except for methimazole and captopril, for which IAS and hypoglycemia incidences were comparable. The BCPNN analysis identified clopidogrel, omeprazole, olmesartan, sitagliptin, and interferon alpha as IAA-positive signals. Hyperinsulinemia/hypoglycemia signals were observed for clopidogrel, sildenafil, sitagliptin, isoniazid, diltiazem, metoprolol, amlodipine, meropenem, rifampin, sertraline, atenolol, and hydralazine. Discordances were observed: sildenafil and esomeprazole showed hypoglycemia-negative signals despite positive IAS associations, whereas amlodipine, olmesartan, metoprolol, glimepiride, valsartan, isosorbide dinitrate, cilostazol, interferon alfa, sertraline, propylthiouracil, imipenem, hydralazine, enalapril, diclofenac, azithromycin, and atenolol demonstrated hypoglycemia-positive but IAS-negative signals.

Discussion

This study is the first to evaluate drug-related IAS comprehensively using the FAERS database.

Key findings and novel pharmacovigilance signals

Evolution of reported drug associations and literature synthesis

Previous studies have attempted to summarize the drugs associated with IAS, although the List varies considerably. Censi et al. identified 26 drugs that potentially induce IAS [5], whereas Cappellani reported 25 drugs [3]. Lin [20] expanded the list to 37 drugs and introduced 15 additional agents, resulting in 41 drugs. Combined with five additional agents identified through a systematic literature review, this yielded a consolidated profile of 46 IAS-associated therapeutic agents.

Novel signals and expanded evidence for known associations

This study newly characterized 12 pharmacological agents, including bevacizumab and olmesartan, and demonstrated statistically significant IAS associations. Although methimazole remains the earliest recognized inducer, clopidogrel has emerged as the most frequently reported agent in contemporary pharmacovigilance data. Notably, neither α-lipoic acid nor tiopronin showed IAS cases in FAERS. This absence may be attributed to the classification of α-lipoic acid as a dietary supplement, which typically falls outside FAERS reporting requirements, and the geographical reporting bias of tiopronin, with only 1,683 total AEs predominantly originating in the United States. This contrasts with the documented tiopronin-IAS associations across multiple studies: Cappellani et al. reported fewer than five cases [3], Lin et al. documented 54 cases [20], and ten cases were recorded in China (Supplementary Table S1).

A review examining the hypoglycemia risk associated with clopidogrel in six randomized controlled trials (RCTs) found no significant association in the overall population but identified a higher risk in the Asian subgroup [27]. Multiple studies have documented clopidogrel-induced IAS [22, 28], with one study reporting 51 cases. Our findings confirm that clopidogrel is a common cause of IAS.

We identified 27 PPI-related IAS cases that exceeded the number of cases in our literature review and those previously reported [20]. This substantial evidence establishes that PPIs significantly contribute to IAS, necessitating increased clinical vigilance. While captopril has definitive literature support as an IAS inducer (Naranjo scores ≥ 7 in five cases) [28–30], isoniazid [31, 32] demonstrated a probable association based on temporal plausibility. Our FAERS data validated both associations, with captopril ranking highest in signal intensity among the antihypertensives (MGPS = 1648.68). For sildenafil, only one case of IAS has been reported [33], whereas 10 cases have been reported in FAERS. However, upon careful examination of the original reports in FAERS, it was found that the IAS reports associated with sildenafil likely pertain to the same patient, as all reports originated in Australia and involved a 62-year-old patient, consistent with the case described in the existing literature, suggesting that the signal for sildenafil may represent a false positive. Although sitagliptin demonstrated a positive IAS signal, cautious interpretation is warranted given its established role as an antidiabetic agent, where hypoglycemia may occur independently of autoimmune etiology.

Methodological considerations

Algorithm performance comparison

The integrated deployment of frequentist (PRR/ROR) and Bayesian (MGPS/BCPNN) methodologies capitalizes on their complementary capacities. Frequentist algorithms deliver robust effect estimation for established associations but require ≥ 3 cases for analytical stability, while Bayesian counterparts employ prior distribution shrinkage to mitigate sparse-data bias, enabling sensitive detection of emerging signals [26]. For drugs with sparse reports (n ≤ 2), the Bayesian MGPS detected potential signals missed by the PRR/ROR (e.g., olmesartan, glimepiride, and meropenem), however, such “NNYN” signals serve primarily as hypothesis-generating tools. Their value lies in guiding targeted monitoring, yet they carry risks of false positives (sparse data bias) and confounding concerns (e.g., drug combinations).

Duplicate reporting mitigation strategies

Patient-level validation revealed substantial signal inflation from duplicate reporting in FAERS, particularly affecting low-volume associations. The sildenafil case exemplifies it. This differential behavior highlights how methodological approaches yield divergent interpretations for single-case scenarios. Such artifacts remain undetectable through standard deduplication protocols, demonstrating the necessity of composite-key validation for agents with limited reports.

Evidence hierarchy validation

Building on the integrated methodological framework (Sect."Algorithm performance comparison"), this synergy directly scaffolds our three-tiered evidence hierarchy, which embeds dual validation dimensions, statistical rigor through case-adapted algorithm concordance, and biological plausibility via a literature consensus. Specifically, level 1 evidence mandates quadruple-method agreement supplemented by ≥ 3 validated cases and mechanistic literature support to ensure unequivocal signal identification; level 2 harnesses Bayesian resilience to capture clinically significant associations that evade frequentist thresholds, accommodating literature-anchored single-case hypotheses (2-C) where statistical power is inherently constrained; level 3 exclusively reserves single-method signals without literature corroboration for surveillance prioritization.

Mechanistic insights

Thiol-containing drug pathogenesis

Our analysis revealed two distinct mechanistic patterns among IAS-associated agents, with T (thiol-related) agents exhibiting strong signals (PRR > 200). These signals align with established mechanisms in which thiol groups (-SH) disrupt insulin disulfide bonds (Cys A6-A11/B7-B19), triggering the formation of autoantibodies [3, 34].

Non-thiol drug immunogenic pathways

Emerging evidence suggests that the immunopathogenesis of non-thiol drug-induced IAS may involve diverse pathways beyond direct insulin modification, although the mechanistic evidence remains limited. For PPIs, such as esomeprazole and pantoprazole, the reactive metabolites covalently bind cysteine residues in gastric H/K-ATPase, potentially generating neoantigens that cross-react with insulin through molecular mimicry [35, 36]. The literature and FAERS data analysis indicated associations between candesartan/valsartan and IAS. We hypothesized that ARB-class drugs may induce IAS through shared mechanisms, although this hypothesis lacks direct evidence. The established link between olmesartan and reversible autoimmune-like enteropathy and hepatitis demonstrates its broad capacity to provoke organ-specific immune dysregulation [37]. Mechanistically, we propose that ARB metabolites trigger IAS via analogous pathways (e.g., indirect haptenization and molecular mimicry). Regarding amlodipine, which was identified as a novel non-thiol signal, its potential IAS induction may involve immunomodulatory pathways, similar to those of ARBs.

The potential contribution of bevacizumab to glycemic dysregulation may involve postulated mechanisms, including immunoglobulin redox activity analogous to Cys34 of albumin, which could alter the tertiary structure of insulin through disulfide shuffling [38]. Alternatively, it might compromise pancreatic islet vascular integrity and glucose sensing, which parallels the mechanism of TNF-α inhibitors [19]. Both hypotheses remain biologically plausible but are unconfirmed. Notably, imipenem has been clinically associated with IAS [39]. We postulate that structurally similar carbapenems, such as meropenem, may generate thiol metabolites capable of directly disrupting insulin disulfide bonds, a potential mechanism that warrants further investigation.

The immunomodulatory effects of DPP-4 inhibitors such as sitagliptin may contribute to the pathogenesis of IAS. DPP-4 (CD26) functions as a co-stimulatory molecule on T lymphocytes and regulates antigen recall, cytotoxic T cell activation, and B cell-mediated immunoglobulin synthesis [40]. The pharmacological inhibition of DPP-4 may disrupt immune tolerance by altering T-cell/B-cell crosstalk, potentially facilitating insulin autoantibody (IAA) production in genetically predisposed individuals.

A crucial but still developing hypothesis is that these distinct pathways might converge on HLA-DRB1*04:06-restricted presentation, further implicating CD4+ T-cell activation by recognizing cross-reactive epitopes [11]. This process could mirror viral-induced IAS, where molecular mimicry between viral antigens and insulin-derived peptides promotes the expansion of autoreactive T-cell clones, potentially amplifying autoreactive responses in genetically susceptible hosts, particularly those with coexisting autoimmune conditions [41]. Robust validation of novel pharmacovigilance signals is essential and requires metabolite characterization and epitope mapping studies, especially given the frequent polypharmacy that can confound associations.

Demographic disparities and reporting biases

Age and gender distribution

In a study reviewing 294 IAS cases, the mean age at presentation was 54 ± 19 years, with over 70% of the cases involving individuals older than 45. Our findings similarly identified older patients as the predominant demographic in IAS cases, consistent with previous research [18]. Patel observed a female-to-male ratio of 1.2:1, which increased to 1.7:1 in individuals aged 16–45 [18]. In contrast, our analysis revealed male predominance, diverging from prior findings. The male predominance in FAERS cases contrasts with literature reports of female-biased IAS in methimazole-treated cohorts [42]. This discrepancy likely reflects regional prescribing patterns; clopidogrel, a top IAS inducer, is preferentially prescribed to older males for cardiovascular prophylaxis [22]. In our literature review, females comprised 63.81% of IAS cases associated with antithyroid drugs, predominantly methimazole. At the same time, the underrepresentation of methimazole-related IAS in FAERS, despite its known association since the 1980 s, may reflect diminished reporting incentives for labelled AEs (Weber effect) [43]. Additionally, methimazole-induced hypoglycemia is often transient and misattributed to Graves’ disease, particularly in young females [44]. Consequently, FAERS data are skewed toward clopidogrel-associated IAS cases (predominantly older males), amplifying the observed male predominance.

Geographic reporting patterns

The pronounced geographical imbalance in IAS reporting, with Asia contributing 85.17% of literature cases, aligns with our results [5, 20]. High polymorphism of DRB1, which encodes the DRβ chain, is a critical factor underlying the differential immune responses of different individuals to various antigens [45]. Evidence consistently shows that HLA-DRB1*04:06 prevalence exceeds 15% in East Asian populations (Han, Manchu) versus < 1% in Caucasians, with its unique serine-37 residue enhancing binding to thiol-modified insulin epitopes [5]. This creates a synergistic risk with thiol-containing drugs, such as methimazole, as demonstrated by studies reporting 92% of Asian methimazole-induced IAS cases occurring in HLA-DRB1*04:06 carriers [11]. In Western populations, susceptibility patterns diverge significantly. HLA-DRB1*04:03 is the predominant risk allele, accounting for 67% of α-lipoic acid-induced IAS cases across European and American cohorts [46]. Although HLA-DRB1*04:03 is reported in 75% of cases associated with clopidogrel, its broad geographical distribution (documented in China, France, and America) contrasts sharply with region-specific drug-allele associations observed with methimazole and α-lipoic acid [11, 22, 47]. This suggests that the immunogenicity of clopidogrel may involve a different mechanism from that of thiol-triggered IAS.

Clinical implications

Level 1 agents: mandatory monitoring protocols

Non-diabetic hypoglycemia arises from various causes and poses diagnostic challenges owing to the lack of unified guidelines [17]. A recent study that listed common HH-associated drugs highlighted the importance of identifying and preventing drug-associated HH [6]. The persistent disparity between hypoglycemia reports and confirmed IAS cases (as exemplified by amlodipine with 275 hypoglycemia reports versus three IAS reports) signals widespread underdiagnosis, likely reflecting limited access to insulin autoantibody testing. IAS should be written into the medication guidance of the drug at level 1 as a rare but serious side effect to attract the attention of clinicians. For level 1 agents (clopidogrel, captopril, PPIs), clinicians should prioritize hypoglycemia vigilance through enhanced glucose monitoring in high-risk demographics (e.g., elderly Asian patients) during the initial 2–8 weeks of therapy, with patients instructed to recognize early symptoms (sweating, tremors, confusion), carry fast-acting carbohydrates, and seek immediate medical help if symptoms persist for > 10 min after sugar intake [17]. To address reporting disparities stemming from genetic and diagnostic variations, we recommend implementing ethnicity-aware vigilance protocols that prioritize HLA-DRB1*04:06 screening for Asians receiving thiol drugs and HLA-DRB1*04:03 screening for Westerners using α-lipoic acid, standardizing diagnostic criteria to include insulin autoantibody testing with targeted HLA subtyping, and establishing global registries to capture haplotype-drug interactions.

Level 2 agents: targeted surveillance approaches

Level 2 agents warrant tailored precautions: for 2A/B agents, baseline metabolic assessment is recommended; for 2 C agents (sildenafil, diltiazem), clinicians should acknowledge the hypothetical nature of these signals. Level 3 agents (cilostazol and famotidine) require no specific hypoglycemia monitoring, but merit inclusion in routine pharmacovigilance reporting.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, despite systematic efforts to eliminate duplicate entries through CASEID matching, residual redundancy may persist, owing to incomplete case narratives in the FAERS database (e.g., sildenafil). Such redundancy may inflate false-positive signals, particularly for low-volume drug-event associations. Second, as FAERS predominantly captures AEs reported for FDA-approved drugs through voluntary submissions, this introduces a potential underrepresentation of non-US cases and geographic bias. Third, the diagnostic complexity of IAS exacerbates these limitations, because misattribution of the primary causative agent may occur when clinicians prioritize concomitant medications over less familiar drugs. Fourth, while disproportionality analysis detects statistical drug-AE signals, it cannot establish causality or quantify actual incidence rates. Fifth, FAERS data may overrepresent males due to region-specific drug utilization (e.g., clopidogrel in cardiovascular prevention) and underreport mild cases in females. Sixth, the absence of HLA genotyping in FAERS limits direct mechanistic validation, although the literature supports observed pharmacogenetic trends. This gap is particularly significant for clopidogrel-associated IAS, where limited case numbers in literature reports (n = 4 genotyped cases) necessitate cautious interpretation of the apparent pan-ethnic HLA-DRB1*04:03 distribution. Finally, frequent polypharmacy in reported cases introduces attribution uncertainty, particularly for non-thiol agents such as amlodipine, for which established mechanisms are lacking. Concomitant medication noise may obscure authentic signal-drug relationships and complicate the mechanistic interpretation of atypical IAS triggers.

Future research directions

Future research on novel pharmacovigilance signals, including bevacizumab, sitagliptin, amlodipine, olmesartan, glimepiride, candesartan, isoniazid, and meropenem, must prioritize mechanistic clarification through laboratory studies of drug-insulin interactions, clinical validation via multicenter monitoring of hypoglycemia incidence during therapy, and genetic susceptibility characterization using case–control designs. Concurrently, the clinical implementation of standardized IAS diagnostics remains feasible by modifying existing hypoglycemia protocols. This requires integrating insulin autoantibody testing into routine evaluations of unexplained hypoglycemia occurring during high-risk drug exposure, with a particular urgency for elderly patients initiating thiol-containing agents or proton pump inhibitors.

Conclusion

This comprehensive pharmacovigilance study established the first evidence-based hierarchy for IAS, identifying 58 agents with potential associations. Clopidogrel, captopril, omeprazole, methimazole, and esomeprazole emerged as the most frequently reported IAS inducers, with thiol-containing drugs demonstrating the strongest mechanistic links. Novel pharmacovigilance signals were identified for bevacizumab, sitagliptin, amlodipine, olmesartan, glimepiride, and meropenem, previously uncharacterized associations warranting prioritized clinical assessment. Epidemiologically, IAS predominantly affects the older population (median age 66 years), with a male predominance reflecting sex-specific prescribing patterns of high-risk cardiovascular agents. Significant global underreporting persists due to diagnostic complexity, as evidenced by widespread misclassification of IAS as hypoglycemia and regional reporting disparities. The developed three-tiered evidence framework, which integrates methodological concordance, case counts, and literature validation, provides a robust tool for risk prioritization, urging heightened clinical vigilance for unexplained hypoglycemia during therapy with high-risk agents, particularly thiol-containing drugs. Standardization of diagnostic criteria and enhanced pharmacogenetic profiling represent an urgent need for improving the detection and elucidation of gene-drug interactions.
