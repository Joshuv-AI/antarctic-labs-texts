Neonatal outcomes among pregnant women with COVID-19: a systematic scoping review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yongqiu Li, Ying Lu, Huilin Tang, Eliot A. Spector, Xiaoxiao Wen, Kimberly Germinal, Alexandra Milfort, Yi Guo, Sarah Bost, Elizabeth Shenkman, Jiang Bian, Hui Hu, Jingchuan Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent findings on the neonatal outcomes among pregnant women infected with COVID-19 remain inconclusive. The purpose of this systematic scoping review and meta-analysis was to summarize the literature regarding this topic and provide an overview of the methodologies and results present in current research.MethodsPubMed was searched up to December 2022 to identify observational studies that reported neonatal outcomes among children delivered by mothers diagnosed with COVID-19 during pregnancy. Outcomes of interest included vertical transmission to neonates, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, and neonatal death. Qualitative analysis and meta-analysis were applied to summarize and synthesize the results.ResultsOut of an initial selection of 13,387 studies, 187 were included in this systematic scoping review. There was high heterogeneity in the epidemiologic study design, sample size, and outcomes of interest. Most studies focused on neonatal outcomes from birth to day 14 rather than the full neonatal period. Conflicting conclusions were drawn regarding outcomes among neonates delivered by COVID-19-positive mothers. Results of meta-analysis revealed that maternal COVID-19 infection was moderately associated with the risk of vertical transmission to neonates (Incidence Rate [IR], 2.66%; 95%CI, 2.11–3.35%), neonatal intensive care unit admission (IR, 16.43%; 95%CI, 14.59–18.45%), and neonatal death (IR, 1.29%; 95%CI, 0.95–1.74%), and these risks seemed to be increased with the severity of maternal COVID-19.ConclusionsThis review identified heterogeneity in the epidemiologic studies. Maternal COVID-19 infection was associated with the risk of adverse neonatal outcomes and these risks seemed to be increased with the severity of maternal COVID-19.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08090-1.

Background

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), has developed into a global pandemic since the first case was reported in December 2019. As of November 2021, over 262 million cases have been diagnosed and 5.2 million lives have been lost globally [1]. Pregnant women are vulnerable to viral respiratory infection due to the physiological changes during pregnancy that affect the immune system and cardiopulmonary function. Moreover, COVID-19 may have the potential to transmit vertically from infected mothers to newborn infants through various pathways: transmission across the placenta, transmission during the birthing process, and transmission after birth [2]. As concerns rose over the safety of pregnant women and their newborns, a growing body of research and subsequent reviews have been conducted to investigate the effects of COVID-19 infection on pregnancy and birth outcomes. Recent systematic reviews have reported that pregnant women with COVID-19 are at increased risk of preterm delivery [3, 4], cesarean section [3, 4], preeclampsia [4, 5], and low birthweight of newborns [3, 4].

Yet, there is a paucity of reviews on the outcomes of neonates born to mothers infected with COVID-19 during pregnancy, especially those with follow-up after birth. To our knowledge, most existing studies have focused on pregnancy outcomes and neonatal findings are either limited or conflicted across studies. Additionally, some limitations were noted in the existing literature, including small sample sizes from case reports or case series, different criteria for diagnoses, different inclusion criteria for testing, and different designs of comparison groups. Conflicting findings regarding COVID-19 and adverse neonatal and early childhood outcomes can complicate the development of healthcare policies and future research directions. Therefore, the purpose of this systematic scoping review and meta-analysis was to map the extent and scope of the available evidence regarding outcomes among neonates born to mothers infected with COVID-19 during pregnancy, provide a summary description of the results, and inform potential knowledge gaps as well as implications for future research.

Methods

This review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [6].

Eligibility criteria

Only studies from January 2020 and onwards were included given the timeframe of the COVID-19 pandemic. Studies were included only if they reported outcomes among neonates delivered by mothers diagnosed with COVID-19 during pregnancy (e.g., neonatal outcomes such as neonatal death or neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, or findings on vertical transmission). Cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional studies were the only study designs allowed for inclusion, and thus publications under the following categories were excluded: correspondence, letter to editors, commentary, comments, editorial, brief communication, reviews, or protocols. Additionally, due to a high volume of case reports and series in midst of the COVID-19 pandemic, and high overlap of such studies in previous reviews, these publications were also excluded. Only publications in English were allowed. No restrictions were placed on the temporality of study designs; both retrospective and prospective studies were included [7].

Information sources and search strategy

The PubMed database was searched in December 2022. Additionally, citations of relevant reviews were screened for potentially eligible articles. A team of two reviewers (YQL, YL) and a project lead (HT) iteratively refined the search strategy. The search strategies for PubMed were present in Supplemental Table S1. The query was developed based on reviews of similar outcomes, as well as inputs from domain experts.

Selection of studies

Identified citations were imported into the Covidence systematic review software for screening and review [8]. During all stages of the selection process (title/abstract screening and full-text review), two reviewers were assigned per each publication. Disagreements during abstract screening were resolved by either the project lead (HH) or a third reviewer (XW). Two reviewers (ES and YQL, or YQL and YL) screened titles and abstracts for relevant studies. After title and abstract screening, we retrieved the full texts for further evaluation by a total of six reviewers (ES, YQL, XW, YL, KG, AM).

Data extraction

The data was extracted by the reviewers (ES, YQL, XW, YL, KG, and AM). One reviewer independently extracted the data from the paper using a pre-designed data-extraction form, which was double-checked by another reviewer.

Reviewers extracted data on article characteristics (e.g. publication date, country of origin), methodological characteristics (e.g. study design, data collection period, setting, COVID-19 diagnosis criteria, matching factors or control group information), results (e.g. neonatal outcomes, COVID-19 exposure trimester, other pregnancy and birth outcomes). Quantitative data was extracted, if present in the article, for the following outcomes: neonatal death, NICU admission, and neonatal COVID-19 test positivity rate. These outcomes were chosen because they were found to be the most consistently reported. Results were extracted for the exposure group, control group (if applicable), and the crude effect size with 95% confidence interval (CI), the adjusted effect size (if applicable).

Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence

No critical appraisal was conducted on the included articles.

Statistical analysis

We performed qualitative analysis and meta-analysis to summarize and synthesize the results. Data were summarized for design, country, setting, and neonatal outcome measures. In the meta-analysis, data from each study were pooled for each of the primary neonatal outcomes (death, NICU admission, COVID-19 test positivity). We applied the “metaprop” Package in R to calculate the pooled estimates with Logit transformation and Jackson method for 95% CI for the outcomes of interest using a random-effects model [9]. A continuity correction of 0.5 in studies with zero cell frequencies was applied. Heterogeneity across different studies was calculated using Cochran’s Q test and I square statistics [10].

Results

Literature selection

Of the 13,387 articles retrieved from PubMed, 324 potential studies underwent full-text review after duplicate removal and title/abstract screening. 137 studies were excluded during the full-text review process due to non-English language, ineligible study design, irrelevant outcomes, or publication type. A total of 187 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis. Study characteristics

Of those 187 studies, the majority of the publications was conducted in the US [11–48] (n = 38, 20.3%), followed by India [49–68] (n = 20, 10.6%) and Turkey [69–86] (n = 18, 9.6%) (Fig. 2). Additionally, 13 collaboration studies involved two or more countries [87–99]. Cohort studies were the most common design [11–25, 27–29, 31–38, 40, 42–84, 86–93, 95–179] (n = 164, 87.7%), followed by case-control [26, 30, 39, 41, 85, 94, 180–188] (n = 15, 8.0%). Within the framework of our risk exposure-outcome relationship, however, some of the case-control studies could potentially be considered as belonging to a cohort design. Specifically, four studies were labeled ‘case-control’ studies by the authors, but the cases and controls were mothers with and without confirmed COVID-19 [41, 187, 188], respectively, or infants born to mothers with and without COVID-19 [39], rather than neonates with and without COVID-19 infection themselves. Among the explicit cohort studies, 89 were prospective [13, 18, 23, 24, 27, 29, 31, 32, 34, 37, 40, 42, 43, 45, 47, 55–57, 60–64, 69, 73–75, 77–79, 81, 82, 84, 88, 91, 93, 95–97, 99, 100, 102, 103, 107, 109–111, 113, 115, 119, 120, 122, 125, 126, 129–137, 139, 141–143, 145, 147–149, 151–154, 156, 158, 159, 161–163, 166, 172, 174, 175, 177–179], 72 were retrospective [11, 12, 14–17, 19–22, 25, 28, 33, 35, 36, 38, 44, 46, 48–54, 58, 59, 65–68, 70–72, 76, 80, 83, 86, 87, 89, 90, 92, 98, 101, 104, 105, 108, 112, 114, 116–118, 121, 123, 124, 127, 128, 138, 140, 144, 146, 150, 155, 164, 165, 167–171, 173, 176] and three were ambi-directional [106, 157, 160]. A total of 115 studies did not employ comparison groups; among the 62 with comparison groups [15, 21, 23–25, 27, 28, 34–36, 40, 42, 47, 49, 53, 58, 63–65, 67, 71, 72, 77, 82, 86, 87, 89, 93, 95, 96, 100, 102, 111, 114, 116, 119, 129, 130, 133, 137, 139, 141, 143, 144, 146–148, 152, 153, 155–158, 161, 169, 172, 174, 176–179], the most common comparisons were to mothers who did not test positive or to deliveries in prior years. A total of 101 studies gathered data from single centers [13, 15, 19, 20, 22, 23, 26, 29, 30, 34, 35, 39–41, 44, 47–55, 57, 58, 60–75, 77–80, 82–84, 86, 100, 102, 104, 109, 110, 112–114, 121, 124, 126–129, 132, 137, 138, 140, 142, 144–146, 148, 150, 157, 159, 161, 164, 166–172, 176, 177, 179–181, 184–193], while other were conducted in multi-center settings [11, 12, 14, 16–18, 21, 24, 25, 27, 28, 31–33, 36–38, 42, 43, 45, 46, 56, 59, 76, 81, 85, 87–99, 101, 103, 105–108, 111, 115–120, 122, 123, 125, 130, 131, 133–136, 139, 141, 143, 147, 149, 151–156, 158, 160, 162, 163, 165, 173–175, 178, 182, 183, 194–196]. Analysis of results

The most common neonatal outcomes in the included studies were COVID-19 test result, NICU admission, and neonatal death. Other outcomes such as fetal asphyxia, sepsis, respiratory distress, auditory brainstem responses (ABS) test results, and transient evoked otoacoustic emotion (TEOAC) were also reported. Among the 187 studies, 144 studies [11–14, 16–18, 20, 22, 23, 26, 28–33, 36–40, 42–50, 52, 54, 55, 57–68, 70, 71, 75–85, 88, 89, 93, 94, 96– 105, 107–110, 112–114, 117, 118, 120, 122–124, 126–133, 135–138, 140–142, 145, 146, 149–153, 156, 157, 159–173, 175, 176, 179–195] that reported COVID-19 testing results in neonates born to mothers diagnosed with COVID-19 during pregnancy, the positivity rate ranged from 0 to 34/200 (17.0%). Almost half of these studies(n = 69, 36.9%) reported that no neonates were COVID-19 positive [12, 14, 17, 20, 22, 23, 28, 30, 33, 39–41, 43, 44, 46, 49, 57, 59, 63, 64, 71, 78, 80–85, 100, 101, 104, 107, 108, 113, 118, 120, 126, 128, 132, 133, 135, 140– 142, 147, 150, 151, 157, 159, 164,166–169, 171, 172, 175–177, 179, 184–186, 188, 191–194]. NICU admission rate was reported in 128 studies [12, 14–24, 27, 28, 31–37, 39, 41–43, 45, 48–54, 56–59, 61, 62, 65, 67, 69, 70, 72, 73, 75, 76, 79–82, 84, 86, 88–90, 92–95, 98–103, 105–108, 110–112, 115–120, 122, 125–131, 134, 136–140, 144, 145, 147, 149, 151–156, 158–161, 163–165, 168, 169, 172, 174, 175, 178, 179, 181–183, 185–187, 191–194], including two studies [58, 164] in which no neonates were admitted to the NICU and two studies [89, 126] where the highest admission rate was seen (100%) because of institutional policy. A total of 127 studies [11, 12, 14, 15, 17, 21, 24, 25, 27–32, 34, 36, 38, 40, 41, 43, 45, 49–52, 54–62, 64–66, 68, 71, 72, 76, 78, 80, 82, 83, 85–88, 90–95, 97–101, 103, 106, 108, 110–112, 114–117, 119–122, 125–132, 134, 136, 138, 140, 142–144, 146–148, 150–154, 156, 158–160, 162, 164, 168, 170–173, 175–178, 180, 181, 183, 186–194, 196, 197] reported mortality among neonates born to COVID-19 positive mothers, of which 54 studies reported no neonatal death [14, 17, 27–30, 36, 40, 51, 56, 58, 61, 62, 64, 71, 72, 80, 82, 85, 95, 99–101, 112, 115–117, 119, 120, 128, 129, 131, 136, 138, 142, 147, 150, 156, 160, 164, 168, 170–172, 175–177, 188–190, 193, 196, 198].

Among the included studies, 119 studies (63.6%) explicitly reported the mothers’ COVID-19 exposure timeframe, of which 36 studies included mothers diagnosed with COVID-19 only in the third trimester [11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 28, 33, 44, 48, 49, 52, 54, 70, 79, 85, 103, 104, 106, 108–112, 114, 140, 153, 160, 162, 165, 171, 174, 178, 180, 182, 188, 194]. 20 studies included mothers diagnosed with COVID-19 during the second and third trimesters [20, 30, 34, 47, 65, 81, 94, 102, 105, 116, 119, 146, 147, 151, 152, 159, 166, 173, 185, 193], and 58 included mothers diagnosed with COVID-19 during the entire pregnancy timeline (all trimester) [13, 15, 21, 24, 27, 29, 32, 35–37, 41, 51, 55, 57–62, 64, 68, 69, 72, 75, 77, 80, 84, 86–88, 95, 97, 98, 113, 117, 121, 127–129, 131, 132, 135, 137, 141, 142, 144, 145, 148, 149, 157, 158, 163, 164, 167, 169, 170, 186, 191, 192]. All 187 studies reported pregnancy/birth/maternal outcome measures, including but not limited to the mode of delivery, preterm delivery, Apgar score, pregnancy loss, birth weight, and fetal distress.

NICU Neonatal Intensive Care Unit

Of the 187 included studies, 148 studies reported the COVID-19 test results of 41,453 neonates delivered by mothers diagnosed with COVID-19. Among these neonates, 2.66% (95%CI, 2.11–3.35%) tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 (Fig. S1). When considering the severity of mothers’ COVID-19 symptoms, among neonates born to COVID-19 positive mothers with severe or moderate symptoms, 4.74% (1.78%, 12.03%)tested positive (Fig. S2); while 2.03% (1.40%, 2.94%) for those who were born to mothers with mild or no symptoms (Fig. S3).

Among the 187 included studies, 133 studies provided data on NICU admission outcomes. Within these studies, among 66,243 neonates born to mothers diagnosed with COVID-19 in the 133 studies, 16.43% (14.59%, 18.45%) were admitted to NICU (Fig. S4). And among neonates born to COVID-19 positive mothers with severe or moderate symptoms, 30.32% (21.08%, 41.48%) were admitted to NICU (Fig. S5), while 20.53% (14.93%, 27.55%) for neonates born to COVID-19 positive mothers with mild or no symptoms (Fig. S6).

Regarding neonatal death, among the 187 studies analyzed, 47,674 neonates from 127 studies, who were born to pregnant women with COVID-19, 1.29% (0.95%, 1.74%) were reported to be neonatal deaths (Fig. S7). Among the 1842 newborns born to mothers with severe or moderate COVID-19 symptoms, 23 neonatal deaths were observed (2.41% (1.35%, 4.26%)) (Fig. S8), whereas among the 3626 newborns born to mothers with mild or no symptoms, incidence of neonatal death was 0.40% (0.22%, 0.72%) (Fig. S9).

There was significant heterogeneity among the studies in the above meta-analyses, except for the outcomes of COVID-19 test results and neonatal death among neonates born to mothers with mild or no symptoms.

Discussion

This study is a comprehensive systematic scoping review with meta-analysis focusing on outcomes among neonates delivered by mothers diagnosed with COVID-19 during pregnancy. Unlike other publications, such as Zohra S Lassi’s review [199], which included a combination of case-control studies, cohort studies, and case series, and John Allotey’s review [200], which only included cohort studies, this study exclusively incorporated case-control studies and cohort studies, ensuring a more specific and focused analysis. Additionally, while this study followed a similar approach to other publications by extracting neonatal outcomes and maternal outcomes reported in each study, we mainly focused on three primary neonatal outcomes, such as neonatal death, NICU admission, and COVID-19 test results, to provide a more targeted and concise evaluation. Furthermore, although Zohra S Lassi’s review [199] included over 400 studies, most studies on a similar topic had less than 100 studies included, and this study conducted a search in December 2022, leading to a substantial increase in the number of included studies compared to the majority of previous publications. As a result, this study represents a comprehensive and up-to-date analysis with a significantly larger pool, 187 studies of relevant studies. In the 187 identified studies, we have observed high heterogeneity in the epidemiologic study design, sample size, and outcomes of interest.

The majority of included studies employed a cohort design. Over half of the studies did not include a comparison group. This could partly be due to the rapidly evolving and hectic nature of the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have forced researchers to expedite data collection despite methodological concerns. As a result of increased pressure in the medical community to streamline COVID-19 research, questionable methodological quality has been noted as a concern among clinical trials involving the disease [201]. Limitations in the existing research infrastructure have been made more apparent by the COVID-19 pandemic, which has amplified problems such as a lack of registries for most study types, a relative paucity of original research aims regarding COVID-19-related outcomes, and methodological and reporting issues involving preprints [202]. In the present review, some of these problems are apparent. A deluge of studies was excluded due to the lack of a defined study design. Of the remaining studies, the low prevalence of control groups and multi-center designs was a consistent methodological issue that should be addressed by future research.

Many studies focused solely on or including the third trimester, which indicated that the third trimester is a significant area of research and potentially an essential period for neonatal outcomes. Physiological intensifications during this period may worsen the effects of COVID-19 on both the mother and the neonate, thus raising the probability of negative outcomes such as NICU admissions and vertical transmission. This is consistent with the existing literature, which suggests that vertical transmission of SARS-CoV-2 in utero is most likely to occur in the late third trimester of pregnancy [203]. However, the effects of COVID-19 on the first and second trimesters of pregnancy have been less well-studied. To address this issue, future studies should focus on the early stages of pregnancy and enhance our understanding of the risks associated with COVID-19 in all trimesters.

Furthermore, diagnostic criteria for COVID-19 infection were highly consistent across studies, with most studies using RT-PCR. Many studies that used other criteria (e.g., chest x-ray) were conducted early in the pandemic before RT-PCR was the standard for COVID-19 diagnosis [204]. The consistency of using RT-PCR across studies makes it possible to reliably characterize the virus in different specimens, which is essential for assessing the mechanisms of vertical transmission. Vertical transmission of SARS-CoV-2 may occur across the placenta, during the birthing process (intrapartum), and after birth (postpartum) through different routes of transmission to newborns, such as placental (transplacental) contact with body fluids during delivery or direct postnatal breastfeeding contact, contact with body fluids during labor and delivery, or direct contact during postpartum breastfeeding, among other different routes of transmission to newborns [2]. Although our study mainly considered on outcomes shortly after birth, pointing to intrapartum and early postpartum transmission as the primary focus, the use of RT-PCR in prenatal and neonatal specimens has the potential to detect transplacental transmission if evidence of such transmission exists in the data. Future studies should attempt to explore the specific pathways of vertical transmission to improve our understanding of when and how SARS-CoV-2 is transmitted from mother to newborn, with potential implications for prophylaxis and treatment regimens during pregnancy and delivery.

Moreover, neonatal and maternal outcomes were measured inconsistently across the research. However, at least one of the selected primary neonatal outcomes (neonatal death, NICU admission, vertical transmission) was reported in most studies. According to the previous study [205], there was a low degree of follow-up with children delivered by mothers infected with COVID-19 during pregnancy. Most outcomes reported in this review were limited to 0–14 days after birth. To gain a better understanding of the long-term effects of maternal COVID-19, future studies should focus on increasing the follow-up period for both the children and mothers. Participants from the present studies could be contacted prospectively to examine early childhood outcomes that may extend past the neonatal period. However, well-designed studies are needed to ascertain whether the observed outcomes, particularly neonatal death, were the direct effects of COVID-19 infection, or indirect effects mediated by adverse pregnancy or birth outcomes such as pre-term birth and low birthweight. Regarding neonatal deaths, future research should include multicenter studies specifically investigating the causes of neonatal deaths. These investigations should aim to differentiate between deaths resulting directly from vertical transmission of the virus and deaths resulting indirectly from hypoxic conditions, inflammatory responses, or other complications associated with maternal disease.

Previous reviews on the effects of maternal COVID-19 exposure on neonatal outcomes have been limited by a small body of research, with a focus primarily on birth (< 24 h) and neonatal outcomes, or the inclusion of numerous case series/reports [206–208]. In contrast, this systematic scoping review and meta-analysis encompasses the most extensive collection of literature to date about the research question at hand. Notably, the exclusion of case series and reports adds further relevance to our findings.

The results of the meta-analysis conducted in this study showed that there were adverse neonatal outcomes among pregnant women with COVID-19, and these risks increased with disease severity. Our findings revealed that 2.66% (2.11%, 3.35%) of newborns born to SARS-Cov-2-positive mothers also tested positive for the virus. These findings aligned with a similar meta-analysis by John Allotey [200], which reported a 1.8% rate of neonates testing positive for the virus. Additionally, the result of the study was in line with the research of Zohra S Lassi [199], indicating that overall NICU admission is approximately 23.7% and is more common among mothers with severe COVID-19. It is important to note that there was an increased risk of adverse neonatal outcomes among mothers with moderate to severe COVID-19, which was supported by previous findings. One retrospective cohort study found that pregnant women with moderate to severe COVID-19 were at higher risk of preterm delivery, lower birth weight, neonatal infection, as well as neonatal ICU admission than those with asymptomatic/mild COVID-19 (p < 0.001) [105]. Therefore, early detection and necessary measures to alleviate COVID-19 symptoms would help improve neonatal outcomes.

There were several limitations in this review. First, since we only included cohort, case-control and cross-sectional study designs, our review may be limited in the findings available for analysis. There is a wealth of data included in case reports and series, but these were excluded due to methodological concerns. Second, due to the study design, this study has inherent limitations because the focus is to provide readily available information on the adverse outcomes among neonates delivered by mothers infected with COVID-19 during pregnancy. Third, different institutions have different protocols for handling our primary neonatal outcomes (e.g., NICU admission and COVID-19 test results), which contributes to the significant heterogeneity between studies. For example, at some institutions [41, 209, 210], those born to COVID-19 positive mothers would automatically be placed into NICU. In the case of test results, there was not a consistent standard of testing for neonates between facilities. Fourth, quality assessment was not performed on the included studies since the aim of this systematic review was to provide a broad overview of the available evidence regarding the neonatal outcomes among pregnant women with COVID-19. The results would be useful for identifying research gaps, generating hypotheses, or informing future research directions.

Conclusions

This systematic scoping review was conducted with the aim of elucidating the literature surrounding adverse outcomes experienced by neonates delivered by mothers infected with COVID-19 during pregnancy. A large body of literature was identified, with most of the studies employing a cohort design. Adverse neonatal outcomes were observed among pregnant women with COVID-19, and these risks increased with disease severity. This study found significant heterogeneity across the studies. Further research should prioritize the examination of long-term childhood outcomes related to maternal COVID-19 infection by employing longitudinal follow-up, conducting multi-center studies, and implementing enhanced study designs.
