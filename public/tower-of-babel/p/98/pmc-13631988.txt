Trends and socioeconomic inequalities in contraceptive use, skilled birth attendance, and fertility in Malawi (2015–2024)
Authors: Chimwemwe Ngoma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalawi has made significant strides in maternal and reproductive health, yet the underlying drivers of these shifts remain insufficiently understood. This study examines changes in modern contraceptive use, skilled birth attendance, and fertility between 2015 and 16 and 2024, with a focus on socioeconomic inequalities and underlying mechanisms.MethodsA secondary analysis was conducted of the 2015–16 and 2024 Malawi Demographic and Health Surveys (MDHS), using harmonized Individual Recode (IR) and Births Recode (KR) datasets. Modern contraceptive use and fertility were analysed using the pooled IR sample (N = 46,149 women aged 15–49 years); skilled birth attendance was analysed using the pooled KR sample, restricted to each woman’s most recent birth in the recall period covered by the delivery-assistance module (five years for 2015–16; three years for 2024, owing to a shorter module reference period in that survey round), yielding N = 20,554. Survey-weighted logistic regression models were used to estimate associations for modern contraceptive use and skilled birth attendance, while fertility (children ever born) was modeled using Poisson regression. Interaction terms were included to assess changes in socioeconomic gradients over time. A Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition was applied to modern contraceptive use to distinguish compositional (endowment) and coefficient effects.ResultsModern contraceptive use increased from 45.0% (95% CI: 44.1–46.0) in 2015–16 to 53.1% (95% CI: 51.9–54.2) in 2024. Skilled birth attendance also increased, from 90.5% (95% CI: 89.6–91.4) in 2015–16 to 96.2% (95% CI: 95.5–96.9) in 2024, broadly consistent with the rise reported in the official 2024 MDHS report. Fertility also declined, with women in 2024 having lower expected numbers of children ever born (IRR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.84–0.91). Adjusted models showed higher odds of modern contraceptive use in 2024 (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.27–1.79); the adjusted odds of skilled birth attendance were higher in 2024 than in 2015–16, though the effect did not reach conventional statistical significance (OR = 1.63, 95% CI: 0.94–2.85, p = 0.083). Decomposition results indicate that the increase in contraceptive use was driven almost entirely by coefficient effects, with no statistically significant contribution from compositional (endowment) changes.ConclusionReproductive health trends in Malawi between 2015 and 16 and 2024 show largely positive patterns, with rising contraceptive use, declining fertility, and a descriptive rise in skilled birth attendance that was not statistically significant after adjustment; persistent socioeconomic gradients in skilled birth attendance, particularly by education and wealth, indicate that gains have not been evenly distributed. Strengthening maternal health services while sustaining gains in family planning should remain a key policy priority.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28516-5.

Introduction

Reproductive health outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) remain a main concern for global public health, with persistent high fertility, uneven contraceptive uptake, and substantial maternal health service disparities shaping population dynamics and health inequalities [1, 2]. Despite considerable progress in expanding access to family planning and maternal care in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), SSA remains behind other regions in key reproductive health indicators, reflecting deep structural, socioeconomic, and contextual constraints [1, 3, 4].

Modern contraceptive prevalence is a critical marker of reproductive autonomy and a key determinant of fertility decline, maternal mortality reduction, and women’s health improvements [5, 6]. Although modern contraceptive use has increased globally over recent decades, uptake in SSA remains lower and more unevenly distributed than in other regions. This gap is substantial: globally, the proportion of women with a need for family planning satisfied by modern methods rose from 67% in 1990 to 77% in 2021, whereas in sub-Saharan Africa the same indicator rose from 24% in 1990 to 56% in 2021 – the fastest rate of increase of any world region, yet still leaving sub-Saharan Africa among the regions with the lowest use of modern contraception [2, 7]. Multilevel analyses of recent Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) data across SSA countries confirm that wealth, education, residence, and community norms strongly shape modern contraceptive utilization, with rural and poorer women consistently exhibiting lower uptake [8]. Socioeconomic inequality in family planning access has been highlighted across regional DHS analyses, showing that contraceptive use remains concentrated among wealthier and more educated women, reinforcing broader health disparities [9].

Malawi provides a key example of these regional dynamics. National DHS results from the 2015–16 round indicated approximately a 32%-point increase in modern contraceptive prevalence among women of reproductive age, from around 13% in the 1990s to over 45% by 2015–16 [10]. Yet even with these gains, socioeconomic gradients persisted, with lower contraceptive usage among women with limited education and lower wealth quintiles [11]. Emerging findings from the 2024 Malawi Demographic and Health Survey (MDHS) highlight the continued importance of contraceptive access monitoring, as this survey offers updated indicators on fertility, family planning, and maternal health trends [12].

Patterns of fertility decline are closely linked to changes in contraceptive use, education, and broader socioeconomic conditions, yet the pace and distribution of fertility change vary within and across contexts [13]. Fertility in SSA, while declining in recent decades, remains higher than in many other regions, owing to persistent social and cultural determinants of high desired family size, including community norms and expectations surrounding parenthood and motherhood [2]. In Malawi specifically, successive DHS reports suggest total fertility rates (TFR) have declined steadily over time, though rates remain elevated relative to global averages [10, 14]. Differential fertility dynamics by education and socioeconomic status have been documented in various DHS analyses, with more educated and economically advantaged women demonstrating lower fertility and more rapid fertility decline [15–17].

Maternal health service utilization, particularly skilled birth attendance, is another core dimension of reproductive health that is sensitive to socioeconomic and contextual factors [18]. While many SSA countries have seen improvements in facility-based delivery and skilled attendance over time, these gains are not uniform and remain stratified by wealth and residence, as documented in regional and national DHS trend assessments [18–20]. In Malawi, maternal health indicators, including skilled birth attendance, have historically improved alongside investments in health infrastructure and policy initiatives, yet gaps remain, especially among the poorest and most remote populations [10, 14].

Although many DHS-based studies describe the prevalence and determinants of reproductive health outcomes at a single point in time, fewer analyses examine how socioeconomic patterning changes across survey rounds and the mechanisms underlying these trends [21]. Traditional analyses provide valuable snapshots of disparity but may not fully explain whether observed changes derive from compositional shifts or from changes in behavioral responses, access environments, or health system factors. The Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition framework offers one approach to partition differences between survey waves into endowment and coefficient effects, yielding deeper insight into whether trends reflect shifts in population structure or changes in associations between predictors and outcomes [22, 23].

This study addresses these gaps by utilizing harmonized, pooled data from the 2015–16 and 2024 Malawi DHS to examine trends in modern contraceptive use, skilled birth attendance, and fertility, with a particular focus on socioeconomic inequalities and how these have evolved over time. This study adds to a detailed understanding of Malawi’s reproductive health transitions by combining descriptive, multivariable, and decomposition analyses. It does this by placing national trends within larger regional patterns and highlighting enduring disparities that call for focused policy responses.

Methods

This study utilized nationally representative data from the 2015–16 and 2024 rounds of the MDHS. Analyses were restricted to women of reproductive age (15–49 years). Modern contraceptive use and fertility were analysed using the Individual Recode (IR) datasets, which contain one record per woman. The two IR survey waves were harmonized and pooled into a single analytic dataset, with a binary indicator distinguishing survey year (0 = 2015–16; 1 = 2024). The final pooled IR sample comprised 46,149 women (24,562 from 2015 to 16 and 21,587 from 2024). Skilled birth attendance was analysed using the Births Recode (BR/KR) datasets, which contain one record per birth; these were restricted to each woman’s most recent live birth, yielding one record per woman. For 2015–16, the relevant recall period is the five years preceding the survey, consistent with fertility and contraceptive use; for 2024, the delivery-assistance module was administered only for births in the three years preceding the survey, and the analytic sample was restricted accordingly. The pooled KR-derived sample comprised 20,554 women (13,448 from 2015 to 16 and 7,106 from 2024). The IR-based sample and the KR-based sample are distinct analytic populations and are reported separately throughout.

All analyses accounted for the complex survey design, incorporating sampling weights, clustering at the primary sampling unit level, and stratification. Sampling weights were denormalized and rescaled to reflect relative population sizes across survey waves, ensuring appropriate proportional contribution to pooled estimates.

Variables were harmonized to ensure comparability across survey years. Educational attainment was categorized as no education, primary, and secondary or higher. Place of residence was dichotomized as urban versus rural. Household socioeconomic status was measured using a standardized DHS wealth index, which was re-standardized across waves and divided into approximately equal-sized groups using quantile cut points; this produced four, rather than five, distinct groups because of ties in the standardized index near one quantile boundary. These four wealth groups (rather than conventional quintiles) are used throughout the analysis. Age and region were included as covariates to account for demographic and geographic variation. Harmonization followed DHS recode manuals and country-specific questionnaires to minimize inconsistencies.

Three primary outcomes were examined: (1) modern contraceptive use (current use of any modern method, including female and male sterilization, the contraceptive pill, intrauterine device, injectables, implants, male and female condoms, and emergency contraception), (2) skilled birth attendance (delivery assisted by a trained health professional), and (3) fertility, measured as children ever born (CEB). Survey-weighted descriptive statistics were computed using Stata’s survey commands. Skilled birth attendance was derived from the most recent birth and defined using the DHS delivery-assistance variables m3a (doctor) and m3b (nurse/midwife), each a separate binary indicator; a birth was coded as skilled if either was positive. A separate “clinical officer” category is not recorded in the Malawi delivery-assistance roster in either survey round. The DHS Program’s own documentation states that the reference period for this indicator was shortened in DHS-8 relative to earlier phases [24]. In the Malawi data analysed here, this was confirmed empirically and quantified directly: in the 2015–16 file, the delivery-assistance module was complete for every birth in the five years preceding the survey, with no missingness at any birth age; in the 2024 file, the module was complete only for births up to 35 months old, with both m3a and m3b entirely missing for all older births (36–59 months) – a hard cutoff with no overlap between the two groups. The 2024 skilled-birth-attendance sample was therefore restricted to births in the three years preceding the survey to match the data actually collected, while fertility and contraceptive use for 2024 continued to use the full five-year and current-use definitions, respectively, since those indicators were not subject to the same restriction. This cross-wave difference in recall period is a feature of the DHS-8 questionnaire design rather than an analytic choice and is discussed further in the Limitations.

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate associations between survey wave and binary outcomes (modern contraceptive use and skilled birth attendance), adjusting for education, wealth group, residence, region, and age. Interaction terms between survey wave and key socioeconomic variables were included to assess whether associations differed across time. Fertility, measured as CEB, was modeled using Poisson regression, a commonly used approach for count outcomes in DHS-based fertility analyses. This specification allows for the estimation of incidence rate ratios while accounting for differential exposure to childbearing through the inclusion of age. Although alternative count models such as negative binomial regression may be appropriate in the presence of overdispersion, Poisson models have been widely applied in comparable DHS studies and provide consistent estimates of relative differences in fertility across groups [25, 26].

To assess the extent to which changes in modern contraceptive use were driven by shifts in population composition versus changes in behavioral or structural relationships, a Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition was conducted using logit models, restricted to modern contraceptive use; it was not applied to skilled birth attendance, given the additional cross-wave harmonization required for that indicator, or to fertility, a count outcome for which the Poisson specification already reported is more appropriate. The decomposition partitioned differences between survey waves into endowment effects (differences in characteristics), coefficient effects (differences in associations), and interaction effects. The decomposition was estimated on the same pooled Individual Recode sample as the modern contraceptive use logit model, using probability weights; because the decomposition command does not support the complex survey design (clustering and stratification) used elsewhere in this study, its standard errors, confidence intervals, and p-values should be interpreted as approximate rather than design-adjusted.

All analyses were conducted in Stata 15 [27] with fully reproducible scripts documenting data management, harmonization, and statistical modeling. Statistical significance was assessed at the 5% level using 95% confidence intervals. Given the repeated cross-sectional design, findings are interpreted as associations and compositional changes over time rather than causal effects.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of the study population

Table 1 presents the weighted characteristics of women aged 15–49 in the 2015–16 and 2024 survey waves. The pooled IR sample comprised 46,149 women (24,562 in 2015–16 and 21,587 in 2024). The educational composition of the population shifted modestly over time, with the proportion of women with no education declining from 12.1% in 2015–16 to 7.1% in 2024, and a corresponding increase in secondary or higher education (25.8% to 27.7%) (p < 0.001).

Overall levels of modern contraceptive use, skilled birth attendance, and fertility are shown in Table 1. In pooled estimates, 48.7% of women reported using a modern contraceptive method, 92.4% of women with a birth in the recall period covered by the delivery-assistance module (the most recent birth in the preceding 5 years for 2015–16, or in the preceding 3 years for 2024 – see Methods) reported skilled attendance at birth, and the mean CEB was 2.66. However, these pooled values mask substantial differences across survey waves, which are explored in subsequent analyses. Trends in modern contraceptive use and skilled birth attendance

Figure 1 displays predicted probabilities of modern contraceptive use by survey wave. Adjusted estimates from logistic regression models indicate a substantial increase in modern contraceptive use, rising from 45.0% (95% CI: 44.1–46.0) in 2015–16 to 53.1% (95% CI: 51.9–54.2) in 2024.

Skilled birth attendance also increased over the same period. As shown in Fig. 2, predicted probabilities were 90.5% (95% CI: 89.6–91.4) in 2015–16 and 96.2% (95% CI: 95.5–96.9) in 2024. This descriptive difference was statistically significant in the unadjusted comparison; the adjusted survey-wave effect was in the same direction but did not reach conventional statistical significance once education, wealth, urban/rural residence, region, and age were taken into account (see below).

Multivariable regression results (Table 2) confirm the trend for modern contraceptive use; the skilled birth attendance trend is in the same direction but is only borderline in the adjusted model. After adjusting for socioeconomic and demographic factors, the odds of modern contraceptive use were significantly higher in 2024 compared to 2015–16 (β = 0.41, p < 0.001), while the odds of skilled birth attendance were higher in 2024 but did not reach conventional statistical significance (β = 0.49, p = 0.083).

Socioeconomic patterning and changing inequalities

Socioeconomic gradients were evident across both outcomes, but their evolution over time differed. For modern contraceptive use, wealth was positively associated with uptake, although interaction terms indicate a weakening of this gradient in 2024. Specifically, the interaction between survey wave and wealth groups was negative and statistically significant for middle and higher wealth groups (e.g., β = −0.21 for wealth group 3, p = 0.002), suggesting that increases in contraceptive use were relatively greater among poorer women.

Education showed a weaker and less consistent association with modern contraceptive use. While women with secondary or higher education had lower odds of use in the baseline period (β = −0.36, p < 0.001), interaction terms were not statistically significant. This counterintuitive finding may reflect residual confounding or unobserved differences in fertility preferences, method choice, or access to specific contraceptive methods across education groups, rather than a true inverse relationship between education and contraceptive use.

For skilled birth attendance, education and wealth remained significant predictors. Secondary or higher education (β = 0.98, p < 0.001) and primary education (β = 0.39, p < 0.001) were both associated with higher odds of skilled attendance relative to no education, and the wealthiest group (Q4) showed significantly higher odds than the poorest group (β = 0.43, p = 0.002). Urban residence was not significantly associated with skilled birth attendance once these factors were accounted for (β = 0.35, p = 0.134), and the interaction between survey wave and urban residence was not statistically significant (β = −0.20, p = 0.617), indicating no evidence that the urban–rural gap in skilled birth attendance changed differentially over time.

Fertility trends and differentials

Results from Poisson regression models (Table 3) indicate a significant decline in fertility over time. Women in 2024 had lower fertility compared to those in 2015–16 (β = −0.14, p < 0.001), corresponding to a reduction in the expected CEB.

Strong socioeconomic gradients in fertility were observed. Women with secondary or higher education had significantly lower fertility (β = −0.33, p < 0.001), and fertility declined progressively across wealth groups. Urban residence was also associated with lower fertility (β = −0.06, p < 0.001).

Decomposition of changes in modern contraceptive use

Blinder–Oaxaca decomposition results (Table 4) indicate that the increase in modern contraceptive use between 2015 and 16 and 2024 was driven primarily by changes in coefficients rather than changes in population composition.

The overall difference in modern contraceptive use between survey waves was − 0.078 (p < 0.001). Differences in endowments contributed only a small, statistically non-significant share (0.002, p = 0.099). The large majority of the change was attributable to coefficient effects (− 0.081, p < 0.001), indicating that the relationship between socioeconomic characteristics and contraceptive use strengthened over time; the interaction term was small and non-significant (0.001, p = 0.534).

Decomposition by covariate reveals that wealth contributed substantially to coefficient effects across groups, with significant or borderline-significant contributions from each wealth group, while education (secondary or higher) was the largest single contributor to endowment effects, though its coefficient effect was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that improvements in contraceptive uptake were driven less by shifts in population structure and more by changes in behavior, access, or program effectiveness.

Discussion

This study analyzed nationally representative data from the 2015–16 and 2024 MDHS to assess trends in modern contraceptive use, skilled birth attendance, and fertility, and to examine their socioeconomic patterning. The findings indicate a significant increase in modern contraceptive use, a parallel descriptive rise in skilled birth attendance that did not reach statistical significance after adjustment, and continued reductions in fertility, alongside persistent wealth- and education-related inequalities.

The increase in modern contraceptive use observed in this study is consistent with broader evidence from sub-Saharan Africa showing upward trends in contraceptive prevalence across recent DHS rounds [5]. Although the region has historically had lower contraceptive prevalence than other regions, several countries—including Malawi—have experienced substantial gains over time [28]. Recent DHS-based analyses further indicate that contraceptive uptake continues to rise but remains strongly shaped by socioeconomic and behavioral factors [29, 30]. Malawi-specific studies also highlight the continued importance of access barriers, knowledge gaps, and health system factors in shaping contraceptive use, particularly among younger women [29, 31].

The decomposition results provide additional insight, showing that the increase in contraceptive use was driven almost entirely by changes in coefficients rather than population composition. This suggests that behavioral responses, service delivery, and policy environments have played a more important role than shifts in education or wealth distributions. This interpretation is consistent with recent work emphasizing the role of programmatic and behavioral factors in driving contraceptive uptake [21]. Malawi-based evidence similarly highlights the importance of service delivery mechanisms, including community-based distribution and health education, in increasing uptake [32, 33]. Together, these findings suggest that recent gains in contraceptive use in Malawi are more likely linked to improvements in access and program effectiveness than to structural population changes.

Socioeconomic inequalities in contraceptive use remain evident, although the results suggest some attenuation of wealth gradients over time. Wealth-related disparities in contraceptive use are well documented in DHS analyses, reflecting differences in access to services, information, and reproductive autonomy [34]. The observed weakening of these gradients suggests that family planning programs may increasingly be reaching poorer women, consistent with evidence from multi-country DHS analyses showing reductions in inequality in demand satisfied by modern methods [35]. However, persistent differences across wealth groups indicate that structural barriers—such as access, knowledge, and autonomy—remain important determinants of contraceptive behavior [36].

Education showed more modest associations with contraceptive use in the adjusted models, which aligns with prior Malawi DHS-based findings indicating that the independent effect of education often diminishes when wealth and residence are taken into account [29]. This likely reflects the interconnected nature of education, socioeconomic status, and access to health services.

Consistent with the positive trends observed for contraceptive use, skilled birth attendance also rose over the study period, from 90.5% to 96.2%. This is consistent with the long-term improving trend in skilled birth attendance documented across successive Malawi DHS rounds since the 1990s, and with the 2024 MDHS final report, which similarly describes a continued rise in skilled birth attendance. The adjusted survey-wave effect was positive but did not reach conventional statistical significance (p = 0.083), suggesting the increase, while consistent with the descriptive trend, should be interpreted with some caution. This upward trend is broadly consistent with patterns reported elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa, where increases in facility delivery and skilled attendance have also been observed in recent years [34]. Nevertheless, persistent socioeconomic inequalities in skilled birth attendance are well established, with wealthier and more educated women consistently more likely to access skilled care [34]. The results of this study reaffirm these patterns, with strong effects of education and wealth on skilled birth attendance, consistent with evidence linking women’s socioeconomic status and empowerment to maternal health service utilization [37].

The continued decline in CEB is consistent with long-term fertility transitions in Malawi and the broader sub-Saharan African region. Historically, Malawi has experienced high fertility levels, but recent DHS data have documented gradual declines over time [30]. Malawi-specific studies suggest that fertility reductions are closely linked to increased contraceptive use, delayed marriage, and improvements in female education [26, 38]. The convergence in fertility across educational groups observed in this study further suggests that fertility decline is becoming more widespread across socioeconomic strata.

Finally, the decomposition analysis reinforces the importance of structural and programmatic factors in shaping reproductive health outcomes. The finding that coefficient effects account for virtually all of the increase in contraceptive use, with no statistically significant contribution from compositional change, aligns with literature emphasizing the role of health system investments, policy commitment, and service delivery innovations in influencing reproductive behavior [39].

Overall, these findings underscore that contraceptive use, fertility, and skilled birth attendance operate through largely separate mechanisms. Contraceptive uptake depends mainly on method access and household decision-making, while skilled birth attendance depends on health system capacity and facility access. Although all three indicators moved in a favorable direction over this period, gains in one domain should not be assumed to be causally linked to gains in another, since each is shaped by a distinct set of structural and behavioral determinants. This study thus extends previous Malawi DHS analyses by jointly examining contraceptive use, maternal health service utilization, and fertility within a decomposition framework, providing new insight into the drivers of reproductive health change over time.

Limitations of the study

This study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the analysis is based on repeated cross-sectional data from the Demographic and Health Surveys, which precludes causal inference and does not allow for the tracking of individual-level changes over time. The findings therefore reflect population-level associations rather than causal effects.

Second, although DHS surveys are designed to ensure comparability across waves, and both the 2015–16 and 2024 MDHS questionnaires were based on The DHS Program’s standardized model questionnaires – which are intentionally kept stable across rounds to preserve trend comparability, the two questionnaire instruments were not directly compared to confirm that wording for these specific items was unchanged. Indicators such as contraceptive use and skilled birth attendance may still be sensitive to subtle changes in question wording or reporting practices across survey rounds.

Third, the analysis relies on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly for reproductive health behaviours. However, such limitations are well documented in DHS-based research and are unlikely to systematically bias comparisons across survey waves.

Fourth, modern contraceptive use and fertility were estimated on the Individual Recode (one record per woman, N = 46,149), while skilled birth attendance was estimated on the Births Recode restricted to each woman’s most recent birth (N = 20,554). These samples are not identical, and the skilled birth attendance estimates therefore reflect only women with a birth in the relevant recall period. Critically, this recall period itself differed by survey round: in 2015–16, the delivery-assistance module covered the five years preceding the survey, consistent with fertility and contraceptive use, whereas in 2024 it covered only the three years preceding the survey, reflecting a change in the underlying DHS-8 questionnaire design. The skilled-birth-attendance trend reported here therefore compares a 5-year window in 2015–16 with a 3-year window in 2024; while both estimate the same indicator among women with a recent birth, the change in recall period is a feature of the survey instrument rather than the population, and some caution is warranted in interpreting the magnitude of change between waves.

Fifth, the standardized wealth index split into four groups rather than five conventional quintiles, due to ties near one quantile boundary; results are reported by wealth group throughout.

Sixth, the decomposition analysis provides a useful framework for distinguishing between compositional changes and shifts in associations; however, the results are sensitive to model specification, are not adjusted for the complex survey design, and do not identify the underlying causal mechanisms driving observed differences.

Finally, while the findings are nationally representative of women of reproductive age in Malawi, they may not be generalizable to other populations or contexts. In addition, the interval between survey waves limits the ability to capture short-term dynamics or attribute changes to specific policy interventions.

Conclusion

This study highlights an uneven but largely positive pattern of reproductive health change in Malawi. Modern contraceptive use increased, fertility declined, and skilled birth attendance also rose, from 90.5% to 96.2%, though the adjusted survey-wave effect did not reach conventional statistical significance. Education and household wealth remained strong, significant predictors of skilled birth attendance throughout the period, indicating that socioeconomic inequalities in access to skilled care persist even as national coverage improves. These results underscore the need for integrated reproductive health strategies that address both family planning and maternal care, recognizing that these are distinct outcomes that may require different interventions.
