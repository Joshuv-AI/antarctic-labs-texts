Toward comprehensive palliative care training: addressing gender and training stage differences in medical education
Authors: Alia Alawneh, Sakhr Alshwayyat, Ayman Shatnawi, Abdalwahab Alenezy, Karam Maraqa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPalliative care (PC) education is globally recommended; however, many regions lack sufficient training. This study aimed to evaluate medical students’ confidence in PC and pain management and to compare confidence levels according to year of training and both male and female genders.MethodsThis observational study was conducted at a medical school in northern Jordan. A convenience sample of medical students were asked to fill a survey comprising the Self-Efficacy in Palliative Care Scale (SEPC) and Thanatophobia Scale (TS).ResultsIn total, 289 students were enrolled in this study. In their first year of clinical training, 137 were female and 130 were students. In the SEPC, discussing the patient’s death with the patient was the domain in which the students were least confident. Confidence in working on a multi-professional PC team was recognized as the highest skill area among the participants. Confidence in students’ ability to assess patients’ pain needs was identified as the most confident skill, whereas their ability to prescribe appropriate pain control medications was the lowest. There was a significant difference in TS between the female and male medical students.ConclusionAn integrated pain and PC curriculum in medical schools is essential to help students achieve confidence in pain management and palliative care.Keywords: Curriculum, medical education, palliative care, pain management, students

Introduction

Palliative care (PC) is a crucial part of healthcare, as described by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2020. It provides comprehensive support, including symptom management and psychological and spiritual care, to enhance quality of life for patients with serious illnesses and their families [1]. PC does not prolong life but can enhance quality of life by alleviating pain, emotional struggles, and complex grief, which affect patients [2–4].

PC depends on healthcare providers’ knowledge, attitudes, and skills across multiple domains including pain management, psychological support, and holistic care. Unfortunately, many institutions employ professionals who lack the expertise required to address pain effectively, which remains a critical component of palliative care [5]. As doctors inevitably care for dying patients, it is crucial that future doctors are well prepared for this [6]. Despite the increasing awareness of the importance of PC, there is a gap in medical education [7].

Most medical curricula, particularly in developing countries, lack dedicated courses on palliative care, which contributes to insufficient education on pain management, symptom control (e.g., dyspnea, delirium, anorexia, asthenia, and psychological distress), and end-of-life care during medical school [8,9].

Research on palliative care (PC) in developing countries is limited, and the application of developed countries’ perspectives on end-of-life care is often ineffective [10], because of differences in available resources, cultural values, religious beliefs, and societal attitudes toward death and dying. Three studies were conducted in Saudi Arabia to evaluate residents’ knowledge of PC. One of these studies, conducted in hospitals in Eastern Province, revealed low levels of understanding among residents regarding PC [11].

To provide comprehensive care to patients with life-threatening illnesses, it is essential to integrate PC into undergraduate medical education in Jordan as in many other countries. At Jordan university of science and technology, the medical school lasts 6 years. The official teaching language is English. The first three years are spent studying basic sciences, during which students receive didactic teaching sessions related to medical and research ethics. During their clinical years, they receive two seminars dedicated to pain management and PC. PC clinical rotation is available as an elective rotation.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the level of confidence in providing PC and pain management among undergraduate medical students and to compare between students in their first year of clinical training (4th year medical students) and students in their last year of clinical training (6th year-graduating medical students) and to compare between female and male medical students.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This study assessed the PC education of undergraduate medical students in Jordan. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Informed consent was obtained electronically before the survey and no information was collected. This study was approved by the Jordan University of Science and Technology Ethics Review Board (protocol number: 2023/789). A 30-question survey was distributed to both fourth year and sixth year medical students. The study was conducted between January and February 2024 during the first half of the academic year, coinciding with the early phase of clinical rotations for fourth-year students and the final clinical placements for sixth-year students.

During their fourth year of medical school, students commenced their first year of clinical training, which continued for an additional two years until graduation.

A total of 289 questionnaires were collected from a sample of fourth- and sixth-year medical students.

Students’ confidence and attitudes toward PC were assessed using the Self-Efficacy in Palliative Care Scale (SEPC), and Thanatophobia Scale (TS). This study did not involve the participation of patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of findings. Questionnaires were distributed online through official student liaison communication channels. A total of 417 students were invited to participate (219 males and 198 females; 195 fourth-year medical students and 222 sixth-year medical students). A total of 289 students completed the survey, yielding an overall response rate of 69.3%. The response rate was 69.4% for males and 69.2% for females. Stratified by year, the response rates were 66.7% for fourth-year students and 71.6% for sixth-year students.

Measurements, sample size and statistical methods

We evaluated PC education in undergraduate medical students through self-efficacy in PC scale, which consists of 23 questions regarding communication, pain management, and multidisciplinary teamwork. And we assessed death-related fear using a TS. Each item was scored on a scale of 1–7 points. The interpretation of the score was [1–4] lower agreement [5–7], higher agreement. These scales were validated by Mason et al [12], and subsequently validated in different countries [13–15]. The primary measure was the confidence level in PC among medical students. Secondary outcome measure was the difference in confidence level between medical students in their final year and medical students in their first year of clinical training, and between female and male medical students. All students in their fourth and sixth years of medical training were invited to participate via direct invitations sent to their points of contact through the students’ liaison. We used G*Power 3.1 software to calculate the minimum sample size required to detect an effect.

To calculate the sample size for our study, we chose the chi-square test because of its appropriateness for analyzing categorical data, anticipating a medium effect size based on standard benchmarks in educational and psychological research. We set the significance level at 0.05 and the power at 0.80, which led us to calculate a necessary sample size of approximately 87 participants, assuming a medium effect size in the absence of specific prior data. Statistical analyses were conducted using R language (v4.3.2), utilizing packages such as ‘readxl,’ ‘tidyverse,’ ‘Hmisc,’ ‘data. table,’ ‘Table 1,’ ‘MatchIt,’ and ‘tableone.’ Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were represented using frequency and percentage values. Both chi-square and t-tests were applied to analyze the respective variables, with statistical significance set at a p-value <0.05.

Results

Demographic data

A total of 289 students participated in this study. The calculated sample size of 87 was the minimum number of participants required to achieve the desired power to detect a medium effect size in this study. This suggests that the actual sample size of 289 participants exceeded the minimum required for statistical significance, assuming a medium effect size.

Among the participants, there was a nearly equal distribution of males 52.6%, n = 152) and females 47.4%, n = 137). The participants were divided into two groups: four-year medical students (n = 130) and six-year medical students (n = 159). The mean age of participants was 22.5. Communication

We analyzed the communication skills necessary for PC. Table 2 displays participants’ responses and confidence levels for these skills. Discussing the patient’s death to occur with the patient was the domain in which students had least confident, scored 45.8 (26.1). In contrast, discussing the likely effect of cancer with the patient’s family ranked first among the domains which students reported highest confidence 67.3 (24.7). There was a statistically significant difference between 4th and 6th year medical students in all eight questions regarding communication in PC. The highest communication skill in males was confidence in discussing the likely effect of cancer with the patient’s family 71.9 (22.9), while the lowest was confidence in discussing the patient’s death with the family upon bereavement 50.5 (27.9). Regarding females, confidence in discussing the likely effect of cancer with the patient’s family was the most dominant skill 62.2 (25.7), whereas the lowest was confidence in discussing the patient’s death to occur with patient 39.8 (24.1).

Pain management

Pain management skills were reported by the participants (Table 3). Confidence in students’ ability to assess the patient’s needs was identified as the most confident skill in the overall responses with 61.5 (25.2) for 4th year and 74.5 (19.5) for 6th year, whereas confidence in the ability to prescribe appropriate and adequate pain control medication was identified as the lowest confidence skill between the participants with 53.2 (27.2) for 4th year and 66.7 (23.9) for the 6th year student. There were no significant differences in confidence in pain management skills (Table 3). The lowest skill overall in both females and males was confidence in the ability to prescribe appropriate and adequate pain control medication 60.6 (26.3).

Thanatophobia Scale

Of 4th-year students, 51.5% felt uncomfortable when they began to discuss death, in contrast to 61.0% of the 6th-year medical students. Additionally, 33.8% of 4th-year students feel uncomfortable when a dying patient wanted to say goodbye, whereas 43.4% of 6th-year students report similar discomfort. Of the females, 63.5% expressed feeling helpless when dealing with terminal patients in their ward, and 44.7% of males. When asked about the prospect of becoming a personal physician for a dying patient, 64.2% of females conveyed a lack of enthusiasm compared to 44.7% of males. Additionally, in the context of discussing death with patients, 56.2% of females felt discomfort, while only 31.6% of males reported a similar unease. Discussion

The findings of this study underscore the need for PC education reform among medical students in Jordan. Despite observable improvements as students approached graduation, the scores for 6th-year students failing to exceed 75% indicate an urgent requirement for enhanced educational strategies during clinical years. This aligns with research suggesting that employing diverse and interactive training methods such as simulation, video technology, and case-based discussions can substantially benefit learning outcomes in PC [16–18]. Our results reflect this need, as even 6th-year students displayed limited confidence in several core PC skills.

The challenges in delivering effective PC education are not unique to Jordan. Similar studies from the UAE highlight multiple obstacles, including reduced student awareness, inconsistent clinical experiences, and a lack of specialized faculty, which aligns with the barriers identified in our study [19]. The global consensus suggests a shift toward integrating PC into the core curriculum of medical education, rather than treating it as an elective, to ensure that all medical students develop the necessary competencies in this field [18,20]. Our findings revealed disparities in PC skills between 4th- and 6th-year students, indicating gaps in consistent exposure to this training.

Gender disparities in PC education, particularly in communication skills and the handling of end-of-life care scenarios, were evident, with female students displaying less confidence and greater anxiety than male students. This phenomenon is supported by broader research indicating that female students may experience deeper emotional responses to palliative scenarios, reflecting the finding that female medical students have deeper reflections related to their experiences with death and dying [21]. Such differences necessitate tailored educational approaches to ensure that male and female students are equally prepared and supported in their learning and future clinical practices. Female dominance in the PC specialty suggests that coping mechanisms are indeed adequate for women pursuing this career despite higher anxiety related to death and more profound emotional reactions to end-of-life situations [22–25]. Our results support this, as female students demonstrated lower confidence in discussing patient death, highlighting the need for gender-sensitive educational strategies.

Faculty development is crucial to improving the delivery of PC education. Enhanced training programs for educators can foster better instructional strategies and improve curriculum quality by integrating core PC competencies, such as communication and multidisciplinary teamwork [26,27]. Such programs not only benefit educators by expanding their expertise but also enrich the student learning experience, preparing them more effectively for real-world challenges. Our findings highlight gaps in students’ confidence regarding multidisciplinary teamwork, indicating a potential benefit of further faculty development in this area.

Interdisciplinary approaches have been advocated in the literature for their effectiveness in fostering collaborative competencies among healthcare students. Incorporating diverse healthcare perspectives through interprofessional education has shown significant benefits including improved patient care outcomes and increased professional satisfaction among healthcare providers [28]. These approaches allow students from various disciplines to learn together and from each other, enhancing their understanding of the importance of each role in comprehensive care. Our results reflect this benefit, as students reported higher confidence in team-based PC skills, thus supporting the effectiveness of interdisciplinary training.

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size that exceeds the minimum requirement, which enhances the statistical power and reliability of the findings. The inclusion of both 4th and 6th-year medical students offers a comparative perspective on the progression of PC competencies throughout medical training. Notably, this study is one of the few investigations to examine gender disparities in PC education, providing valuable insights into confidence and anxiety differences between male and female students. Additionally, the study addressed critical aspects of PC, including communication, pain management, multidisciplinary teamwork, and cultural sensitivity, providing a comprehensive assessment of educational gaps. However, limitations exist, such as the study’s reliance on self-reported confidence levels, which may not accurately reflect actual competencies or clinical performance. Moreover, while a palliative care elective was available to final-year students, we did not collect specific data on how many students completed this elective or its potential impact on confidence levels, which limited our ability to assess its influence on study outcomes. Furthermore, subgroup analyses comparing students who completed the elective with those who did not were not possible due to the absence of these data. In addition, the study was conducted within a single country, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions. Cultural factors specific to Jordan might also influence the responses, suggesting that similar studies in different cultural contexts would provide further insights.

Conclusion

Our study revealed significant gaps in PC confidence and competency among undergraduate medical students in Jordan, particularly in the areas of communication, pain management, and multidisciplinary teamwork. Although final-year students demonstrated slightly higher confidence than their fourth-year counterparts, both groups fell short of the proficiency required to provide optimal end-of-life care. Gender disparities, with female students expressing lower confidence and heightened anxiety, highlight the importance of a gender-sensitive approach to palliative education. Integrating PC into the core medical curriculum, enhancing faculty development, and adopting interdisciplinary training models could bridge these educational gaps and prepare future physicians to deliver compassionate and effective PC.
