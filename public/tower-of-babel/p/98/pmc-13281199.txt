Structural Lipidomics Uncovers CC Location-Specific Lipid Signatures and the Response to Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor in dMMR/MSI‑H Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Yiwei Tou, Wei Sun, Pai Liu, Jiuyi Sun, Taiyang Ma, Yun-Hong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipidomics offers valuable insights for cancer research. Paternò–Büchi (PB) reaction-based LC-MS/MS enables precise lipid structural resolution at the CC location level. Although mismatch repair-deficient or microsatellite instability-high (dMMR/MSI-H) status predicts anti-PD-1 response in colorectal cancer (CRC), nearly half of the patients do not benefit, underscoring the need for better biomarkers. Here, we applied sequential LC-MS, LC-MS/MS, and LC-PB-MS/MS to profile serum lipids from 58 dMMR/MSI-H CRC patients receiving anti-PD-1 treatment. The resulting lipidomic data were subsequently explored to identify predictive biomarkers for efficacy. We identified 814 glycerophospholipids, 285 of which were resolved at the CC location level. Comparative analyses revealed 56 differential lipids between patients achieving complete response (CR) and progressive disease (PD) before treatment and 214 after therapy (p < 0.05, VIP >1), most elevated in CR groups. Correlation and clustering patterns indicated coordinated lipid remodeling, and principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated group separation at both the baseline and post-treatment. Key lipids associated with treatment response included PC­(14:0_18:2­(Δ9,Δ12)), PG(31:1), PI(39:7), PG(41:7), LPC(24:0), PE­(O-42:9), PE(43:5), PC­(16:0_20:5­(Δ5,Δ8,Δ11,Δ14,Δ17)), LPE­(18:2­(Δ11,Δ14)), and PG(16:0_20:2) before treatment and PI(44:1), PI(34:1), PC­(18:1­(Δ11)_20:2­(Δ8,Δ14)), PI­(44:0), PG(42:4), PC­(O-16:0/18:1­(Δ10)), PC­(15:0_18:1­(Δ10)), PC­(14:0_18:1­(Δ9)), PI­(16:0_18:1­(Δ15)), and PC­(16:0_16:1­(Δ7)) after treatment. Overall, LC-PB-MS/MS enables deep structural lipidomics, uncovering lipid signatures capable of stratifying dMMR/MSI-H CRC patients by therapeutic outcome. The identified lipid markers hold promise for monitoring treatment and for developing novel therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Cancer is a tremendous global health burden, with an estimated 19.3 million new cases and 10 million deaths reported worldwide in 2020. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) targeting PD-1/PD-L1 have transformed cancer therapy and now are the standard of care for many advanced or even early-stage carcinomas. Despite these advances, the clinical benefits of ICIs remain limited. Response rates to ICI monotherapy are typically below 20%, and even patients who initially respond often develop progressive disease due to acquired resistance. , Mechanistically, resistance was believed to arise from suppression or absence of effector T cells within the tumor microenvironment.

To enhance efficacy and overcome resistance, researchers have focused on biomarkers that identify patients most likely to benefit from ICIs, and besides, pinpoint the complementary pathways contributing to resistance, thereby guiding future targeted therapies. Multiple omics approaches have been instrumental in biomarker discovery. Among them, lipidomics has attracted increasing attention, as metabolic reprogramming is closely associated with cancer biology. −

Notably, the Ω Analyzer system (PURSPEC Technology (China) Ltd.) integrating Paternò–Büchi reactions with mass spectrometry − can comprehensively characterize the fine lipid structure at multiple levelsfrom the headgroup at the class level to CC double bond positional isomers. Such fine-scale characterization is critical as lipid functions are strongly indicated by their structural features. For example, fatty acyl composition influences cell membrane fluidity, stability, and permeability, while CC double bonds modulate membrane elasticity, intermolecular interactions, and susceptibility to oxidation. Oxidized or truncated lipids generated through these processes participate in signaling and inflammatory responses and have been implicated in cancer and diabetes. −

dMMR/MSI-H is the most established biomarker for predicting anti-PD-1 efficacy for CRC patients, with an overall response rate (ORR) of ∼50% among enriched populations. However, nearly half of these patients fail to benefit, underscoring the urgent need for additional predictive markers. In this study, we investigated the structural lipidomic profiles of serum from dMMR/MSI-H CRC patients before and after anti-PD-1 therapy. We aimed to identify novel lipid-based biomarkers that are potentially capable of improving therapeutic outcomes.

Experimental Section

Cancer Patients Treated with Anti-PD-1 Antibody

In a phase 2 clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03704246), 100 patients ≥18 years with advanced (centrally assessed) dMMR/MSI-H solid tumors were evaluated for the efficacy and safety of an anti-PD-1 antibody administrated every 3 weeks. The major criteria for patient enrollment are described in the Supporting Information.

The treatment efficacy was evaluated by RECIST1.1 (Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1). Briefly, Complete Response (CR) indicated that target tumor lesions and nontarget tumor lesions all disappeared. Partial Response (PR) indicated that the sum of diameters (SOD) of the target lesions shrank by more than 30% compared to the basal level, and that the nontarget lesions did not progress, and that no new lesion was discovered. Progressive Disease (PD) meant that the SOD increased by more rather than 20% compared to the smallest SOD observed yet, and/or that the nontarget lesions progressed, and/or that new lesions appeared. Stable disease (SD) depicted the situation that the SOD was between the level of PR and PD, while nontarget lesions did not progress, and that no new lesion was discovered. Confirmed Best of Response (BOR) was the best response recorded from the start of treatment until disease progression. For instance, if a patient achieved PR in the first efficacy evaluation (6 weeks after first dose), PR in the second evaluation (12 weeks after first dose), CR in the third evaluation (18 weeks after first dose), and PD in fourth evaluation (24 weeks after first dose), then his or her BOR was CR.

Out of the 100 enrolled patients, baseline blood samples from 58 CRC patients were collected and post-treatment blood samples were then collected from 48 of these 58 patients. Baseline characteristics of the 58 CRC patients are shown in Table S1. All the procedures related to these samples were compliant with all relevant ethical regulations set by the Ethics Committee of National Cancer Center/Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College before study initiation. Informed consent was obtained from all of the patients.

Serum Sample Collection and Preparation

Serum samples before and after anti-PD-1 treatment were collected from 58 CRC patients. The acquired samples were grouped as follows: 7 samples collected before treatment (BT) from patients who achieved CR as BOR (BTCR), 31 collected before treatment from those who achieved PR (BTPR), 5 collected before treatment from those who achieved SD (BTSD), 15 collected before treatment from those who experienced PD (BTPD), 4 collected after treatment (AT) from those who achieved CR (ATCR), 27 collected after treatment from those who achieved PR (ATPR), 5 collected after treatment from those who achieved SD (ATSD), and 12 collected after treatment from those who experienced PD (ATPD). The serum samples were stored at −80 °C.

Lipid Extraction and LC-MS Analysis

Lipid extraction is depicted in the Supporting Information. Equal volumes of samples were pooled as the quality control (QC) samples to evaluate the system stability. Each sample will separately go through LC-MS, LC-MS/MS, and LC-Paternò–Büchi (PB)–MS/MS analysis and be characterized at the species level, molecular species level, and CC location level, respectively. The detail steps for basic LC-MS are described in the Supporting Information.

Quality Control (QC)

The instrument stability, experimental repeatability, and reliability of data were comprehensively evaluated using multiple approaches, including TIC chromatogram comparison, relative standard deviation analysis of lipid molecular intensity, PCA, and correlation analysis of QC samples. The results showed that the analysis system in this experiment has good stability and the experimental data were stable and reliable, thereby ensuring the effectiveness of subsequent data analysis. For instance, as for QC analysis at the species level and the molecular species level, 14 and 27 injections of QC samples were, respectively, interspersed during the UPLC-MS analysis of lipid extracts. PCA was performed on QC samples and 4 groups of samples. As shown in Figure S1a,b, QC samples clustered together, indicating good instrument stability and reliability of data during the analysis. Moreover, correlation analysis was conducted on QC samples at the species level and the molecular species level, respectively. The results are shown in Figure S1c,d. The minimum correlation coefficient between QC samples exceeded 0.94 and 0.95, indicating a strong correlation among the QC samples. This further demonstrated good instrument stability and reliability of the data during the analysis. Due to limited space, other data involving QC were not published.

Overview of Data Analysis

In this study, the Ω Analyzer system (PURSPEC Technology (China) Ltd.) integrated with LC-MS was used for structural lipidomics analysis. Structural identification of lipids was performed using Ω Explorer software and a built-in lipid database (PURSPEC Technology (China) Ltd.), while data analysis and visualization were conducted via R programming language. For lipid structural identification, lipids were identified at the species, molecular species, and CC location level. For lipid quantitative analysis, statistical analysis and visualization of lipid data at the above three structural levels were performed. The data analysis workflow is shown in Figure S1e, and more details of data processing can be found in the Supporting Information.

Lipid Quantitative Analysis

Since minor alterations were detected in the comparative analysis involving PR and SD groups (unpublished data), only the BTCR vs BTPD and ATCR vs ATPD comparison groups were demonstrated in the following study. Lipid quantitative analysis employed univariate and multivariate statistical analysis at the three levels: the species level, the molecular species level, and the CC location level. Details are described in the Supporting Information.

Differential Lipids Analysis

Through univariate and multivariate statistical analyses of lipids at the three structural levels, this study identified lipids with p value < 0.05 in univariate statistical analysis and VIP >1 in multivariate statistical analysis as differential lipids. These differential lipids were displayed using box plots. Additionally, correlation analysis, hierarchical clustering analysis, and further differential lipid analysis were performed on these lipids. Refer to the Supporting Information for details.

Results and Discussion

Most previously reported lipidomic studies in cancer have focused on diagnostic biomarkers for cancer detection, which included PC 34:1, PE 36:2, SM d18:1/16:0, HexCer d18:1/24:0, HexCer d18:1/24:1, FA 26:0, FA 28:0, FA 26:1, FFA(22:2), FFA(22:4), FFA(20:1), etc. Only rarely has lipidomics been applied in the context of immunotherapy, such as for predicting immune-related adverse events. Compared with traditional lipidomics, in-depth PB-MS-based lipidomics provides an extra dimension of the lipid structure with more information disclosed. For instance, this technique has been used to discriminate drug-resistant cancer cells and identify precise lipid isomer-based diagnostic markers in lung cancer. The significance of in-depth structural lipidomics was reviewed by Yikun Liu, Wenpeng Zhang, and many others.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study using in-depth lipidomics to explore potential efficacy monitoring biomarkers for immune-checkpoint inhibitors. By leveraging LC-PB-MS/MS, we uncovered previously inaccessible CC location-specific lipid alterations that distinguish patients achieving a complete response from those with progressive disease, both before and after anti-PD-1 therapy (see Figures a and a,f and S2 and S6). Our findings revealed that responders (CR) are characterized by a coordinated upregulation of specific unsaturated glycerophospholipids, pinpointing a set of lipids with strong potential as predictive and monitoring biomarkers.

Differential Lipids Analysis

The lipid composition of cells is known to be influenced by their extracellular environment; consequently, serum lipidomics may, to some extent, reflect cellular lipid profiles. In our study, serum lipids with p value < 0.05 in univariate statistical analysis and VIP >1 in multivariate statistical analysis were selected as differential lipids for further analysis. A total of 56 differential lipids before treatment [comprising 5(8.9%) LPCs, 5(8.9%) LPEs, 29(51.8%) PCs, 8(14.3%) PEs, 5(8.9%) PGs, 3(5.4%) PIs, and 1(1.8%) PS] and 214 after treatment [comprising 7(3.3%) LPCs, 5(2.3%) LPEs, 104(48.6%) PCs, 58(27.1%) PEs, 15(7.0%) PGs, 21(9.8%) PIs, and 4(1.9%) PSs] were identified between the CR and PD groups (Tables S2 and S3). The substantial increase in the number of differential lipids post-treatment suggests a dynamic and extensive lipid remodeling process induced by or in response to anti-PD-1 therapy.

Notably, the majority of these differential lipids were unsaturated and upregulated in the CR (responder) group. This observation aligns with the emerging understanding of lipid metabolism in antitumor immunity. Unsaturated fatty acids, particularly polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), and their phospholipid derivatives are key modulators of membrane properties and signaling cascades. Their upregulation in responders may reflect a metabolic state conducive to effective immune surveillance. For instance, Ω-3 PUFAs have been reported to enhance the expression of immune activation markers (e.g., PD-L1, CD3) and cytokines (e.g., IL-12, IFN-γ), while suppressing immunosuppressive factors like CXCL12, collectively potentiating the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in preclinical models. Furthermore, unsaturated lipids are substrates for oxidation, generating bioactive oxylipins that can facilitate immune signaling and inflammatory responses within the tumor microenvironment. A higher level of unsaturated lipids was suggested to increase cell membrane fluidity in the tumor microenvironment, which could boost the therapeutic effect of anti-PD-1 treatment via CD8+T cells, NK cells, and Vγ9 Vδ2 T-cells. , Additionally, DHA was indicated to reduce the expression of PD-L1 in cancer cells by promoting PD-L1 ubiquitin-proteasome degradation and FASN-DHHC5-CSN5-dependent PD-L1 degradation, leading to reduction of PD-L1 and PD-1 interaction, and reversing PD-L1-mediated immune suppression, which in turn contributed to the inhibitory effect on tumor growth.

The major classes of differential lipids identified were phosphatidylcholines (PCs) and phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs), both before and after treatment. This finding is mechanistically significant in the context of PD-1/PD-L1 signaling and T cell function. Anti-PD-1 therapy primarily reactivates exhausted CD8+ T cells. Recent evidence indicates that the PD-L1/PD-1 axis can suppress the expression of phospholipid phosphatase 1 (PLPP1), an enzyme that is crucial for the synthesis of PCs and PEs from unsaturated phospholipid precursors. This suppression leads to the accumulation of toxic unsaturated phospholipids within CD8+ T cells, promoting ferroptosis and impairing antitumor immunity. Anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy can rescue this process, restoring PLPP1 activity, reducing toxic lipid accumulation, and promoting the generation of protective PCs and PEs, thereby revitalizing T cell function. Beyond this direct link, PEs are also pivotal for the differentiation and function of T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, which are essential for humoral immunity, while PCs are involved in regulating the balance between proinflammatory T helper 17 (Th17) cells and immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs). Thus, the prominence of PCs and PEs in our differential lipid profiles underscores their central role in shaping the immune landscape during immunotherapy.

The 56 differential lipids identified at the baseline (BTCR vs BTPD) likely represent pre-existing metabolic signatures that predispose patients to respond or not respond to anti-PD-1 therapy. Among these, PCs constituted the largest proportion, suggesting that the baseline phospholipid metabolism of the host, possibly reflecting systemic immune readiness or tumor immunogenicity, is a key determinant of early therapeutic response. Following treatment, the number of differential lipids surged to 214. This expanded lipid signature, still dominated by PCs but with a notably increased proportion of PEs, may reflect the dynamic immunological and metabolic reprogramming induced by therapy. These post-treatment lipids could serve as indicators of whether an effective and sustained antitumor immune response has been successfully engaged, holding promise as biomarkers for monitoring long-term therapeutic benefit.

Hierarchical Clustering Analysis, BTCR vs BTPD

Figures S18a–c and show the results of hierarchical clustering analysis of differential lipids at the species level, the molecular species level, the CC location level, and all-structural levels. Notably, clustering based on the all-structural levels provided the clearest separation between BTCR and BTPD groups, as this combined data set contained the largest number of differential lipids and captured information missing at individual levels. For instance, at the molecular species level, no downregulated lipids were identified in the BTCR group to aid differentiation, and only one such lipid was found at the CC location level. This underscores the advantage of integrating multilevel structural data to achieve a more comprehensive and robust discrimination between clinical groups.

Clustering heatmap of differential lipids of the samples BTCR vs BTPD at all-structural levels.

The value of deep structural resolution is further exemplified by observations at the CC location level (Figure S18c). Here, LPE­(18:1­(Δ13) was downregulated in the CR group, while its close structural analogue, LPE­(18:1­(Δ15) was upregulated. These two lipids are indistinguishable at the species and molecular species levels, and their divergent expression patterns could be resolved only through CC location-specific analysis. This finding suggests that despite their structural similarity, these isomers may exert opposing functions in the context of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy. Specifically, the downregulation of LPE­(18:1­(Δ13)) and upregulation of LPE­(18:1­(Δ15)) in future responders may reflect a favorable pretreatment metabolic state. The ability to target such isomer-specific pathways could potentially enhance the short-term therapeutic outcomes.

Another illustrative example involves PC­(18:1­(Δ15)_18:3­(Δ9,Δ12,Δ15)) and PC­(18:1­(Δ12)_18:3­(Δ9,Δ12,Δ15)), both of which were upregulated in the CR group. Their concurrent elevation suggests a potential functional redundancy in supporting anti-PD-1 efficacy. This redundancy has important clinical implications: if the two isomers indeed have overlapping functions, selectively inhibiting only one isomeror its upstream enzymemay be insufficient to sustain therapeutic benefit and could contribute to acquired resistance. Thus, understanding the functional interplay between structurally similar lipid isomers is crucial for designing more effective and durable therapeutic strategies.

Hierarchical Clustering Analysis, ATPD vs ATCR

Figure S19a–d shows the results of hierarchical clustering analysis of differential lipids at the species level, the molecular species level, the CC location level, and all-structural levels. Consistent with the baseline analysis, clustering using all-structural levels yielded the most distinct separation between ATCR and ATPD groups, again highlighting the power of multilevel integration. Furthermore, the number of differential lipids identified in the post-treatment comparison was substantially higher than in the pretreatment comparison (ATPD vs ATCR > BTPD vs BTCR). This quantitative increase suggests that the lipidomic landscape associated with achieving a complete response becomes significantly more complex and extensive after therapy, reflecting the dynamic and multifaceted metabolic reprogramming accompanying successful long-term immune engagement.

Deep structural analysis at the CC location level (Figure S19c) again revealed isomer-specific patterns invisible at lower resolution. For example, PC­(18:1­(Δ13)_20:2­(Δ8,Δ11)) was downregulated in the CR group, whereas its isomer PC­(18:1­(Δ12)_20:2­(Δ8,Δ11)) was upregulated in the CR group. This divergent regulation, detectable only at the CC level, implies distinct functional roles for these isomers in sustaining a long-term response. Targeting these specific isomers or their metabolic pathways could provide a strategy to modulate and prolong the therapeutic benefit.

Moreover, as observed in the baseline comparison, multiple structurally similar lipids with different CC locations were uniformly upregulated in the post-treatment CR group. Examples include PC­(18:1­(Δ15)_20:4­(Δ8,Δ11,Δ14,Δ17)), PC­(18:1­(Δ12)_20:4­(Δ8,Δ11,Δ14,Δ17)), PC­(18:1­(Δ9)_20:4­(Δ8,Δ11,Δ14,Δ17)), PC­(18:1­(Δ15)_18:3­(Δ6,Δ9,Δ12)), PC­(18:1­(Δ12)_18:3­(Δ6,Δ9,Δ12)), PE­(18:1­(Δ11)_18:2­(Δ9,Δ12)), PE­(18:1­(Δ12)_18:2­(Δ9,Δ12)), and so on. The coordinated upregulation of these isomer families again points to a potential functional redundancy. This redundancy implies that strategies aimed at a single isomer may be circumvented by compensatory activity from its analogs, potentially contributing to late-onset acquired resistance. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the functional specificity and redundancy within these isomer networks will be essential for developing next-generation combination therapies that can sustain long-term disease control.

Principal Component Analysis

Figure a–d shows the results of PCA using differential lipids in BTCR vs BTPD groups, indicating certain separation trends between the two groups of samples at the species level, the CC location level, and all-structural levels, while the two groups of samples cannot be separated at the molecular species level. This pattern suggests that certain pretreatment lipid biomarkers exist at the species level, the CC location level, and all-structural levels to distinguish anti-PD-1 treatment responder (CR) from nonresponders (PD), while such biomarkers may be lacking at the molecular species level. Due to the scarcity of analogous studies, only several previously reported lipids were available for comparison. In the present study, elevated pretreatment levels of PC­(14:0_18:2­(Δ9,Δ12))a specific isomer within the PC(14:0_18:2) molecular specieswere associated with a favorable response to anti-PD-1 therapy, suggesting its potential role in the initiation of immunotherapy efficacy. In contrast, the broader molecular species PC(14:0_18:2) has been implicated in tumor-promoting processes. For example, elevated levels of PC(14:0_18:2) have been identified as a diagnostic biomarker for early-stage cervical cancer, indicating a link with tumorigenesis. Similarly, higher levels of PC(14:0_18:2) were associated with poor prognosis and increased postoperative recurrence risk in smoking-related lung cancer patients, underscoring its potential role in tumor relapse. Likewise, increased post-treatment levels of PC­(15:0_18:1­(Δ10)) and PC­(16:0_16:1­(Δ7))specific isoforms of PC(15:0_18:1) and PC(16:0_16:1), respectivelycorrelated with improved response to anti-PD-1 treatment, implying its function in sustaining therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, elevated levels of the general forms PC(15:0_18:1) and PC(16:0_16:1) have been proposed as diagnostic markers for NSCLC and CRC, indicating their involvement in cancer development. These findings collectively suggest that lipids belonging to the same molecular species but differing in double-bond positions may exert distinctand at times opposingphysiological functions.

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size, particularly in the CR and PD subgroups, is modest. Although we employed rigorous cross-validation to mitigate overfitting, our findings require validation in larger, multicenter prospective cohorts. Second, due to the immature interdisciplinary public databases involving lipidomics and proteomics, we currently could not pinpoint the corresponding enzymes based on the differential lipids identified in this study. Thus, future work focused on validating these markers and deciphering their mechanistic roles will be essential for translating these findings into clinical practice and developing novel metabolism-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

Our deep structural lipidomics via LC-PB-MS/MS has successfully moved beyond canonical lipid profiling to reveal CC location-specific lipid signatures that are intimately linked to ICI outcomes in dMMR/MSI-H CRC. We identified a panel of lipids, including specific phosphatidylinositol and phosphatidylcholine isomers, that effectively stratify patients based on their response to anti-PD-1 therapy, both at baseline and post-treatment. These lipidomic signatures hold significant promise as novel biomarkers for predicting and monitoring immunotherapy efficacy, potentially paving the way for future therapeutic strategies targeting lipid metabolism.
