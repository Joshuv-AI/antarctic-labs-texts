Response to Letter to the Editor re: a phase II study of intrapatient dose escalation of biweekly trifluridine/tipiracil plus bevacizumab for colorectal cancer (E-BiTS study)
Authors: M Wakabayashi, H Taniguchi
Published: 2025

We greatly appreciate the interest in the results of the E-BiTS study, particularly the antitumor effect of intrapatient dose escalation of biweekly trifluridine/tipiracil (FTD/TPI) plus bevacizumab (BEV) in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer according to KRASG12 status.1 We had collected RAS mutational data and conducted a post hoc analysis of the antitumor effect according to KRASG12 status in the per-protocol set (n = 33). Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Four covariates—Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (0 versus 1), number of metastatic organs (1 versus ≥2), previous lines of therapy (1-2 versus ≥3), and previous tumor resection (yes versus no)—were included in a Cox proportional hazards model to calculate the hazard ratios (HRs) of OS and PFS.

Among the 33 patients, 14 had RAS wild-type, 15 had KRASG12 mutations, and 4 had non-KRASG12 mutations. Baseline characteristics did not differ among three groups. The disease control rate was numerically higher in patients with KRASG12 mutations compared with those with RAS wild-type (80.0% versus 57.1%, P = 0.25). Median PFS was 5.5 months [95% confidence interval (CI) 1.9-6.1 months], 5.6 months (95% CI 1.9-11.5 months), and 5.2 months (95% CI 3.7 months-not reached) for RAS wild-type, KRASG12 mutations, and non-KRASG12 mutations, respectively. Median OS was not reached (95% CI 7.3 months-not reached), 17.6 months (95% CI 6.6 months-not assessed), and 11.3 months (95% CI 5.5 months-not assessed) in the same groups. There were no statistically significant differences in PFS or OS between RAS wild-type and KRASG12 mutations (adjusted HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.29-1.51, P = 0.32; adjusted HR 1.35, 95% CI 0.41-4.45, P = 0.62), or between patients without KRASG12 mutations and with KRASG12 mutations (adjusted HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.31-1.52, P = 0.36; adjusted HR 1.49, 95% CI 0.51-4.35, P = 0.46).

In summary, this post hoc analysis did not demonstrate any significant differences in PFS or OS according to KRASG12 status. Although the E-BiTS trial was limited by its small sample size and definitive conclusions cannot be drawn, our findings suggest that intrapatient dose escalation of biweekly FTD/TPI plus BEV provides comparable efficacy irrespective of KRASG12 status. In line with Dr Rizzo’s thoughtful remarks, it is possible that the dose-escalation strategy contributed to mitigating the reduced efficacy of FTD/TPI previously observed in patients with KRASG12 mutations. Notably, prior studies have also reported that under BEV combination, KRASG12 mutations are not predictive of diminished FTD/TPI efficacy,2,3 which is consistent with our results. Collectively, these observations support the hypothesis that KRASG12 mutations may act as a resistance factor in the setting of FTD/TPI monotherapy, but that this effect can be attenuated by BEV and potentially by dose escalation. However, this remains inconclusive, and larger studies are warranted to clarify the clinical relevance of KRASG12 mutations in this context.
