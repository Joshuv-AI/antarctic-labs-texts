Ossification of the Posterior Longitudinal Ligament in a Chilean Population: A CT-Based Prevalence Study in Patients Aged 60 Years and Older
Authors: Julio Urrutia, Hugo Demandes, Nicolas Rotman, Pablo Cikutovic
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The prevalence of ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) has been primarily studied in East Asian countries; its prevalence in Western populations remains to be elucidated. We aimed to determine the prevalence of OPLL in a Chilean population aged 60 years and older using fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography and CT (PET-CT) as screening tool.Methods:We evaluated patients between 60 and 73 years consecutively studied with PET-CT in a University Hospital. We assessed whole-body CT scans in both sagittal and axial views to detect the presence of OPLL. We used the Mann-Whitney test to analyze continuous variables, the Fisher test for categorical variables, and a logistic regression analysis to determine the independent effect of age and sex on the presence of OPLL.Results:We studied 1,009 patients (median age = 66 years); 597 were males (59.17%). Nineteen patients had OPLL, with a prevalence of 1.88% (1.04 to 2.72). OPLL was more prevalent in men (2.68%) than in women (0.73%; P = 0.02). All cases presented cervical involvement; only one had cervical and thoracic OPLL. The median age of patients with OPLL (64 years) and without OPLL (66 years) was not statistically different (P = 0.09). Male sex independently influenced the presence of OPLL (odds ratio = 3.85 [1.11 to 13.33]), age did not (odds ratio = 0.89 [0.78 to 1.02]).Discussion:This is the first study evaluating the prevalence of OPLL in Latin America. It shows a prevalence alike that in non-Asian populations in the United States and lower than the prevalence described in Asian populations.

Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament is (OPLL) is a hyperostotic condition characterized by bone formation of the posterior longitudinal ligament. OPLL can lead to spinal stenosis and spinal cord compression, resulting in neurological involvement, including myelopathy, which has been well-described in Japan and other East Asian populations.1 The prevalence of OPLL has primarily been studied in East Asian countries, but its prevalence in Western populations remains to be elucidated. In Asian countries, the prevalence varies from 4.1% to 8.9%,2-5 whereas in Western countries, very few studies have been conducted, with reported prevalences ranging from 1.8% to 2.5%.6,7 Interestingly, the prevalence of OPLL appears to be more closely related to ethnicity than to geographic or environmental factors, as evidenced by a study in the United States showing that the prevalence among Asian Americans was twice that of other ethnic groups in the same country.6 To our knowledge, no studies evaluating the prevalence of OPLL have been conducted in Latin American countries.

The first studies assessing OPLL prevalence relied on plain radiographs8-10; however, the diagnostic accuracy of this tool is low. Although CT scans are the benchmark imaging modality for detecting OPLL, their use in prevalence studies is limited due to the radiation exposure involved because research strategies should employ a screening tool independent of the presence of spinal symptoms. Recently, a few studies conducted in East Asian countries have used whole-body positron emission tomography and CT (PET-CT) to evaluate the prevalence of OPLL, reporting rates of 6.3% in Japan,2 4.1% in China,3 and 8.9% in South Korea.4 The advantage of using PET-CT is its ability to assess spinal conditions unrelated to spine symptoms, allowing for accurate imaging of the cervical and thoracic spine, in both sagittal and axial views2-4 (Figure 1).

The aim of our study was to determine the prevalence of OPLL in the cervical (C-OPLL) and thoracic (T-OPLL) spine in a Chilean population using whole-body PET-CT as a screening tool.

Methods

We studied 1,009 patients aged between 60 and 73 years who consecutively underwent fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose PET and CT for cancer screening in a tertiary care university hospital and its health network in Santiago de Chile, from September 2009 to October 2023. We excluded patients younger than 60 years and those with evidence of cervical or thoracic spine surgery on images. In cases of patients with more than one CT scan within the study period, we selected the last examination for assessment.

All whole-body CT scans were obtained in the supine position with a Siemens Biograph Truepoint PET-CT scanner (Siemens AG) using the following settings: slice thickness 3 mm; pixel size 0.4 mm; tube rotation speed 0.6 seconds; beam collimation 40 × 0.25 mm; beam pitch 0.875; tube current 120 mA; voltage 120 kV. The scans were done from the head to midthigh; axial plane images and reconstructed sagittal plane images were read on a DICOM viewer.

We defined OPLL as OPLL with a thickness of more than 2 mm in the axial plane image, as described by Fujimori et al.2 OPLL was classified into continuous, segmental, mixed, and localized types.11

Images were assessed using the following steps. First, one orthopaedic spine fellow and one senior orthopaedic resident independently evaluated the entire cervical and thoracic spine of the 1,009 included patients in both sagittal and axial views of the CT to detect any case with potential ossification inside the spinal canal (including OPLL and degenerative conditions). Fifty of these cases were examined by the two reviewers to determine interobserver agreement, and 2 months later, they were reevaluated to determine intraobserver agreement.

In this first assessment, the evaluators identified 105 potential cases. These 105 patients were later reevaluated simultaneously in a consensus meeting by an experienced fellowship-trained musculoskeletal radiologist and the senior author of this study (a fellowship-trained orthopaedic spine surgeon). This second assessment diagnosed the patients with OPLL using the already described criteria; all these cases were diagnosed by consensus. These two experts also evaluated the presence of diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) in patients with OPLL.

To determine the sample size, we assumed a condition with a 2% prevalence; using an error margin of 5% and a confidence level of 95%, the minimal sample size was calculated to be 753 patients. Statistical analyses were done using the Statistical Program for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 18 (SPSS).

We tested the sample for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Prevalence was expressed as a proportion of patients, with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Continuous data were described as median and range, and categorical variables as percentages. We used the Mann-Whitney test to analyze continuous variables, and the Fisher exact test for categorical variables. A logistic regression analysis was done to determine the independent effect of age and sex on the presence of OPLL; the results were expressed as an odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From a total of 1,009 patients evaluated, 597 were men (59.17%) and 412 women (40.83%). The median age of our cohort was 66 years (range: 60 to 73 years).

Among those 1,009 patients, 19 had OPLL (16 men and three women), with a prevalence of 1.88% (1.04 to 2.72). Men had a higher prevalence (2.68%) than women (0.73%; P = 0.02). All cases in our sample presented C-OPLL, with one case only also showing T-OPLL (0.09% prevalence). The median age of patients with OPLL (64 years) was not markedly different from those without OPLL (66 years; P = 0.09).

Logistic regression analysis showed that male sex independently affected the presence of OPLL (OR = 3.85 [1.11 to 13.33]); however, age was not an independent predictor of the presence of OPLL (OR = 0.89 [0.78 to 1.02]). Four patients with OPLL exhibited concurrent DISH. Regarding the types of OPLL, we found three segmental, three mixed, and 13 localized.

Discussion

Our study assessed the prevalence of OPLL in a Chilean population using PET-CT as a screening tool. We found a prevalence of 1.88% of OPLL, with only one case with thoracic involvement in our sample.

The prevalence of OPLL has been extensively studied in Asian populations.1-5,8-10,12 Initially, prevalence assessments relied on plain radiographs as a screening tool, which reported lower rates than studies using more advanced imaging techniques, such as CT scans. Plain radiograph-based studies in East Asian populations showed a prevalence of C-OPLL ranging from 0.6% to 3.2%,8-10 with lower prevalence of T-OPLL. However, plain radiographs lack sensitivity for detecting all cases of OPLL, particularly small ossifications; in addition, cases affecting the lower cervical and upper thoracic spine may be obscured by the shoulder girdle.

Newer imaging modalities, particularly CT scans, have enhanced accuracy in detecting OPLL and distinguishing it from spondylosis and other degenerative conditions. However, research assessing the prevalence of spinal conditions in large populations should employ a screening tool independent of spinal symptoms to reduce selection bias. In our article, we did not intend to assess bone remodeling or metabolic activity but rather to leverage the CT component of this imaging modality. Using PET-CT scans obtained for various clinical indications, we aimed to conduct a prevalence study of OPLL in a large, asymptomatic population. Given that CT imaging is the benchmark for OPLL detection, this approach allows for an opportunistic and unbiased assessment of its prevalence.

Sohn et al,5 using thyroid CT scans, reported a C-OPLL prevalence of 4.6% in a Korean population. More recent studies conducted in trauma center emergency departments evaluated OPLL prevalence,6,7,12 with reported C-OPLL rates ranging from 2.2% in the United States to 5.12% in India. Notably, in one of these studies, conducted in the United States, Fujimori et al6 found that the prevalence of OPLL in Asian Americans was markedly higher than that in Caucasian Americans. However, this methodology is limited because polytrauma patients, who constitute a notable portion of the study population, tend to be younger and predominantly male, whereas OPLL is more common in male patients and has been reported predominantly in middle-aged or older adults.2,4,5

Recently, PET-CT has been used as a screening tool to determine OPLL prevalence in Japanese,2 Chinese,3 and South Korean4 populations. This advanced imaging technique allows evaluation of the entire spine in sagittal and axial views in patients regardless of spinal symptoms. These studies revealed C-spine prevalence rates of 6.3% in Japanese, 4.1% in Chinese, and 8.2% in South Koreans, with T-OPLL rates of 1.6%, 2.25%, and 1.8%, respectively. Our study, which is the first to assess OPLL prevalence in a Latin American population, showed a lower prevalence compared with these three previous studies using the same methodology, with a prevalence of only 1.88% involving the C-spine, and only one case of T-OPLL, which can be explained by the different ethnic background.

Our findings are consistent with the very scant studies evaluating OPLL prevalence in Western populations. To our knowledge, only two previous CT-based studies have assessed prevalence outside Asia, both in the United States. Fujimori et al6 reported a prevalence of 2.5% with markedly higher prevalence in Asian Americans (4.8%) compared with Caucasian Americans (1.3%) and negligibly higher compared with Hispanic Americans (1.9%) or African Americans (2.1%). Similarly, Bakhsh et al7 found an overall OPLL prevalence of 2.5%, with a 6% prevalence in Asian Americans compared with 2% in the rest of the cohort.

In our study, individual ethnicity was not specifically documented. However, a comprehensive genomic analysis of the Chilean population13 provides relevant context. That study indicates that Chileans (particularly those from the central regions, where our study cohort was recruited) have predominantly European ancestry (∼55%), with a substantial Native American component (∼42%) and minimal African admixture (∼2%). This genetic composition is similar to that of other Latin American populations of mixed ancestry, although individuals from central Chile tend to have a higher proportion of European heritage. Of note, although Native American ancestry may share ancient genetic ties with East Asian populations, the implications of this relationship for OPLL susceptibility remain unclear. Importantly, the population analyzed in our study, from central Chile, is genetically more similar to Western Europeans than to East Asians.

The low prevalence of T-OPLL in our study is unsurprising. Although one study showed that more than 50% of C-OPLL patients also had T-OPLL or lumbar OPLL, this finding pertained to a cohort of Japanese patients.14 Genetic background is a contributing factor in OPLL,15 with Japanese individuals potentially having not only higher OPLL prevalence but also more involvement of areas beyond the cervical spine. In addition, given T-OPLL prevalence is consistently lower than the C-OPLL prevalence, it is expected to find only a small number of T-OPLL cases in our cohort.

In our study, multivariate analysis demonstrated that sex independently influenced the presence of OPLL, which is consistent with findings from previous research.5,6,12,16 Although some studies suggest a genetic component in OPLL etiology,15,17 the exact causative genes remain undefined. Although there is no established evidence of sex chromosomes' role in OPLL pathogenesis, genetic factors involving the collagen alpha2 (XI) locus of chromosome 6 play a specific role in the etiology of OPLL, potentially explaining the increased prevalence in male patients.18

Conversely, age was not identified as an independent predictor of OPLL presence in our study. Although some previous reports have shown notable age-related differences in OPLL prevalence,4,7 these studies included populations with a broader age spectrum compared with our cohort. They included many young patients with low OPLL prevalence, whereas this condition tends to be concentrated in patients older than 60 years.16

In our study, four patients diagnosed with OPLL also presented with DISH (21.05%). The prevalence of co-occurrence of OPLL and DISH has been documented to range from 8.1%7 to 36%,2 with one study aligning closely with the concurrent rate observed in our study.3 Although both conditions are characterized by hyperostosis and are more commonly seen in older male patients, further research is warranted to elucidate the precise association between them.

Our study has several limitations. First, it relied on patients undergoing examinations with PET-CT for cancer screening, who may not represent the general population; however, obtaining CT scans in healthy subjects for research purposes would be unethical due to the radiation exposure. Moreover, OPLL is associated with specific risk factors that may not directly align with those of a cancer-screening cohort; however, this distinction reinforces the validity of our approach. Because this population is not expected to have an inherently higher prevalence of OPLL, it minimizes the risk of overestimation due to selection bias. Conversely, individual ethnicity was not recorded in our patient cohort; furthermore, because of regional and ethnic genetic heterogeneity across Latin America, our findings may not be generalizable to all Latin American populations. In addition, we lacked access to clinical or laboratory data, and thus, we could only evaluate age and sex as variables influencing the prevalence of OPLL. Also, we evaluated patients in a small age range, which may explain why age was not an independent predictor of OPLL. Finally, our study only included patients from a big tertiary care academic center in Santiago, Chile; although these patients may not represent the complete country population, our institution covers an ample range of population in our country, including those with public and private insurances.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to report OPLL prevalence in a Latin American country and the first conducted outside East Asia using this methodology. The prevalence of OPLL is consistent with non-Asian populations in the United States and lower than the prevalence described in East-Asian countries. Given the effect of ethnic background on OPLL prevalence, our study contributes to a better understanding of this disorder, especially in the Western hemisphere.
