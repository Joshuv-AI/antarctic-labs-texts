Decoding missense variants pleiotropy in the immune GPCR P2RY8
Authors: Siyuan Shen, Renxuan Luo, Zhenhua Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) form the largest family of cell surface receptors and remain a central focus in pharmacology and drug discovery. Despite extensive structural and pharmacological studies, the functional impact of missense variation across GPCRs remains poorly understood, particularly for receptors involved in immune regulation. In this issue of Cell Genomics, LaFlam et al.1 systematically map P2RY8 variant functions using deep mutational scanning (DMS) combined with structural biology approaches, revealing pleiotropy and mechanisms linking GPCR variation to B cell confinement and lymphoma.

Main text

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are evolutionarily conserved molecular machines that translate extracellular cues into diverse intracellular responses through precise conformational transitions and partner coupling. Comprising more than 300 amino acids, each residue contributes to the receptor’s structural integrity, ligand recognition, and signaling specificity. Among them, P2RY8, a B cell confinement receptor, has been recognized as a key regulator that restricts B cells within germinal centers, thereby restraining cell migration and proliferation.2 However, the structural basis of P2RY8 signaling and the functional impact of its naturally occurring missense variants in humans have remained largely unexplored. In this issue of Cell Genomics, LaFlam et al. provide a comprehensive functional atlas of human P2RY8 variants. By integrating deep mutational scanning (DMS), cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), and in silico prediction, they reveal that P2RY8 missense variants exhibit pleiotropic effects on protein function and B cell confinement in distinct ways.

DMS, also known as multiplexed assays of variant effect,3 can assess variant effects across multiple phenotypes and cell types and offer direct data on functional consequences (Figure 1). Recently, this powerful approach has been widely applied to study GPCRs. For example, Howard et al. determined the functional importance of every residue in GPR68 activation by generating ∼9,500 mutants and measuring their effects on signaling and surface expression, which provided a framework for understanding signaling complexity of proton-sensing receptors.4 Current variant effect prediction (VEP) methods, though effective for identifying loss-of-function mutations in Mendelian diseases, remain limited in detecting cancer driver gain-of-function mutations and capturing diverse phenotypic consequences of missense variants.5 In this study, LaFlam et al. performed DMS of P2RY8, generating ∼7,000 variants and screening the receptor surface expression, cell migration, and proliferation, thus revealing extensive phenotypic pleiotropy, showing that individual mutations differentially affect receptor expression, signaling, and B cell confinement.1 The authors further incorporated the DMS dataset into computational modeling to refine VEP, markedly enhancing the interpretability of clinical genomics data involving GPCR mutations (Figure 1).Figure 1Deep mutational scanning for functional annotation of B cell confinement receptor P2RY8 missense variantsThis figure illustrates the comprehensive applications of DMS in characterizing B cell confinement receptor P2RY8 variants. By using cryo-EM, the authors first determined the active-state structure of P2RY8 bound to its endogenous ligand, S-geranylgeranyl-L-glutathione (GGG), in complex with the Gα13 protein. Structural analysis revealed two important regions of P2RY8 function: the GGG-binding pocket and the Gα13 interface. The cryo-EM structure shows that both the glutathione head group and the proximal geranylgeranyl tail of GGG are critically required for activation. DMS highlighted pocket residues as mutation-intolerant, and functionally validated variants reduce migration inhibition and proliferation restraint (Figure 1). On the cytoplasmic side of P2RY8, key residues involved in the interactions with Gα13 are realized as signaling hubs of B cell confinement. Taken together, these results explain how ligand recognition and G-protein coupling are translated into the control of B cell migration and proliferation. Moreover, pathogenic P2RY8 variants identified in diffuse large B cell lymphoma and Burkitt lymphoma were shown to impair receptor function and disrupt B cell confinement, suggesting that these mutations may promote tumor cell escape from germinal center control.

This work holds significant implications for future GPCR pharmacology and drug discovery. As mentioned above, a GPCR can be regarded as a complex molecular machine composed of more than 300 residues, each contributing to its structural integrity, conformation, signal transduction, and function. DMS enables systematic functional annotation of GPCRs by linking thousands of missense variants to measurable phenotypes, thereby generating a comprehensive functional map across the entire receptor. Through this high-throughput approach, researchers can rapidly identify residues essential for ligand recognition, conformational stability, and intracellular coupling, thereby overcoming the traditional limitations of classical mutagenesis.6 Moreover, DMS offers clear translational value by enabling rapid evaluation of how naturally occurring or disease-associated mutations affect GPCR function.7 By pinpointing key residues within orthosteric and allosteric binding pockets, DMS data can guide the design of novel allosteric drugs that retain efficacy in patients carrying loss-of-function or altered-sensitivity variants. Such insights hold promise for overcoming the clinical limitations of conventional orthosteric drugs, paving the way for precision pharmacology across the GPCR.8

In summary, LaFlam et al. exemplify the power of integrating DMS, cryo-EM structure determination, and machine learning to decode the pleiotropic effects of missense variants in the immune GPCR P2RY8.1 By revealing how individual residue substitutions influence receptor expression, signaling, and cellular behavior, this study not only deepens our mechanistic understanding of B cell confinement receptor P2RY8 but also establishes a broadly applicable framework for variant interpretation. With the growing appreciation of immune signaling as a unifying language of intercellular communication, highlighted by the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, studies of immune GPCRs are gaining new prominence.9 By linking variant effects to receptor function and immune phenotypes, this work inspires new perspectives on how DMS can illuminate receptor-driven control of immune homeostasis and pathology.

Looking ahead, translating large-scale variant annotations into clinically actionable insights remains a formidable but crucial challenge. Incorporating DMS-derived functional data into clinical variant interpretation and phenotype assessment could substantially improve variant classification, thereby guiding individualized therapeutic decisions.10 Ultimately, the integration of DMS, structural biology, and computational prediction is poised to redefine GPCR pharmacology research, spanning from the elucidation of molecular mechanisms to the realization of data-driven precision medicine (Figure 1).
