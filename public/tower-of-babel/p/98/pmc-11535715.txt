Cohort profile: the PRIMETIME 1 (PRecIsion MEdicine based on kidney TIssue Molecular interrogation in diabetic nEphropathy) cohort – a longitudinal Danish nationwide study of individuals with diabetes and a performed kidney biopsy
Authors: Karina Haar Jensen, Vanja Kosjerina, Ditte Hansen, Frederik Persson, Iain Bressendorff, Marie Møller, Peter Rossing, Rikke Borg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIndividuals with diabetes and chronic kidney disease are at high risk of kidney failure, cardiovascular events and premature mortality and more research is warranted to further improve the prevention and management of complications. The PRecIsion MEdicine based on kidney TIssue Molecular interrogation in diabetic nEphropathy (PRIMETIME) 1 cohort is designed to study clinical characteristics, diagnostic accuracy and prognostic markers in a population with diabetes and kidney disease classified by biopsy.ParticipantsThis retrospective Danish nationwide cohort includes 2586 individuals with diabetes who have undergone a kidney biopsy between the 1980s and 2022.Findings to dateBy combining multiple registries and medical databases, we comprehensively describe the cohort with data on demographics, socioeconomic status, lifestyle, laboratory measurements, medication use and comorbidity at the time of diagnosis of diabetes and at the time of kidney biopsy within types of kidney disease. In individuals with type 1 diabetes, 132 cases of diabetic nephropathy were identified, with 70.5% of them being men, with a median (IQR) age of 50 (41–59) years at biopsy, and a median (IQR) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) 37 (23–5X) mL/min/1.73 m2. Among individuals with type 2 diabetes and diabetic nephropathy (n=599), 76.8% were male aged 61.9 (55–99) years at biopsy and with an eGFR 40 (24–6X) mL/min/1.73 m2.Future plansThe national population registries in Denmark provide a long follow-up period and a distinct opportunity to use superior registry data. This cohort offers a unique opportunity to study the prognostic value of findings in kidney biopsies, the impact of comorbidity and the effect of therapy at the time of kidney biopsy, with a follow-up period increasing over time with running updates.

Introduction

The last decades have brought improvements in the treatment and prognosis of people with diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD), nevertheless, this population is still at high risk for kidney failure, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure and premature mortality.1 Many details about the pathophysiology of diabetic kidney disease are unknown, and the significant variability in severity of the disease and rate of progression between individuals is not fully understood.2

Nordic European countries, including Denmark, have a long history of population registries in national administrative registries and medical databases, which are valuable resources for research.3 All residents born in or immigrated to Denmark have a personal identification number, enabling the opportunity to collect information on the entire population with follow-up restricted only by emigration or death.4

With this unique possibility for data capture, linkage, volume and quality of data, we established a cohort of individuals with diabetes who have undergone a kidney biopsy—the PRIMETIME (PRecIsion MEdicine based on kidney TIssue Molecular interrogation in diabetic nEphropathy) 1 cohort. The cohort was established as the first (explaining the number 1) of multiple work packages within the PRIMETIME study, addressing the need to improve precision medicine in CKD in diabetes by studying biopsy-verified kidney disease in diabetes.

This paper aims to detail the data sources and definitions and describe the cohort at the time of diabetes diagnosis and biopsy.

Cohort description

Study design and participants

The design and eligibility criteria of the PRIMETIME 1 cohort have been described in detail previously.5 In brief, the PRIMETIME 1 cohort includes 2586 adults with diabetes diagnosed between 1977 and 2020, who have undergone a kidney biopsy after being diagnosed with diabetes. Biopsy dates ranged between the 1980s and 2022. The cohort was identified by linking the Danish Pathology Registry to a nationwide diabetes register, DMreg, a register created and maintained primarily by researchers with the purpose of scientific research.5 Details on diabetes-defining information and how types of diabetes were defined have previously been published.6 The DMreg and the Danish Pathology Registry are regularly updated, enabling continuous cohort expansion over time. We previously demonstrated a new way of classifying the type of kidney disease in the cohort by using histopathology codes from the Systematised Nomenclature Of Medicine second edition system captured from the Danish Pathology Registry, and based on this method, we defined types of kidney disease as diabetic nephropathy (DN), mixed disease (coexistence of DN and non-DN (NDN)), NDN, normal histology and ‘could not be classified’.5

Data sources and collection

Several databases and registries, most of them with national coverage, provided available data on demographics, socioeconomic status, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, laboratory measurements, medication use and comorbidity.

Data were obtained from more than one source when possible to enhance the completeness of data. A comprehensive overview of collected data, their definition and sources is presented in online supplemental table 1, and highlights will be described in the following sections. In addition to the data described in the following sections, data on the date and causes of death were collected from the Danish Registry for Causes of Death.7

Demographics

Continent of origin was sourced from the Central Population Registry, which holds information on the Danish personal identification number (CPR number), civil status and tracks migrations.4 The place of origin was based on information on the parent’s country of birth and citizenship, but if there were no available data on the parents, a combination of the country of birth and citizenship for the individual was used.8 Categories of the highest level of education were consistent with the International Standard Classification of Education,9 and data were collected from the Attainment Register with the latest data update in 2019.10 11 Data on occupation were drawn from Statistics Denmark (The Income Statistics Register) and followed their Socio-Economic Classification system version 2013, with the categories employed, unemployed, out of the workforce and others.12 13

Lifestyle

BMI, smoking status and blood pressure were assembled from the Danish Adult Diabetes Registry and Steno Diabetes Center Copenhagen Electronic Patient Records (SDCC EPR).

The primary source in this context was The Danish Adult Diabetes Registry (DADR), a clinical quality database with the primary purpose of monitoring the quality of care in diabetes by clinicians yearly reporting to the database.14 The database started including data from outpatient clinics in 2004 and expanded with data from general practitioners in 2006, with some variance in coverage of data over the years.14 Steno Diabetes Center Copenhagen is an outpatient clinic that specialises in the treatment of type 1 diabetes (T1D) and more complicated cases of type 2 diabetes (T2D). Individuals treated in the clinic are from a geographically restricted area of the Capital Region of Denmark, and as such, information from the electronic patient records was limited to individuals treated here. BMI data from the electronic patient records were available from 1993 to 2022 while smoking status and blood pressure data were available from 1998 to 2022.

Laboratory data

Laboratory data were compiled from the Laboratory Results for Research. The origins of data in the registry are IT systems used in the laboratories’ daily work, ensuring registration as a standard.15 Formally, the register holds complete data on clinical biochemistry from 2015 and onwards. However, some regions of Denmark have complete coverage since 2011 and with sporadic reported data back to 2008.15 Available biochemistry data illustrate that completeness was not attained for all five Danish regions in 2015. Some laboratory data were additionally collected from the DADR, and all variables, except for alanine aminotransferase, were also collected from SDCC EPR. For laboratory data, the electronic patient records covered from 1998 to 2022. All laboratory variables are identified by using the Nomenclature, Properties, and Units coding system, and a list of definitions is provided in online supplemental table 2.

Medication

All data on redeemed drugs were compiled from the Danish National Prescription Registry, which has been considered complete since 1995.16All drugs are classified according to the Anatomical Therapeutical Chemical classification system, and a list of all considered drugs for this profile is provided in online supplemental table 3. Drug classes were categorised as antihypertensives including renin angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitors, lipid-lowering agents, platelet inhibitors and glucose-lowering agents, including glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors.

Comorbidity

All comorbidities described in this cohort profile were defined by the International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) codes derived from the National Patient Registry. Among others, data in the National Patient Registry cover primary and, if relevant, secondary diagnoses related to hospital contacts.17 The registry holds data from 1977 and onwards; however, before 1995, diagnoses were registered with ICD codes from the eighth revision. For now, we do not include any ICD-8 encoded comorbidities; still, some events before 1995 were captured since some hospital contacts include the period before and after 1995. By law, it is required for Danish hospitals to report standardised data to the registry, and this has also been the case for data from private hospitals and private outpatient clinics since 2003; however, reporting from the private sector is scarce.17 18

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases included ischaemic heart disease (non-myocardial infarction and myocardial infarction), macrovascular atherosclerotic disease and cerebrovascular disease (haemorrhagic stroke, ischaemic stroke and transient cerebral ischaemia).

Hypertension was defined as a combination of ICD-10 encoded diagnosis and/or dispensed drug prescriptions, information drawn from the Danish National Prescription Registry. Dispensed drugs from at least two drug classes of antihypertensive medication within 180 days were definable for hypertension, a method previously validated by Olesen et al, who reported a positive predictive value of 80% and a specificity of 94.7% of this method.19 Amputations were defined by ICD-10 codes and were categorised as a composite of minor, medium and upper amputations. Neuropathy was also defined by ICD-10 codes and by using data from SDCC EPR with data available from 1998 to 2018. By using biothesiometry measurements registered in electronic patient records, we defined neuropathy as >25 Volt bilaterally, unilateral if amputated, a definition used in the clinical setting. We found neuropathy data in SDCC EPR on 9% of our population.

Retinopathy was reported as a composite of maculopathy and retinopathy, defined by relevant ICD-10 codes registered in the National Patient Registry, registration in SDCC EPR (data are available from 1998 to 2022) or registration in the Danish Registry of Diabetic Retinopathy. We found data on retinopathy in 58% and 12% of our population in the Danish Registry of Diabetic Retinopathy and SDCC EPR, respectively. The Danish Registry of Diabetic Retinopathy has the purpose to monitor the quality of screening and the development of diabetic eye complications. The registry holds data from July 2007 and onwards, however, with mandatory national coverage from 2013.20

Data on the stage of CKD were compiled from the National Patient Registry (registered ICD-10 codes), the DADR and the Laboratory Results for Research (measurements of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)). CKD based on laboratory data was categorised as no, moderate, severe and end-stage kidney disease, with the eGFR ranges of ≥60, 59–30, 29–15 and<15 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively. The eGFR had to be confirmed by two measurements with at least a 60-day interval. All included ICD-10 codes for comorbidities are listed in online supplemental table 4.

Data management and database linkage

Collected data were placed on the protected servers of Statistics Denmark. Statistics Denmark is an agency responsible for collecting, storing and disseminating data on the Danish population and is driven by the Danish government.21All data were linked at an individual level by using the Danish personal identification number, but data visible to researchers was pseudonymised. Data were checked for completeness and correctness, and unrealistic values were removed.

Descriptive statistics

Individuals with T1D and T2D are described separately. Both groups are described twice, at the time of diabetes diagnosis and kidney biopsy. The acceptable time intervals for measurements at the time of the diagnosis of diabetes were defined in four different ways: (A) for education, any time before and a maximum of 1 year after the time of diagnosis of diabetes; (B) for occupation, a maximum of 1 year before and after time of diagnosis of diabetes; (C) for smoking status and BMI, the time closest to and maximum 2 years before and after the time of diagnosis of diabetes or (D) for blood pressure and laboratory data, the time closest to and a maximum of 1 year before and after the time of diagnosis of diabetes (online supplemental table 1).

The time interval for measurements at the time of kidney biopsy was defined in three different ways: (A) for education, any time before and a maximum of 1 year after kidney biopsy; (B) for occupation and blood pressure, the time closest to the time of kidney biopsy and a maximum 1 year before kidney biopsy and (C) for BMI, smoking status and laboratory data, the time closest to and maximum 2 years before the time of kidney biopsy (online supplemental table 1). The inconsistency in defining the time interval of baseline measurements was necessary considering primarily two conditions. Disparities in the regularity of updates of different data sources, and differences in the frequency of clinical measurements and registrations.

All descriptive data are presented within subgroups of type of kidney disease, and for those with T2D, the overall population is also described. In T1D, the subgroup with normal histology is not described due to the data sensitivity. Rules set up by Statistics Denmark ensure that no microdata are exported from their servers, and the term data sensitivity here covers the need to censure data where there is a risk of impairing the anonymity of individuals.22 For example, less than three observations, calculations based on groups of less than five, and median with quartiles with risk of individual identification were censured. This also means there are differences in rounding and that some numbers are censured and marked as X, or if completely censored marked as NA.

Data are summarised as medians with IQRs for non-normally distributed data, means with SDs for normally distributed data, and as numbers and percentages for categorical variables. If relevant, the number and percentages of missing values are reported. Data management and data analyses were performed in R V.4.2.2.

Patient and public involvement

The study has been presented to a patient involvement group with diabetes. The patient group highlighted the use of registers in research positively, and advice concerning future analyses was collected.

We included 327 individuals with T1D and 2259 with T2D in the PRIMETIME 1 cohort. Discussion

Transparency is a cornerstone of publishing research. This cohort profile provides full transparency regarding the methodology of compiling a wide range of data, featuring extensive repeated measures, on a national cohort of individuals with diabetes and a kidney biopsy. Furthermore, the descriptive profile of the cohort gives an impression of the available data that allow us to study this cohort in the future.

The Danish population is relatively small, with 5 964 059 persons in December 2023. The majority is of Danish descent (84.6%) while only 9.7% of the immigrants and descendants are from non-western countries.23 24

All citizens in Denmark have free and equal access to healthcare, still, social inequality is a risk factor for disparities in health and disease across areas in Denmark.25 26 As discussed in detail previously, the study population is representative of the source population, and we believe that groups of biopsy-verified types of kidney disease within this cohort are representative of DN, NDN and mixed disease in diabetes.5

A review of 48 studies, that presented data on the prevalence of biopsy-verified kidney disease in diabetes, only identified 5 small studies that were not based on kidney biopsies performed on clinical indications.27 The PRIMETIME 1 cohort is also a selected cohort of individuals with an indication for a clinical kidney biopsy, and with the fact that not all types of kidney diseases could be classified, we refrain from interpreting the prevalence of each type of kidney disease. However, studying individuals with clinical biopsies allows us to investigate a cohort receiving standard clinical care in future studies.

A Spanish retrospective multicentre study aiming to study the prevalence and prediction of NDN in diabetes included 832 individuals with kidney biopsies and diabetes.28 They assessed renal measurements and outcomes, fasting blood glucose and death up until 10 years after kidney biopsy.28 The PRIMETIME 1 study has the potential to study continuously updated additional outcomes, such as cardiovascular diseases, with a follow-up period increasing over time. To illustrate, the compiled dataset was updated with an additional 2 years of follow-up (including 2022) since the PRIMETIME 1 cohort was presented in 2023.5

To our knowledge, the three largest studies on individuals with diabetes undergoing a kidney biopsy to date were performed by Bermejo et al (n=832),28 Liu et al (n=1604)29 and Sharma et al (n=620).30 Even if we do not count the individuals whose type of kidney disease could not be classified in this present cohort, we still have the largest retrospective cohort including follow-up data.

The Danish National Patient Registry, one of our primary data sources, holds statutory data on diagnoses related to hospital contacts. Therefore, information on conditions only treated in primary care is expected to be under-reported.17 18 We do not expect this to be a significant problem for most reported comorbidities in this study since they are primarily diagnosed and treated in hospitals. Retinopathy and neuropathy are examples of comorbidities that are not primarily treated in a hospital but outsourced to the private sector. However, the information gap is decreased by using alternative sources of data such as the SDCC EPR and the Danish Registry of Diabetic Retinopathy. Still, we must remember that information from electronic patient records is only available for a part of the population, and the data sources cover different periods.

In general, the interpretation of retinopathy and neuropathy prevalence should be approached cautiously, acknowledging the likelihood of under-reporting, particularly for milder forms.

When using the Danish National Patient Registry, the ICD-10 coding system is essential, and the classification system ensures linkage and comparison of data from other countries.3 However, the system introduces the possibility of variability in coding practice because of variations between departments and personnel and changes in practice over time.18 As such, the use of ICD-10 codes from the Danish National Patient Registry should be validated for each disease and this has been done for most of the comorbidities described in this study.

Validity of ICD-10 encoded cardiovascular diseases

The validity of ICD-10 codes, derived from the Danish National Patient Registry, defining comorbidity has been investigated by Schmidt et al in a systematic review.18 For acute coronary syndrome, unstable angina and acute myocardial infarction, the positive predictive value (PPV) ranged from 27.5% to 100%, with most studies finding PPV over 80%.18 Another study by Sundbøll et al performed between 2010 and 2012 validated ICD-10 applied to cardiovascular diseases. They concluded that the validity of these ICD-10 is overall high when medical records when they used as a standard reference. For subgroups of diagnosis in angina pectoris and myocardial infarction, they found PPV ranging from 88% to 97%.31 For aortic diseases, Sundbøll et al found PPV ranging from 92% to 100% within subgroups, and for heart failure, PPV was 76%.31 Schmidt et al also identified studies with high PPV of heart failure ranging from 77.9% to 100%.18 For overall cerebrovascular disease, stroke, stroke with complications, unspecified stroke, subarachnoid and intracerebral haemorrhage, Schmidt et al found a PPV ranging from 47% to 100%, and for peripheral vascular disease, a single study was identified where the PPV was reported as 100%.18

Validation of ICD-10 encoded arterial hypertension in the Danish National Patient Registry has also been evaluated and one study found a PPV of 92% when using medical records as a standard reference.31 Another study on young men also found a high PPV of 88% when using dispensed antihypertensive medication as a standard reference, but this study held information from 1977 to 2010, and therefore, both ICD-8 and ICD-10 codes were included.32 In addition to the ICD-10 coded definition of hypertension, the diagnosis was defined based on another validated method that includes dispensed antihypertensive medication.19

Validity of ICD-10 encoded comorbidities

To our knowledge, validation of ICD-10 codes derived from the Danish National Patient Registry related to amputations, retinopathy and neuropathy has not been investigated. Validation of ICD-10 encoded amputation was studied in Northern Jutland with 63% of 554 patient cases being correctly registered when using medical records as standard reference.33

A study from 2019 concluded that ICD-10 codes for polyneuropathy and ‘diabetes with neurological complications’ are in concordance with medical records with a PPV of 78%.34 Likewise, another study found a PPV of 82% for ICD-10-encoded diagnosis of diabetes with chronic complications in general.35 The validity of ICD-10 encoded CKD from the Danish National Patient Registry was found to be high with a PPV of 100% in a study looking at moderate/severe kidney disease35 but with less ability to identify CKD compared with the use of laboratory-based algorithms.36 An American study agreed that there is a high validity in using ICD-encoded capture of CKD in general, though, with limited ability to identify the stage of CKD. However, they defined CKD by using ICD-9 codes and not ICD-10 codes as in this present study.37

In conclusion, there is a varying validity of ICD-10 encoded comorbidity reported in other studies. It is important to keep in mind that the studies differed in sample size, periods of coverage, use of ICD version, contact type and what was used as standard reference (discharge summaries, medical records or strict diagnostic criteria).1831 32 3437

The validity of ICD-10 encoded data on comorbidity from the Danish National Patient Registry is generally considered good, however, with a varying risk of under-reporting comorbidities.1831 32 3436

Data coverage

In general, we consider data on medication to be complete since 1995. The fact that pharmacies in Denmark are required to register all prescriptions dispensed to get coverage by the healthcare system (financed by the government) ensures the high validity of the registry.16 Treatment patterns in diabetes and complications have changed over the last 20 years with the introduction of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists. Both drugs were approved by the European Medicines Agency as a treatment for T2D in 2012 and at the end of 2006, respectively.38 39 As such, these drugs were implemented during the follow-up of this cohort and explain the low usage of these drugs. However, the scope of this study is not to address drug effects.

In the latest report of data and quality of treatment in the DADR, data coverage was described as 90.2% for individuals with diabetes treated at hospitals, and 55% of general practitioners reported to the database.40 Reporting to the database from the primary healthcare sector is crucial since the vast majority (approximately 80%) of individuals with T2D are treated here, and thus only part of the Danish population with diabetes is represented in the DADR.

Since none of our data sources cover the entire study period and not all data sources are complete, we do not have completeness of data. However, to some extent, missingness will decrease over time because of the increasing quality of many of the registries. Furthermore, a major strength of this study is the use of several different data sources to decrease the information gaps. The standard registration to registries and medical databases in Denmark for nearly 30 years ensures a large representative cohort with a long follow-up time, increasing over time as we update our cohort. The PRIMETIME 1 cohort is a resource for populations-based epidemiologic research in diabetes and CKD.

Perspectives and future plans

Several existing biopsy registries include clinical data and provide the opportunity to perform epidemiological studies on biopsy-verified kidney disease in general.41 However, to our knowledge, there are no existing comparable national cohorts designed specifically to study individuals with diabetes and biopsy-verified kidney disease in depth or with the opportunity to collect information continuously on an individual level.

The observational retrospective design of this cohort is a limitation concerning inferring causality in future studies, but the scope will be to investigate the prognostic importance of the kidney biopsy diagnosis, the impact of comorbidity, and the effect of therapy at the time of kidney biopsy.

Collaboration

The full dataset is only available to researchers employed at Steno Diabetes Center Copenhagen with approved access to the project and is barred from publication because of confidentiality. The PRIMETIME research group encourages national and international collaboration on similar projects.
