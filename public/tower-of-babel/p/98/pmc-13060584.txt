Measurement-based assessment reveals key drivers and mitigation potential of methane emissions from China’s wastewater treatment
Authors: Chu Sun, Yihao Liu, Philippe Ciais, Gregoire Broquet, Bo Zheng, Haikun Wang, Huilin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wastewater treatment is an increasingly important yet poorly quantified source of anthropogenic methane (CH4). Here we report facility-level emissions based on atmospheric measurements from 105 wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) across five climatic-economic regions in China—the largest dataset to date. We found that emission factors are primarily driven by organic load and concentration. Using updated facility-level emission factors, our analysis shows that emissions from Chinese WWTP, driven by rising organic loads, grew by 12% per year since 2003, reaching 254 ± 26 Gg CH4 year−1 in 2023. However, the rapid expansion of WWTPs lowered the average emission factor for the urban domestic wastewater sector, limiting total emissions growth to 32% over the same period. Scenario modeling suggests that, under current technology, emissions will peak around 2040. Deploying low-emission configurations and CH4 recovery technologies could advance the peak by 15 years and reduce 2040 emissions by 23%. Incorporating such measures into China’s decarbonization strategy will be essential for achieving climate mitigation goals.

INTRODUCTION

Methane (CH4), a potent short-lived climate pollutant, has a 100-year global warming potential 27 to 30 times higher than carbon dioxide (CO2) on a mass basis (1). With an atmospheric lifetime of ~9 years (2), mitigating its emissions represents one of the most effective short-term strategies for curbing global warming and supporting the achievement of carbon peak targets. The anthropogenic CH4 sources correspond to diverse activities, such as fossil fuel extraction, agriculture (enteric fermentation and rice cultivation), and waste management. Wastewater treatment alone accounts for 5 to 8% of global anthropogenic CH4 emissions (3). According to the Emissions Database for Global Atmospheric Research (v8.0), emissions from this sector have increased linearly at a rate of 0.90 ± 0.37 Tg CH4 year−1 since 2000 (4), a trend projected to continue due to population growth and urbanization-driven expansion of wastewater infrastructure (5).

China’s greenhouse gas (GHG) mitigation efforts hold global importance given its status as the world’s largest emitter (6). Rapid urbanization and improved living standards have driven parallel increases in both wastewater generation (48.5 × 109 m3 in 2022) and chemical oxygen demand (COD) loads—a measure of the amount of oxygen required to oxidize organic and inorganic matter in the wastewater (7). This makes China the world’s second-largest producer of municipal wastewater. China has implemented massive upgrades to its wastewater treatment infrastructure over the past two decades. This transformation features two trends: a sixfold expansion of centralized waste water treatment plant (WWTP) capacity (2002–2022) mirrored by a 39% decline in decentralized treatment systems (DTSs) (8). By 2019, WWTPs was responsible for ~65% of the sector’s total emissions (9), despite its reduced emission factor (EF) compared to DTS. These systematic shifts have introduced challenges for accurately estimating CH4 emissions across urban wastewater sector.

Municipal WWTPs include a diverse set of biological treatment configurations (e.g., anaerobic/anoxic/oxic (AAO), oxidation ditch (OD), and biological aerated filter), which differ in reactor hydraulics, mixing, biomass retention, oxygen transfer regimes, and redox zoning (Supplementary Text) (10). These design and operational differences systematically alter the balance between methanogenesis and methane oxidation, as well as the extent to which dissolved CH4 is physically stripped during aeration and other high-turbulence operations, thereby modulating net plant-level CH4 EFs. Given this complexity, the use of fixed, single-value EFs has limited the accuracy of CH4 inventories for WWTPs. Early efforts to quantify WWTP emissions based on direct measurements of individual treatment units with approaches such as point source gas sampling and wastewater sampling (11–13) often introduced errors by neglecting fugitive leaks or assuming spatially uniform release rates. More recent approaches rely on atmospheric concentration measurements with mobile tracer experiments or remote sensing techniques to accurately capture whole-facility CH4 fluxes (14–18). However, these measurements are labor intensive in nature and were so far only performed on a few large plants with a limited representativeness of all water treatment conditions. For example, the reference EFs used by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) are derived from a compilation of only 15 facilities across different studies, using a mix of direct measurements of treatment units and atmospheric concentration measurements at plant level (19). Moore et al. (20) made efforts to perform atmospheric concentration measurements campaigns at 63 US WWTPs, yet their omission of key covariates and the wide EF range observed (2.4 × 10−3 to 1.0 gCH4/gCOD) hindered the development of a systematic, transferrable framework to predict EFs. Consequently, a unified method for deriving plant-specific CH4 EFs and extrapolating to national scale remains a pressing unsolved challenge.

To bridge this critical knowledge gap, we performed systematic atmospheric measurements in plumes of CH4 covering 105 WWTPs in five Chinese cities using a mobile lab, establishing the most comprehensive facility-level CH4 EF database to date. Our analysis of this unique dataset revealed systematic correlations of EFs with plant capacity and treatment process. By integrating these operational variables with local meteorological data, we developed a predictive model for WWTP CH4 EFs. Combined with a database of facility-level activity levels and facility information for different years, we reconstructed urban WWTP CH4 emissions in mainland China (excluding Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan) over a 20-year period (2003–2023). Last, through multiscenario analysis, we project future emission trajectories through 2060 under various socioeconomic and climate policy pathways.

RESULTS

Variations of WWTP methane emission rates and EFs

We quantified CH4 emission rates (ERs) of each site and estimated EFs defined as ER normalized by COD removal flux of 105 WWTPs across five major cities in China (Fig. 1). These quantifications are based on 254 hours of measurements on 44 individual days. Our measurement campaigns were repeated in winter, spring, and summer at 13 facilities in Nanjing. The measured plants represent more than 70% of each city’s treatment capacity, spanning a full range of plant capacities (1.0 × 103 to 1.2 × 106 m3/day; table S2), treatment technologies (21–23), and meteorological conditions (table S3). To disentangle the drivers of EF variabilities, we organized our data along three axes, i.e., city, technology, and capacity scale. Overall, treatment technology and plant capacity both exert a measurable influence on CH4 ER and EF of WWTPs.

The CH4 ERs from the surveyed plants spanned nearly a 500-fold range, going from 0.16 to 73.9 kg CH4 hour−1, with a median (10th/90th percentile) of 6.3 (1.3 to 23.6) kg CH4 hour−1. The EF values range from 0.07 × 10−2 to 0.11 kg CH4 kg−1 COD, with a median (10th/90th percentile) of 1.12 (0.35 to 5.47) × 10−2 kg CH4 kg−1 COD. At the city scale, ERs correlated strongly with instantaneous COD removal flux (R2 > 0.5), although substantial heterogeneity remained (fig. S1). Intercity comparisons also revealed clear contrasts: Xi’an and Nanjing displayed the highest ER but for divergent reasons. The high activity levels tend to explain the high emissions in Nanjing, whereas Xi’an’s high emissions are explained primarily by high EF in the surveyed plants, likely reflecting a greater reliance on the anaerobic/anoxic/oxic-membrane/bioreactor (AAO-MBR) and OD treatment processes which have higher EFs than other technologies (24, 25). A comparison with city-level inventory data (24) demonstrates that while emissions in certain cities (Nanjing and Shenyang) agree with our atmospheric measurements, the inventory failed to capture the significantly elevated emission levels in cities like Xi’an (fig. S2). This discrepancy is likely due to the inventory’s inability to differentiate EFs among WWTPs that use the same treatment process.

The high-capacity, low-EF AAO process has been deployed in major cities (25). In more developed municipalities such as Nanjing, Shenyang, and Guangzhou, AAO plants account for 70% of monitored treatment capacity and contribute more than 50% of total CH4 flux (Fig. 1 and table S4). In comparison, because of their more anaerobic conditions, the anoxic/oxic (AO), AAO-MBR, and “Other” processes exhibit substantially higher EF values (26, 27): The mean EF of both AO and other processes exceeds that of AAO by more than twofold (fig. S3). These higher-EF processes are more prevalent in less economically developed cities and regions, such as Zibo and Xi’an, where elevated proportions of AO, AAO-MBR systems contribute to higher city-wide average EF. Large plants (with a waste water treatment capacity ≥ 60,000 m3/day) display much lower EF than small facilities (capacity < 60,000 m3/day) even with the same process. The mean EF of large plants is only 28% of that of small plants (fig. S4). This may be because large plants have more comprehensive management practices or more stringent odor control systems (i.e., better gas tightness).

Overall, we found that the default EF value recommended by IPCC 2006 is substantially overestimated across all cities and process types compared to our field measurements (28). In contrast, the revised EF in IPCC 2019 is based on a summary of actual measurement data from 14 full-scale WWTPs (from the United States, the Netherlands, Denmark, Sweden, South Korea, and China), making it a more reasonable reference (19). Its value lies at the lower end of our observed range and aligns primarily with the EFs of large facilities or those using AAO processes.

Key drivers of EFs

We built linear statistical models to predict CH4 EF for China’s WWTPs. A total of 21 variables related to the WWTP conditions were used as predictors, including operational activity data (e.g., COD removal flux) and environmental variables (table S1). Of all measured plants, we used data from 81 plants where complete predictors were available to fit three models, each using a different weighting/classification method. After variable screening and stepwise selection (29), we selected the four most influential variables for all three models: (i) COD removal flux at hourly scale (COD_f), (ii) influent COD concentration at hourly scale (COD_c), (iii) treatment-process type (process), and (iv) hourly mean air temperature (temperature), including all pairwise interactions. The best-performing model achieved the relative emission flux error of 13.5% and the R2 value of 0.69 on test data (table S5). This model also achieved good performance under low EF conditions (EF < 0.04 kg CH4 kg−1 COD) with residuals from both training and test subsets symmetrically distributed around zero (fig. S5).

Among the four predictor variables, “COD_f” and “COD_c” are the dominant influencing variables, with mean absolute SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values (30) of 0.006 and 0.005, respectively. The mean absolute SHAP represents the average magnitude of each variable’s contribution to the model-predicted EF for individual plants (SHAP values are expressed in the same units as EF). The modeled and measured data show a consistent decrease of CH4 EFs with increasing COD removal flux (Fig. 2 and fig. S5). This is because larger plants generally have more advanced processes such as AAO (table S2) and better operations (31, 32). Furthermore, we found that small- and medium-scale plants typically exhibit relatively higher EFs compared to large-scale plants, despite low influent COD concentrations. This is because low-COD concentration plants remove organic matter more slowly (fig. S6). This explains the negative correlation between COD concentration and EF shown in Fig. 2. In contrast, conventional older treatment processes such as OD and other processes and higher ambient temperatures contribute to significantly higher EF. Our statistical model also includes the interaction between COD_f and either COD_c or “process” (fig. S5). Advanced processes (e.g., AAO and AO) achieve a lower EF at high loads. The transition point occurs at a COD removal rate of 400 kg hour−1, delineating the operational range for optimal EF for each process.

Our results demonstrate that integrating plant operation parameters (10) with meteorological conditions enables a reliable prediction of EFs of WWTPs. Unlike the systematic overestimation of EF by IPCC 2006, the average IPCC 2019 EF aligns closely with our field measured values for WWTPs with COD removal fluxes above 1000 kg hour−1 (fig. S5) but underestimates values for plants with COD removal fluxes below 1000 kg hour−1. Notably, these plants (<1000 kg hour−1) collectively represent 55.3% of the total urban WWTP activity level.

Urbanization-driven CH4 emissions from Chinese WWTPs

Rapid urbanization and infrastructure expansion have led to marked increases in both the volume and treatment capacity of municipal wastewater in Chinese cities (33). Between 2003 and 2023, the China’s urbanization rate increased by 27% (34), with rising living standards increased per-capita 5-day biochemical oxygen demand (BOD5) generation to 38 g person−1 day−1 in 2023 [1 g BOD5 ≈ 2.4 g COD (28)], nearly matching the IPCC default value of 40 g person−1 day−1 (fig. S7). Because of the relatively low organic matter concentrations, most monitoring datasets in Chinese WWTPs use COD rather than BOD5 to represent organic content. Therefore, COD was used as a proxy to quantify CH4 emissions throughout this study. Meanwhile, China’s wastewater COD treatment capacity increased eightfold over the two decades (8). This increased the proportion of urban domestic wastewater COD treated by centralized WWTPs from 25% in 2002 to 68% in 2023, consistent with the IPCC’s default value for Chinese cities (fig. S7). These rapid transformations had a profound impact on CH4 emissions, but quantifying the exact magnitude of emission change remains challenging because treatment capacities, per-capita wastewater generation, and treatment technologies have all been evolving simultaneously.

By integrating the EFs from our best model with facility-level COD removal flux and facility information (process data) (35) for 2007, 2009, 2012, 2014, and 2019 (Supplementary Text), we estimated that national WWTP CH4 emissions in China increased from 58.8 ± 6.0 Gg CH4 year−1 in 2007 to 243.7 ± 24.8 Gg CH4 year−1 in 2019, representing a fourfold increase (Fig. 3A). Because of the frequent occurrence of eutrophication in water bodies (36), the Chinese government enforced strict wastewater treatment standards [2007, national grade I-A standard: COD < 50 mg/liter (37); 2012, selective implementation of quasi-class IV standard: COD < 30 mg/liter (38)] and issued measures to increase investment in wastewater treatment facilities since after 2007 (39, 40). Consequently, the number of China’s domestic WWTPs increased from 1178 in 2007 to 10,826 in 2019, while their annual COD treatment capacity expanded from 5.8 to 20.0 Tg (fig. S8). In addition, to meet stricter nitrogen and phosphorus removal targets while minimizing sludge production and reducing costs, the existing and new municipal WWTPs rapidly shifted from conventional activated sludge (AS) processes using single-stage aerobic degradation to modern multistage treatment processes such as AAO and AO. These more advanced processes achieved higher pollutant removal rates with lower construction and operating costs (25). Correspondingly, the principal types of urban WWTP CH4 emissions have shifted with time. In 2007, AS plants accounted for 56% of domestic wastewater treatment, whereas by 2019, advanced AAO (33%) and OD (25%) systems had become the predominant processes (Fig. 3A). The rapid expansion of domestic WWTPs also led to an increase in small-scale plants. Between 2007 and 2019, the proportion of small-scale plants increased by 10% (fig. S9). This nationwide technological transition, combined with decreasing plant-average COD removal flux (560 kg hour−1 in 2007 to 210 kg hour−1 in 2019) increased the sector’s mean CH4 emission intensity from 1.0 × 10−2 g CH4 g−1 COD in 2007 to 1.2 × 10−2 g CH4 g−1 COD after 2014. However, the 3.5-fold growth in COD treatment capacity between 2007 and 2019 was the primary driver of WWTP CH4 emission increases during this period.

Between 2003 and 2023, we estimated that CH4 emissions from China’s urban WWTPs grew at a rate of 12% year−1 (11 to 12% year−1). Most of this growth occurred after 2012: Between 2012 and 2019, emissions rose by 120 Gg CH4, accounting for 54% of the total increase over the past two decades (Fig. 3B). This outcome reflects increased investment in urban domestic WWTP construction under China’s 11th (2007) and 12th (2012) Five-Year Plans for urban wastewater management (41, 42). According to recent US Environmental Protection Agency data, urban CH4 emissions from US WWTPs since 2019 are comparable to those from Chinese cities (43). Other bottom-up studies show substantial divergence in estimated CH4 emissions from China’s urban WWTPs (table S6) (24, 44–50). For example, Zhao et al. (49) and Du et al. (48) applied the IPCC 2006 methodology, and estimated emissions for 2014 were 7.3 and 6.4 times higher than those in this study. On the basis of a number of large and single-process plants, Cai et al. (45) and Yan et al. (47) estimated EFs of 0.004 and 0.002 kg CH4 kg−1 COD, respectively. Li et al. (24) combined limited measurements from plants using only one treatment process with literature-derived EFs for different process types, showing a 41% deviation from our findings. These discrepancies underscore the necessity of incorporating COD removal load and treatment process type when estimating national CH4 emissions: Fixed EFs cannot capture the broad range of emissions observed across diverse facilities. Even if more precise information cannot be obtained due to data limitations, facility-level treated wastewater volume and process type information should still be considered essential for constructing accurate national inventories.

Past and future emissions from China’s urban municipal wastewater sector

Beyond centralized WWTPs, domestic organic loads continue to be processed by DTS such as septic tanks and pit latrines or discharged directly to the environment. This fraction of low treatment accounted for 57.8% in 2003 and declined to 30.0% by 2023, as shown in fig. S8. Using IPCC 2019 EF values for the fraction of organic loads without direct measurements, we estimate that China’s national CH4 emissions from urban domestic wastewater increased by 32% over the past two decades (Fig. 4B). The Kaya identity is defined as a framework that links GHG emissions with factors such as energy use, economic development, and population growth, thereby allowing emissions to be decomposed into the contribution of these drivers (51). According to the Kaya identity adapted to waste water emissions (Fig. 4A), the rapid increase in both urban population and per-capita organic matter consumption (COD per capita) has been the primary driver of emission growth. Over the past two decades, while the CH4 emission from urban domestic wastewater has increased only modestly, the COD load from municipal wastewater has risen 3.1 times over the past two decades (fig. S8). This disparity reflects the rapid advancement of urban wastewater treatment in China and a structural transformation in treatment methods. During this period, the COD removal rate increased by 30%, and the proportion of wastewater treated by centralized WWTPs also rose sharply. Notably, DTS and untreated wastewater discharges exhibit average EFs approximately nine and two times higher, respectively, than those from centralized WWTPs. These rapid structural changes have reduced China’s per-capita urban CH4 emissions from 0.7 kg person−1 year−1 (2003) to approximately 0.5 kg person−1 year−1 (2023), which is 30 to 40% of the per capita emissions in the United States (fig. S10).

We performed a scenario analysis of future CH4 emissions from urban domestic wastewater treatment in China by coupling shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs) with climate targets from representative concentration pathways (RCP) (52, 53). Under the baseline scenario (SSP2 + RCP2.4), we project that per capita COD generation, which has already reached the IPCC default level, will continue increasing to approximately 130 g person−1 day−1. This value approaches current levels in west European nations and reflects anticipated changes in income and consumption concepts (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Text) (54, 55). Concurrently, treatment capacity will expand. We project that urban COD removal rates will increase to 91%,while maintaining the current facility size distribution (65% large plants and 35% small plants). Technological advancements, including CH4 recovery technologies (56, 57) and the development and adoption of low-EF treatment processes, are expected to reduce WWTP CH4 emissions by up to 30% by 2060 (table S7). Accounting for temperature-driven feedbacks and projected urban-rural population dynamics (58), we estimate that total CH4 emission from urban wastewater treatment will rise to 545Gg by 2040. Despite the continuous increase in per capita COD generation, the reduction in urban average EFs (Fig. 4B), driven by advancements in WWTP capacity and technology, will dominate the emission decline after 2040.

Under SSP2, SSP4, and SSP5 scenarios, CH4 emissions follow similar trajectories, although SSP5 shows a more rapid post-2045 decline due to enhanced CH4 recovery (Fig. 4D). In contrast, the SSP3 scenario yields the highest post-2050 emissions because constrained economic development not only limits per capita COD growth but also restricts wastewater treatment capacity expansion and technological advancement (fig. S11). The SSP1 scenario demonstrates how improved environmental awareness and adoption of low-carbon dietary practices can slow organic pollutant discharge growth. This favorable trajectory can be achieved through the development and application of several environmentally beneficial technologies, such as microbial advanced oxidation process (59), microalgae-bacteria granular sludge technology (60) and CH4 recovery technologies (56, 57). The implementation of these technologies can substantially reduce the average urban EF substantially, leading to an earlier emission peak (before 2025) and substantially lower emissions than other scenarios (Fig. 4C). The results suggest that achieving China’s decarbonization goals require centralized urban wastewater treatment capacity alongside proactive reduction of organic waste generation and resource recovery initiatives.

DISCUSSION

Our study harnessed a large number of atmospheric measurement campaigns to identify key drivers of CH4 EFs across WWTPs in China and to develop a robust facility-level EF predictive model. By contextualizing these findings within China’s two-decade CH4 emission trends from urban domestic wastewater treatment, we highlight the urgent need for (i) refined emission quantification methods, (ii) targeted mitigation strategies, and (iii) sustained efforts to track evolving treatment practices. As both China and the global community work toward Paris Agreement goals, comprehensive understanding and reduction of emissions from all sectors including domestic wastewater treatment become increasingly critical for effective climate change mitigation (10). Our results offer essential insights to inform future policy and infrastructure planning.

This study provides China’s most extensive field measurement and model analysis of urban WWTP CH4 emissions, enabling robust environmental impact assessment at both facility and national scales. Our results demonstrated that the default EFs from IPCC 2006 substantially overestimate plant-level emissions, particularly for facilities with modernized processes. In contrast, the default EFs from IPCC 2019 underestimate CH4 emissions for certain WWTPs, particularly those using “AO,” “OD,” and other processes, as well as small-scale plants (figs. S3 and S5). Our measurements cover 105 individual plants across five cities and 10 different treatment processes; however, emissions from distributed treatment facilities and directly discharged wastewater are not directly measured but estimated on the basis of default IPCC values. Direct quantifications of these emissions are desirable, although it is technically challenging. EF estimates are associated with uncertainties in site-based observations introduced by the corresponding measurement and modeling frameworks (61), as well as for approximation in activity data. A Monte Carlo analysis attributes ~10.2 ± 2.4% uncertainty to emission estimate (95% confidence interval). Therefore, future campaigns should refine sampling designs and integrate complementary methodologies to reduce EF-related uncertainties for this critical and highly variable GHG source.

Accurate quantification of CH4 emissions from individual WWTPs is essential for designing and implementing effective local mitigation strategies. Current inventories rely on a bottom-up approach that combines fixed EFs with facility-level data. Given the importance of plant-specific EFs, the deployment and enhancement of continuous emission monitoring system for monitoring of COD removal flux and influent COD concentrations would substantially improve the accuracy of inventories. Detailed process documentation and operational data can further refine EF estimates. Moreover, emerging automated platforms that leverage artificial intelligence (10) and machine learning algorithms (62) could integrate diverse data streams and real-time monitoring system outputs to dynamically predict EF variations, maximize spatiotemporal resolution, and automate reporting. Such innovations promise to accelerate the development of more comprehensive and up-to-date emission inventories. Note that both inventory-based emission estimate and extrapolations from a limited number of WWTP quantifications have inherent limitations. We encourage more atmospheric observations using improved techniques to better evaluate CH4 emissions from WWTPs.

We combined our model-derived EF with a comprehensive activity-level database to refine China’s long-term CH4 emission trends from urban domestic wastewater treatment. This retrospective analysis reveals the temporal evolution and underlying drivers of sectoral emissions. Despite a threefold increase in urban COD generation driven by rapid economic growth, CH4 emissions from urban wastewater have remained relatively stable and are projected to decouple from economic expansion by 2030, mirroring improvements observed in industrial wastewater treatment (63). This decoupling reflects China’s success in moving beyond the “pollute-then-treat” paradigm through institutional reform, technological innovation, and market mechanisms (64). In contrast, substantial uncertainties persist in rural areas due to sparse data on treatment coverage and lack of accurate EF data (65). Using IPCC default treatment categories and EFs, total and per-capita CH4 emissions from domestic wastewater in rural areas (1192 Gg and 2.5 kg person−1 year−1) of China are estimated to be 2.4 times and 4.7 times those of urban areas (490 Gg and 0.5 kg person−1 year−1), respectively. Although this result has substantial uncertainty, rapid urbanization and the accompanying shifts in wastewater management practices in developing countries like China are likely to substantially reduce domestic wastewater CH4 emissions.

Reducing CH4 emissions from domestic wastewater is a crucial component of achieving the goals outlined in China’s National Methane Plan and is essential for fulfilling the ultimate ambition of the Paris Agreement (66). Accelerating the construction of wastewater treatment facilities in rural and underdeveloped urban areas, alongside promoting transitions in treatment practices, can help China reach an early peak and achieve rapid reductions in these emissions. Furthermore, process improvements and the wider adoption of biogas recovery technologies in wastewater and sludge treatment offer a pathway for the sector to achieve carbon neutrality. These measures also provide substantial cobenefits, including reductions in terrestrial acidification, and both human and freshwater ecotoxicity. These potential benefits warrant substantial attention from China and other developing countries, underscoring the need for prompt and coordinated action.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Mobile in situ observations

Transect measurements of CH4 mole fractions were made repeatedly downwind of WWTPs (crossing the CH4 enhancement plumes from the sources in these sites) using two high-precision trace gas spectrometers onboard a mobile van, manufactured by LI-COR Biosciences, NE, USA (model LI-7810) and by Picarro Inc., CA, USA (model G2301), respectively. During the measurement, air samples were drawn through a sampling tube on the roof (50 cm higher than the roof to avoid the boundary influence) at a flow rate of 2 liters/min, and a fraction of those was dried through a chemical dryer filled with Mg (ClO4)2 before entering the analyzers. The flow rates of LI-7810 and Picarro G2301 were 0.25 and 0.4 liters/min, respectively. The excess gas was removed from the system using an exhaust pump (details in Supplementary Text). The analyzers were calibrated before and after each city’s experimental activity (5 to 12 days) using high and low-range calibration standards traceable to the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) 2004A scale for CH4.

Global Positioning System data with a recording rate of 1 to 10 Hz was used for geolocation. At each target observation site, a ground meteorological station was installed 1.5 m above ground in an open, upwind location of the emission source. The station recorded synchronized meteorological data (including wind speed, wind direction, temperature, and pressure at 1 Hz) that were unaffected by surrounding structures. We used European Center for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) Re-Analysis fifth generation product (ERA5) reanalysis data to estimate atmospheric stability conditions and atmospheric diffusion parameters during observations.

WWTP sampling

Five cities in China—Nanjing, Shenyang, Zibo, Xi’an, and Guangzhou—were selected for this study, as some of them had unique process (other, AO process in Nanjing and Zibo) or exhibited relatively high CH4 emissions (Shenyang, Xi’an, and Guangzhou) in urban emission studies (67). From November 2023 to December 2024, 141 WWTPs across these cities were sampled. In Nanjing, three observation campaigns were conducted, with repeated measurements at the same WWTPs during winter, spring, and summer to assess seasonal variations in CH4 EFs.

Using publicly available emission permit data in China, supplemented by information from the Continuous Emissions Monitoring System website and China’s Ministry of Ecology and Environment (MEE), we collected data on wastewater treatment processes, treatment capacity, and COD removal for all sampled WWTPs. In each city, we selected WWTPs representing more than 70% of the total treatment capacity (table S4), ensuring city-level emission representativeness. The sampled WWTPs cover typical treatment processes used in China, including AAO, AO, MBR, and OD systems, among others. The surveyed plants that had treatment capacities were classified as either large-scale (≥60,000 m3/day) or small-scale facilities (<60,000 m3/day).

During the observation campaign, mobile measurements were conducted along roads located downwind at distances of at least three times the diameter of targeted WWTP areas, based on wind data from meteorological stations and analysis data from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (68). This distance allows CH4 emissions from the WWTP to be approximated as a point source in the modeling of the plume of enhanced concentrations corresponding to these emissions. Vehicle speed was maintained below 25 km/hour during sampling to ensure adequate data collection within each transect across this plume. For each WWTP observation cycle, at least 10 effective transects were obtained across the emission plume. This ensures mobile sampling efficiency while reducing emission quantification uncertainties caused by turbulence (61).

Methane ER estimate

The inverse Gaussian method was used to quantify CH4 emissions from WWTPs based on the measurement of CH4 across their emission plumes (61). The method relies on a surface total reflection Gaussian dispersion model which estimates the enhancement of CH4 concentration above the CH4 background field in the plume downwind of point sources as a function of the ER of the source Q and of the meteorological conditionsC(x,y,z)=Q2πσyσzu¯·e−y22σy2·[e−(z−h)22σz2+e−(z+h)22σz2](1)where C represents the enhancement of CH4 above background concentrations (background concentrations are defined as the fifth percentile, by transect) at coordinates (x, y, and z) relative to the emission source, the y axis following the wind direction. h is the source height, and u̅ is the average wind speed at the source height during observations. Because of the inconsistency between wind speed measurement height (1.5 m) and emission source height (about 5 m), certain corrections are required. Activity data and national emissions

In all five study cities, the continuous emission monitoring system (CEMS) website provided comprehensive data for WWTP, including hourly inflow and outflow fluxes, concentrations of COD, nitrogen, phosphorus, ammonium nitrogen, pH, and water temperature. The design capacity and process classification of each facility were obtained in their self-monitoring plans. We calculated plant activity levels using the COD difference between influent and effluent. On the basis of our analysis of 48-hour COD removal data—which revealed that measurements taken 42 hours before observation best represented operational activity levels (fig. S13)—we applied a 42-hour lag correction to the COD difference calculations (which will result in an uncertainty of 20%).

To estimate temporal changes in CH4 emissions from WWTPs in China, we integrated multiple data sources to establish a database of facility-level activity levels and facility information spanning 2003–2023 for mainland China (70). We obtained names, locations, and wastewater treated volume of 10,892 WWTPs from China’s MEE 2019 centralized wastewater treatment facility registry. In addition, we extracted WWTP basic information and treatment process types from the MEE’s National Urban Wastewater Treatment Facility Directory (35) for the years 2007–2014. Through systematic integration of these datasets, we developed a comprehensive facility information database spanning 2003–2023. By integrating the facility-level CH4 EFs derived from the generalized linear regression model (GLM)–based model, we estimated the historical CH4 emissions from WWTPs across China. To estimate CH4 emissions from non-WWTP sources (including untreated wastewater and septic tanks), we collected annual domestic COD emission data from MEE and urban population classifications from China’s National Bureau of Statistics. The GLM model

We developed multisource GLMs to predict and correct WWTP EFs under varying conditions, incorporating environmental factors, plant-specific data, and EF training. The model was built using WWTP influent and effluent data and meteorological conditions during observations. Three different approaches were used: (i) Normal GLM, (ii) GLM modeling weighted by COD removal fluxes, and (iii) GLM modeling based on EF categorization were used to build different models, respectively (Supplementary Text). For each predictor variable in each model, we evaluated transformations such as linear, quadratic, and logarithmic by fitting them individually to the EF and comparing Bayesian information criterion and then retained the optimal transformation. Across all three models, the variables found to be influential for predicting EF are consistent (table S8). These variables are the following:

1) Hourly COD removal flux at the WWTP (COD_f)

2) COD concentration at the WWTP inlet (COD_c)

3) Air temperature during the observation period (temperature)

4) WWTP treatment process (process)

To identify the optimal predictive model and its corresponding variable combination, we used a nested fivefold cross-validation framework for all three GLMs (Supplementary Text). The outer fivefold loop (comprising one outer test set and four training sets) provides an unbiased estimate of predictive performance on an independent outer test set. Within each outer training set, an inner fivefold loop is used to select the best variable combination. Base variables were fixed to COD_f, COD_c, temperature, and process. On this basis, we enumerated all possible combinations of up to two pairwise interaction terms (e.g., COD_f × process and COD_c × COD_f) and selected, for each outer training set, the combination yielding the lowest root mean square error (RMSE) in the inner loop. The selected combination was then refitted on the full outer training set and evaluated on the corresponding unseen outer test set to obtain RMSE, mean absolute error, emission flux error, and R2. Across the five outer folds, we recorded the test performance to determine the most stable and best-performing variable combination for each GLM. A comparison of the resulting predictive skill across the three GLMs allowed us to identify the final model (table S5).

To further assess the robustness of the selected model, we applied it to 37 measured WWTPs that were not included in the GLM dataset (data from factories with abnormal EF or multiple repeated measurements were not used). For these facilities, COD removal, air temperature, and treatment process were taken from real observations, while inlet COD concentration was assigned the mean value for each process from the GLM database. Last, this model was used to estimate facility-level EFs for Chinese WWTPs from 2003 to 2023.
