Predicting the severity of eye damage induced in rabbits after sulfur mustard vapor exposure
Authors: Fanny Caffin, Fanny Gros-Désormeaux, Alexandre Igert, Clémence Dumas-Croset, Clara-Lou Kang, Eléana Navarre, Sarah Caron, Christophe Piérard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sulfur mustard (SM) is a chemical blistering warfare agent for which there exists no specific treatment or antidote. Almost 90 % of exposed victims show ocular lesions. Indeed, eyes are the most sensitive organs to airborne exposure. To improve medical care for victims by offering them a prognosis of lesion severity, we evaluated different parameters to predict the severity of SM-induced ocular lesions in a rabbit model. We followed the procedure for SM vapor exposure only on the right cornea of female New Zealand white rabbits using vial caps. Macroscopic eye observations and standardized measurements were performed on the anterior and posterior segments until D35 post-exposure. We set up a severity score based on non-invasive anterior segment observations and measurements, which led to the identification of two subgroups of SM-exposed rabbits: rabbits with severe and moderate ocular lesions. This severity score could be helpful in predicting the progression of SM-induced ocular injuries since subgroups split as early as D7. Our score can thus be used to investigate predictive SM ocular biomarkers and to test new therapeutics.

Introduction

Sulfur mustard (SM) is a chemical blistering warfare agent that was widely used during the First World War and later during the Iran-Iraq war. Though banned by the Chemical Weapons Convention, SM still poses a chemical terrorist threat as observed in Syria in 2015–2016 when it was used by ISIS [1], [2], [3]. In addition to the terrorist risk, there is also the risk of accidental exposure associated with shells buried during the First World War, mainly in northeastern France and Belgium. Indeed, every year, accidental exposures are caused by earthworks, agriculture, or shell collectors.

Currently, no specific treatment or antidote exists to treat SM-induced injuries. Their management is only symptomatic. Early decontamination is crucial to prevent lesions and avoid the spread of toxicity. SM is an alkylating agent that penetrates the body by numerous routes (ocular, pulmonary, and cutaneous) and exerts local toxicity on these areas in addition to possible systemic toxicity or distant organ toxicity (e.g., immunodepression, central nervous system damage [4], [5], [6]). The first symptoms of intoxication usually appear in the eyes, which are the most sensitive organ to airborne exposure. Indeed, ocular lesions are reported in almost 90 % of exposed victims. These lesions may occur immediately or even months or years after the initial exposure [7], [8]. Initial ocular symptoms include irritation, lacrimation, and photophobia after a dose-dependent latency period, leading to keratoconjunctivitis of varying severity and often resulting in chronic damage to the eye tissues [7], [8], [9]. Severity primarily depends on the duration and concentration of exposure as well as individual variability [8]. In addition to the acute symptoms, some victims develop ocular sequelae many years after an apparent remission phase. Indeed, a review reported delayed ocular symptoms in 26 to 93.3 % of Iranian victims [10], characterized by epithelial defects and corneal neovascularization.

Management of ocular injuries mainly consists of relieving pain, wearing black glasses, and preventing infection [9]. In some cases, corneal transplantation is required to treat corneal opacification. However, even after this transplant, opacification may remain recurrent [7].

To test new therapeutics, we developed a model of SM-induced ocular lesions on rabbits. This model allowed us to better characterize the lesions induced by SM by measuring intraocular pressure, corneal thickness, corneal opacity, and neovascularization. As with human victims, previous publications using rabbit models observed the variable severity of injuries. The aim of this present study was to propose a combined score of non-invasive ocular parameters to predict the severity of SM-induced ocular lesions, as the better prediction of lesion severity will lead to improved medical care for victims.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The use of animal models is necessary to study ocular damage weeks after SM exposure. The principles of laboratory animal care were followed in all experiments. This work was performed in accordance with the Directives of the European Communities Council Directive 2010/63/UE for animal experiments and complies with the ARRIVE guidelines. Project was submitted to and approved by the ethics committee of the French Service de Santé des Armées (C2EA-SSA).

Animals

A total of 24 female New Zealand white rabbits (Charles River Laboratories, Saint Germain Nuelles, France) aged 10 weeks and weighing 2.5–3.0 kg were housed individually. The animals were given a standard diet and water ad libitum. An environmental light cycle was set up with 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness per day, with a maximum light intensity of 40 lux to reduce photophobia. Throughout the procedure, the environment was enriched with toys and treats. The animals were monitored daily to ensure their well-being. After their arrival in the animal facility, the rabbits were subjected to 1 week of quarantine followed by 2 weeks of habituation before SM exposure.

Sulfur mustard exposure procedure

SM exposure was performed in the toxicology laboratories of the French Institut de Recherche Biomedical des Armées (IRBA). SM was supplied by the French Direction Générale de l’Armement (DGA). Handling precautions included the use of personal protective equipment and handling under a chemical fume hood.

The animals were randomly divided into two groups: unexposed (SHAM group; n = 8) and exposed animals (SM group; n = 16). On the day of the SM procedure, rabbits were anesthetized with an intramuscular injection of a mixture of ketamine (15.0 mg/kg) and medetomidine (0.25 mg/kg); buprenorphine (0.02 mg/kg, s.c.) was then administered. Under anesthesia, a buprenorphine delivery pump was then implanted (Alzet osmotic pump, Charles River Laboratories France, model 2ML1–10, 10 μl/hr, 7 days). Throughout the procedure, the animals were placed on hotplate to maintain homeothermy. Buprenorphine (0.3 mg/ml) pumps were aseptically implanted in the middle of scapula, and the incision was closed surgically. Once implanted, the pumps delivered a continuous and constant flow of pain control medication for up to a week. Vial caps were prepared in advance by placing a filter paper disk (13 mm diameter) at the bottom. For the SM vapor exposure, the caps were soaked in ophthalmic gel (Ocry-gel®), then 10 μL of neat SM was deposited on the paper filter. Caps were then inverted and fixed onto the rabbit’s right cornea (right eye, RE). A control cap was similarly fixed onto the left cornea (left eye, LE) as an internal control. Caps were removed after 6 min of exposure based on previously determined parameters (data not shown). Animals were kept under the chemical fume hood until eye decontamination, 4 h post-exposure. This preventive eye decontamination was performed using a wash with physiological serum. The rabbits were then returned to their cages for 35 days (end of the study).

For euthanasia, rabbits were first anesthetized using an intramuscular injection of a mixture of ketamine (15.0 mg/kg) and medetomidine (0.25 mg/kg). Second, they were euthanized by the administration of a lethal intracardiac injection of pentabarbital (333 mg/kg). The animal eyes were collected to measure different parameters such as the size of the eyeball (longitudinal and anteroposterior axis) and the volume of the aqueous humor.

Clinical scoring

Since we’ve previously observed in preliminary studies two evolution of lesions post-exposition to SM, we set up a score in order to predict the course of the lesions since first days post-exposure. A severity score was then set up using non-invasive parameters daily measured cornea opacity (CO), the length of neovessels (NV) and the intraocular pressure (IOP). Some of these clinical parameters were based on previous articles by Kadar et al., Gordon et al., and Tewari-Singh et al. [11], [12], [13]. These measurements were performed using veterinary devices, prior to SM vapor exposure and thereafter on a daily basis. Aurora® (Optomed), a versatile non-mydriatic handheld camera that photographs anterior segment allowed to assess the CO and NV whereas the IOP was measured using the TonoVet Plus device (iCare).

A normal cornea is completely transparent and avascular, while corneal edema results in the loss of transparency, visible as corneal opacity. Thus, the opacity measurement is based on the level of transparency ranged from normal transparency (scored 0) to a very severe and diffuse opacity with the complete loss of corneal transparency (scored 4) (Fig. 1A). In order to minimize bias related to inter-operator variability when assessing corneal opacity, the established standardized rating scale ensure that identical classification criteria were applied. In addition, classification is performed blindly during image processing (with photographs of the eyes being evaluated anonymously).Fig. 1A) Illustration of the opacity score. Neovascularization is always considered a pathologic response [14]. Neovessels cannot be resorbed and can induce a decrease in visual acuity that can lead to blindness of corneal origin [15].

The length of NV (LNV) is measured using ImageJ Fiji software. Corneas were divided into four quarters. The LNV was obtained from the ratio between the length of the neovessel and the radius of the cornea. A score ranged from 0 (absence of NV) to 4 (exceeding 75 % of the corneal radius) was attributed for the longest NV for each quarter. The LNV score for each quarter were then summed to obtain a single value (NV score) per animal per day (Fig. 1B).

The IOP was assessed using the TonoVet Plus device (iCare). This tonometer uses a rebound measurement principle in which a probe briefly touches the cornea. This gentle measurement does not require anesthesia nor does it cause a corneal reflex in most animals. The distance between the device and the animal’s eye is controlled with a position indicator. After validating the positioning, the device performs a series of six measurements allowing for reliable measurements. Moreover, each series of measurements was performed in triplicate. For each animals, the difference between the exposed eye (OD) and the control eye (OG) was calculated.

To ensure that all parameters had the same impact on the score, all clinical parameters were converted into percentage then normalized before being added together.

Additional ocular measures

Central cornea thickness (CCT) was evaluated using a handheld pachymeter PachPen® (Accutome). This pachymeter automatically eliminates non-perpendicular measurements on the cornea. The measuring range is 300–999 µm. Measurements were only performed on anesthetized animals before SM exposure (D0) and before euthanasia (D35).

Deformation of the eyeball as in the case of pseudo-keratoconus was assessed by the ratio between the anteroposterior length (AP) and the longitudinal length (LL) obtained using ImageJ Fiji software (Fig. 1D). For each animal, RE was compared with LE. In the absence of difference between RE and LE, the score was 0 (no eyeball deformity). If the RE ratio was higher than the LE ratio, the score was 1 (eyeball deformity). Moreover ratio of cornea length (CL) to LL was calculated to evaluate the deformation of the cornea.

Statistical analysis

Multiple between-group comparisons were performed using two-way ordinary analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test using Prism version 10.2.2 (GraphPad Software). The significance threshold of the p-value was set at 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM).

Results

Clinical signs observed

All exposed rabbits developed typical signs of SM toxicity 24 h post-exposure (conjunctivitis, blepharitis, corneal edema, neovessels and keratoconus…), with different grade of eye lesions severity. No difference was observed between LE and RE of SHAM rabbits. Similarly, SHAM values remained constant over the course of the study. Thus, SHAM values corresponded to the mean RE and LE. No statistical difference was observed between SHAM rabbits and LE of exposed rabbits, indicating that SM did not induce lesions in the non-exposed eye.

Severity score

After HD exposure, a subdivision into two distinct subgroups with moderate symptoms (HDm) and severe symptoms (HDs) based on anterior segment aspect is observed from D14. Indeed, some animals showed apparent spontaneous resorption of ocular lesions (HDm), while others showed a progression of ocular lesions (HDs) (Fig. 3A). The severity score, calculated at D35, combining IOP, CO and NV led to two distinct subgroups with moderate symptoms (HDm) and severe symptoms (HDs) (Fig. 2A). The severity score of the HD group ranged from 4.46 ± 1.7 to 74.96 ± 2.0. The HDm subgroups represented 31 % (n = 5) of the HD group, while the HDs represented 69 % (n = 11). Cornea opacity

With regard to the CO score, a maximum score of 4.0 ± 0.0 for HDm and HDs was observed from D1 to D3 (Fig. 3B). The opacity score then decreased rapidly and began to divide significantly at D14 (HDm: 56.67 ± 11.3; HDs: 74.24 ± 3.5; p < 0.0001). Indeed, the CO score in the HDs subgroup increased progressively from D14 until D35 to reach a mean score of 97.7 ± 2.3, whereas CO decrease for HDm until D35 to reach 41.7 ± 9.5. No neovessels were observed in the SHAM group throughout the study. IOP evolution

With regard to the evolution of IOP, the SHAM values are stable throughout the study, whereas a transient increase in IOP in the exposed eye was observed 5 h after exposure for both HD subgroups. The difference between RE and LE was 3.7 ± 0.7 mmHg for HDm group and 3.7 ± 0.9 mmHg for the HDs group (Fig. 4B). The IOP of both subgroups then decreased on D1. For the HDm subgroup, IOP returned to the SHAM value at D35 (respectively −2.3 ± 1.0 mmHg; −2.4 ± 0.8 mmHg) while IOP in the HDs subgroup continued to decrease (-8.8 ± 1.1 mmHg, p < 0.0001).

CCT

In terms of CCT, all groups had similar corneal thickness at D0 (HDm: 353.00 ± 6.91 µm; HDs: 367.00 ± 3.21 µm; SHAM: 361.30 ± 4.49 µm) (Fig. 5B). At D35, a significant increase was observed in both SM RE groups compared with SHAM (HDm: 650.00 ± 53.99 µm; HDs: 806.00 ± 1.01 µm, SHAM: 364.40 ± 4.78 µm; p < 0.0001). In addition, HDs had significantly higher CCT values than HDm (p < 0.001). Eyeball measurements

Eyeball shape deformation was estimated by the AP/LL ratio after euthanasia at D35. A significant increase in the AP/LL ratio (Fig. 5B) was observed for the HDs group compared with the HDm and SHAM groups (HDs: 101.2 ± 1.5 % vs HDm: 93.6 ± 1.2 %; p < 0.01; and vs SHAM: 93.3 ± 0.9 %; p < 0.001). In addition, the results of the CL/LL ratio indicated no significant change in corneal size (data not shown). Thus, despite the enlargement of the HDs eyeball, the diameter of the cornea remained unchanged. The combination of these two elements, together with the pyramidal shape of the cornea (data not shown), allows us to conclude that pseudo-keratoconus has developed.

Discussion

This study characterizes the differences in the progression of the severity of ocular lesions following SM ocular vapor exposure in a rabbit model. Our results based on our severity score are consistent with the variations observed in SM victims [16], [17] and with the analogue model. Indeed, Horwitz et al. [18] also described two subgroups with late ocular manifestations (54–80 % of animals with an impaired eye versus 20–49 % with a non-impaired eye) 2 weeks after SM exposure with a similar rabbit model. In the same way, Kadar et al. [19] described a proportion of about 50 % impaired corneas 1 month after SM exposure.

Acute SM-induced lesions are well characterized, including inflammation of the anterior segment and corneal NV. The first observed lesion symptom in our model was the corneal opacity. Although the maximum value of corneal opacity occurred earlier in our study than in that of Goswanni et al. [20] (D1 vs D3), this value is subject to the experimenter’s judgement however our blinded scoring system helps minimize inter-operator bias. Moreover, in the study of Goswanni et al., spontaneous resolution was observed after 3 days but without complete resolution on D28 post-exposure, as in our study. The observed corneal edema could be a stromal and/or epithelial edema and could result from injuries localized in the epithelium (leading to osmotic absorption of the tear film fluid), the stroma itself, or the endothelium (leading to absorption of aqueous humor fluid) [14].

Moreover, edema often results in NV of the stroma [14]. As in Goswanni et al. [20] and Gordon et al. [12], the same temporality of NV onset was observed for both subgroups in our study, with a significant corneal NV score occurring as early as D7 after SM exposure and then progressively increasing until the end of the study (D28).

Nevertheless, an absence of effects on the IOP [21] or a transient increase [8], [22] has sometimes been described in SM victims. In a previous study conducted in rabbits exposed to SM [23], no significant effects on IOP were observed. However, the reduced IOP observed in our study is reliable, since the IOP of SHAM corresponds to known healthy values in New Zealand rabbits (15.1 ± 2.0 mmHg data not shown) [24], [25]. Moreover, measurements were performed using the Tonometer TonoVet Plus device (iCare), which was described as highly correlated with manometric IOP [24] in a comparative study of tonometers in rabbits. This tonometer is fitted with an LED system to indicate to experimenters the correct positioning of the device in terms of the distance and inclination from the rabbit’s eye in order to take correct measurements and reduce positioning bias. The IOP measurement is influenced by corneal thickness (a thick cornea, as observed in our study, tends to have an overestimated IOP) [26], thus our IOP measurements seem to be reliable. Moreover, hypotony can be explained, since chemical burns can lead to uveitis, of which hypotony is an important complication in the case of severe uveitis [27], [28], [29]. In addition, hypotony is frequently observed in cases with anterior segment inflammation [28].

Regarding the CCT parameter, multiple studies have already correlated SM exposure and increased CCT [12], [19], [30], even D35 post-exposure as in our study [19], [30]. Increased thickness reflects the accumulation of extracellular fluid in the stroma, resulting in the loss of corneal transparency [31]. Its daily measurement will therefore provide an earlier and more accurate assessment than the opacity score.

While translating our ocular severity score from rabbits to humans in cases of sulfur mustard (SM) exposure presents some challenges, it also offers valuable opportunities for refinement and adaptation. SM exposure victims are naturally more variable than in controlled laboratory experiments, where factors as dose and timing are carefully managed and interindividual variability is minimized. This variability makes it more complex to directly correlate specific exposure doses with observed symptoms, and to distinguish between moderate and severe effects from the same dose. However, most of the ocular symptoms observed in our rabbit model closely align with those reported in humans, with one key difference: intraocular pressure (IOP), which is a component of our severity score. In rabbits, SM exposure leads to a decrease in IOP, whereas it increases in humans. A promising approach to bridge this translational gap between species could be to incorporate the inverse of IOP into our scoring system, ensuring greater relevance to human responses. To further validate and enhance this scoring system future research could focus on studies involving non-human primates, providing even more robust and applicable insights [32].

Conclusion

Our results highlighted the lack of significant difference between SHAM and SM LE, thus confirming that SM does not diffuse from the RE to the LE. Compared with the unexposed eyes, SM RE exhibited: i) severe and prolonged injury of the anterior segment tissues, mainly visible through NV and deep edema, ii) a significant decrease in IOP, and iii) a significant increase in CCT.

Better knowledge of the progression of SM-induced ocular symptoms using efficient parameters may lead to improved medical care for victims. Thanks to our severity score based on the anterior segment observations and measurements, two subgroups of SM-exposed rabbits were identified: rabbits with severe and moderate ocular lesions. This severity score could therefore be helpful in predicting the progression of ocular injuries. Our severity score can also be used to investigate predictive SM ocular biomarkers and to test new therapeutics.
