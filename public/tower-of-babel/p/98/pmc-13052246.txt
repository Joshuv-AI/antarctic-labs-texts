Burden of Care: Stress, Anxiety, Depression, and Quality of Life in Cancer Caregivers in Kuwait: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Hamad Alhamad, Ahmad Alenezi, Haya Shabakoh, Arwa Alshammari, Musaed Z Alnaser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCancer care frequently relies on unpaid family caregivers who face substantial psychological strain that may diminish their quality of life (QoL). Local evidence from Kuwait is limited. In Kuwait, where family-based caregiving is culturally normative, the “hidden workload” of caregiving may be substantial yet under-recognized within routine cancer services.ObjectiveTo estimate the prevalence of mental health symptoms including stress, anxiety, and depression among cancer caregivers in Kuwait; to assess caregivers’ health-related QoL; and to identify mental health symptoms predictors.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey of unpaid family adult caregivers (n=201) of in- and out-patients receiving treatment at major cancer centers in Kuwait. Participants completed a demographics form, the 21-item Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21), and the SF-36 Health Survey. Descriptive statistics summarized caregiver and patient characteristics. Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors of depression, anxiety, and stress among caregivers.ResultsCaregivers were predominantly female (70.6%) and Kuwaiti (78.1%); most supported non-hospitalized patients. Mental health symptoms mean scores measured by DASS-21 were 6 (±3.8) for depression, 5.6 (±4) for anxiety, and 7 (±4.1) for stress, prevalence of mental health symptoms was 61.2% for depression, 44.8% for stress, and 67.2 for anxiety. SF-36 Physical Composite Score (PCS) mean score was 59.2 (±19.4) and Mental Composite Score (MCS) was 52.5 (±20.4). Mental health symptoms were significantly predicted by SF-36 physical and mental composite scores, educational level, duration of caregiving, living with the patient, and type of help received.ConclusionAbout half of cancer caregivers in Kuwait reported depressive symptoms and nearly one in two reported anxiety, with marked role limitations in daily and emotional functioning. Emotional distress was inversely related to health-related QoL. The findings suggest potential implications for caregiver screening and culturally sensitive psychosocial and rehabilitative supports, including occupational therapy to safeguard caregiver well-being and, by extension, the quality of cancer care. Longitudinal studies could explore trajectories of caregiver mental health and evaluate targeted interventions in the Kuwaiti context.Keywords: cancer caregivers, psychological distress, depression, quality of life, Kuwait

Introduction

Cancer, defined as a malignant growth or tumor caused by the uncontrollable proliferation of abnormal cells.1,2 In Kuwait, between 2019 and 2020, the number of recorded cancer cases increased from 4693 to 4825.3,4 This has risen exponentially over the past few years, contributing to a growing population of patients requiring long-term care and support from their relatives.4 Within this context, informal caregivers commonly undertake practical care tasks (eg, transportation, medication routines, symptom monitoring), emotional labor, and care coordination, often alongside employment and family responsibilities.

The rising incidence of cancer presents a myriad of physical, social and psychological challenges for patients and survivors,5 while also introducing new and persistent burdens for caregivers throughout the diagnostic treatment, and post-treatment phases.6–8 A caregiver is an adult, relative or partner who provides unpaid assistance by tending to the daily needs of a person diagnosed with a disease.9,10 Extant Western studies conducted in the past 20 years illustrate that caregiving constitutes a significant cause of anxiety and stress, particularly for unpaid caregivers, adversely impacting their health and quality of life (QoL).11,12 Pressures experienced by family caregivers often emanate from the expectation that they have the requisite education and skills to provide appropriate care for individuals diagnosed with cancer, although such preparation is frequently absent.11,13 Caregiver stress and anxiety have also been linked with knowledge gaps and uncertainties.14 Inadequate information regarding treatment plans and patient management, juxtaposed with the need to make significant lifestyle adjustments while assuming new roles and responsibilities has been identified as notable stressors among caregivers.15,16 Studies that focus on caregivers of cancer patients have widely documented how caregiving responsibilities may evoke high levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and social isolation.17–19

In Kuwait and Middle Eastern contexts, caregiving for individuals with chronic illnesses is predominantly family-based and unpaid, with relatives assuming extensive caregiving responsibilities shaped by strong cultural and social expectations.20 Evidence from Middle Eastern studies suggests that such culturally embedded caregiving roles are associated with heightened psychological burden among caregivers.20,21 However, the majority of research on psychological distress among caregivers originates from Western healthcare settings; a minority looks at caregiving experiences with cancer patients in Middle Eastern or Gulf countries. Accordingly, Kuwaiti data are needed to inform locally feasible caregiver screening pathways and culturally responsive support models.

Caregiver stressors are frequently ongoing, unmanageable, and unpredictable, with one-third of caregivers reporting having a heavy care load.22 This burden of care has detrimental impacts on both the patient and caregiver’s QoL, with a recent study demonstrating how stress impacts caregivers’ capacity.23 Anxiety has been identified as a common psychological response among caregivers of cancer patients, which can undermine their capability to provide caregiving responsibilities and often requires additional psychological support.24 The emotional burden of caregiving is manifested in higher prevalence rates of depression amongst caregivers when compared to the general population.25 Depression, characterised by its adverse impact on mood, physical health, emotional regulation and sleep,26 undermines the QoL of caregivers. Despite strong international evidence, caregiver psychological outcomes remain under-examined in Kuwait in relation to health-related QoL across both mental and physical domains.

In the existing literature, scholars have paid less attention to mental health and health-related QoL, defined as the state of physical, emotional, and psychological well-being of caregivers of cancer patients.13 This omission is significant, as compromised caregiver QoL also impinges on the quality of care provided to patients.27 Despite growing international evidence on caregiver stress and quality of life, research examining the well-being of cancer caregivers in Kuwait remains limited. Against this backdrop, our study explores the prevalence of anxiety, stress, and depression among caregivers of cancer patients in Kuwait as well their quality of life level. Secondarily, we aimed to identify predictors of mental health symptoms among cancer patients care givers in Kuwait with the view to evaluate their impact on QoL. Given the cross-sectional design and convenience sampling, the study is positioned as exploratory and descriptive, intended to generate context-specific evidence and hypotheses for future longitudinal and intervention research.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross-sectional study of unpaid caregivers (n=201) of patients with a confirmed diagnosis of cancer receiving care and treatment in Kuwaiti centres and hospitals between February and April 2023. Eligible participants were caregivers between ages 18 and 75 years who had provided care to a patient diagnosed with cancer according to standard classifications of cancer. Unpaid caregiving was operationally defined as providing assistance without financial compensation (eg, family member, relative, friend). Paid caregivers under the age of 18 and older than 75 were excluded from the study. Participants caring for patients without a confirmed cancer diagnosis, or patients in recovery were also excluded from the study. To support transparency and interpretability, the manuscript reports caregiver and patient characteristics alongside symptom prevalence and QoL patterns.

Outcome Measures and Instruments

Outcome measures for our study were depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms among caregivers of cancer patients in Kuwait, and caregivers’ health-related QoL. Participants completed a two-part questionnaire either in Arabic or English comprising 14 general questions pertaining to demographic data, caregiver-patient relations, patient-specific diagnostic information and patient care and treatment. In the second part of the questionnaire, participants completed the Depression Anxiety and Stress scale-21 (DASS-21),28 and the Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36).29,30

DASS-21 Scoring and Interpretation

DASS-21 comprises 21 items (7 per subscale: depression, anxiety, stress) rated on a 4-point scale (0–3). Subscale scores were computed by summing the 7 relevant items (possible range 0–21 per subscale). Symptom severity categories were interpreted using published DASS-21 cut-offs (Depression: Normal 0–4, Mild 5–6, Moderate 7–10, Severe 11–13, Extremely severe ≥14; Anxiety: Normal 0–3, Mild 4, Moderate 5–7, Severe 8–9, Extremely severe ≥10; Stress: Normal 0–7, Mild 8–9, Moderate 10–12, Severe 13–16, Extremely severe ≥17). For prevalence reporting, “clinically relevant symptoms” were defined a priori as Moderate-or-higher severity. The reliability and validity of the DASS-21 have been supported in prior work.31–33

SF-36 Scoring and Interpretation

The SF-36 yields an 8-domain profile: physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health.29 Higher scores (0–100) reflect better QoL. The SF-36 has demonstrated reliability across mental health conditions and caregiving populations.34–36 DASS-21 subscales were scored by summing relevant items (0–21 per subscale), and symptom severity categories were interpreted using published DASS-21 cut-offs; “clinically relevant symptoms” were defined as Moderate-or-higher severity. DASS 21 normal scores cut-offs used in this study were 5 for depression, 4 for anxiety, and 8 for stress. Higher scores suggest mild to extremely severe mental health symptoms.

Given the conceptual overlap between distress and functional role limitations, we examined associations between DASS-21 subscales and all SF-36 domains to characterize where distress most strongly corresponded to impaired functioning.

The questionnaire was administered to caregivers of cancer patients either via social media or through hard copies at the Husain Makki Al-Juma Centre for Specialised Surgery, Yacob Behbehani Centre for Bone Marrow Transplant, and the Badriya Al-Ahmad Chemo Centre. To minimize response burden and encourage participation, respondents could complete the survey in either Arabic or English, and participation was entirely voluntary.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval for our study was granted by the Kuwait University Health Sciences Centre Ethics committee (approval number 284). Via an information sheet, cancer caregivers received information pertaining to the study goals and objectives. They were informed about the voluntary nature of the research and their right to withdraw from the study at any moment without facing any adverse repercussions. Participants’ rights to anonymity and confidentiality were strictly upheld throughout the study. Online informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation, and only aggregated data are reported in this manuscript.

Statistical Data Analysis

Questionnaire data were analyzed using SPSS-PC, version 28.0 (2021). Descriptive statistics were computed for caregiver socio-demographics and patient clinical characteristics. Variables significantly predicted DASS-21 depression, anxiety, and stress scores were identified using three multiple linear regression with a stepwise feature. A p-value <0.05 was considered significant in all the statistical analyses.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics of Caregivers

Our study included 70.6% (n=142) female respondents and 29.4% (n=59) male respondents (Table 1). Caregivers were predominantly Kuwaiti (78.1%), and most were young adults aged 18–28 years (36.3%) or 29–38 years (27.4%); 60.2% held a bachelor’s degree and 77.6% reported an average income (Table 1). The majority of caregivers supported non-hospitalized patients. Over half reported living with the patient (55.7%), whereas 30.3% did not live with the patient and 13.9% reported the patient was deceased at the time of participation (Table 1). Most caregivers reported receiving caregiving assistance from family members, although a minority reported having no support. Specifically, 60.7% received help from a family member, while 18.4% reported no help (Table 1). Caregiving intensity was substantial: 39.8% provided 6–12 hours/day and 24.9% provided >12 hours/day (Table 1). Cancer patients were predominantly diagnosed with stage III (29.9%, n=60) and stage II (28.9%, n=58) cancer (Table 1). Stage IV accounted for 24.9% (n=50), and Stage I for 16.4% (n=33) (Table 1).

DASS-21 Results

DASS-21 results showed substantial psychological burden among caregivers. In this sample, mean (SD) DASS-21 subscale scores were 6 (±3.8) for depression, 5.6 (±4) for anxiety, and 7 (±4.1) for stress (Table 2). Using the study’s binary symptom categorization, 61.2% (n=123) screened positive for depression symptoms (score ≥ 5), 67.2% (n=135) for anxiety symptoms (score ≥ 4), and 44.8% (n=90) for stress symptoms (score ≥ 8) (Table 2). Descriptive analyses indicated that female participants comprised the majority of caregivers reporting clinically relevant stress symptoms (74.3%), consistent with the gender distribution of the sample and prior caregiving literature. These findings indicate that emotional distress is common among caregivers in Kuwait, with anxiety and depression particularly prominent.

Discussion

Our findings support extant studies which show that stress, anxiety, and depression are prevalent among carers of cancer patients across cultural boundaries.16,18,37,38 Importantly, by applying DASS-21 severity thresholds and reporting mild-or-higher symptoms, the prevalence estimates in this study represent clinically more consequential distress rather than mild, transient symptoms—strengthening the practical relevance for screening discussions. In parallel, the binary symptom categorization indicated even higher proportions screening positive (depression 61.2%, anxiety 67.2%, stress 44.8%), underscoring a high overall emotional symptom burden in this caregiver population (Table 2).

Previous research has established moderate levels of anxiety and depression among caregivers and documented adverse impacts on QoL.8,39 Our results align with these findings, showing that a substantial proportion of caregivers reported moderate-or-higher anxiety and depression symptoms. Given that most caregivers in our sample supported non-hospitalized patients, these findings highlight that caregiver distress is not confined to inpatient or end-of-life contexts, but may occur broadly across treatment phases, including outpatient care and home-based caregiving. Notably, more than half of caregivers lived with the patient and nearly two-thirds provided ≥6 hours/day of care (Table 1), which may help explain why distress was common even outside inpatient contexts.

We found many associations between SF-36 and DASS-21, indicating that when caregivers experienced anxiety, depression, and stress, their quality of life was negatively affected across most SF-36 domains. The literature supports this pattern by conceptualizing QoL as multidimensional.40 The negative impacts of caregiving have been shown to manifest as physical symptoms (pain, fatigue, sleep disturbance) and psychological symptoms (depression, anxiety), with adverse implications for QoL and caregiving competencies. Our findings are also corroborated by a study that reported that caregivers of cancer patients report significant levels of distress and social isolation.19 In our data, role limitations—emotional and physical—were the most affected SF-36 domains, which aligns with the idea that distress translates into reduced ability to fulfill work, family, and social roles.

Family caregivers are frequently integrated into patient therapy in areas such as treatment planning, decision-making and implementation yet often lack training to manage the exigencies of care.41,42 Caregivers’ QoL may be deprioritized amid patient care demands, increasing the risk of persistent distress and reduced functioning. In practice, these findings support the feasibility and value of embedding caregiver distress screening into routine cancer care workflows, alongside brief referral pathways for psychosocial, rehabilitative, and occupational therapy-informed supports. Our regression findings further support this “burden-of-care” pathway: poorer mental QoL (MCS) consistently predicted higher depression, anxiety, and stress, while caregiving intensity indicators (eg, duration of care and living with the patient) remained significant predictors—suggesting that both perceived health status and care demand independently contribute to distress.

While our study contributes new Kuwait-based evidence, it has limitations. The use of a cross-sectional design limits causal inference and temporality. Convenience sampling and uneven representation of cancer types/stages limit generalizability. Self-report measures may introduce reporting bias. Nevertheless, the consistency of associations between distress and multiple QoL domains strengthens confidence in the overall pattern and supports the need for prospective and interventional research in Kuwait. Future studies should consider additional predictors (eg, caregiving duration over months/years, patient functional status, caregiver comorbidity, and social support) and use longitudinal designs to examine trajectories and intervention effects. Given that a “patient deceased” subgroup was present (13.9%), future analyses should also consider stratified or sensitivity approaches to distinguish active caregiving burden from bereavement-related distress (Table 1).

The regression analyses in the present study suggest that depressive symptoms were the most consistent psychological factor associated with poorer health-related quality of life among caregivers of cancer patients. This study findings indicate that depressive symptoms may represent a central component of the psychological burden experienced by caregivers. Previous research in psycho-oncology has similarly shown that depression among informal caregivers is strongly linked to reduced functional capacity, fatigue, emotional exhaustion, and diminished perceived well-being. The broader pattern observed in this study suggests that while stress and anxiety contribute to caregivers’ emotional strain, depressive symptoms may have a more pervasive relationship with both psychological and physical aspects of quality of life (Tables 6–8). These results highlight the importance of considering depressive symptoms as a key indicator of caregiver vulnerability within oncology care contexts.

Clinical and Service Implications

From a clinical and healthcare service perspective, the findings of this study highlight the importance of systematically identifying psychological distress among caregivers of individuals with cancer. The observed associations between depressive symptoms and multiple domains of health-related quality of life suggest that caregivers experiencing higher levels of psychological distress may be at increased risk of reduced functional capacity, emotional strain, and poorer overall well-being. These findings support the need to incorporate routine psychological screening for caregivers within oncology services to facilitate early identification of individuals who may benefit from additional support.

In many Middle Eastern and Gulf societies, including Kuwait, family members frequently assume primary caregiving responsibilities for individuals with cancer, often with limited access to formal caregiver support services. Recognizing caregivers as an essential component of the cancer care continuum may help healthcare systems address the broader psychosocial impact of cancer beyond the patient alone. Integrating caregiver-focused assessments within oncology clinics could enable healthcare providers to better understand caregivers’ psychological needs and identify those experiencing substantial emotional or functional burden.

Multidisciplinary supportive approaches—including psychoeducation, caregiver counseling, peer-support programs, and rehabilitation-oriented interventions—may help enhance caregivers’ coping strategies and maintain their overall well-being. In particular, occupational therapy and psychosocial support services may contribute to assisting caregivers in managing caregiving demands, maintaining participation in meaningful daily activities, and preserving their physical and emotional health. Strengthening caregiver support initiatives within oncology care systems may therefore contribute not only to improved caregiver well-being but also to more sustainable and effective patient care.

Conclusion

Our study reveals a substantial psychological burden among cancer caregivers in Kuwait, emphasizing the need for early detection and supportive interventions to reduce stress, anxiety, and depression symptoms. It is imperative to adopt a holistic approach that emphasizes the health of both patients and caregivers, given the interdependence between caregiver well-being and care quality. Occupational therapy can also play a crucial role by supporting caregiver routines, role balance, stress management strategies, and engagement in meaningful activities. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships and evaluate targeted, culturally responsive caregiver interventions in Kuwait.
