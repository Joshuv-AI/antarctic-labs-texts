Factors associated with occupational fatigue among healthcare workers in Malaysia: a systematic review
Authors: Mohamad Asraf Ahmad Nazari, Siti Sara Yaacob, Kamarulzaman Muzaini, Mohd Fikri Rosely, Nik Khairol Reza Md Yazin, Mariam Mohamad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to the nature of their work, occupational fatigue is an important issue among healthcare workers (HCWs), with implications for workers’ health, patient safety and healthcare organisation performance. However, the evidence on factors of work fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia remains fragmented.ObjectiveTo synthesise the existing evidence on work fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia, with a focus on its associated factors across HCW populations and to examine the types of fatigue instruments used.DesignSystematic literature review.SettingLiterature on fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia.ParticipantStudies on occupational fatigue involving HCWs conducted within the Malaysian context.MethodA systematic literature search was conducted using electronic databases of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Science Direct. Studies must involve HCWs. Studies not in English, focusing on burn-out and different fatigue constructs, such as compassion fatigue, were excluded. Data extracted included study characteristics, setting, fatigue instruments, HCW groups and key findings. Factors associated with fatigue were synthesised thematically. This review followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines, and the quality of the study was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.Main outcome measureFactors associated with work fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia.ResultsOut of 1699 articles screened, 138 studies were within the Malaysian setting. Nine (n=9) articles were finally included. One study, each was conducted nationwide, in Peninsular zones, in Melaka and Kuala Lumpur, also, three studies in Selangor and two in Sarawak. A total of 4662 HCWs participated, of which 1419 were doctors (30.4%), 1014 nurses (21.8%) and 2229 HCWs of various positions (47.8%). Fatigue instruments used were the Occupational Fatigue/Exhaustion Recovery Scale, the Chalder Fatigue Scale, the Fatigue Assessment Scale, the Zoom Exhaustion and Fatigue Scale, and the postacute COVID syndrome checklist. Two studies used electroencephalogram. Thematic synthesis identified factors such as high job demands (66.67%), high effort (11.11%), sustained strain (11.11%), and an environmental/contextual factor related to the COVID-19 pandemic (11.11%). An integrated conceptual framework is proposed.ConclusionOccupational fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia is multifactorial. Limitations include heterogeneity in study design and fatigue assessment tools. Despite the limited number of included studies, the findings provide theory-informed insights into understanding occupational fatigue; however, they should be interpreted cautiously, despite the need for standardised work fatigue surveillance as part of an efficient fatigue management system.RegistrationThis review was registered under PROSPERO with the ID of CRD420251001576.

Introduction

Due to the subjective nature of fatigue, various definitions have been used to define it.13 Recently, according to WHO International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (WHO ICD-11), fatigue has been defined as a feeling of exhaustion and decreased energy due to depletion of energy and resources. Fatigue is also characterised by a reduced capacity for work and reduced efficiency in responding to stimuli, as defined under the code MG22.4

Regarding occupational fatigue, or work fatigue, it comprised of the similar ICD-11 definition; however, manifestations are related to the types of energy or resources involved, such as physical, mental and emotional work fatigue and must occur in connection with workings days.5 Occupational fatigue involves fatigue that occurs during or after work, or in relation to one’s work.6 Winwood et al proposed a type of occupational fatigue assessment instrument based on its duration and temporal aspects, identifying three distinct types: acute fatigue, chronic fatigue and intershift recovery.2 Occupational fatigue affects a worker’s normal functioning, potentially impairing the ability to meet work demands, due to the work tasks or demands exceeding one’s capacity.7 8 Therefore, occupational fatigue is multidimensional, where psychosocial factors also serve as predictors for work fatigue.9

Healthcare workers (HCWs) are prone to developing fatigue due to the nature of their work. Studies at the international level have reported that 72.9% of obstetrics and gynaecology trainees in Australia and New Zealand, 34.2% of nurses in Pakistan, 83% of female medical personnel across 54 hospitals in China and 69% of 417 doctors in the UK experienced fatigue.1013 Globally, 28%–91% of doctors have also been reported to experience fatigue.14 At the global level, nurses have been reported to experience a moderately high level of acute and chronic fatigue.15

Occupational fatigue is also associated with adverse outcomes. Complications include decreased overall performance and work quality, increased risk of compromising patient safety, higher risk to personal health and safety such as raised likelihood of commuting accidents, and the development of metabolic, cardiovascular and mental health related conditions.1619 Burn-out may develop following unresolved fatigue.17 19 Fatigued HCWs are also associated with other outcomes like occupational injuries, such as musculoskeletal disorders,17 and several factors identified include night-shift workers.20 Working night shifts is a recognisable risk factor for fatigue among HCWs.19

In Malaysia, approximately 37% of crashes among HCWs while commuting were attributed to fatigue or sleepiness.21 It was also found that around 86.5% of Malaysian doctors frequently ruminated about work during non-work, affecting fatigue and recovery.14 Despite these findings, the topic of work fatigue among HCWs and its associated factors remains fragmented and underexplored in Malaysia.14 22

This systematic review was guided by a structured research question developed using the Population–Exposure–Outcome (PEO) approach. The population of interest comprised HCWs, where the exposure focused on the associated and work-related factors. The outcome focused on the development of occupational fatigue. Therefore, this systematic review’s primary outcome aimed to examine the existing literature on the factors associated with occupational fatigue development among HCWs within the Malaysian context. The secondary outcome is to identify the fatigue assessment instruments used. Accordingly, this review aimed to synthesise evidence on factors associated with occupational fatigue within the context of established theoretical work models. The synthesis further informed the development of an integrated conceptual framework of occupational fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia.

Methods

This review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and checklist for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.23 The protocol for this review was registered under PROSPERO with the ID of CRD420251001576.

Search strategy

Four major electronic databases were searched in this review: Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed and Science Direct. Search terms consisted of keyword combinations and Medical Subject Headings. Databases were searched using similar search terms operated via the Boolean technique, to ensure a similar level of thoroughness and to minimise discrepancies. The search terms followed the PEO framework as previously mentioned: (occupational fatigue OR work-related fatigue OR work fatigue OR job fatigue OR physical fatigue OR mental fatigue OR emotional fatigue OR social fatigue) AND (healthcare worker* OR health care worker* OR healthcare professional* OR nurse* OR doctor* OR physician* OR clinician* OR medical staff) AND (Malaysia OR Malaysian).

Searches were conducted in December 2025, involving articles from inception until December 2025. The full search strategies for all databases are provided in online supplemental file 1, in accordance with PRISMA-S guidelines. Filters or limits used were: (1) Articles from inception until December 2025, (2) Published in the English language, and (3) Open access articles.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This systematic review included all studies exploring fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia. No restrictions on study design and no specific timeframe were set, to minimise reporting bias and to avoid missing relevant evidence related to the specified setting throughout time. Studies not conducted within the Malaysian setting and not involving HCWs were excluded. Burn-out-focused studies were excluded as burn-out is conceptually distinct from fatigue, where fatigue is a precursor and mainly reversible while burn-out is an almost irreversible late-stage outcome of fatigue.24 Studies assessing other conceptually distinct fatigue (eg, compassion fatigue, alarm fatigue or digital fatigue) were excluded as they represent constructs not directly related to general occupational fatigue. Studies not in English were excluded to ensure standardised interpretation, especially when involving complex theoretical constructs to ensure consistency, particularly during synthesis. Review articles such as scoping or narrative reviews, and grey literature such as unpublished government reports or unpublished works, were also excluded, to ensure evidence gathered was peer-reviewed and validated, thereby enhancing the scientific authority and reproducibility of the findings.

Data extraction and analysis

A researcher, MAAN, extracted search results from the databases. Search results were exported to EndNote (V.21) and compiled in Microsoft Excel using predetermined columns. The compiled studies were first checked for duplication. De-duplication was performed by MAAN using a combination of both automated tools (Microsoft Excel) and manual approaches, where articles were initially sorted (by title, author names, publication year and abstract) and filtered, followed by manual verification. This two-step process ensures accurate identification and minimises unnecessary duplication deletion. Once removed, preliminary screening of title and abstract according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria was conducted by two researchers, MAAN and KZM. Subsequently, full articles of the preliminary screened studies were reviewed for final consideration by two researchers, MAAN and SSY. Disagreement was resolved by consensus or third author involvement (MM). Key information extracted included study characteristics, population, fatigue instruments, key findings and associated factors.

Identified factors associated were synthesised thematically by MAAN and reviewed by other study team members. Data from the included studies were extracted and organised into key domains based on conceptual characteristics, derived from established work-related theoretical frameworks. Findings were compared and interpreted based on these models to provide a theory-informed understanding of work-related fatigue. Finally, an integrated conceptual framework for work fatigue development among HCWs in Malaysia is proposed. MFR and NKRMY were also involved for final review as experts.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this review.

Quality assessment

The quality of all included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for non-randomised studies.25 It comprises three domains: selection, comparability and outcome. The checklist uses a ‘star’ rating system where a study is rated based on domains as per the NOS user manual, with a maximum sum of nine stars. Generally, studies with seven stars and above indicate good quality and lower risk of bias.26 27 NOS assessment of the study articles is shown in table 1 and summarised in table 2.

Regarding study participants’ characteristics, generally, the included studies recruited active, working HCWs from healthcare facilities. Several studies applied exclusion criteria to minimise confounding from comorbidities or underlying conditions. Mohd Fauzi et al excluded those diagnosed with or treated for sleep or mental disorders;14 Su et al excluded HCWs with neurophysiological, psychiatric or medical conditions affecting brain function28 and Xavier et al excluded doctors with neurological or mental illnesses.29 However, another study by Mohd Fauzi et al did not specify specific exclusion criteria.30 Lim et al included participants with a pathological condition (postacute COVID-19 infection),31 while Muhamad et al did not explicitly rule out prior mental health conditions previously experienced by its participants.32

Fatigue assessment tools

Among the nine studies, four studies used the Occupational Fatigue/Exhaustion Recovery (OFER) Scale, the Chalder Fatigue Scale (CFS) (n=2), the Fatigue Assessment Scale (FAS) (1), the Zoom Exhaustion and Fatigue Scale (1), and a self-reported post-COVID symptom checklist adapted from the National Health Service. Two studies incorporated electroencephalogram (EEG) to objectively assess fatigue-related neurophysiological changes.

Accommodating occupational fatigue

Most studies did not describe direct interventions, however they provided recommendations. Suggested measures included workplace education programmes to mitigate fatigue and sleepiness at the workplace, addressing drowsy driving and invigilance,33 monitoring the long-term health effects of COVID-19, providing continuous support among infected HCWs,31 strengthening Fatigue Risk Management Systems (FRMS) at the workplace, including sleep health awareness, road safety initiatives and protecting recovery opportunities—such as allowing sufficient recovery time between shifts.34 FRMS was again highlighted, through providing prescriptive work schedules with ‘buffering hours’ and ‘right to disconnect’ policies after formal working hours.14 Psychological support and ICT training were recommended to address technostress for affected workers.32 Objective fatigue detection via simplified EEG screening and a single-time-point cognitive assessment were also proposed for workers performing high-risk tasks.28

Factors associated with occupational fatigue

Doctors

Four studies involving doctors (n=1419) showed that occupational fatigue was associated with high work demands, particularly pandemic-related workload, on-call duties and sleep deprivation, and work-related activities during non-work time. During the COVID-19 pandemic, mental demand (mean=7.54, SD=1.998), was reported as the highest workload component, followed by temporal, emotional and physical demands (mean=6.29, SD=2.396). However, emotional demands demonstrated significant association with both acute (Adj. β=2.73) and chronic (Adj. β=3.64) fatigue,30 reflecting the psychological burden of clinical care during crisis. Participants also reported higher acute fatigue levels compared with chronic fatigue and recovery.

Doctor’s fatigue and recovery were also affected by work-related rumination outside working hours. Work-related ruminations, such as rumination on being scolded or violated, were associated with both acute fatigue (Adj. β=2.190, 95% CI 1.139 to 3.240) and chronic fatigue (Adj. β=5.089, 95% CI 3.876 to 6.303), and also associated with poor recovery (Adj.β= −3.316, 95% CI −4.516 to −2.117). Rumination on upcoming work was associated with acute fatigue (Adj.β= 2.065, 95% CI 1.031 to 3.099) and chronic fatigue (Adj. β=3.417, 95% CI 1.999 to 4.835).14

Two objective studies were conducted among on-call doctors. In one study, the mean sleep duration during on-call was 1.5 hours, with mean awake duration of 33.3 hours (SD=1.98 hours). Participants also recorded higher fatigue scores of more than 22 (via FAS) during post-call, indicating fatigue.29 In another study, mean sleep duration during on-call was only 2.2 hours (range 0.5–5.0 hours). During postcall, 87.1% of 32 recruited on-call doctors scored more than 15 (via CFS), indicating fatigue.28 Both studies showed on-call doctors reported short sleep duration (1.5–2.2 hours) and prolonged wakefulness (>30 hours), with elevated fatigue scores postcall. EEG findings demonstrated increased θ activity and reduced α/β power, indicating reduced alertness and neurophysiological fatigue state.

Nurses

Three studies involving nurses (n=1014) showed that occupational fatigue was associated with extended working hours, shift work and work demands. A study investigating the relationships between work demand, off-work demand, fatigue and unsafe driving behaviour showed that between-person work demands at T1 were associated with persistent fatigue (γ=1.19, p<0.01), where work demand had significant positive effect on persistent fatigue at T2. Otherwise, while studying the moderated-mediation effect of sleep between individual demands and unsafe driving behaviour showed that sleep quality (γ = −1.40, p<0.001) and sleep duration (γ = −1.32, p<0.1) were inversely associated with fatigue, however, only sleep quality showed significant effect on persistent fatigue.34

In a subsequent scale development study assessing unsafe driving while commuting (UDWC), the mediating role of acute fatigue in the relationship between work demand and unsafe driving was examined, as well as the mediating role of persistent fatigue between off-work demand and unsafe driving. It was shown that acute fatigue does play a role between work demand and UDWC, with β=0.233 (p≤0.001, 95% CI 0.135 to 0.411).33 Working hours showed a clear dose–response relationship with fatigue, with mean fatigue scores on CFS increasing progressively from 7-hour shifts (mean=20.06, SD=4.92), to working more than 14-hour shifts (mean=30.85, SD=4.13).35

HCWs in general

Two studies involved HCWs of various positions. A study among 609 COVID-19 infected HCWs found that fatigue was the commonly reported symptom encompassing postacute COVID syndrome. Fatigue appeared as the most common symptom after 4 weeks (27.9%), persisted even after 3 months (16.24%), and after 6 months (9.2%).31

In a similar timeframe of the COVID-19 pandemic, another study was conducted to measure the level of technostress and the associated factors among HCWs. It was found that around 97% of HCWs had moderate fatigue, however they were less likely to have moderate technostress (β=−2.330, 95% CI 0.013 to 0.722, p=0.023) and high technostress (β=−3.844, 95% CI 0.003 to 0.162, p<0.001), compared with those HCWs with high fatigue.32

Discussion

High job demand

The Job Demands-Control (JD-C) and Job Demands-Resource (JD-R) models proposed that excessive demands deplete an individual’s energy and resources, where imbalance between the high job demands and inadequate control or resources will lead to job strain.36 37 Our systematic review’s findings support this theoretical premise, consistently identifying high job demands as the key associated factor of occupational fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia. Studies revealed that mental, emotional and workload-related demands were associated with increased fatigue levels. Mental demands were the highest workload component reported during the COVID-19 pandemic, while emotional demands demonstrated a strong association with acute and chronic fatigue. This situation illustrates the resource depletion and strain predicted by the JD-C and JD-R frameworks from working in high job demand environments.

On the other hand, the Effort-Recovery (E-R) model posits that insufficient recovery during or after work leads to recovery debt and accumulation of fatigue.24 38 This framework provides understanding of how the job demand of a doctor, specifically work patterns such as performing on-call duties, contribute to occupational fatigue. This work pattern necessitates prolonged working hours and overnight shifts, thereby reducing and disrupting essential recovery opportunities. Our findings support this theoretical proposition, demonstrating that on-call doctors consistently experienced minimal sleep duration and recovery, with their EEG findings objectively supporting the manifestation of a mental fatigue state.

The Stress-Strain (S-S) model posits that prolonged exposure to stressors lead to physiological, psychological and behavioural strain responses.3941 In this context, long working hours and significant between-person work demands function as key stressors for Malaysian HCWs. Long working period indicates more energy expenditure and sustained efforts, and a prolonged exposure to the work demands itself. Our study finding demonstrated that extended working hours were associated with higher fatigue scores among nurses. Fatigue also additionally mediated the relationship between work demand and safety outcomes such as unsafe driving, and underscored the functional and behavioural adaptations of the accumulated strain, which impact performance and safety.

These findings illustrate that high job demands contribute to occupational fatigue among Malaysian HCWs involving multiple pathways. Job demands encompass the psychological, social and organisational aspects of work that require sustained efforts, such as the physical, cognitive or emotional efforts and are associated with physiological and psychological costs.37 Another example of job demands affecting fatigue in Malaysia includes a study in Pahang, where HCWs in clinical departments were found to be associated with acute fatigue compared with those in non-clinical roles.22

High effort

High effort was associated with work fatigue among Malaysian HCWs. The E-R model proposes that insufficient recovery during or after work leads to recovery debt. Therefore, workers may return to work the next day with residual symptoms of inadequate recovery and cumulated fatigue.24 38 Our study supports the proposition, revealing that work-related rumination was associated with both acute and chronic fatigue, showing that continued cognitive engagement with work during non-work time prevents adequate recovery. This finding also aligns with the Effort-Reward Imbalance (ERI) model preposition. According to ERI, when individuals invest high efforts, or demonstrate overcommitment, but perceive low rewards, stress exists.42 43 Not only stress, the imbalance is also linked to strain and fatigue.44

Effort refers to workers’ responses to extrinsic demands and intrinsic motivation, including overcommitment.42 Effort is also conceptualised by three dimensions: intensity, persistence and direction.45 Rumination reflects a cognitive strain response, aligning with the concept of ‘additional effort’ in terms of direction, reflecting energy and resources allocation on work-related concerns and being mentally engaged beyond formal working hours.

Strain

Based on the S-S model, stress refers to the demands faced by workers that could be task-oriented or situation-related, or in combination, while strain reflects how individuals respond and adapt to the stresses physiologically, psychologically or behaviourally.3941 When stress is prolonged or excessive, it can surpass a threshold termed ‘endurance limits’, leading to fatigue manifested as reduced physiological and psychological functioning.

Technostress is a work-related psychosocial strain that occurs when ICT-related demands exceed an individual’s capacity to cope.32 Technostress contributes to work fatigue among Malaysian HCWs, resulting from cognitive overexertion stemming from computer or ICT-related demands that can affect their work performance and concentration. This aligns with the S-S model, where prolonged exposure to stressors leads to cumulative strain and impairs coping capacity.3941

Environmental/contextual factors

Environmental and contextual factors, particularly the COVID-19 pandemic, also contributed towards occupational fatigue among Malaysian HCWs. HCWs experiencing persistent fatigue even after recovery from infection may indicate both pathophysiological and occupational contributions. First, post-COVID-19 pathophysiological changes resulted in long covid syndrome, consistent with other studies.46 47 Second, this finding may also be approached from an occupational perspective. It was reported that more than 50% (53.3%) of COVID-19 infections among HCWs in Malaysia occurred at the workplace,48 suggesting occupational exposure. HCWs served as front-line personnel during the global crisis, where being a front-line HCW was associated with increased fatigue as reported in international studies.49 50 As previously discussed, mental demands were reported as the highest work magnitude during the COVID-19 pandemic, while emotional demands were positively associated with fatigue among HCWs.30 Technostress also emerged during the pandemic period.32 Therefore, multiple work domains may interact and contribute towards work fatigue among HCWs. While fatigue may be pathological or non-pathological, the fatigue definition is now standardised as per WHO and its construct transcends across multiple areas of application, including the concept of a transdiagnostic approach which focuses on individual needs rather than a specific disease.5153 Hence, multiple work models, including the S-S, E-R and JD-R models, may interrelate at a broader level in explaining work fatigue among Malaysian HCWs within this environmental/contextual factor.

Fatigue assessment modalities

Fatigue was primarily assessed using self-reported instruments such as OFER, CFS and FAS. However, modalities like EEG provided additional evidence and more objective neurophysiological fatigue assessment. Different tools assess different domains and evaluate fatigue among general or specifically intended populations. Based on a systematic review assessing the psychometric properties of fatigue instruments among hospital personnel, existing questionnaires such as the SOFI, MFI, OFER and the Need for Recovery Scale may be inadequate for comprehensively assessing fatigue within the population.54

Integration of findings

Based on the thematic analysis presented in table 4, occupational fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia appears to be driven by high job demands, sustained effort, strain responses, and environmental or contextual factors. High job demands initiate energy depletion, consistent with the JD-C and JD-R models, subsequently leading towards strain and fatigue. Sustained exposure to these demands may further increase effort expenditure, and without adequate recovery, fatigue may develop consistent with the E-R model. Prolonged exposure to these conditions may result in strain responses and adaptations, including impaired performance, consistent with the S-S model. Environmental and contextual factors also influence HCWs’ fatigue.

Conceptual positioning

A conceptual distinction between stressors, stress and strain is essential for understanding occupational fatigue. Job demands refer to the stressors that present in a work situation, such as workload, complexity and intensity.36 According to the Karasek’s JD-C, stress is referred to an internal psychological state, which was not measured directly in the model. The JD-C model emphasises the sources of stress, particularly stressors, such as workload and job demand. Strain occurs when job demands are high while control is low. Similar concepts are also reflected in the (1) JD-R model, with inadequate resources in the presence of high demands and (2) ERI model of imbalance between effort and rewards, both contribute towards strain. Therefore, strain may be considered as a key agent, which occurs when a mismatch exists between work demands and the worker’s available resources, control, effort or rewards.

Robert Hockey, the founder of the Compensatory Control Model, proposed that sustained strain responses underlie the development of long-term stress, fatigue and ill health.44 Hockey further argued that effort, rather than demands alone, may act as the primary trigger for strain and fatigue.44 Therefore, theories of fatigue based on work demand management should also account for the influence of effort and controllability.44 Hockey et al demonstrated that fatigue-related effects became more pronounced under high workload only when workers had low control over work. Furthermore, Hockey’s work management model proposed that under sustained strain conditions characterised by high effort and low control, workers may maintain high performance despite experiencing fatigue and anxiety, accompanied by physiological stress responses such as increased epinephrine and cortisol levels.

In terms of burn-out, fatigue has been recognised as both, a risk factor19 and a predictor of burn-out among HCWs.17 55 Although fatigue and burn-out are closely related constructs, they remain conceptually distinct. Burn-out is primarily conceptualised as a work-related phenomenon, although both constructs may be attributed to stress.55 Burn-out is understood as an occupational phenomenon rather than a medical condition and should not be used to describe experiences outside the work context.56 According to WHO ICD-11 and Maslach’s burn-out model, burn-out is defined as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It is characterised by three key features: (1) Exhaustion, (2) Depersonalisation and (3) Reduced efficiency, including the feelings of ineffectiveness, reduced accomplishment and low productivity.57 58 Several organisational risk factors contributing to burn-out have been identified,59 including control, workload, reward, community, values and fairness. Several of these components are also central in work fatigue, reflecting overlapping dimensions between both constructs. The overlap may indicate the prolonged and unresolved fatigue within stressful work environments that may progressively contribute towards burn-out development, rather than both occurring simultaneously. As previously mentioned, fatigue is often characterised by an acutely reversible energy depleted state, whereas burn-out may represent a later-stage outcome of cumulative and unmanaged fatigue, which may not be fully reversible.24

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the limited number of included studies, combined with variations in study populations, may affect the generalisability of certain findings to other HCW positions and should therefore be interpreted with caution, even within the Malaysian context itself. The limited number of studies also affects the overall depth of the evidence synthesis; however, the findings still provide valuable insights into factors contributing to work fatigue within this context. Heterogeneity in study design, methodology, populations and fatigue measurement tools may have limited comparability between studies and precluded quantitative synthesis, while most studies were cross-sectional in design, limiting causal inference. In terms of tools, fatigue was assessed using various self-reported instruments, each with different underlying constructs and dimensions. This variability may affect direct comparison and synthesis across studies. Restriction to English-language studies may also introduce language bias.

Conclusion

This review underscores that occupational fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia is a complex and multifactorial issue. Our synthesis, despite the limited number of included studies, reveals that work fatigue is driven by high job demands, high effort, sustained strain responses and environmental/contextual factors. Despite the limitations, the evidence provides valuable insights in understanding occupational fatigue, a critical issue that is an important precursor to burn-out. Based on the factors identified, this review proposed an integrated conceptual framework for the development of work fatigue among HCWs in Malaysia, where this framework offers a theory-driven understanding of work fatigue informed by established theoretical work models. By integrating these models, the framework illustrates how high demand depletes energy, sustained effort prevents adequate recovery and prolonged exposure leads to cumulative strain, contributing to the manifestation of work fatigue. This conceptualisation provides further understanding beyond usual descriptive reporting,

Findings should be interpreted cautiously due to limitations, including heterogeneity in study design, methodology, populations and fatigue measurement tools variability that may have limited comparability between studies and precluded quantitative synthesis. Consequently, the generalisability of certain findings across other HCW positions should also be approached with tentativeness. Nevertheless, the findings of this study may at least help in recognising important key areas in mitigating this issue. Future research should prioritise longitudinal designs to enhance causality, standardised fatigue assessment and intervention-based approaches, so as to test interventions based on identified pathways and support an effective fatigue management system in healthcare settings.
