Real‐World Use of Semaglutide for Weight Management: Dose Titration, Discontinuation Patterns and Weight Changes
Authors: Mie Dyberg Skandov, Katrine McLain Christensen, Dagmar Abelone Dalin, Janne Unkerskov, Andreas Brønden, Marie Lund, Mikkel Bring Christensen, Kristian Karstoft
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimIn December 2022, semaglutide injections for weight management were marketed in Denmark. Pre‐marketing trials documented high efficacy when adherence to the treatment and the recommended dose titration schedule was followed. This study aimed to describe real‐world use of semaglutide, including dose titration, discontinuation patterns and effectiveness on body weight in adults with overweight or obesity treated in primary care.Materials and MethodsThis retrospective, observational study included adults prescribed semaglutide for weight management between December 2022 and May 2024 in one general practise in Denmark. The primary outcome was the relative mean change in body weight from baseline to 12‐months follow‐up in individuals who had not discontinued treatment and who had a baseline and a follow‐up weight.ResultsA total of 206 individuals were included (76% women; mean age 48 years, standard deviation (SD) 13). Mean baseline body weight was 105 kg (SD 23.8). Twelve months after treatment initiation, 38% (n = 78) had permanently discontinued semaglutide, whilst 15% (n = 30) had temporarily discontinued (paused) semaglutide. Approximately 48% (n = 98) remained on treatment. Amongst these, 40% were treated with ≤ 1.0 mg/week, 30% with 1.7 mg/week and 30% with 2.4 mg/week. Relative mean body weight change after 12 months was −13.6 (95% CI −15.0 to −12.2)%.ConclusionsBased on the experience from utilisation in a single general practise, semaglutide for weight management is frequently discontinued and titrated slower and to lower maintenance doses in real‐world clinical practise than in the approved labelling. Despite this, the observed mean weight loss for individuals who had not discontinued treatment was substantial.Keywords: effectiveness, primary care, real‐world evidence, semaglutide, weight management

Introduction

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA) have emerged over the past years as a huge breakthrough in the pharmacological treatment of overweight and obesity. In December 2022, semaglutide, a GLP‐1RA, was introduced in Denmark as an injectable treatment for weight management as an adjunct to lifestyle modifications.

Premarketing trials investigating semaglutide for weight management demonstrated an average body weight reduction of approximately 15% after 68 weeks of treatment with the recommended dosing regimen [1]. Moreover, observational studies have reported the effectiveness of semaglutide in real‐world settings to be comparable to that observed in the pre‐marketing trials in terms of weight loss in patients where the recommended dosing regimen is met [2, 3]. However, subsequent real‐world data have shown high variability in dose titration and maintenance dose, with substantial deviations from the recommended dosing regimen [4]. As such, a Danish nationwide cohort study based on prescription redemptions indicated that real‐world dose titrations were slower and maintenance doses lower than those applied in the premarketing trials, with only a minority of individuals following the recommended dosing regimen [5]. Logically, lower maintenance doses are believed to attenuate treatment effectiveness [6].

In addition, recent data indicate that approximately half of the individuals starting semaglutide for weight management discontinue this within 12 months [7]. The underlying causes of the high discontinuation rates remain unclear, but warrant investigation, as these rates are substantially higher than those reported in the premarketing trials.

Therefore, given the substantial differences between dosing regimen and discontinuation pattern between the pre‐marketing trials and the available real‐world data, the aim of this study was to describe the real‐world use of semaglutide for weight management, in a primary care facility, focusing on dose titration, discontinuation rates, adverse events and effectiveness on weight loss and cardiovascular biomarkers in individuals with overweight or obesity.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

We performed a retrospective, observational cohort study including all adult (≥ 18 years) individuals who initiated semaglutide injections for weight management prescribed by five medical doctors in a single general practise (GP) in Albertslund municipality (Denmark) between December 2022 and May 2024. In total, approximately 6400 citizens were affiliated to the GP.

Given the observational nature of the study, approval by the ethics committee was not needed. Data handling was approved by Centre for Health, The Capital Region of Denmark (p‐2024‐15717).

Participants

Eligible individuals were identified through a search in the medical record system of the GP, based on the presence of semaglutide for weight management in the record. The date on which the first prescription was redeemed was considered the initiation date of the treatment. If the initiation date was within the defined study period, the individual was included. Individuals were excluded if they (1) had initiated treatment with semaglutide for weight management outside the study period or (2) had semaglutide for weight management prescribed outside the GP.

Data

Baseline characteristics including age, sex, BMI categories, comorbidities potentially associated with obesity, Diabetes Mellitus type 2 (DM2) status, prior bariatric surgery (gastric sleeve or gastric bypass), prior use of GLP‐1R agonists (yes/no) and number and type of currently prescribed drugs related to the relevant comorbidities were obtained from the medical records.

Semaglutide dose titration patterns for each participant were based on data from the Shared Medication Record (‘Fælles Medicinkort’ (FMK)) [8]. A treatment cycle was considered to cover a treatment period of 4 ± 2 weeks. Individuals who redeemed their prescription for one cycle and subsequently redeemed another prescription in the following cycle were classified as ‘on treatment’, and the redeemed dose was recorded. If an individual did not redeem a prescription during a treatment cycle, but later during the study period resumed and redeemed treatment, the individual was classified as ‘paused’. Individuals who did not redeem their prescription and did not resume treatment during the study period were classified as ‘discontinued’.

Effectiveness data (self‐reported body weight, BMI, blood pressure, HbA1c and blood lipids) were obtained from the medical record at baseline and at 12 months follow‐up. Baseline data were assessed within 3 months before the first redeemed prescription. If more than one measurement was performed during this period, results from the measurement closest to the first redeemed prescription were reported as baseline values. The time window for the follow‐up measurement was defined as 12 ± 4 months after the date of first redeemed prescription. If more than one measurement was performed during this period, the measurement closest to the 12‐months' time point was used.

Information about adverse events and the main reason for discontinuation were obtained from the medical records during the treatment period.

Data extraction was done by one person (MDS). Data quality was checked by repeating the data sampling in a subset of individuals (10%). The repeated data sampling was performed by an independent person (KMC) and did not result in any major corrections to the dataset.

Outcomes

The primary effectiveness outcome was the relative mean change in body weight from baseline to 12‐months follow‐up in individuals who had not discontinued treatment. Secondary effectiveness outcomes included absolute change in body weight, BMI, glycated haemoglobin A1c (Hba1c), triglycerides, total cholesterol and cholesterol fractions in individuals who had not discontinued treatment. Moreover, dose titration patterns, discontinuations incl. reasons for discontinuation and adverse effects were assessed.

Statistical Analyses

Baseline data were summarised using descriptive statistics: Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation; SD) and categorical variables were presented in numbers (percentage).

Effectiveness data (change from baseline) were visually checked for normal distribution. Effectiveness was then evaluated using a paired t‐test, with results presented as the mean difference along with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). The primary and secondary outcome analyses included individuals with baseline and 12‐months follow‐up measurements who had not discontinued treatment during the period (pauses allowed). An exploratory analysis of the dose–response relationship between mean semaglutide dose during cycles 5–13 (following the period of dose escalation according to label‐recommendation) and relative weight loss was compared using one‐way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post hoc tests comparing individuals in three distinct dose groups (≤ 1 mg/week, > 1–≤ 1.7 mg/week and > 1.7–≤ 2.4 mg/week) representing typical maintenance dosing intervals in clinical practise [5].

Analysis of covariance was performed to examine the influence of baseline characteristics—including age, sex, prior bariatric surgery, prior use of GLP‐1R agonists and DM2—on the primary outcome.

For the primary outcome, two sensitivity analyses were performed to assess body weight trajectories for all who started treatment. This included all individuals with a recorded baseline weight who did not have a 12‐month body weight measurement, applying (1) a last‐observation‐carried‐forward (LOCF) approach (imputing the last recorded body weight as the 12‐month body weight measurement), and (2) a ‘return to baseline’ approach (imputing the baseline body weight as the 12‐month body weight measurement).

All statistical analyses were conducted using R studio (version 4.4.1). A p‐value of < 0.05 was chosen to denote statistical significance.

Results

Participants

In total, n = 206 individuals were included in the study (Table 1 and Figure S1). Of these, 194 had a registered baseline body weight, and 116 were included in the primary outcome analysis. In total, n = 145 individuals (70%) had baseline and at least one follow‐up body weight measurement at 12 (±4) months, whereas n = 61 (30%) had no baseline or follow‐up body weight measurement at 12 (±4) months (Table S1). There were no major differences in baseline characteristics between individuals who were or were not included in the primary outcome analysis, nor were there any major differences between individuals who had baseline and follow‐up body weight measurements and individuals who had no baseline or follow‐up body weight measurement (Table S1).

Dose Titrations

Dose titration data are illustrated in Figure 1. Amongst the 206 individuals who initiated semaglutide for weight management, 82.5% started at 0.25 mg, 4% at 0.5 mg, 12% at 1.0 mg, 1% at 1.7 mg and 0.5% at 2.4 mg. After 13 cycles, 128 individuals (62%) were still on treatment, whereas 78 individuals (38%) had discontinued treatment (see below). Of those on treatment in cycle 13, however, 64 (50%) had paused treatment at some point during the 12 months, meaning that only 31% of the individuals who initiated semaglutide for weight management remained on the treatment uninterrupted for 12 months (Figure S2). Median duration of treatment pauses was 1 cycle (equal to 28 days). Of those currently on treatment in cycle 13 (n = 98), 4% received 0.25 mg, 6% received 0.5 mg, 31% received 1.0 mg, 30% received 1.7 mg and 30% received 2.4 mg.

Weekly semaglutide dose according to treatment cycle. One cycle covers a period of 4 weeks of treatment (±2 weeks). Each colour represents the dose (mg) redeemed. Within‐bar numbers indicate the number of individuals at each dose, and below‐bar numbers indicate the total number of individuals who had not permanently discontinued treatment.

Half of the individuals (103 of 206) followed the label‐recommended titration from 0.25 mg to 1.0 mg; thereafter, a minority (4 of 206) adhered to the recommended titration scheme with dose escalations every 4th week until the 2.4 mg maintenance dose.

Changes After 12 Months, Covariance Analysis and Sensitivity Analysis

The primary outcome analysis resulted in a mean relative weight change after 12 (±4) months of −13.6 (95% CI −15.0 to −12.2) % (p < 0.001). Individual weight trajectories over the study period for individuals who had not discontinued treatment, are illustrated in Figure S3. Covariance analysis showed that prior use of GLP‐1RA had a significant impact on relative weight loss, whereas other covariates (age, sex, DM2, prior bariatric surgery and baseline weight) had no influence on the primary outcome (Figure 2). Sensitivity analysis using a LOCF and return to baseline approach for participants without 12 months follow‐up body weight measurement, that is, analyses including all 194 individuals with a baseline weight, resulted in a mean relative weight change after 12 months of −10.7 (−11.8 to 9.6) % and −8.9 (−10.2 to −7.7) %, respectively.

GLP‐1RA = glucagon‐like protein‐1 receptor agonist.

For the secondary outcomes, the mean absolute weight change was −14.5 (−16.2 to −12.8) kg, and Body Mass Index (BMI) change was −5.1 (−5.7 to −4.5) kg/m2. Hba1c decreased by 5 (4–6) mmol/mol (p < 0.001), triglycerides levels were reduced by 0.38 (0.19 to 0.57) mmol/L (p < 0.001), total cholesterol by 0.2 (0.0 to 0.4) mmol/L (p = 0.02) and very‐low‐density lipoprotein (VLDL) by 0.1 (0.0 to 0.2) mmol/L (p < 0.01). No significant changes were seen in low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol or high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol fractions.

Weight Loss and Mean Dose

The relative weight loss versus mean dose of semaglutide in cycle 5–13 is shown in Figure 3. Mean weight loss in the three dose groups were: ≤ 1 mg/week = 11.2 (8.9–13.4) %, > 1–≤ 1.7 mg/week = 15.3 (13.0–17.6) % and > 1.7–≤ 2.4 mg/week = 15.4 (12.6–18.1) %. One‐way ANOVA analyses indicated that individuals taking a mean dose ranging between 1.0 mg and 1.7 mg experienced greater weight loss than individuals taking a mean dose ≤ 1 mg (mean difference 4.2% points, 95% CI 0.4%–7.9%, p = 0.024). Weight loss was numerically higher in individuals taking a mean dose ranging between 1.7 and 2.4 mg compared with individuals taking a mean dose ≤ 1 mg but the difference was not statistically significant (4.2%, 95% CI 0.0% to 8.5%, p = 0.052) and no difference was observed between the > 1.7–≤ 2.4 mg and > 1–≤ 1.7 mg groups (0.1%, 95% CI –4.3% to 4.4%, p = 0.999).

The most frequently reported reason for discontinuation was unacceptable adverse events. As such, 50% (21 out of 42) of the individuals for whom the reason for discontinuation was reported indicated adverse events as the primary reason for discontinuation. Amongst these, gastrointestinal (abdominal pain, constipation, nausea, diarrhoea, eructation, gastroesophageal reflux and vomiting) and general (fatigue, dizziness and headache) symptoms were the most frequently reported (data not shown). Moreover, 24% reported lack of financial resources, 15% reported perceived lack of efficacy, 5% reported weight target achieved and 7% reported other reasons.

Discussion

In this real‐world study evaluating data from a single general practise, we report the utilisation pattern of semaglutide for weight management in a primary care setting. We found that more than one‐third of individuals starting treatment with semaglutide for weight management, discontinue this within 1 year. Moreover, individuals who start treatment are typically uptitrated slower and to lower maintenance doses than the specified labelling. Despite this, the observed mean weight loss after 12 months was substantial for individuals who had not discontinued treatment. Importantly, in sensitivity analyses employing different conservative imputations for individuals without baseline or follow‐up body weight measurements, mean weight loss remained around 9% to 11%, that is, beyond the 5% limit for a clinically relevant weight loss [9].

Our findings regarding the dose titration patterns align with those of Ladebo et al., who reported utilisation patterns in all Danish adults who redeemed their first prescription for semaglutide for weight management in 2022–2023 [5]. Given that the present analysis is based on data from a single general practise, it is reassuring that the utilisation patterns align with those observed at the national level. According to the Summary of Product Characteristics, semaglutide for weight management should be administered once weekly, with a dose increase every fourth week until 2.4 mg, which is intended as the maintenance dose [10]. In the current study, less than 2% (4/206) of the individuals adhered to the recommended dose titration schedule, and only 30% (29/98) of those who had not discontinued or paused treatment (equal to 14% (29/206) of those who initiated treatment) were using the 2.4 mg dose 12 months after treatment initiation. Hence, the way semaglutide for weight management is used clinically differs considerably from the way it has been studied in the pre‐marketing trials. Although this pattern is not atypical for newly marketed drugs, it necessitates the evaluation of the clinical effectiveness of semaglutide for weight management.

Amongst individuals who had not discontinued treatment 12 months after initiation, we observed a mean relative weight loss of 13.6%, however, with substantial inter‐individual differences (Figure S3). In pre‐marketing studies, a mean weight loss of approximately 16% after 52 weeks was reported using the trial product (‘on treatment’) estimand [1]. Whereas a direct comparison of the effect estimates between these studies must be performed cautiously due to methodological differences, this difference in weight loss seems minor and questions the need for high doses of semaglutide for weight management in all individuals, considering the established dose–response relationship for adverse effects [11, 12]. In continuation of this, our exploratory dose–response analysis suggested that higher average semaglutide doses do not necessarily result in greater body weight loss. Whilst this observation is interesting, however, it may represent a spurious finding, given that individuals kept on lower doses are likely different from individuals escalated to higher doses. As an example, an individual experiencing a satisfactory weight loss trajectory is likely not increased in dose despite being below the labelled maintenance dose. On the other hand, an individual not experiencing a satisfactory weight loss trajectory is likely increased in dose if tolerability allows this. As such, this analysis must be seen as hypothesis‐generating only and should be evaluated in further studies. Still, the substantial average weight loss in individuals using semaglutide doses lower than recommended shown here, and supported by other real‐world evidence studies [2, 13], may suggest that rigorous implementation of the dose titration schedule given in the Summary of Product Characteristics is not necessary for weight loss effectiveness in all patients and may potentially lead to treatment discontinuations due to adverse effects.

Except for a lower weight loss in individuals who had previously used GLP‐1RA, something which may likely be due to a GLP‐1‐RA‐induced weight loss prior to inclusion in the current study, no other covariates significantly affected semaglutide‐induced weight loss. Individuals with Type 2 diabetes, however, had a numerically lower weight loss than individuals without Type 2 diabetes, something which was also noticed in the premarketing studies [14]. However, the low number of individuals in some subgroups limits the interpretation.

Approximately one third of the included individuals had discontinued treatment 12 months after initiation. Even though lack of effect was reported as the main reason for discontinuation by only a minority of individuals discontinuing treatment, a lower weight loss effectiveness has been reported in individuals who eventually discontinue treatment compared to individuals who do not discontinue treatment [15]. Hence, the high fraction of individuals discontinuing treatment may have introduced selection bias and potential overestimation of the observed weight loss effectiveness in the present study.

Given that the main reason for discontinuation was only available for 42 out of the 78 individuals who discontinued treatment, the reasons for discontinuation must be interpreted cautiously. Amongst individuals with a reported reason for discontinuation, the most frequently reported reason for discontinuation was adverse events. Whereas discontinuations due to adverse effects were also seen in the pre‐marketing trials [1, 14, 16, 17, 18], the fraction of individuals discontinuing due to adverse effects, especially gastrointestinal, was much higher in this study despite the generally slower titration schedule. This highlights the need for weight‐lowering treatments with greater tolerability, if weight‐lowering treatments should be implemented and sustained in a broader population. In Denmark, semaglutide for weight management is not reimbursed, which means that individuals pay for the treatment themselves. This creates a problem with health inequality, as indicated by a lower proportion of individuals with low income being treated with semaglutide for weight management compared to individuals with high income, despite a larger fraction of individuals with low income being overweight or obese [19]. In the coming years, a substantial number of drugs for weight management will likely be approved [20], hopefully resulting in lower prices and financial constraints being less decisive for treatment initiation and maintenance.

In addition to treatment discontinuations, nearly half of the individuals paused treatment one or more times during the study period. The reasons for this are unclear but again highlight that the way semaglutide for weight management is used in the real world differs from the way it was tested in the pre‐marketing trials. In addition to uncertainties concerning the dose that should be applied for re‐initiation of treatment following a treatment pause, this way of using weight loss drugs may lead to weight cycling. Given that pharmacologically induced weight loss has been shown to include loss of a considerable amount of lean body mass [21], and given that weight cycling is known to result in disadvantageous changes in body composition with a net reduction in fat‐free mass and an increase in fat mass [22], such use of weight lowering drugs may be a point for concern. Future studies should evaluate this.

This study has several limitations. First, body weight was self‐reported, which may have inflated the weight loss results, given that self‐reported body weight measurements are typically underestimated, especially amongst overweight and obese individuals [23]. Second, a substantial fraction of the included individuals had missing data for the primary outcome. Whereas no major differences in baseline characteristics were seen between individuals who were or were not included in the primary outcome analysis, this entails uncertainties in the reported weight loss estimates and may also have inflated the weight loss results. Third, adherence and dosing beyond prescription redemption is unknown since some patients may have adjusted their doses without informing their GP. Fourth, reasons for discontinuation were only obtained from approximately half of the discontinuing individuals, leaving uncertainties regarding the relative importance of the stated reasons for discontinuation. That said, the obtained results concerning reasons for discontinuation align with previous reports [15]. Finally, the data were obtained from only one general practise in the Capital region of Denmark, and factors like prescription behaviour, patient characteristics and socioeconomic factors are likely not representative of the entire Danish population limiting generalizability. Nonetheless, the titration patterns resembled those in a nationwide retrospective observational study [5], and baseline characteristics resemble those of the premarketing studies [1], supporting the relevance of our findings.

In conclusion, based on the experience from utilisation in a single general practise, semaglutide for weight management is frequently discontinued, titrated slower and in many patients, to lower doses than in the approved labelling. Despite this, the observed mean weight loss for individuals who do not discontinue treatment is substantial.
