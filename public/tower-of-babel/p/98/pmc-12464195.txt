Neural structural underlying audiovisual working memory and visual dominance under cognitive load
Authors: Li Jiayu, Zhang Qiuzhu, Li Wenjuan, Zhang Junjun, Jin Zhenlan, Li Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAudiovisual working memory (WM) plays a critical role in multisensory cognitive processing, yet its structural neural correlates remain insufficiently understood. This study employed an audiovisual dual n-back task paradigm and voxel-based morphometry (VBM) to investigate gray matter volume (GMV) associations with behavioral performance in 60 healthy individuals. Behavioral results revealed a significant visual dominance effect under high cognitive load: visual performance remained stable across conditions, whereas auditory performance declined. Structural analyses showed modality-specific GMV correlations. Visual performance was positively associated with GMV in the insula, posterior cingulate, hippocampus, and inferior frontal regions, while auditory performance was negatively correlated with GMV in the angular and middle occipital gyri. Notably, the left cuneus exhibited a strong positive correlation with the Δd prime(visual–auditory) difference under high load, suggesting its potential role in cross-modal resource allocation. Furthermore, cognitive overload appeared to disrupt the structure–behavior associations observed under lower load, highlighting a load-dependent dissociation within executive control and sensory integration regions. Introduction

Working memory is a fundamental cognitive system responsible for the temporary storage and manipulation of information required for complex cognitive tasks such as reasoning, problem-solving, and decision-making1–3. Baddeley’s widely accepted model of working memory divides this system into four components: the central executive, the phonological loop, the visuospatial sketchpad, and the episodic buffer. Among these, the central executive serves as an attentional control mechanism, while the other components act as temporary storage subsystems specialized for auditory, visual, and integrated multimodal information1,4. Despite the theoretical acknowledgement of working memory’s multisensory nature, its multisensory structural basis and the neural mechanisms underlying visual dominance within the context of working memory remain insufficiently explored.

Current research on audiovisual working memory has primarily emphasized behavioral outcomes, with relatively limited exploration of the underlying structural neural correlates. Functional imaging studies have suggested the involvement of parietal and frontal regions in the integration and maintenance of audiovisual stimuli, particularly under tasks demanding high cognitive load5–7. However, these studies largely rely on simple tasks, such as delayed matching paradigms, which often suffer from ceiling effects and fail to capture the complexity of real-world cognitive demands. Moreover, the dual n-back paradigm is more commonly used for intervention or training in clinical practice, and the obtained results of simple task may be biased in the process of promotion to clinical application. However, clinical diseases patients with structural brain changes, such as schizophrenia, major depression, ADHD, etc., have audiovisual integration and working memory problems8–10. Furthermore, while behavioral studies consistently report a “visual dominance effect,” where visual information processing outperforms auditory processing in cognitive tasks11–15 this phenomenon remains underexplored in the context of working memory.

Over the past decade, advances in neuroimaging techniques, such as voxel-based morphometry (VBM), have led to another trend in neuroscience research that relates an individual’s behavioral performance to neuroanatomical features such as GMV, providing a powerful tool for investigating the structural correlates of working memory16–18. VBM applies parametric statistical testing to compare segmented brain tissue components voxel by voxel across groups, enabling the quantitative assessment of gray and white matter density and volume. By quantifying morphological differences, reflecting regional volume differences between populations or between individuals19. Although there are some recent studies on task and resting states of audiovisual working memory, the structural basis, especially in the dual n-back task, still remains unclear6,20–22.

To address these gaps, this study employs an audiovisual dual n-back task paradigm combined with VBM analysis to investigate the structural neural correlates of audiovisual working memory in order to find out the reference brain areas more suitable for clinical intervention. Furthermore, by organizing VBM results by voxel count, we provide a comprehensive visualization of the degree of regional brain involvement, facilitating an intuitional understanding of their roles in multisensory working memory.

We hypothesize the following: (1) visual task performance will surpass auditory task performance, particularly under higher cognitive loads; (2) visual task performance will remain relatively stable across load variations, while auditory tasks will show greater sensitivity to increased demand; and (3) distinct brain regions will exhibit modality-specific associations with GMV, highlighting the structural basis of the visual dominance effect in working memory.

By integrating behavioral and neuroanatomical analyses, this study aims to contribute novel insights into the structural underpinnings of audiovisual working memory, and to find reference brain regions that are more suitable for generalization to clinical brain intervention treatment.

Materials and methods

Participants

Seventy − four healthy participants from the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China were recruited for this study. Fourteen participants were excluded: two for pressing buttons for every trial, two for lacking a T1 image, one for incomplete data, and nine for having a low hit rate (hit rate = 0). The data from the remaining sixty subjects (N = twenty − four females, mean ages = 20.60 ± 1.84 years) were then analyzed. All participants were right − handed with no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders and were free of contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans (e.g., dental plates). All participants provided voluntary consent to participate in the experiment. Written consent was obtained from each participant prior to the study. Experimental procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China.

Stimuli and procedure

This study utilized an enhanced version of the paradigm developed by Jaeggi et al.23. As depicted in Fig. 1, the experiment comprised two tasks: a visual task and an auditory task. Visual and auditory stimuli were presented simultaneously. Participants were instructed to perform both tasks simultaneously. In the visual task, a blue square appeared pseudo − randomly at one of eight different locations (top right, top middle, top left, bottom right, bottom middle, bottom left, right, middle, left) on the computer screen in each trial. In the auditory task, one of eight consonants (C, D, G, K, P, Q, T, V) was presented through headphones in each trial. Both visual and auditory stimuli were presented for 500 ms, with a 2500 ms inter − trial interval. Each block contained six visual and six auditory targets, with only two pairs of targets appearing simultaneously in both streams of stimuli. Consequently, each block included a total of 16 targets distributed across 20 + n trials. The analysis comprised four blocks (two blocks for the 2 − back task and two blocks for the 4 − back task).

Audiovisual working memory dual task. The experiment comprised visual task and auditory task. Visual subtask: determine whether the current square occupies the same position as the one presented two or four positions back (eight positions in total). Auditory subtask: determine whether the current letter is same with the 2 − back/4 − back letter (a total of eight letters, C, D, G, K, P, Q, T, V). Visual and auditory stimuli were presented simultaneously. Participants were required to complete visual and auditory subtasks simultaneously. The experiment consisted of two blocks altogether.

Image acquisition

MRI data were acquired using a 3.0 − T GE Sigma scanner (General Electric, Milwaukee, WI, USA) equipped with an 8 − channel phased − array head coil at the MRI Brain Imaging Center of the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China. High − resolution T1 − weighted anatomical images were acquired using the following parameters: TR = 5.96ms; TE = 1.96ms; FOV = 256 × 256 mm2; FA = 9°; matrix size = 256 × 256; slice thickness/gap = 1.0 mm/no gap; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1mm3; number of slices = 176. Participants were instructed to close their eyes, relax, and refrain from engaging in any cognitive activity or falling asleep during the MRI scan.

Data analysis

Behavioral data analysis

RT analysis included only trials with correct responses, and trials with RT greater than three standard deviations were excluded.

Structural imaging data analysis

High − resolution T1 − weighted images were preprocessed using the Statistical Parameter Mapping toolbox (SPM12, https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) and the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12, http://dbm.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat12/). The preprocessing steps were based on the default settings of the CAT12 toolbox, including (1) visually checking for artifacts and orientation of raw images, (2) Segmentation of gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid, (3) inter − subject registration and modulation, (4) using high − dimensional DARTEL normalization normalizing the space to the Montreal Neurological Institute space (1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5mm3), (5) spatial smoothing using an 8 mm full − width − half − maximum Gaussian kernel, and (6) estimating the total intracranial volume (TIV) to subsequently correct for different brain sizes and volumes across participants.

Multiple regression analyses were conducted to investigate the correlation between brain region gray matter volume (GMV) and participants’ behavioral performance, controlling for age, gender, and TIV as covariates of noninterest22,24–27. To eliminate gray − white matter boundary effects, we used a mask with an absolute threshold in these GMV analysis. It means voxels with gray matter or white matter values under 0.2 were discarded. To correct for the results of multiple comparisons, we used a threshold of p < 0.001 (uncorrected) and a cluster size of > 20 voxels. And Bonferroni multiple comparison correction was conducted for the correlation between GMV and behavioral outcomes.

Furthermore, we investigated the involvement of different brain lobes in the audiovisual working memory dual task by quantifying the number of voxels from various lobes based on the VBM analysis results. We visualized these findings using a Sankey diagram for brevity and clarity.

Results

Behavior results

D prime, and RT were subjected to a repeated measures ANOVA with a 2 (loads: 2 − back, 4 − back) × 2 (target modality: visual, auditory) design. Regarding d prime, a main effect of load was observed (F(1,59) = 30.03, p < 0.001, =0.34), while there was no main effect of target modality (F(1,59) = 2.89, p = 0.09, = 0.05). However, the interaction between load and target modality was significant (F(1,59) = 7.91, p < 0.01, = 0.12). Post−hoc t−tests revealed that sensitivity in the 2−back condition was higher than in the 4−back condition for both visual (t(59) = 3.15, p < 0.01) and auditory(t(59) = 5.90, p < 0.001) targets. Moreover, participants demonstrated significantly higher sensitivity to visual targets than to auditory targets under the 4−back condition (t(59) = 3.57, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). There were no significant main effects for RT (load: F(1,59) = 8 × 10−5, p > 0.99, < 0.001; target modality: F(1,59) = 0.03, p = 0.86, =0.001), nor was there an interaction effect (F(1,59) = 0.49, p = 0.49, = 0.008). Additionally, no notable differences were observed between conditions (visual−auditory: 2−back: t(59) = − 0.80, p=0.43; 4−back: t(59)=0.29, p=0.78; 2-back − 4-back: visual: t(59) = − 0.48, p = 0.64; auditory: t(59)=0.34, p = 0.74) (Fig. 2B).

Behavior-to-brain anatomy relations

We explored brain regions whose GMV significantly correlated with participants’ behavior, including d prime and RT in 2 back, 4 back, and 2 - back − 4 - back. Additionally, we included ∆d prime (d primevisual − d primeauditory) for the 4 − back condition to assess the visual dominance effect. Age, gender, and TIV were controlled for in all subsequent analyses.

Involvement of different lobes in audiovisual working memory

We quantified the number of voxels associated with visual and auditory performance in 2-back and 4-back tasks. Although it does not provide fairly strong evidence, this reflects to some extent how many voxels in different regions are anatomically related to audiovisual working memory.

According to Fig. 11, visual tasks involved a greater number of voxels than auditory tasks, accounting for 82.81% of the total voxels involved in the audiovisual task (visual/total: 2351/2839). In visual working memory, the left frontal lobe contributed the most anatomy-related voxels, representing 42.54% (1000/2351) and 35.96% (1021/2839) of the visual and total contributions, respectively. This region exhibited a greater tendency for negative correlation with visual tasks (positive: 25.07%, 256/1021; negative: 72.87%, 744/1021). In contrast, the right frontal lobe involved fewer voxels, but had a higher number of structure-related voxels in visual tasks than in auditory tasks (visual: 138; auditory: 86). A similar pattern was observed in the left parietal lobe, which contributed the most voxels in auditory tasks, accounting for 49.59% (242/488). Compared to visual tasks, the left parietal lobe displayed a greater structural correlation with auditory tasks (visual: 68.75% [242/352]; auditory: 31.25% [110/352]). In contrast to negative correlation, it showed a greater positive correlation with visual tasks (positive: 54.26%, 191/352; negative: 31.25%, 110/352). The right parietal lobe involved very few voxels and was only negatively correlated with auditory tasks. Furthermore, we found that visual tasks rely more on the left hemisphere than auditory tasks (χ² = 18.47, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study investigated the structural neural correlates of audiovisual working memory and the visual dominance effect using a dual-task n-back paradigm coupled with voxel-based morphometry (VBM) analysis. The findings provide novel insights into how GMV in distinct brain regions relates to behavioral performance in audiovisual working memory tasks under varying cognitive loads, emphasizing the distinct anatomical substrates supporting audiovisual WM and the neural basis of visual dominance, and providing a modal-specific reference site for clinical intervention and training.

Audiovisual working memory and neural correlates

The results of VBM analysis highlighted the involvement of multiple cortical and subcortical regions in audiovisual working memory, including the frontal-parietal areas, limbic lobes, temporal lobes, insular lobes, and occipital lobes. These findings align with prior research implicating the executive control network (ECN) in multisensory working memory tasks, particularly in managing cognitive load and maintaining attention during dual-task conditions20,28. Specifically, significant positive correlations between GMV and d-prime scores for visual targets were observed in regions such as the right and left insula, left inferior frontal gyrus, and left posterior cingulate. These regions are known to play a role in integrating sensory information, sustaining attention, and facilitating visuospatial memory storage29–31.

For auditory targets, negative correlations between d-prime and GMV in areas such as the left superior frontal gyrus and angular gyrus, suggesting that the performance of auditory tasks may be limited by the information processing ability of these regions, and differential involvement of these regions in auditory working memory tasks. This result supports the hypothesis that auditory and visual tasks engage distinct domain-specific regions, as previously suggested by domain-general and domain-specific theories of working memory28,32.

Variability in Structure–Behavior correlation induced by cognitive load

In the analysis of visual-target trials, a marked reduction in brain regions showing significant GMV correlations with behavioral performance was observed when comparing the 4-back to the 2-back condition. Specifically, regions such as the insula, left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), hippocampus, left posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and left Heschl’s gyrus were structurally associated with performance in the 2-back task, but these associations were no longer observed under the 4-back condition. Instead, a novel negative correlation emerged in the right IFG.

Although 2-back tasks are typically considered to impose relatively low cognitive load, the audiovisual dual n-back paradigm used in this study significantly increases task complexity. Therefore, even under 2-back conditions, participants may have experienced considerable cognitive demands. Consequently, the 2-back task in this context may represent a high-load condition, while the 4-back task likely induced cognitive overload, potentially leading to functional disruption within the neural system. This cognitive overload may account for the disappearance of structure–behavior correlations previously observed under the 2-back condition.

More specifically, the insula plays a critical role in conflict monitoring and resource allocation and is a key node involved in dynamic switching between the default mode network (DMN) and the central executive network (CEN), which includes the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and PPC. Under low cognitive load, the insula shows stronger connectivity with the DMN, whereas high load typically enhances its coupling with the CEN, facilitating cognitive control and executive functioning33. In this study, the demanding nature of the audiovisual dual n-back task may have already engaged the insula’s high-load pathway in the 2-back condition, while the 4-back condition induced overload, causing the system to fail to switch from DMN to CEN effectively, resulting in dominant DMN connectivity.

The IFG, which often functions in concert with the insula, also exhibited load-dependent dissociation. The left IFG, associated primarily with verbal processing, may reflect participants’ use of subvocal rehearsal strategies34. In contrast, the right IFG, more involved in conflict monitoring and response inhibition, became correlated with behavior only under 4-back conditions. When the insula no longer needs to work hard, the part of the IFG that works in coordination with it naturally reduces its contribution. However, the increased task difficulty may place higher demands on inhibitory control, thereby recruiting the right IFG. Furthermore, in the 4-back condition, maintaining conflict monitoring and response inhibition imposes substantial cognitive demands. A larger GMV in this region may reflect greater inhibitory capacity; however, engaging this function likely consumes already limited cognitive resources, thereby resulting in a negative correlation between GMV and task performance.

Previous research also suggests that the contribution of the left PCC decreases as cognitive load increases, with stronger interactions observed with prefrontal regions under high-load conditions35–37. This aligns with our finding that the left PCC was behaviorally relevant under 2-back but not under 4-back, where instead the right IFG became significant. As for the hippocampus, which is well-established as being critical for memory processing, prior studies indicate that hippocampal damage does not affect performance at low loads, but mainly works under high loads33,38. Our findings of hippocampal involvement in 2-back but not 4-back suggest that the 2-back condition already imposed that it was already in a high-load state at 2-back. At 4-back, due to overload, the role of the hippocampus was not found. Moreover, when performing 4-back, there is a reduction in the relevant regions for each condition as a whole. This further indicates that for the audiovisual dual n-back task, 4-back has caused the cognitive overload of the participants.

Regarding Heschl’s gyrus, under 2-back, due to the binding of auditory and visual information during the task process, when the subjects saw visual stimuli, they associated auditory stimuli. And previous studies have shown that this mental image can also activate or partially activate the corresponding brain regions39,40. Therefore, under the condition of visual targets, we obtained Heschl’s gyrus located in the primary auditory cortex. This effect, however, disappeared under 4-back, likely due to limited cognitive resources and overloading of the neural system.

In summary, we propose a conceptual framework in which cognitive overload disrupts functional integration across brain networks and depletes the cognitive resources required for inhibitory control. This framework provides a biologically plausible explanation for the distinct and complex patterns of GMV–behavior correlations observed under the high-load (2-back) and overload (4-back) task conditions in this study.

The role of the cuneus in audiovisual working memory

Our results demonstrate a positive correlation between GMV in the cuneus and Δd prime (visual–auditory) under the 4-back condition, indicating that the cuneus may play a significant role in visual dominance during dual-modality working memory tasks. Prior research suggests that the cuneus is involved in sustained attention, task response time, executive control, verbal memory, and anticipatory stimulus categorization41–43. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that the cuneus functions as a resource allocator in audiovisual n-back tasks.

Specifically, the cuneus may be engaged in anticipatory allocation before stimulus onset and subsequently involved in distributing cognitive resources following basic processing in primary sensory cortices. This resource allocation role likely extends beyond pure sensory processing, potentially explaining its correlation with Δd prime but not with unimodal d prime. Anatomically, the cuneus is connected anteriorly to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex via the parieto-occipital sulcus, supporting planning functions, and posteriorly to the calcarine sulcus, a key region for visual processing. These structural features, together with its functional connectivity to the dorsal attention network, may predispose the cuneus to prioritize visual processing, especially under high-load conditions where cognitive resources are limited.

No such association was observed in the 2-back condition, nor was visual dominance evident. This might be due to the fact that when 4-back, the competition for resources is more intense, and the regulatory mechanism of the cuneus is forced to have a very significant effect. When it comes to 2-back, resources are abundant, and there is no need for competition. The cuneus, the regulator which prefers visual stimuli, does not need to work hard. Therefore, the existence of visual dominance has not been discovered.

Additionally, GMV in the cuneus was negatively correlated with RTvisual in the 2-back condition and with RTauditory in the 4-back condition. Together with the Δd prime (visual-auditory) findings, these results suggest that under high cognitive load, the cuneus allocates more resources to visual processing, enhancing discrimination at the cost of response latency. Conversely, fewer resources are devoted to auditory processing, which may compromise accuracy but reduce response time. These findings also correspond to the result that our visual tasks are more stable than auditory tasks in the face of load changes.

Limitations and future directions

This study proposes a framework wherein the 2-back task imposes high cognitive load, while the 4-back task induces cognitive overload, leading to disrupted network coordination and increased demand on inhibitory mechanisms. We also highlight a potential role of the left cuneus in cross-modal resource allocation. Together, these findings offer a plausible neurobiological account for the complex pattern of GMV–behavior correlations observed in the present study. However, it is important to emphasize that this overload-induced neural system breakdown is one possible explanation, but not the only one. Future studies should aim to validate these mechanisms using larger samples, more finely tuned task designs, and more diverse neural indicators.

Finally, although we tried a threshold (PFDR < 0.05, k > 20), a significant positive correlation between GMV and visual reaction time was observed only in the precentral gyrus under the 4-back condition (see S1). In order to compare the results in different situations and find the specific areas that are more suitable for clinical intervention, we choose the current threshold(P < 0.001, k > 20). Future research may benefit from integrating structural, functional, and stimulation-based data to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

This study, by integrating structural features with behavioral performance, identified key brain regions associated with audiovisual working memory performance and cognitive load. The results also emphasize the differential contributions of the left and right frontal-parietal regions to different modalities of working memory. Despite some limitations, this study advances our understanding of the neural basis of audiovisual working memory and the visual dominance effect, providing valuable insights for future clinical brain intervention applications.
