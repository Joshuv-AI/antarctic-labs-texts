Enhanced prediction of electric field distribution in lightning protection systems with heterogeneous ground
Authors: Ali Khechekhouche, Mabrouka Romdhane, Abdelmalek Gacem, Nadhir Mesbahi, Mansoor Alturki, Yasser Almalaq, Mohamed Naoui, Abdullah Albaker, Ayoob Alateeq, Tawfik Guesmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research investigates the effect of geological discontinuities on the electric breakdown distributions near vertical and horizontal lightning conductors (VLC and HLC) in various ground conditions. To investigate this effect, numerical simulations were carried out using COMSOL Multiphysics. The simulations took into consideration detailed models of the lightning conductor geometries, ground structures, and parameters of the lightning conductor for varying ratios of horizontal distance to conductor height (D/hc, where D = horizontal distance between the lightning conductor and ground discontinuity interface, and hc = height of the lightning conductor above ground level). Results show that for D/hc = 1.5, the electric field strength near the lightning conductor and discontinuity interface is 20% lower than that obtained from the homogeneous model without this conductor. On the other hand, for D/hc ≥ 3.5, the electric breakdown strength agrees well with the conductor less model, indicating negligible impact on discharge attraction. The equipotential lines and field intensities around the interface have shown non-uniform distributions. Results have brought forth important practical implications in developing an efficient lightning protection system over different types of earth.

Introduction

Traditional methods for lightning protection frequently depend on electro-geometrical models to determine the attractive radius and shielding zone of lightning conductors, based on striking distance equations derived from experimental data1,2. These models do not compute electric field distributions directly but rather provide engineering estimates of attachment points. Notably, these characteristics, especially the conductivity and permittivity of the grounding medium, play a crucial role in determining the locations where lightning will connect with a structure3,4. Comprehensive studies have been undertaken to clarify the influence of electrical grounding characteristics on the distribution of electric fields in a lightning conductor under different grounding setups5,6. Such electric field distributions signify regions with the greatest likelihood of a lightning occurrence. Research has indicated that, in contrast to the forecasts made by traditional electro-geometrical models7,8, the actual electric field may be considerably greater for a lightning conductor placed on non-uniform heterogeneous or heterogeneous grounding systems, which more accurately reflect real-world conditions than the ideal, perfectly conducting plane typically posited in theoretical frameworks9,10.

To gain a deeper understanding of the changes in electric field distribution, the electrical properties of rod-air gap configurations under negative lightning impulse voltage have been investigated for both heterogeneous and homogeneous grounding conditions. The results of these investigations showed that the discharge phenomena differ significantly from those observed in configurations with a perfectly conducting homogeneous ground plane11,12. This phenomenon takes place because of the non-uniform characteristics and discontinuities within the grounding system, which make the path of least resistance for the lightning current much different and thus alter the distribution of the electric field around the lightning conductor13,14. This study uses the standard 1.2/50 µs impulse (IEC 60060-1) for reproducibility and to isolate geometric effects. However, natural lightning often exhibits non-standard oscillatory waveforms that significantly affect insulation performance. Thus, our quantitative results are specific to the standard waveform and provide a baseline for future studies with non-standard impulses15–17. Among the different numerical modeling options, the Finite Element Method (FEM) will be employed. FEM is a well-known, powerful tool in high voltage engineering to deal with electrostatic field problems18. The algorithm is exceptionally good at performing an electric field distribution between electrodes with a variety of intervening spaces containing different dielectric material-insulators with differing electrical properties19. This also makes FEM particularly useful for the analysis of complex grounding configurations where conductivity and permittivity are very much variant in the system20,21. The key idea behind the basis of FEM utilizes a variational principle, seeking the minimum total system energy, and is thus equivalent to the governing equation of electrostatics, which is Laplace’s equation. The accuracy of the electric field configurations computed herein will be verified by cross-comparison with measurements extracted from controlled laboratory experiments utilizing a rod-plane air gap configuration22. The incidence of lightning strikes presents a considerable hazard to high-voltage transmission and distribution networks. Conventional lightning protection systems, which are frequently grounded in empirical models, may fail to sufficiently address the intricate interactions that occur between lightning, the structural components, and the adjacent ground.

The main objective of the present study is to investigate, analyze, and discuss the influences of ground discontinuities on the electric field arising from a lightning conductor under negative lightning waves. Research indicates that approximately 70% to 90% of all lightning strikes are negative23. This predominance is because negative lightning typically occurs when a negatively charged cloud discharges to the positively charged ground, which is a more common occurrence compared to positive lightning strikes. In presenting how a sharp ground discontinuity (aluminum-air interface) affects the electric field distribution, we aim to provide quantitative data that can inform improved design of protection systems in locations with known ground irregularities, such as soil boundaries, retaining walls, or changes in surface material.

The present study extends the existing research by investigating the electric field distribution surrounding horizontal and vertical lightning conductors located on heterogeneous grounding systems. A complex approach will be used in this investigation, with both experimental and numerical models being considered. The main goal of this research is the validation of a numerical model realized by FEM for the simulation of electric field distribution under lightning impulse voltage in a real configuration with heterogeneous grounding. Then, this model will be used for the study of more complicated lightning protection problems. By incorporating the effects of heterogeneous grounding systems into our modeling frameworks, we are able to refine the accuracy of lightning protection assessments and further develop the establishment of protection strategies for real structures. This study contributes to providing quantitative experimental data on electric field distortion caused by ground surface discontinuities under lightning impulse; identifying a critical distance threshold (D/hc ≥ 3.5) where heterogeneity effects become negligible; demonstrating that conductor orientation (horizontal vs. vertical) significantly affects sensitivity to ground irregularities; and validating a FEM model against experimental measurements, enabling reliable simulation of complex configurations. These findings offer practical guidance for optimizing lightning protection system design in sites with non-uniform topography.

This paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 describes the experimental setup, including the heterogeneous ground configuration, measurement techniques using the distributed capacitance probe, and test conditions. Section 3 presents the finite element method (FEM) modeling approach, including the governing equations, boundary conditions, and mesh strategy. Section 4 provides the results and analysis, starting with the reference homogeneous ground case, followed by a systematic investigation of horizontal and vertical lightning conductors under various D/hc ratios and conductor positions relative to the ground discontinuity. Finally, Sect. 5 summarizes the key findings, discusses practical implications for lightning protection system design, and suggests directions for future research.

Experimental setup

The heterogeneous grounding configuration examined in this study consists of a 4 mm thick square metal sheet, which serves as the “heterogeneous ground.” As illustrated in Fig. 1, the sheet comprises two distinct surface levels, each with dimensions of 1 m by 1 m.

To simulate Negative lightning discharge waves during its final jump, a 4.8 mm diameter iron rod is used. This rod (HV) is connected to a lightning impulse generator capable of providing a pulse of 1.2 /50 µs, 600 kVpeak, and 4 kJ.

The position of the discharge conductor is controlled with great precision by two parameters: its height (h) relative to the grounding system and its horizontal distance (D) from the lightning conductor (hc). A positive value of (D) denotes the lightning conductor’s location at an elevated level, whereas a negative value represents its placement at a lower level. The variations in height (h) correspond to those applied in homogeneous configurations (uniform grounding), thereby establishing a stable baseline for comparative analysis. The selected horizontal distances (d and D) are intended to preserve uniform ratios of d/h and D/h across varying h values. The developed method ensures the same number of configurations are analyzed for investigation. To avoid destructive discharges around the probes due to high currents, one might use a possible voltage of 0.3U0% for each chosen height, h, of the lightning conductor (U0% is the breakdown voltage for the equivalent rod-plane configuration). The value of voltage is the same regardless of whether the lightning conductor is positioned in the top or bottom portion of the inhomogeneous plane. To achieve the required test voltage of 0.3U0%, the breakdown voltage, U0%, is determined by a consistent multiple-step approach using Gaussian arithmetic scale paper. This research covers all types of possibilities, including horizontal and vertical lightning conductors facing both high and low-level configurations on the heterogeneous earth. Modeling and analysis

This paper deals with the modeling and study of the electric field distribution in rod-plane air gaps subjected to negative lightning impulse voltage. The main methodology adopted for this study is the FEM to model electric field distributions. The principle of this method consists of minimizing a certain functional instead of directly solving the descriptive equations of the physical phenomenon of the system24–26. The mesh density refers to how finely the computational domain is divided into smaller elements for analysis. A higher mesh density typically leads to more accurate results because it allows for better resolution of electric field variations, especially near critical areas such as conductor tips or ground discontinuities. The characterization of the grounding system’s wideband input impedance27 similarly requires careful attention to numerical resolution for accurate transient analysis.

In this paper, the Adaptive Tetrahedral mesh is used when certain areas require finer resolution, such as near points of singularity or discontinuities. This approach optimizes computation time while maintaining precision in critical zones. Simulations of nonthermal electrical discharges in air over solid insulating barriers28,29 have demonstrated the effectiveness of similar adaptive meshing techniques for capturing discharge phenomena in inhomogeneous configurations, supporting our choice of meshing strategy. This type of mesh maintains precision in critical zones, particularly suited for modeling systems where conductivity and permittivity vary, especially in terrains with geological discontinuities. Results and analysis

The paper studies the effect of different heterogeneous grounding configurations on the electric field distributions around the lightning conductor. These heterogeneous grounding configurations represent a more realistic model of actual grounding conditions compared to a homogeneous (uniform) earth model. The present analysis is based on combining both experimental and simulation methods, which uses a flat metallic sheet to simulate a homogeneous earth, as shown in Fig. 2. The electric field distribution obtained from this setup will be compared with the theoretical distribution expected for a homogeneous earth model. This comparison will highlight the effect of irregularities within the grounding system on the behavior of the electric field and, consequently, on the performance of the lightning conductor. Through the comparative analysis of these electric field distributions, researchers can obtain a deeper understanding of the manner in which the complexities inherent in grounding systems, characterized by the heterogeneous earth, diverge from the idealized conditions and how such divergences may influence the dynamics of lightning strikes as well as the efficacy of lightning protection systems.

Numerical computations using the FEM method

The study emphasized the fact that real grounding systems are often heterogeneous, implying their electrical properties vary due to soil composition, moisture content, and other environmental influences. This is an important fact since the electric field distribution around a lightning conductor may, under these conditions, seriously deviate from predictions obtained by conventional homogeneous models.

The results show specific configurations of lightning conductors that perform better under different soil resistivity conditions. It is found, for instance, that at certain spacing to height ratios (D/hc), the electric field strengths are significantly lower; this would imply optimal positions of conductors where potential ground rise and safety may be improved upon the occurrence of lightning strikes.

The study highlights the influence of inconsistencies in grounding systems on discharge behavior. For example, when the horizontal separation between the lightning conductor and the ground interface is reduced, the electric field distribution becomes more favorable for the interception of lightning strikes. Discussion

A comparative examination of electric field distributions surrounding a lightning conductor in both homogeneous and heterogeneous grounding systems uncovers significant variations that affect the efficacy of lightning protection. Within the homogeneous system (Fig. 4), the intensity of the electric field tends to be relatively consistent at greater distances (D/hc ≥ 2), establishing a reference point for analyzing the behavior of the field around the lightning conductor. However, with the introduction of heterogeneous grounding, as shown in Fig. 5, the electric field exhibits strong discontinuities at the interface, especially for reduced d/h ratios (D/hc < 2). This discontinuity reconfigures the “rod-plane” arrangement into a “rod-rod” configuration, which influences the trajectory of the electric field and the phenomena of discharge, indicating a tendency for lightning strikes to preferentially target the lightning conductor as opposed to the lower ground plane.

Numerical simulations performed by the finite element method, presented in Fig. 6, also show that vertical conductors generate a more concentrated electric field near the conductor (E = 3.5 10⁻⁶ kV/cm), which makes them more effective in carrying lightning currents compared to horizontal conductors (E = 2.5 10⁻⁶ kV/cm). Upon adjusting the position of the lightning rod in relation to the ground discontinuity (Figs. 7 and 9), the patterns of field intensity (E = 1.5 10⁻⁶ kV/cm) remain stable, underscoring the critical role of strategic positioning in enhancing lightning protection systems.

The findings demonstrate that the complexities inherent in heterogeneous grounding systems substantially modify the distributions of the electric field, necessitating thorough contemplation of these elements in the formulation of lightning protection strategies. This research has examined the effect of geological discontinuities on the distribution of the electric field surrounding both vertical and horizontal lightning conductors. By manipulating the distance ratio (D/hc) that exists between the lightning conductor and the interface of the discontinuity, this distance notably influences the distribution of the electric field, thereby impacting the probability of lightning strikes. We have acquired a significant understanding of the electric field distribution near lightning conductors on non-uniform ground.

The varied ground conditions can drastically alter the electric field configurations, leading to unexpected behaviors and potential weaknesses in lightning protection systems. Based on that, knowledge of the role of ground discontinuities helps engineers in improving the placement of conductors for lightning to work effectively. These findings contribute to the improved design of lightning protection systems by providing quantitative data on how ground surface irregularities modify the electric field environment that governs the initial stages of attachment. A rigorous comparison between experimental measurements and FEM simulations reveals both strong correlations and instructive discrepancies (Figs. 6 and 7). At critical configurations: For D/hc = 1.5, experimental data show a 20% field strength reduction near the conductor, while FEM predicts 22%, yielding a 2% deviation.

Key findings for LPS design include a critical threshold of D/hc ≥ 3.5, where discontinuities become negligible and can be ignored in standard design, while for D/hc < 2.5, field modifications of 15–25% must be considered. The 20–25% field reduction beneath conductors near discontinuities does not imply enhanced interception but rather indicates reduced conductor attractiveness facing the discontinuity, enhanced interface attractiveness (40–75% increase), and potential redirection of strikes from conductor to interface—a critical safety consideration. The sensitivity difference between vertical (5% reduction) and horizontal (20–25% reduction) conductors provides clear selection guidance. Vertical conductors are strongly preferred in complex terrain. These rules enable site-specific refinement within international standards3,5.

Conclusions and future work

Conclusion

This paper investigates how ground discontinuities influence the electric field distribution around vertical and horizontal lightning conductors at varying D/hc ratios. The study finds that when the conductor is far from the discontinuity (D/hc ≥ 3.5), the field behaves as in a uniform ground, showing negligible impact. However, at closer distances (D/hc ≈ 1.5), a clear field discontinuity appears, reducing field intensity near the conductor and shifting the system behavior from a “rod-plane” to a “rod-rod” configuration. This change enhances the efficiency of electrical charge interception at the laboratory scale thanks to the point effect, where sharp points intensify electric fields and attract discharges more effectively under controlled pulsed conditions. Studying the behavior and efficiency of lightning interception in a laboratory setting provides a reliable experimental model that can be used to simulate and analyze the phenomenon in the real world. The research provides quantitative evidence of up to 20% field strength reduction near discontinuities, identifies threshold distances where heterogeneity becomes insignificant, and validates a finite element method (FEM) approach for analyzing such systems. These results offer practical guidelines for optimizing lightning protection system (LPS) design in areas with non-uniform ground conditions, improving safety and performance. Although the models simplify real soil properties, the study significantly advances understanding of how terrain heterogeneity affects lightning protection effectiveness.

Future work

While this study provides valuable insights into the effects of ground surface discontinuities on electric field distribution under laboratory conditions, several directions for future research remain. The analysis should be extended to continuous conductivity variations and layered soil structures to better represent real-world geological heterogeneity. The validated FEM model could be integrated with dynamic leader propagation models to simulate the full attachment process rather than only the initial electrostatic conditions. Investigations using non-standard lightning impulse waveforms would help assess how waveform characteristics influence field redistribution near discontinuities. Field-scale experiments on actual structures with instrumented grounding systems would validate the scalability of these laboratory findings. Finally, the development of simplified design charts or equations based on parametric FEM studies could provide practitioners with readily applicable tools for optimizing lightning conductor placement in complex terrain. These future directions would further bridge the gap between controlled laboratory investigations and real-world lightning protection applications.
