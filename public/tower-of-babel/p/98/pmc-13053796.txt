Neuropathic Pain Management in Rehabilitation: Investigating Prescribing Practices and Pain Scores in Inpatients at a Rehabilitation Hospital
Authors: Ritmay Spiteri, Muriel Belizzi, Andrei Agius Anastasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pain is a prevalent and complex symptom in neurorehabilitation, with differentiation between neuropathic pain (NP) and musculoskeletal pain (MSK) often proving challenging. This study evaluates the effectiveness of the Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs (LANSS) and Douleur Neuropathique en 4 Questions (DN4) tools in distinguishing NP from MSK pain, aiming to guide appropriate analgesic selection in a rehabilitation setting. Seventy inpatients undergoing active rehabilitation (32 males, 38 females) aged 18–91 years (mean: 67 years) were assessed using LANSS and DN4 scores over a 2-week period. Prescribed analgesia was recorded and correlated with pain classification based on these scores. The results showed that stroke patients exhibited a higher prevalence of NP, reflected in elevated LANSS and DN4 scores. Spinal cord injury (SCI) patients had the highest total scores, often necessitating dual therapy with pregabalin and amitriptyline. Conversely, patients with fractures had the lowest scores, suggesting predominantly MSK pain, with minimal neuropathic features. Pregabalin was prescribed to 29% of stroke patients but only 6% of fracture patients. In conclusion, LANSS and DN4 effectively identified NP, with DN4 proving easier to interpret. Findings underscore the need for targeted analgesic strategies in neurorehabilitation particularly that of NP in stroke and SCI patients. This is still under-researched in literature, compared to conditions like diabetic neuropathy, and future studies are needed to explore tailored pain management protocols in rehabilitation patients.

INTRODUCTION

Pain, as defined by the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP), is “an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage.” It is a complex, multidimensional, and subjective phenomenon that can be broadly categorized into 2 main types: nociceptive and neuropathic pain (NP) [1].

Nociceptive pain serves as a protective mechanism, activating the nervous system in response to harmful stimuli sub-divided into somatic and visceral pain depending on the origin of the disturbance.

NP, on the other hand, is defined by the IASP as “pain arising as a direct consequence of a lesion or disease affecting the somatosensory system.” Differentiating NP from nociceptive pain can be challenging due to overlapping symptoms [2].

Differentiating between pain types ensures selection of interventions aligns with pathophysiology. Identifying neuropathic involvement avoids unnecessary escalation of dosage or surgery in musculoskeletal treatments, instead directing toward appropriate nerve-focused diagnostics (e.g., electromyography, nerve conduction studies, imaging) when needed. Especially in the context of rehabilitation settings, mechanistic diagnosis informs multidisciplinary care plans—blending pharmacologic, physical, educational, and psychological therapies tailored to pain phenotype [3].

To aid in this differentiation, several validated screening tools are employed in clinical practice, including the Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs (LANSS) and the Douleur Neuropathique en 4 Questions (DN4) [4].

The LANSS is a 7-item instrument that combines symptom-based questions with findings from a brief sensory examination; a score greater than 12 is suggestive of NP [5]. The DN4 is a shorter, 10-item questionnaire assessing altered sensations such as tingling, burning, itching, and numbness. A score of 5 or more is considered indicative of NP [6].

The LANSS is less frequently used as a standalone tool in rehabilitation settings, primarily due to its lower sensitivity in this population. It is often employed alongside the DN4 to mitigate the risk of false negatives; however, its specificity remains high when NP is identified [7].

DN4 is primarily used in the rehab setting as an initial screening tool, for physiotherapy intake and longitudinal tracking, useful for its high sensitivity (85%–100%, moderate to high specificity in spinal cord injury (SCI) patients with robust across validated settings and different translations available [8].

The primary goal of identifying NP is to guide the selection of analgesics based on the underlying pain mechanism, thereby enhancing the efficacy of pain management strategies. Current treatment guidelines—predominantly based on evidence from randomized controlled trials involving patients with painful diabetic neuropathy [9] recommend 3 first-line pharmacological agents: (1) antidepressants with combined norepinephrine and serotonin reuptake inhibition (e.g., tricyclic antidepressants [TCAs] and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors [SNRIs]), (2) calcium channel α2-δ ligands (e.g., gabapentin and pregabalin), and (3) topical lidocaine (5% patch). Opioids and tramadol are generally regarded as second-line therapies, except in specific clinical scenarios where first-line options may be contraindicated or ineffective.

Although diabetic neuropathy is supported by extensive mechanistic and clinical evidence, NP associated with SCI and central post-stroke pain (CPSP) remains comparatively underexplored across multiple domains. Prevalence estimates for SCI-related pain vary widely, while CPSP incidence is inconsistently reported and frequently underrecognized.

Clinical research trials investigating diabetic neuropathy are numerous and methodologically rigorous clinical trials targeting SCI-related NP and CPSP—2 major diagnoses within our inpatient rehabilitation cohort—are relatively few and often suffer from methodological limitations, emphasising the importance of further research [10].

This study was conducted to examine prescribing patterns of analgesic medications within a rehabilitation hospital, with a specific focus on the identification of NP using 2 validated diagnostic tools: the LANSS and the DN4. The primary objective was to evaluate the extent to which current analgesic regimens align with the underlying pain mechanisms, particularly in differentiating nociceptive from NP. Findings from this analysis aim to inform evidence-based optimization of pain management strategies within the rehabilitation setting. Furthermore, the study provides epidemiological data on the incidence and prevalence of NP in this patient population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

All patients admitted for inpatient rehabilitation at Karin Grech Hospital during a 2-week period in August 2024 were considered for inclusion in this study.

Diagnoses included stroke (ischemic and haemorrhagic), SCI, fractures, and other conditions such as amputations, traumatic brain injury (TBI), Guillain-Barré syndrome, and multiple sclerosis.

Patient files were reviewed to assess drug history, confirm diagnoses, and exclude individuals with cognitive impairment. Cognitive impairment was defined as a Mini-Mental State Examination score of less than 24 or a Montreal Cognitive Assessment score of less than 26. Patients who were unable to provide a clinical history due to conditions such as aphasia, delirium, or other stroke- or psychiatry-related impairments in communication were excluded from the study.

Verbal consent was obtained from all eligible participants prior to starting questionnaires.

Patients who reported pain were subsequently interviewed regarding its characteristics using the LANSS and DN4 scores exploring average pain levels and any peaks experienced throughout the day by patients having varied analgesia schedules.

Analgesia medications considered in this study as per National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines on NP management were antidepressants with combined norepinephrine and serotonin reuptake inhibition (e.g., TCAs and SNRIs), (2) calcium channel α2-δ ligands (e.g., gabapentin and pregabalin). For musculoskeletal pain (MSK), World Health Organization analgesic ladder was adopted, including non-opioid analgesics (paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug), weak opioids (codeine, tramadol) and strong opioids (morphine, fentanyl).

Ethical approval was obtained from the Karin Grech Rehabilitation Hospital and the Data Protection Officer (roberta.messina@gov.mt).

To ensure consistency, a single researcher conducted all data collection. For statistical analysis, SPSS for Windows version 16.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) was used. Correlation analyses were performed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

RESULTS

Patient selection and demographics

During the study period, 100 inpatients were initially screened. Following application of the exclusion criteria, 70 patients met the inclusion criteria (32 males, 38 females), with ages ranging from 18 to 91 years (mean age: 67 years) (Fig. 1). The cohort included patients with ischemic stroke (n = 14), haemorrhagic stroke (n = 3), SCI (n = 7), fractures (n = 38), and other neurological or trauma-related conditions such as TBI, multiple sclerosis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, and limb amputations (n = 8).

NP scores by diagnosis

Comparison of LANSS scores across diagnostic categories revealed notable variability in NP severity (Fig. 2). Patients with haemorrhagic stroke exhibited the highest median LANSS score (15.4), with a narrow interquartile range (IQR), indicating consistently high levels of NP. SCI patients demonstrated similarly elevated LANSS scores (median: 12), albeit with a wider IQR, suggesting heterogeneous pain experiences. Ischemic stroke patients had a median LANSS score of 12.5, within the NP range, with an IQR of 7.5–15.

Lower LANSS scores were observed in patients with other neurological conditions (median: 7.5) and those with fractures (median: 6), suggestive of predominantly nociceptive (somatic) pain. The lowest median scores were recorded among amputees (2.4) and patients with TBI (0.5), with tightly clustered IQRs, reflecting minimal inter-patient variability.

DN4 scores followed a similar pattern (Fig. 3), with elevated median scores in haemorrhagic stroke (6.8), ischemic stroke (6.4), and SCI (5.8) cohorts. Haemorrhagic stroke patients had a narrow IQR (6.5–7.5), indicating uniformity in symptom severity, whereas SCI patients displayed greater variability (IQR: 6.0–8.0). In contrast, median DN4 scores were substantially lower in patients with fractures (4.0), other neurological conditions (3.5), amputations (1.3), and TBI (0.5), potentially reflecting a lower prevalence of neuropathic mechanisms in these groups.

Analgesia use and correlation with NP scores

Analysis of DN4 scores in relation to analgesic prescribing patterns revealed that patients receiving pregabalin monotherapy had a high median DN4 score (7.8; IQR: 6.0–9.0), consistent with significant NP (Fig. 4). The highest DN4 median score was observed in patients receiving selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) as adjunct therapy (median: 8.1; IQR: 6.0–8.0), suggesting suboptimal monotherapy efficacy and the need for combination treatment. Amitriptyline monotherapy was associated with a median DN4 score of 6.5 (IQR: 5.0–8.0), while combination therapy with pregabalin and amitriptyline resulted in a slightly lower median DN4 score of 5.9 (IQR: 4.5–6.5), indicating partial symptom control.

DN4, Douleur Neuropathique en 4 Questions; SSRI, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor.

Conversely, patients prescribed paracetamol/codeine had a lower median DN4 score (3.5; IQR: 3.0–4.0), reflective of predominantly somatic pain. Patients who were not prescribed analgesia exhibited a median DN4 score of 0 (IQR: 0–0.5), indicating minimal or absent NP.

Similar trends were observed in LANSS scores (Fig. 5). Pregabalin monotherapy was associated with a median LANSS score of 15, indicative of NP. Amitriptyline monotherapy yielded a slightly lower median score of 12.5, while patients receiving pregabalin in combination with an SSRI exhibited the highest median score (16.5). In contrast, patients with fractures had a median LANSS score of 7, and those not receiving analgesia had negligible scores, further validating the use of the LANSS tool in differentiating neuropathic from MSK.

A positive correlation was observed between LANSS and DN4 scores (Spearman’s correlation, p < 0.05), supporting the validity of both tools in assessing NP (Fig. 6).

Analgesic prescriptions by diagnosis

Pregabalin was the most prescribed agent for patients with stroke-related NP (29%). Among SCI patients, dual therapy with pregabalin and a TCA was most frequently used (29%). For fracture-related pain, paracetamol/codeine was the primary analgesic in 91% of cases, with pregabalin prescribed in a minority (6%) due to comorbid poorly controlled diabetes (Fig. 7).

These findings underscore the utility of LANSS and DN4 in accurately distinguishing between neuropathic and somatic pain mechanisms, enabling more precise and individualized analgesic strategies in the rehabilitation setting.

DISCUSSION

This study highlights the heterogeneity of pain types and analgesic prescribing patterns in an inpatient rehabilitation setting. Patients with stroke and SCI exhibited the highest DN4 and LANSS scores, indicating a greater burden of NP. This corresponded with higher rates of neuropathic analgesic use, including pregabalin, TCAs, and SSRIs, in these groups.

Both LANSS and DN4 proved effective in distinguishing neuropathic from nociceptive pain. Patients with low scores often had no prescribed analgesia, supporting the clinical utility of these tools. While LANSS and DN4 have previously been validated in diabetic neuropathy [9] this study confirms their applicability in other neurological populations, particularly stroke and SCI. DN4 was more practical for routine use, consistent with findings by Wang et al. [6] due to its brevity and ease of administration.

Yadav and Joshi [11] reported NP in 79.3% of SCI patients with complete lesions (American Spinal Injury Association A) and 73.2% in those with incomplete lesions. Similar prevalence rates, albeit lower, were found in studies by Afsar et al. [12], Werhagen et al. [13], and Margot-Duclot et al. [14] with reported rates of 53%, 40%, and 40%, respectively. Differences in classification (e.g., not distinguishing between at- or below-level pain) may account for variability.

NP following stroke is less extensively studied. A meta-analysis by Liampas et al. [15] found prevalence exceeding 10%, predominantly within the first month post-stroke. At Karin Grech Rehabilitation Hospital, 9 of 13 stroke patients exhibited NP within 8 months post-event, and one patient had symptoms persisting beyond 12 months. Klit et al. [16] noted that most studies in this domain emphasize CPSP, which includes hemiplegic shoulder pain, spasticity-related pain, and neuralgias. In their population-based study, pinprick hyperalgesia was present in 57%, cold allodynia in 40%, and brush-evoked dysesthesia in 51% of patients with CPSP [17].

Analgesic prescribing patterns reflected pain classification. Among stroke patients, 29% received pregabalin, 6% amitriptyline, 6% both, and 12% duloxetine. Additionally, 35% received paracetamol, codeine, or tramadol, while 12% required no analgesia—correlating with LANSS and DN4 scores of zero. Among SCI patients, 29% received dual therapy (pregabalin and amitriptyline), 14% were on monotherapy, and 43% received simple analgesics. All SCI patients had some form of regular analgesia. Similarly, all patients with fractures were prescribed analgesics—primarily paracetamol or codeine (91%).

These findings support prior work by Casale et al. [18], who found that 91% of patients reported pain interference during rehabilitation. Orthopaedic, neurological, and rheumatological conditions were most associated with chronic pain. Among specialists surveyed, 44% reported that inflammatory/nociceptive pain accounted for > 50% of cases; 20% reported NP as the main contributor, and 21% cited mixed pain.

Prescribing approaches differed between geriatric and physical rehabilitation wards. Geriatric wards less frequently used pregabalin, amitriptyline, or tramadol and initiated lower doses (e.g., 25 mg pregabalin). Duloxetine was preferred in older adults due to a better side effect profile. Pickering et al. [19] proposed an algorithm for elderly patients favouring gabapentin/pregabalin or SNRIs (e.g., venlafaxine, duloxetine) as first-line agents, while avoiding TCAs. A “start low, go slow” monotherapy approach was recommended due to altered pharmacokinetics in aging.

A minority of patients with low LANSS/DN4 scores still received neuropathic agents, possibly reflecting effective symptom control or limitations in tool accuracy. Both LANSS and DN4 rely on patient-reported symptoms, which may be compromised in cases of cognitive impairment or communication barriers. DN4’s shorter format may be more practical in such settings.

Ultimately, both LANSS and DN4 were effective in identifying NP in this prospective study. Our analytical approach remained primarily descriptive because the study was designed as an exploratory pilot investigation aimed at characterizing pain profiles and prescribing patterns rather than testing specific causal hypotheses. In addition, several key covariates that would be required for robust multivariable modelling—such as functional status, socioeconomic factors, detailed comorbidity burden, and temporal factors related to pain onset and treatment—were either not captured systematically or contained substantial missing data. Incorporating these incomplete variables into regression analyses risked producing unstable or biased estimates, and the limited sample size further reduced the feasibility of reliable multivariate modelling.

We acknowledge, however, that the absence of multivariable adjustment limits the ability to account for residual confounding. As a result, the statistically significant findings observed—particularly regarding DN4 performance—may have been influenced by unmeasured or uncontrolled factors, including physician-specific prescribing practices, variability in clinical decision-making, heterogeneity in patient characteristics (e.g., age, comorbidities), and small subgroup sizes such as the amputee cohort. These potential confounders may partly explain subgroup differences and should be considered when interpreting the results. As noted by Finnerup et al. [20], future work should prioritize more comprehensive data collection and adequately powered studies that allow for multivariate regression analyses to determine the independent contribution of patient-, clinician-, and context-related factors. Our pilot findings may therefore serve as a foundation for future, more definitive research. Furthermore, current guidelines—such as those by NICE—are largely based on diabetic neuropathy [21], which may not reflect the complexities of post-stroke or pain. Addressing this gap will allow for more evidence-based updates to NP management guidelines.

The LANSS and the DN4 questionnaires were effective in detecting NP, with DN4 demonstrating greater clinical utility due to its brevity and ease of administration.

Analgesic prescriptions showed strong concordance with LANSS and DN4 scores. Patients with stroke and SCI exhibited higher rates of NP and were more frequently prescribed pregabalin, TCAs, and SSRIs.

Despite the clinical relevance, NP in stroke and SCI remains under-investigated compared to diabetic neuropathy. Further research is warranted to guide evidence-based management and optimisation of guidelines in these populations.
