Differential Response to Interleukin‐1 Blockade With Anakinra on Cardiorespiratory Fitness in Patients With Heart Failure With Preserved Ejection Fraction Stratified According to Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction
Authors: Michele Golino, Francesco Moroni, Salvatore Carbone, Giuliana Corna, Cory Trankle, Hayley E Billingsley, Marco G Del Buono, Azita H Talasaz, Georgia K Thomas, Roberto De Ponti, Jeremy Turlington, Roshanak Markley, Ross Arena, Justin M Canada, Benjamin Van Tassell, Antonio Abbate
Published: 2023

Patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) are a heterogeneous clinical population. Among patients with HFpEF, those with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥60% may have a different response to treatment than those with LVEF 50% to 59%, with a less clear benefit of inhibitors of the renin‐angiotensin system, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors, 1 and possibly also sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors. 2 , 3 Interleukin 1 (IL‐1) is a proinflammatory cytokine being explored as a therapeutic target to improve cardiorespiratory fitness in heart failure. However, IL‐1 blockade has shown to have less consistent effects in patients with HFpEF compared with patients with LVEF <50%. 4 , 5 The aim of the present analysis is to determine changes in cardiorespiratory fitness and biomarkers in patients with HFpEF treated with anakinra, an IL‐1 blocker, stratified according to resting LVEF.

We analyzed data from patients with HFpEF (defined as a presence of heart failure symptoms, LVEF ≥50%, and evidence of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction or elevated filling pressures, as previously published) and elevated CRP (C‐reactive protein; ≥2 mg/L). 4 , 5 The analysis included the pooled active anakinra treatment arms of 2 previously published randomized‐controlled trials of anakinra in HFpEF. 4 , 5 All subjects provided informed consent under protocols approved by the Virginia Commonwealth University Institutional Review Board. Patients with significant valvular diseases, acute or chronic infections, as well as rheumatologic or collagen diseases were excluded. 4 , 5 All patients were treated with anakinra, a recombinant IL‐1 receptor antagonist, 100 mg subcutaneously daily, and were stratified into 2 groups: LVEF 50% to 59% and LVEF ≥60%. All patients underwent: (1) a cardiopulmonary exercise test to measure peak oxygen consumption (VO2), peak respiratory exchange ratio, the oxygen uptake efficiency slope, and the minute ventilation/carbon dioxide production slope; and (2) biomarker assessment (CRP and NT‐proBNP [N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide]) at baseline and during treatment with anakinra.

Individual patient data were pooled in a single prespecified data set and analyzed. Continuous and categorical variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and number (percentage) and were compared using Mann‐Whitney and Fisher exact tests, respectively. The within‐group paired difference was compared using Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. We calculated the delta (Δ) values as the absolute difference between treatment and baseline measurements, and we compared them between the 2 different groups using the Mann‐Whitney test. A 2‐sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request and dependent on review of the institutional review board.

We included 32 patients with HFpEF (median age, 55 [interquartile range, 51–64] years); 24 (75%) self‐identified as women, and 19 (59%) self‐identified as Black race. Of 32 patients, 15 (47%) had LVEF 50% to 59%, and 17 (53%) had LVEF ≥60%. The duration of anakinra treatment was 12 (2–12) weeks in both groups (P=0.83). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in the Table. In patients with LVEF 50% to 59% and ≥60%, baseline peak VO2 was 14.1 (13.1–17.3) and 13.8 (11.8–18.6) mL × kg−1 × min−1, respectively (P=0.82). A statistically significant increase in peak VO2 (from 13.8 [11.8–18.6] to 15.8 [12.7–19.9] mL × kg−1 × min−1; P=0.033) was seen in patients with LVEF ≥60%, whereas in those with LVEF 50% to 59%, there were no significant changes (peak VO2, from 14.1 [13.1–17.3] to 14.1 [11.0–17.3] mL × kg−1 × min−1; P=0.25). A statistically significant greater increase in the interval changes in peak VO2 was seen in patients with LVEF ≥60% versus those with LVEF 50% to 59% (1.7 [from −0.7 to 2.3] versus 0 [from −1.1 to 0.3] mL × kg−1 per min−1, respectively; P=0.011) (Table). No significant differences in peak respiratory exchange ratio, oxygen uptake efficiency slope, and minute ventilation/carbon dioxide production slope between groups were noted.

Baseline CRP values were 6.3 (3.8–16.9) and 6.2 (3.4–15.9) mg/L in patients with LVEF 50% to 59% and ≥60%, respectively (P=0.74). Treatment with anakinra resulted in a significant reduction in CRP in both groups (from 6.3 [3.8–16.9] mg/L to 1.4 [0.9–10.0] mg/L, P=0.006; and from 6.2 [3.4–15.9] mg/L to 3.4 [1.0–6.7] mg/L, P=0.003, in patients with LVEF 50% to 59% and ≥60%, respectively). Interval changes in CRP were −3.5 (from −6.0 to −2.1) and −3 (from −11.4 to −0.6) mg/L in patients with LVEF 50% to 59% and ≥60%, respectively (P=0.91). No significant between‐ or within‐group differences in NT‐proBNP levels were noted. Interval changes in NT‐proBNP were −57 (from −201 to −1) and −10 (from −63 to 20) pg/mL in patients with LVEF 50% to 59% and ≥60%, respectively (P=0.41; Table).

In a subset of patients (9 with LVEF 50%–59% and 7 with LVEF ≥60%), we also analyzed the body composition through the bioelectrical impedance analysis at baseline and during treatment with anakinra, to measure peak VO2 adjusted for fat‐free mass (FFM), a more accurate measure of cardiorespiratory fitness in patients with obesity. We found no significant between‐ or within‐group differences for the bioelectrical impedance analysis parameters (data not shown). The interval changes in peak VO2 relative to FFM after treatment with anakinra were significantly greater in patients with LVEF ≥60% (2.2 [from 0.4 to 3.3] mL × kgFFM −1 × min−1) than in those with LVEF 50% to 59% (−0.7 [from −5.5 to 0.8] mL × kgFFM −1 × min−1; P=0.05) (Table).

In summary, patients with HFpEF show a differential response to IL‐1 blockade with anakinra based on resting LVEF, with those with LVEF ≥60% having a greater improvement in cardiorespiratory fitness, measured as peak VO2, despite a similar reduction in CRP. The differences in peak VO2 could not be fully explained by differences in body composition at baseline or changes over time. Whether the differences in LVEF reflect a different pathophysiological mechanism or other factors (ie, a difference in response to heart failure therapies) cannot be determined. Further studies are needed to validate these data and explore IL‐1 blockade in this subset of patients.
