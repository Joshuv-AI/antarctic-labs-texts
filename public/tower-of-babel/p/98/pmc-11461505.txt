A perspective view of salt crystallization from solution in porous media: morphology, mechanism, and salt efflorescence
Authors: Qiang Li, Zhongwei Wang, Han Guo, Jing Zhao, Hongjie Luo, Xiao Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalt efflorescence is one of the major hazards to cultural heritages, masonries, and highways etc. It is now generally accepted that damages caused by salt efflorescence are mainly due to continuous cycles of salt crystallization/dissolution or hydration/dehydration in confined spaces. The position where salt efflorescence occurs and its type are closely related to the degree of damages caused by salt efflorescence. It is known that water is the key environmental factor determining the salt crystallization position. But influence of the correlation between water supply and evaporation on the position of salt crystallization is still not clearly understood. In this work, a set of experiments are designed to investigate salt efflorescence in porous matrix. It is found that the types and positions of salt efflorescence have little to do with nucleation, but are mainly governed by crystal growth, which is controlled by the rates of water evaporation, water and salt supply, capillary forces and surface properties of the porous matrices.Keywords: Salt weathering, Porous media, Synchrotron & micro-CT, WhiskerSubject terms: Environmental impact, Characterization and analytical techniques

Introduction

Salt crystallization is a universally common and important physical phenomenon which has attracted scientist’s lots of attention for the past centuries. Salt crystallization from solution has been widely observed in many biologically and physiologically relevant systems, which can be either structurally very simple like in a salt solution or extremely complex like in an organism. They include producing crystals for industrial applications, polymorphism of APIs for pharmaceutical applications, formation of kidney stones or uric acid crystals in human bodies and more1–3.

Nucleation and crystal growth are two most important aspects of crystallization process, which basically determine the structural and functional attributes of the crystals from the atomic to the macroscopic scale4. A great deal of effort has been expended to investigate how molecules in solution form nuclei, then grow. Many models and simulations have been proposed5–8. By taking advantage of powerful and cutting-edge instruments, nuclei formed with several molecules can be detected nowadays9–11.

Salt crystallization from solution is the key factor to salt weathering, which often leads to various forms of deterioration, such as loss of cohesion, chalking, spalling, cracking in stone or brick masonries, soil, highways, natural and cultural heritages etc. Salt weathering can be classified as efflorescence and subflorescence based on salt crystallizing on or beneath the solid surface, in which subflorescence is often considered to be more harmful to heritages12. Salt weathering will not only weaken the mechanical strength of these objects to cause potential safety issues and structural damages, but also destroy the surface characteristics of buildings or heritages which carry important and valuable historical, cultural, and artistic information. Salt crystallization on the solid surface is also the big challenge for seawater desalination by solar evaporation technique, in which salt crystallization on/in the porous evaporator can block water evaporation and even break the evaporator13,14.

Many research works have been carried out to understand the mechanism and the kinetic process of salt efflorescence, in order to find solutions to reduce and control the salt damage15–19. It is now generally accepted that salt weathering mainly comes from continuous cycles of salt crystallization/dissolution or hydration/dehydration of salts in confined pore space. Such processes accompanying with volume variations, crystallization pressure, capillary forces etc. will generate mechanical stress upon the material and result in damages20–24.

Crystallization is a complex process. Crystallization in pores makes it even more complex. More recently, many advanced and sensitive analytical tools have been applied in combination to study in-pore crystallization experimentally. Many new observations have been found and significant progress has been made in theoretical models. Now, we have much better understanding about nucleation and crystal growth of in-pore crystallization than before. However, current models or simulation approaches still fail to predict where salt efflorescence will occur with respect to the spatiotemporal domain of an object, such as a masonry, a monument, or an earthen site. Meanwhile, different forms of salt efflorescence have been long observed. But the mechanisms that control the development of different salt efflorescence by the crystal growth remain poorly understood.

Here we design and carry out a set of experiments to investigate the salt migration in porous matrix, the space domain of salt nucleation and crystal growth, the formation of different salt efflorescence including salt crust and fibrils. The work focuses on the whole physicochemical process of salt crystallization in porous inorganic matrix and different efflorescence formation on the surface of the matrix.

Experimental

Materials and sample preparation

NaCl (AR grade) and Na2SO4 (AR grade) were purchased from Acros Organics, used as received. Deionized H2O was obtained from an in-house Millipore Milli-Q purification system. NaCl aqueous solution (252.25 g/L, 4.31 M) and Na2SO4 aqueous solution (195.0 g/L, 1.38 M), both 70 percent of their saturation at 20 °C, were prepared for later uses.

The simulated brick sample used in experiment was acquired from the Ancient Building Protection Research Institute of Datong in Shanxi, which were fired in a traditional brick kiln between the temperature of 750–850 °C. The porosity of the brick was about 35.75% with an average pore diameter around 290 nm. Samples of 20 mm in diameter and 20 mm in height were used in the whisker experiment. Black quartz sand was obtained from Zhengzhou Minghai Quartz Sand Factory. The brick and sand samples were ultrasonically cleaned with deionized water until the conductivity of washing water was below 0.055 μS/cm. The washed samples were dried in a vacuum oven at 110 °C for 6 h.

Instrumentation and methods

Synchrotron CT measurements and crystallization front experiments

Synchrotron CT measurements was performed at the X-ray imaging and biomedical applications beamline (BL13W1) at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. The photon energy is 25 keV, and X-ray imaging detector with a pixel size of 1.625 μm. Brick samples of 3.2 mm in diameter and 30 mm in height were used in synchrotron CT experiments. Firstly, the CT structure images of the samples without any treatment were obtained, and then the samples were wetted by dipping with NaCl solution (252.25 g/L, ~ 70% of saturation) and Na2SO4 solution (195.0 g/L, ~ 70% of saturation) respectively. Then the crystallization and dissolution cycle experiments were carried out according to the procedures in reference20 (slight adjustments are applied). The CT experiments were conducted after the completion of each crystallization/dissolution cycle. Conditions for subflorescence: 35 °C, 30% RH, 6 h; 15 °C, 90% RH, 6 h. Conditions for efflorescence: 35 °C, 90% RH, 6 h; 15 °C, 90% RH, 6 h; after 37 cycles, the samples sat at 35 °C in a closed vessel with a pinhole for 4 months until crystallization was observed.

Crystallization front experiments were carried out as described in Fig. 1. The position of salt solution supply in packed quartz sample is controlled through a connecter by the water height in the reservoir (copper sulfate is used due to its observable blue color). The sand is 1–2 mm in diameter and packed in a transparent acrylic box of 40 × 14 × 1 cm3. The environmental conditions are controlled at 35 °C and 30% RH. The experiments are continued until salt fronts can be obviously observed.

Results and discussion

Spatiotemporal domain of soluble salt nucleation and crystal growth

In a porous matrix, salt can crystallize in two possible domains: beneath the surface (referred as subflorescence) and on the surface (referred as efflorescence)24. In subflorescence, when the salt crystallizes really close to, but still beneath the surface, such subflorescence is referred as crypto-efflorescence and it is reasonable to believe that crypto-efflorescence is more harmful to porous cultural heritages21. It is difficult to observe subflorescence (or crypto-efflorescence) directly, since it is beneath the surface. Traditional optical or electron microscopy is not competent for this purpose. In Fig. 3, salt weathering on brick samples induced by repeated crystallization/dissolution of NaCl aqueous solutions is studied by micro-CT, whose experimental details is described in Section “Synchrotron CT measurements and crystallization front experiments”. Figure 3a is a diagram of brick sample used. The small sample size is limited by the instrument. After three crystallization/dissolution cycles of NaCl, see the caption in Fig. 3 for cyclization conditions, white NaCl crystals can be seen on the brick sample surface by naked eyes. But more interesting information is revealed by micro-CT as shown in Fig. 3d-e, which are also horizonal and vertical cross-section micro-CT images of the brick sample. An obvious ring of NaCl deposit, about 150 μm beneath the surface, is clearly observed. Layered spalling of the brick samples after eight crystallization/dissolution cycles of NaCl and Na2SO4 are shown in Fig. 3f-i respectively. It is obvious that salt crystallization/dissolution can do great damage to porous inorganic objects and damage caused by Na2SO4 can be more serious, at least three layers spalling can be observed in Fig. 3h-i.

Carefully examining the salt crystals inside the sample network, it can be judged that salt crystallizes preferentially on the edge of smaller pores. Kelvin equation25 is demonstrated in Eq. 1 and Young–Laplace equation is demonstrated in Eq. 2. In these equations, pL0 is the vapor pressure of the bulk liquid phase at zero curvature, ML is the molar mass of the liquid, σLA is the surface energy of the liquid in air, ρL is the density of liquid, R is the gas constant, T is the temperature, r is the curvature (in our cases, r is negative) and Δp is the gauge pressure (difference of liquid pressure at certain meniscus and ambient pressure).

It is well understood that the water equilibrium in water wettable porous media is affected by water content26. In our cases, water content decreases due to drying. At lower water content, even when liquid regions are disconnected, the vapor pressure and liquid pressure will be equalized throughout the sample. Kelvin equation tells that water in smaller pores (higher r) have lower vapor pressure while Young–Laplace equation tells that water in smaller pores has lower liquid pressure. In order to achieve that balance, water has to occupy high curvature area like smaller pores and throats25.

Meanwhile, smaller pores may have lower Gibbs free energy barrier of crystallization process which facilitates crystallization27. Both factors can contribute to the preferential crystallization of salts within smaller pores.

Water evaporation and capillary forces are the major driving forces that water and salt molecules transport toward the sample surface (salts transport with water)28. Two kinetic processes related to water involve here, water evaporation and transportation in the matrix (water adsorption and water condensation can also have strong effects, but they are neglected currently for simplicity). The rate of water evaporation is denoted as μe and that of water transportation in the matrix is denoted as μt. There are two scenarios. One is μt < μe, the other one is μt ≥ μe. In the first scenario as in the experiments in Fig. 3, since surface water supply is less than its water loss, the sample surface gradually loses water and eventually becomes dry. The vapor/liquid interface moves towards into the matrix. As known, μe will decrease as it goes deep into the porous matrix due to the increased vapor diffusive transport resistance29. At certain depth, a new and stable vapor/liquid interface will form where μt = μe. More salt molecules will migrate with water to this new vapor/liquid interface and accumulate there. As water evaporates, salt concentration increases until it crystallizes upon saturation30,31. Thus, the salt line observed in Fig. 3 is formed at the position where the water migration from inner and the water evaporation reach equilibrium. In the case of Fig. 3, that depth is around 150 μm. A hypothesis descried the whole process is illustrated in Fig. 4.

An illustration of vapor/liquid interface (dry front) formation in porous matrix due to equilibrium between water molecule evaporation and migration. The lattice constructed by circles represents porous matrix; blue color represents liquid water (the bluer, the higher water content); red represents salt crystals; arrows indicate water evaporation.

The equilibrium position of vapor/liquid interface is mainly controlled by two factors: one is the environment conditions, such as temperature, humidity, ventilation etc., and the other one is water supply in the matrix. By controlling these two factors, the position of vapor/liquid interface, i.e., the spatiotemporal domain of salt crystallization, can be manipulated. A set of experiments are carried out as described in Fig. 1. In Fig. 1, by controlling the height of salt solution in the reservoir, salt lines at different height in the matrix can be achieved as shown in Fig. 5a and b. And further, by changing the environment conditions to more severe drying states multiple salt lines can be formed in the matrix as shown in Fig. 5c. Apparently, as the environment conditions change to a more severe drying state, a new vapor/liquid interface forms deeper into the matrix where new salt line forms. The number of formed salt lines is indicative of times of environment changing. Similar results with sodium chloride are shown in the supporting information.

(a) one salt line formed; (b) one line formed in deeper place; (c) multiple salt lines formed. Environmental condition: 35 °C, 30% RH.

This set of experiments further strength our proposed model in Fig. 4 that salt molecules accumulate and crystallize at domains at which liquid water and water vapor reach equilibrium. Similar phenomena are not rare. Lopez et al. studied the soil moisture and salt content at Neleger station in eastern Siberia during summer, and they also found that salt concentrations formed peaks with respect to the depth by following the soil electrical conductivities32. Although they attributed that to leaching caused by rainwater, our results suggest that there may exist other possibilities.

In the latter scenario (μt ≥ μe), the object surface also being the water–vapor interface, will remain wet. It appears the solution (as indicated by blue color) fills the pores and smaller pores are favored due to larger capillary forces. It is difficult for salt to crystallize under this high humidity as shown in Fig. 5b. No salt crystallization is observed on the sample surface or inside the sample after 37 high/low temperature circles (35 °C, 90% RH, 6 h; 15 °C, 90% RH, 6 h), shown in Fig. 6b. Salt eventually crystallizes after 4 months at 35 °C in a closed vessel for water to evaporate slowly. But in this case, salt only crystallizes on the surface as shown in Fig. 6c and Fig. 6e. No salt crystallization is observed inside the brick (Fig. 6d) as all salt comes to the surface.

Morphological studies on salt efflorescence on the surface

As discussed previously, when μt ≥ μe (high humidity and/or quick water supply), salt within a porous object can transport to its surface, accumulate, and eventually crystallize on the surface. Salt crust is commonly observed33,34. It is known that nanometer thickness of water film can form on a smooth solid surface35. The formation and thickness of the water film is largely depended on the surface hydrophilicity36. Water film will lead to the formation of salt crust with water evaporation as shown in Fig. 7a-d and the experimental set-up is described in Fig. 2. SEM pictures in Fig. 7b and Fig. 7d show that the crusts are consist of closed packed micro-crystals of NaCl or Na2SO4. In Fig. 7a, salt crust displacement is also observed, which might be related to salt dissolution/precipitation and/or to mechanical effects caused by so-called crystallization pressure37.

(a) An OM and(b) a SEM picture of sodium sulfate crust formed on brick samples in our laboratory (the sulfate crust is thick and formed an arch, leaving voids beneath it)37,46,47; (c) an OM and (d) a SEM picture of sodium chloride crust formed on brick samples in our laboratory. (e) a picture of “tree-like” sodium chloride efflorescence formed on earthen wall in an excavated tomb in Qinghai, China; (f) an OM picture of “tree-like” sodium sulfate efflorescence formed on a brick sample in our laboratory; (g) a CT picture of “tree-like” sodium sulfate efflorescence formed on a brick sample in our laboratory; (h) enlargement of rectangular area in (g).

In many cases, the salt does not distribute homogeneously on the surface as the NaCl crust shown in Fig. 6c. There are often much more salt in certain area than other area on the surface. This patchy morphology is possibly due to the wettability discontinuity, which is often observed on porous surface with structural heterogeneities12,38. Patchy efflorescence, as shown in Fig. 7c, occurs as a set of isolated wet patches nucleate due to saturation of the salt solution and grow due to continuous water evaporation24. It is also reported that such patchy morphology may also come from spatial variations in the evaporation flux39. But based on the scale of our experiments, variations in the evaporation flux can be neglected.

Beside crusty or patchy efflorescence, fibrous efflorescence with “tree-like” morphology is also ubiquitous and phenomenal in Nature. Figure 7e is a picture taken in an excavated tomb in Qinghai, China. The whole earthen wall is covered by “tree-like” NaCl crystals. However, very little knowledge about the formation mechanism of fibrous efflorescence is known (How does it form? Under what condition will it form?).

In our laboratory, by large quantity of parallel experiments, we find out that slower salt solution feed and evaporation (smaller μt and μe, μt ≥ μe is still satisfied) favor the “tree-like” morphology. In the experiment of Fig. 2a, when the temperature and relative humidity are kept at 25 ºC and 50%RH, crusty efflorescence is formed in open environment with good ventilation and direct salt solution supply. Meanwhile, “tree-like” efflorescence is formed in close vessels (25 ºC, 90%RH) as shown Fig. 7f with limited ventilation and indirect salt solution supply in experiment of Fig. 2b. If we examined more carefully in Fig. 7a, “tree-like” crystals can also be found on the inner surface of the arched crust because of the limited slower evaporation in the void covered by the crust. Figure 7g is a CT image of fibrillar crystals grow on a brick sample under limited ventilation. CT image shows that not only the crystal fibrils grow all over the outer surface but also on the inner surface of cracks which are connected to the open air. Interestingly and reasonably, the fibrils inside the cracks grow toward the opening (ventilation direction) as shown in Fig. 7h.

SEMs give a much clearer view of these fibrils than CT. As shown in Fig. 8a-b, the salt fibrils are around tens of microns in width and hundreds of microns in length, consist of sub-five-micron primary particles. More importantly and out of our original expectations, SEM pictures show that there is no branching or fractal feature in the fibrils. They are more like whiskers instead of “tree-like” as previously reported35. Figure 8c further shows the fibrils are hollow, a phenomenon observed and reported for the first time. The cross-sections of the fibril’s tip, middle and lower part are examined by SEM as shown in Fig. 8d-f. Hollow structure can be clearly observed. The fibrils are formed by packing of micron-sized ellipsoidal crystals with an obvious void in the middle. One thing needs to point out is that the cross-section SEMs in Fig. 8d-f are from different fibrils (it is impossible to get cross sections at different positions of one fibril).

Overall, we propose a hypothesis which is illustrated in Fig. 9a. At higher μe in open air (to maintain μt ≥ μe, μt is even bigger), enough water transports to the surface to form relative dense and continuous water film, in which salt molecules transport and crystallize freely as in solution. As they crystallize, salt crust is formed. At smaller μt and μe (μt ≥ μe), no water film is formed on the surface. The salt crystallization on the surface is confined by the mass transport below, similar to the so-called “diffusion limited aggregation” cases, in which fractals generally are obtained40. In our cases, water molecules migrate toward surface by capillary forces and surface tension (surface tension of salt solution increases with its concentration41. Both driving forces are perpendicular to the surface. Thus, the tips of the aggregates are the preferential sites (or “trapping sites”) for next particles (or “walkers”) to grow upon42. Under this condition, as predicted and explained theoretically by Giuseppe Rossi43, Pierre Devillard and H. Eugene Stanleyas well others, whiskers are formed44.

(a) Proposed mechanism for the formation of hollow fibril structure; (b) Overall description of salt efflorescence in porous matrix.

During the whisker growth, salt-containing water will enter the space inside packed crystals by capillary forces and some inner crystals will re-dissolve into water to generate voids. Now the water can be evaporated from the top and the sides. The former situation causes dissolved salt to crystallize on the top of packed crystals and it is obvious that the capillary forces determine the theoretical height of the fibrils45. In the latter case, the dissolved salt will crystallize on the side surface, resulting in denser wall, as observed in Fig. 7c. The processes will eventually lead to the formation of hollow fibrillar structure. The environmental conditions for formation of various salt efflorescence are further summarized in Fig. 9b.

Sometimes, fibrils can also be formed at relative faster evaporation (relatively larger μe) conditions. In these situations, the salt first forms a layer of salt crust, a layer often referred as “mushy layer”40–45. It may reduce the μt and μe by blocking the pores, thus the fibrils formation condition is satisfied. The fibrils growing on a 100 μm thick “mushy layer” is clearly shown in Fig. 10.

Conclusions

Soluble salts can dissolve/crystallize repeatedly in porous media due to capillary forces and water evaporation, leading to efflorescence. Various forms of efflorescence have little to do with nucleation, but are mainly governed by crystal growth, which is controlled by the evaporation rate (μe), water supply (μt) and salt supply, capillary forces and the surface properties of porous media etc. As summarized in Fig. 9b, when μt < μe, the matrix surface is dry and salt crystallizes within the matrix. Depending on the gap between μt and μe, crypto-efflorescence (small gap) or subflorescence (large gap) will form respectively. When μt ≥ μe, the matrix surface is wet and salt crystallizes on the surface. Depending on the value of μt, salt crust (large μt) or hollow fibrils (small μt) will form respectively.(Supplementary Information).
