Association Between Plasma Phosphorylated Tau‐217 and Cognition in Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Trycia Kouchache, Tevy Chan, Sophie Sun, Lang Liu, Aline Delva, Ziv Gan‐Or, Pedro Rosa‐Neto, Jean‐François Gagnon, Alain Dagher, Ronald B. Postuma, Madeleine Sharp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlzheimer's disease (AD) co‐pathology contributes to dementia in PD, but its role in earlier cognitive impairment remains uncertain.ObjectiveTo determine if p‐tau217, a biomarker of early AD, is associated with cognitive impairment in PD.MethodsPlasma p‐tau217 levels in 167 PD patients without dementia and 63 controls were related to performance on standard neuropsychological testing, and to cognitive impairment as defined by a MoCA score <26 and by self‐report. Plasma GFAP, NfL and APOE ε4 carrier status were also examined.ResultsNo significant differences in p‐tau217, GFAP and NfL level were observed between groups (pFDR > 0.08). Higher p‐tau217 was associated with worse visuospatial function and greater self‐reported cognitive impairment, but these associations did not survive correction (pFDR > 0.08). There was no association with cognitive impairment (pFDR > 0.08).ConclusionThese results suggest that co‐morbid AD pathology is not a major contributor to early cognitive changes in this sample of PD patients without dementia. © 2026 The Author(s). Movement Disorders published by Wiley Periodicals LLC on behalf of International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society.

Cognitive impairment affects ~10% of early Parkinson's disease (PD) patients and up to 75% in later stages, but the timing and severity of cognitive impairment vary considerably. 1 , 2 , 3 Coexisting Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology may contribute to this heterogeneity. 1 , 4 Autopsy studies demonstrate significant amyloid‐β (Aβ) plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles in up to 40% of PD dementia (PDD) cases. 1 , 5 Aβ and tau positron emission tomography (PET) further suggest that AD pathology correlates with cognition. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 However, most postmortem and PET studies focused on PDD, the later stage of PD‐associated cognitive impairment, leaving open questions about the degree to which AD pathology contributes to earlier/milder cognitive impairment in PD.

Plasma phosphorylated tau at threonine 217 (p‐tau217) is a sensitive marker of AD co‐pathology, which may outperform phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (p‐tau181). 10 , 11 It may reflect early tau phosphorylation events closely linked to early Aβ accumulation, potentially detecting early AD pathology before widespread tau accumulation has occurred. 12

The role of p‐tau217 in PD remains largely unexplored. To date, most studies have measured Aβ42 and/or p‐tau181, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 with Aβ42 generally lower in cognitively impaired PD patients. 17 , 18 Particularly, a recent study found cognitively healthy PD patients had lower p‐tau181 levels than age‐matched controls, which, together with inconsistent associations between p‐tau181 and cognition in PD, suggests a limited or delayed role of AD pathology in PD cognition. 16 , 19 , 20 This contrasts with PDD, where the role of AD pathology/p‐tau181 is more clearly established. 4

This study sought to determine whether plasma p‐tau217 is associated with cognitive impairment and domain‐specific cognitive performance in PD patients without dementia.

Patients and Methods

Participants

We used data from the Quebec Parkinson Network, a registry of individuals with PD and healthy controls, as previously described. 21 , 22 Full details are provided in the Supplement. To focus on earlier cognitive impairment, we excluded participants classified as having dementia, based on Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) <18 and self‐reported interference with normal activities (MDS‐UPDRS [Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale] I.I ≥3). 23 , 24 , 25

Plasma Biomarkers and Genotyping

Plasma sample processing is detailed in the Supplement. p‐tau217 was available for 223 participants, whereas GFAP and NfL were measured in a subset of 126 participants. Plasma p‐tau217 positivity was defined using a 0.156‐pg/mL cutoff derived from receiver operating characteristic analysis maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity = 0.89, specificity = 0.96) in 100 individuals across cognitively unimpaired, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and dementia stages (Chan et al. under review). 26

APOE ε4 carrier status was available for 84 PD participants. 27 Participants with one ε4 allele were classified as carriers; no participant carried two ε4 alleles.

Measurements of Cognition

Participants completed neuropsychological testing as previously described (22, Supplement). In brief, testing covered domains of attention/working memory, executive function, memory, language, and visuospatial function. We used a MoCA cutoff score of <26 to identify participants with cognitive impairment. 24 , 28 MDS‐UPDRS Item I.1 evaluates subjective cognitive impairment reported by the patient. 29 We classified patients with a score ≥2 reporting at least “clinically evident cognitive dysfunction” but only minimal interference with the ability to carry out normal activities and social interactions as having significant self‐reported cognitive impairment.

Statistical Analyses

Between‐group comparisons of demographics and cognitive performance were performed using t tests, Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, and χ2 test as appropriate. Linear regressions related plasma biomarker levels to neuropsychological performance, MDS‐UPDRS (I, II, and III), and cognitive impairment status, and compared biomarker levels across APOE ε4 carrier status. Biomarker concentrations were z scored for regressions to allow for comparison of effect sizes. All models controlled for age, sex, education, and disease duration. All P‐values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Statistical analyses were conducted using R, version 4.3.1 (June 16, 2023).

Group Differences in Plasma Biomarker Levels

PD patients and controls did not differ significantly after correction for multiple comparisons with respect to adjusted p‐tau217, GFAP, and NfL, although mean GFAP and NFL levels were numerically higher in PD (Table 1; Fig. S1).

Plasma Biomarkers and Cognition

In the PD group, higher plasma p‐tau217 was associated with poorer visuospatial function, and higher GFAP was associated with poorer memory; however, neither association remained significant after FDR correction. No other cognitive domain was associated with plasma p‐tau217, GFAP, or NfL levels (Table 2). Unadjusted correlations are shown in Figure S2.

We found similar patterns when examining cognitive performance in individuals above versus below the p‐tau217 cutoff (P FDR = 0.35; Table S1). Similarly, no significant differences in plasma biomarker levels were observed between PD patients with or without abnormal MoCA (P FDR > 0.31; Table S2). In patients with self‐reported cognitive impairment, p‐tau217 levels were higher (P = 0.03), but this group difference did not survive FDR correction (P FDR = 0.08; Table S2).

APOE ε4 Status

APOE ε4 carriers had significantly higher p‐tau217 levels than noncarriers (P FDR = 0.014; Fig. S4). However, APOE4 status was not significantly associated with any measure of cognitive performance, and there were no interactions between plasma p‐tau217 and APOE4 status on cognitive performance (P FDR > 0.86; Table S4).

Plasma Biomarkers and Overall PD Severity

No significant associations were observed for p‐tau217 or GFAP using any subscale of MDS‐UPDRS. However, higher NfL levels were significantly associated with worse Part I scores (β = 0.35, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.10–0.60, P FDR = 0.03) and worse Part II scores (β = 0.39, 95% CI = 0.17–0.61, P FDR = 0.006), but not with Part III scores (P FDR = 0.20; Table S3).

Discussion

This study examined whether plasma p‐tau217 is associated with cognitive impairment and domain‐specific cognitive performance in PD. We identified several key findings. First, p‐tau217 levels were not higher in PD patients compared to controls, despite worse cognitive function in the PD group. Second, contrary to our predictions, plasma p‐tau217 levels were not associated with neuropsychological performance, nor with bedside cognitive testing and self‐reported cognitive impairment. Third, APOE ε4 carriers did not exhibit stronger associations between p‐tau217 levels and cognitive performance compared with noncarriers across any cognitive domain, and APOE ε4 status was not associated with cognitive performance. These findings suggest that AD co‐pathology may not be a major contributor to the earlier stages of cognitive impairment in PD.

Our findings are broadly consistent with studies relying on p‐tau181, which found that p‐tau181 is not increased in PD patients without dementia and does not correlate with cognitive performance. 30 , 31 Interestingly, studies measuring cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Aβ42 have shown that CSF Aβ42 levels may follow a different pattern in early PD; Aβ42 is lower (ie, more abnormal) than in controls, and low CSF Aβ42 has been more consistently associated with cognitive impairment in early PD. 19 , 32 This suggests potential divergence between CSF Aβ42 and p‐tau levels in early PD.

The absence of clear links between p‐tau217 and cognition in PD is in keeping with the long‐standing dual cognitive syndrome hypothesis for PD, which posits that early cognitive changes are mediated by dopaminergic loss 33 , 34 and other subcortical neuromodulatory systems, 22 whereas the more advanced state of dementia is more related to cortical degeneration due to spreading PD pathology and/or comorbid AD pathology. 35 , 36 Longitudinal data are required to determine if p‐tau‐217 would exhibit stronger associations in PD patients with more advanced cognitive impairment.

Our results are also notable in light of a recent study showing that among individuals with idiopathic/isolated rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (iRBD), p‐tau181 strongly predicted eventual conversion to dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). 37 Due to the very long prodromal period of Lewy body disease and the fact that AD pathology is common in older adults, this might suggest that even relatively low levels of co‐occurring AD pathology may combine with synuclein pathology to precipitate a primary dementia phenotype (ie, DLB). By contrast, those with little AD pathology might present with a motor‐predominant phenotype (ie, PD). Therefore, those with diagnosed motor PD may have effectively been “filtered out” from having significant AD pathology. Then, over time, age‐related accumulation of AD pathology may contribute to progression to PDD.

Neither GFAP nor NfL plasma levels were significantly associated with cognitive measures in PD, nor were they higher in PD than in controls. Though previous examinations of GFAP and NfL in PD have yielded heterogeneous results, most studies point to elevated levels in patients with dementia, which were excluded from this cohort. 30 , 38 , 39

We also examined associations with other PD symptom domains. Neither p‐tau217 nor GFAP exhibited significant associations with nonmotor or motor features. However, higher NfL levels were associated with greater impairments in self‐reported nonmotor and motor aspects of daily living. These findings are consistent with a recent study suggesting that NfL is associated with PD severity. 40

Our study has several limitations. First, we were unable to assess Aβ42/40 status in our sample. p‐tau217 is a sensitive marker of AD pathology in AD cohorts, where it is associated with cognitive impairment severity in MCI‐AD populations. 41 However, its ability to detect AD pathology in PD remains to be validated with Aβ or tau PET, which would also allow for the necessary development of PD‐specific cutoffs. Longitudinal studies combining fluid with PET imaging‐derived markers of both tau and Aβ accumulation will also be necessary to further determine causal links between AD pathology and cognition in PD, and to disentangle these effects from other mechanisms of neurodegeneration.

In conclusion, our study found that plasma p‐tau217 was not clearly associated with cognitive impairment in the earlier stages of PD. This lack of association may reflect a limited burden of AD co‐pathology in our sample, but we cannot rule out that p‐tau217 has limited sensitivity to detect AD pathology in PD. Early cognitive impairment in PD may primarily result from PD pathology‐related mechanisms, with the effects of comorbid pathology being more evident only in those who develop dementia. Overall, these findings suggest that p‐tau217 alone is unlikely to fully capture MCI in PD, underscoring the need to consider both AD‐related and PD‐specific processes when investigating cognitive impairment in PD.
