Involvement of the DNA Demethylase Thymine DNA Glycosylase in Anticancer Drug Resistance
Authors: Kyoung Ah Kang, Mei Jing Piao, Herath Mudiyanselage Maheshika Madhuwanthi Senavirathna, Hye-Jin Boo, Sang Pil Yoon, Joo Mi Yi, Jinny Park, Jin Won Hyun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Anticancer drug resistance remains a significant challenge to the efficacy of cancer treatment, with DNA repair enzymes contributing to this resistance. We hypothesized that thymine DNA glycosylases (TDGs) may be involved in anticancer drug resistance given their dual function of DNA repair and demethylation as well as investigated their possible involvement in the induction of β-catenin in SNUC5 cells resistant to 5-fluorouracil (SNUC5/5-FUR) and oxaliplatin (SNUC5/OXTR). The expression of TDG and phospho-β-catenin increased in both resistant cell types when compared to that in SNUC5 cells. Moreover, knockdown of TDG significantly suppressed phospho-β-catenin expression in both resistant cell types, resulting in enhanced sensitivity to anticancer drugs. TDG binding to the β-catenin promoter was stronger in both resistant cell types than in SNUC5 cells, showing a decreased methylation pattern in the CpG islands of the β-catenin promoter. Furthermore, another DNA demethylase, ten-eleven translocation 1 (TET1), showed the same pattern as TDG in both resistant cell types. Additionally, TDG significantly interacted more with TET1 in both resistant cell types than in SNUC5 cells, enhancing binding to the same locus in the β-catenin promoter. These findings suggest that TDG may be a promising target molecule for overcoming drug resistance in colorectal cancer.

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common type of cancer worldwide, with over 600,000 CRC-related deaths occurring annually (Ahmad et al., 2021). The CRC rate is higher in Africa, North America, and Europe, and increases with age (Rawla et al., 2019). In recent decades, CRC incidence has been increasing in Korea, which had the second-highest CRC incidence worldwide in 2018, at an estimated rate of 44.5 per 100,000 individuals per year (Bray et al., 2018).

Chemotherapy is vital in CRC therapy; however, patients with CRC have a 5-year survival rate of only 10–15%, primarily due to the increase in chemotherapy resistance and the lack of alternative therapies (Baviskar et al., 2021; Guinney et al., 2015). Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms of drug resistance is necessary to improve CRC chemotherapy.

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) and oxaliplatin (OXT) have been used as chemotherapy agents for patients with CRC since the 1950s (Sethy and Kundu, 2021; Yaffee et al., 2015). 5-FU synthetic nucleotide analogs inhibits thymidylate synthase activity and incorporates its metabolite into DNA, leading to cytotoxicity (Blondy et al., 2020). OXT third-generation platinum drugs induces cell death by forming platinum-DNA adducts (Martinez-Balibrea et al., 2015). Therefore, tolerance to the disruption of DNA replication generated by 5-FU or OXT, which avoids replication fork stalling and subsequent cell death, may contribute to 5-FU and OXT resistance.

Mutations that deregulate the WNT/β-catenin signaling cascade are among the most common genetic alterations observed in CRC progression (Farooqi et al., 2019). The WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway is crucial for embryonic development and maintaining cell homeostasis and is linked to cancer cell proliferation, invasion, stem cell renewal or differentiation, apoptosis, and chemotherapy resistance (Liu et al., 2021). Furthermore, one of the key pathways involved in various CRC processes is the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway (Zhao et al., 2022). During WNT signaling, β-catenin translocates to the nucleus and recruits transcriptional activators, including p300/CREB binding protein (CBP) and histone acetyltransferase (Xu et al., 2014), which interact with the T-cell factor/lymphocyte enhancer factor (TCF/LEF) to activate gene expression (Li et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2014). Mutations in β-catenin or adenomatous polyposis coli genes interfere with the phosphorylation and normal ubiquitination/degradation of the corresponding proteins, resulting in intranuclear accumulation of abnormal β-catenin and aberrant gene expression; this leads to an epithelial to mesenchymal transition, which is critical for both metastasis and resistance to anticancer drugs (Chang et al., 2020; Du et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2019). Downregulation of the WNT/β-catenin pathway, which inhibits multidrug resistance mutation 1 (MDR1) expression, can be achieved via the overexpression of miR-506, sensitizing CRC cells to OXT (Zhou et al., 2017). The disruption of the MDR1 efflux pump, following inhibition of transient receptor potential channel 5, can sensitize CRC to 5-FU by attenuating the WNT/β-catenin pathway (Wang et al., 2015). Therefore, downregulation of WNT/β-catenin signaling is a promising approach to overcoming chemoresistance in CRC.

Thymine DNA glycosylase (TDG) is T/G or U/G mismatch specific. It removes mismatched thymine or uracil base from the ribose ring and subsequently processes base excision repair. Moreover, TDG interacts with transcription factors, including retinoic acid receptor α and p300/CBP, maintains epigenetic stability, and promotes active DNA demethylation, which is responsible for regulating the transcription of genes (DeNizio et al., 2021; Hassan et al., 2017; Kolendowski et al., 2018; McGregor et al., 2023; Onabote et al., 2022). For example, TDG binds to DNA methyltransferase 3 (DNMT3) and inhibits its methylation activity in vitro (Sandoval and Reich, 2021). 5-Carboxylcytosine, produced from 5-methylcytosine (5-mC) and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5-hmC) via oxidation by the ten-eleven translocation (TET) family of dioxygenases, can be specifically recognized and removed by TDG, thereby enabling active DNA demethylation (Fu et al., 2019). Additionally, demethylation mediated by TET and TDG is directly involved in the mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition, which initiates reprogramming (Hu et al., 2014). In WNT signaling, TDG acts as a co-activator, binding directly to TCF/LEF and forming complexes with β-catenin/TCF and CBP. This subsequently stabilizes these factors on the promoter of the WNT target gene, facilitating the transcriptional activation activity of β-catenin/TCF (Jia et al., 2014; Miao et al., 2014). However, the detailed mechanisms remain unclear.

In this study, we shed light on this mechanism by providing compelling evidence for TDG’s novel function as a coactivator in 5-FU or OXT resistance via β-catenin signaling.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

5-FU and OXT (Sigma-Aldrich Co., St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were used to prepare stock solutions (280 mM and 30 mM). N-acetyl cysteine (NAC; Sigma-Aldrich Co.) was dissolved in sterile water to prepare stock solutions (1 M). Additionally, these stock solutions were refrigerated at –20°C.

Cell culture

Korean CRC cells, SNUC5, were purchased from the Korean Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Korea). Established SNUC5 cells resistant to 5-FU (SNUC5/5-FUR) or OXT (SNUC5/OXTR) were obtained from the Research Center for Resistant Cells of Chosun University (Gwangju, Korea). SNUC5/5-FUR cells were treated with 140 μM 5-FU twice weekly for at least 6 months and then sub-cultured to establish a stable cell line (Kang et al., 2014). SNUC5/OXTR cells were stabilized by subculturing them weekly for 6 months in RPMI 1640 medium containing 7 μM OXT.

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

For RT-PCR, amplification of TDG and β-catenin cDNA was performed using the following thermocycling program: at 94°C for 5 min for initial denaturation, 35 cycles of 94°C for 1 min, 56°C for 1 min, 72°C for 1 min, and final elongation at 72°C for 7 min on the PCR detection system (T-100 cycler; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Furthermore, qRT-PCR reaction conditions were as follows: predenaturing at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min on the real-time PCR detection system (CFX96; Bio-Rad Laboratories) (Kang et al., 2014). Supplementary Table 1 presents the primers used.

Western blot analysis

Extracted proteins (40 µg) were electrophoresed, transferred onto membranes, and incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 2). The membrane was then incubated with secondary immunoglobulin G-horseradish peroxidase conjugates (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA), and detected using a western blot detection kit (Amersham, Little Chalfont, UK).

Immunocytochemistry

Cells on chamber slides fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min were permeabilized with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. The cells were then incubated with blocking buffer (PBS containing 3% bovine serum albumin) for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 2) diluted in blocking buffer for 2 h. Cells were incubated with FITC-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Fluorescently stained cells were imaged using a confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) using the LSM 510 program (Kang et al., 2014).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR)

The DNA recovered from the immunoprecipitated complexes, using a simple ChIPTM kit (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), was subjected to qPCR. The TDG, TET1 or IgG binding sites to β-catenin promoter were designed as four sites (A-D); A site (–2506/–1024 of β-catenin), B site (–1124/–600 of β-catenin), C site (–614/–270 of β-catenin), and D site (–260/+30 of β-catenin), as described in the previous studies (Kang et al., 2014; Xu et al., 2014). Supplementary Table 3 presents the ChIP primers used.

Luciferase assay

The β-catenin promoter-luciferase plasmid (pGL2-β-catenin-Luc; A-D site) was obtained from Cosmogenetech Institute (Seoul, Korea). The plasmids were named pGL2-A site (–2506/–1024 of β-catenin), pGL2-B site (–1124/–600 of β-catenin), pGL2-C site (–614/–270 of β-catenin), and pGL2-D site (–260/+30 of β-catenin), according to the distance from the transcription start site of β-catenin. Cells were transiently transfected with plasmids (pGL2-β-catenin-Luc) using Lipofectamine (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA). Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using a dual luciferase assay kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA)

Transfection of siRNA

A concentration of 10-20 nM siRNAs against TDG and TET1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) transfected into cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) (Kang et al., 2014).

Hoechst 33342 staining

Cells were treated with a 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 5-FU or OXT for 48 h, stained using Hoechst 33342 dye, and then observed under a fluorescence microscope (Kang et al., 2014). The intensity of apoptotic bodies was calculated as follows: apoptotic index = (number of apoptotic cells in the treated group/total number of cells in the treated group)/(number of apoptotic cells in the control group/total number of cells in the control group).

Propidium iodide (PI) staining

Cells were stained using PI (Sigma-Aldrich Co.), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PI-stained cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson Biosciences, Heidelberg, Germany) (Fernando et al., 2022). Overall, 10,000 cells were analyzed per measurement, and data were analyzed using the BD FACSDiva™ software version 6.0 (Becton Dickinson Biosciences).

Caspase-3 activity assay

Caspase-3 activity was determined using a colorimetric caspase-3 assay kit (ab39401; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Baharara et al., 2018).

TET activity

The fixed and permeabilized cells were incubated with 5-mC, 5-hmC, and 5-formylcytosine (5-fC) antibodies, followed by incubation with a FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 1 h. Fluorescently stained cells were imaged using a confocal microscope (Kang et al., 2014).

Quantitative methylation-specific PCR (qMSP)

After DNA was modified with bisulfite using the Methylamp™ DNA modification kit (Epigentek, Pittsburgh, PA, USA), qMSP and MSP were performed using an EpiScope® MSP (TaKaRa Bio, Shiga, Japan) and Epitect MSP kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), respectively (Kang et al., 2014; Miao et al., 2014). Using bioinformatics, we investigated potential CpG islands located 1592 bp upstream of the translation start site in the β-catenin promoter region utilizing the NCBI database. Within the β-catenin promoter region, two CpG-rich islands were identified at –1124 to –600 and –614 to –270 bp upstream of the translation start site. qMSP and MSP primers were designed in the promoter region spanning from –620 to –260 bp, which contained 16 CpG sites, using the methPrimer program (https://www.urogene.org/methprimer/). Supplementary Table 4 presents the primers used.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

A mouse/rabbit red starter Duolink kit (Sigma-Aldrich Co.) was used for PLA. The cells were incubated with rabbit TET1 (1:100) and mouse TDG (1:100) antibodies, then reacted with PLA probes at 37°C for 1 h, and imaged using a confocal microscope (Kang et al., 2024).

Cell viability assay

A density of 2×103 cells/well was inoculated in a 96-well plate, treated with 5-FU or OXT, and incubated for 48 h. The resulting formazan crystals produced in each well after reaction with MTT solution for 4 h were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide, and the OD value was obtained at 540 nm (Fernando et al., 2022).

Detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Cells were fluorescently stained with 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA), and the fluorescence of DCF was analyzed using a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) with CellQuest software for detection (Kang et al., 2024).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the values of measurements were expressed as mean ± standard error. The results were analyzed for pairwise differences using analysis of variance and Tukey’s test. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05, 0.01, and 0.001. Statistical analysis was performed using SigmaStat v3.5 (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA).

RESULTS

TDG and β-catenin expression and the binding affinity of TDG to the β-catenin promoter in SNUC5 cells and their resistant variants

The cytotoxic effects of 5-FU and OXT on SNUC5 cells and their resistant variants, SNUC5/5-FUR and SNUC5/OXTR, were compared. The IC50 of 5-FU and OXT was 13 μM and 19 μM in SNUC5 cells and 2830 μM and 86 μM in SNUC5/5-FUR and SNUC5/OXTR cells, in which resistance was induced by continuous culture in 140 μM 5-FU and 7 μM OXT, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1A).

5-FU and OXT induced apoptosis in SNUC5 cells and their resistant variants, as indicated by Hoechst 33342 staining. 5-FU induced apoptosis in SNUC5 (32 of apoptosis index) and SNUC5/5-FUR (29 of apoptosis index) cells, whereas OXT induced apoptosis in SNUC5 (28 of apoptosis index) and SNUC5/OXTR cells (25 of apoptosis index) (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Sub-G1 population and caspase-3 activity showed the similar results of Hoechst 33342 staining (Supplementary Fig. S1C, S1D). The mRNA levels of TDG, a transcriptional cofactor for β-catenin, and its target β-catenin were higher in both the resistant cell types than those in the SNUC5 cells (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the protein expression levels of TDG, β-catenin, and phospho-β-catenin were higher in both the resistant cell types than those in the SNUC5 cells (Fig. 1B). Levels of nuclear TDG and β-catenin translocated from the cytosol to the nucleus were higher in both resistant cell types than those in the SNUC5 cells (Fig. 1C).

We then evaluated whether TDG could directly bind to the four CpG islands (sites A-D) of the β-catenin promoter in SNUC5 cells and their resistant variants using a ChIP-qPCR assay (Supplementary Fig. S1E). Levels of TDG bound to the β-catenin promoter region were higher in both the resistant cell types than those in the SNUC5 cells, particularly at site C (Fig. 1D). To confirm that the identified TDG binding site within the β-catenin promoter region had transcription activity, we performed promoter-luciferase reporter assay using luciferase reporter plasmid containing the TDG-predicted binding sites in the β-catenin promoter (β-catenin-Luc-A-D). Reporter assays revealed that binding sites C and D significantly activated β-catenin promoter-driven reporter activity in both the resistant cell types when compared to that in the SNUC5 cells (Fig. 1E). The siTDG RNA-transfected cells significantly reduced the mRNA and protein expression of β-catenin and phospho β-catenin in the SNUC5 cells and both resistant cell types (Supplementary Fig. S1F, Fig. 1F, 1G). Additionally, the viability of siTDG RNA-transfected cells was significantly decreased after 5-FU or OXT treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1G). Moreover, 5-FU or OXT significantly increased the number of apoptotic bodies in anticancer drug-untreated siTDG-transfected cells (Fig. 1H, Supplementary Fig. S1H). Furthermore, sub-G1 population and caspase-3 activity showed a similar pattern with Hoechst 33342 staining (Fig. 1I, Supplementary Fig. S1I, Fig. 1J). Moreover, the viability of siβ-catenin RNA-transfected cells significantly decreased after 5-FU or OXT treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1J, S1K). In addition, 5-FU or OXT significantly increased apoptosis in anticancer drug-untreated siβ-catenin-transfected cells (Fig. 1K, Supplementary Fig. S1L, Fig. 1L, Supplementary Fig. S1M, Fig. 1M). These results suggested that TDG could positively regulate β-catenin expression in chemo-resistant cancer cells.

Effect of the DNA demethylase TET1 on β-catenin expression in SNUC5 cells and their resistant variants

Recently, we reported that the expression of the DNA demethylase TET1 was higher in the SNUC5/5-FUR cells than that in the SNUC5 cells (Kang et al., 2014). DNMT1 expression was not significantly different between SNUC5 cells and their resistant variants, whereas that of TET1 was higher in the resistant variants than that in the SNUC5 cells (Fig. 2A). ChIP-qPCR showed that the binding of TET1 to the β-catenin promoter region was higher in both resistant cell types than that in the SNUC5 cells, particularly at site C (Fig. 2B). Additionally, after siTET1 RNA-transfection, mRNA and protein expression of β-catenin was significantly lower in the SNUC5 cells and their resistant variants (Supplementary Fig. S2A, Fig. 2C, 2D). Similar to siTDG-transfected cells, 5-FU and OXT decreased the viability of siTET1 RNA-transfected cells (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Moreover, 5-FU or OXT treatment in siTET1-transfected cells resulted in significantly more apoptotic bodies than did 5-FU or OXT treatment of siControl-transfected and anticancer drug-untreated siTET1-transfected cells using Hoechst 33342, sub-G1 population, and caspase-3 activity (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. S2C, Fig. 2F, Supplementary Fig. S2D, Fig. 2G). These results suggested that the DNA demethylase TET1 increased β-catenin expression in chemo-resistant cancer cells.

Furthermore, when analyzing double siRNA knockdown of TDG and TET1 in the resistant variant cells, mRNA and protein expression of β-catenin was significantly lower in the resistant variant cells (Supplementary Fig. S2E, Fig. 2H, 2I, Supplementary Fig. S2F). Moreover, 5-FU or OXT further decreased the viability of double siRNA-transfected cells than single siRNA-transfected cells (Fig. 2J).

DNA methylation level at the β-catenin promoter region in SNUC5 cells and their resistant variants

The DNA demethylase activities of TDG and TET were assessed by measuring the levels of 5-mC, 5-hmC, and 5-fC. 5-mC level was lower in the resistant variants than that in the SNUC5 cells; however, 5-hmC and 5-fC levels were higher in the resistant variants than those in the SNUC5 cells, as shown by confocal imaging (Fig. 3A) and dot blot analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Next, we examined the methylation status of the β-catenin promoter using qMSP and MSP, whose primers were designed to analyze these CpG sites in the promoter region (from –2760 to +27 bp) across its transcriptional start site. There are no differences between SNUC5 and the resistant variant cells at sites A and B, but an evident difference was observed at sites C and D, with lower methylation levels at site C in the resistant variants than those in the SNUC5 cells (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, the interaction between TET1 and TDG at the β-catenin promoter region was stronger in the resistant variants than that in the SNUC5 cells, as shown by PLA assay (Fig. 3C) and immunoprecipitation (Supplementary Fig. S3B). The siTDG or siTET1 RNA-treated cells showed increased methylation at site C in the β-catenin promoter region in both resistant variant cells (Fig. 3D). Therefore, changes in β-catenin transcription in chemo-resistant variants were most likely due to epigenetic regulation, particularly the demethylation of the promoter.

Involvement of intracellular ROS in TDG and β-catenin expression and the binding affinity of TDG to the β-catenin promoter in SNUC5 cells and resistant variants

ROS levels were higher in the resistant variants than those in the SNUC5 cells; however, the antioxidant N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) attenuated these ROS levels (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. S4A). Additionally, NAC decreased the mRNA expression of TDG, TET1, and β-catenin (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. S4B) as well as the protein levels of TDG, TET1, β-catenin, and phospho-β-catenin (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. S4C). Furthermore, the enhanced binding affinity of TDG and TET1 to site C of the β-catenin promoter was attenuated by NAC in SNUC5 cells and their resistant variants (Fig. 4D, Supplementary Fig. S4D), leading to decreased β-catenin promoter-driven reporter activity (Fig. 4E), weaker interaction between TDG and TET1 (Fig. 4F, Supplementary Fig. S4E), and higher methylation levels in the β-catenin promoter (Fig. 4G). NAC treatment decreased cell viability (Supplementary Fig. S4F) and increased apoptosis (Fig. 4H, Supplementary Fig. S4G, Fig. 4I, Supplementary Fig. S4H, Fig. 4J). These results suggested that cancer resistance induced by ROS through β-catenin was mediated by TDG and TET1.

DISCUSSION

Previously, we reported that the induction of 5-FU resistance to CRC cells involves changes in the expression of nuclear factor (erythroid 2)-like 2 (NRF2) and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), which are induced by epigenetic modification of the DNA demethylase TET1 (Kang et al., 2014). Epigenetic alteration is a major cause of drug resistance in various cancers, including CRC (Hsu et al., 2018; Ilango et al., 2020; Miranda Furtado et al., 2019). This suggests that epigenetic modulators may be used to overcome drug resistance in CRC and reduce the drug resistance of conventional chemotherapeutic agents in clinical settings, thereby increasing their efficacy. In this study, we showed that the expression of β-catenin, TDG, and TET1 was upregulated in the SNUC5/5-FUR and SNUC5/OXTR cells compared to that in the parental cells (SNUC5). Phosphorylated β-catenin at Ser33/37/Thr41 in the cytosol is the degradation target; however, when it accumulates in the nucleus, it can activate downstream genes involved in cell proliferation, survival, and migration (Uzun et al., 2023). We demonstrated that the expression of phospho-β-catenin in the nucleus was upregulated in both resistant cells compared to that in SNUC5 cells (Fig. 1B).

Several studies have examined the activity of DNA glycosylases in response to 5-FU, with varying conclusions regarding their relative contributions to 5-FU resistance (Kuznetsova et al., 2017; Nagaria et al., 2013; Yan et al., 2016). Inhibition of uracil DNA glycosylase (UDG) increases the sensitivity of cancer cells to 5-FU by causing DNA damage through replication fork collapse (Yan et al., 2016). Reportedly, TDG promotes transactivation of β-catenin/TCFs by cooperating with CBP, and TDG interacts with the transcription factor TCF4 and coactivator p300 in the WNT pathway of CRC (Jia et al., 2014; Xu et al., 2014). Despite these reports suggesting that TDG is a factor necessary for CRC growth by strengthening WNT signaling, there are few studies on the role of TDG in regulating anticancer drug-resistant CRC. Furthermore, TDG is associated with demethylation of the β-catenin promoter; nevertheless, it remains unclear whether TDG directly induces drug resistance through β-catenin activation or if additional Wnt-related targets are involved. Therefore, β-catenin may not be the only TDG target gene, and future studies will comprehensively identify other potential target genes regulated by TDG using whole-genome analysis methods including ChIP-sequencing or reduced-representation bisulfite sequencing. Moreover, TET1 is involved in the resistance to anticancer drugs, such as 5-FU, in CRC (Kang et al., 2014; Lu et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2015). Consistently, our results showed that TDG and TET1 expression were elevated in SNUC5/5-FUR and SNUC5/OXTR cells compared to that in SNUC5 cells. Moreover, we found that the resistant variants and SNUC5 cells transfected with siRNA against TDG or TET1 showed increased cytotoxicity and were more sensitive to 5-FU or OXT.

Many studies have shown that cancer cells that become resistant to 5-FU or OXT adapt to oxidative stress by upregulating antioxidant systems, such as ROS modulator 1 (ROMO1), NRF2, manganese superoxide dismutase, and thioredoxin reductase (Chun and Joo, 2022; Hwang et al., 2007; Payandeh et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2017). Treatment with siRNA against ROMO1 blocked 5-FU-induced ROS production, indicating that 5-FU stimulated ROS generation through ROMO1 induction (Hwang et al., 2007). Additionally, OXT-resistant colon cancer cells showed increased NRF2 expression and exhibited chemotherapeutic effects by inhibiting NRF2 signaling (Payandeh et al., 2021). ROS can affect the genome through epigenetic changes, and it is significant in human carcinogenesis (Urso et al., 2020). Furthermore, ROS generation has been implicated in alterations in DNA methylation patterns (Bhat et al., 2018). Several oncogenes are activated by ROS-mediated demethylation of CpG island-rich promoter regions (Kang et al., 2014; Sengupta et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022). For instance, upon exposure to oxidative stress, long interspersed nuclear element-1, a major hallmark of cancer, accrues hypomethylation patterns, and its expression is ultimately increased in bladder cancer (Kloypan et al., 2015; Mendes et al., 2021). Additionally, we reported that 5-FU induced hypomethylation of the NRF2 promoter via ROS production (Kang et al., 2014). In this study, we also showed that ROS levels were elevated in resistant variants compared to those in SNUC5 cells. Moreover, NAC, a ROS scavenger, decreased the expression levels of TDG, TET1, β-catenin, and phospho-β-catenin, and increased apoptosis in both resistant cell lines. However, our current study reveals an association between ROS levels and TDG/TET1 expression. Further research is needed to determine whether ROS directly regulates TDG and TET1 through a promoter luciferase assay or indirectly via other transcription factors.

One of the most extensively studied areas of epigenetics in carcinogenesis is DNA methylation, which has emerged as a major focus of pharmacological intervention in clinical trials. To activate target genes, TDG interacts with various transcription factors. In addition to TET, TDG is also involved in the active DNA demethylation pathway, which involves the removal of 5-mC from DNA and replacing with unmethylated cytosine (Onabote et al., 2022). In this study, the 5-mC level was lower; however, those of 5-hmC and 5-fC were significantly higher in both resistant cell lines than those in the SNUC5 cells. Moreover, the induction of TDG and TET1 led to activation of β-catenin in both resistant cell lines. Additionally, the C region in Supplementary Fig. S1E is rich in common motifs of transcription factors such as TCF/LEF, which is also known to interact with TDG and TET1 as confirmed by various studies (Miao et al., 2014; Xu et al., 2014). Furthermore, TET1 oxidizes 5-mC to 5-hmC/5-fC, and then TDG recognizes and removes these modified bases (Onodera et al., 2021). The binding affinity of TDG and TET1 in both resistant cell lines is more enhanced than that in the SNUC5 cells (Fig. 4F). This protein–protein interaction via PLA supports this cooperative mechanism.

In conclusion, these results suggested that in CRC with induced resistance to 5-FU or OXT, alterations in β-catenin expression were accompanied by epigenetic modification via DNA demethylation (Fig. 5). These findings provide mechanistic insights into the epigenetic regulation of cancer cell proliferation and drug resistance, supporting clinical trials of anticancer drugs as therapeutic interventions for cancer therapy and revealing TDG as a target for epigenetic therapies. Moreover, future work could evaluate the anti-cancer drug-resistant colon cancer model on patient-derived clinical samples or other colon cancer cell lines to confirm its validation. Our results suggest that DNA demethylases may represent a potential therapeutic target for overcoming drug resistance to anti-cancer drug treatment in CRC, which warrants further investigation in future studies.
