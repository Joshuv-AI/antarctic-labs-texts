Exogenous pipecolic acid ameliorates lead-induced phytotoxicity in chili (Capsicum annuum L.) by enhancing antioxidant capacity and physiological homeostasis
Authors: Rashid Iqbal, Hassan Mehmood, Asma Majeed, Ghulam Murtaza, Aziza Huseynova, Sajid Ullah, Allah Ditta, Badr Aldahmash, Ahmed Rady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHeavy metal toxicity disrupts plant cellular homeostasis by inducing oxidative stress and impairing the key signaling networks that regulate plant growth and defense. Small signaling metabolites, such as pipecolic acid (PipA), a known regulator of systemic acquired resistance, may play a critical role in modulating plant responses to abiotic stresses. This study investigated the signaling and physiological roles of exogenously applied PipA in mitigating lead (Pb)-induced phytotoxicity in chili (Capsicum annuum L.). Eight treatments comprising an absolute control, Pb stress alone (800 mg kg−1 soil) and single or combined PipA applications (0.5 mM via seed priming, soil drenching, and foliar spray) were administered in homogenized soil media. Lead stress alone drastically impaired plant performance, as the root length decreased by 45.9%, shoot length decreased by 45.8%, total yield decreased by 46.7%, relative water content (RWC) decreased by 32.11%, membrane stability index (MSI) decreased by 42.07%, photosynthetic rate (Pn) decreased by 56.86%, stomatal conductance (gs) decreased by 57.01%, malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration increased by 237.92%, hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation increased by 260.71%, fruit length decreased by 48.38%, vitamin C concentration decreased by 39.92%, and root lead accumulation increased by 73525.21%. In contrast, combined PipA applications significantly reversed these deteriorations, particularly those resulting from seed priming and foliar spray, which increased the root length by 35.94%, enhanced Pn by 105.36%, elevated the gs by 113.76%, and reduced the contents of MDA by 60.54 and also reduced the form of accumulated H2O2 by 63.37% relative to that in stressed plants. Dual delivery systems are synergistic in activating antioxidant enzymes, stabilizing membranes, optimizing gas exchange, and minimizing heavy metal translocation. PipA supplementation showed beneficial effects on reprogramming stress-responsive pathways and holds promise for enhancing crop resilience under chronic metal stress conditions. Reclaiming (polluted) agroecosystems should be accomplished through the adoption of integrated bioremediation protocols using exogenous PipA. Collectively, the evidence suggests that combining both exogenous PipA application methods (initial treatment via seed priming and subsequent treatment via foliage) is a viable, biologically compatible, and practical/flexible way to overcome Pb-induced physiological effects on crop yield in contaminated agricultural ecosystems.Keywords: Phytoremediation, capsicum, metabolite, toxicity, yield, antioxidant, bioremediation

Introduction

Heavy metal contamination, especially lead (Pb), represents a stealthy threat to the environment and poses risks to agricultural output and food safety. 1 , 2 The accumulation of Pb in arable soil is attributed to anthropogenic pollutants, such as industrial emissions, mining activities, excessive use of phosphate fertilizers, and municipal sewage sludge. 3 , 4 Chili (Capsicum annuum L.) is a key horticultural crop with the potential to play a crucial role in contributing significantly to the global economy and nutrition sector, as it is an important source of dietary vitamins and carotenoids. 5-7 Chili is primarily grown in tropical and subtropical regions, supporting rural livelihoods and export-based economies (often not aligned with political power) and ensuring food security in Asia, Africa, and Latin America. 8 Nevertheless, chili plants are highly sensitive to Pb toxicity, which severely impacts seed germination, plant growth, and flowering when grown in contaminated substrates. 9 , 10

In root uptake, Pb ions cause serious physiological and biochemical disturbances by leading to excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), induction of lipid peroxidation and damage to the integrity of the plasma membranes. 11 Pb also competitively replaces critical micronutrients, including zinc, iron and manganese, in metalloenzymes, thereby inhibiting photosynthetic electron transport, ribulose-1, 5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase activity and carbon pathways. 12 , 13 It also disrupts stomatal regulation, lowers chlorophyll biosynthesis and relative water content, reduces transpiration and decreases the efficiency of gaseous exchange. 14 , 15 These disturbances in chili plants translate into retarded root architecture, lowered shoot biomass, impaired fruit set and reduced nutritional value, which ultimately leads to potential dietary exposure to accumulated toxins because of the bioaccumulation of tissue-damaging toxins. 16 , 17 guarantee sustainable crop production. Therefore, the creation of sustainable mitigatory measures that regain physiological homeostasis to the least extent of translocation of Pb into the edible tissues is crucial to guarantee sustainable crop production.

The use of synthetic chelators to amend the soil and inoculate it with microorganisms is one of the conventional methods to alleviate stress caused by heavy metals in crops; however, these methods are often costly, persistent in the environment and have unpredictable effectiveness in the field. 18 , 19 Synthetic chelators, including EDTA, are good at mobilizing metals; however, they have secondary contamination and can leach heavy metals out of groundwater, posing a threat to the stability of the ecosystem. 20 Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria and biofertilizers have demonstrated reliability; however, their effectiveness is highly dependent on the composition of soil microbiomes and environmental changes. 21 Moreover, the vast majority of remediation mechanisms are based on soil immobilization and phytoextraction principles, ignoring the effect of physiological enhancement of crop resistance to active metals and the maintenance of yield quality under active metal exposure. 22 , 23 Such knowledge gaps highlight the importance of biocompatible, signaling-based compounds that can systemically prime plant defense networks and have no phytotoxic effects or alterations to soil chemistry.

Pipecolic acid (PipA), another lysine-derived non-proteinogenic amino acid, has recently emerged as a significant endogenous signalling molecule that coordinates systemic acquired resistance with abiotic stress adaptation. Exogenous PipA applications have been shown to induce antioxidant enzyme networks and proline and glycine betaine accumulation to stabilize osmotic potential; PipA has also been shown to enhance glutathione-mediated detoxification cascades. In addition, PipA may limit the xylem-mediated transport of heavy metals to maintain the integrity of the photosynthetic apparatus, thereby preserving plant productivity under stress. Despite these properties, there has been limited investigation into how PipA is delivered when applied to solanaceous crops exposed to Pb stress, especially regarding the optimum delivery avenues that will enhance stress resilience while minimizing the metal load in edible plant tissues.

Recently, pipecolic acid (PipA), a non-proteinogenic cyclic amino acid derived from lysine metabolism, has become the focus of research because it is a potent endogenous signaling molecule that can orchestrate systemic acquired resistance and abiotic stress tolerance in plants. 24 , 25 PipA is also involved in cellular redox homeostasis by elevating the levels of antioxidant enzyme systems, stabilizing the osmotic potential by accumulating proline and glycine betaine and stabilizing glutathione-mediated detoxification pathways. 26 , 27 It can chelate metal ions, restricting xylem-mediated translocation to aerial tissues and maintaining the integrity of the photosynthetic apparatus by preserving chlorophyll fluorescence and stomatal conductance. 28 , 29 Despite these promising qualities, the use of exogenous PipA is underutilized concerning the best delivery channels and combination of synergistically employed treatments in solanaceous crops subjected to Pb contamination, especially in terms of optimal delivery channels and combinations of synergistically utilized treatments. 30 Each has a unique physiological entry point; however, the relative efficacy of each and their potential to enable the maximum stress-resilient effect and minimize the accumulation of Pb in fruit have not been comparatively assessed. 31

Because of the potential economic impacts on chili production systems and the increased global prevalence of Pb contamination in soils, there is an urgent need to implement specific plant-oriented remediation methods. This study investigated the amelioration of exogenously applied PipA in chili plants growing in Pb-contaminated soils using single or combined application methods of exogenous PipA with respect to the performance, oxidative stress, antioxidant defense mechanisms, nutrient homeostasis, and fruit quality of the plants. This study will offer agroecosystem-wide application regime optimization and biochemical mechanisms for deciphering PipA as a mitigating agent against the sequential onset of metal toxicity in crops, which will help inform a scalable agroecosystem strategy that can enhance crop resilience in the face of chronic contaminated agroecosystems. Thus, the objective of this work was to determine how various application regimes of PipA modify the physiological, biochemical, and yield-based responses of chili under lead stress, which will eventually incorporate a working mitigation panel into the optimization of mitigation frameworks to support sustainable chili production under lead stress.

Materials and methods

Experimental site, growing season, and duration

The experiment was conducted under controlled wire house conditions. The local climate was arid to semi-arid. In mid-February 2025, the process of sowing and substrate preparation began. Early March 2025 marked the transplantation stage, following which the early vegetative phase transitioned into the reproductive phase from April to May 2025. Physiological maturity harvesting occurred in mid-June 2025. The total time spent on the entire experiment ranged from 120 d between the planting time and the time when the commercial harvest of the product was reached. The window (February to June) was chosen to align with the optimal thermoperiod (20–35 °C day/15–22 °C night) of Capsicum annuum L. phenological development under semi-controlled conditions, thus minimizing confounding effects arising from thermal stress (Hanson and Kretchman, 2004). The wire house infrastructure provided protection against avian predation, seed-eating reptiles, and uncontrolled precipitation, while maintaining a consistent ambient photoperiod and a set of diurnal high and low temperatures.

Soil physical and chemical characteristics

Soil sampling was performed in the identified research area, and samples in pots were collected. The soil samples were air-dried at room temperature and passed through a 2-mm sieve. The samples were characterized before filling the pots. The physical and chemical properties of the experimental soil are listed in Table 1. A high pH (8.1–8.2) and low content of organic matter indicated the limited ability of the native Pb complexation complex and thus increased the bioavailability of Pb and ensured that the stress was induced consistently throughout the experiment.

Soil preparation and lead stress induction

The soil used in the experiment, with pH levels ranging from 8.1 to 8.4 and low organic matter content, suggests a restricted ability for native lead complexation. This characteristic ensured uniform stress induction. Analytical-grade lead nitrate [Pb(NO3)2] was dissolved in deionized water to induce heavy metal stress at a target concentration of 800 mg of elemental Pb kg−1 dry soil. Each 8.0 kg soil pot was administered with 10.22 g of Pb(NO3)2, which was evenly distributed across the soil surface and thoroughly integrated into the pre-moistened soil to achieve uniformity. Ambient conditions were maintained in the spiked soil to equilibrate the conditions for 14 d before sowing. Prior to spiking the soil sample with lead, the researchers collected baseline values of lead levels in the soil samples (Table 1). All pots had an open top to allow natural air exchange. During experimentation, lead concentrations in the soil were maintained by applying deionized irrigation water to all pots, protecting the pots with an overhead cover to prevent rainwater from leaching through the soil, and performing post-harvest re-analysis of all soil samples to verify that the treatment remained unchanged.

Program and design of the experiment

The experiment was conducted in a completely randomized design (CRD) with four independent biological replicates per treatment, with 32 experimental units. The units were made of high-density polyethylene pots containing 8.0 kg of homogenized, pre-equilibrated soil. The randomization of pots was performed weekly to overcome microenvironmental gradients, and the inter-pot separation was maintained at 25 cm to avoid canopy interference. The concentration of PipA used in the various treatments was kept at a constant concentration of 0.5 mM across the different treatments, as shown in Table 2.

Soil preparation, lead stress induction and pipecolic acid (PipA) application

Analytical-grade lead nitrate [Pb(NO3)2] was dissolved in deionized water to induce heavy metal stress. To allow time to stabilize the soil before sowing, the soil was equilibrated at 70% water-holding capacity (WHC) under ambient conditions for 14 d. Pipecolic acid (PipA) was applied via seed priming, soil drenching, and foliar spray: certified Rashid chili seeds were moistened with a 0.5 mM PipA solution in the shade for 12 h at 25 °C. Following moistening, the seeds were allowed to dry in the shade for 12 h at 25 °C. The seeds in the control treatments were primed in deionized water under equal conditions. It should be noted that the PipA solution (0.5 mM) was directly applied to the rhizosphere at the 4-leaf stage (except at the start of flowering, 60 DAS). The solution was added after a calibrated pipette was used to deliver the solution in a small amount (20 mL) to ensure that the solution was applied in a small volume (20 mL) to facilitate rhizospheric penetration. While for foliar spraying, a 0.5 mM PipA solution mixed with 0.1% (v/v) Tween-20 (non-ionic surfactant) was applied to the canopy at the vegetative peak (35 DAS) and early fruit set (65 DAS). They were sprayed at a single level of 10 mL of plant at 08:00–10:00 am to minimize photodegradation, stomatal closure, and leaf runoff. Only deionized water containing 0.1% Tween-20 was used for the control plants. In previous testing, concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 0.9 mM were determined to be ideal dosages for PipA use in tomato and pepper; these results represent a continuation of the same course of research and development to determine an ideal concentration based on previous laboratory experiences. Separate studies were conducted for two separate experiments, with a foliar application occurring on day 35 post-emergence (first vegetative growth) and 65 (early fruit development). In both cases, 10 mL of PipA per plant was applied via foliar spraying during the peak hours of the morning (8:00–10:00). This time frame was chosen to provide optimal conditions for stomatal absorption while minimizing the photodecomposition of PipA and runoff from treated leaf surfaces. Data were collected from fully developed leaf sets of the third- to fourth-leaves from the terminal stem for all biochemical measurements.

Crop management and agronomic practices

A water-holding capacity of 75 ± 5% was maintained throughout the experiment using gravimetric measurements. Water purity was ensured using only deionized water to exclude exogenous metal contamination from the water. Fertilization was performed at soil test levels, that is, 120 kg N ha−1 (as urea), 60 kg P2O5 ha−1 (as single superphosphate), and 60 kg K2O ha−1 (as potassium sulfate). Equivalent doses of top-dressed nitrogen were applied thrice at 20, 45, and 70 d after sowing (DAS). Preventive biosecurity in a wire house is the basis for managing pests and diseases. No chemical pesticides or fungicides were used to prevent confounding biochemical and physiological effects. 32

Collection and preparation of samples

Harvesting was performed at 120 DAS, when the fruit color had changed to complete physiological maturity. The plants were carefully uprooted, and the soil in the rhizosphere was gently shaken off. The roots were first rinsed with a deionized water solution and then rinsed with deionized water, which removed the surface-adsorbed Pb. The plant tissues were divided into roots, shoots, and fruits. The immediate analogs were recorded using a calibrated analytical balance, and the immediate weight data were stored as a record. Representative subsamples were swiftly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until biochemical and molecular analyses. The extracted biomass was partitioned, and the sample was subjected to analysis of nutrients and determination of the quantity of heavy metals. The leaf area was measured using a WinFOLIA Pro leaf area analyzer (Model T221B, 2016, Regent Instruments Inc., Netherlands). 33

Photosynthetic efficiency and gas exchange characteristics

Non-destructive estimation of chlorophyll concentration was performed using a SPAD-502 meter (Minolta, Japan) on three fully expanded leaves of each plant, and various values were converted to absolute chlorophyll concentration using established regression equations. 38 , 39 The carotenoid concentration was determined spectrophotometrically, as described by Lichtenthaler and Wellburn. 40 The photosystem II (Fv/Fm) quantum yield was measured at the end of dark adaptation after 30 min of darkness using a portable chlorophyll fluorometer. 41 The parameters of gas exchange, including the net photosynthetic rate (A), transpiration rate (E), stomatal conductance (gs), and sub-stomatal CO2 concentration (Ci) under ambient CO2, relative humidity, and photosynthetic photon flux density, were measured between 09:00 and 11:00 AM. The leaves were allowed to acclimatize in the cuvette for over 2 min prior to data logging. 42

Oxidative stress markers and antioxidant enzyme activities

Malondialdehyde (MDA), 43 hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) 44 , and superoxide radical (O2− ) 45 concentrations were quantified using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction method; H2O2 content was quantified via the xylenol orange assay; and superoxide radical (O2− ) production was quantified using the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction method. Superoxide dismutase (SOD; EC 1.15.1.1) activity was measured by observing the inhibition of NBT photoreduction at a wavelength of 560 nm. 46 For peroxidase (POD; EC 1.11.1.7) activity, guaiacol was used as a substrate, 47 catalase (CAT; EC 1.11.1.6) activity was monitored by the breakdown of H2O2 using an H2O2 monitor 48 and ascorbate peroxidase (APX; EC 1.11.1.11) activity was assessed by measuring ascorbate oxidation at 290 nm in a 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) with 0.1 mM H2O2 and 0.5 mM ascorbate. 49 Each of these activities (U mg−1 of soluble protein) is defined as the unit of measurement that corresponds to the amount of enzyme necessary to conduct the reaction converting 1 μmol of substrate per minute when measured under assay testing conditions (e.g., temperature and pH).

Osmoprotectants and non-enzymatic antioxidants

The extracts were shipped in 3% sulfosalicylic acid and measured using the acid ninhydrin reaction method, with absorbance measured at 520 nm. Glycine betaine was quantified by oxidizing the compound with periodic acid in sulfuric acid and reacting the product with chromotropic acid, and the reaction was measured at 570 nm. 50 Minor extracts of ascorbic acid in metaphosphoric acid were quantified using the 2,6-dichloroindophenol titration method. 51 The Folin‒Ciocalteu reagent was used to measure the total phenolic content, 52 and the aluminum chloride colorimetric assay was used to measure the total flavonoid content. 53 Anthocyanins were extracted using methanol, which was acidified and quantified spectrophotometrically. 54

Glutathione homeostasis and redox balance

Cold perchloric acid was used to extract reduced (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG). To determine GSH, the 5,57-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) recycling assay was used to obtain GSH values. 55 To determine GSSG, a derivatization method using 2-vinylpyridine was used to shift GSH. 56 The GSH/GSSG ratio was computed based on the respective concentrations. The proportional distribution of the GSH and GSSG pools determines the glutathione redox state. 57 , 58

Fruits quality and yield attributes

Fruits were harvested when they reached physiological maturity, and assessed for morphological and quality characteristics. Digital calipers were used to measure fruit length and diameter. Fruits per plant were counted, and after drying in an oven, representative samples of the fruits were taken, and the average fresh weight and the average dry weight were taken. The total soluble solids content was determined using a digital refractometer. 59 The titratable acidity (TA) was determined by titrating fruit juice against 0.1 N NaOH to a pH endpoint of 8.1 and expressed as the percentage of citric acid. 60 The pH of the fruits was directly measured using a calibrated pH meter. 61 The reduction method used to quantify vitamin C levels involved the reduction of the 2,6-dichloroindophenol dye. 62 The harvest index was estimated as the ratio of the economic yield (dry weight) to the total above-ground dry biomass. 63

Accumulation and translocation dynamics: tissue-specific

Endogenous pipecolic acid (PipA) was extracted from frozen roots, shoots, leaves, and fruits using an 80% methanolic solution with high-resolution liquid chromatography equipped with tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS). 64 The amount of lead in roots, shoots, leaves, and fruits was determined after digestion with nitric and perchloric acids. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) was used to measure tissue Pb concentrations. 65 The translocation factor (TF) was determined as the ratio of shoot Pb concentration to root Pb concentration. The factors of the bioconcentrations (BCF) of the roots, shoots, leaves, and fruits were calculated as the ratio of the tissue concentration of Pb to the available concentration of Pb in the soil. 66

Macronutrient and micronutrient concentrations

The dried shoot tissues were ground to a fine powder using a mortar, digested in a 3:1 mixture of concentrated HNO3 and HClO4 and digested at 250 °C until clear before being diluted in deionized water and filtered. The contents of nitrogen were calculated by the use of the Kjeldahl distillation. 69 The amount of phosphorus was estimated colorimetrically using the molybdenum blue method. 70 Potassium, calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, manganese, and copper levels were quantified using flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS). 71

Statistical analysis

The data were processed using the Statistix 8.1 software (Analytical Software, Tallahassee, FL, USA) and subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in accordance with the protocol of statistical analysis. 72 Prior to analysis, the data were assessed for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene's test). The number of biological replicates (four) also conformed to the conventional protocols for controlled pot-based physiological trials, providing adequate statistical power for Tukey's honest significant difference (HSD) post hoc separation at p ne 0.05. 73 The interrelationships among the parameters to be measured were identified by computing Pearson correlation coefficients at p ≤ 0.05 and p ≤ 0.01. 74 These were plotted in graphical presentations, bar charts, correlation figure radar charts, and principal component analysis were generated using OriginPro 2021 software (Origin Lab, Northampton, MA, USA). 75 All tabular data were summarized and converted into MS Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA).

Results

Modulation of soil physico-chemical properties and nutrient availability by PipA application under lead (Pb) stress

Oxidative stress markers and activities of antioxidant enzymes

The application of pipecolic acid (PipA) is among those that significantly (p ≤ 0.05) modulated the oxidative stress markers and the activities of antioxidant enzymes in chili plants subjected to Pb stress, as Figure 3 shows. In comparison, exposure to Pb stress alone, rather than in combination with an initial exposure, caused extensive oxidative damage, increasing the malondialdehyde (MDA) content by 237.92%, the hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content by 260.71%, the superoxide radical (O2-) content by 219.50%, and electrolyte leakage (EL) by 279.68%, and at the same time instigates the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) by 84.19%, peroxidase (POD) by 76.44%, catalase (CAT) by 73.82%, and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) by 69.55%. In comparison with Pb stress alone, the application of Pb + seed priming (0.5 mM PipA) significantly alleviated oxidative damage by decreasing the MDA by 24.11%, H₂O₂ by 25.42%, O2− by 24.02%, and EL by 24.27%, while further enhancing SOD by 29.16%, POD by 22.32%, CAT by 18.18%, and APX by 27.81%. The Pb + Soil Drenching (0.5 mM PipA) treatment produced stronger ameliorative effects, reducing MDA by 31.41%, H₂O₂ by 34.11%, O2− by 30.73%, and EL by 31.03%, while elevating SOD by 39.04%, POD by 37.82%, CAT by 30.60%, and APX by 39.33% relative to Pb stress alone. Similarly, Pb (800 mg kg⁻¹) + Foliar Spray (0.5 mM PipA) significantly reduced the MDA by 34.14%, H₂O₂ by 36.07%, O2− by 34.60%, and EL by 36.37%, while further upregulating SOD by 46.72%, POD by 43.93%, CAT by 33.69%, and APX by 49.87% over Pb stress alone. The combined Pb + Seed Priming (0.5 mM) + Soil Drench (0.5 mM PipA) treatment resulted in greater protection, with MDA decreasing by 48.90%, H₂O₂ by 47.87%, O2− by 48.07%, and EL by 47.07%, alongside marked enhancements of SOD by 67.06%, POD by 59.50%, CAT by 53.39%, and APX by 70.96% compared to Pb stress alone. The most pronounced ameliorative effects were observed under Pb + Seed Priming (0.5 mM) + Foliar Spray (0.5 mM PipA), where MDA decreased by 60.54%, H₂O₂ by 63.37%, O2− by 62.24%, and EL by 65.14%, while SOD, POD, CAT, and APX activities were maximally enhanced by 89.07%, 91.22%, 76.47%, and 94.76%, respectively, in comparison to Pb stress alone, demonstrating the highest oxidative mitigation and enzymatic activation among all delivery combinations. The Pb + Soil Drenching (0.5 mM) + Foliar Spray (0.5 mM PipA) treatment also produced strong responses, with MDA decreasing by 56.87%, H₂O₂ by 57.63%, O2− by 57.72%, and EL by 59.49%, while SOD, POD, CAT, and APX activities increased by 83.06%, 76.42%, 70.04%, and 83.38%, respectively, relative to Pb stress alone. Finally, when the most effective treatment, Pb + Seed Priming (0.5 mM) + Foliar Spray (0.5 mM PipA), was compared with the control, MDA, H₂O₂, O2− , and EL still remained elevated by 33.35%, 32.11%, 20.65%, and 32.36%, respectively, whereas SOD, POD, CAT, and APX activities were markedly enhanced by 248.23%, 237.38%, 206.73%, and 230.21%, respectively, indicating that this dual application strategy not only minimized residual oxidative damage but also sustained a strong antioxidant defense capacity well above non-stressed control levels under continued Pb exposure.

The application of pipecolic acid (PipA) significantly (p ≤ 0.05) modulated glutathione homeostasis and redox balance in chili plants subjected to lead stress, as shown in Figure 5. Discussion

The growing crisis of heavy metal contamination of arable land is one of the most critical issues affecting global food security and requires immediate research on sustainable agronomic interventions that can be used to curb the toxicity of high-value vegetable crops. 76 Current research is necessary because of the lack of studies on the effectiveness of the exogenous ameliorative agent pipecolic acid (PipA) against lead-induced toxicity in chili (Capsicum annuum L), which has inestimable economic and nutritional value. Although the multiple signaling contributions of PipA to plant immunity have been thoroughly reported, the possibility of PipA regulating physiological and biochemical responses to heavy metal stress has not been extensively studied. This study demonstrates the innovativeness of using PipA not only as a defense signal but also as a flexible rhizospheric and foliar amendment to reprogram the plant stress‒response machinery. This research indicates that PipA application is a powerful phytoremediation and crop protection tool with a multifaceted strategy that increases tolerance through a combination of nutrient uptake, antioxidant efficacy, and translocation of heavy metals to edible tissues.

Physico-chemical analysis of the rhizosphere showed that lead contamination seriously affects soil health by immobilizing nutrients that are required and increasing the availability of metals. The addition of lead had a significant effect on the availability of lead in total and available amounts and simultaneously affected the availability of phosphorus, potassium, calcium, and magnesium. Deficiencies in these nutrients occur because lead adsorption onto cation exchange sites displaces essential divalent cations and forms insoluble complexes with phosphate ions. 77-79 However, by applying PipA, particularly when combined with other treatments, such as seed priming and foliar spray, such deleterious effects can be reversed. PipA treatments significantly improved the availability and bioavailability of phosphorus, potassium, calcium, and magnesium without affecting the availability of lead in the soil. This was stimulated by the capacity of PipA to regulate rhizospheric chemistry through the exudation of organic acids that chelate lead and by increasing microbial activity, which solubilizes phosphate and enhances cation exchange capacity. 30 , 80 , 81 The reported increase in the level of organic matter and decrease in the level of electrical conductivity under PipA treatments also supported the restoration of a favorable soil environment, which would support root proliferation and nutrient uptake.

Morphological analyses showed that the stress caused by lead had a severe cost on the vegetative growth and build-up of the dry weight of chili plants, as there was a marked reduction in root and shoot length and biomass accumulation in chili plants. This inhibitory action was ascribed to the inhibition of cell division and elongation processes, which are extremely sensitive to interference by heavy metals. 9 , 82 These growth inhibitions were strongly alleviated by the application of PipA, with the most significant recovery being with the combination of seed priming and foliar spray treatments. This reinstatement of growth parameters was mediated by the fact that it was accompanied by better nutrient uptake and maintenance of turgor pressure in the cells, which is critical for expansion growth. 83 Moreover, the increase in biomass indicated that the application of PipA was effective in overcoming the metabolic costs of stress responses that were redirected towards structural development and not defense mechanisms alone. 11 The positive relationship between the accumulation of biomass and nutrient availability in the principal component analysis also supported the hypothesis that PipA promoted a more efficient strategy to utilize available resources in the face of toxic conditions.

Physiological studies have reported significant adverse effects of Pb on water relations and photosynthetic efficiency, as indicated by a significant decline in the relative water content, stability index of membranes, and gas exchange parameters. Pb results in stomatal closure, contaminates the photosynthetic apparatus, and induces a decreased net photosynthetic rate, transpiration, and stomatal conductance. The reduction in SPAD values and chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv/Fm) was also contributed to by disturbances of the chlorophyll biosynthesis pathway and the degradation of thylakoid membranes by reactive oxygen species (ROS). 84 , 85 However, PipA treatment improved these physiological parameters and demonstrated robust recovery of the photosynthetic machinery. The improvement in relative water content and membrane stability provided by PipA demonstrates that PipA is a stimulator of osmotic adjustment and stabilization of membrane lipids and prevents leakage and cell deterioration. This stabilization of membrane fluidity and stomatal activity allowed further carbon assimilation, which was facilitated by the high levels of sub-stomatal CO2− concentration and photosynthetic rate recorded in PipA-treated plants. 27

Biochemical biosensing analyses confirmed that Pb stress induced an oxidative burst, which triggered the release of large amounts of hydrogen peroxide, superoxide radicals, and malondialdehyde, and massive increases in electrolyte leakage. This oxidative damage was a direct result of Fenton-like reactions mediated by Pb ions, which generated hydroxyl radicals that were able to peroxidize lipids and denature proteins. 86 , 87 PipA application greatly reduced these oxidative indicators and suggested that it is a strong suppressor of ROS production. The possible breakdown of the defense system was curbed by the activation of the enzymatic antioxidant defense system. The superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and peroxidase (POD) activities were highly enhanced following PipA treatment. The concerted action of SOD, which converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide, and CAT/APX, which detoxifies hydrogen peroxide to water, is important in the elimination of excess ROS. 88 , 89 The strong positive correlation between the activities of antioxidant enzymes and decreased lipid peroxidation in the heatmap supported the conclusion that PipA activated the antioxidant defense network of the plant, providing a shield against oxidative stress caused by Pb.

In addition to the enzymatic defense system, non-enzymatic antioxidants and osmoprotectants are crucial for stress tolerance. Stress caused by lead results in the accumulation of proline and glycine betaine, which is a typical response to stress to maintain osmotic equilibrium and free radical scavenging. It is also proposed that the levels of biogenic osmolytes, along with phenolic compounds and flavonoids, are further enhanced through the application of PipA, which is another factor supporting this hypothesis. Proline is probably a hydroxyl radical scavenger and protein structure stabilizer, whereas glycine betaine plays a role in the stabilization of thylakoid membranes and enzyme complexes. 90 , 91 Elevated levels of ascorbic acid under PipA treatment and restoration of the glutathione pool (GSH/GSSG ratio) are of particular interest. The ascorbate–glutathione cycle is the central process in detoxifying against oxidized damage to the body, specifically, oxidized glutathione (GSH) levels in the cell. 92 , 93

In addition to chlorophyll degradation, lead likely affected nutrient homeostasis, resulting in a reduction in nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, manganese, and copper. The lead (probably) has acted by interfering with the nutrient transporters and competing with the nutrient transporters and the mentioned nutrient transporters. PipA treatment greatly increased the accumulation of these essential nutrients in the shoots and rejuvenated them to a high levels, similar to those in the control. This recovery was mediated by enhanced root architecture and the induction of nutrient transport machinery by PipA signaling, which facilitated this recovery. In particular, the recovery of iron and zinc levels was vital for the operation of several different metalloenzymes engaged in photosynthesis and respiration. 94 , 95 This was highly observed following principal component analysis, which effectively segregated nutrient-rich treatments and stressed native nutrient-deficient plants.

The present study discusses the major problems associated with heavy metal translocation and bioaccumulation in the edible parts of plants. Lead stress resulted in a notable decrease in lead concentration in the roots, shoots, leaves, and fruits, with high bioconcentration factors. Plants are trying to immobilize lead in the roots associated with plants, but instead, a vast quantity is transported in the aerial parts that can pose a drastic food safety hazard. The use of PipA significantly reduced the lead concentration in the shoots, leaves, and fruit and reduced the translocation factor (TF) and bioconcentration factor (BCF). This type of phytostabilization suggests that PipA contributed to the chelation of lead in the rhizosphere and root cell wall, thus preventing its radial transport into the xylem and subsequently into the shoots. Similar mechanisms have been suggested to explain other organic amendments, in which the complexation of heavy metals decreases their mobility and bioavailability. 96 , 97 A notable finding was the decrease in lead accretion in fruits, which implies that the application of PipA would be a feasible measure for producing safe food crops in soils contaminated with lead.

The parameters of yield and fruit quality reflected the physiological and biochemical responses and showed a sudden decrease during lead stress and a significant increase with PipA treatment. Lead poisoning reduces fruit length, diameter, number of fruits and total soluble solids (TSS) and impairs quality attributes, including vitamin C levels and TSS. 98 , 99 The use of PipA significantly enhanced these yielding factors and recovered fruit quality. This increase in vitamin C and TSS contents was probably attributed to the increase in the photosynthetic rate and accessibility of carbohydrates, which acted as intermediates in the synthesis of these metabolites. 100 , 101 Support of fruit pH and decrease in titratable acidity showed that normal metabolic progression occurred during fruit ripening, but was interfered with by lead stress. 102 , 103 The radar chart visualization confirmed the overall effectiveness of the treatment to restore the productivity and quality of the treatment in a holistic manner by occupying the morphological and biochemical space occupied by the healthy control plants compared to the stressed plants.

Statistical validation of the physiological processes was fully conducted using correlation analysis, multivariate correlation analysis, and PCA biplots. The fact that the Pb accumulation/oxidative stress marker and growth/yield parameter recorded new prediction values with a high negative coefficient confirmed the hypothesis that Pb accumulation was the main cause of yield loss. The positive associations among the PipA treatment, antioxidant activity, nutrient uptake, and yield characteristics explained the interrelatedness of the ameliorative response. The fact that the treatments were grouped together around the control and combined with the PipA treatments compared to the lead-stressed group of samples strongly supports the conclusion that dual application strategies (such as seed priming followed by foliar spray) offered the best protection. These statistical techniques not only provide a summary of complex data, but also provide eloquent proof of how the systemic reprogramming of chili plants in response to PipA would enable them to survive in lead-contaminated environments.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that exogenous pipecolic acid significantly ameliorates lead-induced physiological disruption, oxidative damage, and nutrient imbalance in chili plants. The synergistic application of seed priming and foliar spray at 0.5 mM PipA proved most effective, enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, stabilizing membrane integrity, improving photosynthetic efficiency, and substantially reducing Pb translocation to edible tissues. These outcomes indicate that PipA functions as a multi-target biostimulant capable of reprogramming physiological homeostasis under heavy metal stress. Although pot-level findings warrant validation under field conditions and factorial dose‒response frameworks, the integrated use of PipA presents a scalable and environmentally compatible strategy for safeguarding crop productivity and food safety in Pb-contaminated agroecosystems.
