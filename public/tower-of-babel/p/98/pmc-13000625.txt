Brand Manufacturer Coupons and Pharmaceutical Product Hopping
Authors: Jason B. Gibbons, Michael DiStefano, Gerard F. Anderson, Aaron S. Kesselheim, Mariana Socal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionAre manufacturer copay coupon programs associated with product hopping among biologic drugs nearing exclusivity loss?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 382 550 prescription claims for 47 biologic products, coupon use for biologic line extensions diverged significantly from biologics with earlier approval as originators approached 12 years on the market. Among products with older originators, biologic line extensions had more generous coupon programs: coupons offset more of patient cost-sharing,− and postbuydown costs were lower than biologics with earlier approval.MeaningThese findings suggest that manufacturer coupons were associated with product hopping during periods of expected biosimilar entry, suggesting that monitoring coupon activity may help identify potential anticompetitive behavior.

Introduction

Drug coupon programs are a common form of patient assistance offered by brand manufacturers that reduce patients’ out-of-pocket (OOP) costs at the point of sale.1 Coupons are distributed through pharmacies, prescriber offices, and manufacturer websites and are generally available to any commercially insured patient.1 When patients present a coupon at the pharmacy, the manufacturer reimburses the pharmacy for the discount. While prescribers select the drug and formulation, patients and pharmacists typically initiate coupon use.1 Importantly, coupons lower patient cost-sharing without reducing list prices, meaning insurers continue to pay the full negotiated amount when a coupon is used.

While manufacturers argue that coupons improve access for price-sensitive patients and promote adherence, the evidence is mixed.2,3 Some studies find little to no sustained reduction in patient spending, with others reporting higher long-term OOP costs.2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9 Separate studies of coupon availability have found that coupons are used overwhelmingly for drugs in competitive therapeutic classes relative to classes with only a single drug.3 Coupons have also been associated with the use of brand name drugs in place of lower-cost generic alternatives.4,5,6,7,8,9 Because coupons can steer patient and prescriber choices, some public and state payers have restricted their use.10 Medicare and Medicaid prohibit coupons, citing federal antikickback statutes,11 while Massachusetts and California limit them to drugs without generic equivalents.12

A largely unexplored question is whether manufacturers deploy coupons strategically to facilitate pharmaceutical product hopping, ie, the practice of shifting patients from an older drug to a reformulated version that retains exclusivity. Product hopping typically occurs as an originator nears loss of exclusivity, with manufacturers promoting newer line extensions through lower prices, marketing, or withdrawal of the original formulation.13,14,15,16,17,18 Product hopping is associated with substantial costs on patients and payers through delayed access to lower-cost alternatives19,20,21,22,23,24 and is estimated to cost the US health system more than $5 billion annually13,14,15,16. In this study, we examine whether coupon use and generosity increase on new biologic line extensions during the period in which biologics are first at risk of losing exclusivity by using generalized linear probability models and a large dataset of prescription drug claims.

Methods

This cross-sectional study received an exempt determination with a waiver of informed consent from the Johns Hopkins institutional review board because it used deidentified claims data. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cross-sectional studies.

Data Sources

This study focuses on biologic drugs, which provide an important context for examining strategic couponing by firms. Although biologics represent only approximately 2% of all prescriptions, they account for approximately 40% of US drug spending.25,26,27 Their high prices also make coupon use particularly salient, as do their profitability to manufacturers.27 As biosimilar competition expands, biologic manufacturers will likely face growing incentives to preserve market share through tactics such as product hopping.

We identified biologic drugs of interest using the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Purple Book, which links products to their originators and lists approval dates for biologics and biosimilars.28 These data were supplemented with Drugs@FDA29 for approval information, FDA’s drug label database for therapeutic indications,30 and the National Drug Code directory to link products to pharmacy claims.31

Prescription data came from the IQVIA Formulary Impact Analyzer, which includes anonymized, all-payer pharmacy claims from approximately 55 000 pharmacies between October 1, 2017, and September 30, 2019.32 Each claim indicates whether a manufacturer coupon was used, the coupon’s buy-down amount, patient OOP costs before and after the coupon, and total claim cost, consistent with prior coupon studies.33

Study Sample

The study focused on 2 groups of biologic drugs: candidate product hops (hereafter, candidate hops), which are line extensions approved between October 2017 and September 2019, and comparator biologics, which include the originator and any line extensions approved before October 2017. For example, in the adalimumab product line, the originator is the original Humira formulation (40 mg/0.8 mL injection) approved in 2002. Comparator products include this originator plus subsequent Humira formulations approved before October 2017, such as the 40 mg/0.4 mL pen device. Candidate hops include newer formulations approved during our study window, such as the citrate-free 40 mg/0.4 mL formulation, which was approved in 2018. This structure allows us to compare coupon patterns between newer line extensions (candidate hops) that may be used for product hopping and established products (comparators) within the same therapeutic product line. We classified candidate hops as products approved under FDA as submission types 2 (new dosage), 3 (new form), or 5 (new active ingredient), following Gowda et al.34 Type 4 (combination) products were excluded because originators could not be clearly identified. Comparator biologics were identified through the FDA Purple Book as products from the same manufacturer with the same active moiety but approved before October 2017. This process yielded an initial set of 22 candidate hops and 39 comparator products across 15 product lines. However, we excluded product lines if multiple products had only 1 claim in either the candidate or the comparator category (nivolumab, rituximab), as these lacked sufficient data to reliably estimate coupon patterns. Additionally, we excluded product lines whose comparator formulations are primarily administered intravenously and accessed through the medical benefit rather than the pharmacy benefit (abatacept, ranibizumab, tocilizumab), as manufacturer coupons cannot be readily applied in clinician-administered settings and coupon-driven product hopping is implausible for these products. Following these exclusions, the final sample included 17 candidate hops and 30 comparator products across 10 product lines (eTable in Supplement 1). Following product sample identification, claims for each drug were drawn from the IQVIA data using National Drug Codes. We excluded all claims for patients with Medicare or Medicaid coverage or for prescriptions dispensed in Massachusetts or California, as these states and programs restricted coupon use during the study period.

Outcomes

The first and primary outcome, manufacturer coupon use, was a binary indicator equal to 1 for a prescription drug claim that used a manufacturer coupon. For claims flagged as having a manufacturer coupon applied, we identified our second outcome, the coupon buy-down amount, which is the dollar amount provided by the coupon that counted toward the patient’s OOP cost. Our third outcome was the proportion of the coupon discount relative to the claim cost (ie, the buy-down amount divided by the total claim cost, which combines patient prebuydown OOP cost and payer payments). Our final outcome was the postcoupon (referred to as postbuydown) patient OOP spending, calculated as the difference between the patient’s prebuydown OOP cost and the buydown amount for claims indicating coupon use.

Exposures

The primary exposure was the interaction between an indicator variable specifying whether the drug was a candidate hop or a comparator product and an indicator for whether the product’s originator had been approved 12 or more years before the claim. We dichotomize time since approval to before and after 12 years, given that the earliest FDA approval time of biosimilars is 12 years following entry.23

Covariates

We included several controls to adjust for possible confounding. The first set of control variables included the change in the type of candidate product hops compared to the earliest-approved drug (ie, the originator product). Possible change types include changes in dosage strength (eg, 10 mg to 15 mg), changes in form (eg, modifications in the physical characteristics of the package or labeling), and changes in route of administration. The reference group for the change type covariates was the categorized originator products, which are considered no change by default. We also controlled for the prebuydown patient OOP cost and payer costs standardized per 30-day supply, patient sex assigned at birth, patient age at the time of the claim fill, and the claim fill year.

Statistical Analysis

We estimated linear probability models with product line random effects and product line–clustered standard errors to examine associations between candidate hop status, time since originator approval, and each of our 4 outcomes. The patient claim served as the unit of analysis, enabling observation of coupon use and spending patterns across product line lifecycles. Product line random effects account for factors at the product line level that may shape coupon program design, such as pricing strategy and promotional investment.

The primary exposures were candidate hop status (candidate hop vs comparator product) and time since originator approval (≥12 years vs <12 years). The interaction between these exposures captures whether coupon use for candidate hops increased differentially once originators reached biosimilar eligibility, relative to the change observed for comparator products at the same threshold. A positive interaction coefficient would indicate that manufacturers increased coupon support for candidate hops relative to comparators as products neared the end of market exclusivity.

Models adjusted for formulation change type (strength, form, or route of administration, with originator as reference), prebuydown patient OOP and payer costs per 30-day supply (excluding the model using percentage of patient OOP cost represented by the coupon as an outcome, given that patient OOP prebuydown cost is part of the denominator), patient age and sex, and calendar year–fixed effects to account for secular trends in coupon use. The proportion of OOP cost covered by coupons excluded prebuydown patient OOP cost as a control, since it is part of the outcomes denominator and would introduce divisibility bias. All hypothesis tests were 2-sided with a significance threshold of P < .05. Differences in means between candidate hops and comparator products were assessed using 2-sample t tests, and differences in proportions were assessed using 2-sample proportion tests. Stratum-specific outcomes were estimated using linear combinations of regression coefficients (lincom command in Stata software version 18 [StataCorp]), with 95% CIs that accounted for covariance between the main outcome and interaction term. Full model specification is provided in the eAppendix in Supplement 1. Analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and Stata between February and September 2025.

Results

The final analytic sample included 43 088 commercially insured patients (22 113 [51.3%] female; mean [SD] age, 47.6 [15.2] years), with 382 550 prescription claims across 47 biologic products. To arrive at this sample from an initial 509 497 prescription claims for 55 764 patients, we excluded 123 264 claims (24.2%) and 12 280 patients with Medicare or Medicaid coverage or prescriptions dispensed in Massachusetts or California, which restricted the use of coupons. We then excluded 3683 claims and 396 patients who were prescribed products with insufficient claims and formulations accessed primarily through the medical benefit. The Figure tracks the percentage of total claims with manufacturer coupons applied from 2017 to 2019, separately for candidate hops and comparator products. At the start of the study period, comparator products had a higher share of couponed claims than candidate hops. However, coupon use for comparator products declined, while coupon use for candidate hops increased sharply. By the end of the period, patients prescribed candidate hop products were approximately 4 times as likely to use a coupon (20% vs 5%). A numerically smaller share of candidate hops (41.2%) than comparator products (66.7%) had originators approved more than 12 years before the end of the study period, although this difference was not statistically significant (P = .09) (Table 1). Candidate hops were prescribed to 2573 patients across 11 122 claims, compared with 40 515 patients and 371 428 claims for prescribed comparator products. Candidate hops had fewer patients per product (mean [SD] 250.7 [270.5] patients vs 4384.7 [4865.3] patients; P = .01) and claims per product (mean [SD], 1011.1 [1145.1] claims vs 26 530.6 [28 361.1] claims, P = .007) than comparator products. Patients filling candidate hop prescriptions were more likely to be female than those filling comparator prescriptions (57.7% vs 51.0%; P < .001), while age did not differ significantly (unadjusted mean [SD] age, 44.5 [13.1 years vs 47.8 [15.3] years; P = .77) (Table 1).

Costs were consistently higher for candidate hops. The mean (SD) total claim cost per 30-day supply was $1586.10 ($2711.49) compared with $1186.36 ($1756.02) for comparator products (P < .001). Payer costs were likewise higher (mean [SD] cost, $1372.38 [$2810.65] vs $1082.38 [$1749.03]; P < .001). Before buydowns, mean (SD) patient OOP spending was $213.43 ($197.46) for candidate hops and $108.15 ($502.22) for comparator products (P < .001), while postbuydown OOP costs remained higher for candidate hops (mean [SD] cost, $151.97 [$830.47] vs $77.26 [$458.91]; P < .001). Among couponed claims, buydown amounts were also higher for candidate hops (mean [SD] amount, $200.01 [$407.22] vs $161.83 [$322.49]; P < .001). Finally, candidate hops were more likely than comparator products to involve form changes (52.9% vs 23.3%; P = .04), while differences in strength changes were not statistically significant (29.4% vs 20.0%; P = .46). Route-of-administration changes occurred only among candidate hops (17.6% vs 0%).

Because the analysis was conducted at the claim level, we constructed claim-weighted values for variables included in our regression models (excluding spending amounts, which are naturally claim-weighted). When weighted by claims proportionally, a mean (SD) of 0.65 (0.48) of candidate hop claims originated from products whose originators were approved 12 or more years earlier, compared with 0.82 (0.38) of comparator claims (P < .001). Table 2 presents regression results showing how time since originator approval interacted with candidate hop status to estimate coupon use. The intercept indicated a baseline estimated probability of coupon use of 9.5% (95% CI, 2.6% to 16.4%) for comparator products whose originators were approved fewer than 12 years prior (P = .007), holding all other covariates at zero. Neither the main outcome of candidate hop status nor 12 or more years since originator approval was statistically significant. However, the interaction term was statistically significant at 19.0 (95% CI, 1.7 to 36.4) percentage points (P = .03), indicating that the association between candidate hop status and coupon use differed depending on time since originator approval. Following Knol and VanderWeele,35 we present stratum-specific estimates in Table 3; however, neither stratum-specific estimate reached statistical significance. Table 4 shows the coupon amount, the proportion of OOP spending offset by the coupon, and postbuydown OOP costs among claims with coupons applied. Neither the main outcome of candidate hop status nor the interaction terms reached statistical significance for any secondary outcome, indicating no evidence that coupon generosity changed differentially as originators approached the 12-year threshold.

Despite the lack of significant interactions, stratum-specific estimates suggested that candidate hops and comparators differed significantly in coupon generosity among products whose originators had been approved for at least 12 years (Table 3). In this stratum, candidate hops had $99 (95% CI, $71 to $126) higher coupon amounts (P < .001), coupons covering 38.8 (95% CI, 28.0 to 49.7) percentage points more of patient cost-sharing (P < .001), and $124 (95% CI, −$183 to −$65) lower postbuydown OOP costs (P < .001) compared with comparator products. Estimates to examine whether coupon generosity changed for candidate hops as originators reached the 12-year threshold were not statistically significant (Table 3).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study found that among products whose originators were approved for at least 12 years, candidate hops had substantially more generous coupon programs than comparators. However, we found no associated difference in coupon generosity for candidate hops before and after their originators crossed the 12-year threshold. Together, these findings suggest that candidate hops were designed with inherently generous coupon programs from launch, and manufacturers strategically deployed these coupons more frequently as biosimilar competition approached. This pattern of building in coupon generosity upfront and timing more intense deployment to coincide with impending exclusivity loss may help obtain and retain patients on reformulated products with extended market protection, potentially dampening biosimilar uptake and raising antitrust concerns. Regulators might address the potential for product hopping by restricting biologic manufacturers from deploying new coupon programs during periods surrounding exclusivity loss. This might include preemptive restrictions starting 1 to 2 years before and after presumptive exclusivity loss. Regulators could also directly monitor coupon programs and the intensity of coupon use as a potential early-warning signal for product hopping. Incorporating coupon use data into previously proposed product hop alert systems could make these tools more sensitive than approaches relying solely on patent and approval timelines.34

From a legal perspective, coupons for candidate hop tied to older originators covered nearly 40 percentage points more of patient cost-sharing than comparators, a substantial targeted subsidy that could be considered an exclusionary practice under Sherman Act §2. This logic resembles the holdings in LePage’s Inc. v 3M36 and SmithKline Corp. v Eli Lilly & Co.,37 in which loyalty rebates conditioned on volume across product lines were held to potentially foreclose competition. Courts should consider coupon magnitude and timing as indicators of anticompetitive intent.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Only pharmacy claims were examined, which limits the generalizability of results to biologics administered exclusively in a medical setting, where biologics are more commonly delivered. Similarly, the approach to identifying candidate hops did not consider combination products. The results are also not necessarily generalizable to small-molecule drugs, due to structural differences between markets and the nature of generic competition. There are also several limitations relating to internal validity. First, we used a manual approach to identify drugs within the same product line, as the FDA does not routinely identify the originator product for each drug. Defining an originator product as the first approved product for an active ingredient by the same manufacturer may not have accurately identified all relevant products. Second, the cost information in the pharmaceutical claims examined in this study does not account for rebates that drug manufacturers offer to payers to reduce the cost of the drug. Rebates are confidential transactions that occur after the point of drug dispensing, and they can reduce the spending by insurers but not by patients. Thus, the estimates of payer costs for a 30-day supply may be overstated. Third, candidate hops accounted for only 2.9% of claims in our sample, reflecting their recent approval and limited uptake during the study period. This imbalance may limit the statistical precision of some estimates.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study, we found an increase in drug manufacturer coupon use as the originator approached 12 years since approval, relative to comparators with similarly aged originators. Among products with older originators, candidate hops had more generous coupon programs than comparators, covering nearly 40 percentage points more of patient cost-sharing. Regulators and legislators should consider monitoring or restricting coupon programs during biologic exclusivity transitions to address potential financial and competitive harms.
