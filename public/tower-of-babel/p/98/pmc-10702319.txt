Selenium Nanoparticles Stabilized by β-Glucan Nanotubes from Black Fungus and Their Effects on the Proliferation, Apoptosis, and Cell Cycle of HepG2 Cells
Authors: Zihan He, Haoying Song, Jingjing Cheng, Wenxiu Qiu, Junjie Hu, Zhenpeng Qiu, Qi Wang, Cong Chang, Guohua Zheng, Yan Meng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Selenium nanoparticles (Se NPs) have significant anticancer effects, but their poor water solubility and dispersibility limit their further applications in biomedical fields. Biomacromolecules have often been used as dispersants or stabilizers in synthesized Se NPs because they can enhance the dispersibility of Se NPs and reduce their side effects. Our previous studies reported a triple-helix β-glucan (BFP) from the fruiting bodies of black fungus, which showed a good self-assembly ability in constructing hollow nanotubes for loading metal nanoparticles. Therefore, in the present work, BFP nanotubes were designed as carriers to entrap large amounts of Se NPs in order to enhance their stability and anticancer effects. The results showed that Se NPs were successfully synthesized and loaded inside the BFP nanotubes, and the composite (BFP-Se) exhibited high stability and dispersibility due to the covalent Se–O bonds between the Se NPs and the hydroxyl groups on the BFP nanotubes. Moreover, BFP-Se showed significant effects on the proliferation, apoptosis, and cell cycle of HepG2 cells compared to those exhibited by Se NPs. The mechanism was associated with BFP, which acted as a dispersant or stabilizer, resulting in the enhanced cellular uptake of the Se NPs. BFP also activated the death receptor-mediated and mitochondria-mediated apoptotic pathways in HepG2 cells. These results suggest that BFP-Se has potential applications in biomedical fields, especially for the treatment of human liver cancers.

Introduction

Nanoparticle (NP)-based drug delivery systems have received much attention in recent years, especially for cancer treatments.1 NPs have the potential to improve the stability and solubility of encapsulated cargos, promote transportation across membranes, and prolong circulation times to increase safety and efficacy.2,3 Generally, NPs can be categorized as either organic or inorganic nanomaterials.3−6 Recently, inorganic nanoparticles such as metal NPs, carbonaceous NPs, magnetic NPs, and quantum dots have been intensely investigated for diagnostic purposes in oncology, especially in the improvement of computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques. Moreover, inorganic NPs have been found to exhibit potential bioactive advantages over traditional anticancer drugs. For example, Lu et al. reported that gold NPs exhibited apparent toxicity toward H22 and HepG2 cells by disrupting normal proteins in the absence of radiation.7 It has also been confirmed that silver NPs inhibit liver cancer cells in a dose- and incubation-time-dependent manner by inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and cell apoptosis in HepG2 cell lines.7−9

Selenium nanoparticles (Se NPs) are a new source of selenium that provides better bioavailability than organic selenium and reduces the risk of selenium toxicity caused by inorganic selenium. It has been reported that the bioactivity and degradability of Se NPs in vivo are significantly better than other metal NPs such as silver, gold, and platinum NPs.10 For example, an in vitro cytotoxic study showed that Se NPs were excellent in inhibiting HepG2 cell growth, which may be attributed to the excessive generation of ROS.10 However, Se NPs tend to aggregate into huge sizes without dispersants, which can result in decreased stability, bioactivity, and bioavailability. Therefore, surfactants or dispersants are usually added during the preparation process of Se NPs to make them more dispersed in aqueous solutions.11 Common surfactants that have been reportedly used with Se NPs are usually chemical molecules or synthesized polymers, leading to higher toxicity, complex preparation processes, and difficulty in clinical translation.

Natural polysaccharides are sustainable polymers with abundant resources, and they are famous for their bioactivity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability.12,13 Several polysaccharides have been developed as adjuvants to treat cancers in clinical settings, such as lentinan,14 schizophyllan,15,16 and astragalus polysaccharide.17 Moreover, polysaccharides have abundant hydroxyl groups, which can react with the surface of Se NPs via hydrogen bonds, resulting in dispersible and stable Se NPs. Therefore, it is not hard to imagine that polysaccharide-based Se NPs can enhance the bioactivity of Se NPs and reduce their aggregation-induced side effects. For instance, Gao et al. reported that Polyporus umbellatus polysaccharide-stabilized Se NPs (PUP-Se NPs) can significantly inhibit the cell viabilities of cancer cells, such as MCF-7, HepG2, and HeLa cells.18 Cui et al. also reported that laminarin polysaccharide-decorated selenium nanoparticles (LP-Se NPs) could exert cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells by inhibiting autophagy and inducing apoptosis.14 Lentinan-complexed Se NPs (LNT-Se) have also been reported to target the mitochondria of tumor cells via the TLR4/TRAF3/MFN1 pathway, to induce cell apoptosis, and to inhibit inflammatory cytokine secretion in ascites.19

Black fungus, a homologous fungus used for medicinal and food, has a long history in China. Polysaccharides from black fungus have numerous biological activities, such as antioxidant activity, immune regulatory activity, antitumor activity, etc.20,21 Our previous study first reported a triple-helix β-1, 3-d-glucan (coded as BFP) from the fruiting bodies of black fungus. BFP could self-assemble into dendritic nanotubes in water, which have been used to load different kinds of nanoparticles, including gold and silver NPs, aggregation-induced emission (AIE) dyes, doxorubicin (DOX), etc.22−24 Additionally, BFP showed promising anti-hepatocellular carcinoma activity with no toxic side effects.24 Thus, BFP is a potential carrier for Se NPs due to its self-assembly behaviors and biological activities. Therefore, in this study, BFP nanotubes were loaded with Se NPs, and the formation mechanism of the composite (BFP-Se) was explored. Moreover, the effects of BFP-Se on the cell proliferation, apoptosis, and cell cycle patterns in HepG2 cells were thoroughly investigated. This study has critical applications for the further development and utilization of BFP-Se and the functional study of polysaccharides in black fungus.

Results and Discussion

Preparation and Evaluation of BFP-Loaded Se NPs (BFP-Se)

In the present work, cotton-like BFP was extracted from the fruiting bodies of black fungus (Figure 1a,b). The total sugar content of BFP was 94.10% measured by using the phenol-sulfuric acid method (Figure S1), which indicated the purity of BFP. The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum of BFP is shown in Figure 3e, and the 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of BFP are shown in Figures S2 and S3, respectively, as well as in Table S1; these results match well with our previous report.23 The chemical structure of BFP is displayed in Figure 1c. In our previous study, we confirmed the existence of hydrophobic cavities in the dendritic nanotubes formed by BFP with the help of an AIE dye.24 BFP nanotubes have already been used to load silver NPs, gold NPs, AIE dyes, and DOX drugs. In the present work, BFP still had the form of obvious nanotubes in water (Figure 1d), and the surface of the BFP nanotubes was smooth under SEM observation after freeze-drying (Figure 1e); the average diameter of the nanotubes was about 128 nm (Figure 1f).

Photographs of (a) the fruiting bodies of black fungus and (b) the cotton-like BFP samples were captured in the laboratory by author Z.H. (c) The chemical structure of BFP. (d) TEM and (e) SEM images of BFP, and (f) the corresponding diameter distribution.

The BFP nanotubes were used to prepare Se NPs, and the related results are displayed in Figure 2. It is obvious that the Se NPs without a dispersant tended to aggregate into larger particles with sizes >200 nm (Figure 2a). However, the Se NPs in the BFP-Se samples were uniformly dispersed inside the hollow nanotubes, having a small particle size of about 75 nm (Figure 2b,c). Next, the size of BFP-Se was analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS). Figure S4 shows the size distribution of BFP-Se. The Se NPs were mainly embedded in the trunk of the BFP hollow nanofibers, which had an average diameter of approximately 200 nm. The particle size and zeta potential of BFP-Se under different conditions are explored later on in this section. The results of a related elemental composition analysis (via EDS, energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy) indicated the presence of Se, further confirming the existence of the Se NPs in the BFP nanotubes (Figure 2d). An SEM image and the corresponding mapping images (Figure 2e) of BFP-Se also showed that the Se element was uniformly distributed in the BFP nanotubes. Moreover, it was observed that the surface of the BFP nanotubes was smooth, confirming that the Se NPs were dispersed inside well.

TEM images of (a) the Se NPs and (b) BFP-Se. The corresponding (c) size distribution and (d) EDS spectrum of the Se NPs in BFP-Se. (e) An SEM image and the corresponding elemental distribution mapping images of BFP-Se.

To further confirm the formation mechanism of BFP-Se, the interaction between the BFP nanotubes and the Se NPs was explored. As shown in Figure 3a,b, the XPS spectra of BFP-Se exhibited the full range of the Se element, with clear Se 3d peaks visible upon magnification. The binding energy of the O 1s peak of BFP-Se was 532.8 eV, and compared to that of BFP (532.5 eV), this indicates that Se–O bonds were generated between the Se NPs and the −OH groups on the sugar chains of BFP (Figure 3c). The electrons on the oxygen atom of BFP were transferred to Se to form C–O–Se for stabilizing and dispersing the Se NPs. Interestingly, the formation of BFP-Se resulted in an increase in the C 1s energy spectrum from 284.1 to 284.7 eV. This may be attributed to the stronger interaction between the O atom linked to C, leading to a change in the C 1s binding energy (Figure 3d). Moreover, as shown in Figure 3e, the FT-IR spectra of BFP and BFP-Se were similar, indicating that the skeletal structure of BFP remains unaltered. The characteristic peaks at 3345 and 1064 cm–1 in the FT-IR spectra correspond to the stretching vibration of −OH groups and the asymmetric stretching vibration of C–O, respectively. The shift of −OH and C–O of BFP-Se to lower wavenumbers suggested that the interaction of the BFP nanotubes with the Se NPs affected the C–O bonds of BFP and decreased the free −OH groups of BFP. To further confirm whether the Se NPs were dispersed inside the BFP nanotubes, X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to evaluate the crystallization behavior of BFP-Se. The obtained XRD results showed two sharp, high diffraction intensity crystallographic peaks for the Se NPs, corresponding to the (100) and (101) crystallographic planes of the Se NPs. In comparison, these peaks were not observed in the XRD results of BFP-Se (Figure 3f); this suggests that the Se NPs may have been completely immobilized inside the BFP nanotubes, thus leading to the absence of these typical Se NP peaks.

(a) The fully scanned X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra and the (b) Se 3d, (c) O 1s, and (d) C 1s XPS spectra of BFP and BFP-Se powders. (e) The FT-IR spectra of BFP and BFP-Se powders and (f) the XRD spectra of BFP, BFP-Se, and Se NP powders.

To further validate the stability of BFP-Se, the particle size and zeta potential of the sample under different conditions were analyzed. As displayed in Figure 4b, the size of BFP-Se was around 200 nm, and the zeta potential was around −10 mV with no obvious changes even after being stored for 20 days at low temperatures. Moreover, the size and zeta potential of BFP-Se were maintained in serum solution, indicating that the prepared BFP-Se would be stable in blood circulation (Figure 4c,d). The stability of BFP-Se in buffers with different pH values (5.0–7.4) was also explored (Figure 4e,f), and the results showed that the size was kept stable, while there were slight fluctuations in the zeta potential of BFP-Se.

Stability of the BFP-Se aqueous solution (a, b) during low-temperature storage, (c, d) in serum after different times, and (e, f) in buffers with different pH values.

Interaction between BFP-Se and HepG2 Cells

To investigate the inhibitory effects of BFP-Se on HepG2 cells, the in vitro cytotoxicity was determined by the MTT (methyl thiazolyl tetrazolium) assay. As shown in Figure 5, BFP had no significant inhibition activity on the HepG2 cells, and the survival rate of the HepG2 cells was still higher than 75% even after 72 h at a concentration of 200 μg/mL, indicating BFP was safe with no cytotoxicity. The cytotoxicity of free Se NPs increased lightly with incubation time and concentration. The results were strange because Se NPs have been shown to have the potential to target specific cancer cells by passive targeting.25 As mentioned above though, in the absence of BFP, Se NPs aggregate into large particles and thus might exhibit poor absorption to cancer cells; this results in diminished cytotoxicity.

Survival rate of HepG2 cells after incubation with (a) BFP, (b) BFP-Se, and (c) Se NPs for 24, 48, and 72 h. (d) The cell cycle analysis of HepG2 cells treated with BFP, Se NPs, and BFP-Se for 48 h (compared with the control, **p < 0.01; n = 3).

In comparison, the survival rate of the cells in the BFP-Se group dramatically decreased with incubation time, even at a lower concentration. Although the effects of BFP and Se NPs alone are relatively weak, the combination of these two bioactive components exhibits significant synergistic effects. This may be related to BFP promoting the absorption of nanoparticles, and it may also be related to the antitumor activity of polysaccharides themselves.26

The main reason for the proliferation inhibition on cancer cells caused by cancer suppressors is the induction of apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, or both biochemical pathways simultaneously.27 It was not hard to imagine that the inhibition of BFP-Se on the proliferation of HepG2 cells may be due to apoptosis or cell cycle arrest. A cell cycle assay was employed to clarify the proliferation inhibition mechanism of BFP-Se. In normal cell cycles, there is a gap between the end of mitosis and the start of DNA replication, and the G1 to S transition is an important point of no return in the cell cycle.28 As shown in Figure 5d, the percentage of the S phase in the BFP-Se group increased from 28.45 to 38.80%, while that of the G1 phase decreased from 37.07 to 27.24% compared to the control group, indicating that cell proliferation inhibition was induced by arresting the cell cycle at the S phase. Moreover, the cell population of BFP-Se in the S phase increased, which was accompanied by a decrease in the G0/G1 phase and no significant change in the G2/M phase; together, this indicates the termination of DNA synthesis. These results suggest that cell cycle arrest might be one of the mechanisms by which BFP-Se inhibits the proliferation of HepG2 cells.

In general, apoptosis plays a crucial role in several biological systems. Apoptosis is also considered a key mechanism for the anticancer activity of Se and Se compounds. The apoptosis of HepG2 cells induced by BFP-Se and the other samples were detected by flow cytometry (Figure 6a). BFP-Se significantly induced apoptosis when compared with the control group, while the Se NPs had almost no effect on inducing apoptosis. These results were consistent with the preliminary cytotoxicity experiments. Certain vital features including cytoplasm shrinkage, nuclear chromatin condensation, membrane dysfunction, and the formation of apoptotic bodies usually characterize apoptosis. As shown in Figure 6b, the nuclei were severely crinkled in the BFP-Se group compared to those in the other groups, as indicated by the bright blue dots, which would trigger more cells to undergo apoptosis. Therefore, the inhibition effects of BFP-Se on HepG2 cells could be attributed to the induction of apoptosis.

Cell Uptake Assay

Cellular uptake is an important factor affecting the antitumor activity of nanomedicines.29 The better synergistic effect on HepG2 cells by BFP-Se might be due to the better cellular uptake of BFP-Se than of Se NPs. The Se NPs had difficulty entering the tumor cells due to their severe aggregation into larger aggregates; these would be washed away during the cell treatment process, thus leading to no obvious toxicity to HepG2 cells. Meanwhile, polysaccharide chain conformations also play crucial roles in enhancing biological activity. Currently, the most accepted hypothesis is that the chain conformation is related to the binding of the polysaccharide chain to the receptor. It has been substantiated that the receptor better recognizes a polysaccharide with a triple helical conformation due to its extended shape resulting from the higher stiffness, which can produce stronger immune reactions and inhibit the proliferation of tumor cells.30 BFP-Se had a smaller particle size, and the outer BFP with β-(1 → 3)-d-glucan in its main chains was more biocompatible to the receptors on the cells; this made it easier for the HepG2 cells to uptake BFP-Se, which inhibited the proliferation of the cancer cells. Therefore, it is significant to study the cellular entry behavior and mechanism of BFP-Se.

To better observe the cell uptake behavior of BFP-Se, BFP was first labeled with coumarin 6 (termed C6-BFP-Se), a marker that can emit green fluorescence. The nuclei and lysosomal sites of the cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 and lysotracker-red, respectively. The endocytosis of C6-BFP-Se during co-incubation with HepG2 cells in 2 h was observed qualitatively using fluorescence inverted microscopy. As shown in Figure 7a, there was noticeable red and green fluorescence in the cells after 1 h of incubation, indicating that C6-BFP-Se had entered the cytoplasm of the cells. As incubation time increased, the fluorescence started to overlap significantly after 1.5 h, showing prominent yellow fluorescence, suggesting that more C6-BFP-Se entered the lysosomes. Flow cytometry was further employed to illustrate the changes in C6-BFP-Se uptake by HepG2 cells in quantitative terms. As shown in Figure 7b, the fluorescence intensity in the cells increased significantly after 1 h of co-incubation, indicating that the entry of C6-BFP-Se into the HepG2 cells was time-dependent; these results are consistent with the aforementioned fluorescence inversion microscopy results.

(a) HepG2 cells were treated with C6-BFP-Se for 2 h and observed under a fluorescence microscope, and (b) the average fluorescence intensity was determined via flow cytometry. The average fluorescence inhibition ratio of HepG2 cells at (c) both 4 and 37 °C and (d) after pretreatment with various endocytosis inhibitors (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01; n = 3).

The uptake mechanism of nanomaterials has essential impacts on bioactivities. Therefore, the cell entry mechanism was examined in terms of energy and endocytosis inhibitors. In general, the energy-dependent behavior was verified by measuring the average fluorescence intensity ratio of the cells after co-incubation at 4 and 37 °C. In the present work, the fluorescence intensity in the cells at 4 °C was half that at 37 °C (Figure 7c), indicating the energy-dependent behavior of C6-BFP-Se entry into the cells. Generally, the endocytic pathways of cells are divided into three main categories: phagocytosis, reticulin-mediated endocytosis, and niche protein-mediated endocytosis. To investigate the impact of endocytosis on the uptake of BFP-Se by HepG2 cells, various inhibitors were used to block the endocytic process prior to incubation with BFP-Se. An analysis of the fluorescence intensity in the cell lysates revealed that the uptake of BFP-Se by HepG2 cells was significantly reduced to 34.87% and 28.47% when pretreated with chlorpromazine and sucrose, respectively (Figure 7d), indicating that C6-BFP-Se may have entered the cells in a reticulin-mediated manner. Meanwhile, the fluorescence intensity of the cells pretreated with goldfinch isoflavone (genistein) and indomethacin was slightly reduced to 55.63% and 53.63% from the control group, respectively, indicating that the presence of a small amount of C6-BFP-Se entered the cells through the fractal protein-mediated way. It is clear that C6-BFP-Se was taken up by the HepG2 cells through both a reticulin-mediated manner and a fractal protein-mediated manner.

The Mechanism of BFP-Se-Induced Apoptosis in HepG2 cells

Numerous studies have consistently demonstrated that the excessive production of ROS can disrupt the redox homeostasis within tumor cells; this disruption subsequently results in oxidative damage to important cellular components such as proteins, lipids, and DNA. Furthermore, it can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and ultimately trigger cell apoptosis or necrosis.31 In this work, the ROS amount in HepG2 cells after co-incubation was qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed by using fluorescence inverted microscopy and flow cytometry. As shown in Figure 8a,b, bright green fluorescence, which represents ROS production, was observed in the BFP-Se group, and the value was the highest compared with other groups, suggesting that the induction of apoptosis in HepG2 cells by BFP-Se might be related to the accumulation of ROS.

(a, b) ROS amount in HepG2 cells after the co-incubation of BFP-Se and the other samples by fluorescence inverted microscopy and flow cytometry. (c, d) The changes in the mitochondrial membrane potential of BFP-Se and the other samples by fluorescence inverted microscopy and flow cytometry. Compared with the control group, **p < 0.01; n = 3.

Moreover, mitochondrion is the primary organelle responsible for the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are closely linked to cell apoptosis. Excessive ROS production can lead to impaired mitochondrial function, including a reduction in the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and the activation of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway.32 By triggering ROS overproduction, Se NPs induce caspase-3 activation and PARP cleavage, thereby leading to mitochondria-mediated apoptosis.25 The changes in the mitochondrial membrane potential in each group were further examined to investigate the importance of mitochondria in cell apoptosis. As displayed in Figure 8c,d, the mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated based on the green and red fluorescence ratio. It is obvious that BFP-Se increased the amount of JC-1 monomer, leading to a decline in the mitochondrial membrane potential of the cells; this, in turn, impaired the mitochondrial function. This result suggests that BFP-Se may trigger apoptosis in HepG2 cells by activating the endogenous mitochondrial pathway.

It is widely known that the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway is controlled and regulated by the Bcl-2 protein family. Bcl-2 is a crucial apoptosis protein that plays a role in the mitochondrial pathway. It regulates the mitochondrial membrane permeability in conjunction with the pro-apoptotic protein Bax.33−35 The downregulation of Bcl-2 and upregulation of Bax lead to an increase in the mitochondrial membrane permeability, resulting in the release of apoptosis activators like cytochrome c. This increased amount of cytochrome c can then cause Apaf-1 to bind to caspase-9.6 The activation of caspase-9 further induces the activation of downstream caspase-3, resulting in programmed cell death. Therefore, the increased expression of Bax or cleaved caspase-9 and the decreased expression of Bcl-2 demonstrate that the mitochondrial pathway mediates apoptosis. As shown in Figure 9, compared with the control group, there was a decrease in the expression of the caspase-9 and caspase-3 proteins and a significant decrease in the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 in the BFP-Se group. In contrast, the expressions of the sheared pro-apoptotic proteins cleaved caspase-9 and cleaved caspase-3 were increased, which is consistent with the previously observed cytotoxicity results.

(a) An electrophoresis plot of protein expression in HepG2 cells, and a statistical plot of protein expression in HepG2 cells (compared with the control group, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01; n = 3).

The possible signal pathway of BFP-Se for the apoptosis of HepG2 cells is shown in Scheme 1. In detail, BFP-Se is first consumed by HepG2 cells through clathrin- and caveolae-independent endocytosis. Subsequently, it is absorbed and degraded by lysosomes, leading to the release of Se NPs and causing an increase in intracellular ROS production. This, in turn, leads to mitochondrial apoptosis in the cancer cells. In summary, the inhibition activity of BFP-Se is the result of death receptor-mediated and mitochondria-mediated apoptotic pathways.

Conclusion

In this study, self-assembled nanotubes constructed by triple-helix polysaccharides derived from black fungus (BFP) were utilized as carriers to load Se NPs. The findings showed that Se NPs with an average size of 75 nm were uniformly immobilized inside the BFP nanotubes through Se–O bonds. BFP-Se exhibited high stability and dispersibility, which led to a significant enhancement in its uptake by HepG2 cells through clathrin- and caveolae-independent endocytosis pathways, especially compared to Se NPs. Additionally, the impact of BFP-Se on HepG2 cells was determined to be related to the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitochondrial dysfunction, and the activation of caspase-3/caspase-9. These findings suggest that BFP-Se has potential in the application of drug delivery systems, particularly for the treatment of human liver cancers.

Experimental Procedures

Materials and Reagents

The black fungus used in this study (Figure 1a) was originally from Fangxian County of the Hubei province, and the BFP polysaccharide appeared as white and cotton-like (Figure 1b). The human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines (HepG2) were purchased from the Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were all obtained from HyClone (Logan, Utah, U.S.A.). Methyl thiazolyl tetrazolium (MTT) was obtained from BomeiBio (Hefei, China). Coumarin 6 (C6) and Hoechst 33342 were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). JC-1 and reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kits were obtained from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Na2SeO3, ascorbic acid (Vc), and other reagents used in this work were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical (Shanghai, China). Ultrapure water was obtained from a purified water device with a milli-Q apparatus (Millipore, Bedford, Massachusetts, U.S.A.).

Methods

Preparation and Characterization of BFP-Loaded Se NPs (BFP-Se)

A BFP solution (1.0 mg/mL) was mixed with sodium selenite (1.0 M) under magnetic stirring at 25 °C for 12 h. Then, a freshly prepared ascorbic acid (Vc) solution was added by dripping it slowly into the mixture (Vc:Na2SeO3 = 6:1). After 12 h, the product was dialyzed against deionized water for 3 days at 25 °C. The preparation of pure Se NPs was the same as the above method, but an equal volume of deionized water was required to replace the aqueous solution of BFP. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, GeminiSEM 300, Germany) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100, Japan) analyses were completed to observe the internal morphology of the BFP and BFP-Se samples. The interaction between the Se NPs and BFP was explored by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, model 1600, PerkinElmer, U.S.A.), wide-angle X-ray diffraction (XRD-6000, Shimadzu, Japan), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, U.S.A.). The content of Se in BFP-Se was determined by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, NexION 300X, PerkinElmer, U.S.A.). The stability of BFP-Se under different conditions was investigated by completing particle size and zeta potential analyses with a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (ZEN3600, Malvern, U.K.).

Cell Proliferation Inhibition Assay

The anti-proliferation effects of BFP, the Se NPs, and BFP-Se on HepG2 cells were evaluated by the MTT assay at 570 nm, according to methods used in previously reported studies.36,37 Briefly, cells were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Then the BFP, Se NPs, and BFP-Se solutions with a final multigradient concentration of 0–400 μg/mL were added into the cells and co-cultured for 24, 48, and 72 h. Then, 20 μL of the MTT reagent (10%) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h, and the resultant absorbance was measured at 570 nm to quantify the cell survival. Each sample was repeated three times to ensure the reliability of the results.

Cell Cycle Assay

The cell cycle distribution was determined by flow cytometry.6 First, the HepG2 cells were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After treatment with the BFP, Se NPs, and BFP-Se samples, the cells were harvested with 0.25% trypsin and washed with PBS. Then, the cells were centrifuged at 1100 rpm for 5 min before being fixed with prechilled 75% ethanol at −20 °C for 4 h. The fixed cells were washed and incubated with RNase A and propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. The stained cells were determined using a flow cytometer (NovoCyte D2060R, ACEA, China), and the data were analyzed by the FlowJo software.

Cell Uptake Assay

The fluorescein dye coumarin 6 was added to the reaction system before mixing BFP with Vc, as has been previously described.38 The HepG2 cells were incubated with equal amounts of C6-BFP-Se at different times (1, 1.5, and 2 h); this was followed by staining the cells with the lysosome dye lysotracker-red (50 nM) for 30 min and the Hirst dye Hoechst 33342 (10 μg/mL) for 15 min before observation.

The effect of temperature on cell uptake was studied by flow cytometry. The attached HepG2 cells were placed in a refrigerator at 4 °C and in an incubator at 37 °C for 1 h. They were then incubated with a medium containing equal amounts of C6-BFP-Se for 1 h before the cells were measured by flow cytometry. The cellular uptake mechanism of BFP-Se by the HepG2 cancer cells was studied with several endocytosis inhibitors, including chlorpromazine (120 μM), sucrose (400 mM), genistein (200 μM), indometacin (279 μM), and amiloride (20 μM). The average fluorescence intensity was measured by flow cytometry.

The Apoptosis Effect of BFP-Se on HepG2 Cells

The apoptosis effect of BFP-Se on HepG2 cells was determined by an intracellular ROS generation analysis, a mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) assay, and western blotting. HepG2 cells in a 6-well plate were incubated and treated with BFP, Se NPs, and BFP-Se for 48 h. The cells were washed with PBS and incubated with dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) for 20 min under darkness. The fluorescence intensity of the cells was visualized using a fluorescence microscope. As for the quantitative studies, the cells were harvested, and the intracellular ROS level was determined using flow cytometry.

The fluorescent probe JC-1 was used to detect the change of ΔΨm of HepG2 cells induced by BFP-Se and the other samples. The HepG2 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with the samples for 48 h, suspended in a JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. After being washed twice with a JC-1 staining buffer, the fluorescence intensity of the cells was visualized by a fluorescence microscope. As for the quantitative studies, the cells were harvested, and the status of ΔΨm was determined using flow cytometry.

The protein expressions were tested with a western blot assay. HepG2 cells were incubated and then exposed to BFP-Se and the other samples for 48 h. After that, the cells were harvested and lysed in a cell lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 30 min on ice. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The total protein concentration was assayed using a BCA test kit (Bio-Rad, U.S.A.). After 8–12% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and a semidry transfer to nitrocellulose membranes, protein extraction was blocked with PBS–5% fat-free dried milk for 1.5 h, and the membranes were incubated at 4 °C overnight with TBST-containing specific primary antibodies. Then, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (CST, Beverly, Massachusetts, U.S.A.) and horseradish peroxidase at a dilution of 1:10 000 for 1 h. Finally, the protein bands were visualized under a G: BOX gel imaging system.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using the Prism 9 software (GraphPad, San Diego, California, U.S.A.). The data collected from at least three independent experiments are shown as the average ± the standard deviation (SD). Two groups and three or more groups were compared by two-tailed unpaired t-test and analysis of variance, respectively. p < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.
