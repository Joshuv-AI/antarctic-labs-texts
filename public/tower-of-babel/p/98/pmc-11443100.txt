Evaluating the efficacy of uniformly designed square mesh resin 3D printed scaffolds in directing the orientation of electrospun PCL nanofibers
Authors: Evan Fair, Jacob Bornstein, Timothy Lyons, Phillip Sgobba, Alana Hayes, Megan Rourke, Isaac Macwan, Naser Haghbin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Replicating the architecture of extracellular matrices (ECM) is crucial in tissue engineering to support tissues’ natural structure and functionality. The ECM’s structure plays a significant role in directing cell alignment. Electrospinning is an effective technique for fabricating nanofibrous substrates that mimic the architecture of extracellular matrices (ECM). This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of resin 3D-printed scaffolds made from a low-conductivity material (i.e., a resin composed of methacrylated oligomers, monomers, and photoinitiators) in directing the alignment of electrospun polycaprolactone (PCL) nanofibers. Six 3D-printed scaffolds were fabricated using stereolithography (SLA) technology and strategically positioned on an aluminum foil collector plate during electrospinning. The structured geometry of the scaffolds, rather than the local electric field distribution, is hypothesized to guide nanofiber alignment. Images acquired through the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used to analyze and statistically quantify the nanofibrous scaffolds to evaluate the alignment of nanofibers over the scaffolds compared to a set of randomly deposited control nanofiber samples in the absence of the 3D printed scaffolds. SEM images also showed significant alignment of nanofibers within the pores of scaffolds, using histograms as a means for indicating the distribution of orientation angles. Statistical analysis revealed that this distribution deviates from normality due to the deviations in the tails and the existence of relatively smaller peaks at angles relative to 0°, particularly within a range of ± 50° and ± 40°. It is further found that the average peak orientation angle relative to 0° had a maximum probability of 0.014. Furthermore, the statistical analysis confirmed the distribution and significant differences in orientation between test samples with 3D-printed scaffolds and control samples. These findings demonstrate the effectiveness of resin 3D-printed scaffolds, particularly their geometric filtering effect, leading to controlled nanofiber alignment, which is proposed to be beneficial for enhancing cell adhesion, proliferation, and cell migration in tissue engineering applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-72711-6.

Introduction

The precise alignment of cells in a specific direction is crucial in tissue engineering for accurately replicating the natural structure and functionality of different tissues, such as the heart1, veins2, muscles3, and tendons4. Achieving this alignment involves designing extracellular matrix (ECM)-based substrates that support and reinforce the unique architectures found in different tissues5.

Aligned PCL nanofibers, which mimic the linear architecture of ECM components6, significantly influence cell orientation and elongation, replicating natural ECM structures to promote tissue development6. This alignment is essential for cell migration, proliferation, and differentiation7, providing topographical cues that guide cellular pathways for tissue regeneration7. In musculoskeletal tissues, aligned PCL nanofibers direct the formation of muscle fibers and bone trabeculae, enhancing mechanical properties and functional integration8. Their orientation also impacts intracellular signaling pathways, promoting gene expression and cytoskeletal regulation7. In nerve tissue, aligned nanofibers guide axonal growth, enhancing nerve regeneration and functional recovery9. Similarly, in cardiac tissue, they promote cardiomyocyte alignment, essential for effective heart function10.

Recent advances in electrospinning have been instrumental in the fabrication of nanofibrous substrates that closely resemble the architecture of natural ECM11. For instance, the electrospinning technique utilized different polymeric compounds to create varied nanofiber morphologies and optimize the geometry of fabricated electrospun scaffolds as demonstrated by Jun et al.12, which closely resembled the ECM. Similarly the electrospinning technique has been shown to have significantly expanded its ability to mimic tissue properties13. Furthermore, this technique is known to have enhanced tissue regeneration14, by enhancing ECM strength and strain behavior15, improving cellular infiltration16, and guiding cell behavior17, highlighting its role as a pivotal tool in tissue engineering18.

One effective method of aligning nanofibers, and consequently aligning cells on the electrospun nanofiber layer, involves using 3D-printed scaffolds to direct the orientation of nanofibers during the electrospinning process. For example, Vyas et al.19 developed a micro- and nano-scale (dual-scale) structure using 3D printing and electrospinning, enabling the alignment of nanofibers within the microscale pores of the scaffold. Yu et al.20 integrated 3D bioprinting with electrospinning to construct hierarchical structures, combining gel microfibers and Polycaprolactone (PCL) nanofibers in a grid-like arrangement for controlled pore structure and biomimetic qualities. Lee et al.21 utilized stereolithography and electrospinning for a 3D biomimetic neural structure, combining hydrogel scaffolding with aligned PCL and PCL-gelatin microfibers to support neural cell growth and differentiation. Kozior et al.22 focused on the adhesion between electrospun fibers and 3D printed substrates, finding that material choice, such as using Thermoplastic Polyurethane (TPU) over Polylactic Acid (PLA), significantly affects fiber-substrate bonding. Kim et al.23 designed 3D bi-functionalized nerve conduits with a mix of aligned and random nanofibers via a modified electrospinning setup. The created structures showed specific functional properties, such as selective permeability and enhanced nerve cell proliferation. Thus, based on the existing literature, it is found that the integration of 3D printing and electrospinning in fabricating dual-scale structures has been explored extensively, emphasizing the influence of 3D-printed scaffolds on nanofiber orientation during the electrospinning process.

Building upon this foundation, here we come up with a study to investigate the efficacy of a uniform square mesh resin 3D-printed scaffold in directing and aligning electrospun PCL nanofibers during the electrospinning process. The rationale for selecting this uniform square mesh 3D-printed scaffold was based on multiple pieces of evidence from the existing literature, suggesting it can create a substrate that aligns PCL nanofibers and forms a suitable extracellular matrix (ECM). A previous study24 showed that such scaffolds significantly enhanced cellular colonization, including improved cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation due to the high surface area, interconnected porous network, and ECM-like structure provided by the mesh design. This research examines the extent to which the scaffold can modify nanofiber orientation and analyzes the distribution of orientation angles in relation to the scaffold’s position on a stationary metal plate collector used in electrospinning. While previous research has utilized similar dual-scale structures for cell directionality, the comprehensive statistical analysis reveals the influence of the 3D printed scaffold on the alignment of PCL nanofibers and maps the orientation angles.

This study also discusses two main hypotheses for the mechanisms governing the nanofiber alignment during electrospinning by modeling the electrospinning process and analyzing the electric field gradients utilizing finite element analysis. One hypothesis suggests that the electrostatic fields generated during electrospinning influence nanofiber alignment, with the nanofibers functioning as electrodes and the pores acting as insulators, introducing a directional charge that aligns the fibers. Another hypothesis suggests that the geometry and dimensions of the 3D printed scaffold modulates the deposition and alignment of nanofibers during the electrospinning process. The structured geometry of the scaffold may act as a physical guide, directing the nanofibers into alignment. Furthermore, the top layer of the micro scaffold might exert a filtering effect, analogous to a series of bridges, where only fibers with a relatively perpendicular orientation are allowed to adsorb. This selective attachment could enhance the alignment and uniformity of the nanofiber network, where aligned fibers preferentially deposit in specific orientations due to the scaffold’s patterned geometry.

This study is built on established findings that electrospun PCL nanofibers on 3D-printed scaffolds significantly enhance cellular interactions, such as cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation. Previous research by Vyas et al.19 demonstrated the efficacy of dual-scale scaffolds with aligned nanofibers in promoting cell alignment and migration, while Xiao et al.25 showed that 3D nanofiber scaffolds improve cell penetration and host tissue integration. This work advanced these concepts by focusing on optimizing fabrication and alignment methodologies, setting the groundwork for future studies that will include in vitro validation of cellular interactions.

While previous studies have primarily focused on modifying electrospinning collectors—such as parallel substrates, rotating drums, flat plates, or patterned grids (e.g.25)—to influence nanofiber alignment, or on using square mesh 3D-printed scaffolds for cell orientation and direction (e.g.19), the novelty of this paper lies in determining the efficacy of fiber alignment on square mesh resin 3D-printed scaffolds. This has not been thoroughly assessed or examined through a rigorous statistical framework. By assessing the extent to which 3D-printed scaffold geometry affects the orientation of nanofibers, this work establishes a new standard by employing advanced statistical methods, offering a quantitative evaluation of scaffold performance. This research also seeks to hypothesize the primary factors influencing nanofiber orientation. This approach not only contributes to the theoretical understanding of scaffold-nanofiber interactions but also provides practical insights that can be leveraged to enhance the design and application of tissue engineering scaffolds.

Methodology

Workflow for creating dual-scale structures using 3D printing and electrospinning

Figure 1 represents a schematic depicting the workflow of creating a micro- and nano-scale (dual-scale) structure through the processes of 3D printing and electrospinning. Six square mesh 3D-printed scaffolds were produced using Stereolithography (SLA) technology (Fig. 1A) with a Form 3B + printer from Formlabs Inc., MA, USA, and Formlabs Grey V4 Resin, achieving a layer resolution of 25 μm. The six 3D-printed scaffolds were then mounted onto the electrospinning collector plate (Fig. 1B). Figure 1C shows a blow-up depiction of the collector plate with aligned nanofibers across the individual 3D-printed scaffold compared to a random collection of electrospun nanofibers outside the 3D-printed scaffolds on the aluminum foil over the collector plate (Fig. 1D). The effectiveness of these 3D-printed scaffolds in directing nanofibers is further critically evaluated using image analysis and statistical techniques to thoroughly check the quality and hence the reliable production of aligned nanofibers for applications in tissue engineering.

Prior to the electrospinning process, Poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL) was dispersed in acetone to form a 15% w/v PCL polymer solution. The solution was prepared by mixing 7.5 g of dry PCL powder with 50 mL of acetone, stirring at 750 rpm for 15 min, followed by 1500 rpm for 30 min, both at 45 °C using a hot plate (Model: LCH-MSB-HD) with a LACHOI Magnetic Stirrer. After the hot plate dispersion, the solution was tested for viscosity using a Brookfield DV-E viscometer with a 02 spindle size. 200 mL of the PCL dispersion was sonicated for 20 min before filling it in a 200 mL beaker. The viscosity measurements were all performed back-to-back in one sitting, and the centiPoise (cP) values were recorded at the 2-minute mark. With spindle size 02, the average viscosity for the 15% w/v dispersion was found to be ~ 180 cP. Before filling the syringe for electrospinning, the solution was first bath sonicated at 80 °C for 20 min using a Heated Ultrasonic Cleaner (Model: VGT-1620QTD, MTI Corporation, Richmond, CA), out of which 10 mL was used at a time for electrospinning. This procedure, involving bath sonication, was used before filling the electrospinning syringe for each trial.

The 3D-printed scaffold was designed as a 3D mesh model (Fig. 2A) with dimensions of 6.9 mm in length and 5.8 mm in width. It comprises of four layers of 0.3 mm wide struts, spaced 0.8 mm apart, with alternating layers oriented perpendicularly to each other. This 3D mesh structure was designed based on findings from previous studies24, which demonstrated that the meshes in scaffolds significantly enhance cellular colonization because of their beneficial effects on tissue regeneration. Upon completion, the 3D-printed scaffolds underwent a cleansing process in 99% Isopropyl alcohol using the Form Wash system for 20 min to eliminate any residual uncured resin. Then, each 3D-printed scaffold was air-dried and then post-cured under UV light for 40 min using the Form Cure system to enhance the material properties, such as stability, durability, and chemical resistance. The dimensions of the 3D-printed scaffolds were verified with a digital microscope (VR-5000 series, Keyence, Japan) to determine the difference in accuracy between the model and the fabricated product, ensuring they matched the precise specifications of the digital mesh model used in the design. This verification, achieved by scanning the top surface of each scaffold and measuring the distances between the struts, confirmed the 3D printing process’s accuracy and the integrity of the scaffold’s architecture.

Although the 3D-printed scaffolds were uniformly designed with a consistent square mesh pattern, their orientation on the aluminum foil varied. Scaffolds S1, S2, and S3 were oriented in the same direction (Fig. 2B), while scaffolds S4, S5, and S6 were rotated 90° relative to the first set (Fig. 2C). The six 3D-printed scaffolds were strategically positioned on the collector plate, as depicted in Fig. 2D. This dual orientation setup at different locations on the aluminum foil was implemented to investigate the influence of scaffold orientation and location on nanofiber alignment. All scaffolds were firmly secured on aluminum foil placed on the metal plate collector using copper tape to ensure stability during the electrospinning process. This variation was intentionally implemented to investigate the influence of scaffold orientation on nanofiber alignment. The PCL solution was then electrospun onto the six scaffolds using the NanoSpinner Electrospinning Device (NS Plus, Inovenso) under identical conditions: a flow rate of 1.5 mL/h, an 18-gauge needle, 15 cm distance between the needle and the collector plate, and a voltage of 17 kV. The electrospinning process was carried out for 30 min to achieve the desired nanofibrous structure thickness of 132 μm.

After the electrospinning process, the 3D-printed scaffolds containing the oriented nanofibers were carefully removed from the copper tape and underlying aluminum foil using tweezers. The positions of the 3D-printed scaffolds on the aluminum foil were preserved to quantify the effect of location on the orientation of the deposited nanofibers. The orientation of the nanofibers on the 3D-printed scaffolds was further examined through scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using Phenom XL Desktop SEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For SEM imaging, the 3D-printed scaffolds were secured on aluminum SEM stubs with conductive adhesive carbon tape, ensuring a consistent orientation for imaging. SEM images for nanofiber orientation analysis were captured, starting with S1-S6 as per Fig. 2D. This was followed by imaging at five squares on each scaffold—top left (A), top right (B), center (C), bottom left (D), and bottom right (E), as designated in Fig. 2E. Finally, within these squares, SEM imaging was conducted at five specific locations within each pore (p, q, r, s, and t as seen in Fig. 2F), ensuring a comprehensive examination of nanofiber orientation across all 3D-printed scaffolds. SEM images were also captured from electrospun nanofibers at six random locations on the aluminum foil outside the 3D-printed scaffolds (Fig. 1D). This was done to compare the alignment and distribution of nanofibers on the scaffolds with those in areas without scaffolds, providing a baseline to evaluate the effectiveness of the 3D-printed scaffolds in directing nanofiber orientation.

Image analysis of nanofiber orientation

The ImageJ software suite was utilized to analyze SEM images to assess the orientation of nanofibers on the 3D-printed scaffolds. The ‘Threshold’ function was applied to precisely distinguish the nanofibers from the background. This step was pivotal for addressing potential salt-and-pepper noise, which could stem from various sources, including scattered light, sensor imperfections, or nanofibers located in the deeper layers of the micro-scaffold. To ensure accurate analysis, the ‘Make Rectangle Set First’ function was used to avoid analyzing the bottom label in SEM images and to reduce artifacts from the image border. The ‘Despeckle’ function was also employed to refine the images further, reducing noise and facilitating the subsequent application of ‘Find Edges’ to outline the nanofibers accurately. Utilizing the ‘Directionality’ plugin, directionality histograms were generated, offering a quantified view of the nanofibers’ orientation across a range of − 89° to + 90°, with a bin size of 180. This approach allowed for an in-depth quantification of the alignment and distribution of nanofibers. A macro was utilized to automate this process for large volumes of images, which can be found in the supplementary (Sect. 1, Fig. SF.1). The derived data, indicating the percentage alignment of nanofibers at each angle, was extracted and organized in a Microsoft Excel file. The procedure for determining the directionality for the nanofiber alignment was put through a validation procedure where the angle orientations were found to be at minimum 97.4% accurate with respect to the digitally analyzed SEM images. This image analysis procedure can be found in the supplementary (Sect. Square Mesh Res, Fig. SF.2). Nanofiber histograms, showing the probability versus orientation angle with respect to the x-axis (Fig. 2A), were generated using OriginPro 2024b (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA).

Statistical snalysis of nanofiber orientation data

The statistical analysis of samples S1 through S6 and 30 control samples was conducted using a combination of Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S)25, Anderson–Darling (AD), and Ryan–Joiner (R–J) normality tests26. The control samples were imaged, and the orientation data were extracted. To facilitate comparison, six control samples were grouped together and averaged to produce six Control Histograms, ensuring a clear representation of the control data. These methods were chosen for their robustness and reliability in assessing distribution characteristics, particularly focusing on normality and the presence of heavy tails27. To visually assess the normality of the data distributions, Q–Q (Quantile–Quantile) plots28 were generated for each sample. These plots compare the quantiles of the sample data to the quantiles of a normal distribution. Points along the reference line in these plots suggest normality, while deviations highlight departures from normality, particularly in the tails. Finally, Mann-Whitney U tests29 were conducted to compare the medians of the sample groups (S1–S3 and S4–S6) against the control group. This non-parametric test was used to determine if there were significant differences between the distributions of the samples and the control group. A significance level of P-value < 0.0530 was used to identify significant differences. Minitab (Minitab, LLC, PA, USA) version 18.1 was utilized for statistical analysis.

Effect of the 3D-printed scaffold on the electric field gradients in electrospinning

It was of interest to determine whether the alignment of nanofibers is influenced by alterations in the electric field distribution or by the geometric effect of the resin 3D-printed scaffold. Therefore, COMSOL Multiphysics was used to model the influence of 3D-printed scaffolds on electric field gradients within the space between the nozzle tip and the metal plate collector in the electrospinning process. The nozzle tip (diameter: 0.8 mm) and the air box (dimensions: 40 mm × 40 mm × 165 mm) representing the electrospinning chamber were modeled directly within COMSOL. The CAD model of the 3D-printed scaffolds was imported from SolidWorks into COMSOL and placed on the aluminum foil in the arrangement shown in Fig. 2D. The 3D model was analyzed using the AC/DC module to quantify the electrostatic behavior utilizing the Electric Fields and Currents interface. Different materials were accurately assigned to each component: air for the surrounding space, aluminum 6063-T83 for the collector foil, steel AISI 4340 for the nozzle tip, and a custom material for the 3D-printed scaffold with the specified relative permittivity of 105 and resistivity of 108 Ω m, as per the datasheets for the non-conductive resin material obtained from Formlabs (i.e., Formlabs Grey V4 Resin), the resin 3D-printed scaffolds were made from a low-conductivity material composed of methacrylated oligomers, monomers, and photoinitiators. The aluminum foil collector was modeled in a size of 40 mm × 40 mm × 0.5 mm. A high voltage of 17 kV was applied at the nozzle tip, generating an electric field between the nozzle and the aluminum foil collector, where the 3D-printed scaffolds were placed. The finite element analysis (FEA) was employed to compute and visualize the electric field intensity and distribution, considering the actual dimensions and material properties used in the physical setup. The mesh type used was a Physics-Controlled mesh with the element size set to extremely fine option in COMSOL. Boundary conditions were defined to accurately represent the physical setup, including ground and electric potential application. A detailed analysis of the electric field with and without the presence of the 3D printed scaffolds can be found in the supplementary, Sect. 7 (Table. ST.6), which demonstrates the influence of the square mesh geometry on the electric field in X and Y axes and the two diagonals (Fig. 8) on top of the scaffold, middle of the scaffold, and the bottom of the scaffolds during the electrospinning process.

Results and discussion

The CAD model of the 3D-printed scaffold (Fig. 3A) had a physical size of 6.9 mm in length and 5.8 mm in width, featuring four layers of 0.3 mm wide struts spaced 0.8 mm apart and arranged perpendicularly to each other. However, the actual size of the 3D scaffolds (Fig. 3B) was smaller than the physical size of the CAD model. Figure 3C illustrates the percentage change in dimensions of the six scaffolds, revealing greater contraction in the X direction. It was found that the 3D-printed scaffolds exhibited an average shrinkage of approximately 14% in the X direction and 10% in the Y direction.

It is understood that anisotropic shrinkage in Stereolithography (SLA) 3D printing presents a significant challenge, impacting printed part’s dimensional accuracy and mechanical properties to deviate from those anticipated by the theoretical models31. This phenomenon was characterized by differential shrinkage rates along various axes, leading to warpage and distortion. Thermal and curing stress32, alongside geometric dimensions33, were the primary factors influencing this anisotropic behavior. Existing literature31,33) partially supported these influences. For instance, studies32,34 indicated that differential curing depths due to varying light penetration and exposure during the printing process led to anisotropic shrinkage, particularly along the X-axis, where layers were more exposed to thermal stresses31. Additionally, larger geometric dimensions tend to amplify cumulative shrinkage, as supported by the findings in33, where larger parts experienced significant shrinkage due to the extended distance over which stresses were distributed33. Based on these insights, the observed disparity in shrinkage could most probably be attributed to two main factors. Firstly, the SLA 3D printing process induced greater thermal and curing stresses along the horizontal plane where layers were stacked, causing more contraction along the X-axis. Secondly, the scaffold’s extended length along the X-axis exacerbated overall contraction due to the increased distance over which these stresses accumulated, leading to a higher percentage of shrinkage compared to the Y-axis.

While Fig. 4 shows all peaks relative to 0°, there are also noticeable secondary peaks at other angles relative to this prominent peak. To address the differences between all the scaffolds based on their position on the electrospinning collector and influence of electrospinning parameters, combined histograms were prepared for all six scaffolds, each with the five pore locations and each location containing the five positions. This large data set was then statistically analyzed to quantify the alignment patterns and assess the significance of the predominant peak (Fig. 5). To achieve this, 25 SEM images and corresponding histograms were collected from various positions within the scaffold, generating this extensive dataset that was then analyzed by combining the histograms. The 25 SEM images mentioned were taken across the six 3D-printed scaffolds (Fig. 2D). Each scaffold contains 5 pores (Fig. 2E), and within each pore, 5 positions (Fig. 2F) were sampled. This results in a total of 6 scaffolds × 5 pores/scaffold × 5 positions/pore = 150 sample positions. This comprehensive examination provided a detailed overview of the nanofiber orientation across the entire scaffold, as shown in Fig. 5. This approach helped in understanding the overall distribution and variability of fiber alignment within the scaffold and further assisted in understanding the relationship between the geometry of the scaffold and fiber orientation.

Figure 6 shows the combined histograms representing nanofiber orientation angles, derived from multiple measurements within each sample (S1–S6) on the 3D-printed scaffolds. These histograms illustrate the probability distribution of fiber alignment angles ranging from − 89° to 90°. The data highlights the likelihood of fibers aligning at various angles in each sample, providing insights into the degree of orientation and variability among the samples. Representative SEM images from each of the six samples are presented in Fig. 5. Sample S1 (Fig. 6A) exhibits a prominent peak around 0°, with a maximum probability of approximately 0.014 with a standard deviation of 0.00305, indicating significant fiber alignment relative to this angle. Sample S2 (Fig. 6B) shows a broader distribution with peaks reaching probabilities of about 0.011 with a standard deviation of 0.00304, suggesting some alignment but with more variability. Sample S3 (Fig. 6C) has a broad distribution with multiple smaller peaks, indicating varied orientations and less uniform alignment, with a peak probability of around 0.012 with a standard deviation of 0.0053. Sample S4 (Fig. 6D) presents a wide distribution with several small peaks, indicating diverse fiber orientations, with probabilities around 0.010 with a standard deviation of 0.0040. Sample S5 (Fig. 6E) shows a central peak around 0° but also notable variability in angles, reflecting mixed alignment, with a peak probability of about 0.013 with a standard deviation of 0.0053. Sample S6 (Fig. 6F) displays a central peak around 0°, similar to S1, indicating significant alignment but with a broader distribution, suggesting some variability, with a peak probability of approximately 0.014 with a standard deviation of 0.0041.

The presence of some randomness in fiber alignment, as observed in Fig. 5, reflected the inherent complexities of the electrospinning process and provided a realistic assessment of the scaffold’s effectiveness in directing nanofiber orientation. Unlike previous studies (e.g.19) that focused on using scaffolds for cell orientation and direction, this study evaluated the effectiveness of the 3D-printed scaffold geometry in influencing nanofiber alignment, despite the inherent variability associated with the electrospinning process, including fluctuations in voltage35 and inconsistencies in flow rate36. The results demonstrated that, while complete uniformity was not achieved, the scaffold significantly reduced randomness compared to scenarios without scaffolds, as evidenced by the broader angular distribution shown in Fig. 6. This study’s innovative statistical approach, which integrated multiple normality tests and quantile-quantile plots, provided a comprehensive analysis of the alignment patterns, offering deeper insights into the future research contribution of specific scaffold parameters, such as length, height, and thickness, to fiber alignment. These findings confirmed the scaffold’s effectiveness and established a baseline for optimizing scaffold designs for various tissue engineering applications where controlled fiber orientation is critical.

The normality of samples S1–S6 was evaluated using three statistical tests: Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S), Anderson–Darling (A–D), and Ryan–Joiner (R–J). Additionally, Q–Q (Quantile–Quantile) plots were utilized to visually assess the normality of the data. While the K–S, A–D, and R–J tests provided quantitative measures of normality, the Q–Q plots offered a graphical method to compare the sample distributions to a normal distribution. The K–S test compared the sample distribution with a normal distribution by measuring the maximum distance between them. All samples showed significant deviations from normality, with P-values < 0.010, which was much less than the 0.05 threshold value typically used to determine normality. This indicates that the distribution does not conform to normality due to deviations in the tails and the existence of relatively smaller peaks at angles other than 0°, particularly around − 20° and 20°. The A-D test, which gave more weight to the tails of the distribution, and the R–J test, which focused more on the center of the distribution similar to the Shapiro-Wilk test, confirmed these results, indicating non-normality across all samples. Given this non-normality, parametric tests, such as the T-test and ANOVA (for more than two samples), were unsuitable, necessitating the use of non-parametric tests like the Mann–Whitney U test, which did not assume normality and was ideal for comparing differences between independent samples. This approach ensured accurate analysis of the non-normally distributed data from samples S1–S6.

The Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted to compare the medians of different pairs of samples (S1–S6) to evaluate the statistical significance of differences between them. The test compared the medians of each pair of samples using non-parametric methods, which do not assume a normal distribution of the data. For each pair, descriptive statistics, including sample size and median values, were calculated. The estimated differences in medians were provided along with 95.01% confidence intervals.

In the pair comparisons of S1 and S2, S2 and S3, S3 and S4, S5 and S6, S4 and S6, S2 and S4, S2 and S5, and S2 and S6, the P-values were all greater than 0.05, indicating no significant differences between the medians of these sample pairs. Specifically, the P-values for these comparisons were 0.503, 0.231, 0.086, 0.742, 0.200, 0.214, 0.994, and 0.582, respectively.

However, the comparison between S4 and S5 showed a significant difference in medians, with a P-value of 0.012. This suggests a statistically significant difference between the medians of S4 and S5. Given the multiple comparisons conducted, this significant result for S4 and S5 was determined to be a type I error (false positive). To control type I errors, a Bonferroni correction was applied, which adjusts the significance threshold based on the number of comparisons made. The Bonferroni-corrected significance level in this context would be 0.05 divided by the number of comparisons37. Given the high number of comparisons, the adjusted threshold would be lower, indicating that the P-value of 0.012 may not indicate a true significant difference after correction. This suggests that the difference observed between S4 and S5 could indeed be due to a type I error and may not represent a true significant difference.

Moreover, the stringent nature of the Bonferroni correction increases the likelihood of type II errors (false negatives), where true differences might be overlooked37,38. Given the non-parametric nature of the Mann–Whitney U test and the sample sizes involved, the power of the test is lower compared to parametric tests, which inherently increases the risk of type II errors. This means that while the Bonferroni correction effectively controls type I errors, it also raises the chance that real differences between samples might not be detected.

Overall, the Mann-Whitney U tests provided valuable insights into the differences between sample pairs, confirming that, with the exception of the potential type I error in S4 and S5, the medians of the other sample pairs were statistically similar. This non-parametric approach ensured accurate analysis of the non-normally distributed data from samples S1–S6, supporting the reliability of the study’s findings. All samples, except for the potentially spurious significance between S4 and S5, are not statistically significant and can be considered as one set of data. The statistical data based on these three tests, K–S, A–D, and R–J, along with the Mann–Whitney U tests and Q–Q plots can be found in the supplementary, Sect. 2, Table ST.1., Sect. 3, Table ST.2.

Furthermore, this comprehensive analysis indicated that the location and orientation of the 3D-printed scaffolds on the collector did not significantly affect the nanofiber alignment and distribution indicating that it was controlled predominantly by the 3D printed geometry. Specifically, three of the scaffold samples (S1, S2, and S3) were positioned perpendicular to the other samples (S4, S5, and S6), as shown in Fig. 2D. The observed variations in nanofiber orientation within each sample were likely due to random deposition effects rather than systematic differences related to scaffold positioning or orientation. This was supported by the statistical analysis, which showed no significant differences in median nanofiber alignment angles between differently oriented scaffold samples (P-values > 0.05). Thus, the electrospinning process proved effective in producing consistently aligned nanofibers across various scaffold positions, ensuring uniform fiber distribution irrespective of the scaffolds’ spatial arrangement on the collector (Fig. 2D).

Based on all statistical analyses of the combined histograms in Fig. 6, it was concluded that there are significant peaks relative to 0 degrees, suggesting a high probability of the distribution of orientation angles around 0 degrees. The median probability value across samples S1–S6 was found to be 0.004623. It was found that the orientation angle ranged between ± 50° and ± 40° consistently exhibits probabilities above this median (0.004623), highlighting significant alignment within this range. This predominant alignment was likely due to the standard electrospinning setup and process parameters that favor fiber alignment along a particular direction. Secondary peaks outside this range were present but were found to be generally smaller. It is anticipated that these secondary peaks are due to the presence of additional influencing factors, such as local interactions between the scaffold and nanofibers, and environmental conditions (e.g., humidity and temperature variations). The combined data suggested that while 0 degrees is the reference for the primary alignment, the broader range between ± 50° and ± 40° captured the significant alignment characteristics of the nanofibers across the different sampled scaffolds.

These findings, as demonstrated in Fig. 8D, supported the hypothesis that the geometric properties of the scaffold, rather than the electrostatic fields, were the primary factor influencing nanofiber alignment. The structured geometry of the scaffold likely acted as a physical guide, effectively directing the nanofibers’ alignment. Additionally, the top layer of the micro scaffold might have functioned as a filtering mechanism, similar to a series of bridges, where only fibers with a relatively perpendicular orientation were allowed to attach.

The geometry of a 3D-printed scaffold can significantly influence electrospun fiber alignment by serving as a physical guide during the deposition process. Based on the statistical analysis and insights from COMSOL simulations, it was anticipated that fiber alignment was influenced primarily by the scaffold geometry rather than by any significant changes in the electric field. It was also recognized that nonspecific interactions, such as electrostatics, Van der Waals forces, and hydrophobic interactions, may contribute to fiber alignment, but their impact was secondary to the scaffold’s structured design. The crowding of electric field lines around the corners of the 3D-printed scaffold was a critical phenomenon that likely contributed to the alignment of nanofibers over the scaffold. However, this alignment was largely driven by the scaffold’s physical geometry, which filtered fibers based on their orientation. Nanofibers perpendicular to the scaffold’s top layer were more likely to attach and span the gap, while those aligned parallel to the scaffold’s channels tended to fall into the cavities between them. Thus, scaffold geometry served as the dominant factor in fiber alignment. This selective attachment could enhance the alignment and uniformity of the nanofiber network, where aligned nanofibers preferentially deposit in specific orientations due to the scaffold’s patterned geometry. Additional data on the COMSOL modeling and analysis can be found in the supplementary, Sect. 7, Table ST.6.

Studies have shown that scaffolds fabricated using methacrylate-based resins in SLA 3D printing exhibit low hydrophilicity39, low surface energy39, and minimal surface roughness40. The PCL (polycaprolactone) nanofibers also exhibit low hydrophilicity41, which further limits their interaction with the scaffold. These surface characteristics, combined with the resin’s low conductivity, reduce the influence of electrostatic interactions and Van der Waals forces between the scaffold and PCL nanofibers. While the COMSOL analysis supports that the filtering effect of scaffold geometry is the dominant factor in directing nanofiber alignment, the potential contributions of molecular interactions, though likely diminished, cannot be entirely ruled out. Notably, we observed that nanofiber alignment did not occur on flat areas of the resin scaffold, further emphasizing the critical role of geometry. Overall, the physical design of the scaffold, combined with these surface properties, predominantly directs the orientation of nanofibers during the electrospinning process.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the effectiveness of uniform square mesh resin 3D-printed scaffolds in directing the alignment of electrospun polycaprolactone (PCL) nanofibers. The structured geometry of the 3D-printed scaffolds was the primary factor influencing nanofiber alignment, guiding the fibers into significant alignment within the scaffold pores, as evidenced by SEM imaging and statistical analysis. Histograms of nanofiber orientation angles showed a concentration between ± 40° and ± 50°, with a peak alignment around 0° and a standard deviation of 0.0035, indicating a clear directional alignment relative to the x-axis of the scaffold. This finding contrasts with the common assumption of random nanofiber distribution over a stationary collector and was further supported by SEM images that consistently demonstrated aligned nanofibers within the scaffold pores. Statistical analysis confirmed non-normality, suggesting that while the scaffold effectively guided nanofiber alignment, some variability remained due to environmental factors and local scaffold-nanofiber interactions. The potential filtering effect of the scaffold geometry, which may selectively enhance alignment by guiding nanofibers perpendicular to the top layer, was also noted.

Future research should explore different scaffold geometries and materials to refine nanofiber alignment for tissue engineering applications. Investigating various shapes and sizes of 3D-printed scaffolds could provide insights into optimizing scaffold design for specific tissue engineering needs. Additionally, varying electrospinning parameters, such as voltage, distance, and flow rate, in conjunction with different scaffold designs, could further enhance control over nanofiber alignment. Finally, future studies should also consider the realistic distribution of nanofiber orientations when designing tissue engineering scaffolds, potentially developing methods to either narrow the orientation distribution or strategically use the range of orientations to create more complex tissue structures that better mimic natural tissues.
