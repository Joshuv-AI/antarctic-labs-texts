Visual and refractive outcomes of small incision lenticule extraction planned with a VISULYZE-generated nomogram for myopia and astigmatism: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Ying Lin, Xin Wei, Huanjun Su, Yu Li, Baoyu Huang, Minli Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSmall-incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) is a widely used procedure for correcting myopia and myopic astigmatism. However, variability in refractive outcomes, particularly astigmatic correction, remains a concern. Introduction

Small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) has become a widely adopted refractive surgical procedure for correcting myopia and myopic astigmatism due to its flapless design, minimal invasiveness, and favorable biomechanical properties [1–3]. Compared to excimer laser–based procedures, SMILE has been associated with reduced postoperative dry eye symptoms and a lower incidence of flap-related complications [4, 5]. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated that SMILE provides safe, effective, and stable refractive outcomes for myopia and low to moderate astigmatism [6–8].

However, variability in refractive predictability, particularly in astigmatism correction, remains a concern. Studies have reported undercorrection, overcorrection, and astigmatic axis deviation following SMILE, even in eyes with low cylindrical error [6, 9]. These discrepancies may be due to various factors, including variability in subjective refraction, limitations of conventional nomogram-based planning, cyclotorsional misalignment, and the lack of intraoperative eye tracking or dynamic axis compensation [10, 11].

Nomogram planning plays a crucial role in improving refractive predictability by compensating for systematic treatment biases. Traditional nomograms rely on accumulated surgical experience and postoperative outcomes, adjusting laser parameters to minimize over- or undercorrection trends [12, 13]. However, they may not fully account for surgeon-specific factors or changes in surgical technique over time.

Recent advances in outcome-based, data-driven nomogram generation have improved refractive predictability by analyzing postoperative results to identify reproducible errors and refine treatment planning [12–14]. These approaches have been particularly beneficial in reducing systematic bias in spherical and cylindrical corrections, especially in procedures with limited intraoperative compensation.

VISULYZE (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) is a software tool designed to support outcome-based nomogram planning for SMILE. By retrospectively analyzing postoperative outcomes, VISULYZE-generated nomograms help identify consistent trends in refractive errors and guide adjustments in treatment parameters at the planning stage [14]. The clinical relevance of VISULYZE lies in its ability to formalize and standardize nomogram refinement in routine practice.

Accurate astigmatism correction is highly sensitive to rotational alignment. Even small deviations in axis alignment can significantly reduce the effectiveness of cylindrical correction [15, 16]. Therefore, improving control of astigmatic axis deviation may meaningfully impact postoperative visual outcomes.

Postoperative changes in higher-order aberrations (HOAs), particularly coma and spherical aberration, have been reported after SMILE and may affect visual quality under low-light conditions [16–18]. These changes are linked to factors such as optical zone centration, astigmatic correction accuracy, and corneal wound healing [18, 19]. Thus, differences in nomogram planning and centration can affect postoperative optical quality.

Despite increasing clinical use of VISULYZE-generated nomograms, comparative studies on their effects on visual acuity, refractive predictability, astigmatic axis accuracy, and higher-order aberrations remain limited. This study aimed to compare the visual, refractive, and optical outcomes of SMILE planned with a VISULYZE-generated nomogram versus a conventional nomogram in patients with moderate myopia and astigmatism. The goal was to determine whether using the VISULYZE-generated nomogram could improve astigmatic axis accuracy and reduce postoperative increases in higher-order aberrations.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective comparative study was conducted at the Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University between June and August 2025. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. Written informed consent for the use of de-identified clinical data was obtained from all participants prior to surgery.

Subjects

This retrospective study included 120 eyes from 60 consecutive patients with moderate myopia and myopic astigmatism who underwent small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) during the study period. Eyes were allocated to groups based on the nomogram planning strategy applied in routine clinical practice. Group allocation was performed at the eye level, and both eyes were included. A single patient with both eyes was independently assigned to either the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group (Observation group, 60 eyes), in which SMILE was planned using a VISULYZE-generated nomogram based on retrospective postoperative refractive outcomes, or the conventional nomogram group (Control group, 60 eyes), in which SMILE was planned using the surgeon’s conventional experience-based nomogram without software-assisted adjustment. Statistical analyses accounted for inter-eye correlation using mixed-effects models to appropriately consider the independence of observations.

The inclusion criteria were age between 18 and 40 years, stable refractive error (change ≤ 0.50 D within the preceding year), spherical equivalent between − 3.00 D and − 6.00 D, cylindrical refractive error ≤ 2.00 D, and corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) of 20/20 or better. Exclusion criteria included corneal ectatic disorders (including keratoconus), previous ocular surgery, ocular pathology such as glaucoma, cataract, or uveitis, systemic connective tissue diseases, and clinically significant dry eye.

Because both eyes from the same patient could be included, statistical analyses accounted for inter-eye correlation using mixed-effects models.

Preoperative examination

All patients underwent a comprehensive preoperative ophthalmic examination, including uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA), corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), manifest and cycloplegic refraction, intraocular pressure measurement, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and dilated fundus examination. Central corneal thickness and keratometric parameters were assessed using Oculyzer (Alcon, USA).

Corneal topography was obtained using the Topolyzer system (Alcon, USA) under natural lighting conditions. Ocular wavefront aberrations were measured using a Hartmann–Shack aberrometer (WASCA Analyzer, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Germany) and reconstructed over a standardized pupil diameter of 4.0 mm. Higher-order aberrations (HOAs), including coma, spherical aberration, and trefoil, were recorded as root mean square (RMS) values.

Surgical technique

All SMILE procedures were performed by the same experienced surgeon using the VisuMax femtosecond laser platform (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Germany). Surgical parameters were identical between groups, including a cap thickness of 120 μm, cap diameter of 7.5 mm, lenticule diameter of 6.5 mm, side-cut length of 2.0 mm, side-cut angle of 90°, pulse energy of 130 nJ, and spot and track spacing of 4.5 μm. The only between-group difference was the nomogram planning strategy used to determine the laser input parameters.

VISULYZE (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) is a proprietary software platform designed to generate outcome-based SMILE nomograms. The system retrospectively analyzes previous postoperative refractive outcomes to identify consistent trends in spherical and cylindrical errors and guide adjustments in laser input parameters. Input variables include preoperative spherical equivalent and cylindrical power, corneal topography and keratometry, and prior postoperative refractive results. The algorithm is primarily regression-based with proprietary refinements and integrates historical outcome data to optimize nomogram recommendations for individual patients. In this study, eyes in the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group had their laser input parameters determined using this software-based nomogram, whereas eyes in the conventional nomogram group had laser parameters determined according to the surgeon’s conventional experience-based nomogram without software-assisted adjustment.

During SMILE, centration and alignment were performed by the surgeon under the operating microscope according to the standard docking and fixation procedure of the VisuMax platform. No additional software-assisted intraoperative tracking or axis-compensation procedure was used beyond the routine platform settings.

Postoperative medication

Postoperative medication regimens were identical for both groups. Patients received levofloxacin ophthalmic solution (Santen, Japan) four times daily for one week and tobramycin–dexamethasone ophthalmic solution (Alcon, USA) four times daily for one week. This was followed by 0.1% fluorometholone ophthalmic solution (Santen, Japan) three times daily for three weeks and preservative-free artificial tear supplementation four times daily for four weeks.

Outcome measures

Clinical outcomes were assessed preoperatively and at 3 months postoperatively. Primary outcome measures included uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA), corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), spherical equivalent refraction, and astigmatic axis deviation. Using the Alpins method to quantify changes in astigmatic axis position through vector analysis. Secondary outcome measures included corneal higher-order aberrations, specifically total HOA RMS, coma, spherical aberration, and trefoil.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Data distribution was assessed for normality prior to analysis. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t tests or nonparametric tests as appropriate. Within-group comparisons were conducted using paired t tests. To control for the correlation between eyes from the same patient, mixed-effects models were used for appropriate data analysis. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Visual acuity outcomes

At 3 months postoperatively, uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA) of 20/20 or better was achieved in 55 of 60 eyes (92%) in the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group and 46 of 60 eyes (76%) in the conventional nomogram group (P < 0.001; Fig. 1). At 3 months postoperatively, corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) of 20/16 or better was achieved in 25 of 60 eyes (42%) in the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group and 20 of 60 eyes (34%) in the conventional nomogram group. CDVA remained unchanged in 38 of 60 eyes (64%) in each group. In the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group, 18 eyes (30%) gained one line and 2 eyes (3%) gained two lines, whereas 2 eyes (3%) lost one line and no eyes lost two or more lines. In the conventional nomogram group, 15 eyes (25%) gained one line and 7 eyes (12%) lost one line, with no eyes losing two or more lines. Differences between groups in line gains and losses were statistically significant (P < 0.03) (Fig. 2).

Refractive predictability

The relationship between attempted and achieved spherical equivalent correction is shown in Fig. 3. In the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group, linear regression analysis yielded the equation y = 0.97x − 0.22 (R² = 0.92). In the conventional nomogram group, the regression equation was y = 0.90x − 0.69 (R² = 0.91). The slope of 0.97 suggests a high degree of refractive predictability, while the intercept of − 0.22 reflects a slight systemic refractive shift, indicating a small bias in the refractive outcomes that was consistently observed across the group. Specifically, 93% of eyes were within ± 0.50 D, and 98% of eyes were within ± 1.00 D.

Astigmatic correction accuracy

At 3 months postoperatively, 50 of 60 eyes (84%) in the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group had an astigmatic axis deviation within 15°, and 28 of 60 eyes (47%) were within 5°, compared with 40 of 60 eyes (67%) and 20 of 60 eyes (33%), respectively, in the conventional nomogram group. Although these proportions were numerically higher in the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group, the between-group differences did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.058 for within 15° and P = 0.19 for within 5°).

The distribution of postoperative residual astigmatism is shown in Fig. 4. A greater proportion of eyes in the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group achieved residual astigmatism within ± 0.50 D compared with the conventional nomogram group (83% vs. 65%, P < 0.05). Refractive outcomes within ± 0.13 D were observed in 35% of eyes in the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group and 30% of eyes in the conventional nomogram group. The proportion of eyes with hyperopic overcorrection between + 1.01 D and + 2.00 D was lower in the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group (1%) than in the conventional nomogram group (6%) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we compared the visual, refractive, and optical outcomes of SMILE planned using a VISULYZE-generated nomogram with those of SMILE planned using a conventional nomogram in patients with moderate myopia and myopic astigmatism. Although the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group showed a numerical trend toward closer astigmatic axis alignment, the key axis-deviation comparisons did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously as numerical trends rather than statistically confirmed between-group differences.

Accurate correction of astigmatism remains a recognized challenge in SMILE. Previous studies have shown that, although SMILE generally provides predictable spherical correction, residual astigmatism and astigmatic axis deviation may still persist even when preoperative planning is carefully performed [6, 9, 20–21]. These findings underscore the sensitivity of astigmatic correction to rotational alignment and nomogram planning. In this context, the numerical differences in axis deviation observed between groups in the present study may suggest a possible association between nomogram planning strategy and the consistency of cylindrical correction, particularly with respect to postoperative astigmatic alignment. However, these axis-related findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the key comparisons did not reach statistical significance.

Differences in postoperative refractive predictability were observed between groups. The VISULYZE-generated nomogram group showed a higher proportion of eyes with lower residual astigmatism and a smaller degree of hyperopic overcorrection than the conventional nomogram group. Similar patterns have been described in studies of outcome-based nomogram planning, in which data-driven adjustment of treatment parameters may be associated with reduced systematic refractive bias [12, 13]. In SMILE, refractive outcomes may be influenced by multiple factors, including rotational misalignment, centration, and lenticule positioning, all of which can affect the accuracy of cylindrical correction. Previous studies have shown that even small degrees of rotational misalignment may lead to postoperative axis deviation and thereby influence refractive outcomes [14]. In the present study, the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group also showed numerical trends toward more favorable astigmatic axis alignment; however, these axis-related comparisons did not reach statistical significance and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Consistent with previous reports on outcome-based nomogram planning using VISULYZE, Qi Wan’ and Park S’ studies have described good postoperative astigmatism predictability, including relatively high proportions of eyes achieving residual cylinder ≤ 0.50 D at 3 months [22, 23]. In our research, with respect to astigmatic axis deviation and residual astigmatism, the VISULYZE group showed numerical trends toward closer alignment, although differences did not reach statistical significance. Qi Wan and Park S also found better astigmatic axis outcomes after outcome-based nomogram refinement [22, 23]. Additional research on regression-derived nomograms for SMILE has similarly suggested that nomogram refinement may be associated with improved refractive predictability in myopic astigmatism correction [14]. Machine learning–based nomogram models have also been investigated for cylinder prediction, further supporting the potential association of data-driven planning with improved refractive outcomes in SMILE surgery [24].

Beyond refractive accuracy, postoperative optical quality is an important determinant of functional visual performance after SMILE. Previous studies have reported postoperative increases in higher-order aberrations (HOAs), particularly coma and spherical aberration, following SMILE, which may affect image quality under mesopic or scotopic conditions and reduce contrast sensitivity and low-light visual performance [16, 17, 23, 24]. Mechanistically, these increases in HOAs are thought to result from surgical centration errors, optical zone decentration, asymmetric tissue removal, and changes in corneal asphericity due to the lenticule cutting and extraction process. For example, decentration of the optical zone relative to the visual axis can induce asymmetric corneal curvature, leading to elevated coma and trefoil aberrations, while altered anterior corneal shape and wound healing may contribute to increased spherical aberration postoperatively [25, 26].

In the current retrospective study, postoperative increases in total higher-order aberrations and coma were observed in both groups. These increases were numerically smaller in the VISULYZE-generated nomogram group. These findings may indicate an association between nomogram planning strategy and early postoperative optical outcomes after SMILE. One possible explanation is that outcome-based nomogram refinement could contribute to observed differences, potentially by reducing systematic treatment bias [24, 26].

Recent advances in clinician-accessible, outcome-based nomogram generation have facilitated personalized SMILE planning. VISULYZE can be considered part of this broader trend, as it uses historical postoperative outcomes to guide laser input parameter adjustments in a data-driven manner. For example, “Codeless development of a customized SMILE nomogram using a large language model: a practical framework for clinicians” [27] describes a workflow enabling clinicians to generate individualized SMILE nomograms without extensive programming or statistical expertise. These approaches, including other machine learning–assisted and data-driven methods, highlight a growing trend in personalized refractive planning, allowing real-world postoperative outcomes to iteratively inform treatment adjustments. While our study utilized the VISULYZE-generated nomogram, newer clinician-oriented methods provide complementary strategies for refining SMILE treatment planning and underscore the potential for broader adoption of personalized nomogram approaches. However, due to differences in study design and patient populations, as well as the retrospective nature of our analysis, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and further validation is needed before these methods can be considered standard in routine clinical practice [28, 29].

Several limitations of the present study should be considered. First, the retrospective nonrandomized design represents an important limitation. Group allocation was based on the nomogram planning strategy used in routine clinical practice rather than on formal randomization or a predefined allocation protocol, which may have introduced selection bias and residual confounding despite the generally comparable baseline characteristics between groups. Although the two groups were enrolled contemporaneously during the same study period, thereby reducing the likelihood that temporal changes in workflow, surgeon experience, or perioperative management substantially influenced the results, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Second, the postoperative follow-up period was limited to 3 months. Although this duration is sufficient for evaluating early visual and refractive outcomes, longer follow-up is needed to assess long-term stability and potential late changes in higher-order aberrations. Third, subjective measures of visual quality, including glare, halos, and contrast sensitivity, were not evaluated and should be considered in future studies to provide a more comprehensive assessment of patient-reported outcomes [22, 23].

In conclusion, SMILE planned with a VISULYZE-generated nomogram was associated with differences in early visual, refractive, and optical outcomes compared with conventional nomogram planning. Findings related to astigmatic axis deviation should be interpreted cautiously, as they reflected numerical trends rather than statistically confirmed between-group differences. Overall, these findings suggest a possible association between nomogram planning strategy and early postoperative refractive predictability and optical outcomes following SMILE. Due to the retrospective, nonrandomized study design, these findings indicate associations rather than causal effects, and further prospective studies are needed to confirm the observations.
