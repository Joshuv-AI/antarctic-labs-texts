Effects of early renal replacement therapy on the outcomes of critically ill patients with acute kidney injury: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Qinghua Wang, Lijun Li, Rongshan Li, Yankun Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the efficacy of early renal replacement therapy (RRT) in critically ill patients with acute kidney injury (AKI).Methods:Keywords included early initiation, AKI, and RRT. The inclusion criteria were based on the population, intervention, comparator, outcomes, and study designs. An electronic search was conducted, and the following databases were used: PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CINAHL, and ClinicalTrials.gov. The primary outcome was 28-day mortality.Results:Twenty six studies were eligible for this meta-analysis. The pooled 28-day mortality in 25 studies was 44.7% (1770/3956) and 47.8% (1851/3875) in early and late RRT groups, respectively, and a significant difference was observed between groups (risk ratios [RR] = 0.81; 95% confidence intervals [CI] = 0.72–0.91; I2 = 73%). No significant difference was observed in RRT dependence rate (RR = 1.21; 95% CI = 0.99–1.49; I2 = 28%), recovery of renal function (RR = 1.35; 95% CI = 0.99–1.84; I2 = 91%), and duration of RRT (standard error of the mean = 0.23; 95% CI = −0.94–1.40; I2 = 99%). Subgroup analyses showed that early RRT was associated with lower mortality in surgical patients (RR = 0.60; 95% CI = 0.44–0.80; I2 = 74%) and in single-center settings (RR = 0.64; 95% CI = 0.52–0.79; I2 = 64%).Conclusions:For critically ill patients with AKI, early initiation of RRT can reduce 28-day mortality by 19% (relative risk reduction of 19%, absolute risk reduction of 3.1%) compared to late initiation, a statistically significant difference. However, clinicians should be alert to the possible adverse effect such as hypotension, catheter-related infection, and hypophosphatemia of early RRT.

1. Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is an independent risk factor and a common clinical complication in critically ill patients.[1,2] Results of an epidemiological survey published in the Lancet in 2019 showed that the incidence of AKI was approximately 10% to 15% in hospitalized patients and over 50% in intensive care patients.[3] At present, there is no effective progress in the drug treatment for AKI.[4] Extracorporeal renal replacement therapy (RRT), represented by intermittent hemodialysis (IHD), sustained low-efficiency dialysis, and other mixed forms of RRT, as well as continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), are still commonly used in critically ill patients with severe AKI.

However, despite the increasing availability of RRT options for patients with different clinical characteristics of AKI, there is still a lack of conclusive evidence for the superiority of any RRT model in terms of survival rate.[5,6] Whether or not to initiate, the timing of initiation, dose adjustment, mode of RRT, and choice of different anticoagulant methods all contribute to avoid serious complications and improve clinical outcomes, which currently requires the judgment of experienced clinicians.

Major clinical applications of RRT include the treatment of life-threatening complications of acute AKI, such as severe hyperkalemia, pulmonary edema, refractory metabolic acidosis, uremic pericarditis, and uremic encephalopathy.[7] However, there is no conclusion on the appropriate time to start RRT for these indications. In clinical practice, the start time is often individualized depending on the experience of clinicians.[8] Conflicting results from previous studies evaluating the optimal timing of RRT initiation have also led to differences in clinical practice in treating AKI.[9] The following multicenter RCTs are also shown that there is currently no uniform conclusion on this issue. Early initiation of RRT therapy was positively correlated with fluid and electrolyte balance, acid-base homeostasis, and uremic toxin clearance.[9,10] A recent multicenter trial found a possible survival benefit in critically ill AKI patients with accelerated initiation compared to a standard initiation RRT strategy, but this is likely to depend on the definition of the early initiation of RRT.[11]

The timing of initiating RRT in critically ill patients with AKI remains a topic of significant debate. This uncertainty is primarily driven by conflicting evidence from studies of varying scale and design. While several earlier, smaller randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies suggested a potential survival benefit with an early RRT strategy,[12,13] recent large, high-quality RCTs such as the Standard versus Accelerated Renal Replacement Therapy in Acute Kidney Injury (STARRT-AKI) and Initiation of Dialysis Early Against Late in the Intensive Care Unit trials have challenged this notion. The STARRT-AKI trial, for instance, demonstrated no significant difference in 28-day or 90-day mortality between accelerated and standard strategy groups.[14] Moreover, it raised safety concerns by reporting a higher rate of adverse events in the accelerated strategy group (23.0% vs 16.5%), including complications from vascular access, catheter-related infections, and hypophosphatemia.[14,15] This direct contradiction between earlier promising findings and recent neutral results from large RCTs creates a state of clinical equipoise. Therefore, an updated meta-analysis that incorporates these latest high-quality evidences is crucial to reconcile these discrepancies and provide more definitive conclusions.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Research design

The present meta-analysis was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement.[16]

2.2. Search strategy and data sources

The data sources include these electronic databases: PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CINAHL, and ClinicalTrials.gov (up to October 25, 2025). The following keywords were used: (earl* or accelerat* or tim* or Initiation) AND ((acute kidney) or (acute renal) or anuria or oliguria or (organ failure)) AND (hemodialysis or (renal replacement) or hemofiltration or hemodiafiltration or dialy*). There was no language limitation, and we also retrieved articles by manual screening.

2.3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were based on the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study designs (PICOS) structure: (P): population with a diagnosis of AKI; (I) patients receiving any form of RRT at an early stage, including continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), intermittent hemodialysis (IHD), and sustained low-efficiency dialysis (SLED). Definitions of early RRT need to be provided; (C) RRT was performed later than the intervention group; (O) mortality and safety data; (S) analytical studies or experimental studies. Besides, we searched all references in relevant articles and reviews to get other eligible studies. Reviews, conference abstracts, case reports, letters, and animal trials were excluded from this study.

2.4. Data extraction

The following information was included: the authors of the articles, publication year, study location, study design, study site, study population, RRT mode, number of patients with sepsis, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment scores, and definition of early RTT. The primary outcome was 28-day mortality, and the secondary outcomes were renal function recovery, survivor dependence on RRT, recovery of renal function, duration of RRT, and adverse events. Information was extracted from eligible studies by 2 authors independently. Disagreements were resolved by discussion among all authors.

2.5. Data analysis

The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool was used to assess the quality of the risk of bias of the included studies.[16] The software Review Manager, version 5.4.1 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK) and StataSE 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station) were selected to perform the statistical analysis. The Cochrane Q and I2 statistics were used to test the heterogeneity among all studies. I2 > 50% indicates the existence of heterogeneity, .10 of the P-value or 50% of the I2 was considered the critical value of heterogeneity. The fixed and random effects models were used to analyze the homogenous and heterogeneous dataset. Combined risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used for outcome analyses.

To explore sources of heterogeneity, we performed meta‑regression analyses across characteristics including different study population (mixed/surgical), study site (multicountry/Europe/Asia/North America/South America/Oceania), study center (multicenter/single center), modality of RRT (Mixed/CRRT/IHD), portion of patients with sepsis and definition biomarkers of early RRT (serum urea/urine output/BUN/time after randomization), and then conducted subgroup analyses for factors identified as potential contributors to heterogeneity to assess their impact on the pooled effect estimates. We did a sensitivity analysis with the leave-one-out method in Stata to assess the robustness of the results. Publication bias was analyzed by funnel plot. A probability value below .05 was regarded as statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Study selection and study characteristics

In sum, 8397 articles were identified in electronic and manual searches. However, 2315 articles were excluded for duplication. In addition, 5245 records were excluded after reviewing the title and abstract, and we excluded 32 records after reviewing the full text of 58 articles. Finally, 26 articles[11-14,17-37] were included in this meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

3.2. Study quality

Figure 2 shows the potential risk of bias in the included studies. Assessed by the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool, all of the 26 studies obtained acceptable quality evaluation scores (5–6 points; Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R487). The only caveat is that none of the included studies blinded participants and personnel due to the specificity of the intervention, which leads to potential performance bias. In addition, 12 studies did not mention the details of random sequence generation, adding to concerns about selection bias.

3.3. Primary outcome

The pooled 28-day mortality in the included 25 studies was 44.7% (1770/3956), and 47.8% (1851/3875) in early and late RRT groups, respectively, and a significant difference was observed between groups (RR = 0.81; 95% CI = 0.72–0.91; I2 = 73%; Fig. 3). The funnel plot was used to demonstrate the publication bias (Fig. 4).

3.4. Meta regression and subgroup analysis

We performed regression analyses for the primary endpoint, which identified differences between study population (P = .015) and study center (P = .004) as the main source of between‑study heterogeneity. Accordingly, subgroup analysis was performed based on these 2 factors.

In 10 studies with only surgical participants, the early RRT group’s mortality was lower (RR = 0.60; 95% CI = 0.44–0.80; I2 = 74%; Fig. 5). In 14 single center studies, the early RRT group’s mortality was lower than the late group’s (RR = 0.64; 95% CI = 0.52–0.79; I2 = 64%; Fig. 6).

3.6. Secondary outcomes

In 8 studies recorded the RRT dependence rate in survivors on day 28, no significant difference was observed between early and late RRT groups (RR = 1.21; 95% CI = 0.99–1.49; I2 = 28%; Fig. 8). No significant difference was observed in 9 studies reported the rate of recovery of renal function (RR = 1.35; 95% CI = 0.99–1.84; I2 = 91%; Fig. 9). The duration of RRT in the hospital was reported in 10 studies, a similar result was found in the 2 groups (standard error of the mean = 0.23; 95% CI = −0.94–1.40; I2 = 99%; Fig. 10).

3.7. Adverse events

This meta-analysis assessed the rate of hemorrhage, hypotension, arrhythmia, catheter-related infection, hypokalemia, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, and hypophosphatemia. As a result, higher risks of hypotension, catheter-related infection, and hypophosphatemia was observed in early RRT groups.

The overall effect of 6 studies showed the early RRT group had a higher risk of hypotension than the late RRT group (RR = 1.40; 95% CI = 1.21–1.61; I2 = 12%). The early RRT group had a higher catheter-related infection rate than the late RRT group shown by the overall effect of 4 studies (RR = 1.45; 95% CI = 1.04–2.03; I2 = 0%). According to the overall effect of 3 studies, the rate of hypophosphatemia was higher in the early RRT group than in the late group (RR = 1.80; 95% CI = 1.22–2.65; I2 = 53%). No significant differences were observed between early and late groups when it comes to hemorrhage (RR = 0.88; 95% CI = 0.68–1.13; I2 = 0%) in 10 studies, arrhythmia (RR = 1.08; 95% CI = 0.86–1.37; I2 = 46%) in 8 studies, hypokalemia (RR = 1.02; 95% CI = 0.80–1.31; I2 = 0%) in 4 studies, and hyperkalemia (RR = 0.27; 95% CI = 0.01–5.85; I2 = 79%) in 2 studies. The pooled analysis could not perform in the metabolic acidosis group since only 1 study reported the risk of these adverse events (RR = 0.54; 95% CI = 0.33–0.88; P = .01).

4. Discussion

This meta-analysis included 7933 AKI patients receiving RRT therapy, with 4004 patients in the early RRT group and 3929 in the late group. This comprehensive meta-analysis, incorporating recent high-quality evidence, reveals a complex and nuanced picture regarding the timing of RRT initiation in critically ill patients with AKI. While the pooled analysis of 25 studies suggests a potential survival benefit associated with an early RRT strategy, this overall finding is characterized by substantial heterogeneity and must be interpreted with caution in the context of study-level characteristics and recent landmark trials. There were significant differences in study centers (single or multicenter), and RRT modality (CRRT, mixed).

Our pooled result contrasts with the neutral findings of large, multicenter randomized trials such as the STARRT-AKI trial,[14] which found no overall mortality benefit from accelerated initiation of RRT. Several factors likely account for this discrepancy. First, the apparent benefit in our meta-analysis was chiefly driven by single-center and smaller studies, which are more vulnerable to selection bias, center-specific practice patterns, and small-study effects. Second, there is marked heterogeneity in how “early” RRT was defined across studies. Large RCTs including STARRT-AKI primarily used time-from-eligibility thresholds, whereas several single-center studies that reported benefit used physiologic or biochemical triggers (e.g., urine output or BUN thresholds). In our subgroup analyses, studies using BUN- or urine-output–based definitions showed a consistent signal of benefit, whereas time-based definitions did not. Third, differences in case-mix (e.g., proportion of surgical patients), RRT modality (CRRT vs intermittent or mixed), and local expertise may further modify treatment effects. Taken together, these findings suggest that the null result of STARRT-AKI does not wholly negate the possibility that carefully selected patients – identified by physiologic markers rather than arbitrary time windows – might derive benefit from earlier RRT. However, given the strength and generalizability of STARRT-AKI, our pooled estimate should be viewed cautiously and interpreted as hypothesis-generating regarding specific subgroups and initiation criteria.

Moreover, the mortality rate of RRT in actual clinical practice is closely related to the location of patients, the selection of RRT modality, and the definition of early start RRT. In previous meta-analyses, only RCT designs were included; most were multicenter studies. However, most of the newly included studies (7/11) in our meta-analysis were retrospective observational studies performed in single center; therefore, the sample representativeness and results extrapolation were inferior to multicenter studies. In addition, subgroup analysis was conducted to explain our result further and explore why so many single-center studies suggest the advantage of early RRT.

Subgroup analysis suggested lower mortality with early RRT among surgical patients; however, these results stem from studies with very high heterogeneity. This finding is partly consistent with prior meta-analyses that included a higher proportion of surgical cohorts,[38-41] but differences in surgical case mix, timing definitions and RRT modality likely account for inconsistent results. Therefore, the apparent benefit in surgical populations remains uncertain and requires confirmation by larger, methodologically harmonized multicenter studies. In addition to this, different from previous studies,[38] subgroup analysis of patients who only received CRRT therapy provided lower mortality in early RRT groups. Although this may be related to the fact that more participants in the included studies selected CRRT were surgical patients, the effect of early RRT mode selection on mortality still needs to be explored in more high-quality literatures. Another important result was the initiation biomarkers researchers used to define the early RRT, including serum urea, urine output, BUN, and time after randomization.

In view of the heterogeneity of RRT start time definitions in these relevant articles, this meta-analysis innovatively analyzed the differences in AKI mortality in studies using different biomarkers to define RRT initiation. The urine output group and BUN group showed lower mortality in early RRT patients. This also confirms the results of the previous multicenter RCTS.[11] Furthermore, the definition of “early” RRT emerged as a critical modifier of the treatment effect. The mortality benefit was particularly associated with definitions based on urine output and BUN criteria, rather than time-based criteria commonly used in large RCTs. This indicates that physiologic biomarkers of AKI severity might be more effective than arbitrary time thresholds in identifying patients who are most likely to benefit from early intervention. This crucial distinction highlights that how we define “early” is as important as when we initiate therapy. Besides, the literatures included in previous[38] meta-analyses did not include studies on BUN as the biomarker of early RRT initiation. Moreover, studies that selected BUN as the biomarker of early RRT initiation all confirmed that early RRT improved the mortality of patients. Although the relevant literature is limited, there is still literature pointing out a strong correlation between nitrogen loss and BUN in patients receiving CRRT under specific treatment conditions.[42]

The profound heterogeneity in defining “early” RRT across studies is not merely a methodological nuisance but likely a key determinant of the conflicting results observed in the literature. Our analysis incorporated studies with a spectrum of initiation criteria, and examining their individual outcomes reveals instructive patterns. The study by Moreira et al, which employed a strict time-based criterion (initiation within 24 hours of severe AKI diagnosis), found no significant difference in 28-day or 76-day mortality between early and late groups, despite the early group having higher illness severity.[35] This aligns with the neutral findings of large RCTs like STARRT-AKI that also relied heavily on time-from-eligibility. In stark contrast, the study by Chon et al defined “early” using a combined physiologic and time-based approach (Risk, Injury, Failure, Loss, and End stage classification (RIFLE)-I criteria within 24 hours of sepsis onset) and demonstrated a significant survival benefit at 28 days for the early group.[12] Similarly, Oh et al found that earlier initiation relative to vasopressor support (Tvaso-CRRT < 24 hours) was an independent predictor of survival.[37] This dichotomy suggests that definitions anchored to specific physiologic milestones of AKI severity (e.g., reaching a certain RIFLE stage or the need for vasopressors) or markers of volume status (e.g., urine output) may be more effective in identifying a patient phenotype that derives genuine benefit from timely RRT, compared to arbitrary time thresholds alone. Therefore, the pooled estimate of our meta-analysis should be interpreted as an average of these varying effects, where the signal for benefit is likely driven by the subset of studies that used more physiologically nuanced “early” definitions. This observation moves the clinical question beyond “if” early RRT works, to “for whom” and “based on what trigger” it might be beneficial.

No differences were observed in the recovery of renal function, RRT dependency, or duration of RRT in the early and late groups. This result is consistent with the results of previous RCT meta-analyses. However, in another small sample meta-analysis, better renal recovery was associated with early RRT.[43] Previous studies have shown that an independent predictor of renal failure in RRT patients does not include RRT initiation time. Considering the credibility of the small sample meta-analysis, we stick to the current analysis results that early RRT was not associated with greater renal recovery, lower dialysis dependence, and shorter RRT duration.

The safety of early initiation of RRT has been a concern for clinicians and researchers. Multiple studies and meta-analyses[44] have suggested that early RRT increases the risk of replacement therapy-related adverse events. Several common adverse events during RRT were evaluated in our meta-analysis. The incidence of hemorrhage, arrhythmia, hypokalemia, and hyperkalemia were similar between groups. However, the early RRT group experienced the higher risks of hypotension, catheter-related infection, and hypophosphatemia than the late RRT group. Previous meta-analyses also point to the same risk. The decision to initiate RRT early must therefore balance the potential for benefit in specific subpopulations against the consistently higher risks of hypotension, catheter-related infection, and hypophosphatemia that we observed. This risk-benefit calculus favors a cautious, individualized approach rather than a blanket policy of early RRT for all.

4.1. Limitations

Although several large randomized controlled trials of reasonable quality were included in this meta-analysis to improve the level of evidence, there are still several concerning limitations. The inclusion of studies showed significant differences in the definition of “early RRT” (time window, biochemical threshold, or AKI stage, etc), which was the main source of heterogeneity in this study and limited the determination of a single optimal initiation point; the high proportion of single-center studies, along with differences between single-center and multi-center subgroup analyses, suggests potential biases due to center effects and practice preferences, which could affect extrapolation; most of these studies are retrospective and are susceptible to confounding effects. Clinicians may prioritize initiating RRT earlier for patients with better prognoses or higher expected benefits, thus limiting causal interpretation; current evidence supports the potential benefit of initiating RRT earlier in similar populations, but it does not yet provide a uniform optimal timing for clinical practice. More large-scale studies with uniform definitions, rigorous randomization, and multiple centers are needed to validate this.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, this meta-analysis does not support a universal strategy of early RRT for all critically ill patients with AKI. The potential survival benefit appears to be highly context-dependent, primarily observed in surgical populations and single-center settings, and may be contingent upon using specific physiologic biomarkers (like urine output or BUN) to define RRT initiation. Clinicians should be aware that this potential benefit comes with a quantifiable increase in the risk of procedure-related complications. Future guidelines and practice should move beyond the simple “early versus late” dichotomy and towards a more personalized algorithm that integrates patient phenotype, clinical context, and biomarker-driven triggers to identify the optimal timing of RRT for individual patients.
