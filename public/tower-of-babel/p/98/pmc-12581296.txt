Bleeding and thrombotic events in patients with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia: a two-decade single-center experience in Thailand
Authors: Yotsathon Chantarawichian, Bundarika Suwanawiboon, Theera Ruchutrakool, Yingyong Chinthammitr, Tarinee Rungjirajittranon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHeparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is a prothrombotic disorder with potential bleeding complications, yet data from Asian populations are scarce.ObjectivesOur study aims to determine bleeding and thrombotic incidence and identify associated factors in Thai HIT patients.MethodsWe retrospectively and prospectively studied patients with suspected or confirmed HIT at Siriraj Hospital (January 2004‒December 2024). Fondaparinux was the sole non-heparin anticoagulant. Suspected HIT required a positive immunoassay or platelet aggregation test.ResultsForty-one patients (mean age, 66.8 years) were included, of whom 33 had confirmed HIT. Five had thrombosis, 16 (39%) required intensive-care-unit admission, and 29 (70.7%) had renal impairment. Among these 29, 13 developed new-onset acute kidney injury, and 16 had pre-existing renal disease (14 progressed to acute kidney injury). Most received fondaparinux (n = 25), whereas 5 received regional citrate, 1 warfarin, and 10 no anticoagulation. Overall mortality was 29.3%. One non-anticoagulated patient developed pulmonary embolism. Bleeding incidence was 1.22% per day, affecting 11 patients (26.8%), of whom 10 had renal impairment and 7 met International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria for major bleeding. Bleeding rates did not differ between confirmed and suspected HIT (24.2% vs. 37.5%; P = .658). Pre-existing chronic kidney disease (hazard ratio, 2.78; 95% CI, 0.84‒9.26; P = .095) and sustained low-efficiency dialysis (hazard ratio, 4.60; 95% CI, 1.50‒14.09; P = .008) were associated with bleeding.ConclusionsIn this Thai cohort, HIT conferred a high bleeding risk, especially in patients with renal impairment. These findings underscore the need for renal-safe non-heparin anticoagulants in resource-limited settings.Trial registrationTCTR20241227001.Keywords: Asian, Bleeding, Fondaparinux, Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, Thrombosis

Introduction

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an immune-mediated condition that fosters thrombogenesis [1]. Thrombosis rates range from 20% to 60%, even among patients treated with non-heparin anticoagulants [2]. Despite this hypercoagulable state, bleeding remains a serious concern. Kuter et al. reported a major bleeding rate of 36% in HIT patients, increasing to 48% in those with HIT and thrombosis [3]. Non-heparin anticoagulants elevate bleeding risk, alongside other factors such as intensive-care-unit (ICU) admission, severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 25 × 109/L), and renal dysfunction [4].

Renal dysfunction, particularly in patients undergoing renal replacement therapy, increases the likelihood of HIT. These patients are frequently exposed to heparin to prevent clot formation in dialysis circuits. Although low-molecular-weight heparin carries a lower HIT risk than unfractionated heparin, its use is contraindicated in renal dysfunction due to impaired clearance [5, 6]. Various non-heparin anticoagulants exist for suspected or confirmed HIT, but their availability can be restricted in some regions. At our center, fondaparinux—primarily eliminated via the kidneys—is the only parenteral non-heparin anticoagulant. While many studies confirm its safety and efficacy in HIT, its real-world use in patients with renal dysfunction warrants caution because of drug accumulation and increased bleeding risk [7–10].

Most HIT data come from Western populations. However, evidence for Asian populations, who generally exhibit a lower thrombotic risk in non-HIT contexts, is limited [11]. This study aimed to assess the incidence of thrombosis in HIT and the incidence of bleeding, with particular focus on renal dysfunction in a setting where fondaparinux is the sole non-heparin alternative. We also investigated factors associated with bleeding complications. Our findings may help guide management in resource-limited settings with similar constraints.

Methods

Study design and participants

This ambispective, single-center cohort study was conducted at Siriraj Hospital, Thailand’s largest tertiary-care hospital, between January 1, 2004, and December 31, 2024. It was registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20241227001) on December 27, 2024 and approved by the institutional review board and ethics committee. Informed consent was waived for the retrospective component due to data de-identification, whereas prospectively enrolled patients provided consent. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Terminology

The 4Ts score (Thrombocytopenia, Timing of platelet count fall, Thrombosis or other sequelae, and oTher causes of thrombocytopenia) is a validated clinical scoring system for estimating the pretest probability of HIT. Patients with confirmed acute HIT were defined as those with a 4Ts score ≥ 4 and positive results on both immunoassay and functional assay [12]. In contrast, patients with a 4Ts score ≥ 4 and a positive result on either the immunoassay or the functional assay were classified as having suspected HIT. This distinction reflected intermittent limitations in access to comprehensive laboratory testing at our center.

The immunoassay employed was an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; Zymutest HIA IgG, Hyphen BioMed, Neuville-sur-Oise, France). Heparin-induced platelet aggregation served as the functional assay, wherein platelet-rich plasma from a healthy volunteer (blood group O) was incubated with the patient’s serum for 1 h. Low-dose heparin (0.1 and 0.5 U/mL), high-dose heparin (100 U/mL), and normal saline were subsequently added. Platelet aggregation was assessed by light transmission using an AggRAM Analyzer (Helena Laboratories, Beaumont, TX, USA).

Renal status was classified using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula [13]. Patients with a glomerular filtration rate below 30 mL/min/1.73 m² were categorized as having renal insufficiency. Bleeding outcomes were defined according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria [14]. Major bleeding included fatal bleeding, bleeding in a critical organ or area, or bleeding that caused a hemoglobin drop of ≥ 2 g/dL or required transfusion of 2 or more units of whole blood or red cells. Thrombotic events were defined as any clinically suspected arterial or venous thrombosis confirmed by imaging studies. Bleeding and thrombotic events were retrieved from medical records for the retrospective cohort and prospectively recorded in a registry for the prospective cohort.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults (age ≥ 18 years) who were suspected or confirmed to have HIT, with or without thrombosis, and were admitted to Siriraj Hospital. Patients with insufficient medical records, particularly those in the retrospective cohort, were excluded.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcomes were bleeding and thrombotic events among patients with HIT at our center. The secondary outcomes were the incidence of bleeding and thrombosis stratified by renal function, overall mortality, and factors associated with bleeding.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), depending on data distribution, and compared using the independent t-test or Mann‒Whitney U test.

The incidence of bleeding, thrombotic events, and mortality was presented as proportions with 95% CIs. Incidence rates were reported as events per 100 patient-days. To assess the relationship between clinical factors and outcomes, including bleeding and thrombosis, cumulative incidence functions were estimated while accounting for death as a competing event. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were calculated using Fine and Gray’s subdistribution hazard model to evaluate the effect of covariates on each event in the presence of competing risks.

All statistical analyses were performed using PASW Statistics, version 18.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA) and STATA, release 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). A two-tailed P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 41 patients with HIT were included, comprising 33 (80.5%) with confirmed HIT and 8 (19.5%) with suspected HIT (Fig. 1). Of these, 3 patients were enrolled prospectively. Their mean age was 66.8 ± 13.3 years, and 51.2% were female. Patients were admitted to the ICU (39.0%), general medical ward (39.0%), or general surgical ward (22.0%). Heparin was administered to 22 patients (53.7%) for therapeutic indications. Only 5 patients developed thrombosis (lower-extremity deep vein thrombosis, n = 2; pulmonary embolism, n = 1; catheter-related thrombosis, n = 1; and progressive arterial occlusion, n = 1). At HIT diagnosis, the mean hemoglobin level was 9.2 ± 1.6 g/dL. The median white blood cell count was 10.2 × 10⁹/L (IQR, 6.6‒16.0), and the median platelet count was 56.0 × 10⁹/L (IQR, 43.0‒73.0). The median prothrombin time was 14.8 s (IQR, 13.2‒16.1), and the median activated partial thromboplastin time was 33.8 s (IQR, 26.3‒49.7). The mean fibrinogen level was 382.9 ± 160.3 mg/dL, and the median D-dimer level was 5420 µg FEU/L (IQR, 2630‒8470). Thirty-three patients with confirmed HIT tested positive in both the immunoassay and, subsequently, the functional assay, whereas eight patients with suspected HIT were positive only in the heparin-induced platelet aggregation assay, as the immunoassay was not consistently available at our center. Fondaparinux was used in 25 patients (61.0%) at the time of HIT diagnosis. Ten patients (24.4%) received no thromboprophylaxis, 5 (12.2%) received citrate flush for extracorporeal circuit circulation, and 1 (2.4%) continued warfarin therapy. The median time to platelet normalization was 6 days (IQR 3–10). The median length of admission stay was 33 days (IQR 21–61) (Table 1).

Among the 41 patients, 29 (70.7%) had renal insufficiency, defined as a glomerular filtration rate below 30 mL/min/1.73 m² from any cause. Of these 29, 13 presented with acute kidney injury without prior kidney disease, 14 had acute-on-chronic kidney disease, and 2 had stable chronic kidney disease. At HIT diagnosis, 16 (55.2%) required renal replacement therapy. Initial dialysis modalities were continuous renal replacement therapy in 7 patients, intermittent hemodialysis in 7, and sustained low-efficiency dialysis in 2. Almost half (48.3%) transitioned to different dialysis modalities during their hospital stay based on clinical changes (Table 2).

Patients with renal insufficiency were slightly older than those without renal insufficiency (mean age, 67.7 ± 13.6 years vs. 64.6 ± 12.9 years; P =.509). ICU admission was more common among these patients (55.2%) than admission to the general medicine ward (34.5%) or surgical ward (10.3%; (P <.001). A marginally higher proportion received prophylactic (55.2%) rather than therapeutic (44.8%) anticoagulation. Their median creatinine level was 1.65 mg/dL (IQR, 1.24‒4.12). After HIT diagnosis, fondaparinux remained the most commonly used anticoagulant (62.1%), often at a reduced dose of 2.5 mg on alternate days. Local citrate flush was used in 17.2%, no anticoagulation in 17.2%, and warfarin in 3.4% (Table 1).

Clinical outcomes of patients

Among 41 patients, the overall bleeding complication rate after HIT diagnosis was 26.8% (11/41), with 63.6% (7/11) meeting the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria for major bleeding [14]. Renal insufficiency was present in 10 of these 11 patients, leading to a higher bleeding incidence (34.5%) compared with those with normal renal function (8.3%; P =.128). Major bleeding was likewise more common in those with renal insufficiency (20.7% vs. 8.3%; P =.651), although this difference was not statistically significant. The median time to bleeding was 12 days (IQR, 7‒19). No patients experienced bleeding events beyond 30 days after admission. Patients who bled more frequently required dialysis and ICU admission (43.8%) than general-ward care (16.0%), although this did not reach significance (P =.067). Bleeding rates were similar between confirmed and suspected HIT (24.2% vs. 37.5%; P =.658). Among those receiving fondaparinux, 28.0% (7/25) experienced bleeding events, nearly all with underlying renal impairment. Only 1 patient (3.4%) with renal insufficiency developed thrombosis post-diagnosis, presenting with a fatal pulmonary embolism confirmed by computed tomography pulmonary angiogram while off anticoagulation.

The overall mortality was 29.3% (12/41), with 91.7% of deaths occurring in patients with renal insufficiency (P =.073). Sepsis was the primary cause of death in 11 patients, and 1 patient died from pulmonary embolism. The median hospital stay was 33 days (IQR, 21‒61).

Incidence rates of all bleeding events

Because no bleeding events occurred beyond 30 days after admission, all incidence rates and hazard ratios were calculated within the first 30 days of follow-up. The overall incidence rate of all bleeding events in this study was 1.22 per 100 patient-days (95% CI, 0.67‒2.19; Fig. 2A). When stratified by renal status, patients with renal insufficiency had a higher incidence of bleeding (1.60 per 100 patient-days; 95% CI, 0.86‒2.97). The rate among patients with normal renal function was 0.36 per 100 patient-days (95% CI, 0.05‒2.54). However, this difference was not statistically significant (HR, 3.22; 95% CI, 0.39‒26.62; P =.279; Fig. 2B). Among patients with pre-existing renal disease, there was a trend toward a higher bleeding rate of 2.68 per 100 patient-days (95% CI, 1.28‒5.63). The corresponding HR was 2.78 (95% CI, 0.84‒9.26; P =.095; Table 3).

In the renal insufficiency group, those requiring renal replacement therapy had higher bleeding rates than those who did not undergo dialysis. Patients who started sustained low-efficiency dialysis at the time of HIT diagnosis had the highest bleeding incidence, at 7.41 per 100 patient-days (95% CI, 1.85‒29.62). The corresponding HR was 4.60 (95% CI, 1.50‒14.09; P =.008). Table 3 presents the bleeding incidence rates across different patient subgroups.

Factors associated with bleeding

Univariate analysis showed a trend toward an increased bleeding risk in patients with pre-existing renal disease (crude HR, 2.78; 95% CI, 0.84‒9.26; P =.095). In contrast, patients treated with sustained low-efficiency dialysis had a significantly higher risk of bleeding (crude HR, 4.60; 95% CI, 1.50‒14.09; P =.008). Neither ICU admission nor fondaparinux use was significantly linked to bleeding complications (HR, 1.57; 95% CI, 0.64‒3.81; P =.322; and HR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.29‒3.09; P =.922, respectively).

Multivariate analysis echoed this finding, showing that sustained low-efficiency dialysis use was associated with a trend toward higher bleeding risk after adjusting for pre-existing renal disease and type of anticoagulation (adjusted HR 3.66; 95% CI, 0.99–13.46; P= 0.051).

Discussion

In this study, the overall thrombosis rate was 12.2% among patients with HIT, which is lower than rates reported in previous studies (20%‒60%) [2–4, 15]. Notably, only 1 patient experienced recurrent or progressive thrombosis, and that individual had not received anticoagulant therapy. Similar observations have been made in Asian populations. Wang et al. reported a low incidence of HIT combined with thrombosis in Chinese patients [16], and Kotwal et al. found that only 1 of 11 Indian patients with probable HIT developed clinical thrombosis [17]. A key predictor of HIT, particularly of HIT with thrombosis, is high optical density on ELISA-based immunoassays [18]. However, few studies have identified additional clinical predictors. One investigation linked female sex, orthopedic or trauma admissions, and neoplastic disease to higher thrombotic risk in HIT patients [19]. Our results suggest that ethnicity may also influence thrombotic risk, as Asian patients in both our cohort and prior studies displayed lower thrombosis rates. However, this observation should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size. In addition, the use of Fondaparinux in our cohort may have contributed to the lower thrombosis rate, as previous studies have shown that it is associated with a lower risk of recurrent thromboembolism compared with other anticoagulants [20]. Finally, the limited use of screening Doppler ultrasonography at our center may have led to underestimation of asymptomatic thrombotic events.

Although most HIT research emphasizes thrombotic complications, bleeding events remain underreported, especially in Asian populations. In our study, bleeding rates were notable and similar to those described in Western cohorts. Interestingly, patients without functional assay confirmation had comparable bleeding risks to those with confirmed HIT. We also found a trend toward higher bleeding rates in patients with impaired renal function, aligning with previous research [4, 21]. This issue was especially pronounced in patients requiring dialysis, where the choice of dialysis modality seemed influential. Those diagnosed with HIT and initiated on sustained low-efficiency dialysis appeared to have higher bleeding rates, possibly reflecting their more fragile clinical status. Patients with renal insufficiency also showed impaired liver function tests, which may have further increased bleeding risk by reducing hepatic synthesis of coagulation factors. Importantly, chronic kidney disease is associated with increased thrombotic and bleeding risks independent of HIT, which may have contributed to the high bleeding event rates in our cohort, where 16 patients had pre-existing renal disease. The apparent association between sustained low-efficiency dialysis and bleeding should be interpreted with caution, as only two patients received this modality.

The selection of anticoagulant therapy also played a critical role. Fondaparinux was the primary anticoagulant in our cohort and is renally excreted, raising concerns about pharmacodynamic alterations in dialysis. Sustained low-efficiency dialysis’s slower clearance compared to intermittent hemodialysis can diminish drug elimination, potentially prolonging anticoagulant effects [22, 23]. In addition, shifting between dialysis modalities in unstable patients adds further complexity and may exacerbate variability in drug clearance.

Our findings suggest that bleeding rates in both suspected and confirmed HIT are relatively high, whereas thrombotic events appear less frequent than the rates reported in Western populations. Current guidelines recommend starting therapeutic-dose non-heparin anticoagulants in suspected HIT, with intermediate dosing as an option for patients at increased bleeding risk [12]. Given our results, it may be prudent to question whether the recommended intensity of anticoagulation is optimal for populations with a lower thrombotic burden. Integrating bleeding risk assessment into clinical practice could help identify patients who might benefit from individualized strategies, including mechanical thromboprophylaxis alone in select cases.

Managing HIT in patients with renal impairment, particularly those requiring dialysis, remains challenging—especially in resource-limited environments with restricted access to non-heparin anticoagulants. Further research is needed to clarify the incidence of bleeding and thrombosis in Asian patients with HIT and to establish effective dosing and treatment protocols that appropriately balance these competing risks.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may have introduced information bias, and some clinical data were missing. Second, the sample size was relatively small, although we enrolled all patients diagnosed with HIT at our center during the study period. Given the low incidence of HIT—particularly in our population—this cohort still offers valuable insights. Third, our analysis included both confirmed HIT cases and approximately one-quarter of suspected cases, as it was not always feasible to perform both immunoassays and functional tests in all patients. This limitation may have introduced heterogeneity into the study population. In addition, the absence of functional assays using washed platelets, rather than platelet-rich plasma, may have reduced the certainty of HIT diagnosis and could have influenced the observed associations. Fourth, the study spanned a 20-year period, during which changes in treatment protocols and diagnostic practices may have influenced outcome ascertainment. Fifth, the low use of Doppler ultrasonography for detecting asymptomatic thrombosis may have led to underestimation of the true thrombotic incidence. Similarly, minor bleeding events may have been underreported, particularly in the retrospective cohort. Lastly, the study was unable to identify risk factors for progressive thrombosis due to the very low number of thrombotic events in our population.

Nevertheless, our study contributes important data on bleeding and thrombotic outcomes in an Asian HIT cohort, where robust evidence remains scarce. It also highlights real-world management challenges in resource-limited settings that lack diverse non-heparin anticoagulants and face a high burden of renal impairment. The comparatively low thrombosis rate we observed in Asian patients reinforces the need for larger, region-specific studies and for treatment strategies tailored to this population.

Conclusions

Our study revealed a high rate of bleeding events among HIT patients, compared with relatively fewer thrombotic complications. This risk was more pronounced in those with renal impairment, especially individuals requiring renal replacement therapy. These findings underscore the need to balance thrombotic prevention with bleeding risks in this population.
