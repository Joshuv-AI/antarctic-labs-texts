Beaver Tail Liver: A Morphological Study of Variation in the Left Lobe of the Liver
Authors: Hetal Hareshkumar Modi, Hiteshkumar Muktilal Chauhan, Himanshu Kantibhai Prajapati, Jinesh Baldevbhai Rathod
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The liver, the largest gland in a wedge-shape, is situated in the right hypochondrium, and exhibits variation in its lobes. Knowledge of variations in the lobes of the liver is crucial for accurate radiological diagnosis and successful abdominal surgeries. The elongation of the left lobe of the liver has gained significant attention in segmentectomy and liver transplant surgeries. During October and November 2023, cross-sectional observational research was done on 53 human cadaveric liver specimens obtained from different medical colleges in Gujarat, India. The livers were meticulously examined to determine the prevalence of Beaver tail liver and measurements such as length, breadth, and thickness were recorded and statistically analyzed. The study revealed that out of the 53 livers examined, 29 (54.72%) were classified as normal, while 19 (35.85%) fell into Netter’s type 2 to 7 based on Netter’s classification. Interestingly, 5 (09.43%) livers exhibited an elongated left lobe, referred to as a “sliver liver,” which did not fit into any of the Netter’s types. The beaver tail liver was found to be longer and broader compared to normal liver of the same size and weight (p<0.001). This study highlights the wide range of variations that can be observed in the lobes of the liver. The findings of this study will serve as a valuable resource for radiologists, anatomists, and surgeons, aiding them in accurate diagnoses and surgical procedures, thus minimizing the risk of misdiagnosis.

INTRODUCTION

The liver, a wedge-shaped gland, develops from an endodermal hepatic bud, sprouting from the distal part of the foregut loop. It almost fills the abdominal cavity during the third month of intrauterine life. During the embryonic period, it serves as the site of hemopoiesis but is eventually replaced by the spleen and bone marrow.1 In early embryonic life both the right and left lobes of the liver are uniform in size. However, in adult humans, the left lobe tends to be smaller; either due to atrophy or reduced growth.2 This discrepancy in size can be attributed to the right fetal portal vein connected to the umbilical vein in a smooth right-hand curve, allowing the right lobe to continue development. On the other hand, the left fetal portal vein and portal trunk form an acute angle, potentially causing a circulatory disadvantage for the left vascular lobe. Consequently, the left lobe may not keep pace with the growth of the right lobe and may decrease in size.34

Hypoxemia and portal blood flow are linked with liver hypertrophy. Apart from the embryological cause, other variables related to liver enlargement include liver cirrhosis, lower age, insulin or glutamine supplementation, and surgical or radiological procedures involving portal or hepatic vein embolization.56

The liver may have accessory lobes such as Reidel’s lobe, which refers to an elongation of the anterior border of the right lobe.7 Another rare variant is the beaver tail liver or sliver of the liver, which involves an elongation of the left lobe towards the left hypochondrium, surrounding the spleen. This is a rare anatomical variant with normal liver parenchyma.89 According to Hammond,10 the elongated part of the left lobe is typically folded upon itself, incurved, and surrounds the spleen.

The beaver tail liver may be associated with nonspecific abdominal or epigastric pain due to stretching of abdominal organs and it is commonly found in females.1112 In cases of abdominal injury in the left hypochondrium, the spleen is usually affected, but if present, the beaver tail liver may also be injured. Splenic and liver tissue exhibit identical echogenicity and density on ultrasound and CT scan. Therefore, liver injury can be misdiagnosed as a subcapsular hematoma of the spleen, hemorrhage in the perisplenic area, splenic inflammation, or splenic tumor.1314 On CT scan, the Hounsfield unit (HU) of the liver (50 to 65 HU) is greater than that of the spleen (40 to 60 HU), but patients with mild hepatic steatosis may show decreased hepatic attenuation.81315 It is crucial to examine the perisplenic region for the presence of hepatic veins and/or portal veins using color Doppler to differentiate between splenic and hepatic tissue. Failure to recognize these differences can have serious consequences during invasive abdominal procedures.9

The current study is aimed to determine the prevalence, morphological features, and measurements of beaver tail liver, and compared it with normal livers of the same size.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

During October and November 2023, a cross-sectional observational study was conducted on 53 adult human cadaveric livers obtained during routine dissection in the anatomy department of various medical colleges in Gujarat, India. The study received approval from the institutional review board and ethics committee of the medical college and hospital under EC approval no. 412/2023, dated 12/09/2023.

The dissection process involved opening the abdomen through a midline incision, exposing the liver by incising the superficial fascia, abdominal muscles, and parietal peritoneum. The liver was carefully removed following guidelines according to Cunningham’s manual of practical anatomy.16 The specimens were preserved in a 10% formalin, and any liver with signs of disease, surgery, or damage were excluded from the study.

The livers were examined for their morphological characteristics. The anatomical left and right lobes of the liver were divided by the falciform ligament attachment, groove for ligamentum teres hepatis, and groove for ligamentum venosum. They were classified according to Netter’s classification type 1 to type 7 on morphological variations. In addition, the frequency of tongue-like elongation of the left lobe of the liver was recorded (Fig. 1). Detailed morphological features such as color, consistency, textures, length, breadth, and thickness of these beaver tail livers were measured and recorded according to specific reference points mentioned below. These sliver liver’s morphological features and measurements were compared with normal livers of identical weight, size, and shape.

1. Length

The measurement was taken from three different points to the apex of the left lobe using a soft measuring tape (Fig. 2). The mean and standard deviations were calculated.

L1: Extending from the upper end of the fissure for the ligamentum venosum to the apex of the left lobe.

L2: Extending from the tuber omentalis to the apex of the left lobe.

L3: Extending from the lower end of the fissure for the ligamentum teres to the apex of the left lobe.

2. Breadth

It was measured (above-downwards) from the following two sites using the vernier caliper (Fig. 3).

B1: At the base of the left lobe - adjacent to the fissure for the ligamentum teres and the ligamentum venosum.

B2: At the apex of the left lobe.

3. Thickness

It was measured (anteroposteriorly) from the following two sites using the vernier caliper (Fig. 3).

T1: Middle of the base of the left lobe (adjacent to the fissure for the ligamentum teres and the ligamentum venosum).

T2: Near the apex of the left lobe.

Analysis of collected data was done to calculate the mean, standard deviations, t-value, and p-value of length, breadth, and thickness of beaver tail liver and normal liver by using Epi Info™ for Windows, CDC, Atlanta, version 7.2.

RESULTS

All 53 livers were examined for their morphological characteristics. Out of these 53 livers, 29 (54.72%) livers were normal in appearance, and 19 (35.85 %) livers were of Netter’s type 2 to 7 as per Netter’s classification. In contrast, 5 (09.43%) livers exhibit elongation of the left lobe, which did not fit in any Netter’s type (Table 1).

The elongated part of the left lobe was observed to be flattened from above downwards. The color, consistency, texture, and histological structure of the beaver tail livers were found to be similar to that of normal livers. Other lobes and fissures of the beaver tail livers appeared normal.

The beaver tail livers had statistically significant length (p<0.001 for L1 and L2, p<0.05 for L3) and breadth (p<0.001 for B2) than normal livers of the same weight and size (Tables 2 and 3). However, there was no statistically significant difference in the breadth (B1) and thickness of the beaver tail livers compared to normal livers (Tables 3 and 4).

DISCUSSION

The left lobe of the liver is situated on the left side of the falciform ligament, fissure for ligamentum venosum and ligamentum teres hepatis. The liver is situated in the right hypochondrium, epigastrium, and left hypochondrium. When the left lobe of the liver elongates and crosses the left mid-clavicular line, it is considered a beaver tail liver.9

A fibrous appendix of the liver, composed of fibrous connective tissue, is also found at the left extremity of the left lobe.3 The histology of the elongated part of the left lobe in this research displayed the typical microscopic structure of the hepatic lobule, ruling out the possibility of being an appendix of the liver.

Abdalla et al.17 discovered in their study that in over 10% of patients, the left lobe constitutes less than 25% of the total liver volume. The remnant volume of the liver after transplantation surgery must be at least 30%. Anything less than 30% can threaten the donor’s life. If the volume of the left lobe is greater than the total volume of the liver, as seen in a beaver tail liver, the residual liver from a right lobe donor is likely to be sufficient.18

Kapoor et al.13 reported a case of a 67-year-old male patient with a history of cholecystectomy, whose initial ultrasound revealed fatty degeneration in couinad segment 4 and splenomegaly. Finally, an abdominal MRI revealed fatty degeneration of 3.5 cm at the surgical site, and coincidentally, a beaver tail liver was discovered.

Jones et al.19 reported a case of an elongated left lobe of the liver that was initially misdiagnosed as a subcapsular hemorrhage of the spleen. Rogulj et al.8 reported that a 2-year-old asymptomatic patient’s thoracic radiograph demonstrated a left paracardial shadow of obscured origin. But on further investigation through a multi-slice CT scan of the thorax and abdomen revealed that it was a beaver tail liver.

Beaver tail liver was present in 9 (12.86%) livers in Singh and Rabi,20 12.5% liver in Chaudhari et al.,21 14% liver in Dawani et al.,22 04% liver in Neginhal and Kulkarni,23 5(7.14%) liver in Mansur et al.,24 7 (9.33%) liver in Sharma et al.,25 9 (15%) liver in Arya et al.26 studies. In the present study, it was found in 5 (09.43%) livers, which is consistent with the prevalence reported in other studies.

The mean length of the beaver tail liver found by Sultana27 was 13.33 cm and the standard deviation (SD) was ±3.790. Baruah and Choudhury4 found 3 beaver-tailed livers with their mean lengths of 13.13 cm, 19.5 cm, and 13.43 cm, each with respective SD of ±3.790, ±5.246, and ±3.172. In the present study, the beaver-tail liver had a mean length of 12.4 cm with SD±2.69. The length of the beaver tail livers (L1 and L2) was found to be statistically significantly different (p<0.001) compared to normal livers of the same weight and size as indicated in Table 2.

Baruah and Choudhury4 noted that the beaver-tail liver had a breadth of 6.63 cm at the base and 4.23 cm at the apex. The thickness at the base and apex was measured to be 0.87 cm and 0.43 cm respectively. In the present study, the breadth of the beaver-tail liver at the base and apex was 8.30 cm and 3.94 cm respectively, while the thickness at the base and apex was 3.52 cm and 0.92 cm respectively. The breadth of the beaver tail livers (B2) was found to be statistically significantly different (p<0.001), however, there was no statistically significant difference in the breadth (B1) and thickness of the beaver tail livers compared to normal livers (Tables 3 and 4).

The beaver tail liver was typically flattened from above downward. The color, texture, and histological structure of the beaver tail liver resemble to the normal liver. In terms of dimensions, beaver tail liver is usually longer and wider compared to normal liver of equivalent size and weight. To gain deeper insights into the implications of the beaver-tail liver, it would be beneficial to expand this study through collaboration with radiologists and gastro-surgeons, utilizing ultrasound, CT scan, and assisted biopsy techniques.

In conclusion, elongated tongue-like expansion of the left lobe of the liver should be kept in mind when considering various radiological investigations or surgical procedures involving the perisplenic area. The knowledge of such variation is important for surgeons, radiologists, and anatomists to prevent misdiagnosis and provide appropriate therapeutic care.
