Unintentional Weight Loss Does Not Predict Poor Outcomes in Zenker Diverticulum
Authors: Ari D Schuman, Jacqui Allen, Mekibib Altaye, Milan R Amin, Semirra L Bayan, Peter C Belafsky, Brad W DeSilva, Shumon I Dhar, Dale C Ekbom, Aaron D Friedman, Mark Fritz, Glendon M Gardner, Elizabeth A Guardiani, Christopher Johnson, Jan Kasperbauer, Brandon Kim, Brittany N Krekeler, Maggie A Kuhn, Yue Ma, Lyndsay L Madden, Laura Matrka, Ross M Mayerhoff, Cyrus Piraka, Apoorva Ramaswamy, Clark A Rosen, Haley C Sibley, Meredith Tabangin, Carter Wright, Z Mike Yang, Vyvy N Young, Gregory Postma, Rebecca J Howell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveWeight loss is frequently considered a cardinal presenting symptom for patients with Zenker diverticulum (ZD). We hypothesized that weight loss in patients with cricopharyngeal muscle dysfunction (CPMD), with or without diverticula, was associated with the length of symptoms and size of diverticulum.MethodsProspective, multicenter observational study of CPMD patients with and without diverticula reporting unintentional weight loss (+WL) or no weight loss (−WL) prior to enrollment in the Prospective Outcomes of Cricopharyngeal Hypertonicity (POuCH) database. Complications and symptom response using a patient reported outcome measure, the Eating Assessment Tool (EAT‐10), were compared.ResultsOf 427 patients, 101 (23.6%) were +WL. Median weight loss was 14 pounds (interquartile range [IQR] 10–25). +WL cohort had a mean age of 71.5 (standard deviation [SD] 11.5) vs. 67.9 in −WL (SD 11.6, p = 0.007). Mean preoperative EAT‐10 score was 24.7 in +WL (SD 9.7) and 14.8 in −WL (SD 8.8, p < 0.0001). Higher preoperative weight loss was associated with higher preoperative EAT‐10 (p < 0.0001, Spearman's ρ = 0.43). Size of diverticulum was associated with WL (p = 0.003), but weight loss was highest in both large diverticula and CP bars. Duration of symptoms was not associated with weight loss (p = 0.30). Postoperative complications in +WL and −WL patients were similar (p = 0.78).ConclusionUnintentional weight loss is common with ZD. Patients with worse dysphagia are more likely to have more weight loss. Weight loss does not increase with the size of the diverticulum or length of symptoms. Surgery has a similar safety profile with greater improvement in dysphagia in patients reporting weight loss.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: dysphagia, outcomes, quality of life, Zenker diverticulum

Introduction

An early clinical indicator for presentation of Zenker diverticulum (ZD) and cricopharyngeal muscle dysfunction (CPMD) is weight loss. Degree of dysphagia, length of symptoms, and size of the diverticulum are also often used as clinical indicators of severity of the disease, which have been included in the literature since the earliest papers on hypopharyngeal diverticula written in the 19th century, and are mentioned in some form in many papers written since [1, 2, 3, 4]. Although published data vary, two studies indicate a correlation between size of pouch and dysphagia symptoms (CITE), whereas others show correlation between restricted UES opening and regurgitation with dysphagia in ZD [5, 6, 7, 8]. However, any one of these dysphagia symptoms from ZD, including sensation of UES opening or frequent regurgitation, can impact quality and degree of oral intake thus affecting the patient's ability to maintain a stable weight.

However, despite the supposed common knowledge of weight loss in this condition, there are few large cohorts showing the incidence of unintentional weight loss for patients presenting with ZD and CPMD. Siboni et al. demonstrated a 16.5% rate of preoperative weight loss in their paper on transoral stapling in 139 patients at a single institution [9]. A retrospective extension at the same institution had a similar 16.4% rate of weight loss [10]. Using national databases, weight loss as a predictor of complications found no association but had a weight loss rate of only 3.3%–5.4% without specificity on the amount or intentionality of weight loss [11, 12].

The Prospective Outcomes of Cricopharyngeus Hypertonicity (POuCH) collaborative has sought to characterize the symptoms and outcomes of ZD and CPMD on a large scale across multiple institutions, with a goal of expanding our understanding of these diseases in a more generalizable way. In an early version of this cohort, with 250 patients, a history of unintentional weight loss was seen in 64 patients (26.7%) [13]. Since then, the POuCH collaborative has expanded, offering a larger cohort of patients to study and the opportunity to more specifically analyze the role of weight loss. We sought to determine the incidence of preoperative unintentional weight loss in this multi‐institutional database, with a hypothesis that weight loss would be associated with the degree of dysphagia and the severity of the underlying pathology, measured by pouch size and duration of symptoms.

Methods

The Prospective Outcomes of Cricopharyngeus Hypertonicity (POuCH) collaborative is a prospectively collected database including 13 institutions. Patients are enrolled if they have a presenting diagnosis of CPMD or ZD. All patients included are 18 years old or older. All data from the cohort is kept in REDCap [14]. Every institution has obtained Institutional Review Board approval (IRB 2016‐6955). For every patient enrolled, demographic information is obtained, as well as initial imaging characteristics and preoperative factors including but not limited to unintentional weight loss. All patients have both index and postoperative patient‐reported outcome measures. In this study, the Eating Assessment Tool‐10 (EAT‐10) was used [15].

Unintentional weight loss in the year prior to enrollment is included in the questionnaire required from every patient at the time they enter the study. When a patient reports unintentional weight loss, the amount is also recorded. Weight loss was considered for this analysis both as a binary (patients with unintentional weight loss, +WL, and those without, −WL) and as a continuous variable based on the amount of weight loss in pounds, then analyzed as an outcome measure based on preoperative exposures such as esophagram findings, sociodemographic characteristics, and EAT‐10 scores. Cricopharyngeal bars were grouped with ZD under 1 cm. ZD were otherwise separated into groups of 1–2, 3–4, and 5–6 cm. In a second analysis, preoperative weight loss was considered as a binary exposure for postoperative outcomes, including both degree of improvement and complications. Statistical analysis was performed using parametric and non‐parametric methods as appropriate, including Pearson's and Spearman's correlation coefficients for the continuous outcome.

Results

A total of 427 patients were included in the study. Of these, 101 +WL were identified (23.6%). Median weight loss over the prior year was 14.0 pounds (interquartile range [IQR] 10–25) among 83 patients who reported the magnitude of weight loss. The +WL patients were significantly older than −WL at a mean of 71.5 years old (SD 11.5) compared with 67.9 (11.6) (p = 0.007). The proportion of male and female patients was similar in the two groups.

Patients who had lost weight had a higher mean preoperative EAT‐10 score at 24.7 (SD 9.8) compared with −WL 14.8 (8.8, p < 0.0001). Patients had symptoms for a similar length of time. Pouch size varied between +WL and −WL groups (p = 0.003). In the +WL group, the proportion of patients with CP bars (16.7%) was higher than the −WL group (11.0%). This was also true for large pouches (5–6 cm, 15.5% +WL vs. 4.6% −WL). The proportion of patients with 1–2‐cm pouches was larger in the −WL group (39.3% +WL vs. 47.0% −WL), as was the proportion of those with 3–4‐cm pouches (27.4% +WL vs. 33.7% −WL). Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort study of patients with ZD and CPMD, we found a high rate of unintentional weight loss at 23.7%, tracking with prior data published from the POuCH database [9, 10, 13]. The lowest rate reported in the literature to date was collected from registry data, which can provide vital broad assessments but relies heavily on prior documentation to adequately assess risk factors [11]. In contrast, while the 23.7% reported in this study is slightly higher than other retrospective single‐institution studies, those numbers are on a similar order of magnitude at 16.5% [9, 10, 16].

Expanding on this prior work, our study specifically examined the preoperative risk factors associated with weight loss, with the unique ability to assess weight loss not only as a binary but as a continuous variable. Older patients and patients with a higher degree of dysphagia reported on EAT‐10 scores were more likely to have weight loss. Further, the quantity of weight loss was directly correlated with the severity of preoperative dysphagia. Even when considering that weight loss is a question in EAT‐10 scores, the difference between the median scores (nearly 10 points) would still be six assuming all patients answered the question with the maximum score of four. Age was not statistically significant when considered as a continuous variable.

We originally hypothesized that the amount of weight loss would be associated with the size of diverticulum, where patients with larger diverticula would be more likely to have weight loss and have more weight loss. In contrast to this, patients who had weight loss had a bimodal distribution of pouch size, with larger proportions of both cricopharyngeal bars (including < 1‐cm pouches) and large pouches (5–6 cm or more). Weight loss was highest among patients with a CP bar or pouches under 1 cm. One explanation for the role of the cricopharyngeal bar may be other sources of dysphagia driving the weight loss, but patients with weight loss were no more likely to have an abnormal esophagram. Future evaluation will require manometry to analyze the role of dysmotility more specifically.

Patients experience the severity of their dysphagia differently, and prior work on correlation of dysphagia to findings on videofluoroscopic swallow studies has shown that the narrowness of the upper esophageal sphincter is associated with dysphagia severity more so than pouch size [6]. Of note, interquartile ranges (from the 25th to 75th percentile of the data) were overlapping across categories, suggesting similar amounts of weight loss regardless of the size of the pouch. Pouch size as measured by esophagram, either by radiologist or the enrolling surgeon, may also not be a perfect measurement given the limits of the test. However, given that the differences were seen in the largest and smallest groups, this is a more reliable finding than small changes between a 2‐ and 3‐cm pouch.

Considering the duration of symptoms, our initial hypothesis was that patients with a longer duration of symptoms were more likely to have weight loss. Again, contradicting our initial assumption, there were two groups. The highest amounts of weight loss among patients with symptoms for under 1 year or for more than 10 years. This may suggest two separate cohorts: those that present quickly due to severe symptoms or those that experience symptoms for a longer time but wait for the severity to be adequate (i.e., to have weight loss) before presenting for evaluation.

As a secondary goal, using the prospectively collected data, we also considered weight loss as an independent indicator for surgical outcomes. With weight loss as a potential indicator of malnutrition, we hypothesized that +WL may have more complications. However, postoperative outcomes were similar in +WL and −WL groups. There was a slight difference in postoperative EAT‐10 scores, but the median scores within the +WL and −WL groups were within normal limits. There was no increase in complication rates among those with weight loss. This is despite concerns that weight loss may suggest underlying malnutrition with subsequent poor surgical outcomes. Prior work on the role of nutrition in ZD suggested that patients with hypoalbuminemia as reported in the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program had equivalent complication rates [12]. In other head and neck surgery, multiple groups have found an association with poor wound healing outcomes and low prealbumin in microvascular free tissue transfer to the head and neck [17, 18]. However, more recent data from the nutrition literature suggests that albumin and prealbumin are more accurate markers of inflammation than nutritional status [19, 20]. Given the role of inflammation in malignancy, this may be a more helpful marker when planning surgery for cancer rather than a more indolent process such as CPMD or ZD.

In addition to malnutrition specifically, unintentional weight loss is often considered as an overall marker of frailty and sarcopenia in older adults [21, 22]. Frailty is the combination of factors resulting in vulnerability to medical and surgical complications associated with aging and declining physical function [23, 24, 25, 26]. Research across surgical disciplines, including Otolaryngology, has suggested frailty as a risk factor for poor outcomes in major surgeries, especially medical complications [27]. A recent paper from Bass et al. examined this in the National Inpatient Sample, considered frailty using an index that incorporated weight loss, and found no increase in surgical complications [28]. Medical complications (e.g., pneumonia) were increased in their cohort. Our prospectively collected data considering weight loss specifically does support the finding of equal surgical outcomes and complications. However, we did not find an increased rate of medical complications between those with and without weight loss.

The study is limited by the lack of available nutritional data or detailed information on functional status to allow for a nuanced understanding of patients' ability to recover after surgery. With that information, an algorithm for choosing patients for nutritional optimization, with alternate enteral access if needed, would be the next step. Our data on weight loss is self‐reported, as is the amount of weight lost. However, given that findings are similar across binary and continuous analysis, these self‐reported measures appear reliable. Extensive prior research has shown that a more comprehensive evaluation is needed to fully understand nutritional status and frailty in adults [29]. In line with this, our data suggest that weight loss alone is not an adequate factor to predict poor postoperative outcomes and may not represent frailty. While this is the largest prospective study on CPMD with and without diverticula, a more definitive conclusion on this may require a larger cohort, as adverse postoperative outcomes are relatively rare. The rates reported here are very similar at an 11.1% complication rate in +WL compared with 11.0% in −WL, leading to a reasonable conclusion that there is no association between weight loss and complications.

Conclusion

Among patients in the POuCH collaborative, unintentional weight loss is relatively common, occurring in 23.7% at presentation. Patients with weight loss are more likely to have more severe dysphagia on their preoperative evaluation. There is no association between increasing size of pouch or length of symptoms and the likelihood of preoperative weight loss. Postoperative outcomes are similar in those with and without weight loss, and no increased rate of complications is seen. Therefore, surgery has a similar safety profile with greater improvement in dysphagia in patients reporting weight loss.
