The effect of vitamin D supplementation on the liver function in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Mahdi Vajdi, Negin Nikrad, Mahsa Khajeh, Nooshin Noshadi, Mahsa Maroofi, Melika Darzi, Fatemeh Pourteymour Fard Tabrizi, Gholamreza Askari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmpirical studies examining the impact of vitamin D supplementation on the liver function in non-alcoholic fatty liver (NAFLD) disease patients have yielded incongruous outcomes. In this systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), we investigated the effect of vitamin D supplementation on liver enzymes (alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP)) in NAFLD patients.Methods A systematic literature search was conducted using scientific databases including PubMed/Medline, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane library and Scopus, through May 2026. The search strategy included terms for the interventions (vitamin D, cholecalciferol, calcitriol) and outcomes (ALT, AST, ALP, and GGT) in NAFLD patients. Weighted/standardized mean differences (WMD, SMD) and 95% CI were analyzed utilizing a random-effects model.ResultsOverall, 16 RCTs met our inclusion criteria. The current meta-analysis demonstrates a significant reduction in ALT (SMD: -0.45; 95% CI: − 0.87 to -0.04; P = 0.032), and ALP (WMD: -12.91 IU/L; 95% CI: − 18.75 to -7.08; P < 0.001). However, analysis of studies indicated a non-significant effect on AST, GGT. Subgroup analysis showed that the effect of vitamin D on AST is more pronounced in studies with a dose ≤ 3500 IU/day, duration > 12 weeks and using cholecalciferol (as opposed to calcitriol).ConclusionThe findings of this study indicate that vitamin D supplementation may be associated with improvements in ALT and ALP levels, while no significant effects were observed for AST and GGT. These results suggest a possible beneficial role of vitamin D in supporting liver function among patients with NAFLD; however, the evidence remains inconclusive. Future studies are needed to investigate the effect of vitamin D on health status in in NAFLD patients.PROSPERO codeCRD42024545499Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13098-026-02225-w.Keywords: Vitamin D, Liver function, Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Meta-analysis

Introduction

In the recent decades non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has been causing considerable concern as its global prevalence has raised to more than 30% in general population [1, 2], 70% in overweight and 75% in obese population [3]. This chronic disease is characterized by fat agglomeration in the liver in the absence of substantial alcohol intake [4], consisting of a wide spectrum starting from fat accumulation in more than 5% of hepatocytes to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis and its end stage manifestation cirrhosis, which may lead to liver transplant [5]. NAFLD-induced complications include life threatening events such as cardiovascular disease [6] and hepatocellular carcinoma [7, 8], highlighting the importance of comprehension of its pathogenesis and therapeutic methods.

The pathogenesis of NAFLD is associated with obesity, specifically visceral obesity, insulin resistance (IR), type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome [9] and chronic inflammation [10]. Evidence show that insulin resistance and obesity contribute to increased inflammation [11, 12], on the other hand IR may increase de novo hepatic lipogenesis, exacerbating the fat accumulation in the liver [13]. Lifestyle modifications including weight loss and physical activity successfully reduce steatosis and are considered as the primary recommended treatment in NAFLD patients [14, 15]. Moreover several drugs have been investigated in this field with some of them showing significant results but rising debates considering their adverse effects [16], nevertheless, there is currently no approved medicinal therapeutic intervention for NAFLD [17]. Due to controversies around drug prescription in NAFLD, nutritional adjuvant therapies such as supplementation with omega 3 fatty acids [18] and vitamin E [15] are widely investigated in clinical trials and practical medicine, and with the aim of finding efficient therapies for NAFLD more studies are being carried out in this area.

In the recent literature vitamin D has been well investigated and indicated as being the major determinative in calcium homeostasis [19]. Moreover, this fat-soluble vitamin has gained considerable interest due to its extra-skeletal functions such as regulating immune system [20, 21] which leads to its preventive role in adverse respiratory outcomes [22], as well as reducing inflammation and oxidative stress [23]. Notably, it has been receiving much attention as recent studies show how vitamin D deficiency may contribute to insulin resistance through its role in insulin signaling pathway, insulin secretion, indirect antioxidant properties, maintaining normal function of adipose tissue and anti-inflammatory functions [24, 25]. Since studies show lower serum vitamin D levels are associated with NAFLD [26, 27], considering favorable effect of vitamin D in reducing IR and inflammation makes it possible to evaluate it as an adjuvant nutritional treatment in NAFLD [28]. Although there has been vast literature examining the effect of vitamin D supplementation in ameliorating insulin resistance, studies examining its effect in NAFLD are limited and controversial [29–31], moreover several meta-analyzes has been carried out in this field [32, 33] but they don’t cover recent clinical trials and liver enzymes examined in those studies are limited, thus, this systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis was conducted to determine the effect of vitamin D supplementation on liver enzymes (alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP)) in NAFLD patients.

Methods

We carried out this systematic review and meta-analysis following the Cochrane Handbook for Systemic Reviews of Interventions [34] and the Guidelines of PRISMA [35] (Supplementary Table 1). The study protocol of the article was registered at The International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42024545499).

Search strategy

A systematic search in five electronic databases including Pubmed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane library and Scopus was conducted through May 2026 to find relevant randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The following mesh and non-mesh terms formed the search strategy: (Vitamin D OR calcitriol OR 1,25 (OH)2D3 OR ergocalciferol OR vitamin D2 OR vitamin D3 OR cholecalciferol) AND (nonalcoholic fatty liver disease OR NAFLD OR fatty liver OR fatty liver disease). There was no time or language restriction included in our search, furthermore, we had a manual search through the references of the related original studies to identify any additional articles that were missed through our primary database search.

Eligibility criteria

First, to determine the eligibility for inclusion, a screening was carried out by reviewing the titles and abstracts of the RCTs and was followed by a full-text review if necessary. Prior to data pooling and analysis multiple authors were involved in eligibility determination. The manuscript screening was independently conducted by two researchers (MV, BA) based on PICOS tool (participants, intervention, comparison, outcome and study design). Thus, studies had to meet the following inclusion criteria to be included in the meta-analysis: parallel or cross-over design RCTs, carried out on NAFLD patients, consisting of an intervention group receiving vitamin D supplementation and a vitamin D-free control group as a comparison, investigating the effect of vitamin D supplementation on liver enzymes including ALT, AST, GGT and ALP. Furthermore, the exclusion criteria consisted of the following items: any study designs except RCTs, inaccessible abstract or full text, participants including children, pregnant women or non-NAFLD patients, absence of a comparison group, presence of other supplementations in addition to vitamin D, unreported means and standard deviations (SD) for liver enzymes.

Data extraction

Data extraction from studies which met the inclusion criteria was performed by two independent researchers (MV, MK) using an exclusive extraction form, consisting of: first author’s name, country and year of the trial, study design, health status of the subjects, sample size, participants per group, mean age, duration of trial, type and dosage of administration for intervention and control groups and mean changes and their SDs of the outcomes related to our study. Wherever the outcome was not reported in the form of mean and its SD, it was calculated from the available reported outcome [including 95% confidence intervals (CI), standard error (SE), median and interquartile range (IQR)] by using a standardized formula [34].

Quality assessment

The risk of bias was assessed by two independent reviewers based on The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for RCTs. This tool assesses the risk of bias in RCTs across seven domains including random sequence generation, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessments, selective reporting, allocation concealment, incomplete outcome data, and other sources of bias. We will assign a judgment of “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear risk” for each domain based on the Cochrane guideline.

Statistical analysis

STATA, version 14 software (Stata Corp, College Station, Texas, USA) was used to conduct all the analyses regarding the changes of values between pre- and post-trial measures, while P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The mean and SD measures of the changes in serum level of liver enzymes (ALT, AST, ALP and GGT) were used to calculate the pooled effect sizes. Where the standard deviation was not reported, it was calculated manually using a standardized formula: SD2 = [(SD baseline2 + SD final2) – (2 R* SD baseline + SD final)] [36]. The pooled effect size of vitamin D supplementation on liver enzymes was estimated as weighted/standardized mean differences (WMD, SMD) with 95% confidence interval (95% CI) using random-effects model [37]. Q-test and I2 index were used to determine the heterogeneity between studies, while I2 > 50% is considered substantial heterogeneity [38]. Subgroup analyses were also carried out to indicate any additional sources of heterogeneity, with subgroups consisting of the following categories: mean age of participants, sex of participants, dosage of vitamin D supplementation, duration of intervention and number of participants. Moreover, we utilized “the leave-one-out method” [34], for sensitivity analysis to differentiate any source of heterogeneity. We utilized Egger’s regression, Begg’s test and contour-enhanced funnel plots to assess publication bias [39, 40].

Results

Literature search

The step-by-step procedure of study selection is depicted in a flow chart in Fig. 1. Using an extensive search strategy, 2,178 publications were retrieved from online databases of which 1,005 remained after the removal of duplicates. Following the review of titles and abstracts from 1,005 studies, 977 articles did not meet the inclusion criteria and were consequently excluded from further analysis. Twelve of the remaining studies were excluded from further consideration for full text analysis for the following reasons: participants of < 18 years old (n = 3), lack of control groups (n = 5); and combination of vitamin D with other drugs (n = 4). Finally, 16 studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. Of the studies examined, 16 explored the effect of vitamin D on ALT [30, 31, 41–54], six presented data on ALP [31, 44, 47–49, 54], 15 were on AST [30, 31, 41–45, 47–54], and six trials were on GGT [31, 42, 47, 51, 53, 54].

The publication years of the included studies spanned from 2014 to 2023, and the ages of individuals in these studies were between 27 and 66 years. Effect of vitamin D on ALT levels

The effect of the vitamin D supplementation on ALT was evaluated in 16 studies with 18 treatment arms (826 cases and 722 controls). When contrasted with the placebo group, the administration of vitamin D as an intervention resulted in a notable reduction in ALT levels (SMD: -0.45; 95% CI: − 0.87 to -0.04; P = 0.032; I2 = 93.1%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). According to the findings from the subgroup analysis, vitamin D supplementation led to a significant decrease in AST across all subgroups, except in RCTs with ≤ 70 subjects (supplementary Table 3).

Forest plot illustrating weighted mean difference and 95% confidence intervals for the impact of vitamin D on ALT

Effect of vitamin D on AST levels

An analysis of 15 studies, with 17 effect sizes (775 cases and 692 controls) that gauged AST levels after administering vitamin D supplementation, indicated no significant changes in AST levels (SMD: -0.60; 95% CI: − 1.19 to 0.00; P = 0.052; I2= 96.2%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). The subgroup analysis results indicated a significant reduction in AST levels in all subgroups following vitamin D supplementation, except RCTs with a sample size of 70 or less, and those using calcitriol as the intervention (supplementary Table 3).

Forest plot illustrating weighted mean difference and 95% confidence intervals for the impact of vitamin D on AST

Effect of vitamin D on ALP levels

Six studies, comprising seven treatment arms (287 cases and 292 controls), examined the impact of vitamin D supplementation on ALP levels and observed a significant decrease in ALP (WMD: -12.91 IU/L; 95% CI: − 18.75 to -7.08; P < 0.001; I2= 90.8%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). The observed heterogeneity in the results could be due to the sample size, vitamin D dosage, duration of intervention, and the subjects’ ages. The subgroup analysis demonstrated a substantial decrease in ALP in all subgroups following vitamin D supplementation (supplementary Table 3).

Forest plot illustrating weighted mean difference and 95% confidence intervals for the impact of vitamin D on ALP

Effect of vitamin D on GGT levels

Six trials with seven treatment arms examined the influence of vitamin D supplementation on GGT levels (457 cases and 374 controls). No significant changes in GGT levels were observed after vitamin D supplementation as compared with the control group, according to the pooled estimate from the random-effects model (WMD: -1.04 IU/L; 95% CI: − 3.30 to 1.23; P = 0.369; I2= 47.4%; P = 0.077) (Fig. 5). According to the subgroup analysis, RCTs with an intervention dosage of ≤ 3500 IU/d, with durations > 12 weeks, participants with ages > 45 years, sample size > 70, and cholecalciferol type of intervention, led to a significant increase in GGT compared to other subgroups (supplementary Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Each study’s influence on the total effect size was assessed using a sensitivity analysis. As per this analysis, no single trial had a substantial effect on the total effect size of ALP. However, the exclusion of studies by Hussain M et al. [30], Gad A I et al. [52], Lorvand Amiri H et al. [43], Nadjarzadeh A et al. [44], Sakpal M et al. [46], and Shidfar F et al. [50] resulted in negligible overall effect sizes for ALT. Omitting one trial [51] highlighted its importance on the comprehensive impacts of vitamin D on GGT levels. In addition, the impact of vitamin D on AST was notably changed after omitting the studies conducted by Dabbaghmanesh M H et al. [47], Mansourian Hosseini S et al. [48], and Sharifi N et al. [45] (supplementary Fig. 1a-d).

Publication bias

The tests for publication bias were executed by employing Begg’s test, Egger’s test, and a visual inspection of the funnel plots. There was an absence of publication bias evidence in clinical trials studying the influence of vitamin D on AST, ALT, and GGT. On the other hand, a notable publication bias was detected for ALP (Begg’s test P = 1.000; Egger’s test P = 0.022). However, after trim and fill analysis (no imputed study), the ALP results remained unchanged (supplementary Fig. 2a-d).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we evaluated the impact of vitamin D supplementation on liver function in patients with NAFLD. Unlike earlier meta-analyses, our study incorporates a larger and more up-to-date evidence base, including 16 randomized controlled trials published up to May 2026. In addition, detailed subgroup analyses were performed based on intervention type and duration, which provide deeper insights into the specific conditions under which vitamin D supplementation may exert beneficial effects in NAFLD. In the current study, the meta-analysis of the clinical trials suggested that vitamin D supplementation might reduce ALT and ALP by -0.45 and − 12.91 IU/L, respectively. While some trials support the beneficial effects of vitamin D on liver enzymes such as ALT and ALP, the results are inconsistent across different populations and conditions. Studies have shown that administering vitamin D supplements can result in decreased ALT levels in individuals with NAFLD [55, 56], indicating potential liver function improvement, particularly in those with atypical reference values for liver enzymes [57]. However, a meta-analysis conducted in 2020 included a review of 8 studies suggesting that vitamin D supplementation may not significantly impact ALT concentrations in patients with NAFLD [58]. Moreover, as in this study, the pooled data analysis indicated a decrease in ALT and AST levels, stratifying the included trials in different subgroups demonstrates the same result with the exception of ALT in trials involving 70 or fewer subjects. Lowering ALP levels in patients with NAFLD could be clinically important, as it may reflect reducing liver fibrosis progression, which is linked to better liver-related outcomes, reduced risk of progression to cirrhosis and liver failure [59].

One possible explanation for these findings is the involvement of vitamin D in regulating insulin resistance, a key factor in the development of NAFLD. Vitamin D contributes to improved glucose metabolism by enhancing both insulin secretion and sensitivity, while also exerting anti-inflammatory effects [60]. Collectively, these benefits support better liver health in NAFLD, as vitamin D not only improves metabolic function but also directly influences hepatic inflammation and fibrosis [61, 62].

An examination of 15 studies that measured AST levels following vitamin D supplementation suggested a potential reduction in AST levels. Contrasting outcomes to those observed in our study, a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Wei et al. [58] stated that vitamin D does not affect the levels of liver enzymes such as AST. Another meta-analysis conducted in 2022 involving four studies revealed that there was no significant impact on AST levels following vitamin D supplementation among individuals with NAFLD [28]. Subgroup results show that AST decreases in all subgroups of dose, duration of intervention, and type of intervention, but AST in studies with fewer participants is equal to 70 people.

The overall findings indicated that vitamin D supplementation did not have a notable impact on GGT. Nevertheless, the subgroup analysis revealed an interesting finding that warrants further investigation. Specifically, when vitamin D was administered at a lower dose (below 3500) and for a longer duration (exceeding 12 weeks), a notable increase in GGT levels was observed. This unexpected outcome may indicate a potential interaction between vitamin D supplementation and liver function, particularly in relation to GGT levels. The subgroup analysis conducted in this study revealed a notable difference in the impact of vitamin D supplementation on GGT levels between two distinct age groups. Specifically, the findings indicated that individuals aged above 45 years experienced an increase in GGT levels following vitamin D supplementation. In contrast, the effect of vitamin D on GGT levels was not found to be significant in individuals aged 45 years and younger. This age-dependent variation in the response to vitamin D supplementation suggests a complex interplay between age-related changes in liver function and the effects of vitamin D on GGT levels. Moreover, the proposed mechanism linking the reduction in Mic60 levels due to aging to the development of aging-associated NAFLD provides a compelling rationale for the observed age-dependent effects of vitamin D on GGT levels. The potential role of vitamin D3 in positively regulating mitochondrial contact site and cristae organizing system (MICOS) 60 expression offers a mechanistic explanation for how vitamin D supplementation could mitigate age-induced NAFLD, by modulating mitochondrial function through the regulation of MICOS 60 expression, vitamin D may exert protective effects against age-related liver pathologies, including NAFLD [63].

The varying effects of different forms of vitamin D on ALT and GGT levels is an important finding in understanding how vitamin D supplementation impacts liver enzymes. The type of vitamin D intervention, whether cholecalciferol or calcitriol, plays a significant role in influencing ALT and GGT levels. cholecalciferol supplementation leads to notable changes in ALT and GGT levels, indicating a distinct physiological response and potential modulation of liver enzyme activity. On the other hand, calcitriol supplementation does not result in significant alterations in ALT and GGT levels, suggesting a different mechanism of action or potentially lower impact on liver enzymes compared to cholecalciferol. This difference underscores the importance of considering the specific form of vitamin D used in supplementation when evaluating its effects on liver function. Cholecalciferol is emphasized as the most important form for nutritional purposes because of its stability and effectiveness [64]. However, based on some of the evidence currently available, there was no distinct variation in the impact on liver function in adults with chronic liver diseases when comparing cholecalciferol and calcitriol as forms of vitamin D supplementation [65]. Further research is needed to uncover the underlying mechanisms behind the significant impact of cholecalciferol on ALT and GGT levels and explore the clinical implications for optimizing vitamin D supplementation strategies in liver function.

The varying impact of vitamin D supplementation on liver enzymes can likely be attributed to differences in their biological functions and tissue distribution. ALT and ALP, being more specific to the liver, seem particularly responsive to vitamin D’s anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects on hepatocytes and cholangiocytes [66]. On the other hand, AST and GGT are less specific indicators—AST is expressed in several tissues outside the liver, while GGT reflects a wider spectrum of oxidative stress and biliary activity—making them less sensitive to changes induced by vitamin D [67]. Overall, these results suggest that the vitamin D/VDR pathway may exert a stronger influence on hepatocellular damage and biliary function, as evidenced by improvements in ALT and ALP, whereas its effects on AST and GGT appear limited or less discernible [68, 69].

Limitations and Strengths

The current study has several strengths. It is an updated systematic review and meta-analysis incorporating the largest possible number of available studies to investigate how vitamin D affects liver function in NAFLD patients. By strictly following specific criteria, we were able to conduct subgroup analyses within this meta-analysis. The reliability of this review is highlighted by its consistent methodology. To better understand the effects of vitamin D, we used subgroup analysis to account for the differences in the studies included. Various statistical methods were used to evaluate how vitamin D impacts liver enzymes. Since there are few studies in this area, our findings mainly summarize existing information and suggest ideas for future research. It is important to be cautious when interpreting the results and to conduct more trials in the future to confirm and build upon these findings.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis explored the effects of vitamin D supplementation on liver function in NAFLD patients. The analysis showed that vitamin D supplementation may reduce AST, ALT and ALP levels. The study also found that vitamin D supplementation did not have a notable effect on GGT levels overall, but interestingly, a subgroup analysis suggested a potential increase in GGT levels with lower doses and longer durations of supplementation. Cholecalciferol supplementation led to notable changes in these enzymes, while calcitriol did not have a significant effect. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings and explore the potential clinical implications for liver disease management.
