Successful Treatment of Localized Eruptive Pruritic Papular Porokeratosis with A Single Session of Cryotherapy: A Case Report
Authors: Xiaoyi Wen, Wenjia Nie, Juhua Liu, Lifang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEruptive pruritic papular porokeratosis (EPPP) is a rare subtype of porokeratosis characterized by acute-onset pruritic papules, and conventional therapies often yield unsatisfactory results. While cryotherapy has been proposed as a potential intervention, successful cases with long-term follow-up remain sparse. We report a 58-year-old male with EPPP confined to the thighs for 2 years, refractory to oral antihistamines and topical glucocorticoids. The patient received a single session of liquid nitrogen spray cryotherapy (spray distance 2 cm, 10–15 seconds per lesion, one freeze-thaw cycle) and was followed up for 3 years. Complete lesion regression and pruritus resolution were achieved 2 weeks post-treatment, with no recurrence observed. The only adverse events were mild persistent hyperpigmentation and superficial atrophic scars, which did not affect cosmetic outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of EPPP successfully managed with single-session cryotherapy. This case supplements clinical evidence for cryotherapy in EPPP, offering an effective, safe, and economical alternative for patients with limited resources, poor adherence, or contraindications to systemic therapy.Keywords: liquid nitrogen cryotherapy, single-session therapy, refractory dermatosis, long-term follow-up, cutaneous porokeratosis

Introduction

Eruptive pruritic papular porokeratosis (EPPP) is a rare variant of porokeratosis characterized by acute-onset papules or annular lesions accompanied by intense pruritus.1 First described by Kanzaki in 1992,1 EPPP is particularly rare with only several dozen cases reported globally to date.2 While spontaneous regression of lesions has been documented in approximately 75% of cases with a median duration of 6 months,2 pruritus and lesions persist in some patients.3,4 Furthermore, severe pruritus, which is the core symptom of EPPP, significantly impairs patients’ quality of life, emphasizing an unmet need for active clinical intervention.5

Currently, there is no standard treatment for EPPP. Conventional therapies, including glucocorticoids, antihistamines, acitretin, and 5-fluorouracil, have shown limited efficacy.1–4 Vitamin D3 analogs demonstrated efficacy in some cases, but require long-term adherence.6,7 Emerging agents such as Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors and biologics have shown rapid pruritus resolution and lesion clearance in several reported cases.5,8–11 However, they are primarily indicated for patients with extensive lesions,5,8–11 contraindicated in those with underlying malignancies or active infections, and further limited by high costs. While cryotherapy has been proposed as a potential physical intervention for EPPP,12 clinical evidences formally validating its efficacy, especially long-term efficacy, remains scarce.

Herein, we present a case of a 58-year-old male with EPPP who achieved complete lesion regression and 3-year recurrence-free remission following a single session of liquid nitrogen cryotherapy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first formally reported case of EPPP successfully managed with single-session liquid nitrogen cryotherapy. This case provides novel clinical evidence supporting the long-term efficacy of cryotherapy in EPPP, highlighting its cost-effectiveness and safety, particularly for patients who decline systemic medications or have limited economic resources.

Case Presentation

A 58-year-old Han Chinese male presented to our department with pruritic papules on both thighs for 2 years. Two years earlier, the patient developed scattered erythematous papules approximately the size of millet to mung beans on the extensor aspects of both thighs without an obvious trigger, accompanied by severe pruritus. The patient, a teacher, denied any significant ultraviolet exposure or other identifiable environmental or occupational exacerbating factors prior to onset. He had previously been diagnosed with “prurigo” and “eczema” at other institutions and had received treatments including hydrocortisone butyrate cream, oral antihistamines, and traditional Chinese medicine. While pruritus was partially alleviated during treatment, the number of papules increased, and pruritus worsened following discontinuation of medications. The patient had no history of diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, malignancy, or autoimmune disorders, and no family history of similar skin conditions.

General physical examination revealed no abnormalities in systemic organs, and no palpable superficial lymphadenopathy was noted. Dermatological examination showed approximately 30 scattered brownish-red keratotic papules on the extensor aspects of both thighs; with fine adherent scales on the surface of some lesions (Figure 1). No similar lesions were observed on the face, trunk, palms, or soles. Laboratory and imaging investigations, including blood routine, urine routine, liver and kidney function tests, and chest radiography, revealed no abnormalities. Histopathological examination of a representative lesion from the extensor aspect of the right thigh demonstrated hyperkeratosis, a cornoid lamella with an absent granular layer beneath it, and dyskeratotic cells in the epidermis; perivascular lymphocytic infiltration was identified in the superficial dermis, with no eosinophils detected (Figure 2).

Based on clinical and pathological findings, the diagnosis of localized EPPP was confirmed. Given the localized distribution and limited number of lesions, liquid nitrogen spray cryotherapy was administered. The treatment parameters were: spray distance 2 cm, freezing duration 10–15 seconds per lesion, with one freeze-thaw cycle.

Two weeks after treatment, all papules had completely resolved, pruritus was significantly relieved, and hypopigmentation was observed at the treatment sites (Figure 3a and b). Six months after treatment, no recurrence of papules or pruritus was noted, with only scattered hyperpigmentation at the treatment sites and no new lesions (Figure 3c and d). During the 3-year follow-up period, there was no recurrence of papules or pruritus; the treatment sites showed persistent mild hyperpigmentation and mild superficial atrophic scars, with no new lesions (Figure 3e and f). Overall, the treatment was well tolerated without the need for local anaesthesia, with mild superficial atrophic scarring as the only residual finding, which did not compromise the overall cosmetic outcome.

Discussion

Over half of EPPP patients have a pre-existing history of other porokeratosis subtypes, most commonly disseminated superficial porokeratosis (DSP).2 They typically present as acute-onset pruritic papules with annular margins, which facilitates differentiation.2 Notably, our patient lacked a personal or family history of DSP, and his lesions lacked the classic annular margins. This atypical clinical phenotype likely contributed to the diagnostic dilemma. In the context of refractory responses to conventional therapies, skin biopsy emerged as the definitive diagnostic tool. Histopathological examination revealed characteristic columnar parakeratosis and dermal lymphocytic infiltration, confirming the diagnosis of EPPP. These findings highlight that EPPP should be included in the differential diagnosis of pruritic papules, even in the absence of a prior DSP or family history of porokeratosis. Skin biopsy in such cases is essential to avoid diagnostic delays.

EPPP remains a therapeutic challenge in clinical practice due to its intense pruritus and the lack of a standardized treatment regimen.10 As highlighted, conventional pharmacological therapies often yield inconsistent outcomes and demand strict long-term adherence.1–4,6,7 Furthermore, while JAK inhibitors and biologics provide rapid relief for extensive disease,5,8–11 they are generally not indicated for localized lesions. Their clinical utility is also restricted by inherent contraindications, a significant concern given that 31.2% of EPPP cases are complicated by concurrent malignant tumors.2 Given these limitations, we considered cryotherapy a suitable alternative. It has demonstrated efficacy in other porokeratosis variants, such as porokeratosis ptychotropica13 and linear porokeratosis.14 Although previously proposed as an intervention for EPPP,12 no published reports have documented its successful clinical use. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first case report of EPPP successfully treated with cryotherapy, which resulted in sustained clearance after a single session. This suggests that it is an efficient, well-tolerated, and cost-effective treatment option, particularly suitable for localized disease.

Given the rarity of EPPP, a well-defined recurrence rate has not been established. Nevertheless, the few documented recurrences underscore the necessity of long-term surveillance. Our literature review identified two such cases,3,4 both occurring after the tapering or discontinuation of conventional therapies: one relapse 5 months after clearance with Oren-gedoku-to,3 and the other following an acitretin dose reduction.4 This pattern indicates that sustained remission cannot be assumed even after initial treatment success. In our patient, recurrence-free status has been maintained for 3 years following cryotherapy. This prolonged remission supports both the potential durability of cryotherapy and the critical value of systematic monitoring.

We acknowledge that the reported 75% spontaneous resolution rate in EPPP complicates the definitive attribution of sustained remission solely to cryotherapy.2 However, several clinical observations in the present case suggest a significant therapeutic role for this intervention. First, prior to cryotherapy, the patient’s lesions had persisted for 2 years without any signs of spontaneous clearance and had proven refractory to multiple conventional therapies. Moreover, a compelling temporal relationship was observed: within 2 weeks post-cryotherapy, all papules had completely resolved and the severe pruritus was significantly relieved, accompanied by hypopigmentation at the treatment sites. This rapid and site-specific response aligns closely with the intervention timeline, contrasting sharply with the protracted and fluctuating course of spontaneous resolution, which has a reported median duration of 6 months. Finally, the achieved remission has been maintained for 3 years. This duration notably exceeds the short relapse intervals documented in rare recurrent cases, such as the aforementioned 5-month period.3 Therefore, while a contribution from the natural disease course cannot be entirely ruled out, the clinical timeline strongly suggests that cryotherapy was instrumental in rapidly terminating disease activity and inducing long-term remission in this refractory case.

Notably, this single-session success differs from reports of porokeratosis ptychotropica¹³ and linear porokeratosis,14 which usually require multiple treatments. This disparity is likely attributable to EPPP’s distinct morphology and our patient’s specific presentation. The discrete, superficial, and small keratotic papules of EPPP facilitate complete ablation in a single freeze-thaw cycle. Coupled with the limited number of localized lesions in this case, comprehensive single-session coverage was highly feasible. Consequently, EPPP may represent a particularly cryo-responsive variant compared to subtypes characterized by prominent hyperkeratosis or thick plaques. The therapeutic success may be explained by a dual mechanism combining immediate physical destruction and subsequent immunomodulation.15 The direct cytodestructive effect of liquid nitrogen unequivocally ablates the abnormal keratinocyte clones constituting the cornoid lamella.16 Cryoinjury may initiate localized immune responses,16 potentially involving T cell-mediated clearance of pathological keratinocytes, as observed in spontaneous regression of EPPP.1,17 Although immunohistochemical analyses were not performed to directly validate immune activation, the clinical outcome supports the notion of synergistic physical and immunological effects. Future research incorporating correlative immunological assays is warranted to substantiate this hypothesis.

We recognize the inherent limitations of this single-case report. The conclusions drawn herein cannot be extrapolated to the broader EPPP population, and the efficacy and safety of cryotherapy for this condition warrant further validation in larger prospective studies. Additionally, given that the patient achieved complete remission with a single session of cryotherapy using fixed parameters, this study did not explore alternative parameter combinations, which restricts the development of standardized protocols for diverse clinical scenarios. Furthermore, the proposed immunomodulatory mechanism underlying cryotherapy’s long-term recurrence-free efficacy remains speculative, as no targeted assays were conducted to verify the relevant regulatory pathways.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case demonstrates the efficacy and safety of liquid nitrogen cryotherapy in managing EPPP. As a safe, effective and cost-efficient therapeutic approach, it is particularly well-suited for EPPP patients with localized lesions, while also serving as a practical alternative for those who cannot access or tolerate systemic therapies. Further investigations are warranted to optimize cryotherapy parameters and clarify the potential immunomodulatory mechanisms responsible for its long-term recurrence-free outcomes in EPPP.
