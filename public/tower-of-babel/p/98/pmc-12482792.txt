A case of juvenile chronic encapsulated intracerebral hematoma requiring differential diagnosis, successfully treated with craniotomy and hematoma evacuation
Authors: Yodai Kikuchi, Shogo Dofuku, Rika Nakamura, Ken Kazama, Hideaki Imai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Chronic encapsulated intracerebral hematoma (CEIH) is a rare condition with an unclear pathogenesis. Moreover, no consensus has been established regarding its treatment. Here, we report a case of juvenile subcortical CEIH successfully treated with complete resection, including the capsule.Case Description: A 26-year-old woman with no notable medical history had experienced headaches for 2 months and 3 weeks before presentation and underwent extraction of the right third molar 1 week before admission. She presented to our hospital complaining of a headache. Visual field testing revealed left upper quadrantanopia. Head CT showed a 5 cm ring-shaped hyperdense lesion in the right temporal lobe, and cerebral angiography revealed no significant vascular abnormalities. On hospital day 10, she underwent craniotomy and removal of the lesion. Intraoperative findings revealed that the hematoma was encapsulated, and en bloc resection was achieved. Histopathological examination showed fibrous connective tissue with fibroblast proliferation covering a hematoma composed of fibrin and red blood cells, consistent with CEIH. The postoperative course was smooth, no new neurological deficits were observed, and both the headache and visual field disturbance improved. She was discharged home 2 weeks after surgery.Conclusion: Although the pathogenesis of CEIH remains unclear and there is no consensus on treatment, complete resection, including the capsule, was considered the appropriate therapeutic approach in this case, given reports of recurrence and poor outcomes associated with residual capsule tissue or coexistence with cavernous malformations.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic encapsulated intracerebral hematoma (CEIH) is a rare condition with an unclear pathogenesis. It is considered a distinct form of intracerebral hematoma characterized by recurrent bleeding and stepwise progression of symptoms. However, no consensus has been established regarding its treatment.[2] Here, we report a case of juvenile subcortical CEIH successfully treated with complete resection, including the capsule, and discuss relevant literature.

Clinical course after admission

After admission, acetaminophen (400 mg), loxoprofen (60 mg), and intravenous glycerol (300 mL) were administered 3 times daily, but her headache persisted, requiring frequent analgesics and initiation of anti-edema therapy. Based on preoperative evaluations, the differential diagnoses included brain tumor, brain abscess, cerebral hemorrhage secondary to cavernous malformation, and CEIH. For diagnostic confirmation, a craniotomy was performed on hospital day 10.

Intraoperatively, a hematoma encapsulated by a fibrous membrane was observed. The capsule was clearly demarcated from the surrounding normal brain tissue. After aspirating the internal hematoma to achieve internal decompression, we were able to resect the entire hematoma en bloc, including the capsule [Figure 4]. The hematoma mainly consisted of old brownish material and was easily aspirated.

(a) Intraoperative findings and (b) resected specimen. The arrows suggest the hematoma was clearly demarcated from surrounding normal brain tissue, with old brownish components inside.

Histopathological examination revealed a hematoma composed of fibrin and red blood cells, covered by fibrous connective tissue with proliferating fibroblasts. The connective tissue also showed capillary proliferation, lymphocytic infiltration, hemosiderin deposition, and extravasation of red blood cells – findings consistent with CEIH [Figure 5].

Postoperative course

Postoperative head CT and MRI confirmed complete resection of the lesion without any complications [Figure 6]. The patient’s headache and visual field disturbance improved, and she was discharged home with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0 2 weeks after surgery. Follow-up imaging, including head CT at 1 month and MRI at 3 months postoperatively, showed no recurrence of hemorrhage and an overall favorable clinical course. Informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient and her family.

DISCUSSION

CEIH is a rare pathological condition with limited case reports in the literature. It was first described by Yashon and Konsnik[10] in 1978 as a “chronic expanding intracerebral hematoma.” Hirsh et al.[2] later proposed the term “chronic encapsulated intracerebral hematoma (CEIH)” in 1981, referring to hematomas that become encapsulated and show progressive symptoms. In 2022, Scalia et al.[6] published a systematic review of 40 reports comprising 58 cases. The mean age of onset was 41.4 ± 20.5 years, with 65.5% male and 34.5% female. The most common clinical presentations were increased intracranial pressure (48.2%), seizures (29.3%), and motor deficits (24.1%). The most frequent lesion location was the subcortical cerebrum (77%), followed by deep structures such as the basal ganglia (12%), thalamus (2%), and cerebellum (9%).

Proposed etiologies for CEIH include vascular malformations such as arteriovenous malformations, cavernous malformations, and angiographically occult vascular malformations, as well as trauma and chronic inflammation following hypertensive hemorrhage.[3,4] Pathogenetic theories include fibroblast proliferation originating from vascular malformations (Hirsh et al.),[2] stimulation of fibroblasts in the pia mater by hemorrhage (Fiumara et al.),[1] and chronic inflammation around the hematoma.[11]

In the present case, episodes of acute right temporal headache 2 months and 3 weeks before admission suggested increased intracranial pressure and potential recurrent hemorrhage. The clinical course was consistent with previously reported CEIH cases.[11] Histologically, CEIH is characterized by fibrous and granulation tissue containing neovascularization and extravasated blood cells.[8] In our case, fibrous tissue with fibroblast proliferation, capillary proliferation, hemosiderin deposition, and red blood cell extravasation was observed. Reactive gliosis and infiltration of lymphocytes and plasma cells were also noted around the hematoma. Immunohistochemistry showed no evidence of light chain restriction or EBER-positive cells, ruling out neoplastic processes and supporting a reactive inflammatory response – findings compatible with CEIH.

CEIH often presents with ring-enhancing lesions on gadolinium-enhanced MRI, and its progressive symptoms make differentiation from brain tumors or abscesses challenging.[5] In our case, differential diagnoses included brain tumor (ruled out due to negative tumor markers and lack of thallium uptake), brain abscess (ruled out by normal inflammatory markers and absence of clinical infection), and vascular malformations.

There is no established consensus on the optimal treatment of CEIH.[5] Some reports support complete resection, including the capsule, to prevent recurrence, as in this case.[4] Others have described favorable outcomes with endoscopic hematoma evacuation or embolization of feeding arteries in cases associated with AVMs.[7,8,11] However, residual capsule tissue has been linked to persistent bleeding and recurrence.[9] In cases of CEIH, open craniotomy offers several advantages, including immediate decompression and the ability to perform complete resection of the capsule, allowing for definitive histopathological diagnosis. However, craniotomy is relatively invasive compared to other approaches such as endoscopic evacuation or endovascular treatment. These minimally invasive strategies may be more appropriate in patients who are poor surgical candidates or when vascular lesions are suspected. In the present case, no vascular malformations were detected, and considering the patient’s young age, persistent symptoms, and overall health status, we opted for open craniotomy to achieve both therapeutic and diagnostic goals. Thus, we consider complete resection, including the capsule, to be the most appropriate treatment strategy.

CONCLUSION

We report a case of juvenile subcortical CEIH that was successfully treated with craniotomy and complete hematoma resection. In young patients with subcortical mass lesions, differential diagnoses typically include brain tumors or abscesses. However, CEIH should also be considered. Given the risk of recurrence associated with residual capsule tissue and potential coexistence with vascular malformations, complete resection, including the capsule, is recommended.
