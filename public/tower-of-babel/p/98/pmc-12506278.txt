Effect of AI-assisted diagnosis on adenomas of different sizes: a meta-analysis with evidence from RCTs and trial sequential analysis
Authors: Dengchao Wang, Youyun Shi, Jinshan Liu, Anyin Wang, Yong Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe application of Artificial Intelligence (AI)-assisted techniques in adenoma detection during colonoscopy has increased. However, the effectiveness of AI in improving adenoma detection rates (ADR) and reducing adenoma miss rates (AMR) across varying adenoma sizes has yet to be thoroughly assessed.MethodsRelevant literature was systematically retrieved from PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, ClinicalTrials.gov, VIP Information Database (VIP), Wanfang Data, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM) for randomized controlled trials (RCTs). A meta-analysis was conducted to assess the impact of AI-assisted techniques on ADR and AMR in colorectal adenomas of different sizes (≤ 5 mm, 6–9 mm, ≥ 10 mm). RevMan 5.4 and Stata 17.0 were utilized for statistical analysis. GRADE methodology was applied to rate the certainty of evidence, complemented by TSA to assess the reliability of the outcomes.ResultsA total of 19 RCTs comprising 15,462 participants were included in the analysis. In the detection of adenomas ≤ 5 mm, the AI-assisted group demonstrated superior performance with a significantly increased ADR (RR = 1.43, 95% CI (1.29, 1.59), P < 0.00001) and markedly reduced AMR (RR = 0.35, 95% CI (0.28, 0.44), P < 0.00001) compared to conventional methods. For 6–9 mm adenomas, AI assistance significantly increased the ADR (RR = 1.24, 95% CI (1.12, 1.36), P < 0.0001), though its effect on AMR was not statistically significant (RR = 0.59, 95% CI (0.24, 1.43), P = 0.24). For adenomas ≥ 10 mm, ADR was significantly higher in the AI-assisted group (RR = 1.21, 95% CI (1.07, 1.37), P = 0.003), but the difference in AMR was not statistically significant (RR = 0.24, 95% CI (0.05, 1.19), P = 0.08). Publication bias analysis suggested potential bias in ADR results for 6–9 mm adenomas. TSA analysis showed that AI-assisted technology significantly improved ADR across all adenoma size categories and effectively reduced AMR for lesions ≤ 5 mm.ConclusionAI-assisted techniques demonstrate higher diagnostic rates for adenomas of ≤ 5 mm, 6–9 mm, and ≥ 10 mm, with a notable reduction in AMR for lesions ≤ 5 mm. While AI can enhance diagnostic quality during colonoscopy, the overall evidence quality remains low, and potential publication bias warrants caution. Further large-scale RCTs are needed to confirm its clinical significance.Keywords: Artificial intelligence-assisted, Colonoscopy, Adenoma miss rate, Adenoma detection rate, Meta-analysis, Trial sequential analysis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the most common and deadly malignancies globally, imposing a substantial burden on global health and economics each year [1]. The early recognition and resection of colorectal adenomas, recognized as critical antecedents of CRC, are fundamental to preventive strategies [2]. Adenoma detection rate (ADR) and adenoma miss rate (AMR) are essential quality indicators of colonoscopy, closely associated with the occurrence of interval colorectal cancer [3]. However, conventional colonoscopy has significant limitations in detecting small adenomas, with miss rates reaching up to 30% [4].

Advancements in artificial intelligence-based diagnostic methods have, in recent years, contributed to significant improvements in colonoscopy performance [5]. Studies have shown that deep learning-based computer-aided detection (CADe) systems exhibit remarkable advantages in increasing ADR and reducing AMR, particularly for small adenomas [6, 7]. However, existing meta-analyses predominantly focus on evaluating the overall efficacy of AI in adenoma detection, lacking systematic analyses of its performance across different adenoma size categories (≤ 5 mm, 6–9 mm, and ≥ 10 mm) [7–10]. Moreover, many of these studies fail to integrate advanced statistical methods to assess the strength and stability of the evidence. While clinical guidelines traditionally prioritize advanced adenomas (≥ 10 mm or with high-risk histological features) in surveillance strategies, emerging evidence has shown that multiple diminutive adenomas may carry comparable risks [11]. For instance, Kim et al. reported that patients with ≥ 3 diminutive adenomas had a similar 5-year risk of metachronous advanced neoplasia as those with baseline advanced adenoma [12]. This suggests that even diminutive lesions can be clinically significant, and accurate detection—especially through AI—may have important implications for long-term colorectal cancer prevention.

Compared with previous studies, this study introduces several innovations. First, we focus on evaluating the diagnostic performance of AI-assisted techniques across adenomas of varying sizes, aiming to uncover potential differences in detecting small and large adenomas. Second, we include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) as the primary data source to ensure high-quality and comparable data. We also incorporate trial sequential analysis (TSA) and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to rigorously assess the reliability and clinical applicability of the results. TSA dynamically evaluates the adequacy of sample size and minimizes the influence of random errors, while GRADE systematically assesses the strength of the evidence, providing robust support for clinical practice. Thus, this study aims to systematically analyze the performance of AI-assisted techniques in ADR and AMR across adenomas of different sizes by combining a meta-analysis of RCTs with TSA and GRADE evidence evaluation. Our findings aim to provide scientific insights to optimize the clinical application of AI in colonoscopy.

Materials and methods

The systematic review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) [13] guidelines to maintain a transparent and structured methodology. It has been registered with INPLASY under the identifier INPLASY202540038.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Population: The study enrolled individuals aged 18 and above who underwent colonoscopy mainly for colorectal cancer screening, follow-up, or diagnostic purposes. (2) Intervention: Colonoscopy with adenoma detection aided by AI diagnostic technology. (3) Comparison: Conventional colonoscopy without AI assistance, primarily represented by high-definition white-light endoscopy. (4) Outcomes: ADR for lesions ≤ 5 mm; AMR for lesions ≤ 5 mm; ADR for lesions 6–9 mm; AMR for lesions 6–9 mm; ADR for lesions ≥ 10 mm; AMR for lesions ≥ 10 mm. ADR is defined as the percentage of individuals found to have ≥ 1 adenoma on colonoscopy [14]. AMR is defined as the proportion of adenomas that are missed during the initial examination and later detected in a repeat colonoscopy conducted on the same day [15]. (5) Study Design: This review incorporated RCTs published in English and Chinese, regardless of publication timeframe.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Studies that did not explicitly report data related to ADR or AMR. (2) Studies that did not involve AI-assisted diagnostic technology. (3) Studies were excluded if ADR and AMR data could not be categorized according to adenoma size (≤ 5 mm, 6–9 mm, ≥ 10 mm). (4) Non-randomized investigations, including cohort and case-control studies. (5) Conference abstracts, review articles, or unpublished gray literature that have not undergone peer review. (6) Duplicate publications.

Search strategy

This study conducted a comprehensive literature search across the following databases, PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, ClinicalTrials.gov, VIP Information Database (VIP), Wanfang Data, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM). A comprehensive search was conducted covering entries from the start of each database to March 16, 2025. The search approach integrated MeSH terms with free-text keywords, utilizing Boolean operators such as AND and OR. Keywords included: adenoma, polyps, neoplasms, artificial intelligence, AI, machine learning, deep learning, neural networks, computer-aided, CAD, colonoscopy, endoscopy, randomized controlled trial, and RCT. For example, the PubMed search strategy was as follows: (((((“Adenoma“[Mesh]) OR (Polyps)) OR (Neoplasms)) AND ((“Artificial Intelligence“[Mesh]) OR (AI OR Machine Learning OR Deep Learning OR Neural Networks OR Computer-Aided OR CAD))) AND ((“Colonoscopy“[Mesh]) OR (Endoscopy))) AND (Randomized Controlled Trial OR RCT). Reference lists of the included studies were also manually examined to identify additional relevant articles potentially missed in the primary search. To ensure comprehensiveness and rigor, two independent researchers conducted the literature search, and the results were merged and de-duplicated.

Data retrieval and methodological quality assessment

Relevant data were extracted independently by two researchers, followed by cross-checking to verify accuracy. Non-English studies were also included, and translations were verified by bilingual researchers. The extracted data covered the following aspects: (1) General details: author names, publication year, and the country where the study was conducted. (2) Participant characteristics: Age, overall sample size, gender distribution, and Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS) score. (3) Intervention and comparison: Specific AI-assisted diagnostic techniques and diagnostic methods used in the control group. (4) Outcome measures: ADR for lesions ≤ 5 mm, AMR for lesions ≤ 5 mm, ADR for lesions 6–9 mm, AMR for lesions 6–9 mm, ADR for lesions ≥ 10 mm, AMR for lesions ≥ 10 mm. Assessment of the included studies’ quality was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 1.0). The evaluation encompassed seven key aspects: random sequence generation, concealment of allocation, blinding of both participants and personnel, blinding of outcome evaluators, data completeness, selective reporting, and other possible sources of bias. Each domain was rated as having a “low”, “unclear”, or “high” risk of bias [16]. Two researchers conducted the assessments independently and recorded the results. Discrepancies were resolved through team discussions, and a third-party expert was consulted if necessary.

Statistical methods

RevMan 5.4 and Stata 17.0 were employed for statistical analysis. For binary outcomes, relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. The degree of heterogeneity was interpreted based on the I² statistic: below 50% suggested low heterogeneity, between 50% and 75% indicated moderate heterogeneity, and values above 75% reflected high heterogeneity. A random-effects model was applied when heterogeneity was considered notable (P < 0.1 or I² >50%) to account for between-study variability and provide conservative estimates. Otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used [17]. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore possible sources of significant heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by removing each study one at a time to assess the stability of the overall findings. Funnel plots generated via RevMan 5.4 were used to visually assess potential publication bias, while Egger’s and Begg’s tests were applied in Stata 17.0 for quantitative evaluation. Publication bias was not evaluated when fewer than 10 studies were included [18]. All statistical analyses were two-tailed, with a P-value less than 0.05 regarded as indicative of statistical significance.

GRADE evidence quality assessment

Evidence certainty for outcomes was systematically assessed using the GRADE methodology. According to the GRADE system, the certainty of evidence is classified into four tiers: high, moderate, low, and very low. The evaluation encompasses five key components: risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias [19]. Two researchers independently conducted the GRADE assessments. Discrepancies were resolved through team discussions. The results, including evidence grading and reasons for downgrading, were displayed in a summary of findings (SoF) table.

TSA

TSA was performed to determine whether the accrued sample size met the required information size (RIS) and to minimize the risk of random errors. TSA helps determine whether firm conclusions can be drawn based on the current evidence or whether more trials are needed. TSA 0.9 software was used for this analysis. The RIS was calculated based on the predefined effect size (e.g., relative risk), significance level (α = 0.05, two-sided), statistical power (1-β = 0.80), and event rates in the control and experimental groups. The RIS represents the minimum sample size required to ensure 80% statistical power. The effect size and event rate were determined based on the results of the meta-analysis conducted in this study. TSA monitoring boundaries were constructed based on the RIS. When the cumulative Z-curve crossed the monitoring boundary, it indicated that the current evidence was sufficient to support the robustness of the results and that additional samples were unnecessary. If the Z-curve did not cross the boundary, the results should be interpreted with caution, and further assessment of whether to expand the sample size would be required. If the cumulative sample size reached the RIS but the Z-curve did not cross the monitoring boundary, it suggested that the existing data were insufficient to draw definitive conclusions, and further studies might be needed. For cases where the cumulative sample size did not meet the RIS, the results were evaluated in combination with sensitivity analyses [20].

Results

Basic information of included literature

A systematic search across multiple databases identified 839 potentially relevant studies, with one additional study retrieved through manual searching. After removing duplicates, 689 articles remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 27 articles were selected for full-text review. Following detailed evaluation, 19 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis [21–39]. All included studies were randomized controlled trials (RCTs), with three published in Chinese [23, 35, 38]. The total sample size across the included studies was 15,462 participants, including 7,715 males and 7,747 females. Results of methodological quality evaluation

All 19 RCTs adequately described the methods used for random sequence generation. Among these, 13 studies [21–25, 27–31, 36, 37, 39] implemented allocation concealment. One study [23] applied blinding for participants and researchers, while 14 studies [21–31, 36, 37, 39] reported blinding of outcome assessors. Four studies [23, 34, 35, 38] were not registered as clinical trials, making it impossible to determine whether selective reporting bias was present. No evidence of incomplete outcome data or other obvious sources of bias was identified in any of the included studies (Figs. 2 and 3).

Meta-analysis results

ADR for lesions ≤ 5 mm

Fifteen studies [21–23, 25–31, 33, 35–37, 39] reported ADR (defined as the proportion of patients with ≥ 1 adenoma detected during colonoscopy) for lesions ≤ 5 mm. In the AI-assisted group, the ADR was 1765/6724 (26.2%), while in the conventional group, it was 1264/6792 (18.6%). Given the presence of moderate heterogeneity across studies (P = 0.003, I² = 58%), a random-effects model was applied to synthesize the effect estimates. The analysis showed that the ADR in the AI-assisted group was significantly higher than that in the conventional group [RR = 1.43, 95% CI (1.29, 1.59), P < 0.00001]. Country-specific subgroup analyses were conducted. (1) China group: This subgroup included nine studies [21, 23, 26, 31, 33, 34, 37–39]. The ADR in the AI-assisted group was 939/4146 (22.6%), compared to 588/4198 (14.0%) in the conventional group. Given the low heterogeneity (P = 0.24; I² = 23%), the fixed-effects model revealed a significantly elevated ADR in the AI-assisted group compared to the conventional group [RR = 1.63, 95% CI (1.46, 1.83), P < 0.00001]. (2) Non-China group: This subgroup included six studies [22, 25, 27–30]. The ADR in the AI-assisted group was 826/2578 (32.0%), compared to 678/2594 (26.1%) in the conventional group. With no evidence of heterogeneity (P = 0.46; I² = 0%), the fixed-effects model was selected for analysis. Results showed that ADR was significantly improved in the AI-assisted group relative to the conventional group [RR = 1.24, 95% CI (1.14, 1.34), P < 0.00001], as shown in in Fig. 4A.

A Comparison of ADR for lesions ≤ 5 mm between the two groups. B Comparison of AMR for lesions ≤ 5 mm between the two groups

AMR for lesions ≤ 5 mm

Three studies [24, 32, 34] reported AMR (defined as the proportion of adenomas missed during initial colonoscopy and found on same-day repeat examination) for lesions ≤ 5 mm. The AMR in the AI-assisted group was 81/734 (11.0%), compared to 260/841 (30.9%) in the conventional group. Low heterogeneity was found among the included studies (P = 0.34, I² = 7%), supporting the use of a fixed-effects model for meta-analysis. AMR was found to be significantly lower in the AI-assisted group than in the conventional group [RR = 0.35, 95% CI (0.28, 0.44), P < 0.00001], as shown in Fig. 4B.

ADR for lesions 6–9 mm

Thirteen studies [21–23, 25–30, 33, 35, 37, 39] reported ADR for lesions 6–9 mm. The ADR in the AI-assisted group was 764/6296 (12.1%), compared to 625/6353 (9.8%) in the conventional group. Only low heterogeneity was identified across studies (P = 0.16, I² = 28%), warranting the use of a fixed-effects model for meta-analysis. ADR was found to be markedly elevated in the AI-assisted group relative to the conventional group [RR = 1.24, 95% CI (1.12, 1.36), P < 0.0001], as shown in Fig. 5A.

A Comparison of ADR for llesions 6–9 mm between the two groups. B Comparison of AMR for lesions 6–9 mm between the two groups

AMR for lesions 6–9 mm

Three studies [24, 32, 34] reported AMR for lesions 6–9 mm. In the AI-assisted group, the AMR was 45/580 (7.7%), compared to 114/683 (16.7%) in the conventional group. Meta-analysis utilized a random-effects model to account for observed high heterogeneity (P = 0.004, I² = 82%). AMR did not differ significantly between the AI-assisted group and the conventional group [RR = 0.59, 95% CI (0.24, 1.43), P = 0.24]. Subgroup analyses based on the country of the study were performed: (1) China group: This subgroup included one study [34], which was analyzed descriptively. The AI-assisted group included 184 participants with 25 missed adenomas, while the conventional group included 185 participants with 87 missed adenomas. (2) Non-China group: This subgroup included two studies [24, 32]. The AMR in the AI-assisted group was 20/396 (5.1%), compared to 27/498 (5.4%) in the conventional group. No statistical heterogeneity was observed (P = 0.96, I² = 0%). A fixed-effects model was used for the meta-analysis, showing no statistically significant difference in AMR between the AI-assisted and conventional groups [RR = 0.92, 95% CI (0.53, 1.59), P = 0.77], as shown in Fig. 5B.

ADR for lesions ≥ 10 mm

Fourteen studies [21–23, 25–30, 33, 35–37, 39] reported ADR for lesions ≥ 10 mm. The ADR in the AI-assisted group was 494/6683 (7.4%), compared to 410/6735 (6.1%) in the conventional group. Given the absence of statistical heterogeneity (P = 0.45, I² = 0%), the meta-analysis employed a fixed-effects approach. ADR was significantly higher in the AI-assisted group compared to the conventional group [RR = 1.21, 95% CI (1.07, 1.37), P = 0.003], as shown in Fig. 6A.

A Comparison of ADR for lesions ≥ 10 mm between the two groups. B Comparison of AMR for lesions ≥ 10 mm between the two groups

AMR for lesions ≥ 10 mm

Three studies [24, 32, 34] reported AMR for lesions ≥ 10 mm. In the AI-assisted group, the AMR was 63/584 (10.7%), compared to 39/667 (5.8%) in the conventional group. Significant statistical heterogeneity was observed among studies (P = 0.009, I² = 79%), so a random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. No statistically significant difference in AMR was found between the AI-assisted and conventional groups [RR = 0.57, 95% CI (0.08, 3.96), P = 0.57]. Subgroup analyses based on the country of the study were performed: (1) China group: This subgroup included one study [34], analyzed descriptively. In this study, the AI-assisted group included 184 participants with 61 missed adenomas, while the conventional group included 185 participants with 28 missed adenomas. (2) Non-China group: This subgroup included two studies [24, 32]. In this subgroup, the AMR was 2/400 (0.5%) in the AI-assisted group and 11/482 (2.3%) in the conventional group. A fixed-effects model was appropriate given the nonsignificant heterogeneity (P = 0.28, I² = 13%). AMR did not differ significantly between the AI-assisted group and the conventional group [RR = 0.24, 95% CI (0.05, 1.19), P = 0.08], as shown in Fig. 6B.

Results of publication bias analysis

Publication bias was assessed for outcome measures with more than 10 included studies: ADR for lesions ≤ 5 mm, 6–9 mm, and ≥ 10 mm. Corresponding funnel plots are presented in Fig. 7A and B, and C. The funnel plot for ADR in lesions measuring 6–9 mm showed asymmetry. Begg’s test yielded P-values of 0.235, 0.033, and 0.324, while Egger’s test yielded P-values of 0.207, 0.003, and 0.107 for the three respective outcomes. These results suggest no significant publication bias for ADR in lesions ≤ 5 mm or ≥ 10 mm. However, there appears to be a higher risk of publication bias for ADR in lesions 6–9 mm.

Discussion

In recent years, AI-assisted technology has gained significant attention in colonoscopy, particularly for its potential to enhance the detection rate of colorectal adenomas and reduce the risk of missed diagnoses [5]. This study systematically evaluated the impact of AI-assisted technology on ADR and AMR across different adenoma sizes (≤ 5 mm, 6–9 mm, ≥ 10 mm). TSA analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the current evidence. The results showed that AI-assisted technology significantly improved ADR across all adenoma size groups. Its effect was most pronounced for adenomas ≤ 5 mm, along with a significant reduction in AMR. For adenomas 6–9 mm and ≥ 10 mm, the ADR in the AI-assisted group was also superior to that in the conventional group, although no significant differences in AMR were observed. TSA analysis showed that the cumulative sample sizes for ADR in all three adenoma size groups (≤ 5 mm, 6–9 mm, and ≥ 10 mm), as well as for AMR in the ≤ 5 mm group, reached the RIS, supporting the statistical significance of the corresponding results. These findings further confirm the clinical value of AI-assisted technology in colonoscopy.

AI-assisted technology has demonstrated significant advantages in detecting colorectal adenomas ≤ 5 mm. ADR for adenomas ≤ 5 mm was significantly higher in the AI-assisted group than in the conventional group, consistent with findings from previous studies. For example, Barua et al. conducted a systematic review and found that AI technology significantly improved the ADR of small adenomas, especially those ≤ 5 mm, with an average increase of 0.15 adenomas detected per colonoscopy [40]. For adenomas 6–9 mm and ≥ 10 mm, AI-assisted technology also showed notable advantages. In this study, the ADR for adenomas 6–9 mm was significantly higher in the AI-assisted group than in the conventional group. Similarly, the ADR for adenomas ≥ 10 mm was superior in the AI-assisted group. Notably, for adenomas ≥ 10 mm, AI technology enhanced detection efficiency by improving the clarity of lesion boundaries and morphological features [41]. Despite the superior performance of AI technology in detecting adenomas of various sizes, certain limitations remain. For instance, the sensitivity of different AI systems in detecting large adenomas may vary depending on the source of the training data. Furthermore, although AI technologies have demonstrated excellent performance in detecting diminutive lesions (≤ 5 mm), their widespread application may raise concerns about overdiagnosis and increased costs related to endoscopic procedures and pathological examinations. Although previous clinical guidelines have primarily focused on advanced pathological features of large adenomas (e.g., ≥ 10 mm), more recent studies indicate that diminutive adenomas (≤ 5 mm) may also carry clinical significance. For example, Kim et al. reported that patients with ≥ 3 diminutive adenomas had a similar 5-year risk of developing metachronous advanced neoplasia as those with baseline advanced adenomas [12], suggesting that small lesions may also represent a high-risk population. This evidence provides support for potential adjustments in surveillance intervals—even when only diminutive lesions are detected, higher detection rates achieved through AI assistance may justify considering shorter follow-up intervals.

This study also found that, among lesions measuring ≤ 5 mm, the AMR in the AI-assisted group was significantly lower than in the conventional group. AI-assisted technologies effectively reduced the AMR for small adenomas by optimizing image recognition and enabling real-time intervention, demonstrating particularly strong performance in anatomically complex regions such as the rectum and cecum [24]. Since the detection of small adenomas often depends on the operator’s experience, the integration of AI holds promise for improving diagnostic consistency and standardization, especially in primary care hospitals, among less experienced physicians, or during large-scale screening programs [42]. Therefore, AI-assisted technologies can serve as important tools for endoscopy quality control and may be incorporated into future clinical practice guidelines. However, their long-term clinical benefits still require further validation—particularly whether they can sustainably reduce the incidence and mortality of colorectal cancer remains uncertain due to a lack of prospective studies. It should also be recognized that not all diminutive adenomas carry clinical significance. A considerable proportion of ≤ 5 mm lesions are non-neoplastic with low risk of progression [43]. Consequently, while AI can enhance ADR, it may also lead to an increase in the number of pathological examinations and polypectomies, thereby escalating resource consumption and raising concerns of over-intervention [44]. Against this backdrop, there is an urgent need to systematically evaluate variations in AI system performance and their economic feasibility. Existing research suggests that the widespread adoption of AI involves additional costs, including equipment acquisition, maintenance, extended procedure time, and training, which may increase the average cost of colonoscopy [45]. This issue is particularly critical for resource-limited medical institutions and healthcare systems. Future research should therefore integrate economic modeling with multicenter practical data, focusing on the precise identification of diminutive lesions with clinical progression potential. A systematic evaluation of AI’s cost-effectiveness and policy applicability is also needed to provide evidence-based guidance for its rational implementation in clinical and healthcare systems. For adenomas 6–9 mm and ≥ 10 mm, there was no statistically significant difference in AMR between the AI-assisted and conventional groups. A possible explanation is that larger adenomas are relatively easier to detect with conventional endoscopy, reducing the additional advantage of AI in minimizing AMR [24]. However, the AMR analysis for lesions by size was limited by the inclusion of only three studies per subgroup, which may compromise the stability and reliability of the results. Moreover, substantial heterogeneity across studies further weakens the clinical interpretability and external validity of the findings. In addition, a significant publication bias observed in the ADR data for lesions measuring 6–9 mm may have led to an overestimation of the AI-assisted detection effect due to the preferential reporting of positive outcomes.

The principles by which AI technology improves colorectal ADR can be summarized as follows: (1) Accurate image recognition based on deep learning: Modern AI systems leverage deep learning algorithms to rapidly process high-resolution endoscopic images and extract complex feature patterns, including adenoma color, morphology, and texture. These algorithms are particularly effective in identifying small, flat, or hidden adenomas, which are often overlooked by the human eye [46, 47]. (2) Intervention through real-time alert systems: AI systems provide visual and auditory alerts during colonoscopy to flag potential lesions, significantly enhancing the endoscopist’s attention. This reduces the likelihood of missed diagnoses caused by fatigue or technical variability. Studies have shown that such real-time interventions are especially critical for detecting small adenomas with unclear boundaries [24, 33, 48]. (3) Improved consistency in endoscopic procedures: Variability among endoscopists is a key factor affecting ADR. AI-assisted technology standardizes the detection process, significantly reducing inter-operator variability and ensuring more consistent ADR across different endoscopists [49]. (4) Enhanced edge recognition of lesions: AI optimizes algorithms to more accurately identify the edges and morphological features of adenomas sized 6–9 mm and ≥ 10 mm. This capability is particularly valuable for detecting irregularly shaped lesions [50, 51].

This study systematically evaluated publication bias, evidence quality, and sample size robustness, providing additional support for the reliability of the results. In the publication bias analysis, funnel plots combined with Begg’s and Egger’s tests showed no significant publication bias for ADR of adenomas ≤ 5 mm and ≥ 10 mm. However, potential publication bias was identified for ADR of adenomas 6–9 mm. This bias may arise due to the inclusion of small-sample studies, selective reporting of significant results, and the limited number of studies with heterogeneous designs. Such publication bias could lead to an overestimation of the effect size in pooled estimates. Future research should focus on improving the publication of small-sample and negative studies, as well as enhancing transparency through comprehensive study registration systems. GRADE indicated that ADR and AMR for adenomas ≤ 5 mm and 6–9 mm were of low-quality evidence. ADR for adenomas ≥ 10 mm was rated as moderate-quality evidence, while AMR for adenomas ≥ 10 mm was rated as very low-quality evidence. The primary factors affecting evidence quality included heterogeneity, inconsistency, and risk of bias across studies. For AMR analyses, limited sample size and high heterogeneity further lowered the evidence grades to very low. Although this study conducted subgroup analyses between China and non-China regions based on healthcare systems, patient populations, and technological availability, some outcome indicators still showed heterogeneity between subgroups. However, due to limited data, it was not possible to further explore potential confounding factors such as differences in technology, training levels, and patient demographic characteristics. These findings suggest that although the advantages of AI-assisted technology in adenoma detection are evident, larger-scale, high-quality studies are needed to further validate its effects. TSA analysis showed that the ADR for adenomas ≤ 5 mm, 6–9 mm, and ≥ 10 mm had all reached the RIS, and the cumulative Z-score curve crossed the significance boundary, indicating that the current evidence is sufficient to support statistical significance. This further strengthens the credibility of AI-assisted technology in improving ADR while reducing the risk of false-positive conclusions caused by random errors.

Limitations

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the effectiveness of AI-assisted technology in detecting colorectal adenomas of different sizes in terms of ADR and AMR; however, several important limitations remain: (1) Heterogeneity: Significant heterogeneity was observed in some outcome measures. Although subgroup analyses partially reduced this heterogeneity, it may still be attributable to differences in AI system types, physician experience, and diagnostic environments across studies. Additionally, factors such as patient age and bowel preparation quality may also contribute to heterogeneity in certain outcomes. (2) Limited sample size: Some outcome indicators included only three studies, potentially affecting the stability and generalizability of the results. Moreover, sample size imbalance in certain subgroups—such as comparisons between China and non-China regions—may have limited the comprehensiveness of the analysis. (3) Risk of bias and evidence quality: Inconsistencies in randomization methods, allocation concealment, and blinding implementation were found among the included RCTs, introducing potential risk of bias. In particular, the real-time and visible nature of AI systems during endoscopic procedures makes blinding of operators difficult, which is a common challenge in AI-related studies. This unblinded design may have led to an overestimation of AI effectiveness. According to the GRADE evaluation, many outcome indicators were rated as low or very low quality, mainly due to risks of bias, heterogeneity, and small sample sizes. These issues reduce the certainty of the conclusions and limit their direct applicability in clinical practice. (4) Potential publication bias: Evidence of potential publication bias was identified in ADR for 6–9 mm lesions, possibly due to underreporting of small-sample or negative studies, leading to an overestimation of AI effectiveness. Future research should promote the publication of small or negative studies and improve transparency through study registration systems. (5) Limitations in real-world applicability: Although AI technology has demonstrated strong performance in adenoma detection, the evidence in this study is primarily based on RCTs conducted in controlled settings, which may not fully reflect the complexity of real-world clinical practice. Factors such as time constraints, equipment variability, and patient heterogeneity may influence actual outcomes. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to daily practice. In addition, this study did not assess the real-world feasibility or cost-effectiveness of AI implementation, including system deployment, equipment maintenance, and training costs—all of which are critical for practical application. (6) Lack of long-term clinical outcomes: This study mainly focused on surrogate endpoints such as ADR and AMR. While these are clinically relevant, most included studies had short follow-up periods and did not report long-term outcomes such as interval colorectal cancer incidence or mortality. (7) Absence of economic evaluation: The economic impact of AI-assisted colonoscopy, including system setup, maintenance, personnel training costs, and comparison with conventional methods, was not assessed. Given the importance of resource allocation and policy-making, future research should include comprehensive cost-effectiveness analyses. (8) Technological obsolescence: With the rapid evolution of AI, some of the systems used in the included studies may no longer represent the most current technologies. Differences in algorithm performance compared to newer systems may affect the relevance of the findings. Continued research is needed to evaluate how advances in AI algorithms influence diagnostic performance over time.

Conclusion

Meta-analysis and TSA validated the effectiveness of AI-assisted technology in colorectal adenoma detection. The results demonstrated a significant improvement in ADR across different adenoma sizes, with particularly strong advantages for adenomas ≤ 5 mm, and a notable reduction in AMR. However, the overall certainty of evidence assessed by GRADE remains low to very low for most outcomes, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation. To establish its clinical significance, further rigorous, large-scale multicenter RCTs are warranted.
