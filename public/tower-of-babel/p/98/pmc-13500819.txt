Solar and thermal dual-band electrochromic windows for energy-efficient buildings
Authors: Jian Chen, Jianwei Hu, Banghai Wang, Huajie Zhang, Jie Liang, Peiru Shi, Jia Zhu, Yan Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Windows mediate over 60% of solar gain (0.38‒2.5 μm) in summer and more than 40% of radiative loss (2.5‒20 μm) in winter. Traditional electrochromic (EC) smart windows can manipulate heat only in visible and near-infrared wavelength regions by adjustable transmission, while neglecting the thermal radiation of long-wave infrared emissivity (ɛLWIR). Here, we report a solar and thermal dual-band EC (STDEC) window that independently modulates solar transmission (∆Tsol = 52.2%) and ɛLWIR (∆ɛLWIR = 0.87) by integrating a dynamic copper-bismuth (Cu–Bi)-based reversible metal electrodeposition system with a spatial rotation architecture. Consequently, the STDEC window lowers summer daytime temperatures by up to 22.2 °C and raises winter nighttime temperatures by up to 1.5 °C versus normal glass in outdoor thermal tests. Furthermore, building energy simulations suggest that our window demonstrates higher year-round energy savings and thus global carbon dioxide (CO2) emission reduction. This STDEC window provides a sustainable pathway towards energy-efficient buildings.

Introduction

Enhancing energy efficiency in buildings plays a pivotal role in advancing sustainable urban development1,2. Currently, buildings represent about 30% of global energy consumption3, among which one-quarter is wasted on additional heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) owing to conventional windows that have high optical transmittance and low thermal resistance4–6. A comprehensive thermal analysis reveals 62% of heat gain originated from the window-transmitted solar radiation (0.38‒2.5 μm) in summer and 44% of heat loss stemmed from window-mediated thermal radiation (2.5‒20 μm) in winter (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Note 1). These static windows struggle to meet seasonal heating and cooling requirements throughout the whole year in many urban areas (Supplementary Fig. 2). Therefore, achieving dynamic dual-band regulation of both solar radiation (0.38‒2.5 μm) and thermal radiation (2.5‒20 μm) through windows is critical for energy-efficient buildings (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Radiative cooling is a promising dual-band thermal management technology for buildings, which rejects solar radiation with high solar reflectance (0.38‒2.5 μm) and emits thermal radiation into outer space with high emissivity through the atmosphere’s long-wave infrared (LWIR) transmission window (8‒13 μm)7–13. However, the fixed optical properties result in persistent overcooling during cold seasons, increasing heating demand that could offset summer cooling savings14. Developing switchable radiative cooling materials that can dynamically modulate solar and thermal radiative characteristics in response to different seasonal weather conditions is therefore greatly needed. Existing efforts in dynamic radiative cooling, including temperature-dependent materials15–20, mechanical actuation21–23 and electrochromic (EC) modulation24,25, have demonstrated promising capabilities in dynamically regulating the solar and thermal dual-band spectral properties to maximize energy savings.

Dynamic EC tuning is especially preferred, as it is highly beneficial for scalable integration with established electronic control systems of buildings. However, in existing EC-based dynamic radiative cooling devices, there is always a trade-off between solar transmission (Tsol) and LWIR emissivity (ɛLWIR) for window applications25–28. For example, in a hot summer, a high ɛLWIR can enhance heat dissipation through radiative cooling, but a simultaneously reported high Tsol can readily harvest solar radiation and consequently raise indoor temperatures25,28. Emerging dual-band EC radiative smart windows now enable the synergistic regulation of solar transmittance and thermal radiation by incorporating polyaniline (PANI)29 or liquid crystals30. However, these windows still face challenges related to limited spectral modulation capabilities, poor bistability, and high operation voltage/power consumption.

Reversible metal electrodeposition (RME)-based EC windows present a promising alternative to address the above problems31–36. These windows achieve high solar transmission modulation from less than 0.1% at the depositing state to up to 80% at the stripping state because of the high extinction coefficient of a 20‒30 nm metal deposition layer31,37,38, operating at ultralow driving voltages (under 1 V) compared with 60 V of liquid crystal-based EC windows30,39. Moreover, bistability is the property of an EC smart window to maintain a specific transmission state without continuous energy consumption37,40. RME enables EC smart windows with good bistability (over 24 h)32,37,41, significantly outperforming traditional EC materials with bistability for less than 4 h42–44. These directly minimize operational energy demands of windows while switching or long-term maintaining optical states, as required for next-generation energy-neutral buildings. However, RME-based EC smart windows capable of simultaneous solar and thermal modulation have rarely been reported to date.

In this work, we demonstrate a solar and thermal dual-band EC (STDEC) window based on RME (Fig. 1a‒c), engineered to achieve an ultrabroadband, independent and synergistic control over both solar and thermal spectra. The STDEC window achieves high solar transmission modulation (∆Tsol, 0.38‒2.5 μm) of 52.2% with an RME process; synchronously shows good LWIR emissivity modulation (∆ɛLWIR, 2.5‒20 μm) of 0.87 via occupant-friendly, mechanically-assisted rotation operation (Supplementary Fig. 4). The low working voltage (±1 V) and good bistability properties (beyond 36 h) of the STDEC window contribute to a low annual operational energy usage of <1 MJ m−2. Results

Regulation mechanism of the STDEC window

For our STDEC window, its working principle, structure and spectral properties are illustrated in Fig. 1. In winter (Fig. 1a, left), the window is designed with a high Tsol for capturing solar radiation and a low ɛLWIR on the front side to minimize heat loss (heating mode presented in Fig. 1c). In summer (Fig. 1a, right), the window is configured to have a black appearance instead of a specular one to decrease glare and thermal reflection affecting nearby buildings and pedestrians. The black-state window exhibits a low Tsol to prevent violent solar radiation from entering the room (cooling mode illustrated in Fig. 1c). By spatially rotating the orientation of the window (Supplementary Fig. 4), the low ɛLWIR side is positioned at the back (indoors) to decrease secondary solar heat gain, while the high ɛLWIR side faces the front (outdoors) to promote radiative cooling (cooling mode illustrated in Fig. 1c).

To implement this synergistic regulation between Tsol and ɛLWIR, we designed and constructed the STDEC window using a RME technique along with a spatial rotation mechanism (Fig. 1b). The window consists of a double-sided indium tin oxide (ITO) as the working electrode; a copper (Cu) frame encircling the device as the counter electrode; a transparent gel electrolyte layer that supplies metal cations; and a polyethylene (PE) membrane (Fig. 1b, left). The working electrode features a low sheet resistance (8 ohm/sq), a high Tsol (~66%; Supplementary Fig. 5) and a low ɛLWIR (~0.08; Fig. 1c). The inner ITO layer of the working electrode was modified with platinum nanoparticles (Pt NPs) to ensure a uniform and dense deposition of the metal film (Supplementary Fig. 6). The PE membrane is an ultra-broadband transparent substrate in the spectral range of 0.38–20 μm (Supplementary Fig. 7), which has a minimal effect on the thermal emissivity of the underlying electrolyte. As a result, our STDEC window can achieve a high Tsol of 52.24% and a low front-side ɛLWIR of 0.08 (ITO) in its initial state (as shown on the left of Fig. 1b, c during heating mode). This high Tsol can be cyclically restored through the complete bleaching of the RME film (Supplementary Fig. 8). When a negative voltage of –1 V is applied, the RME film is gradually deposited onto the transparent working electrode, resulting in the STDEC window displaying a black opaque appearance over time. As an illustrative example, we applied Cu–bismuth (Cu–Bi) as the black RME material (Fig. 1b, right) due to its reliable electrochemical reversibility and stable cycling performance (Supplementary Note 2), which can be expanded to other metal films with strong plasmonic absorption, such as Cu–zinc (Cu–Zn)35, Cu45 or silver (Ag)46 under controlled conditions. To further enhance heat dissipation from the building through radiative cooling, we rotated the orientation of the STDEC window in space to ensure that the PE side faces outward, taking advantage of the aqueous electrolyte′s high thermal emissivity resulting from its strong infrared absorption (Supplementary Fig. 9). Our STDEC window delivers a low Tsol of 0.06% and a high front side ɛLWIR of 0.95 at the depositing state (as shown in Fig. 1c during cooling mode).

Optical and thermal performance of the STDEC window

To achieve optimal spectral performance for solar and thermal regulation, as shown in Fig. 1c, it is crucial to consider the quality of the deposited Cu–Bi film and the parameters of the ITO layer on the working electrode. We characterize the surface morphology for the deposited Cu–Bi film, comparing cases with and without the modification of Pt NPs on the working ITO electrode. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) image shows that the Cu–Bi electrodeposits without Pt NPs modification exhibit a rough, porous and interrupted surface morphology (Supplementary Fig. 10). This leads to inhomogeneous coloration similar to the coffee-ring effect (Supplementary Fig. 11) and a hysteretic optical response (Supplementary Fig. 12) between the center and edges of the EC device. In contrast, the Cu–Bi electrodeposits modified with Pt NPs facilitate homogeneous nucleation during deposition, resulting in a highly compact and uniform morphology with high surface coverage (Supplementary Fig. 13). Selecting the appropriate ITO thickness for the STDEC window is essential to optimizing both solar and thermal performance. The ITO layer serves two purposes: it functions as a transparent conductive layer and as an infrared-reflective layer. Increasing the thickness of the ITO layer from 13 to 260 nm reduces the sheet resistance of the working electrode from 300 to 5 ohm sq−1 (see Supplementary Table 1). This decreased sheet resistance (e.g., 5 and 8 ohm sq−1) helps to minimize the voltage drop from edge to center of our STDEC window (Supplementary Fig. 15), which promotes even deposition and tinting of the Cu–Bi films (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17), and consequently protects against strong solar radiation in hot weather. Furthermore, to evaluate the influence of ITO thickness on thermal performance, we examined the LWIR reflectance spectrum utilizing a Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer. As illustrated in Fig. 2b, thicker ITO layers demonstrate high thermal reflectance, exceeding 90%, with measurements of 92.12% at 185 nm and 94.10% at 260 nm, respectively, indicating their effectiveness in blocking thermal radiation. The thermal effects caused by the high thermal reflectance of these thicker ITO films are visualized by a thermal camera (Fig. 2c). The LWIR images reveal that thicker ITO layers (e.g., 185 and 260 nm) have a noticeably lower surface temperature, proving that increasing ITO thickness can diminish heat radiation. However, the overly thick ITO layer (like 260 nm) blocks a considerable portion of incoming solar radiation (Supplementary Fig. 18), resulting in low Tvis of around 60% and Tsol of approximately 40% (Supplementary Table 1), which fails to satisfy our high solar transmittance criteria for the STDEC window. Thus, we selected a 185-nm-thick ITO layer for the working electrode in the following study, which provides an optimal balance in both solar and thermal performance.

Based on these careful parameter design of STDEC components, our STDEC window achieves a broadband contrast in the Tsol from 52.24% at the stripping state to 0.06% at the depositing state, and in the ɛLWIR from 0.08 at the ITO side to 0.95 at the PE side (Fig. 1c). Accordingly, the window appears transparent to black when photographed with a regular camera (Fig. 2di–iii), whereas it changes from cool (blue color) to warm (red color) under an infrared camera (Fig. 2div–vi). These visible and infrared images clearly demonstrate the synergistic solar and thermal modulation for annual solar and radiative thermal management. The STDEC window has competitive solar regulation (∆Tsol = 52.2%) and large thermal regulation (∆ɛLWIR = 0.87) in comparison to the existing EC regulators25,27–30,42,47,48 (Fig. 2e). A more comprehensive comparison is presented in Supplementary Table 2.

EC performance

In addition to their spectral characteristics, the cycling capability of STDEC smart windows is an important factor. The cycling performance of these windows is primarily influenced by the RME operation, since the mechanical rotation process greatly outperforms the electrochemical process. Figure 2f illustrates the cycling performance evolution of solar transmittance modulation during multiple RME processes. A large transmittance contrast persists for 2,000 cycles, exhibiting good durability, with only a 1% degradation observed after prolonged cycling tests. Even under environmental stresses such as extended UV exposure (Supplementary Fig. 19), weathering (Supplementary Fig. 20), and temperature/humidity fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. 21), the STDEC window retains over 90% of its optical contrast after 1,000 cycles, demonstrating robust environmental durability. Furthermore, the switching time of a ~ 10 cm2 STDEC window is evaluated to characterize the response speed of the Cu–Bi material by recording the transmittance intensity of clear and colored states at 550 nm using a fiber optic spectrometer, indicating a bleaching time of 6.6 s for Cu–Bi stripping and a coloration time of 13.8 s for Cu–Bi depositing (Supplementary Fig. 22).

The STDEC window showcases impressive bistability and ultralow energy consumption. In the open-circuit state, the STDEC window can keep its black state for a staggering 36 h with less than 2% optical loss and its clear state for the same duration without any optical loss (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 23), indicating the extra electrical power is negligible to maintain its optical state. Thus, we conclude that energy is consumed only during the switching process. To estimate the average power consumption of our STDEC window, we monitored current-time traces during the transparent and black switches (applying ±1 V within 60 s), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 24. The calculation indicates that the power consumption for our STDEC window is merely 3.35 mW cm−2, which is regarded as low energy consumption (<10 mW cm−2) in the field of EC smart window technology. Given the good bistability performance and ultralow power consumption, we calculated the year-round electricity required to switch all the STDEC windows in a medium-sized office building. A detailed description and calculation are available in Supplementary Note 3. The annual electricity demand is insignificant (Fig. 2h), representing only 0.14% to achieve the black state and 0.27% to attain the privacy state of the total annual electricity usage of a baseline building49, even when considering a cautious estimate of the windows being switched five times daily.

Color neutrality of the windows is also a crucial factor to consider. Most commercially available EC smart windows often exhibit a blue tint50, which can impact the visual experience of individuals inside and their view of the outside environment. A neutral color transition (clear-to-gray-to-black) with low haze values (Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Table 3) that minimally distorts the view through the windows is more beneficial. To clarify the color transition process related to the RME process in our STDEC window, we utilized the CIE L*a*b* color space to examine the colors associated with each optical state, where L* is lightness, while a* and b* correspond to the green-red and blue-yellow color ratios, respectively. During the deposition of the Cu–Bi film, their color coordinates decrease significantly in terms of L*, while the corresponding projections in the a* and b* plane are primarily focused in the center (Supplementary Fig. 26 and Supplementary Table 4), suggesting a neutral color transition from colorless transparency to gray and black. Upon stripping, the L* color coordinate rises markedly, returning to its original level after complete bleaching, while the a* and b* projections remain centered (Supplementary Fig. 26 and Supplementary Table 5). This indicates a reverse neutral color transition from black back to the initial transparent state. Outdoor thermal measurement

All-season solar and radiative thermal management was experimentally measured through the real-world outdoor thermal performance of the STDEC window across different seasons (e.g., summer and winter) at Nanjing University, Nanjing, China (32°07′15′′N, 118°57′17′′E). To ensure an accurate temperature measurement, we built a testing chamber as shown in Supplementary Fig. 27. This chamber was made up of polystyrene foam for thermal insulation and sunlight absorbers inside that transform transmitted solar irradiation into heat, preventing measurement errors caused by light scattering from the white, rough foam walls. The exterior was shielded by aluminum (Al) foil to reduce sunlight absorption. The thermocouples were positioned at the center of the chambers to enable continuous real-time temperature tracking. The weather station near the testing chambers can monitor all necessary weather data, including solar irradiance, ambient temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed (Supplementary Fig. 28).

In the hot summer, we conducted temperature measurements with the windows positioned both horizontally and vertically. In the horizontal test, we placed four types of samples—normal glass, low-E glass, standard RME-based EC window (lacking a back ITO low-E coating for thermal regulation), and STDEC window—on top of the testing chambers to serve as sky windows (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 29). We conducted continuous thermal measurement for the four testing chambers over 72 h from 23 August 2024 to 25 August 2024. The STDEC window consistently maintained a lower temperature compared to other window samples under clear sky conditions (Supplementary Fig. 30). During office hours (9:00‒17:00; Fig. 3b), the indoor air temperature of the STDEC window was on average 14.1 °C lower than that of the normal glass. This cooling effect reached its maximum of 22.2 °C at midday (Supplementary Fig. 31), occurring under conditions of about 46% relative humidity, approximately 1 m s−1 wind speed (Supplementary Fig. 32), and a solar irradiance of around 830 W m−2 (Supplementary Fig. 30). The STDEC window delivered a temperature reduction of 4.5 °C (Supplementary Fig. 33) compared with the standard RME-based EC window. This improvement is attributed to the reliable emissivity design of the STDEC window, which features a high emissivity on the front side for radiative cooling heat dissipation and a low emissivity on the back side to minimize secondary thermal radiation (Supplementary Fig. 29). At night (19:00‒24:00), the STDEC window exhibited a temperature that was 1.3 °C cooler than the ambient temperature of 31.9 °C (Supplementary Fig. 34) at a high relative humidity of up to 68% and a wind speed of 1.4 m s−1 (Supplementary Fig. 35). This means that even in high humidity environments, the heat built up in the testing chamber during the daytime can be efficiently dissipated into the outer space through radiative cooling. Subsequently, we observed a comparable cooling effect when window samples were positioned vertically facing south. In contrast to normal glass and EC windows, our STDEC window demonstrated an average temperature reduction of 14.9 °C and 2.9 °C (Supplementary Fig. 36), respectively, during the noon hours (12:00‒14:00) on 18 September 2024. In cold winter, we also conducted experiments on daytime solar heating and nighttime insulation performance. We applied a voltage of 1 V to gradually dissolve the black Cu–Bi film of the STDEC window over time, making it transparent (Supplementary Fig. 37) and thereby enhancing solar heating. Concurrently, we rotated the orientation of the STDEC window in space to position the low-emissivity side outward (Supplementary Fig. 37), which helped reduce internal heat loss and improve heat retention. As anticipated, the STDEC window exhibited an average temperature of 12.3 °C and 1.5 °C higher than the ambient temperature and the RME-based transparent EC window (more details about outdoor temperature performance can be found in Supplementary Fig. 38), respectively, during the winter daytime from 10:00 to 14:00 on 29 November 2024, with a solar irradiance of approximately 600 W m−2 (Supplementary Fig. 39). To further assess the insulation effect in cold nighttime conditions (Fig. 3c), we carried out a thermal experiment in Nanjing during the winter, specifically from 00:00 to 04:00 on 29 November 2024. The STDEC window demonstrated temperatures that were 1.3 °C to 1.5 °C warmer than the other reference window samples (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 40). This is due to the fact that the suppression of thermal radiation is essential for raising the interior temperature when there is no solar energy available. Over the course of the year, the designed STDEC window has an impressive capability to control indoor temperatures in response to seasonal variations. This adaptable characteristic provides substantial building energy savings, outshining the other reference window options.

Energy-saving building application

To evaluate the energy-saving efficacy of the STDEC window and its potential to reduce carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions, we performed energy simulations for a building application (Fig. 4a). We investigated the year-round heating and cooling energy consumption (Fig. 4b) in a reference medium office building model (total floor area of 4982.19 m2 and window-to-wall ratio of 33% as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 41) with low-/high-emissivity and varied Tsol windows, utilizing EnergyPlus. This calculation suggests that the yearly cooling energy consumption is clearly lower with high-emissivity windows (represented by the blue blank frame in Fig. 4b) than with low-emissivity ones, especially at low Tsol conditions. In contrast, the annual heating energy usage is greatly reduced when using low-emissivity windows (shown by the solid red dot in Fig. 4b) compared to high-emissivity windows. To determine the most effective use of the STDEC window, we then examined its energy-saving performance on a global scale, concentrating on 19 cities that encompass all climate zones. The reference medium office building with normal glass windows was established as the baseline for our analysis, after which we modified the optical and thermal properties of the windows, in accordance with our measurements detailed in Supplementary Table 6. We compared the annual HVAC energy consumption of a building fitted with the STDEC window with that of buildings fitted with normal glass, low-E glass, and RME-based EC windows. The findings indicate that buildings with STDEC windows have considerably reduced year-round HVAC energy consumption compared to those with normal glass windows (Supplementary Fig. 43). Supplementary Fig. 44 presents a color map that shows the yearly energy savings achieved by using the STDEC windows for heating and cooling in a medium office building across various cities worldwide. Cities with extreme hot and cold climates (Fig. 4c), such as Bangkok, Abu Dhabi, and Churchill, experienced the highest energy savings (over 80 MJ m−2 even when accounting for the techno-economic assessment of the STDEC windows; Supplementary Note 4) compared to cities with more variable weather, such as Nanjing (30.8 MJ m−2) and Santiago (27.7 MJ m−2). Notably, many cities in subtropical and temperate zones, despite experiencing considerable weather and seasonal fluctuations, can still achieve an annual HVAC energy saving percentage of about 20.2% (Fig. 4c). Globally, the average year-round energy conservation for building HVAC systems using the STDEC window can reach 19.5% (Fig. 4d), substantially surpassing the 9.3% savings from commercial low-E glass and the 9.1% from RME-based EC windows. The calculation results indicate that the STDEC window, which adapts to the changing weather conditions, can provide higher energy benefits across all climate zones around the world.

To further illustrate the positive impact of the STDEC window on sustainability in building operations, we computed the equivalent CO2 emission reductions that resulted from the annual energy savings. More details about the CO2 emission reductions for the 19 selected cities worldwide are available in Supplementary Table 7. The CO2 emission reduction map (Fig. 4e) also highlighted the increased potential of the STDEC window in very hot and cold areas. Applying the STDEC window in the reference medium office building resulted in an average annual decrease of 39.3 tons of CO2 emissions compared with the baseline (Supplementary Fig. 45).

Discussion

In this study, we present the design and implementation of an STDEC window that integrates RME techniques for Cu–Bi films with an easy-to-use rotating functionality, capable of independently and synergistically managing solar and thermal spectra. The STDEC window offers several advantages over existing and emerging EC smart-window strategies. First, it can actively achieve an ultrabroadband spectral tuning with impressive solar transmittance modulation of 52.2% and thermal emittance modulation of 0.87, which is beneficial for all-season cooling in summer and heating in winter within the same STDEC window. Second, it eliminates the need for expensive infrared transparent conducting electrodes (e.g., monolayer graphene, BaF2 and ZnS), which are essential for achieving thermal emittance modulation in a common infrared emissivity regulator. Finally, it can be processed by using solution methods and does not require vacuum sputtering for thick EC films51, reducing manufacturing costs and carbon footprints. All of these electrically controlled STDEC windows can be incorporated into an Internet of Things (IoT) system52,53 for smart buildings to improve the indoor microclimate and occupants′ comfort by responding to external weather conditions and the specific preferences of individuals. Despite its advantages, the STDEC window requires further improvements in operational stability, environmental durability and lifetime. Exploring alternative electrolytes represents a promising engineering route to extend window durability. In particular, developing electrolytes capable of long-term operation under high UV exposure, high humidity, and extreme high- and low-temperature conditions will be crucial for global climate adaptability. Moreover, its commercial application calls for the meter-scale architectural windows, which can be addressed through device structural design with highly conductive and transparent electrodes54, patterned electrodes, or modular units, representing a critical direction for future research and technological development. We anticipate that this work can accelerate the commercialization of smart windows and hold promise for future energy-efficient and sustainable cities, substantially reducing carbon emissions and energy consumption.

Methods

Preparation of the transparent gel electrolyte

Chemicals were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without any additional purification. The transparent gel electrolyte consists of 15 mM CuCl2, 5 mM BiCl3, 10 mM HCl, 1 M LiBr, and 0.2 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA; Mw = 47000), all dissolved in deionized water. The electrolyte was stirred in an oil bath at a temperature of 98 °C. For enhanced performance in cycle measurements, we have modified the electrolyte by increasing the concentrations of HCl and LiBr to 30 mM and 3 M, respectively.

Fabrication of the STDEC and the standard RME-based EC windows

The STDEC window and the standard REM-based EC window were all constructed in two-electrode configurations. The STDEC window was assembled with a double-sided ITO glass as the working electrode, a Cu frame surrounding the device as the counter electrode, a transparent gel electrolyte layer that provides metal cations, and an infrared-transparent PE membrane as the cover. The inner ITO layer of the working electrode was further modified with 3-nm-thick Pt, which was fabricated using magnetron sputtering at a deposition rate of 2.0 Å s−1. Butyl rubber tape with a thickness of 2 mm and a width of 5 mm was utilized as the spacer and adhesive frame of the STDEC window. Cu tapes with conductive adhesive were used to establish electrical contact to the Pt-coated ITO working electrode and the Cu frame counter electrode, respectively, serving as the wires for the STDEC window. In the standard REM-based EC window, all fabrication aspects were consistent with those of the STDEC window. However, the working electrode was replaced with a monohedral ITO glass, and the PE membrane was replaced with a standard glass cover.

Electrochemical characterization

EC and electrochemical performance assessments were conducted with amperometric current-time (i-t) curve and chronoamperometry (CA) analysis on an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760, CH Instruments). In the optical measurements of the samples, the operating voltage was set at –1 V for the constant potential electrodeposition process and +1 V for the constant potential stripping process, respectively.

Material characterization

The surface morphologies of the samples were obtained using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; GeminiSEM560, ZEISS). Elemental mappings were captured by energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS; X-Max 50, OXFORD). The bonding information of the aqueous electrolyte were analyzed through a Fourier-transform infrared spectrophotometer (IS50, Thermo Scientific). The transmissivity and reflectivity spectra for visible range (0.38–0.78 μm) and solar range (0.38–2.5 μm) were obtained with an ultraviolet–visible near-infrared spectroscope (UV-3600, SHIMADZU) fitted with an integrating sphere (ISR-310). Barium sulfate served as the reference for these measurements. Time-resolved measurements of transmittance intensity at 550 nm were monitored by a fiber optic spectrometer (AvaSpec-ULS2048CL-EVO, AVANTES). The emissivity and reflectivity spectra of the samples within the LWIR range (2.5‒20 μm) were measured using a Fourier-transform infrared spectrophotometer (Nicolet iS50R, Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an integrating sphere (4P-GPS-020-SL, Pike). A gold mirror served as the reference. All photographic images were captured using iPhone 15 Pro. All infrared images were taken with an infrared camera (TiX580, Fluke). The cycling stability of the device under extreme environmental conditions was evaluated using a 365 nm UV lamp (6 W), and a constant temperature and humidity chamber (THP-150K, Zhihe).

Outdoor thermal measurement

Experimental examinations of the thermal performance of the STDEC window and other reference samples were conducted under clear sky conditions at Nanjing University, Nanjing, China (32°07′15′′N, 118°57′17′′E) on August 23‒25, September 18, and November 29, 2024. The measurement step involved a testing chamber with external dimensions of 18 × 18 × 20 cm and internal dimensions of 8 × 8 × 15 cm, constructed from polystyrene foam for thermal insulation. The interior was equipped with a sunlight absorber that converts transmitted solar radiation into heat, eliminating measurement inaccuracies caused by light reflected off the uneven white foam walls. The exterior was shielded by Al foil to reduce sunlight absorption and limit conductive heat exchange between the interior and surrounding. The K-type thermocouples were placed at the center of the chambers to enable continuous real-time temperature monitoring with a recorder (MIK R6000C, Asmik). A weather station (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 28) was used to collect meteorological data, including solar irradiance, ambient temperature, relative humidity, and wind speed. For horizontal temperature measurements, the STDEC window and other reference samples were positioned on top of the chambers to act as sky windows. For vertical temperature measurements, these samples were arranged vertically, oriented toward the south.

Building energy saving calculation

The building energy saving calculation was conducted using EnergyPlus version 9.0.1. The energy savings of the window specimens were assessed through a whole-building energy simulation. A standard 3-story medium office building model (Supplementary Fig. 41) developed by the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory of the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) was used as the prototype55, with a floor dimension of 49.9 m (163.8 ft, length) × 33.3 m (109.2 ft, width) × 4.0 m (13 ft, height), and a window-to-wall ratio of 33%. This widely recognized model for residential buildings in the U.S. acts as a benchmark in the building energy sector, complying with applicable building codes and energy efficiency standards. It includes typical operation and occupancy schedules, as well as specifications for typical lighting/miscellaneous loads and HVAC systems. In the building model, the indoor air temperature setpoints were held constantly at 20 °C in cold weather and at 24 °C in hot weather, and the external weather used hourly weather data for a typical meteorological year. The cooling and heating loads were calculated hourly all year round.

We assessed how the solar transmission (Tsol) and long-wave infrared emissivity (ɛLWIR) affect the building cooling and heating energy consumption (Supplementary Fig. 3) based on the building model described above. Beijing, China, which has four distinct seasons, was chosen for the simulation. The baseline building model was installed with the conventional window properties (default value as downloaded). We modified the window parameters (Tsol ranging from 10 to 90% and ɛLWIR ranging from 0.1 to 0.9) to reveal their material impact on the cooling and heating energy usage of the building. The results of this analysis were obtained as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 3. The lowest energy usage for cooling is observed at ɛLWIR = 0.9 and Tsol = 10%, whereas the least energy usage for heating occurs at ɛLWIR = 0.1 and Tsol = 90%.

The same model, deposited in Supplementary Fig. 41, was proposed for global building energy efficiency simulations. Specifically, the building energy-saving performance was evaluated by measuring differences in values among various specimen types, such as STDEC window, standard RME-based EC window without thermal regulation, commercial low-E glass, and clear normal glass (baseline). In the simulation, the glass specimens were modeled as the windows of the office building around the reference medium. The optical properties of different glass specimens used in the building energy simulation are provided in Supplementary Table 6. By simulating the building model in both cooling and heating modes, we can determine the respective energy loads, and by dynamically modifying the Tsol and ɛLWIR of the window specimens, the building can function in the most energy-efficient mode. The total annual energy consumption is calculated by aggregating the hourly energy costs. Typical locations in 19 climate zones worldwide were chosen for simulation: Bangkok, Thailand (Zone 0 A, extremely hot humid), Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates (Zone 0B, extremely hot dry), Rio De Janeiro, Brazil (Zone 1 A, very hot humid), New Delhi, India (Zone 1B, very hot dry), Hong Kong, China (Zone 2 A, hot humid), Lima, Peru (Zone 2B, hot dry), Nanjing, China (Zone 3 A, warm humid), San Diego, U.S. (Zone 3B, warm dry), Santiago, Chile (Zone 3 C, warm marine), Beijing, China (Zone 4 A, mixed humid), Tianjin, China (Zone 4B, mixed dry), Pamplona, Spain (Zone 4 C, mixed marine), Graz, Austria (Zone 5 A, cool humid), Klamath Falls, U.S. (Zone 5B, cool dry), Port Hardy, Canada (Zone 5 C, cool marine), Helsinki, Finland (Zone 6 A, cold humid), Helana, U.S. (Zone 6B, cold dry), Edmonton, Canada (Zone 7, very cold), Churchill, Canada (Zone 8, subarctic/arctic). The building energy savings per unit area are based on the total floor area.

The CO2 emissions were calculated using the emission factors for electricity generation and natural gas combustion from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the U.S. DOE. The emission factors are as follows27,56: 1 kWh of electricity produces 0.709 kg of CO2, and 1 m3 of natural gas generates 1.935 kg of CO2 (https://www.epa.gov/energy/greenhouse-gas-equivalencies-calculator). A conversion factor of 25.866 m3 GJ−1 was applied to obtain the volume of natural gas consumed (https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/units-and-calculators/energy-conversion-calculators.php)27,56. We assumed that CO2 emissions from cooling are derived from electricity consumption, while those from heating are attributable to natural gas use. Therefore, we can determine the yearly CO2 emission reductions of the reference medium office building (see Supplementary Table 7 for details).
