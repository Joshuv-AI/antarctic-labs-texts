Bimekizumab Efficacy in Psoriasis by Subgroups: Post Hoc Analysis of Phase 3/3b Clinical Trials
Authors: Bruce Strober, Wolf-Henning Boehncke, James G. Krueger, Nina Magnolo, Ronald Vender, Richard B. Warren, José Manuel López Pinto, Sarah Kavanagh, Bengt Hoepken, Paolo Gisondi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPatient demographics, disease characteristics, and treatment history can impact the efficacy of biologic treatments in patients with psoriasis. Understanding the efficacy of biologics, such as bimekizumab, across diverse patient subgroups is important for optimising treatment outcomes. Here, we assess whether high overall clinical responses observed in bimekizumab-treated patients with moderate to severe plaque psoriasis are consistent across subgroups, both versus comparators and with long-term treatment.MethodsData were analysed post hoc from the BE SURE, BE VIVID, and BE READY phase 3 trials, their open-label extension (OLE) BE BRIGHT, and the BE RADIANT phase 3b trial. Patients received either bimekizumab or adalimumab to week 24 (BE SURE), bimekizumab or ustekinumab to week 52 (BE VIVID), and bimekizumab or secukinumab to week 48 (BE RADIANT). In the 3-year pooled analysis (all trials), included patients received bimekizumab continuously from baseline into the OLE. Subgroups of patients with moderate to severe plaque psoriasis were defined by age, sex, weight, disease duration, disease severity, nail involvement (modified Nail Psoriasis Severity Index > 0), and prior biologic exposure, at baseline. The proportions of patients achieving complete skin clearance (PASI 100; 100% improvement from baseline in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index) in each subgroup are reported alongside 95% confidence intervals (CI). Modified non-responder imputation (mNRI) was used for missing data.ResultsFollowing comparator-controlled periods, the proportion of bimekizumab-treated patients who achieved PASI 100 was consistent across subgroups and numerically greater versus patients who received adalimumab (to week 24), ustekinumab (to week 52), and secukinumab (to week 48) in all subgroups. Among patients who received bimekizumab continuously for 3 years (N = 1107), PASI 100 response rates remained consistent across age (68.6% [40 to < 65 years]–73.7% [≥ 65 years]), sex (69.6% [male]–71.4% [female]), weight (63.4% [≥ 103.9 kg]–75.5% [< 74.3 kg]), disease duration (65.5% [≤ 5 years]–71.1% [> 20 years]), disease severity (67.7% [PASI 12 to < 15]–71.1% [PASI ≥ 20]), nail involvement (69.1% [yes]/71.3% [no]), and prior biologic exposure (71.7% [yes]/69.1% [no]; prior anti-TNF exposure 67.6% [yes]/70.6% [no]) subgroups; 95% CIs overlapped in all instances, indicating no meaningful differences between subgroups.ConclusionBimekizumab demonstrated consistently high long-term efficacy in patients with psoriasis across diverse subgroups. Higher rates of PASI 100 were achieved with bimekizumab versus adalimumab, ustekinumab, and secukinumab, across all subgroups. These results highlight bimekizumab as an effective treatment for a broad range of people living with psoriasis.Trial RegistrationNCT03412747, NCT03370133, NCT03410992, NCT03598790, NCT03536884.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-025-01557-1.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a common, chronic, immune-related disease of the skin, estimated to affect 56 million adults globally [1], approximately 20–30% of whom have moderate to severe disease [2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease study in 2019, there were 4.6 million incident cases of psoriasis worldwide [3, 4]. Given the significant physical, mental, and social impacts associated with the disease, psoriasis represents a large global health burden [3].

A multitude of different systemic treatment options are available for psoriasis, including conventional non-biologics, biologics, and small molecule therapies [5]. The introduction of novel biologic interleukin (IL) inhibitors has made complete skin clearance an achievable treatment goal for patients with psoriasis, greatly increasing the potential for improved quality of life [6]. However, there is significant diversity among the global patient population living with psoriasis and it is important to understand the relationship between patient characteristics and the efficacy of biologic therapies to optimise treatment outcomes.

Patients with moderate to severe psoriasis are in a state of chronic systemic inflammation [7], and therefore often have increased rates of comorbidities such as obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and cardiovascular disease caused by increased insulin resistance [8–12]. Several post hoc subgroup analyses have identified obesity and T2DM as factors which significantly impact response to biologic therapies [13–15]. Additionally, while switching between biologic therapies can improve clinical outcomes for some patients, previous exposure to biologics has been linked with an impaired response to subsequent biologic therapies [16–18]. Given the chronic nature of psoriasis and the range of different therapies available, discontinuing and switching treatments is common and patients often receive more than one biologic therapy [19–23]. Furthermore, patient age, and duration and severity of disease, may also be associated with numerical differences in clinical outcomes following biologic treatment [15, 24]. There is therefore still a need for treatments that consistently provide high rates of clinical responses across a variety of patients, regardless of patient and disease characteristics [24].

Bimekizumab is a monoclonal IgG1 antibody that selectively inhibits IL-17F in addition to IL-17A, both of which are pivotal drivers of psoriasis pathogenesis [25–27]. Bimekizumab has demonstrated rapid onset of response and superior efficacy versus adalimumab, ustekinumab, and secukinumab in multiple phase 3/3b studies in patients with moderate to severe plaque psoriasis, with durable clinical responses in the long term [28–32]. Whilst post hoc analyses in patient subgroups stratified according to patient demographics, disease characteristics, and treatment histories have been performed for several other biologics [15–17, 24], such analyses for bimekizumab have not yet been reported. It is important to establish whether the efficacy of bimekizumab is consistent across heterogeneous subgroups of patients with psoriasis, particularly those with higher prevalence of the disease and increased rates of treatment failure, as these groups are most relevant for dermatologists to aid clinical decision-making.

The purpose of this post hoc analysis was to evaluate the consistency of clinical responses to bimekizumab across diverse subgroups based on baseline demographics, disease characteristics, and treatment histories. To achieve this, we investigated the long-term efficacy of bimekizumab over 3 years in these patient subgroups, and also compared response rates with adalimumab, ustekinumab, and secukinumab, over periods of up to 1 year.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

Data were analysed post hoc from the BE SURE (NCT03412747), BE VIVID (NCT03370133), and BE READY (NCT03410992) phase 3 trials, their open-label extension (OLE) BE BRIGHT (NCT03598790), and the BE RADIANT phase 3b trial (NCT03536884). Study designs and eligibility criteria have been published previously for all trials [28–32]. Study protocols, amendments, and patient informed consent were reviewed by a national, regional, or independent ethics committee or institutional review board. All studies were conducted in accordance with current version of the applicable regulatory and International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice requirements, the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [33], and the local laws of the countries involved. All participants provided informed written consent documented in accordance with local regulations.

In BE SURE, patients were randomised 1:1:1 to bimekizumab 320 mg every 4 weeks (Q4W) for 56 weeks; bimekizumab 320 mg Q4W to week 16, then every 8 weeks (Q8W) to week 56; or adalimumab 80 mg at baseline, followed by 40 mg at week 1 then every 2 weeks (Q2W), then bimekizumab 320 mg Q4W from weeks 24 to 56 [32].

In BE VIVID, patients were randomised 4:2:1 to bimekizumab, ustekinumab, or placebo. Bimekizumab 320 mg was administered Q4W to week 52. Ustekinumab 45 mg or 90 mg (in patients weighing ≤ 100 kg or > 100 kg, respectively) [34] was administered at baseline and week 4, then every 12 weeks (Q12W) thereafter [30].

In BE RADIANT, patients were randomised 1:1 to bimekizumab or secukinumab. Bimekizumab 320 mg was administered Q4W to week 16, then patients were re-randomised 1:2 to bimekizumab Q4W or Q8W to week 48. Secukinumab was administered 300 mg weekly to week 4, then Q4W to week 48 [31].

In BE READY, patients were randomised 4:1 to bimekizumab 320 mg Q4W or placebo. Bimekizumab-treated patients achieving 90% improvement from baseline in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI 90) at week 16 were re-randomised (1:1:1) to receive bimekizumab 320 mg Q4W, bimekizumab 320 mg Q8W, or placebo to week 56 [29].

Patients who completed the BE VIVID, BE SURE, and BE READY trials were eligible to enter BE BRIGHT; patients who completed the 48-week double-blinded period of BE RADIANT were eligible to enter its OLE [28, 35]. On OLE entry, patients received bimekizumab 320 mg Q4W or Q8W for up to 3 years (BE RADIANT OLE week 96) or 4 years (BE BRIGHT OLE week 144) of total treatment. All remaining bimekizumab Q4W patients were switched to bimekizumab Q8W at OLE week 16/48 (BE RADIANT/BE BRIGHT) or their next scheduled clinic visit.

Outcomes

Efficacy responses were evaluated across patient subgroups defined according to baseline demographics, disease characteristics, and treatment history. These included age (< 40, 40 to < 65, ≥ 65 years), sex (male, female), weight (quartiles), body mass index (BMI; < 25 kg/m2, 25 to < 30 kg/m2, 30 to < 35 kg/m2, and ≥ 35 kg/m2), disease duration (≤ 5 years, > 5 to 10 years, > 10 to 20 years, and > 20 years), disease severity (PASI 12 to < 15, 15 to < 20, and ≥ 20; Investigator’s Global Assessment [IGA] 3, IGA 4), nail involvement (modified nail psoriasis severity index [mNAPSI] > 0; yes, no), scalp involvement (scalp IGA > 0; yes, no), medical history of psoriatic arthritis (PsA; yes, no) or T2DM (yes, no), prior biologic treatment exposure (yes, no), anti-tumour necrosis factor (TNF) exposure (yes, no), anti-IL-17 exposure (yes, no), anti-IL-23 exposure (yes, no), and number of prior biologics (1, ≥ 2). Weight quartiles were calculated on the basis of the overall distribution in each study population for all treatment arms combined. Categories of disease severity were based on PASI ranges considered to be clinically informative for increasing severity.

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients who achieved 100% improvement from baseline in PASI (PASI 100) in each subgroup. Proportions who achieved PASI 90 in each subgroup are reported in the Supplementary Material.

Statistical Analysis

This analysis included all randomised patients who received bimekizumab or adalimumab, ustekinumab, or secukinumab from baseline in the BE SURE, BE VIVID, and BE RADIANT trials, respectively, and all patients pooled across BE VIVID, BE SURE, BE READY, and BE RADIANT who received bimekizumab continuously from baseline and entered their respective OLEs.

Responses are reported at week 24 in BE SURE, week 52 in BE VIVID, week 48 in BE RADIANT (end of the comparator-controlled periods), and OLE week 96 (year 3; pooled analysis). All patients who received bimekizumab were analysed regardless of dosing regimen (bimekizumab Total). The patient subset who received bimekizumab 320 mg Q4W to week 16 and then Q8W thereafter (bimekizumab Q4W/Q8W; the approved dosing regimen for most patients with psoriasis) [36, 37] were analysed separately for the pooled analysis.

Differences in response rates between subgroups and treatment arms were indicated by non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals (CI), with overlapping CIs indicating no robust difference. Modified non-responder imputation (mNRI) was used for missing data: patients who discontinued treatment due to lack of efficacy or treatment-related adverse events were considered non-responders, and multiple imputation was used for other missing data. Observed cases (OC) are also reported in Supplementary Table 1.

The 3-year pooled analysis included 1107 patients who received bimekizumab continuously and entered the OLEs (bimekizumab Total), of whom 374 received the approved dosing regimen (bimekizumab Q4W/Q8W). Efficacy of Bimekizumab vs Adalimumab by Subgroup After 24 Weeks: BE SURE Study

The overall proportion of patients with complete skin clearance (PASI 100) after 24 weeks of treatment was 68.5% in patients receiving bimekizumab and 31.6% in patients receiving adalimumab. In patients treated with bimekizumab, PASI 100 rates were consistent across all analysed subgroups, with overlapping 95% CIs indicating no meaningful differences in responses (Fig. 1). Compared with adalimumab, the proportions of PASI 100 responders with bimekizumab were consistently numerically higher across all subgroups at week 24; 95% CIs were non-overlapping between bimekizumab and adalimumab in almost all subgroups, indicating meaningful differences between the two treatments (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PASI 100 response rates in bimekizumab- versus adalimumab-treated patients by subgroup at week 24 (mNRI). Efficacy of Bimekizumab vs Ustekinumab by Subgroup After 52 Weeks: BE VIVID Study

After 52 weeks of treatment, 67.5% of bimekizumab-treated patients compared with 41.0% of ustekinumab-treated patients achieved PASI 100. The proportion of PASI 100 responders was consistent across all analysed subgroups of bimekizumab-treated patients, as demonstrated by overlapping 95% CIs (Fig. 2). Discussion

This post hoc analysis is the first to evaluate bimekizumab’s efficacy across diverse patient subgroups, incorporating both head-to head phase 3/3b trial data and long-term outcomes. Consistently high proportions of patients achieved complete skin clearance (PASI 100) for up to 3 years of continuous bimekizumab treatment across all the diverse patient subgroups analysed. Moreover, in all subgroups, proportions of patients with complete skin clearance were higher for bimekizumab versus adalimumab, ustekinumab, and secukinumab during the comparator-controlled periods. These findings reinforce bimekizumab’s potential as an effective treatment option across patients with heterogeneous baseline characteristics, comorbidities, and treatment histories.

Our results also demonstrate bimekizumab’s efficacy in patient subgroups that have historically proven difficult to treat. Higher body weight and obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) are well-established factors associated with reduced efficacy for biologics and previous studies have demonstrated a negative impact on clinical responses to biologic treatment for psoriasis [14, 15, 38]. For example, in two phase 3 clinical trials, PASI 75 response rates in patients receiving ustekinumab 45 mg were 76.9% in patients ≤ 100 kg vs 54.6% in patients > 100 kg after 28 weeks of treatment [38], leading to the recommendation of an increased dose (90 mg) for patients > 100 kg [34]. Dose optimisation for other biologics like secukinumab has also been evaluated in patients with obesity because of inconsistent efficacy [39]. In our analysis, bimekizumab was consistently more effective than comparators across all weight quartiles. In the highest weight quartile, bimekizumab treatment had higher rates of complete skin clearance compared with adalimumab (≥ 104.3 kg: 61.0% vs 20.2%), ustekinumab (≥ 102.6 kg: 66.9% vs 35.6%), and secukinumab (≥ 102.0 kg: 68.7% vs 36.3%). Notably, for comparator treatment arms, the lowest PASI 100 rate was always observed in the highest weight quartile, whilst for bimekizumab treatment arms this was only true in the 3-year pooled analysis. This pattern may reflect pharmacokinetic challenges in heavier patients, such as increased drug clearance or altered distribution, which could impact long-term outcomes. Nevertheless, findings from this analysis suggest that high efficacy is maintained with bimekizumab in patients with higher body weights, making it a valuable treatment option for these patients without the need for dosing adjustments, except in exceptional circumstances (e.g., body weight ≥ 120 kg: it is recommended to consider increasing dosing frequency at week 16 if PASI 100 is not achieved) [36, 37]. As highlighted by these results, it is important to closely monitor treatment response in patients with higher body weight and to utilise individualised dosing strategies when appropriate.

Prior biologic exposure has also been associated with reduced responses to subsequent biologic treatments [16, 17, 40]. While biologics have shown impressive efficacy in treating moderate to severe psoriasis, only an estimated 53% of patients remain on their first course of biological therapy after 3 years [41]. This suggests that patients often switch biologic treatment to potentially improve their chances of achieving and maintaining long-term efficacy [24, 42]. In this analysis, it seems that prior exposure to biologics may not impact subsequent efficacy with bimekizumab, with the proportion of bimekizumab-treated PASI 100 responders with prior biologic experience being consistent with biologic-naive patients through 3 years (71.7% vs 69.1%). Moreover, greater proportions of biologic-experienced patients treated with bimekizumab achieved PASI 100 at the end of comparator-controlled periods compared with adalimumab, ustekinumab, and secukinumab.

Comorbidities are common in patients with psoriasis and T2DM is another factor that has been shown to negatively impact the efficacy of biologics; increased biologic clearance and reduced clinical responses have been reported in patients with psoriasis and concomitant T2DM, an effect likely compounded by its association with obesity [15, 43, 44]. For example, an analysis of guselkumab-treated patients revealed that the absence of a T2DM diagnosis was significantly associated with complete skin clearance [15]. In contrast, we found that the rate of PASI 100 achievement was consistent among bimekizumab-treated patients regardless of a T2DM diagnosis through 3 years (T2DM diagnosis: 71.6% [n = 86]; no T2DM diagnosis: 70.0% [n = 1021]). However, the number of patients with both psoriasis and T2DM was low across the studies included here, limiting the ability to draw reliable conclusions about the relative efficacy of bimekizumab in these patients.

Previous post hoc subgroup analyses involving the IL-23 inhibitors guselkumab and risankizumab have also focused on patient subgroups which historically exhibit lower responses to biologic treatments, such as patients with higher body weights and prior biologic exposure [45, 46]. At 24 weeks, guselkumab treatment resulted in completely clear skin (IGA 0) in 48.0% (n = 225) and 44.0% (n = 207) of patients weighing 86.4 to < 100 kg and ≥ 100 kg, respectively, and 47.7% (n = 172) of biologic-experienced patients [45]. In our analysis, 24 weeks of bimekizumab treatment resulted in complete skin clearance rates of 63.7% (patients weighing 87.0 to < 104.3 kg), 61.0% (patients weighing ≥ 104.3 kg), and 75.0% (biologic-experienced patients) for these groups. Meanwhile, long-term data show that after approximately 3 years (160 weeks) of risankizumab treatment, 61.1% (n = 262) of patients > 86.6 kg achieved PASI 100 [46]; in our analysis, 66.5% of bimekizumab-treated patients ≥ 87.3 kg achieved PASI 100 after 3 years. While results from different studies and clinical programmes are not directly comparable, considering bimekizumab’s consistently high efficacy in the context of published literature on IL-23 inhibitors, such as guselkumab and risankizumab, may provide insights into the impact of dual IL-17F and IL-17A inhibition on efficacy across subgroups. Further studies exploring these differences directly could be valuable.

Results across patient subgroups of different disease durations can also help to optimise treatment choices and clinical decision-making regarding the appropriate timing for biologic use. In this analysis, bimekizumab treatment displayed high rates of complete skin clearance regardless of patients’ disease duration. The consistent efficacy of bimekizumab across different disease duration subgroups supports its use for patients early in their disease as well as those with long-term disease. Furthermore, if used as a first-line treatment, bimekizumab’s consistently high efficacy in diverse patient subgroups may help to reduce unnecessary switching between biologic therapies as a result of inadequate responses in patients. However, patients with shorter disease duration ≤ 2 years were not analysed here because of the small number of patients in this subgroup. Data in this subgroup would provide valuable insights into how early treatment may impact patients with psoriasis in the short and long term.

Strengths of this study include the granularity of subgroups adding clinical relevance to the results, and the randomisation of patients likely balancing the numbers receiving background study medications between subgroups. Limitations include the post hoc nature of this analysis and the lack of adjustment for multiple comparisons increasing the risk of a false positive, inter-study variation in treatment duration, the small patient numbers in some subgroups, and the lack of long-term comparator data which is limited to up to 1 year in these trials. However, the impact of the lack of an OLE comparator group is minimised for this analysis as it focuses on the variation within subgroups of the bimekizumab treatment arm.

Conclusion

Overall, these results demonstrate bimekizumab’s consistently high efficacy, up to 3 years, across diverse patient subgroups. Moreover, bimekizumab achieved higher rates of complete skin clearance compared with adalimumab, ustekinumab, and secukinumab across all subgroups analysed. These findings reinforce the value of bimekizumab for patients, clinicians, and payers as a treatment option which provides effective and durable disease control across a broad spectrum of patients with moderate to severe psoriasis.
