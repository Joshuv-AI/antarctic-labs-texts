Effects of naturally oxidized n-3 oil sources on performance, egg quality, yolk fatty acid profile, serum biochemical parameters and liver histopathology in laying Japanese quail
Authors: Ahmet Yusuf Şengül
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough n-3-rich oils are widely used to improve the fatty acid profile of eggs, their susceptibility to oxidation may reduce their nutritional benefits and adversely affect animal health. However, the effects of naturally oxidized n-3 oil sources in laying Japanese quail remain unclear. This study therefore evaluated the effects of naturally oxidized plant and animal oil sources on productive performance, egg quality, egg yolk fatty acid profile, serum biochemical parameters, and liver histopathology. A total of 160 laying Japanese quail (Coturnix coturnix japonica) were assigned to four dietary treatments containing 1.5% non-oxidized sunflower oil, naturally oxidized chia oil, naturally oxidized flaxseed oil, or naturally oxidized salmon oil, designated as the Control, CO, FO, and SO groups, respectively. Each treatment consisted of five replicates of eight birds, and the experiment lasted 6 weeks. Egg production and feed conversion ratio were not affected by dietary treatment. However, feed intake decreased in the FO group, whereas egg weight decreased in the FO and SO groups (P < 0.05). Among the egg quality traits, yolk index increased in the CO group, while albumen index decreased in the FO group (P < 0.05); the remaining traits were unaffected. Egg yolk n-3 fatty acid content and the n-3/n-6 ratio increased significantly in the CO and FO groups (P < 0.01), whereas yolk cholesterol concentration and serum biochemical parameters did not differ among treatments. Liver histology was normal in the Control group, while the oxidized oil groups exhibited histopathological alterations, predominantly steatosis, with severity varying among oil sources. These findings indicate that naturally oxidized chia and flaxseed oils can enhance the n-3 fatty acid profile of egg yolk, but their potential effects on performance and liver health should be considered. Therefore, both oil source and oxidative status are important practical considerations when formulating n-3-enriched diets for laying Japanese quail.Keywords: Oxidized oil, Omega-3, Fatty acids, Egg quality, Liver histopathology

Introduction

Poultry and poultry products are among the most important sources of dietary protein worldwide (Mas et al., 2023). Among these products, eggs represent an important source of animal protein because of their high nutritional value and relatively low cost (Gao et al., 2021). Commercial table eggs are generally rich in n-6 fatty acids but contain relatively low levels of n-3 fatty acids (Imran et al., 2015). This fatty acid profile is largely associated with the predominance of n-6 fatty acids in corn and soybean, which are widely used in poultry diets and account for more than half of their total lipid content (Cherian, 2015; Abd El Hack et al., 2024). Growing consumer interest in healthy and functional foods has stimulated efforts to improve the nutritional value of poultry products (Shafey et al., 2015). The fatty acid profile of egg yolk can be modified through dietary manipulation, and lipid sources rich in n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) can improve the lipid quality of egg yolk (Fraeye et al., 2012; Javed et al., 2019).

One of the most widely used strategies for enriching eggs with n-3 PUFA is the dietary inclusion of fish oil, flaxseed oil, or chia oil (Elkin et al., 2015; Coorey et al., 2015; Alagawany et al., 2019; Abd El Hack et al., 2024; Elkin and Harvatine, 2023). Chia and flaxseed oils are rich sources of alpha linolenic acid (ALA), whereas fish oil provides eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) (Shibani et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2016; Dong et al., 2018; Alagawany et al., 2019; Kulczyński et al., 2019; Mendonça et al., 2022). However, fish oil may induce undesirable sensory changes in eggs, whereas the limited conversion of ALA to DHA may hinder the achievement of the desired DHA concentration when plant derived oils are used (Cherian et al., 2007; Pilevar et al., 2011; Gakhar et al., 2012; Coorey et al., 2015; Neijat et al., 2016; Zettel and Hitzmann, 2018; Lee et al., 2021; Irawan et al., 2022).

Nevertheless, chia and flaxseed oils are susceptible to oxidation during processing and storage because of their high ALA contents, while fish oil is also highly prone to oxidative deterioration (Kazuo, 2019; Jung et al., 2021). Exposure to heat, light, oxygen, and transition metals increases the susceptibility of PUFA rich oils to lipid peroxidation (Shurson et al., 2015). During lipid oxidation, hydroperoxides are initially formed and subsequently degraded into secondary and tertiary oxidation products, including aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols, which may adversely affect animal health and productivity (Shurson et al., 2015). Consequently, n-3 rich oils used in poultry diets may undergo natural oxidation during feed production, transportation, and storage as exposure to environmental factors continues. Whereas natural oxidation develops gradually under these conditions, artificial or accelerated oxidation is induced over a shorter period through procedures such as exposure to high temperatures and continuous airflow (Zhang et al., 2022, 2023; Symoniuk et al., 2018).

Studies in poultry have shown that oxidized oils may exert variable effects on productive performance, egg quality, egg yolk fatty acid composition, and serum biochemical parameters. In laying hens, oxidized soybean oil reduced feed intake and egg production, did not alter egg quality traits, and increased total saturated fatty acids while decreasing total PUFA in egg yolk (Arbabi Motlagh et al., 2022). Thermally oxidized soybean oil affected feed conversion ratio and certain serum lipid parameters (Yue et al., 2011), whereas oxidized fish oil had no significant effect on productive performance (Liu et al., 2022). In broiler chickens, increasing the peroxide value of the diet reduced body weight and average daily gain (Liang et al., 2015).

In Japanese quail, however, studies examining the effects of oxidized oils remain limited and have primarily focused on the growing period. Soltan et al. (2023) reported that replacing a fresh sunflower and soybean oil mixture with its oxidized counterpart did not affect growth performance or selected serum biochemical parameters but induced vascular and parenchymal alterations in the liver. Ghasemi Sadabadi et al. (2020) found that increasing the peroxide value of dietary soybean oil reduced body weight gain and impaired feed conversion ratio. Nosser and Khalifa (2020) further reported that thermally oxidized oil increased serum total cholesterol, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and malondialdehyde concentrations while decreasing total antioxidant capacity.

The comparative effects of naturally oxidized plant and animal sources of n-3 fatty acids on productive performance, egg quality, egg yolk fatty acid profile, serum biochemical parameters, and liver histopathology in laying Japanese quail remain insufficiently understood. The present study therefore aimed to compare the effects of naturally oxidized chia, flaxseed, and fish oils on these parameters in laying Japanese quail.

Materials and methods

Dietary oil sources

The study evaluating the effects of dietary supplementation with naturally oxidized plant- and animal-derived n-3 sources in quail diets was conducted at the Poultry Research and Application Unit of the Faculty of Agriculture, Bingöl University (Bingöl, Türkiye; 38°53′ N, 40°29′ E). The sunflower oil used in the control group was obtained fresh from the manufacturer and was not subjected to any oxidation treatment. Cold-pressed chia oil (Tito), cold-pressed flaxseed oil (Oil King), and salmon oil (Matt) used in the experiment were purchased from a local market. The oils were subjected to natural oxidation for 60 days under an average temperature of 27.2°C and a light intensity of 1120 lux (HOBO Datalogger MX2202). The peroxide values of the oxidized oils were determined to be 42.1, 56.7, and 67.3 meq/kg, respectively.

Diet formulation, husbandry and sample collection

A total of 160 laying Japanese quail at 15 weeks of age were used in the study. The experiment was conducted for 6 weeks with 5 replicates, and 8 quail were randomly allocated to each replicate. Four dietary treatment groups were established: the control group received 1.5% sunflower oil (no detectable n-3 fatty acids), whereas the other three groups were supplemented with 1.5% chia oil (CO), flaxseed oil (FO), and salmon oil (SO), respectively. At the beginning of the experiment, body weight and egg production were similar among all groups. The birds were managed and fed in accordance with European Union guidelines for the care and use of animals. Nutrient requirements of the quail were formulated according to the Nutrient Requirements of Poultry published by the National Research Council (NRC, 1994). The fatty acid profiles of the experimental oil sources and basal diet and the composition of the experimental diets are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. The diets were prepared 1 week before the start of the trial and stored in a cool room until the end of the experiment. Feed was offered daily at 50 g/bird, and water was provided ad libitum. The lighting schedule was set at 16 h light and 8 h dark. Feed intake (FI, g), egg production (eggs/hen per day), egg weight, and feed conversion ratio (FCR, g feed/g egg mass) were recorded daily (Pappas et al., 2023). At the end of the experiment, five eggs were randomly collected from each replicate (25 eggs per treatment; 100 eggs in total), and both internal and external egg quality traits were evaluated. During internal quality assessment, eggs were separated into albumen and yolk, and the samples were stored at −24°C until chemical analysis.

Analyses

Egg quality

Internal and external egg quality traits were evaluated (Şengül et al., 2025). External quality measurements included egg width, egg length, egg weight, shell thickness, and shell weight, and the shape index was calculated accordingly. Egg weight was measured using a digital balance (Denver Instrument SI-234, Germany). Egg width and length were determined using a digital caliper (Digital Caliper DIN 862, Germany). After the eggs were broken, the shells separated from the yolk and albumen were dried in an oven at 80°C overnight (Memmert UF30, Germany) and then weighed using a digital balance (Denver Instrument SI-234, Germany). Eggshell thickness (mm), expressed as the mean of measurements taken at the sharp end, blunt end, and equatorial region of the egg, was determined using a digital caliper (Mitutoyo 547-360S Absolute Digimatic, Japan).

For internal egg quality assessment, yolk and albumen weight and height were measured, whereas yolk color was determined using the 15-point DSM Yolk Color Fan scale (Bageridizaj et al., 2006; Pappas et al., 2023). Yolk and albumen heights were measured using a tripod micrometer (Insize 3127, China). Yolk diameter (mm) and albumen diameter (mm; mean of two perpendicular measurements) were determined using a digital caliper. The yolk was separated from the albumen, and yolk weight was measured using a digital balance (Denver Instrument SI-234, Germany). Egg chemical analyses

Egg yolk fatty acid content

The method described by Folch et al. (1957) was used to determine the fatty acid profile in egg yolks. For this purpose, an Optima delta-6-0.25 µm (100 m × 0.25 mm ID) column, an Agilent 7890A/5970C Gas Chromatography/Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) system, and an FID detector were used (Agilent, California, USA). Chromatographic conditions were as follows: Oven temperature started at 120 °C, reached 250 °C (5 °C/min), and was kept at this temperature for 3 minutes. Then it reached 270 °C (2 °C/min), it was kept at this temperature for 16 minutes, and this process lasted 55 minutes in total. The injection volume was 1 µl. Before and after each injection, the syringe was washed 5 times with hexane. The main sample was withdrawn after 2 samples were withdrawn and dropped into the waste bottle. Thus, contamination from the previous sample was prevented. Based on the obtained fatty acid data, n-3 and n-6 fatty acids, saturated fatty acids (SFA), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) were calculated. In addition, the n-3/n-6 ratio, which is important for human health, was also calculated and evaluated (Pappas et al., 2023).

Atherogenicity index = (C12:0+(4×C14:0)+C16:0) / (MUFA+PUFA)

Egg yolk cholesterol content

Samples were taken from crude fats obtained by the method of Folch et al. (1957) for cholesterol analysis. For cholesterol analysis, a silica column (Restek Rtx-65TG, 30 m × 0.32 mm ID, 0.1 μm film thickness; 65% diphenyl / 35% dimethyl-polysiloxane) and a Flame Ionization Detector (FID) were used in the GC system (Restek International, Bellefonte, PA, USA). After the column oven temperature reached 150 °C at a rate of 30 °C/min, it was further increased to 360 °C at a rate of 15 °C/min and held for 3 minutes. In the GC system, the detector temperature was set to 370 °C, the injection port temperature to 300 °C, and the injection split ratio to 1:25. Helium was used as the carrier gas (Cetinkaya and Oz, 2018).

Serum biochemical parameters

At the end of the study (day 42), four birds from each group were randomly slaughtered, and blood samples were collected into BD Vacutainer tubes containing gel and yellow caps. The tubes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes (Universal 320R, Hettich, Tuttlingen, Germany), and the resulting sera were frozen and stored at −80 °C (Elcold Lab 11) until analysis. Total cholesterol, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (Beckman Coulter AST OSR 6109), and calcium (Ca) levels were analyzed by the photometric method using commercial kits (Beckman Coulter OSR) on an autoanalyzer (Olympus AU400 Chemistry Analyzer, OLY-AU400).

Liver histopathology

Liver tissue samples collected after euthanasia were fixed in 10% buffered formaldehyde solution. Following fixation, routine histological tissue processing procedures were performed. In this context, tissues subjected to dehydration, clearing, and impregnation were embedded in paraffin blocks. From each paraffin block, tissue sections of 4 µm thickness were cut using a rotary microtome and mounted on glass slides. After being kept in an oven for approximately 1 h, the tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histopathological examination. Finally, the slides were coverslipped with Entellan and examined under a light microscope.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The replicate cage was considered the experimental unit. Data were analyzed using one way ANOVA, and differences among means were determined by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05.

Egg Yolk Fatty Acid Profile, Atherogenicity Index, and Egg Yolk Cholesterol

The effects of dietary supplementation with different n-3 sources on yolk fatty acid composition are presented in Table 5. Among the groups, the SO group had the highest pentadecanoic acid (C15:0) concentration (P < 0.05). In terms of α-linolenic acid (ALA), dietary inclusion of chia oil and flaxseed oil resulted in a significant increase (P < 0.01), whereas the control and SO groups showed similar ALA levels. The plant-derived n-3 oil sources in the CO and FO groups resulted in lower γ-linolenic acid (GLA) levels than in the control group (P < 0.05). With respect to arachidic acid (C20:0), the FO and SO groups had the lowest concentrations, whereas arachidonic acid (C20:4) was lower in all treatment groups (CO, FO, and SO) than in the control group (P < 0.01). Total yolk n-3 levels were higher in the CO and FO groups than in the other groups, whereas the SO group did not differ from the control group (P < 0.01). In contrast, total n-6 levels were lower in the CO and FO groups than in the other groups (P < 0.05), and the highest n-3/n-6 ratios were also observed in the CO and FO groups. No significant differences were detected for the other fatty acid parameters, the atherogenicity index, or egg yolk cholesterol.

Liver Histopathology

Liver histopathology was also evaluated in quail fed diets supplemented with naturally oxidized n-3 oil sources. Whereas the control group exhibited normal hepatic architecture without pathological alterations, severe steatosis was observed in hepatocytes of the CO group (Fig. 1). The steatosis was characterized by both microvesicular and macrovesicular lipid accumulation. In addition, marked fatty degeneration, mild hydropic degeneration, multifocal necrosis, sinusoidal dilatation, vascular hyperemia, mild hemorrhage, and limited mononuclear leukocyte infiltration were observed. Early fibrotic changes were also evident in some interstitial areas. In the FO group, similar histopathological findings were observed, but with lower severity than in the CO group, including hepatocellular steatosis and vascular alterations such as hyperemia and hemorrhage. The SO group exhibited comparable histopathological alterations of milder severity than those observed in the CO and FO groups. Although hepatic steatosis was also evident in this group, the histological appearance was closer to that of the control group.

Discussion

Previous studies have reported that dietary supplementation with n-3 PUFA sources may reduce feed intake in poultry (Grobas et al., 2001; Ebeid et al., 2008; Gao et al., 2021). However, the absence of a reduction in feed intake in the CO group, despite its high n-3 PUFA content, suggests that feed intake may also be influenced by oil specific factors, including palatability and oxidation related changes in taste and aroma (Rizzi et al., 2009; Gao et al., 2021; Qaisrani et al., 2021). The reduced egg weight observed in the FO group may be partly associated with lower feed intake. In addition, Dunn Horrocks et al. (2011) suggested that flaxseed products may influence egg weight through possible interactions with vitamin B6 dependent amino acid metabolism. The decrease in egg weight observed in the SO group is also consistent with previous reports indicating that fish oil supplementation above 1.25% can adversely affect egg weight (Ebeid et al., 2008; Saleh, 2013). Egg weight is closely related to yolk lipid deposition and may be influenced by the dietary linoleic acid level, which is involved in lipoprotein synthesis (March and Macmillan, 1990). In the present study, groups receiving oils with lower linoleic acid content also had lower egg weights, suggesting a possible contribution of this fatty acid to egg size. In addition, increased dietary n-3 fatty acid intake may reduce circulating triglyceride availability and thereby limit lipid supply for yolk formation (von Schacky, 2006; Kamely et al., 2016). Therefore, the differences in egg weight among studies may be explained by variations in oil source, fatty acid composition, oxidation status, and nutrient utilization.

The effects of dietary n-3 rich oil sources on egg quality traits have been reported inconsistently in the literature. Flaxseed oil supplementation has been reported to affect egg weight or internal egg quality traits in some studies (Kim et al., 2016a; Reda et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2021), whereas other studies found limited or no significant effects on egg quality parameters (Tallarico et al., 2002; Basmacıoglu et al., 2003; Celebi and Macit, 2008; Kim et al., 2016b; El-Zenary et al., 2023). Similarly, fish oil and other n-3 oil sources may influence yolk related quality traits (Ceylan et al., 2011; Mousavi et al., 2017; Saleh, 2013), although negligible effects on several egg quality traits have also been reported (Elkin et al., 2015; Alagawany et al., 2019). These discrepancies may be associated with differences in oil source, inclusion level, fatty acid composition, oxidative status, and nutrient utilization.

In the present study, supplementation of laying quail diets with different naturally oxidized n-3 oil sources affected only certain internal egg quality traits. Chia oil supplementation increased yolk index, whereas flaxseed oil reduced albumen index; the remaining internal and external egg quality parameters were not significantly affected. The type and oxidative status of the oil used in the diet may affect the internal quality characteristics of eggs (Zhang et al., 2021). Yolk index, defined as the ratio of yolk height to yolk diameter, is an important quality criterion and is considered an indicator of the physical integrity of the yolk (Reda et al., 2020). Therefore, the increase in yolk index observed in the CO group suggests that dietary oil source and oxidation related characteristics may influence yolk morphology and yolk related quality traits (Guclu et al., 2008; Ceylan et al., 2011). In addition, previous reports showing that fish oil or n-3 rich oil sources may alter yolk related traits support the view that dietary fatty acid profile can influence yolk quality (Mousavi et al., 2017; Saleh, 2013).

The decrease in albumen index observed in the FO group may indicate that flaxseed oil negatively affected albumen quality. Albumen index reflects albumen viscosity and is considered an important indicator of internal egg quality. Since albumen is synthesized in the oviduct, dietary fatty acids may influence albumen formation through their effects on protein synthesis in oviduct tissue (Whitehead et al., 1991). Previous studies have generally reported limited or inconsistent effects of flaxseed oil on albumen traits and Haugh unit (Reda et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2016b; Altaçlı et al., 2022). However, positive effects of flaxseed supplementation on albumen height and Haugh unit have also been reported (Lee et al., 2016, 2021).

With respect to external egg quality, shell weight, shell thickness, and shape index were not affected by the treatments. Similar findings have been reported in studies showing that dietary oil supplementation does not markedly affect shell weight or shell thickness (Tallarico et al., 2002; Basmacıoglu et al., 2003; Celebi and Macit, 2008). This result is also consistent with reports indicating limited effects of dietary oil sources on external egg quality traits (Elkin et al., 2015; Reda et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2016a; Şengül, 2022; El-Zenary et al., 2023).

In the present study, different naturally oxidized oil sources caused significant changes in C15:0, C18:3 n-3 (ALA), C18:3 n-6 (GLA), C20:0, C20:4 n-6, total n-3 fatty acids, total n-6 fatty acids, and the n-3/n-6 ratio, whereas the other yolk fatty acid parameters were not significantly affected. The most pronounced response was the reduction in arachidonic acid (C20:4 n-6) in the n-3 supplemented groups. Similar reductions in yolk C20:4 n-6 have been reported in hens fed flaxseed oil, fish oil, or flaxseed combined with dried anchovy (Yi et al., 2014; Yalçın and Ünal, 2010). In the present study, yolk arachidonic acid decreased by at least 46% in the n-3 supplemented groups compared with the control group, although the arachidonic acid contents of the control and experimental oils were similar. This suggests that the reduction was not due to direct dietary arachidonic acid supply, but rather to the effect of n-3 fatty acids on arachidonic acid metabolism. This interpretation is supported by previous reports indicating that ALA and its metabolites can reduce C20:4 n-6 formation and displace it from phospholipids (Mathews and van Holde, 1990; Jiang et al., 1991). Moreover, because ALA and linoleic acid use the same Δ6 desaturase pathway, increased dietary ALA may reduce the synthesis of C20:4 n-6 from C18:2 n-6 (Petrović et al., 2012).

Yolk ALA and total n-3 fatty acid concentrations generally reflected the fatty acid composition of the diets. The CO group, which had the highest dietary ALA and total n-3 levels, also had the highest yolk ALA and total n-3 concentrations, whereas the SO group showed a weaker response. This agrees with the view that dietary fatty acid composition directly influences the egg yolk fatty acid profile (Gao et al., 2021). Similar increases in yolk n-3 concentration and the n-3/n-6 ratio after chia oil, flaxseed oil, or fish oil supplementation have also been reported previously (Castro Tamayo et al., 2020; Şengül, 2022; Kamely et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2016a). Likewise, the increase in yolk ALA observed in the CO and FO groups is consistent with studies showing enhanced ALA deposition following dietary chia oil or flaxseed oil supplementation (Çitil et al., 2011; Meireles et al., 2023; Egila et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2016b). In contrast, total yolk n-6 fatty acids were highest in the control group and generally lower in the n-3 supplemented groups, supporting previous findings that dietary n-3 fatty acids may reduce yolk n-6 concentrations (Petrović et al., 2012). However, the SO group showed yolk n-6 levels similar to those of the control group, which may be related to its lower dietary n-3 content compared with the CO and FO groups.

The responses of individual fatty acids such as C15:0, C18:3 n-6, and C20:0 appear to be more variable and may depend on oil source and fatty acid composition. Previous studies have reported inconsistent effects of flaxseed oil or fish oil on these individual fatty acids (Basmacıoglu et al., 2003; Çitil et al., 2011; Petrović et al., 2012; Adabi et al., 2016; Egila et al., 2023; Meireles et al., 2023). In the present study, EPA and DHA increased numerically by up to 50% and 29%, respectively, but these changes were not statistically significant. This limited response may be associated with the oxidative status of the oils. Oxidation can reduce EPA and DHA concentrations in oil and decrease their intestinal assimilation (Dasilva et al., 2018; Phung et al., 2020). In addition, lipid peroxidation may reduce intestinal villus height and thereby limit the absorptive surface area available for nutrient uptake (Qaisrani et al., 2021). Thus, oxidation may have limited the transfer of long chain n-3 fatty acids into egg yolk by reducing both their concentration in the oil and their bioavailability.

The effects of n-3 rich oil sources on yolk cholesterol are also inconsistent in the literature. Some studies reported reduced yolk cholesterol following flaxseed oil or fish oil supplementation, whereas others found no significant effect (Basmacıoglu et al., 2003; Ebeid et al., 2008; Dalle Zotte et al., 2015; Rizzi et al., 2009; Altaçlı et al., 2022). Petrović et al. (2012) also reported no significant effect of flaxseed oil on yolk cholesterol and indicated that age may be an important determinant of this parameter. Although chia seed has been reported to reduce yolk cholesterol, no study was identified that directly evaluated the effect of chia oil on yolk cholesterol (Ayerza and Coates, 2000). In the present study, yolk cholesterol concentration and atherogenicity index were not significantly affected by the dietary treatments. Therefore, these results suggest that, despite changes in some individual fatty acids and total n-3 and n-6 fatty acid levels, the use of different naturally oxidized n-3 oil sources did not markedly alter yolk cholesterol or the overall atherogenic potential of the yolk lipid fraction.

Our results indicated that dietary supplementation with 1.5% naturally oxidized chia oil, flaxseed oil, and salmon oil had no significant effect on ALP, ALT, AST, total cholesterol, or calcium levels. Previous studies conducted in growing Japanese quail have reported that oxidized oils may exert different effects on serum liver enzymes, lipid metabolism, and oxidative status. Nosser and Khalifa (2020) reported that dietary supplementation with 2% oil oxidized by heating at 180°C for 48 h increased serum ALT, AST, total cholesterol, and MDA levels, while decreasing total antioxidant capacity. Similarly, Ghasemi Sadabadi et al. (2020) found that the use of soybean oils oxidized through heating and aeration increased serum AST, ALT, MDA, and total cholesterol levels, whereas SOD and GPx activities were reduced. In contrast, Soltan et al. (2023) reported that the use of recycled frying oil composed of sunflower and soybean oil had no significant effect on serum ALT and total cholesterol levels, although it altered AST activity.

The fact that these studies were based on artificially induced or high temperature driven oxidation processes rather than natural oxidation may partly explain why their findings are not fully consistent with those of the present study, in which naturally oxidized n-3 oil sources were used. Indeed, although oxidation at high temperature and natural oxidation at room temperature are based on the same free radical mechanism, the oxidation products formed may differ (Kaleem et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2022). Moreover, comparisons among oxidized oils based solely on peroxide values may not be sufficient. This is because peroxide value mainly reflects primary oxidation products, whereas secondary oxidation products formed during the oxidation process may also be important in terms of biological effects. In this regard, Tres et al. (2013) reported that peroxide value alone does not adequately reflect oil oxidation and that secondary oxidation indicators should also be evaluated. Therefore, comparing oils obtained through different oxidation methods solely on the basis of peroxide values may be insufficient to explain differences in metabolic responses. This may suggest that the metabolic effects of naturally and artificially oxidized oils can differ.

Studies using unoxidized n-3 oil sources also indicate that the effects of these oils on blood biochemistry may vary depending on the oil source, inclusion level, and experimental conditions. Basmacıoglu et al. (2003) reported that supplementation of laying hen diets with 1.5% fish oil did not affect blood cholesterol levels. Similarly, Alagawany et al. (2020) reported that chia oil supplementation did not affect AST levels, while Reda et al. (2020) found that 1.5% flaxseed oil supplementation did not alter AST levels. However, in the same studies, chia oil and flaxseed oil were reported to increase ALT levels and decrease total cholesterol. Lee et al. (2021) and Neijat et al. (2016) reported that low levels of flaxseed oil supplementation had no significant effect on total cholesterol levels. At higher inclusion levels, the effects on blood biochemistry appear to become more pronounced. Al-Daraji et al. (2010) reported that supplementation with 3% flaxseed oil and 3% fish oil decreased total cholesterol, AST, and ALT levels, while increasing ALP and calcium levels. Mousavi et al. (2017) also reported that 3% fish oil supplementation reduced cholesterol, AST, and ALP levels. Taken together, these findings suggest that the absence of significant effects of 1.5% naturally oxidized chia oil, flaxseed oil, and salmon oil on blood parameters in the present study may be related not only to the oil source and inclusion level, but also to the type of oxidation.

The histopathological findings indicate that dietary supplementation with naturally oxidized n-3 oil sources caused hepatic alterations of varying severity, with the most pronounced effects observed in the CO group. These alterations may be associated with the potential adverse effects of lipid oxidation products derived from naturally oxidized oils on hepatic lipid metabolism and cellular integrity. Zhang et al. (2023) reported that naturally oxidized corn oil altered hepatic lipid metabolism and may have increased lipid accumulation in broilers. Lindblom et al. (2019) also demonstrated that hepatic responses to peroxidized oils may vary depending on the oil source and the degree of oxidation. Similarly, Qaisrani et al. (2021) suggested that oxidative compounds may contribute to tissue damage and necrotic alterations in different organs. In a study conducted in quail, Soltan et al. (2023) reported that dietary supplementation with a 2% mixture of oxidized sunflower and soybean oils caused severe hepatic congestion, vascular dilatation, and compression of the surrounding parenchymal cells. These findings partially correspond to the sinusoidal dilatation, vascular hyperemia, and hemorrhage observed in the groups fed oxidized n-3 oil sources in the present study. Therefore, the differences in the severity of hepatic lesions among the treatment groups may have resulted from differences in the fatty acid composition, oxidative stability, and oxidation products of the oils used.

Nevertheless, the absence of significant changes in serum liver enzymes and other biochemical parameters in the present study is a noteworthy finding. However, unchanged serum biochemical parameters do not necessarily indicate the absence of histopathological alterations in the liver. Indeed, Rinella et al. (2023) reported that aminotransferase levels may frequently remain normal in patients with advanced liver disease associated with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis and that these enzymes should not be used alone to exclude the presence of nonalcoholic steatohepatitis with clinically significant fibrosis. In addition, the lack of a significant change in serum total cholesterol does not exclude hepatic lipid accumulation. Total cholesterol is only a single parameter reflecting circulating lipid metabolism and may not always directly represent lipid deposition in the liver. Wu et al. (2016) reported that the severity of hepatic steatosis was associated with lipid ratios such as TC/HDL C and TG/HDL C rather than total cholesterol alone. This suggests that serum biochemical indicators and tissue level histopathological alterations may not always progress simultaneously.

Recent studies have also shown that hepatic alterations associated with lipid accumulation in laying poultry may be linked to the gut liver axis. Guo et al. (2026) reported that fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome in laying hens is associated with hepatic lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and inflammation, and that changes in cecal metabolites and microbiota may also play a role in this process. Similarly, Zhang et al. (2026) indicated that the gut microbiota and microbial metabolites may influence hepatic lipid metabolism, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Therefore, the absence of significant changes in serum ALP, ALT, AST, and total cholesterol levels in the present study does not exclude hepatic lipid accumulation. The observed hepatic steatosis and degenerative alterations may be associated not only with the direct hepatic effects of lipid oxidation products, but also with metabolic and inflammatory processes related to the gut liver axis. However, since microbiota, bile acid, and metabolomic analyses were not performed in the present study, this mechanism should be confirmed in further studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the use of different naturally oxidized n-3 oil sources in diets for laying Japanese quail did not cause any significant changes in yolk cholesterol concentration or serum biochemical parameters, while it increased yolk n-3 fatty acid content and the n-3/n-6 ratio, particularly in the CO and FO groups. However, no comparable improvement in these parameters was observed in the SO group. Nevertheless, the occurrence of limited changes in certain performance traits and the presence of histopathological findings in liver tissue indicate that the effects of these oil sources on hepatic tissue should also be taken into consideration.
