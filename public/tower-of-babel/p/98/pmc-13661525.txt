The Immediate and sustained benefits of two-week high-frequency aerobic exercise on depressive symptoms and frontal function in subthreshold depression: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Lin Li, Jingxuan Liu, Yuhan Hu, Dongxi Guo, Yifan Zheng, Weihao Ruan, Wenhuan Wang, Tiantian Wang, Shibo Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aerobic exercise (AE) has been shown to improve subthreshold depression (SD). Current interventions typically involve longer durations and a moderate frequency. There is potential to further optimize this approach. This study designed a two-week high-frequency AE program and evaluated its efficacy and neural mechanisms in SD. This single-blind, randomized controlled study randomly assigned 57 female SD participants to AE (n = 29; 6–7 sessions/week) or a sitting control (n = 28). Depression severity and symptoms were assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) at baseline, post-intervention, and two-week follow-up. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy combined with the Verbal Fluency Task was used to assess multiple neural activity metrics in the prefrontal cortex and motor cortex. The AE intervention significantly decreased in BDI-II and PHQ-9 scores, while increasing response and remission rates. Simultaneously, AE significantly reduced activation in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), frontal eye field (FEF), and premotor and supplementary motor areas (PMC-and-SMA). Functional connectivity strength decreased between the DLPFC and the FEF, PMC-and-SMA, and the primary somatosensory cortex (S1), and the PMC-and-SMA and S1. Network efficiency improved within the S1 and FEF, including enhanced node efficiency, increased degree centrality, and reduced shortest path. Moreover, the functional enhancement of PMC-and-SMA and FEF was significantly associated with alleviations in somatic symptoms (sleep disturbances and fatigue). The study demonstrates the feasibility and efficacy of the two-week high-frequency AE protocol in improving SD, with benefits comparable to those of previous AE protocols.

Introduction

The global prevalence of major depressive disorder (MDD) is increasing, and its associated disability-adjusted life years now constitute the predominant burden of mental illness [1]. Reflecting this trend, clinical prevention and control strategies increasingly focus on risk prevention [2]. Subthreshold depression (SD) is defined as a precursor phase to MDD and is characterized by the presence of depressive symptoms that do not meet the clinical diagnostic criteria for MDD [3]. The risk of progression to MDD is 2 to 5 times higher in individuals with SD compared to those who are healthy [4]. The prevalence of SD in the general population is approximately 11.02% [5], while it reaches as high as 40% among Chinese college students [6]. Given this considerable prevalence and elevated progression risk in SD, implementing timely interventions is crucial for preventing the onset of MDD.

The most common treatment for MDD is medication; however, due to the significant adverse effects of the latter, it is not suitable for the SD population [7, 8]. Consequently, non-pharmacological interventions have garnered significant attention [9–12]. Nevertheless, among non-pharmacological interventions, psychotherapy is slow to take effect and relatively costly [13–15], while physical therapy requires specialized equipment and involves complex procedures. In recent years, aerobic exercise (AE) has emerged as a promising intervention due to its ease of implementation, cost-effectiveness, and systemic health benefits. Research indicates that AE’s antidepressant effects are comparable to those of pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy [16], supporting its recommendation as a first-line treatment for mild to moderate depression [15].

At present, AE interventions for individuals with SD generally comprise moderate-intensity exercise sessions with a duration of between 30 and 45 min, conducted 3 to 4 times per week over 8 weeks. As demonstrated by extant research, this protocol has been shown to effectively alleviate depressive symptoms in individuals with SD [17, 18]. Research conducted on the subject of brain mechanisms has indicated that AE has the capacity to induce neuroplasticity changes in subjects diagnosed with SD. These changes have been observed to include spontaneous neural activity (regional homogeneity, low-frequency amplitude) [19, 20], cross-regional functional connectivity [21], and network topological properties (node centrality) [10] in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, middle frontal gyrus, cingulate gyrus, and certain occipito-parietal regions. As indicated by the extant literature, these brain regions are precisely the key areas in which individuals with SD exhibit functional abnormalities in cognitive control and emotional regulation [22]. This suggests that AE may reshape the function of these impaired brain regions, thereby alleviating depressive symptoms [23].

Despite the evident potency of the existing studies in demonstrating the efficacy of AE intervention for SD, in terms of both clinical symptom improvement and underlying brain mechanisms, the currently adopted AE intervention protocols are associated with relatively protracted treatment durations. This may pose certain challenges to their clinical implementation to a certain extent. Heisse et al. [16] also noted that excessively protracted intervention periods result in elevated rates of participant attrition, whilst intervention effects decline over time. This observation is derived from a systematic review of 41 randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Consequently, researchers have attempted to reduce the duration of the intervention and increase the frequency of sessions. A study employing a two-week AE intervention consisting of seven sessions found a significant reduction in depression levels among college students [24]. A further meta-analysis of RCTs has indicated that exercise interventions conducted five times per week (compared to two to four times per week) and lasting 20–29 min per session (compared to 45–49 min) yield more significant antidepressant effects [15]. The aforementioned evidence suggests that short-term, high-frequency AE protocols hold promising prospects for antidepressant treatment. Moreover, evidence from the domain of exercise biochemistry elucidates the biological rationale for this approach. Research has demonstrated that daily running wheel exercise for two consecutive weeks can activate key signaling pathways associated with neuroplasticity and neuronal survival at the molecular level [25]. This finding indicates that short-term, high-frequency exercise can induce substantial changes in brain function, thereby providing experimental evidence to support the investigation into the neural mechanisms by which short-term, high-frequency AE improves SD.

In recent years, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) has become a prevalent brain imaging technique in research due to its non-invasive nature, portability, resilience to motion artefacts, and ecological validity [26]. The team’s preceding fNIRS investigations have demonstrated that individuals with SD manifest substantial impairments in cognitive control. These impairments are characterized by multidimensional functional abnormalities (i.e., local activation, cross-regional functional connectivity, and network topological properties) in critical brain regions, namely the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and the medial cingulate cortex (MC), during the execution of the Verbal Fluency Task (VFT). These abnormalities exhibit a significant correlation with the severity of symptoms [27].

The present study employs an RCT design to investigate the impact of a two-week high-frequency AE regimen (6–7 sessions per week, 30 min per session) on depressive symptoms (immediate and sustained effects) in individuals with SD. Then, utilizing the fNIRS-VFT paradigm, the present study seeks to elucidate the brain mechanisms underlying the short-term, high-frequency AE’s improvement of SD. The present study hypothesizes that the two-week high-frequency AE intervention will significantly improve depressive symptoms in individuals with SD and induce neuroplastic changes in the function of key brain regions in the PFC and MC. Furthermore, correlations between the alleviation of depressive symptoms (both in severity and specific symptoms) and changes in brain function before and after intervention.

Materials and methods

The study employed a single-blind RCT design. Participants could not be blinded due to the nature of the interventions. However, outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation by using coded participant identifiers and removing all group information from the dataset. Participants and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this trial. No important changes were made to the trial protocol after commencement.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1) [28]. Based on recent meta-analyses examining the effects of supervised aerobic exercise on depressive symptoms (e.g., Heissel et al. [16], reporting a large effect size of SMD = −1.03 for supervised exercise interventions; Noetel et al., [29] reporting Hedges’ g = −0.62 for walking/jogging compared with active controls), we adopted a conservative estimate of Cohen’s d = 0.50 (Cohen’s f = 0.25) to ensure adequate power despite potential heterogeneity and the non-clinical nature of our sample. With a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power of 95%, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 54 participants.

Participants

Participants were recruited from the student population at East China Normal University, Shanghai, China, between September 12, 2022, and August 19, 2023. Demographic information, including age and years of education (the total number of years of formal schooling completed, from primary school onward), was collected via a self-reported questionnaire at baseline. SD was determined using a two-stage assessment of depression severity with the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), with a minimum interval of 14 days between assessments.

SD was determined using a two-stage assessment [30, 31]. First, depression severity was assessed with the BDI-II on two occasions separated by at least 14 days. Individuals scoring between 14 and 28 on both assessments—indicating mild to moderate depressive symptoms—were considered as potential SD candidates. Second, to rule out MDD and other severe mental illnesses, trained study staff conducted a structured clinical interview; individuals with a current or past diagnosis of MDD or other severe mental illnesses were excluded. This combined approach, which uses BDI-II cutoff scores together with diagnostic exclusion, has been commonly employed in previous SD research to identify individuals with clinically significant depressive symptoms that do not meet the full criteria for MDD. Additional inclusion criteria were: (a) Chinese Han ethnicity, aged 18–28 years; (b) normal color vision; (c) no history of alcohol or substance abuse; (d) no lower limb injuries (e.g., fractures, sprains) impeding mobility within the past three months; (e) no contraindications to exercise identified by the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q); and (f) not currently taking any medications, including common medications (e.g., cold medicines) as well as antidepressant or psychotropic medications.

Following screening, 62 eligible participants (5 males, 57 females) were identified. Given the very small number of male participants, only the 57 female participants were enrolled in the RCT. The study protocol was approved by the Human Subjects Protection Committee of East China Normal University (Approval No HR-427-2022) and was conducted by the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving detailed information about the study procedures. The study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500110789; http://www.chictr.org.cn/) on October 21, 2025.

The 57 female participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to either the AE group (n = 29) or the sitting control (SC) group (n = 28) using a computer-generated random sequence by an independent researcher. Allocation concealment was implemented using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened only after participants had completed all baseline assessments and provided written informed consent. The personnel who enrolled participants and those who assigned interventions did not have access to the random allocation sequence.

During fNIRS data acquisition, two participants (AE group: n = 1; SC group: n = 1) were excluded from fNIRS analysis due to excessive head motion. During the intervention period, five participants discontinued the study due to influenza (AE group: n = 2; SC group: n = 3). Consequently, 50 participants (AE group: n = 26; SC group: n = 24) completed the intervention and all assessments. Interventions

The AE group participated in a two-week, laboratory-based stationary bicycle exercise program using Monark Z1 ergometers (Sweden), with an intervention frequency of six to seven sessions per week. Each 30-minute session comprised a 5-minute warm-up followed by 25 min of moderate-intensity exercise. In order to control for potential confounding effects of social interaction, the participants engaged in exercising solo in a dedicated laboratory setting. They were under the supervision of an independent researcher, a doctoral student in the field of exercise science. This researcher had a background in exercise physiology and expertise in exercise risk assessment and prescription. Following the American College of Sports Medicine guidelines [32], exercise intensity was assessed based on the percentage of maximum heart rate (% HRmax). Maximum heart rate was calculated using the formula: 220 - age (years). Moderate intensity was defined as 60%–69% of HRmax. Continuous heart rate monitoring was performed throughout the intervention using Polar heart rate monitors (model H7, attached to the inner side of the left upper arm). The average heart rate for the AE group during the intervention period was 128.31 ± 3.20 beats per minute; detailed heart rate changes within a single exercise session are presented in Figure S1. Additionally, immediately following each exercise session, participants completed the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale [33] to provide a subjective assessment of intensity. Both objective heart rate data and subjective RPE scores were utilized to ensure that the exercise intensity met the predefined criteria. Participants were instructed to refrain from engaging in any structured physical activities outside of the prescribed laboratory sessions.

Participants in the SC group were asked to remain sedentary in a separate, quiet laboratory room for the same duration as the AE group’s exercise sessions (30 min per session), with the same frequency of 6–7 sessions per week over the two-week intervention period. During this time, they were allowed to read or use their phones but were instructed to avoid physical activity and maintain a seated posture. During this time, they were instructed to maintain a seated posture without engaging in any physical activity. Throughout the two-week experimental period, the SC group was also instructed to abstain from regular exercise (defined by the research team as structured activity exceeding twice per week, with each session lasting longer than 20 min). To objectively monitor physical activity levels, the SC group wore an accelerometer (ActiGraph wGT3X-BT). Upon completion of the experiment, participants in the SC group were offered the opportunity to participate in the same exercise intervention program as the AE group. The number of sedentary sessions attended by each participant in the SC group was also recorded to assess adherence.

Participants in both groups were asked not to start any new form of exercise, psychotherapy, or medication for depression during the trial. No concomitant care was reported. To assess intervention adherence, the number of sessions completed by each participant in both groups was recorded. The total number of completed interventions was defined as the sum of sessions attended over the two weeks. For the subjects in the AE group, the Borg RPE scale was administered immediately following each exercise session. The mean RPE for each participant was determined by calculating the mean of the RPE scores across all completed sessions.

Harms were defined as any adverse physical or psychological event occurring during the intervention period. Participants were asked to report any discomfort after each session, and all reports were recorded. The trial had no predefined stopping guidelines, as the two-week AE posed minimal risk of serious adverse events.

Questionnaires

The BDI-II [34] was administered to screen participants and assess the severity of their depression; the Chinese version has shown good psychometric properties in college students (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) [35], and in our study, its α was 0.93. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [36] was administered to screen for and assess the depressive symptoms corresponding to the nine diagnostic criteria for MDD in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders; the Chinese version has shown good psychometric properties in college students (Cronbach’s α = 0.85) [37], and in our study, its α was 0.77. The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) [38] was administered to assess anxiety levels for the purpose of checking baseline equivalence across randomized groups. Previous studies in Chinese college students reported good internal consistency (state subscale Cronbach’s α = 0.91, trait subscale Cronbach’s α = 0.88) [39]; in our sample, α was 0.74 and 0.63, respectively, which we consider acceptable for this baseline comparison purpose. The short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF) [40] was administered to assess participants’ anxiety levels and habitual physical activity levels to control for potential interference in the analysis; the Chinese version has acceptable reliability and validity in college students [41]. The PAR-Q [42] was utilized to screen for contraindications to exercise; the Chinese version has demonstrated good validity and reliability, making it an effective tool for pre-exercise safety assessments and health risk screenings among the Chinese population [43]. The RPE scale [44] was utilized to monitor exercise intensity in the AE group participants; it has been recognized as an effective psychophysiological tool for measuring the perception of exercise in healthy, young Chinese young adults during AE [45].

VFT paradigm

The VFT was designed based on previous studies [46, 47] and consisted of a pre-task baseline period (30 s), task period (60 s), and post-task baseline period (60 s) (Fig. 3A). During both the 30-second pre-task and 60-second post-task baseline periods, participants were instructed to repeat the sequence “1, 2, 3, 4, 5” aloud. During the 60-second task period, participants were instructed to generate as many words as possible, each beginning with one of four specific Chinese characters (“上”, “大”, “天”, and “家”). The presentation order of these cue characters was fixed and identical for all participants. The total number of correct words generated during the task period served as the measure of task performance.Fig. 3Schematic illustration of the experimental paradigm and fNIRS channel placement.A The VFT flow chart. fNIRS data acquisition and preprocessing

Cerebral hemodynamic signals during the VFT were acquired using an ETG-7100 near-infrared brain imaging system (Hitachi Medical Corporation, Japan). Two 3×5 probe holders, primarily covering the frontal cortex, were used; each holder contained eight emitters and seven detectors, yielding 22 channels per holder (Fig. 3B). Preprocessing of the fNIRS data was performed using HOMER2 software [48]. A comprehensive description of the fNIRS data acquisition and preprocessing protocol is provided in Table S1. Given the established characteristics of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) signals, including their higher signal-to-noise ratio and more direct association with task-evoked neural activity compared to deoxygenated hemoglobin signals and more direct neurovascular coupling with cognitive tasks [49, 50], subsequent analyses focused exclusively on HbO concentration changes.

fNIRS data analysis

The study utilized the final 10 s of the pre-task resting period as a baseline [51, 52] to minimize potential random errors arising from high-dimensional data computation. The time-series data of HbO concentration changes during the 60-second task period were compared against the baseline. Only channels exhibiting substantial inter-channel differences were selected for subsequent statistical analysis; these channels are listed in Table S1.

During data processing, baseline correction was first performed on the selected channels by subtracting the baseline value from the 60-second task-related time series. Subsequently, the mean value was derived from the corrected time series to quantify local brain region activation levels. Pearson correlation analysis was then employed to calculate correlation coefficients between significantly activated channels and all other channels. These coefficients were standardized using Fisher’s z-transformation. Finally, brain network graph-theoretical parameters were computed using GRETNA software [53]. For graph construction, only positive connections between channel pairs were retained; negative connections were excluded (set to zero). The network was binarized across a sparsity threshold range of 0.10 to 0.40, incremented by 0.01. The sparsity threshold represents the ratio of retained edges to the maximum possible number of edges within the network. The topological parameters examined comprised: global parameters (global efficiency, local efficiency, and clustering coefficient) and nodal parameters (shortest path, nodal efficiency, and degree centrality).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0 (Armonk, NY, United States), with the statistical significance level set at p < 0.050 (two-tailed). All analyses were prespecified in the statistical analysis plan.

For the primary outcome (BDI-II scores, response rate [≥ 30% reduction in BDI-II score from T1], and remission rate [BDI-II score < 13]), both per-protocol (PP) and intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses were conducted. The PP analysis included participants who completed all assessments with valid data (n = 50). For the continuous primary outcome (BDI‑II scores), repeated‑measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was used. For the binary primary outcomes (response and remission rates), the proportions were calculated directly from the observed data in the PP set, and group differences were tested using chi‑square tests. As a sensitivity analysis, the ITT analysis included all 57 randomized participants. For the continuous primary outcome (BDI‑II scores), a linear mixed‑effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) was applied, with group (AE vs. SC), time (T1, T2, T3), and the group × time interaction as fixed effects and participants as random intercepts. For the binary primary outcomes (response and remission rates), non‑responder imputation was used (participants with missing data were classified as non‑responders/non‑remitters), and group differences were then tested using chi‑square tests.

For the secondary outcomes (PHQ-9 item scores and brain function), normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances by Levene’s test. Analyses were performed only in the PP set (n = 50) using repeated-measures ANOVA. A Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons in the post hoc tests or simple effects analyses. Finally, Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine covariation between brain function and clinical symptoms (i.e., the correlation between baseline-to-post-intervention score differences) for the measures that showed significant effects.

Improvement effects of the two-week high-frequency AE in SD

For BDI-II scores (Fig. 4A, B), a 3 × 2 (time × group) repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that a significant time main effect (F = 9.963, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.298), a significant group main effect (F = 12.858, p = 0.001, partial η2 = 0.211) and a significant the time × group interaction effect (F = 4.249, p = 0.020, partial η2 = 0.153). Bonferroni’s simple effects analysis to analyze the significant interaction showed that compared to T1, the AE group exhibited significantly lower scores at T2 and T3 (T2: p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −1.146, 95% CI [4.110, 11.582]; T3: p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −1.091, 95% CI [3.498, 12.194]); compared with the SC group, the AE group exhibited significantly reduced scores at T2 and T3 (T2: p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −1.102, 95% CI [3.937, 12.358]; T3: p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.964, 95% CI [3.659, 14.219]). The SC group did not demonstrate statistically significant within-group differences at any time point. Furthermore, the AE group exhibited a significantly higher remission rate (61.50% vs. 25.00%; χ² = 6.762, p = 0.009) and response rate (53.80% vs. 0.00%; χ² = 17.949, p < 0.001) compared to the SC group at T2 (Table S2). All main effects and time × group interaction effects were non-significant for global parameters (p > 0.050). But there was a time × group significant effect for nodal parameters in Ch32 and Ch35 (Table S10). Bonferroni’s simple effects analysis to analyze the significant interaction showed that compared to T1, the AE group exhibited significantly enhanced node efficiency (Ch32 and Ch35), and degree centrality (Ch32); the shortest path (Ch32) was found to be significantly lower; compared with the SC group, the AE group showed significantly enhanced node efficiency (Ch32 and Ch35) and degree centrality (Ch32) at T2; the shortest path (Ch32) was found to be significantly lower at T2 (Table 2 and Fig. 5E).

The relationship between individual SD brain functional changes and depression severity and symptom improvement

To examine the relationship between changes in the brain’s functionality induced by a two-week AE intervention, and the levels of depression and symptoms exhibited by individuals with SD. To this end, changes in regional activation, inter-regional connectivity, and network topological properties were calculated before and after the intervention, and Pearson correlation analyses were performed between these changes and changes in the BDI-II total score, and on each dimension of the PHQ-9.

The findings indicated that alterations in the BDI-II total score exhibited no substantial correlation with changes in the aforementioned brain functional indicators (p > 0.050). The PHQ-9 sleep symptom score demonstrated a moderate negative correlation with the activation level of Ch37 (r = −0.450, p = 0.021). Similarly, the PHQ-9 fatigue symptom score exhibited a moderate negative correlation with activation levels in Ch37 (r = −0.402, p = 0.042) and Ch8 (r = −0.476, p = 0.014). Additionally, a moderate positive correlation was observed between the PHQ-9 fatigue symptom score and efficiency in Ch35 (r = 0.423, p = 0.034).

Discussion

This study is the first to utilize fNIRS-VFT to evaluate the intervention effects of a two-week high-frequency AE program on individuals with SD, as well as its underlying neural mechanisms. Following the intervention, the AE group demonstrated a substantial decrease in BDI-II scores and a notable improvement in depression severity. Both core and somatic symptoms were significantly alleviated, and these benefits persisted two weeks after the intervention concluded. fNIRS analysis indicated that the two-week high-frequency AE significantly modulated functional activity in multiple brain regions of individuals with SD, involving the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC; Ch9 and Ch40), primary somatosensory cortex (S1; Ch32), premotor and supplementary motor areas (PMC-and-SMA; Ch20, Ch22, and Ch37), and the frontal eye field (FEF; Ch8, Ch18, and Ch35).

The two-week high-frequency AE regimen designed in this study resulted in a significant reduction in intervention duration (75%–83%) and a corresponding decrease in the number of intervention sessions (42%–74%) when compared with previous regimens. The clinical efficacy data demonstrate that following AE intervention, the response rate (61.50% vs. 25.00%) and remission rate (53.80% vs. 0.00%) were both found to be significantly higher than those observed in the SC group. Subsequent results indicate that two weeks after the conclusion of the intervention, the AE group exhibited a 50% remission rate for depression severity. This finding suggests that the protocol has a robust, sustained effect. Furthermore, addressing the limitation of previous studies that often relied on total scores of depression scales while overlooking symptom heterogeneity [54], this study analyzed data using the PHQ-9 scale and found that the improvement benefits of the two-week high-frequency AE program exhibited significant heterogeneity. The benefits were primarily concentrated in the core symptom cluster (depressed mood, loss of interest) and the somatic symptom cluster (fatigue, changes in appetite, sleep disturbances), rather than being evenly distributed across all dimensions. In summary, the two-week high-frequency AE program has been demonstrated to enhance intervention efficiency significantly, whilst also demonstrating clear and lasting clinical benefits. This offers individuals with SD a new exercise intervention strategy that is highly effective and holds potential for clinical application.

The study under consideration focused on the PFC and MC, exploring three aspects of brain function: local activation, functional connectivity, and network topological properties. The study found that two-week high-frequency AE significantly reduced the activation levels of the DLPFC, FEF, and PMC-and-SMA; simultaneously weakening the functional connectivity strength between the DLPFC and the FEF, PMC-and-SMA, and S1, as well as between the PMC-and-SMA and S1; and increasing the node efficiency and degree centrality of the FEF and S1, while shortening their shortest path lengths. Collectively, the observed changes across these three levels point to a single core mechanism—enhanced neural efficiency [55]. This is manifested as follows: during the semantic retrieval and cognitive control required for VFT, the aforementioned brain regions process information with lower neural metabolic consumption, and redundant or interfering connections between brain regions are reduced. Simultaneously, the information integration and transmission capabilities of key nodes (such as the FEF and S1) are enhanced. This pattern is indicative of the brain’s adaptive functional disengagement and reintegration in response to task demands [56], whereby cognitive resources are reallocated by reducing the involvement of non-essential regions and optimizing the efficiency of key nodes. This finding is consistent with previous research: long-term (8–24 weeks), moderate-frequency (3 times per week) AE interventions significantly reduce brain activation levels during cognitive processing in the general population [57]. Furthermore, they improve neural efficiency [58]. Consequently, it can be deduced that the brain functional reorganization induced by two weeks of high-frequency AE intervention exhibits biological consistency with long-term intervention in terms of the core mechanism of enhancing neural efficiency. This suggests that short-term, high-frequency intervention is effective in inducing rapid brain functional adaptation.

The present study examined the brain functional optimization induced by two-week high-frequency AE, primarily reflected in the DLPFC, FEF, PMC-and-SMA, and S1 regions. The DLPFC is primarily responsible for emotional regulation and cognitive reappraisal [59]. Abnormalities in the function of this region have been demonstrated to underlie deficits in negative emotion processing in individuals with SD [60]. Improvements in the function of this brain region may assist individuals in the suppression of negative rumination [61], and the enhancement of reward signal processing [62], thereby contributing to the alleviation of depression. The FEF is principally responsible for the selective acquisition of visual information and is profoundly involved in top-down attentional control and spatial information processing [63]. As a pivotal node in the frontal cortical network, impaired function of the FEF makes it challenging for individuals with depression to actively disengage from negative stimuli [64, 65]. The adaptive changes in FEF function observed in this study (reduced activation, increased node efficiency, and enhanced degree centrality) may enable individuals with SD to shift their attention more flexibly from negative stimuli to task-relevant targets. This may reduce the cognitive resource demands associated with attentional bias and promote improvements in depression. Furthermore, the present study found that AE-induced optimization of FEF function was significantly associated with the alleviation of fatigue symptoms in individuals with SD, suggesting that the reallocation of attentional resources may contribute to the restoration of energy levels in these individuals.

It is evident that both the PMC-and-SMA and S1 constitute fundamental components of the sensorimotor network. The former is responsible for motor control and the execution of internally driven behaviours [66, 67]; reduced excitability in this region may hinder the translation of motor intent into actual action. The latter processes somatosensory input, maintains proprioceptive awareness [68], and is heavily involved in language processing [69]. It has been demonstrated that deregulation within the sensorimotor network and improved node efficiency hold significant clinical implications for alleviating depressive symptoms, particularly psychomotor retardation or agitation [70, 71]. The present study posits that the functional optimization of the PMC-and-SMA and S1 may suggest that the brain can achieve the coupling of motor preparation and sensory feedback with a lower metabolic load. This may help alleviate the motor initiation delay or impulsive motor control impairments commonly observed in individuals with depression [72, 73]. It is also worthy of note that the present study observed that changes in PMC-and-SMA function were associated with improvements in sleep and fatigue symptoms. This may be indicative of a correlation between enhanced neural efficiency and reduced energy depletion, as well as improved sleep regulation, in individuals with SD.

Unfortunately, this study only included female college students. Future research should include individuals with SD from different genders and age groups (such as adolescents and the elderly) to make the findings more generalizable. Furthermore, fNIRS measurements are limited to cortical regions and cannot detect functional activity in subcortical and deep brain structures directly (e.g., the amygdala, hippocampus, and striatum). Changes in the function of these deep structures also play a significant role in the onset and progression of depression. Combining techniques such as fMRI with fNIRS in future studies could further elucidate the neural mechanisms underlying the improvement of SD in two-week, high-frequency AE at the whole-brain level.

Conclusion

In summary, this study suggests that the two-week high-frequency AE significantly reduces the severity of depression in individuals with SD, while concomitantly improving their core and vegetative symptoms. The positive effect persists for at least two weeks after the intervention. Furthermore, the AE intervention effectively alters frontal lobe function in SD, as evidenced by reduced activation, decreased functional connectivity strength, and enhanced network information transmission efficiency. Key brain regions involved include the DLPFC, the FEF, the PMC-and-SMA, and the S1. Furthermore, the functional optimization of two-week high-frequency AE induced EFE and PMC-and-SMA was closely associated with the alleviation of somatic symptoms (sleep disturbances and fatigue) in SD individuals.
