Age estimation using pulp-to-tooth area ratio in maxillary anterior teeth: A study on a Kerala population using Cameriere’s method
Authors: Manickam Selvamani, Sudeendra Prabhu, Roshin Chandri Narayanan, Bastian Thattil Sebastian, Suhana Hameed Sulaikha, Nikhil Raj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Context:Precise age assessment is essential in forensic identification. This study assessed the relationship between pulp-to-tooth area ratio (PTAR) and chronological age and developed population-specific regression models for individuals from Kerala, India.Aim:To estimate the age by pulp-to-tooth area ratio in maxillary anterior teeth using Cameriere’s method in the Kerala sample population.Settings and Design:A retrospective, cross-sectional study utilised digital intra-oral periapical radiographs to estimate age by Cameriere’s approach in persons aged 21 to 70 years, focussing on maxillary anterior teeth.Methods and Material:A total of 561 digital radiographs from persons aged 21 to 70 years were analysed with GIMP software. Regression models were developed and tested using a control group (n = 170 per tooth). The accuracy was evaluated by mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) and compared with existing models.Statistical Analysis Used:The Student t-test, Pearson’s correlation, linear regression, MAE, and RMSE were used to evaluate model performance.Results:The newly derived Indian regression equations demonstrated MAEs of 5.627 years for central incisors, 7.608 years for lateral incisors, and 5.890 years for canines. The canine showed the best accuracy, outperforming Cameriere’s (MAE = 7.223, 6.699) and Jeevan’s (MAE = 6.549) equations. All new models produced acceptable age estimates (MAE <10 years) across age groups.Conclusions:PTAR-based regression models are effective for age estimation in the Kerala population, with the canine providing the most reliable estimates.

INTRODUCTION

The estimation of age continues to pose a considerable challenge for anthropologists and dentists as identifying the age of an individual, whether living or deceased, is a critical issue in forensic investigations.[12] Age estimation in children and adolescents is essential for a wide range of legal purposes and plays a vital role in both civil and criminal cases. It aids in assessing consent, identifying juvenile offenders, investigating abduction and sexual offences, and verifying age for legal adulthood, employment, adoption, and eligibility for marriage, as well as for determining eligibility for senior citizen benefits and retirement entitlements.[34]

Age assessment typically depends on factors such as skeletal maturation, secondary sexual traits, height, and body mass. Dental maturation has proven to be a dependable and constant indicator for this objective.[56] The bulk of current approaches focus on macroscopic examination, with a few especially addressing dental analysis, and an even smaller number utilising radiographic techniques to thoroughly evaluate both skeletal and dental components.[789]

The human body’s most resilient organs are the teeth, which show excellent resistance to a range of environmental stresses such as heat, chemical, and mechanical harm. They are useful markers of biological age because of their unique form and composition, which combine acquired and inherited characteristics. Teeth are a chosen resource for forensic and archaeological research because, unlike bones, their development is mostly unaffected by systemic factors like illnesses, drugs, or hormone abnormalities.[101112]

Dental age estimation is fundamentally based on the morphological, histological, biochemical, and radiological analysis of teeth. In 1950, Gustafson pioneered the first scientific method for age determination through dental examination, evaluating six key parameters: secondary dentin deposition, attrition, periodontosis, cementum apposition, root resorption, and root transparency.[13] However, these techniques are inherently destructive as they require tooth extraction, rendering them unsuitable and ethically inappropriate for use in living individuals. This limitation highlights the non-invasive, simple, and dependable character of radiographic age assessment in living and deceased humans, including skeletal remains. These approaches use child tooth growth and adult secondary dentin deposition to determine age.[141516]

Kvaal et al.[17] devised a non-invasive method for age assessment that obviated tooth extraction by indirectly evaluating secondary dentin deposition using radiographic analysis of pulp radiolucency. They suggested linking diverse length and breadth parameters of the tooth and pulp with chronological age.

Cameriere et al.[18] developed a two-dimensional method for dental age estimation based on measurements of pulp and tooth area. While the maxillary canine was the initial focus, the method was later expanded to include the second molar,[19] mandibular canines,[20] and central and lateral incisors.[9] This approach demonstrated reasonable accuracy, with a mean error ranging from 3 to 4.5 years. However, the authors emphasised the importance of further research to explore the influence of ethnic and cultural factors on the model’s parameters.[20] Supporting this view, subsequent studies have advocated for the validation of age estimation methods on independent populations, with several reporting greater accuracy when population-specific formulas are applied.[182122] The present study aims to assess the applicability of one of Cameriere’s formulas to an Indian population, specifically using maxillary anterior teeth, to determine whether the original model yields accurate results or if a population-specific equation enhances age estimation precision.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A total of 561 radiographic images of tooth from 202 individuals of Indian origin, specifically from Kerala, were retrieved from archives for analysis. Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Mahe Institute of Dental Sciences and Hospital (Reference number: MINDS/EC/018/2020-21; dated 06.05.2021). The radiographs represented individuals aged between 21 and 70 years, stratified into five groups with 10-year age intervals. To ensure regional representativeness, samples were proportionally selected from Kerala’s North, Central, and South administrative zones. Three maxillary anterior teeth—the central incisor, lateral incisor, and canine—were assessed, excluding teeth affected by caries, pathological conditions (attrition, abrasion, fracture, or impaction), or prior endodontic therapy.

Age estimation was carried out using regression models developed by Cameriere et al.[92123] and Jeevan et al.[4] Radiovisiographic images were obtained from the archives of Department of Oral Medicine and Radiology. All radiographs were taken using a KODAK 5000 radiovisiography (RVG) system, employing standardised exposure settings of 7 mA and 60–70 kVp.

The pulp-to-tooth area ratio (PTAR) was determined using the methodology outlined by Acharya AB in the article “Steps in Measuring Tooth Area and Pulp Area Using Tools of GIMP 2.10”.[24] In the present study, image analysis was performed using GIMP software version 2.10.38.

For each participant, the PTAR was measured separately on the maxillary central incisor, lateral incisor, and canine using GIMP software. Chronological (actual) age was obtained from departmental records. All measurements were performed by a single observer to ensure consistency, with repeat assessments conducted to evaluate reliability. All assessments were carried out without prior knowledge of the subjects’ personal data to maintain objectivity. To further assess inter-observer agreement, paired t-tests, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for mean differences, and Pearson correlation coefficients were computed for PTAR values across observers for each tooth region.

The morphological variables (PTAR), age, and gender were recorded in a Microsoft Excel 2021 spreadsheet and analysed as potential predictors for age estimation. Correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate relationships between age and predictive variables. Age estimation was performed using linear regression equations established by Cameriere et al.[92123] and Jeevan et al.[4] for maxillary central incisors, lateral incisors, and canines, respectively. Following radiograph decoding, actual chronological ages were confirmed, and the discrepancy between predicted and actual ages (“error”) was documented. Absolute errors were aggregated to compute the mean absolute error (MAE), while root mean square error (RMSE) was also calculated to validate the accuracy of prior age prediction models.[492123]

Simple linear regression models were constructed for each tooth region to assess PTAR’s predictive value for chronological age, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to compare predicted and actual ages. Finally, population-specific regression models for Indians were developed and applied to a control sample, with results compared against Cameriere’s and Jeevan’s formulas to evaluate accuracy improvements.

The sample size determination was based on the findings of Babshet et al.,[25] who reported a significant correlation of −0.34 between the mandibular incisor, canine, and age, resulting in an effect size of 0.1307.

DISCUSSION

Age estimation is vital in forensic identification, but the usefulness of many anatomical structures is often limited by postmortem degradation. In cases of trauma, fire, or decomposition, environmental factors may render most body parts unsuitable.[20] However, teeth remain highly durable and resistant to such changes, making them reliable indicators for age estimation when other remains are compromised.

Clinical and radiographic examinations of teeth in living individuals are minimally invasive and involve low radiation exposure unlike traditional extraction-based age estimation methods which are invasive and ethically debated. Secondary dentin apposition, an age-related process that reduces pulp cavity size, offers a reliable non-invasive marker.[141516] Cameriere et al.[1820] demonstrated a linear relationship between age and the PTAR in canines. Validation studies on Italian populations showed this method estimates age with a median error of less than 4 years.

The technique has been adapted to digital radiography, including periapical and panoramic images. In Indian populations, RVG has been used to evaluate PTAR in canines and develop population-specific regression equations for age estimation. RVG offers advantages such as lower radiation, better image quality, and greater efficiency, making it a valuable tool in forensic age assessment.[4]

Subsequent studies recommend validating age estimation methods on independent populations, often finding improved accuracy with population-specific formulas.[182122] Thus, this study evaluates the applicability of Cameriere’s formula to an Indian population.

In the current study, we included the age group from 21 years to 70 years as secondary dentin deposition, which reduces pulp size and serves as the primary marker for age, shows significant and linear correlation with chronological age during this adult period. Studies have demonstrated suboptimal accuracy in younger (<21) and older (>70) individuals due to ongoing dental development or increased biological variability and pathologies, making age estimation less reliable at the extremes.[2126]

The current study exclusively involved male participants as other investigations have identified no significant gender disparities in PTAR ratios.[202123] This can be ascribed to the protective dentin encasing the pulp, which mitigates the effects of external influences like as masticatory stress and attrition, irrespective of dietary differences within the varied Indian cohort.[4]

A total of 561 RVG images of maxillary central and lateral incisor and canines were analysed, showing good intra-observer agreement (P < 0.0001), which indicates high measurement reproducibility. These findings are consistent with those of Cameriere et al.,[202123] who reported similar results using digital periapical X-rays in Italian Caucasian and Portuguese populations and Jeevan et al.[4] on the Indian population.

The ageing process results in the constriction of the pulp cavity due to the accumulation of secondary dentin, which is associated with chronological age and can be evaluated using radiographic imaging.[17] This study identified a notable inverse association between age and PTAR in all examined tooth, hence endorsing its application in age estimation.

This study assessed the precision of Cameriere-based pulp/tooth ratio (PTR) methodologies for age estimation in maxillary anterior teeth—central incisor, lateral incisor, and canine—across several adult age cohorts. Our results indicated a moderate overall accuracy for all three teeth, with negligible bias in the estimated mean age at the population level. Significant age-related discrepancies were noted, including an overestimation in younger adults and an underestimation in older persons. The Cameriere et al.[9] technique demonstrated superior prediction performance for central and lateral incisors in the aged group (51–60 years), exhibiting the lowest RMSE and MAE values in this cohort [Tables 2 and 3]. In the present study, we noted overall MAE 9.36 in central incisor and 10.04 in lateral incisor, which is higher than findings of Cameriere et al.,[9] finding 7.03 and 6.64, respectively, who carried out studies on skull collected from the Portugal population. Similarly, Alqarni et al.[27] performed a study in the Saudi Arabian population and concluded SEE of 9.8 for central incisor. These findings challenge the notion that dentin deposition is predominantly governed by environmental and genetic factors and instead highlight the potential influence of racial and cultural determinants in the estimation of chronological age.[18202122] Therefore, a customised regression method of age estimation applied to the indigenous population regionally is recommended for estimating chronological age.

Both Cameriere et al.[2123] and Jeevan et al.[4] methods for canine estimate chronological age with moderate accuracy. Their efficacy varies substantially with age. Jeevan et al.[4] surpassed Cameriere et al.[21] approaches (RMSE: 13.901; MAE: 11.244) with lower (RMSE: 13.309; MAE: 10.677).

PTR models are beneficial but have limitations, especially at extreme ages. Biological variability may explain overestimation in younger people (Jeevan et al.[4] predicted age 39.52 vs. actual age 26.42 years in the age group of 21–30) and underestimation in older adults (Cameriere et al.[24] predicted age 46.07 vs. actual age 64.19 for ages group of 61–70 years).

Middle-aged individuals’ increased model performance (e.g. Jeevan et al.[4] RMSE of 8.651 and MAE of 6.769 for the age group of 41–50) confirms PTR-based approaches’ optimal accuracy once dental maturation stabilises. The model of Jeevan et al.[4] showed marginally enhanced performance regarding total RMSE and MAE, presumably due to its development from an Indian population. This corroborates the prevailing agreement in forensic odontology that population-specific models provide enhanced accuracy.

The above-mentioned studies are conducted on demographically different cohorts, stressing the necessity for regional calibration in forensic age estimates, suggesting population-specific regression models may be useful.

Regression analysis was conducted using PTAR data to derive an Indian-specific formula tailored to a Kerala population [Table 5]. This regression equation was then applied to a control sample comprising maxillary central incisors (n = 176), lateral incisors (n = 172), and canines (n = 172) to evaluate its efficacy in improving age estimation accuracy. A separate control group was used to avoid potential bias associated with applying the equation to the original derivation sample (n = 561). For comparative analysis, Cameriere’s and Jeevan’s formulas[492124] were also applied to the control group [Tables 6 and 7].

Application of the new formula to the central incisor revealed that both the Cameriere et al. and New Formula methods yielded comparable age estimation results. The Cameriere method produced slightly lower error values (MAE = 5.356, RMSE = 6.243) compared to the New Formula (MAE = 5.627, RMSE = 6.793). Age group analysis indicated that the New Formula tended to overestimate age in younger adults (21–30 years), resulting in its highest errors (MAE = 7.201, RMSE = 7.810), while the Cameriere method also showed elevated errors in this group, albeit to a lesser extent. The highest accuracy for the New Formula was noted in the 51–60-year group (MAE = 3.253, RMSE = 3.902), whereas the Cameriere method performed best in the 31–40-year group (MAE = 3.471, RMSE = 4.250). Both methods demonstrated a marked increase in error in the oldest group (61–70 years), consistent with a known reduction in predictive accuracy for PTR methods in advanced age [Table 6].

Similarly, applying the new formula to the lateral incisor showed that, overall, the Cameriere method performed slightly better (MAE = 5.438, RMSE = 6.765) than the New Formula (MAE = 6.196, RMSE = 7.608). The highest errors for both methods occurred in the 21–30-year group, where the New Formula markedly overestimated age (MAE = 8.417, RMSE = 9.427), while the Cameriere method also exhibited elevated errors (MAE = 6.199, RMSE = 7.864), though to a lesser extent. In contrast, the best performance of the New Formula was observed in the 51–60-year group (MAE = 2.995, RMSE = 3.926), indicating improved accuracy in middle-aged individuals. The Cameriere method also performed well in this group (MAE = 3.899, RMSE = 4.563). However, both methods demonstrated a marked decline in accuracy in the oldest age group (61–70 years), particularly for the New Formula (MAE = 10.113, RMSE = 10.876) [Table 6].

For the canine, the New Formula demonstrated the highest overall accuracy, yielding the lowest mean absolute error (MAE = 5.890) and root mean square error (RMSE = 7.182), followed closely by Jeevan’s method (MAE = 6.549, RMSE = 8.009). In both the 31–40 and 41–50-year groups, the New Formula outperformed the other methods, with particularly notable accuracy in the 41–50-year group (MAE = 2.967, RMSE = 3.579), reflecting enhanced predictive performance in middle-aged individuals [Table 7].

CONCLUSION

This study shows that the population-specific regression equations created for the Kerala sample yield rather accurate age estimations based on PTARs. Of the three maxillary anterior teeth examined, the maxillary canine produced the most dependable data, exhibiting the lowest MAE of 5.890 years. In comparison to earlier models like Cameriere’s and Jeevan’s methods, the new formula demonstrated enhanced accuracy, especially for the canine. These findings underscore the necessity of creating region-specific formulas to improve the accuracy of age estimation in forensic and anthropological settings. A more reliable age estimate needs validation through bigger samples encompassing diverse age groups.
