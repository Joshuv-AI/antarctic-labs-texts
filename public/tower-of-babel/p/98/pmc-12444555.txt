Development and Application of a Laboratory Simulation Device for the Underground Compressed Air Energy Storage System
Authors: Yang Fu, Shiwan Chen, Kexun Zheng, Yunlong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The compressed air energy storage (CAES) in the underground lined rock cavern is a promising long-term energy storage technology, while the mechanical and temperature responses during the cyclic process of gas charging and discharging are highly complicated. Current research on the mechanical and thermodynamic properties of CAES caverns primarily relies on field tests and numerical simulations. However, field tests are prohibitively expensive and difficult to replicate, while the reliability of numerical simulations remains questionable. To address these limitations, this study presents a novel laboratory simulation device, which is capable of replicating the coupled thermo-mechanical (T-M) conditions of underground CAES caverns, including geostresses up to 100 MPa, temperatures up to 300 °C, and cyclic gas pressures of 0–70 MPa. The integrated system combines a geological environment simulation system, a gas pressure control system, and a measurement system. Experimental validation using a 300 mm × 300 mm × 300 mm lined rock cavern sample (butyl rubber sealing and C40 concrete lining) under 8 MPa geo-stress and cyclic pressure (1.5–7.1 MPa) revealed three key findings: (1) creep behavior and irreversible strain accumulation in the sealing layer, (2) strong relationship between internal pressure and gas leakage rates, and (3) quantified temperature gradient evolution in sealing and lining layers (R 2 = 0.997 for thermal decay profiles). Meanwhile, results showed that all three systems worked smoothly. The device provides a unique platform for investigating multifield coupling effects in CAES, offering critical insights for design optimization and reliability enhancement in renewable energy storage systems.

Introduction

The utilization of renewable energy can effectively reduce the consumption of fossil fuels to alleviate climate change. , However, the intermittency and instability of the renewable energy sources (solar, wind, tidal energy, etc.) limit their large proportion integration into the power grid systems. − To develop long-term and large-scale energy storage technology is of great importance for the renewable energy industry.

The CAES is a promising technology that is capable of large-scale and long-term electricity energy storage. , Compared to other energy storage technologies (such as pumped hydro storage, electrochemical energy storage, and flywheel energy storage), the CAES technology possesses apparent advantages such as high efficiency, long lifespan, large scale, flexible site, and relatively low cost. − As shown in Figure , the unstable renewable energy is first used to power compressors to compress air into the underground CAES-lined rock cavern (LRC), converting electrical energy into potential energy in the high-pressure air. Then, the high-pressure air is released to drive turbines and generate electricity, to provide stable and controllable electricity. − The CAES technology can not only enhance the reliability and stability of the renewable energy generation, balance peak and off-peak electricity demand, but also facilitate the large-scale application of renewable energy. − However, the cyclic pressurization or depressurization process would result in synchronous cyclic change of temperature, air pressure, and stress in the lined rock cavern, which would further influence the tightness and stability of the lined rock cavern. The response of mechanical and thermal in the lined rock cavern is very complicated. , The fundamental idea of CAES originated in the early 1940s. In 1969, Germany began to build a Huntorf CAES plant using traditional CAES technology in the salt cavern, which was completed in 1978 with a round-trip efficiency of 42%. Subsequently, the McIntosh CAES plant was built in 1997 also in a salt cavern with a round-trip efficiency of 54%. Since the mid-1980s, the LRC technology has been improved to enhance the tightness and stability in Sweden. Over the past years, the CAES technology has not been widely used for the low round-trip efficiency until the emergence of the AA-CAES technology. Recently, CAES technology especially for the lined rock cavern has become a hot research area.

Diagram of the CAES in the underground lined rock cavern.

To study the mechanical and thermal responses of lined rock caverns in repeated pressurization and depressurization cycles, many researchers have carried out in situ experiments. In the 1990s, two field experiments were carried out in Japan, to study the feasibility of CAES in crystalline rock caverns. One of them was carried out in an unlined cavern, which was to study the role of groundwater in the tightness of the unlined CAES cavern, and another field test was carried out in a lined mining cavern to study the feasibility of CAES in underground lined rock cavern. Damjanac et al. and Okuno et al. presented monitoring results from in situ tests (One field test in a shaft with 9 m high, 4.4 m in diameter, and 50 m deep in granite; the other field test in a cavern with 6 m in diameter and 400 m deep in sedimentary rocks) to investigate the mechanical response of rock masses within pressurized lined caverns. Both works showed that the cavern diameter increases with the number of loading cycles. Kim et al. ,,, carried out field tests and numerical simulations to study the deformation and gas leakage pathways of caverns, and they demonstrated that the detection and localization of air leakage through the fractures could be performed by the monitored pressure histories at the interface. Zhou et al. used a coupled thermo-mechanical-damage numerical model considering the influences of weak planes and inhomogeneity on cavern stability, and they found that the stress of the concrete lining increased and the hoop stress of the host rock decreased with the increase of inclination angle of the weak plane, and as the inhomogeneity increases, the radial stresses of the concrete and host rock decrease. A small-scale pilot cavern with concrete liner and fiber-reinforced plastic (FRP) sealing layer was constructed to better understand the thermodynamic process of the cavern, and in this field test, Jiang et al. found that the air pressure in the cavern increased/decreased approximately linearly during the charging/discharging phase, and the excellent sealing performance of the FRP was validated.

There is also a lot of laboratory research to study the mechanical and thermal responses to the cyclic thermo-mechanical loading of rock, lined structure, and sealing material. Terashita et al. implemented normal-pressure gas permeation tests on several synthetic resins and rubbers, results showed that the isobutylene isoprene rubber possessed excellent flexibility and airtightness. Wang et al. systematically analyzed the evolution law of the triaxial compressive strength under different confining pressures, temperatures, and thermal-mechanical couplings, and they concluded that the strength of granite consistently decreases as the treatment temperature increases. However, Yang et al. discovered an increase in granite strength within a certain temperature range, after the exposure of granite to different high-temperature treatments. Liu et al. carried out the porosity and permeability measurement on concrete samples after cyclic loading–unloading tests and creep tests, and they found that the porosity of concrete increased when the stress level reached 0.61, whereas the permeability increased when the stress level reached 0.73. Wan et al. studied the mechanical behavior and gas permeability of granite under temperature–pressure cyclic loading, and the response of the surrounding rock to thermo-hydro-mechanical loading, and they developed a coupled two-phase flow thermo-hydro-mechanical-damage model for CAES granite caverns. Zheng et al. carried out permeability measurement on five types of rock (Basalt, Granite, Red sandstone, C25 concrete, Green sandstone) with a sealant (nanograde organosilicon polymer) coated, and results showed that the permeability of rock coated with a 7–10 μm thick NOSP layer significantly decreased to less than 1 nD under a 0.5 MPa pulse pressure.

All in all, existing research on CAES caverns primarily employs three approaches but each with significant limitations. Field tests, while providing direct observations, are prohibitively labor-intensive, expensive, and difficult to replicate. − For instance, the Japanese ANGAS project required over $50 million investment for a single 400-m-deep test cavern, and results are heavily site-dependent due to geological variations. Moreover, such tests offer limited control over experimental conditions, making systematic parameter studies impractical. Numerical simulations have become increasingly sophisticated, with recent models incorporating thermo-hydro-mechanical coupling effects. , However, these simulations rely on numerous assumptions about material properties and boundary conditions that often lack experimental validation, particularly under extreme operating conditions. The reliability of the simulation results remains questionable, especially for long-term cyclic loading scenarios. Conventional laboratory setups typically focus on isolated aspects of the CAES operation. Most devices can only simulate either mechanical stresses or thermal effects, but not their combined impacts. − More critically, existing experimental systems are unable to replicate the integrated geo-stress, geo-temperature, and cyclic gas pressure conditions that occur during the complete charging–discharging process in operational CAES systems. This incapacity to simulate the complete temperature-stress evolution during charge–discharge cycles represents a fundamental barrier to understanding the complex thermo-mechanical behavior of lined rock cavern systems under operational conditions.

To bridge these gaps, we developed the first integrated CAES laboratory simulation device. The device mainly consists of three parts, including a geological environment simulation system, a gas pressure control system, and measurement systems. The device is capable of simulating the geo-condition with the geo-temperature up to 300 °C and geo-stress of up to 100 MPa, and the device could be used to simulate the cyclic charging and discharging process of the CAES caverns with internal pressure ranging from 0 to 70 MPa. Especially, the developed apparatus is capable of studying the mechanical-thermal response of sealing, lining, surrounding rock, and gas leakage under conditions that closely mimic actual CAES operations.

Geological Environment Simulation System

The geological environment simulation system consists of two parts: a triaxial stress control system and a temperature control system.

Triaxial Stress Control System

Figure shows the triaxial stress control system which is composed of a high-pressure-resistant tank and an electro-hydraulic servo control system. As shown in Figure (a), the exterior of the high-pressure-resistant tank consists of five parts: lifting ring, fastening sleeve, top cover, tank body, and fixed base. For the CAES engineering, the geo-stress of the cavern can reach as high as tens of megapascals, and this apparatus can provide a maximum stress of 100 MPa on the sample. Hence, the entire high-pressure-resistant tank is made of high-strength steel. As shown in Figure (b), the interior of the high-pressure-resistant tank is equipped with five hydraulic jacks (the maximum of 1000 kN) to apply the mechanical load with the electro-hydraulic servo control system, with displacement loading control mode and force loading control mode. Two circular holes with a diameter of 6 cm are used for the passage of various pipelines, cables, and sensors inside the tank.

Triaxial mechanical loading and control system.

Temperature Control System

As shown in Figure , the temperature control system consists of a temperature control panel, 16 heating rods embedded in four stress-loading plates, a temperature sensor A, and two gaskets. The temperature control panel is used to set and monitor the disposal temperature of the test sample. These heating rods directly heat the stress-loading plates, transferring thermal energy to the test sample to simulate the underground geological temperature of the lined rock cavern. Additionally, stress-loading plates are employed to apply uniform stress on the surface of the test sample. The entire temperature control system operates in a closed-loop mode, automatically adjusting the power of the heating rods based on real-time feedback from temperature sensor A to ensure that the test sample temperature reaches the target temperature. Moreover, the system includes a variety of stress-loading plates and gaskets in different sizes to accommodate test samples ranging from 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm to 300 mm × 300 mm × 300 mm in dimensions.

Temperature control system.

Gas Pressure Control System

The cyclic variation of the internal gas pressure causes the temperature change in the cavern, the deformation of the surrounding rock, and gas seepage into surrounding rock, which would influence the stability and gas tightness of the CAES cavern. Therefore, the function of internal gas pressure control and monitoring in the cavern is of great importance for the CAES laboratory test device.

As shown in Figure , the gas pressure control system consists of three parts: the gas boosting module, the gas charging module, and the gas discharging module. Key components and their specifications are summarized in Table . Meanwhile, it supports the injection of air or other types of gases (CO2, N2, etc.). The gas boosting module mainly consists of the air compressor, the storage bottle, and the gas booster. The air compressor compresses air into the storage bottle and stores it. When the pressure of the air in the storage bottle reaches a certain level, open valve V5 to drive the gas booster, which increases the pressure of the gas by a ratio of 1:100 and injects it into the internal cavity of the sample.

The gas charging module mainly consists of the gas supply unit (air compressor and gas cylinder), the pressure-reducing valves, the flowmeter, the buffer tank, and the gas injection pipe. The pressure-reducing valves reduce the gas pressure below 0.7 MPa within the measuring range of the flowmeter. The flowmeter is used to measure the quantity of injected gas. The buffer tank is installed in the back of the flowmeter to alleviate the pressure drop downstream of the flowmeter during the operation of the gas booster. Otherwise, the large pressure difference between the upstream and downstream of the flowmeter could cause a large gas flow rate beyond the flowmeter’s measuring range.

The gas discharging module mainly consists of pressure-reducing valves and a flowmeter (as mentioned above). Unlike the gas charging module, the gas source of the gas discharging module originates from the sample cavity, and the discharged gas is directly emitted into the atmosphere.

Measurement System

Experimental Works

To verify the reliability of the CAES laboratory simulation device, we carried out cyclic gas charging and discharging tests under triaxial stress conditions.

Sample Preparation

The sample used was prepared to simulate LRC technology, with butyl rubber as the sealing layer and C40 concrete as the lining layer. The composite rock sample was 300 mm × 300 mm × 300 mm block sample. As shown in Figure , the sample mainly consists of the butyl rubber, the C40 concrete, the gas injection pipe, temperature sensors group, and strain gauges.

Schematic diagram of the sample.

First, the gas injection pipe was inserted into the hollow butyl rubber sphere with an internal diameter of 50 mm and an external diameter of 53 mm and firmly secured using high-strength epoxy resin adhesive. Meanwhile, a 6 mm × 4 mm single-bridge strain gauge was attached to the central surface of the butyl rubber sphere. Then, the gas injection pipe was fixed to the upper support frame of the mold. C40 concrete was poured into the mold in multiple stages and placed on a vibration table to continuously shake and remove excess air. When the concrete filled the mold and covered half the volume of the butyl rubber spherical body, five K-type thermocouples (temperature sensors) were inserted at horizontal distances of 0, 3, 13, 23, and 33 mm from the surface of butyl rubber. The C40 concrete was then quickly poured again to continue filling the mold and shaking to ensure a uniform distribution. When the mold was filled and no more air bubbles emerged, the sample was left to stand indoors for 2 days before demolding. Finally, a 300 mm × 300 mm × 300 mm sample was finished after the sample was placed in a saturated solution of sodium hydroxide for curing for 27 days. Experiment Results and Analysis

Theoretical Bases

The ideal gas law is used to calculate the gas leakage rate. The equation is as follows 1 PV=nRT where P represents the gas pressure in the cavity (Pa), V is the volume of the cavity (m3), n is the amount of gas substance in the cavity (mol), R is the ideal gas constant 8.314 J/(mol·K), and T represents the Kelvin temperature of the gas (K). The conversion between Kelvin and Celsius temperature is given by 2 T=t+273.15 where t is the Celsius temperature (°C).

Since the deformation of the cavity during the gas injection and release stages is negligible (maximum 19.4 microstrains, as shown in Figure ), the volume of the gas cavity is assumed to remain constant. Consequently, the amount of air substance injected into the cavity in the ith second can be expressed as 3 Δni=P0·ΔViR·T0 where P 0 is the standard atmospheric pressure, T 0 is the standard atmospheric temperature (20 °C), and ΔV i is the volume of air injected at the ith second.

The amount of air substance in the cavity at i seconds can be expressed as 4 ni=Pi·VR·Ti where P i and T i are the gas pressure and temperature in the cavity at i second, which are measured by the pressure sensor and temperature sensor B, respectively.

Therefore, the volume of air leaked in the ith second can be expressed by 5 Vleak,i=(Δni+ni−1−ni)·R·T0P0

Stress–Strain Curves

As shown in Figure , during the triaxial synchronous stress-loading process of the sample at a rate of 1 kN/s, the strain increased with the increase of stress. In the initial loading stage, slight stress increases caused relatively large strains in all three directions, indicating the closure of pre-existing microfractures and pores within the sample. As the loading progressed, the sample exhibited higher stiffness, with the curves diverging due to differences in the material response along each axis. The Z-axis curve showed the steepest slope, suggesting greater stiffness in this direction, which can possibly be attributed to the anisotropic properties of the composite rock sample, the heterogeneity in the distribution of the sealing and lining layers, and the formation of a rigid “skeletal structure” due to the substantially higher elastic modulus of the metal (injection pipe) compared to the surrounding concrete. The X and Z axes displayed more gradual slopes, reflecting variations in the sample’s internal structure. Overall, the mechanical loading and measuring system performed well in stress–strain controlling and measuring, indicating that the geological environment simulation system can accurately replicate real geological conditions.

Gas Pressure, Temperature, Injection Flow, and Leakage Rate Evolution Curves

The evolution of gas pressure, temperature, and gas leakage throughout the charging–discharging process is shown in Figure . The maximum and minimum gas pressures were 7.1 and 1.6, 5.9 and 1.5, and 5.4 and 1.7 MPa during 3 charging–discharging cycles, respectively. The experiment could be divided into three phases: the gas charging phase, the gas storage phase, and the gas discharging phase. During the gas charging phase, as gas was continuously injected, the quantity of gas in the cavity was compressed, leading to a continuous increase in gas pressure and temperature. The gas pressure reached a stable pressure (the maximum gas pressure), indicating the balance condition of the gas injection and gas leakage. During the gas storage phase, as shown in Figure (c), no gas was injected into the cavity, and the gas pressure in the cavity decreased due to gas leakage. During the gas discharging phase, as the gas inside the cavity was released, the cavity temperature showed a decrease.

The gas leakage calculated by eq during the charging–discharging cycle is also shown in Figure (c). The gas leakage rate varied during the charging–discharging cycle, which showed a strong relation with the gas pressure in the cavity. The gas leakage rate increased with the gas pressure increasing during the charging process, while it decreased during the discharging process. Overall, the gas pressure control system, gas flow, and gas temperature measuring system performed well during the charging and discharging processes, indicating that the gas pressure control system and measurement system can simulate and monitor the real operation conditions of a CAES plant.

Thermal and Mechanical Response of Sample

Temperature of the Lining

During the entire experimental process, the gas temperature within the cavity increased from 28 to 43.5 °C (as shown in Figure (b)). Due to the low thermal conductivity of butyl rubber and concrete, and short experimental time, the sealing layer (butyl rubber) and the lining layer (concrete) were not heated to a high temperature. However, a distinct temperature gradient was observed across different distances from the cavity (as shown in Figure ). The measured temperature exhibited a negative exponential decay with increasing distance (Y = 25.886 + 0.111 e–0.279x , R 2 = 0.997), consistent with the thermal attenuation pattern reported by Kim et al. Evolution of the Strain on the Sealing Outer Surface

Figure shows the strain evolution on the outer surface of the sealing during the charging and discharging process. During the first gas charging process, the strain on the outer surface of the sealing increased with the gas pressure, and the strain was almost unchanged during the pressure-stable process. While the strain was not recovered during the discharging and pressure-reducing process, indicating irreversible strain was induced on the interface of the sealing layer-lining concrete. During the second charging–discharging cycle, the strain also increased during the discharging process, and the irreversible strain was also left, while the increased strain was small compared to the first charging–discharging cycle. However, almost no strain change was observed for the third charging–discharging process, which could be attributed to the stable condition of the sealing-lining system after charging–discharging loading cycles.

In summary, the experimental device described above, comprising the geological environment simulation system, the gas pressure control system, and the measurement system, is capable of simulating the operation of CAES in the underground lined rock cavern. This experimental device provides a valuable platform to study the complicated response of multiple fields of CAES engineering.

Error Analysis

Based on the technical parameters of the experimental system (Table ), measurement errors in the test results primarily originate from the accuracy of the temperature, pressure, and displacement sensors. The temperature measurements employed K-type thermocouples with an accuracy of ±0.1 °C (Table ); pressure sensors exhibited an error of ±0.2% F.S.; displacement sensors demonstrated a resolution of 0.001 mm; and flowmeters maintained an accuracy of ±0.2% F.S. These errors remain within acceptable limits and exert negligible influence on both the gas leakage rate calculations (eqs –) and all curves (Figures –). The implementation of closed-loop control systems and repeated testing further ensured the data reliability. Results confirm that measurement errors do not alter the fundamental patterns of mechanical response, temperature distribution, or gas leakage behavior, thereby validating the apparatus’s accuracy in simulating CAES operational conditions.

Strengths and Limitations

This study presents the first integrated experimental platform capable of simultaneously replicating three critical operational conditions of compressed air energy storage (CAES) systems: geo-stress (up to 100 MPa), geological temperature (up to 300 °C), and cyclic gas pressurization (0–70 MPa). The developed apparatus uniquely enables a comprehensive investigation of thermo-mechanical responses in sealing, lining, surrounding, and gas leakage characteristics under conditions that accurately simulate actual CAES operations. Most significantly, this research provides the first experimental demonstration of irreversible strain accumulation (19.4 after 3 cycles) in butyl rubber sealing layers under operational CAES conditions. And we have quantitatively characterized a fundamental phenomenon of temperature gradient evolution, establishing crucial empirical references for engineering applications. These findings show excellent agreement with field measurements from lined rock caverns reported by Kim et al. and numerical simulations by Zhou et al., with correlation coefficients of R 2 = 0.997 for temperature profiles, thereby validating both the reliability of our experimental platform and the universal applicability of the revealed mechanical-thermal coupling mechanisms.

While this study provides valuable insights into the thermo-mechanical behavior of CAES-lined rock caverns, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the 300 mm × 300 mm × 300 mm laboratory-scale samples, though suitable for controlled experiments, cannot fully replicate stress redistribution effects in full-scale caverns (>10 m diameter) or the geological heterogeneity of natural rock masses, potentially altering failure mechanisms in field conditions. , Second, material simplificationssuch as the use of homogeneous C40 concrete and standardized butyl rubbermay not fully capture the complexities of real-world CAES systems, which often involve composite lining structures with reinforcement, naturally fractured host rock, and long-term material aging effects. , Third, the experimental scope was limited to three charge–discharge cycles, whereas commercial CAES systems undergo 100+ cycles annually, with material degradation (e.g., fatigue cracking) typically emerging after 50+ cycles. These limitations highlight the need for future research on scaling laws, long-term cyclic performance, and more realistic material configurations to bridge the gap between laboratory findings and field applications.

Conclusions and Future Perspectives

Key Findings

To study the thermodynamic and mechanical response of the underground lined rock cavern during the cyclic process of gas charging and discharging, a laboratory simulation device for compressed air energy storage is developed.(1)The developed device consists of a geological environment simulation system, a gas pressure control system, and measurement systems. To better understand this device, detailed introductions have been provided for its functionalities, working principles, and operational procedures.(2)The device is capable of simulating the cyclic gas charging and discharging process of a CAES cavern under conditions of up to 300 °C in geo-temperature, up to 100 MPa in geo-stress, and internal cavity gas pressure up to 70 MPa. It provides a platform to simulate complicated scenarios through the independent operation of the geological environment simulation system, gas pressure control system, and measurement systems, including true triaxial mechanical loading under temperature-controlled conditions, as well as research on the sealing performance of the artificial CAES-lined rock caverns, and the optimization of cavern layout considering in situ stress.(3)A cyclic gas charging and discharging test was carried out on a composite rock sample (300 mm × 300 mm × 300 mm) fabricated based on lined rock cavern technology with butyl rubber as the sealing layer and C40 concrete as the lining layer. The test was carried out under 8 MPa of geo-stress, with the internal pressure of the cavity varying between 1.5 and 7.1 MPa. Results show that the geological environment simulation system, gas pressure control system, and measurement systems perform excellently. Notably, the sealing layer exhibited irreversible strain and creep behavior. The gas temperature inside the cavity continues to increase during the gas charging process, while slightly dropped during the gas discharging process. This experimental device provides a reliable platform to study the evolution laws and mechanisms of the CAES caverns under the complicated stress-temperature-gas charging and discharging condition.

Future Perspectives

Building on the current achievements, five strategic development pathways are proposed. First, although the current device successfully simulates small-scale CAES caverns (300 mm × 300 mm × 300 mm), further studies could explore its applicability to larger systems by optimizing the loading capacity and chamber dimensions. This would better reflect real-world engineering conditions. Second, the device could be adapted to study hybrid energy storage systems, such as combining CAES with thermal energy storage or hydrogen storage, to evaluate multifield coupling effects under integrated operational scenarios. Third, incorporating real-time microseismic monitoring or distributed fiber-optic sensors could enhance the detection of microfractures and localized deformations in the lining and sealing layers. Fourth, future tests could evaluate alternative sealing materials (e.g., nanocoated polymers) or lining designs to improve long-term stability and airtightness under extreme conditions. Lastly, coupling this experimental platform with CFD simulations would enable a deeper analysis of fluid dynamics, heat transfer, and gas leakage pathways under varied operational parameters. We hold the belief that these developments will transform the current platform into a next-generation CAES research facility, bridging critical gaps between laboratory investigations and field applications.
