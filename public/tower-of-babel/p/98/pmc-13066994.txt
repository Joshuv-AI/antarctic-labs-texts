CA9-related ferroptosis is a potential therapeutic target of kaempferol anti-oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Jia-Yi Lu, Xin-Miao Zhao, Xian-Bao Liu, Li Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the most common oral malignancy, affecting the tongue, palate, mouth floor, alveolar ridge, and buccal mucosa. Kaempferol, a natural flavonoid, exhibits anti-oral cancer properties, but its mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate kaempferol’s therapeutic potential and molecular mechanisms in OSCC using network pharmacology, molecular docking, and experimental validation.MethodsKaempferol’s components and targets were identified via the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP). OSCC-related genes were sourced from GeneCards and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases. Network and molecular docking analyses were performed using Cytoscape and Autodock, respectively. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID). In vitro and in vivo experiments validated kaempferol’s effects.ResultsKaempferol induced ferroptosis, reducing OSCC cell viability and increasing apoptosis dose-dependently. Carbonic anhydrase IX (CA9) emerged as a core gene mediating kaempferol’s anti-OSCC effects via ferroptosis. Molecular docking confirmed strong binding affinity between kaempferol and CA9 (−6.24 kcal/mol). GO and KEGG analyses revealed kaempferol’s modulation of p53/FoxO/cellular senescence pathways. Experiments confirmed kaempferol’s inhibition of OSCC proliferation and migration through CA9/ferroptosis induction.ConclusionsKaempferol exerts anti-OSCC effects via CA9/ferroptosis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for OSCC.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Kaempferol inhibits oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cell viability/proliferation and migration, suppresses tumor growth in vivo with a favorable safety profile, and promotes ferroptosis; carbonic anhydrase IX (CA9) is identified as a key downstream mediator with strong predicted binding to kaempferol.

What is known and what is new?

• Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent, reactive oxygen species (ROS)/lipid peroxidation-driven regulated cell death and is considered a promising therapeutic avenue in OSCC. CA9 is a hypoxia-related gene critical for tumor adaptation and has been reported as a ferroptosis suppressor gene. Kaempferol has documented anti-oxidant/anti-inflammatory/anti-cancer activities, but direct evidence linking kaempferol, OSCC, and ferroptosis has been limited.

• This study shows that kaempferol increases malondialdehyde and depletes glutathione, and downregulates ferroptosis-defense genes (GPX4, FTH1, NOX1, and COX-2) in OSCC cells. CA9 is upregulated in OSCC and demonstrates strong diagnostic performance (area under the curve =0.963); docking and molecular dynamics support stable kaempferol-CA9 interaction (−6.24 kcal/mol).

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These results support targeting the CA9/ferroptosis axis in OSCC and nominate kaempferol as a multi-target therapeutic candidate. Next steps should prioritize direct biophysical validation of kaempferol-CA9 binding (e.g., surface plasmon resonance or isothermal titration calorimetry) and further translational evaluation.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) poses a significant threat to human health, with a global burden exceeding 300,000 cases annually and a high mortality rate (1). The increasing prevalence of OSCC and its poor 5-year survival rate of 50% highlight the urgent need for effective therapies (2,3). Traditional treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, encounter challenges such as tumor invasion and distant metastasis, leading to limited success in enhancing patient outcomes (4). Targeting premalignant lesions in the oral cavity may offer a potential strategy to prevent OSCC progression and reduce its incidence (5). Therefore, the development of new therapeutic agents with enhanced efficacy and safety profiles is crucial for addressing the impact of OSCC on human survival.

Ferroptosis, a newly recognized programmed cell death characterized by iron-dependent reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a critical focus in tumor treatment research (6,7). Ferroptosis is regarded as an adaptive mechanism to eliminate malignant cells, especially those resistant to chemotherapy, in various cancers, including OSCC (8). Tumor cells exhibit increased iron-requirements for their vigorous growth, making ferroptosis an attractive target for improving survival rates in OSCC patients (9,10). Ferroptosis is characterized by the dysregulation of key factors such as PTGS2 and GPX4, as well as the depletion of ROS scavengers (11). Although its biological functions and molecular mechanisms are not fully understood, ferroptosis has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including tumors, cardiovascular disorders, and inflammatory conditions.

Carbonic anhydrase IX (CA9) is a tightly regulated hypoxia-related gene crucial for cancer cell adaptation to hypoxia and acidosis (12,13). Additionally, CA9 is identified as a ferroptosis suppressor gene (14). Recent studies indicate that inhibiting ferroptosis can alleviate colitis symptoms and restore intestinal epithelial barrier function in intestinal diseases (15,16). These findings underscore the potential of targeting ferroptosis-related factors as a reliable therapeutic strategy for OSCC and other diseases.

Kaempferol, a flavonoid present in many plants, has demonstrated anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-cancer activities (17). Research indicates that kaempferol demonstrates anti-oxidant capabilities by scavenging ROS and inhibiting pro-oxidant enzymes, thereby preventing cellular damage (18). Kaempferol exerts anti-proliferative effects on diverse cancer cell lines by inducing apoptosis, arresting the cell cycle, and downregulating key tumor progression proteins (18,19). Notably, kaempferol’s relatively low toxicity towards normal cells and its potential interactions with estrogen receptors suggest its promising role in hormone-related cancers (20). This study utilized network pharmacology to identify potential therapeutic agents for OSCC, focusing on the anti-OSCC effects of kaempferol. Subsequently, the CA9-mediated ferroptosis signaling pathway was identified as a potential mechanism underlying the anti-OSCC activity of kaempferol. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1488/rc).

Methods

Drugs and reagents

Kaempferol, sourced from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (catalog No. K107144, purity ≥98%, Shanghai, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) and RPMI-1640 medium (catalog No. C11875500) were obtained from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) with high glucose (catalog No. G4612) was sourced from Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Detection kit for bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein content (catalog No. MA0082-2) was purchased from Melone Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). Additionally, a cell cycle detection kit (catalog No. G1700-50T) and methyl thiazolyl tetrazolium (MTT, catalog No. GC307004) were also obtained from Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. A reverse transcription kit was procured from Monad Biotech Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China).

Kaempferol active ingredient and target selection

This study investigates the effects of kaempferol with the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP), you can search for 499 herbs, 3,311 targets, 18,144 compounds, and 837 diseases that are associated with Chinese herbs. Through the TCMSP platform, 195 target genes were identified based on oral bioavailability (OB) ≥30% and drug-likeness (DL) ≥0.18.

Acquisition of OSCC target genes

In the GeneCards database, we searched for ‘oral squamous cell carcinoma’ to identify genes related to OSCC. This approach led to the identification of 3,256 genes containing OSCC-related proteins. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Construction of a common target and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for kaempferol and OSCC

Ferroptosis, kaempferol, and OSCC were grouped into a Venn diagram, using Xiantao Academic Website (https://www.xiantaozi.com/). PPI networks were systematically collected and analyzed using the STRING database. STRING version 12.0 was used to test the common targets for Homo sapiens with a minimum interaction score of 0.900 (highest level of confidence). To create a clear network diagram, Cytoscape 3.8.0 was used to create a PPI network diagram for the common targets. Nodes’ sizes and colors were adjusted for clarity.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis

Functional annotation of the gene list, pre-processed using STRING, was performed via the DAVID database. This analysis identified relevant GO terms—categorizing biological processes (BPs), molecular functions (MFs), and cellular components (CCs)—as well as KEGG pathways. The top 10 significantly enriched entries from each category, selected based on the lowest P values, were visualized using bubble plots and bar charts.

Molecular docking validation analysis of core targets

For molecular docking studies with kaempferol, seven key targets were identified from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) based on the PPI network analysis. We retrieved kaempferol’s three-dimensional structural data from PubChem’s SDF database and converted it to PDB format using Open Babel. AutoDock Vina software was used for molecular docking simulations. The docking results were filtered and analyzed using PyMOL software, which facilitated the visualization of interactions by generating images that illustrate the bonds and bond lengths between the molecule and the target proteins, as well as the overall docking interaction patterns.

Cell culture and viability assessment

A collection of OSCC cell lines from BeNa Culture Collection (Shanghai, China) was used to establish SCC-9, SCC-7, and SCC-25 cell lines. The culture media for SCC-9 (catalog No. TCH-G124), SCC-7 (catalog No. TCM-G824-C), and SCC-25 (catalog No. TCH-G455) were sourced from Cos9XTM (Shanghai, China) and cultured in a CO2 incubator at 37 ℃. Cell proliferation was evaluated via the MTT assay. Specifically, the cell suspensions were inoculated into a 96-well plate at a density of 1×105 cells/mL (100 µL per well). After an overnight incubation, kaempferol was added at concentrations of 0, 10, 50, 100, 200, or 400 µM and incubated for 24 and 48 hours. After this period, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% MTT and incubated for 4 more hours. Thermo Fisher Scientific microplate readers (Waltham, MA, USA) were used to measure 450 nm absorbance.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

From cell samples, total RNA was extracted using a commercial kit, followed by reverse transcription to generate complementary DNA. To test transcription levels of target genes, RT-qPCR with gene-specific primers and SYBR Green dye was conducted. Melting curve analysis confirmed the specificity of the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products. The threshold cycle (CT) method was used to determine the expression levels of target genes in comparison to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), which was used as a reference gene.

Flow cytometry analysis for cell cycle

During the first 48 hours of the experiment, exponentially growing cells were seeded in a six-well plate and exposed to different concentrations of kaempferol. A pancreatic enzyme sans ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was used to digest cells post-incubation, followed by two washes in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and flow cytometry analysis for cell cycle distribution.

5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining

Cells harvested from OSCCs (1×105 per well of six-well plates) were seeded and allowed to adhere. The cells were then treated at 37 ℃ for 2 hours with 50 M EdU reagent (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.). Cells were incubated in 4% formaldehyde for 30 minutes, fixed in 50 L of glycine and treated with Triton X-100. EdU-positive cells were viewed under a fluorescence microscope (Phenix, Shangrao, China).

Transwell assay

Twenty-four-well transwell plate was constructed with the upper chamber containing 100 µL of serum-free medium and the lower chamber containing 600 µL of 10% FBS culture medium 24 hours after transfection. The transwell membranes had a pore size of 0.22 µm. After a further 24-hour incubation, cells that had not migrated were removed from the upper membrane surface. Transwell membranes with a 0.22 µm pore size were used. After an additional 24-hour incubation, non-migrated cells were removed from the upper membrane surface. To quantify the data, the cells were photographed with a light microscope (Phenix) and counted.

Wound-healing assay

After adhesion, sterile pipette tips were used to scratch the monolayer with logarithmic phase cells plated in six-well plates. Multiple washes with sterile PBS were followed by treatment with varying concentrations of drug-containing medium. Images were taken at 12-hour intervals to monitor wound healing.

Animals and experimental design

A protocol was prepared before the study without registration. All animal experiments were performed under a project license (No. IRB3082724) granted by the Ethics Committee of College of Pharmacy, Harbin Medical University, in compliance with Chinese national guidelines for the care and use of animals. Ten male C57BL/6 mice (4–6 weeks old, 21–23 g) were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China) and housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under a 12 hours/12 hours light-dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. To establish the orthotopic tongue tumor model, SCC-7 cells were prepared at a concentration of 5×106 cells/mL, and 20 µL of the suspension (1×106 cells) was injected into the tongue. Mice were randomly assigned to a control group or a kaempferol-treated group. The treatment group received kaempferol (400 µM) every other day for 15 days after tumor establishment. Tumor size and mass were recorded, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (length × width2)/2. At week 4, mice were euthanized and tumor tissues were harvested for gross examination and subsequent histopathological evaluation. Tissue specimens were processed according to World Health Organization (WHO) classification criteria for head and neck tumors, including fixation, dehydration, paraffin embedding, sectioning, and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining.

Biochemical measurement

To evaluate the effects of kaempferol on ferroptosis-related biochemical markers, SCC-7, SCC-9, and SCC-25 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells per well and cultured overnight at 37 ℃ in 5% CO2. Cells were then treated with kaempferol (400 µM) for 48 hours. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and collected by centrifugation at 3,000 ×g for 5 min.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione (GSH) levels were quantified using commercial assay kits (Boxbio, Beijing, China; MDA: AKFA013M; GSH: AKPR008M). Briefly, approximately 5×106 cells were resuspended in 1 mL of extraction buffer provided with the kits and lysed by ultrasonication (200 W; 3 s on/10 s off; 30 cycles). Lysates were centrifuged at 8,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 ℃, and supernatants were kept on ice until analysis. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 532 nm for MDA and 412 nm for GSH, and concentrations were calculated according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical analysis

All data were included in the statistical analysis without any exclusions. A statistician conducted the analysis without bias towards specific groups. Results are expressed as mean values ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from three independent experiments performed concurrently. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v8.1.1, San Diego, CA, USA). We conducted a one-way analysis of variance followed by a Dunnett’s test to compare groups with similar variances and to assess differences between the control and multi-dose treatment groups. A significance threshold of P<0.05 was used.

Results

Kaempferol suppresses OSCC cell viability and migration

We evaluated the effects of kaempferol on OSCC using SCC-9 and SCC-25 cell lines. Cells were incubated for 48 hours with kaempferol at concentrations of 0, 50, 100, 200, and 400 µM. Our data showed kaempferol can anti-OSCC at the concertation of 400 µM for 24 or 48 hours (Figure 1A,1B, Figure S1A,S1B). So, for further study, we used kaempferol at the concertation of 400 µM for 48 hours. The EdU assay further confirmed that kaempferol (400 µM, 48 hours) effectively inhibits cell proliferation in SCC-9 and SCC-25 cell lines (Figure 1C). Transwell and wound healing assays revealed a marked decrease in the migration capacity of OSCC cells following kaempferol (400 µM, 48 hours) treatment (Figure 1D,1E). Furthermore, cell cycle analysis indicated that kaempferol (400 µM, 48 hours) modulates the cell cycle progression in OSCC cells (Figure 1F). In conclusion, these findings underscore kaempferol’s significant therapeutic potential for treating OSCC.

Kaempferol inhibits OSCC in vivo

To evaluate kaempferol’s in vivo effects on OSCC, we developed an SCC-7-induced oral mucosal carcinogenesis model. We first tested the anti-OSCC effect of kaempferol in SCC-7 cells. Our data shown kaempferol (400 µM for 48 hours) can significantly suppress SCC-7 cell viability, proliferation, and migration (Figure S2A-S2E). In vivo study, we found no significant differences in body weight between the control and kaempferol-treated groups (Figure 2A,2B). After a treatment period of 2 weeks, the kaempferol-treated mice exhibited a smaller tongue lesion area compared to the SCC-7 induced group (Figure 2C). Histopathological examinations indicated that the dorsal tongue mucosa in the SCC-7 group primarily showed cancerous changes with severe epithelial dysplasia. Notably, epithelial cells had breached the basement membrane, displayed extensive atypical features, and nuclei appeared hyperchromatic. In contrast, the kaempferol treatment group showed primarily mild and moderate epithelial dysplasia. In contrast, the kaempferol-treated group exhibited primarily mild to moderate epithelial dysplasia. No significant differences in the plasma levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CRE), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were detected between the kaempferol-treated and control groups, suggesting that kaempferol treatment did not cause remarkable hepatic or renal damage (Figure 2D). Furthermore, H&E staining revealed no significant pathological abnormalities in the heart, liver, lung, or kidney of SCC-7-bearing mice (Figure 2E). These results indicate that kaempferol effectively inhibits tumor growth in vivo model of OSCC.

Kaempferol promotes ferroptosis and decreased ferroptosis-relative genes in OSCC cells

Our investigation reveals that kaempferol significantly promotes ferroptosis, a regulated cell death, in OSCC cells. We first test the biochemical events of ferroptosis: a pronounced increase in intracellular MDA levels, coupled with a depletion of GSH (Figure 3A,3B). Kaempferol treatment of OSCC cells can significantly reduce the expression levels of GPX4 (Figure 3C), FTH1 (Figure 3D), NOX1 (Figure 3E), and COX-2 (Figure 3F, P<0.001). These findings underscore the substantial role of kaempferol in modulating ferroptosis-associated factors in OSCC.

CA9 is the potential therapeutic target of kaempferol anti-OSCC via ferroptosis

A Venn diagram showed the overlap of target genes linked to kaempferol, ferroptosis, and OSCC. Analysis identified 11 core genes common to all categories (Figure 4A). To further explore these interactions, we constructed an active component-target gene network using Cytoscape 3.8.0, where yellow rhombic nodes represent co-regulated genes and blue elliptical nodes correspond to kaempferol, OSCC, and ferroptosis (Figure 4B). We assessed hub gene expression levels in OSCC patients using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. Our analysis revealed that CA9 is significantly increased in OSCC compared to normal tissues (Figure 4C). We also evaluated the ROC curve and conducted Kaplan-Meier survival analysis using TCGA data. Results indicated that EGFR and CA9 expression levels correlated with varying survival rates among OSCC patients, with CA9 showing a superior area under the curve (AUC) of 0.963 compared to EGFR’s AUC of 0.767 (Figure 4D). We investigated the role of kaempferol in relation to CA9. Molecular docking analyses revealed that CA9 exhibited the highest binding stability with kaempferol, showing a binding energy of −6.24 kcal/mol (Figure 4E and Table 1). Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulations confirmed that the interaction between kaempferol and CA9 maintained good stability throughout the simulation period (Figure 4F). These findings suggest that CA9 serves as a downstream target of kaempferol in its anti-OSCC activity.

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis of kaempferol target genes

We utilized the DAVID database for GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to examine the 127 genes shared between kaempferol and OSCC. The findings elucidate the intricate cellular mechanisms by which kaempferol may exert therapeutic effects on OSCC. Enrichment analysis indicated that kaempferol primarily operates within the cytoplasm and cell membrane. It seems to affect multiple BPs, such as gene expression regulation, cell proliferation, and apoptosis. Additionally, significant MFs were identified, including binding activities related to proteins, enzymes, ATP, and DNA. Kaempferol is involved in the p53, FoxO, and cellular senescence signaling pathways, potentially contributing to its occurrence to its therapeutic effects on OSCC (Figure 5A,5B). These insights enhance our understanding of how kaempferol may mitigate OSCC through cell cycle regulation.

Discussion

With its rising incidence, OSCC imposes substantial clinical and economic burdens on healthcare systems (21,22). In this study, we assessed candidate therapeutic and adjunctive strategies aimed at improving disease control in clinically challenging contexts, including aggressive behavior, recurrence, and metastasis (23). The findings presented below support the development of more effective management approaches and may help alleviate OSCC-associated burdens for both patients and healthcare systems.

Kaempferol exhibits diverse pharmacological activities, including antitumor, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory effects (24,25). Although some reports suggest that kaempferol may cause tissue injury, particularly in the liver, Al Awadh et al. showed in murine models that aggravated toxicity was confined to the heart and kidneys, and that kaempferol alone did not produce significant injury in these organs (25). In addition, toxicity assessments by Yuan and colleagues indicated that the ethyl acetate fraction of kaempferol extracts induced toxic effects in mice at a dose of 640 mg/kg (26). In our study, kaempferol demonstrated anti-OSCC activity both in vitro and in vivo. Specifically, treatment with kaempferol (400 µM for 48 hours) significantly inhibited OSCC cell growth, proliferation, and migration, and induced cell-cycle arrest. These results suggest that kaempferol is a promising small-molecule candidate for OSCC therapy. In vivo, kaempferol reduced tumor growth and volume with low cytotoxicity. Moreover, liver and kidney function tests indicated no evidence of hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity. Histopathological examination further showed no detectable morphological differences in the heart, liver, lung, or kidney between the sham and kaempferol-treated groups. Collectively, these data indicate that kaempferol exerts anti-OSCC effects in vitro and in vivo with a favorable safety profile.

Ferroptosis, a recently characterized form of regulated cell death, has emerged as a promising therapeutic avenue in multiple diseases, including cancer (27). Accumulating evidence indicates that ferroptosis induction may be particularly effective for tumors with intrinsic resistance to conventional therapies (28). Previous studies have reported that kaempferol can modulate ferroptosis-related processes in several conditions, including oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R)-induced neuronal injury (26), acetaminophen-induced liver injury (29), and doxorubicin-induced myocardial damage (18). However, direct evidence linking kaempferol, OSCC, and ferroptosis remains limited. To test this hypothesis, we first evaluated canonical biochemical hallmarks of ferroptosis, including GSH depletion and MDA accumulation. Our results showed increased MDA levels accompanied by reduced GSH in kaempferol-treated OSCC cells, suggesting that ferroptosis may contribute to kaempferol’s anti-OSCC effects. We further examined ferroptosis-associated markers and found that the expression of COX-2 (30), NOX1 (31), GPX4 (32), and FTL-1—key components of cellular defense against ferroptosis—was significantly decreased. These findings support the notion that kaempferol may exert therapeutic effects in OSCC by modulating ferroptosis-related targets. To explore downstream mechanisms, we applied network pharmacology to identify core targets linking kaempferol, OSCC, and ferroptosis. We mapped 195 predicted targets of kaempferol to OSCC-associated genes and identified 127 overlapping targets (approximately 65%), consistent with a multi-target mode of action. Network analysis further prioritized potential therapeutic targets regulated by kaempferol, including EGFR, PTGS2, CA9, GSK3B, and NOX4. Prior literature has highlighted the crucial roles of p53 family members in tumor progression across cancers such as lung cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma, suggesting complex interactions among these pathways and kaempferol’s mechanisms (33-36). Finally, molecular docking supported these analyses by indicating that CA9 is a putative target protein with strong binding affinity to kaempferol. Together, these results underscore kaempferol’s potential as a multi-target therapeutic agent for OSCC and warrant further investigation into its mechanisms and clinical applicability.

To elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of kaempferol in OSCC, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of its putative target genes. GO enrichment indicated that kaempferol-associated targets were significantly enriched in BPs related to inflammatory responses, protein phosphorylation, and angiogenesis. These results suggest that kaempferol may modulate tumor progression and tumor-microenvironment interactions that are critical to OSCC development. KEGG analysis further revealed significant enrichment of the p53 signaling pathway, implying that kaempferol may exert anti-tumor effects by regulating p53-mediated stress responses and cell-fate decisions, including apoptosis, autophagy, and other forms of regulated cell death. Consistent with this notion, prior studies have shown that the p53 pathway plays an important role in OSCC, including in the context of autophagy induction following KPNA2 inhibition (37). In addition, Hu et al. reported that the FoxO signaling pathway contributes to OSCC progression (38).

Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that kaempferol may inhibit OSCC progression by modulating multiple oncogenic and stress-response pathways. This observation highlights its potential as a multi-target therapeutic candidate. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, target identification and enrichment analyses were based on public databases (e.g., TCMSP and GeneCards) and predefined screening thresholds. These choices may introduce systematic bias and may exclude relevant targets or pathways. Second, docking and molecular dynamics simulations provide only theoretical predictions of binding conformations and affinities. Therefore, direct biophysical assays (e.g., surface plasmon resonance or isothermal titration calorimetry) are required to confirm kaempferol-CA9 binding. In further studies, we will clarify the molecular interactions of kaempferol and its downstream effects in OSCC, which may facilitate the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

This study identifies CA9 as a key target of kaempferol in OSCC and highlights its potential involvement in ferroptosis induction. Experimental results showed that kaempferol modulated CA9 expression, thereby decreasing cell viability and suppressing proliferation and migration, while promoting cell-cycle arrest. By integrating network pharmacology with experimental validation, our work delineates potential pharmacological mechanisms through which kaempferol acts in OSCC and provides a rationale for its further evaluation as a candidate for clinical translation in cancer therapy.
