Glycemic instability in renal decline: a review of HbA1c variability and outcomes in chronic kidney disease
Authors: Fan Zhang, Rong Wu, Yan Bai, Yifei Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), and glycemic assessment is complicated by altered glucose/insulin handling and biased glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) values in advanced kidney disease. Beyond mean HbA1c, long-term visit-to-visit HbA1c variability has emerged as an additional dimension of risk. This review aims to summarize the definition, measurement, and clinical significance of long-term HbA1c variability in diabetes with CKD, and to discuss implications for risk stratification and management, including complementary biomarkers and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM).MethodsWe narratively reviewed observational cohorts, meta-analyses, and dialysis/advanced CKD studies evaluating metrics of long-term glycemic variability (e.g., HbA1c-standard deviation, HbA1c-coefficient of variation, HbA1c variability score) and their associations with renal outcomes, cardiovascular events, and mortality, alongside evidence on alternative biomarkers and CGM-derived indices.ResultsAcross diverse populations, higher HbA1c variability is consistently associated with faster CKD progression, greater risk of ESKD, increased cardiovascular events, and higher all-cause mortality, often independent of mean HbA1c. In advanced CKD and dialysis, HbA1c limitations and treatment-related glucose swings heighten the value of CGM and, in selected settings, glycated albumin/fructosamine.ConclusionLong-term HbA1c variability is a robust prognostic marker in diabetic CKD and should complement mean HbA1c in clinical assessment; prospective trials are needed to test whether variability-targeted strategies improve hard outcomes.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, End-stage kidney disease, Glycated hemoglobin, Variability, Review

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) globally [1, 2]. Managing hyperglycemia in this population is paramount, yet it presents unique and formidable challenges. In advanced CKD, glucose and insulin metabolism are profoundly altered by factors such as impaired renal insulin clearance, reduced gluconeogenesis, and the effects of uremia and dialysis itself, which collectively increase the risk of both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia [2, 3]. Traditional glycemic assessment relies heavily on glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), but its reliability is compromised in CKD. Conditions like anemia, iron therapy, use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, and chronic inflammation can bias HbA1c measurements, making them an imperfect reflection of true glycemia [2–4].

Consequently, the conventional paradigm of targeting a single HbA1c threshold for all patients with diabetes is increasingly questioned in the context of CKD. Clinical guidelines, informed by observational data linking intensive control to increased hypoglycemia risk, often recommend a less stringent HbA1c target (e.g., 7–8%) for patients with advanced CKD [3]. However, a growing body of evidence suggests that the long-term stability of glycemic control, measured as visit-to-visit HbA1c variability, may be as critical as, or even more informative than, the mean HbA1c level alone. This variability reflects fluctuations in glycemic management over time and may signify underlying pathophysiological instability, suboptimal treatment, or susceptibility to complications. Emerging research positions HbA1c variability as a significant independent predictor of adverse renal and cardiovascular outcomes, offering a novel dimension for risk stratification and a potential target for therapeutic intervention in the high-risk population with diabetes and CKD [5–7]. This review aims to synthesize current evidence on long-term (visit-to-visit) HbA1c variability in patients with diabetes and CKD, focusing on its measurement, pathophysiological underpinnings, and associations with kidney disease progression, cardiovascular events, and mortality, while highlighting limitations of HbA1c in advanced CKD and the emerging roles of alternative biomarkers and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)-guided strategies.

Defining and measuring glycemic variability: metrics beyond mean HbA1c

Glycemic variability refers to the amplitude, frequency, and duration of fluctuations in blood glucose levels over time. While short-term variability (within-day fluctuations) can be captured by CGM, long-term variability, spanning months to years, is typically assessed using serial HbA1c measurements obtained during routine clinical visits. Several statistical metrics have been employed to quantify this visit-to-visit variability, each capturing slightly different aspects of glycemic instability.

The most commonly used metrics are the standard deviation (SD) of serial HbA1c values and the coefficient of variation (CV), which is the SD expressed as a percentage of the mean HbA1c, thus accounting for the level of glycemia [6, 8]. Another practical metric is the HbA1c variability score (HVS), defined as the percentage of total HbA1c measurements that vary by more than 0.5% (5.5 mmol/mol) from the immediately preceding value within a specified time window [7, 9]. Higher values on any of these indices indicate greater instability in glycemic control. These measures are generally independent of the mean HbA1c level, meaning that two patients with the same average glycemia can have vastly different variability profiles, with potentially divergent clinical prognoses [5, 8].

The clinical relevance of these metrics is underscored by meta-analyses and large cohort studies. A systematic review confirmed that the highest quantiles of HbA1c-SD, HbA1c-CV, and HVS are consistently associated with increased risks of mortality, cardiovascular events, and progression to CKD in type 2 diabetes [6]. The choice of metric may depend on the clinical setting and available data, but the overarching principle is that sustained instability in glycemic control, as reflected by these parameters, carries independent prognostic weight beyond a single or averaged HbA1c value.

HbA1c variability and the risk of kidney disease progression

A substantial body of observational evidence links greater long-term HbA1c variability to an increased risk of both the development and progression of CKD in diabetes (Table 1). In patients with type 2 diabetes and preserved renal function, higher HbA1c variability predicts the future onset of CKD [10, 11]. The association remains robust in those with established CKD. A large study of patients with diabetes and CKD stages 3–5 found that higher HbA1c variability was independently associated with a composite outcome of CKD progression, acute kidney injury, and worsening albuminuria, even after adjustment for mean HbA1c [7]. Similarly, another cohort study of patients with diabetes and CKD reported that increasing HbA1c variability, measured by either HVS or CV, was strongly associated with progression to ESKD [12].

The relationship appears to extend to type 1 diabetes as well. In a multi-ethnic cohort with preserved estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), higher HbA1c variability, assessed by multiple methods, was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of a ≥ 50% decline in eGFR [13]. The mechanisms linking glycemic variability to kidney injury may be distinct from those of chronic hyperglycemia. Fluctuations in glucose can induce greater oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation than sustained high glucose. Experimental models suggest that glucose variations can trigger the release of kidney injury molecule-1 from immune cells, potentially initiating early renal damage [14].

Interestingly, some studies suggest that the predictive power of HbA1c variability for renal outcomes may be particularly strong for albuminuric CKD phenotypes [15]. Furthermore, variability in other parameters, such as blood pressure, uric acid, and lipids, also contributes to renal risk, and the concomitance of high variability in multiple domains (e.g., HbA1c and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol) may confer the greatest hazard [16, 17]. While most evidence points to harm, one study in patients with stages 3–4 CKD and poor control (HbA1c ≥ 7%) suggested that greater variability associated with a decreasing trend in HbA1c (possibly indicating intensification of therapy) was linked to a lower risk of dialysis, highlighting the complexity of interpreting variability in the context of treatment changes [18].

Glycemic variability frequently coexists with variability in other cardiometabolic parameters, particularly blood pressure. Emerging evidence suggests that visit-to-visit blood pressure variability is an independent predictor of CKD progression, cardiovascular events, and mortality in both the general population and patients with diabetes [19]. Importantly, glycemic and blood pressure variability may share common underlying mechanisms, including autonomic dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation [20]. Studies indicate that the coexistence of high variability across multiple domains confers a substantially greater risk than variability in a single parameter alone. In patients with CKD, where homeostatic regulation is already impaired, the combined burden of glycemic and blood pressure fluctuations may accelerate target organ damage [21]. These findings support a more integrated approach to risk assessment that considers multidimensional variability rather than isolated metrics.

HbA1c variability, cardiovascular outcomes, and mortality in CKD

The detrimental impact of glycemic instability extends beyond the kidneys to encompass cardiovascular health and survival. Numerous studies have established HbA1c variability as an independent risk factor for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and all-cause mortality in broad populations with type 2 diabetes [6, 9]. This association holds significant relevance for the CKD population, which carries an exceptionally high cardiovascular burden.

In patients with diabetes and CKD, higher HbA1c variability is consistently associated with increased mortality. A study focusing on HbA1c variability in type 2 diabetes with CKD found that its average real variability was a more effective predictor of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality than mean HbA1c or the HbA1c-hemoglobin ratio [5]. The risk appears pronounced in dialysis populations. Among diabetic patients on peritoneal dialysis, those in the highest categories of HbA1c variability (measured by CV) had a two- to three-fold increased risk of all-cause mortality compared to those with the most stable control [22]. Another study in peritoneal dialysis patients confirmed that a higher HVS was strongly associated with both all-cause mortality and MACE [27].

The relationship with cardiovascular disease may be modified by kidney function. Some data indicate that the association between high HbA1c variability and increased cardiovascular event risk is significant in patients with preserved renal function but may be attenuated or absent in those with moderate to advanced CKD [28]. This could be related to the overwhelming competing risks in advanced CKD or to the altered pathophysiology and biomarker interpretation in this state. Nonetheless, variability in other clinical parameters remains crucial; visit-to-visit variability in systolic blood pressure is a potent predictor of cardiovascular events, ESKD, and mortality in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes [29–31]. The stress hyperglycemia ratio, an indicator of acute glycemic variability, has also been identified as an independent predictor of long-term MACE in patients with acute coronary syndrome and CKD [32].

Special considerations in advanced CKD and dialysis: limitations of HbA1c and the role of alternative biomarkers

The assessment of glycemic control becomes particularly fraught in advanced CKD (stages 4–5) and in patients receiving dialysis. The very factors that make HbA1c unreliable, altered red cell turnover, inflammation, and frequent interventions, are hallmarks of this stage [2, 3]. For instance, hemoglobin levels often fluctuate due to erythropoiesis-stimulating agent use and iron therapy, directly impacting the HbA1c value independent of glycemia. Furthermore, the glycemic experience itself is uniquely perturbed by renal replacement therapy. During hemodialysis, patients can experience hypoglycemia due to glucose loss into the dialysate and improved insulin sensitivity, followed by rebound hyperglycemia in the post-dialysis period [33, 34]. Conversely, peritoneal dialysis exposes patients to continuous glucose absorption from the dialysate, leading to sustained hyperglycemic excursions and weight gain [35].

Given these limitations, alternative biomarkers have been investigated. Glycated albumin and albumin-corrected fructosamine, which reflect glycemia over a shorter period (2–3 weeks) and are theoretically less affected by hemoglobin disorders, show promise. Studies in peritoneal dialysis patients have found glycated albumin and fructosamine to correlate well with mean glucose levels measured by continuous monitoring [35]. Importantly, variability in glycated albumin has been shown to predict the progression of diabetic nephropathy independently of HbA1c [36]. The ratio of glycated albumin to HbA1c has been proposed as a potential marker of glycemic variability, with a U-shaped association observed with mortality risk in type 2 diabetes [37]. However, these alternative markers are not without their own limitations, as they can be influenced by abnormal protein turnover and serum albumin levels, which are common in advanced CKD and malnutrition. Consequently, no single biomarker is perfectly reliable in this population, necessitating a more nuanced and integrated approach to glycemic assessment.

Several studies have directly compared the performance of alternative glycemic biomarkers with HbA1c in dialysis populations. For example, investigations have demonstrated that glycated albumin correlates more closely with mean glucose levels derived from continuous glucose monitoring than HbA1c in patients undergoing dialysis [38, 39]. These studies highlight that HbA1c may underestimate glycemic exposure due to shortened red blood cell lifespan, whereas glycated albumin provides a more reliable reflection of short-term glycemic control. Similarly, fructosamine has shown improved sensitivity in detecting poor glycemic control in this population. However, both markers remain influenced by protein metabolism and nutritional status, underscoring the need for combined interpretation.

Continuous glucose monitoring: a novel tool for assessing glycemic control and variability in CKD

Continuous glucose monitoring systems represent a paradigm shift in managing diabetes in CKD. By measuring interstitial glucose every few minutes, CGM provides a comprehensive, dynamic picture of glycemic control that transcends the snapshot offered by HbA1c or sporadic fingerstick checks. This is especially valuable in advanced CKD for detecting asymptomatic hypoglycemia, post-dialytic glycemic excursions, and assessing true glycemic variability, all of which are poorly captured by traditional methods [40].

Recent evidence supports the accuracy and clinical utility of CGM in dialysis populations. Studies have shown that CGM-derived metrics, such as time in range, time below range, and the glucose CV, offer actionable insights [40, 41]. For example, CGM reveals the marked glycemic fluctuations around hemodialysis sessions and can identify patients with excessive time in hypoglycemia despite seemingly acceptable HbA1c values, leading to treatment adjustments [33, 41]. In type 2 diabetes, lower time in range and higher glucose CV derived from CGM are significantly associated with an increased risk of CKD progression, worsening albuminuria, and mortality [42].

International guidelines are beginning to acknowledge the role of CGM in this population, suggesting the use of the glucose management indicator (an estimated HbA1c from CGM data) [43–45]. However, discrepancies between laboratory HbA1c and glucose management indicator can occur in ESKD, underscoring the need to interpret CGM data as a complementary stream of information rather than a direct replacement [41]. While observational data are encouraging, evidence from randomized controlled trials remains limited [46]. Barriers to widespread adoption include cost, access, and healthcare provider familiarity [40]. Nonetheless, CGM is emerging as an indispensable tool for personalizing and fine-tuning glycemic management in high-risk patients with diabetes and advanced CKD.

Although the limitations of HbA1c become more pronounced in advanced CKD, the potential value of CGM and alternative biomarkers should not be confined to this population. In earlier stages of CKD, where HbA1c remains relatively reliable, CGM can still provide complementary information by detecting glycemic excursions, postprandial hyperglycemia, and asymptomatic hypoglycemia that are not captured by HbA1c alone [47]. This is particularly relevant in individuals with high glycemic variability or discordance between HbA1c and self-monitored glucose values [48]. Therefore, a combined approach integrating HbA1c with CGM-derived metrics or short-term biomarkers may enhance risk stratification and individualized care even in mild CKD.

Clinical management strategies: targeting glycemic variability in patients with diabetes and CKD

Given the consistent association between glycemic variability and adverse outcomes, clinical management in patients with diabetes and CKD should move beyond a sole focus on mean HbA1c and instead prioritize the stability of glycemic control. In this context, the primary goal is not only to lower glucose levels but also to minimize fluctuations and avoid hypoglycemia, particularly in advanced CKD where physiological resilience is reduced.

Rather than emphasizing specific pharmacological regimens, a variability-oriented framework is more appropriate. Therapeutic decisions should consider the propensity of different interventions to induce glycemic excursions, with preference given to strategies that provide stable glucose control and a low risk of hypoglycemia. In patients requiring insulin, careful titration and avoidance of factors leading to erratic absorption remain essential [49].

Importantly, expanding the assessment framework is central to this approach. CGM offers a direct method to quantify glycemic variability and identify clinically relevant patterns, such as dialysis-related fluctuations, postprandial excursions, and unrecognized hypoglycemia [50]. These insights can support individualized treatment adjustments and may be particularly valuable in patients with high variability or discordance between HbA1c and clinical presentation. Even in the absence of CGM, longitudinal assessment of HbA1c variability can aid in risk stratification.

Finally, glycemic variability should be interpreted within a broader context of systemic instability. Variability in blood pressure and other metabolic parameters frequently coexists and may have additive effects on renal and cardiovascular risk. Therefore, an integrated approach that addresses multiple domains of variability may provide the greatest clinical benefit.

Conclusion and future perspectives

The landscape of diabetes management in chronic kidney disease is shifting from a singular focus on achieving a target HbA1c to a more holistic appreciation of glycemic stability. Substantial evidence from observational studies and emerging data from CGM research converge on a central theme: long-term visit-to-visit HbA1c variability is a robust, independent correlate of adverse renal outcomes, cardiovascular events, and mortality in patients with diabetes and CKD (Table 1). This variability likely reflects a confluence of factors, including treatment adherence, therapeutic efficacy, underlying metabolic instability, and susceptibility to complications.

The clinical implications are clear. Risk stratification in diabetic kidney disease should incorporate an assessment of glycemic variability, in addition to traditional markers like albuminuria and eGFR. Therapeutic strategies should prioritize treatment regimens that minimize wide glucose fluctuations and hypoglycemia, particularly in advanced CKD where the margin for error is narrow. The integration of CGM into the management toolkit offers an unprecedented opportunity to visualize and address glycemic instability in real-time, moving towards truly personalized care.

However, important knowledge gaps persist. Much of the evidence is observational, and causality is not fully established. There is a pressing need for randomized controlled trials to determine whether interventions specifically designed to reduce glycemic variability (guided by CGM or sophisticated analytics of serial HbA1c) can improve hard renal and cardiovascular outcomes. The optimal frequency and mode of CGM use in dialysis patients, its cost-effectiveness, and long-term impact on patient-reported outcomes require further investigation. Similarly, the efficacy and safety of automated insulin delivery systems in this complex population are promising but require rigorous evaluation.

Future research should also explore the biological mechanisms linking glucose fluctuations to tissue injury, particularly in the context of uremia. Furthermore, the development and validation of integrated risk scores that combine variability in HbA1c, blood pressure, and other parameters could enhance bedside prognostication. Ultimately, advancing the care of patients with diabetes and CKD will depend on embracing the complexity of glycemic control, leveraging technology for better assessment, and individualizing therapy to achieve not just a lower average glucose, but a safer and more stable glycemic journey.
