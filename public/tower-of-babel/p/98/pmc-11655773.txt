Reporting guidelines for evidence‐based practice projects: A Delphi study and publication checklist
Authors: Madeleine Whalen, Kim Bissett, Judy Ascenzi, Chakra Budhathoki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence‐based practice (EBP) is the cornerstone of sound patient care. Despite the known importance of disseminating EBP, of the 600 reporting guidelines registered with the EQUATOR network, none exist on reporting an EBP project. This lack of guidance leads to publications that lack rigor and discourages more novice writers who may not know where to begin.AimsThe purpose of this study was to establish Reporting Guidelines for EBP projects by achieving consensus among subject matter experts (SMEs) regarding the required components for a high‐quality report.MethodsThis study followed a modified Delphi technique. The first round solicited free‐text responses regarding reporting sections and sub‐sections. The team analyzed these results and incorporated information from the literature to generate items for the second round. In the second and third rounds, participants ranked each section on a Likert scale of 1–4 (not relevant to extremely relevant) and provided free‐text revision suggestions. Items that reached a 70% consensus moved from one round to the next.ResultsSMEs reached consensus on 6 sections and 30 sub‐sections. Sections address the process for determining best practice recommendations as well as implementation into practice. Headers include (1) “Introduction,” (2) “EBP Design,” (3) “Methods to Generate Recommendations,” (4) “Evidence Findings,” (5) “Implementation,” and (6) “Conclusion.” All items are unaffiliated with specific EBP models, hierarchies, or question formats.Linking Evidence to ActionThe Reporting Guidelines for EBP Projects is a checklist of items with associated descriptors that should be addressed in articles reporting an EBP project. The intention is to provide a roadmap of the items required to publish EBP efforts. It is meant to create a set of expectation for journal editors to provide concrete guidance for authors and elevate the quality and quantity of EBP projects in the literature.Keywords: checklist, Delphi, dissemination, evidence‐based practice, nursing research, publication, reporting guidelines

INTRODUCTION

Evidence‐based practice (EBP) projects integrate the best available scientific evidence with the best available experiential evidence to address a clinical, administrative, or educational problem in health care (Dang et al., 2021). This process is essential to ensuring up‐to‐date and safe patient care, yet, there is a significant gap in the integration of best practices into daily clinical care (Khan et al., 2021). This may be due to a variety of factors, including poor dissemination of efforts to establish best practices and strategies to implement them in a real‐world setting (i.e., EBP projects). With the ever‐expanding amount of available information in health care, it is more important than ever to turn that information into actionable knowledge. This helps avoid redundancy, takes full advantage of the efforts and successes of others, and closes the gap between theoretical evidence and clinical practice.

Reporting guidelines are new, well‐established tools to encourage the rigor of the conduct and reporting of inquiry work. Guidelines provide structure to a manuscript to standardize reporting across journals and to ensure that the information is easily understandable to the reader, can be replicated by another researcher, used by a provider to make clinical decisions, and can be included in a systematic review (UK EQUATOR Centre, 2018). Guidelines come in the form of rubrics or checklists that dictate the minimum amount information that needs to be shared for a reader to understand the methods and findings of a study (Logullo et al., 2020). Although there is inconsistent evidence that checklists have improved the quality of reporting, they serve as a starting place and a destination for all authors, including the most novice (Logullo et al., 2020). Within health care, checklists are a well‐established tool to encourage consistency and thoroughness (Romig et al., 2016). Ensuring the availability of a highly accepted cognitive aid in the form of an EBP reporting guideline capitalizes on this sense of familiarity, potentially helping to minimize barriers to dissemination of EBP projects.

There are over 600 reporting guidelines registered within the Enhancing the Quality and Transparency of Health Research (EQUATOR) Network, yet none provide guidance on reporting an EBP project (UK EQUATOR Centre, 2024). However, checklists do exist to guide reporting on certain components of EBP projects. For example, the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis (PRISMA) guides reporting of systematic reviews. The Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) 2.0 provides guidance on reporting a quality improvement project. However, there is no comprehensive reporting guide for all components of an EBP project, including developing a question, assessing the evidence, generating recommendations, implementing changes, and evaluating results. Additionally, among the top 10 nursing journals, as determined by impact factor, only one uses the term “EBP” in their author guidelines. It is also the only publication to provide concrete guidance for publishing EBP projects, in the form of a self‐published checklist (Dean et al., 2021). While this was an important start to standardizing EBP reports, it was written by a small group of experts with an affiliation with a single model, and there was not a formal research process for soliciting independent feedback or generating consensus. This lack of guidance creates barriers to high‐quality reporting of EBP. One important step to remedy this deficit is to create clear reporting protocols.

Purpose

The purpose of this study was to establish Reporting Guidelines for EBP projects by achieving consensus among subject matter experts (SMEs) regarding the required components for a high‐quality report.

METHODS

Design

The researchers used a Delphi technique to conduct the study. The Delphi method is a well‐established research approach to convert individual expert opinion into group consensus through successive rounds of surveying. The first round of a Delphi survey typically consists of asking open‐ended questions to generate information on the phenomenon of interest. This data undergoes content analysis and is transformed into voting or ranking in two subsequent rounds to move toward consensus (McPherson et al., 2018). Reporting of this study was completed using Hasson et al.'s (2000) Research Guidelines for the Delphi Survey Technique.

Identification of subject matter experts

The research team consisted of 3 nurse experts in EBP with over 30 years of cumulative experience and a statistician with experience in the Delphi technique. The team members included master's and doctorally prepared nurses who have served as editors, authors, and educators of EBP models. To conduct purposive sampling, this group identified a list of contacts with expertise in EBP and writing for publication in nursing journals. These names came from personal and professional connections as well as a review of the list of nursing journal editors from the International Academy of Nursing Editors website.

Recruitment and consent

In September 2022, the principal investigator (MW) emailed the list of potential participants to solicit interest in taking part in the study and tracked responses via Microsoft Excel. Participants could respond to all rounds of the Delphi survey, regardless of contribution to previous rounds, unless they requested to withdraw. After the initial recruitment email, researchers compiled and contacted a list of additional SMEs to increase the pool of potential respondents. All data was collected using the online survey software Qualtrics (Provo, UT), which included an implied‐consent preamble. Participants were asked to provide their name and email address in each round to track response rates.

Definition of consensus

Consensus was defined as at least 70% agreement among SMEs (Sumsion, 1998).

Data collection and analysis

Round 1

SMEs who indicated interest in participating in the study received an email with a link to a Qualtrics survey, with a reminder sent 10–14 days later in September and October of 2022. In addition to requesting the respondent's name and email, the survey provided free‐text response boxes with the directions to list essential sections of a high‐quality report of an EBP project and sub‐sections for each.

After data collection, two members of the research team (KB & MW) exported the results to Excel, independently analyzed the data, and each created a proposed outline. They then met to compare proposals and create a draft guideline. They also reviewed other relevant literature, (PRISMA 2020 Guidelines (Page et al., 2021), SQUIRE 2.0 Guidelines (Ogrinc et al., 2015), Whittemore & Knafl's, 2005) sentinel publication on integrative reviews, and the Evidence‐based Practice Process Quality Assessment [EPQA] Guidelines (Lee et al., 2013)) to identify additional items that may have been omitted and required consideration. The researchers used this analysis to generate a preliminary draft for distribution and voting in Round 2.

Round 2

SMEs who indicated interest in participating in the study received an email with a link and invitation to respond to a second Qualtrics survey with a reminder sent 10–14 days later in January 2023. In this survey, in addition to name and email, respondents provided feedback on 6 sections and 29 sub‐sections generated from Round 1. They were asked to rate each sub‐section from 1 to 4 (1 = not relevant, 2 = relevant with major revisions, 3 = relevant with minor revisions, 4 = relevant, no changes). If a respondent scored an item scored as a 2 or 3, they were asked to provide a suggested revision via a free‐text comment box. Each section also included space for the SME to provide any final recommendations or comments.

Likert responses and free‐text answers were exported to SPSS (version 27) and Excel, respectively, to calculate the count and percentage for each level of agreement and analyze free‐text responses. Incomplete responses were omitted. Items with over 70% agreement as “relevant, no change” were advanced into the next round without edits. For items that received less than 70% agreement, two members of the research team (MW & KB) collaboratively analyzed the free‐text revision suggestions and drafted proposed changes, including re‐wording the item, moving it to a different section, absorbing it into another item, or omitting entirely. Headers were also adjusted to better reflect the items included in each. The researchers used this analysis to generate a final draft for distribution and voting in Round 3. The draft included all items to create context, but SMEs were only able to vote on those with less than 70% agreement.

Round 3

Researchers contacted all SMEs who indicated interest in participating in the study via email to respond to the third and final round of the survey in June 2023. Respondents received an email with a link to a Qualtrics survey and several targeted reminder emails. In the final draft of the survey, participants were provided with 6 sections and 15 sub‐sections that still required consensus and asked to rate each sub‐section between 1 and 4 (1 = not relevant, 2 = relevant with major revisions, 3 = relevant with minor revisions, 4 = relevant, no changes). When items were scored as a 2 or 3, the respondent was asked to provide a suggested revision via a free text comment box. The survey also included a free‐text response option at the end to provide any additional feedback.

The research team exported responses to Excel and SPSS (version 27) for analysis of the level of agreement. Incomplete responses were omitted. Items receiving more than 70% pooled agreement as “relevant” or “relevant with minor revisions” were advanced to the final version with any suggested changes.

Finalizing

Researchers KB and MW compiled the final results and created instructions for each section based on SME comments gathered throughout the process and background knowledge of the EBP process. Abstract headers were identified from each of the sections in the final draft. This version was sent to other EBP experts within the organization and authors of EBP projects for feedback on clarity and usability. These comments were used to make minor revisions to the directions.

Reliability and validity considerations

Researchers employed various quantitative and qualitative research measures to increase reliability. This included ensuring transferability by providing a thorough description of results, ensuring auditability by documenting all raw data, and promoting confirmability by independently analyzing survey responses (Hasson et al., 2000; McPherson et al., 2018; Polit & Beck, 2020). Internal validity (credibility) was encouraged through the recruitment of participants who had both expertise and interest in the topic (content validity) and the 3 rounds of successive surveys (concurrent validity) (Goodman, 1987; Hasson et al., 2000).

Ethical review

The study was approved as exempt research by the hospital's Institutional Review Board (IRB00340456). The principal investigator submitted a “change in research” for each Delphi round.

Round 3

Seven of the 20 SMEs completed the third survey (response rate 35%). See Table 2 for the results of the final round of responses. A 70% consensus of “relevant” or “relevant with minor revisions” was reached for all remaining sub‐headers during Round 3. A final guide was generated with 6 headers and 30 sub‐headers, not including a structured abstract.

Of note, one of the headers (3 sub‐headers) from the analysis of the first round was not carried forward to the second‐round survey due to a transcription error. During Round 2 one of the sub‐headers, “evidence table,” re‐appeared through participants' free‐text responses, indicating test–retest reliability between rounds. The other two omitted sub‐headers (“PRISMA diagram” and “Findings of individual studies”) were not identified by participants as missing items in the free‐text response fields and therefore did not appear in the final checklist. They have been included as options in the directions.

DISCUSSION

A three‐round modified Delphi study with participants representing expertise in EBP and writing for publication came to consensus on 6 sections and 30 sub‐sections, not including the abstract, necessary for reporting a high‐quality EBP project. This guide can be used to standardize and formalize the reporting of EBP projects in the literature.

Items that received immediate and unanimous support “Purpose of the EBP project,” “Best‐evidence recommendations,” and “Model/framework for implementation,” are high‐level items that, if provided alone, give the general gestalt of a project. This is similar to the work of Lee et al. (2013) who achieved 100% consensus on the “Rationale for the EBP project,” “Summarize the main findings, including the strength of evidence,” and “Provide a general interpretation of results” in the first round of their Delphi study to generate a guideline on evaluating the methodological quality of an EBP project. In contrast, implementation considerations were not introduced until the second round of their study.

Lack of consensus during the study is likely due to SME's varying perspectives on the relative importance of reporting the process for generating EBP recommendations versus the process for implementing and evaluating them. Additionally, many items reflected polarity (i.e., being ranked as not relevant by at least one participant and relevant with no changes by the others). This may be reflective of the various backgrounds of the study participants and their perspectives on not only about where EBP is, but where it is going. Likewise, people often feel more inclined to provide feedback on a subject for which they have strong views, or feel their opinion is value‐added (Artino Jr et al., 2022; Tourangeau et al., 2000).

Specifically, disagreements regarding the process for searching the literature, screening the results, documenting the findings, and generating recommendations may be related to the non‐standardized approach to identifying the best evidence (Henrikson & Blasi, 2023). In many cases, EBP teams are selecting the review approach with varying degrees of intentionality (e.g., narrative, integrative, rapid, scoping) and on a case‐by‐case basis, all of which have different rigor and reporting expectations. In fact, even educational series published in nursing journals provide varying directions and emphasis on conducting an unbiased, systematic, and reproducible search and screening strategy, often allowing for a high degree of reviewer subjectivity (Facchiano & Hoffman Snyder, 2012; Facchiano & Snyder, 2012; Fineout‐Overholt et al., 2010). At best, this has led to a lack of transparency in the decision‐making process. At worst, this has led to practice‐change recommendations not based on the full picture of available information (Buccheri & Sharifi, 2017; Henrikson & Blasi, 2023; Roush & Tesoro, 2018). Likewise, many point‐of‐care Evidence Summaries targeted at frontline healthcare providers omit reporting their process for generating the recommendations (e.g., JBI and the Wound Health and Management Collaborative), and some Clinical Practice Guidelines produced by government bodies (e.g., The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence) or professional organizations (e.g., the American College of Physicians) publish their methodology as a separate, online addendum. Inclusion of the process for selecting relevant literature in this guideline is intended to generate trust in the recommendations, assure the process was unbiased, and truly reflect the state of the evidence.

Finally, the results of the study reveal the ever‐evolving nature of EBP in health care. Items such as “Evidence hierarchy” and “PICO,” which have long been considered the cornerstone of evidence‐based literature searches and appraisals, received mixed support and were ultimately made optional or omitted entirely. This appears reflective of current trends in the literature to move away from the rigid structure of the PICO which was originally created for specific comparisons with finite outcomes in evidence‐based medicine (Cullen et al., 2023; Richardson et al., 1995; Schiavenato & Chu, 2021; Waldrop & Jennings‐Dunlap, 2024).

This reporting guideline builds on previous work by Lee et al. (2013) to establish the EPQA guidelines, which were meant to evaluate the methodological quality of an EBP project, but not the process for reporting it. Likewise, it takes the work of Dean et al. (2021), who acknowledged the deficit in guidance to authors for reporting EBP projects and created their own dissemination guide, a step further by using a rigorous research process to vet the items and ensure agreement between SMEs. Finally, this study bridges the gap between the reporting requirements outlined in the PRISMA and SQUIRE 2.0 Guidelines to create a single guide that encapsulates an EBP project from beginning to end.

Limitations

This study contains several limitations. The first is the relatively small sample size. Guidelines on conducting a Delphi study suggest 15 to 60 participants, as low response rates threaten the validity of the data (Hasson et al., 2000). Despite this low number, another similar nursing Delphi study demonstrated similar response rates (Lee et al., 2013). Additionally, other reporting guidelines have relied solely on a literature review without consensus building (O'Brien et al., 2014; Tong et al., 2007).

Another limitation was the omission of 3 items from the results of the first round from the data collection survey distributed in the second round. Analysis of free‐text responses brought one of the items forward again and it was included in the final guide. While this discrepancy threatens the completeness of the tool, it also speaks to the reliability of the responses. By recognizing the missing component, respondents were able to reiterate the need for its inclusion. The lack of recognition of the other items may mean they did not demonstrate durability and may have not been promoted to the final round.

Finally, 3 of the 4 members of the research team are nurses. Likewise, most of the SMEs have a nursing background. While the term “evidence‐based practice” is predominately associated with nursing, EBP projects happen throughout health care. The large influence of nurse opinion may make the guideline less applicable to other disciplines.

Implications for future research

This is the first reporting guideline of its kind. While it fills a significant gap in the literature, it is also only a preliminary step to increase the quality and quantity of dissemination of nurse‐led EBP projects. Similar to the monitoring of other established guidelines, additional research should be done to assess the uptake of the guide, as well as the number of published EBP projects that adhere to the guideline (Stevens et al., 2014). Additionally, due to the low number of participants, predominance of nursing influence, as well as the transcription error, the guide should undergo a second study after a period of implementation to provide any necessary updates, similar to the process to move from the SQUIRE 1.0 to 2.0 Guidelines (Ogrinc et al., 2015).

Aims and use

The Reporting Guidelines for EBP Projects is a checklist of items with associated descriptors that should be addressed in articles reporting the conception and execution of an EBP project. The intention is to provide a roadmap of items required to publish an EBP project. It is meant to create a set of expectation for journal editors to provide concrete guidance for authors and elevate the quality and quantity of EBP projects in the literature.
