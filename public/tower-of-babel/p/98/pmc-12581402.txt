New persistent opioid use among ICU survivors after discharge: incidence, predictors, and nationwide cohort analysis
Authors: Tak Kyu Oh, In-Ae Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong-term opioid dependence after critical illness is an emerging concern, yet the incidence and predictors of persistent opioid use among intensive care unit (ICU) survivors remain incompletely characterized. We aimed to estimate the six-month incidence of new persistent opioid use in opioid-naïve ICU survivors and to identify associated risk factors.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, nationwide cohort study using South Korea’s National Health Insurance Service database. Adults admitted to any ICU between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022, were included if they survived to hospital discharge and remained alive for at least six months, with no opioid prescription in the 12 months preceding admission. New persistent opioid use was defined as at least one outpatient opioid prescription within 90 days after discharge and at least one additional prescription between 91 and 180 days. We performed multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors.ResultsAmong 567,260 opioid-naïve ICU survivors, 23,945 (4.2%) developed new persistent opioid use within six months. Across the cohort, 22,643 (4.0%) received less-potent opioids (tramadol, dihydrocodeine) and 1,643 (0.3%) received potent opioids (morphine, fentanyl, oxycodone, hydromorphone, methadone). Independent predictors included older age (odds ratio [OR] 1.01 per year; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.01–1.02; P < 0.001), female sex (OR 1.13; 95% CI, 1.09–1.16; P < 0.001), socioeconomic disadvantage (Medical Aid, OR 1.30; 95% CI, 1.23–1.38; P < 0.001), malignancy (OR 1.05; 95% CI, 1.01–1.09; P = 0.017), metastatic tumor (OR 1.24; 95% CI, 1.15–1.35; P < 0.001), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (OR 1.80; 95% CI, 1.75–1.89; P < 0.001), and continuous renal replacement therapy (OR 1.24; 95% CI, 1.11–1.37; P < 0.001). The strongest predictor was an early opioid prescription within 30 days of discharge (OR 19.7; 95% CI, 19.1–20.3; P < 0.001). Potency-specific analysis showed potent opioid persistence was largely driven by cancer, while less-potent use was shaped more by demographic and socioeconomic factors.ConclusionsApproximately one in 25 ICU survivors developed new persistent opioid use by six months. Early post-discharge opioid prescription was the dominant risk factor. Risk profiles differed by opioid potency, underscoring the need for early tapering strategies, multimodal non-opioid analgesia, and stewardship programs tailored to patient subgroups and opioid type.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05716-w.Keywords: Intensive care units; Opioid-Related disorders; Analgesics, opioid; Cohort studies; Risk factors

Background

Over the past decade, advances in critical care have substantially improved survival after intensive care unit (ICU) admission [1]. However, many ICU survivors experience persistent physical and psychological impairments—including chronic pain, cognitive dysfunction, and reduced quality of life—collectively referred to as post–intensive care syndrome [2]. Opioids remain a mainstay of acute pain management in critically ill patients [3], but concerns have emerged that peri-ICU exposure may predispose some survivors to prolonged opioid use, with potential downstream harms such as dependence, overdose, and impaired recovery [4].

Prior investigations into persistent opioid use have largely focused on surgical populations. For example, a nationwide U.S. study found that 6–10% of opioid‑naïve patients continued opioid therapy beyond 90 days after both minor and major surgical procedures [5]. Similarly, U.S. veterans surviving postoperative ICU admission demonstrated new persistent opioid use, consistent with broader evidence that complex postoperative recovery often entails extended analgesic needs [6]. Importantly, several studies have also examined ICU cohorts directly, showing rates of new persistent opioid use in the range of 2–4% and identifying factors such as mechanical ventilation and baseline comorbidity as relevant predictors [7–9]. Nonetheless, existing ICU-focused literature remains limited in scope, with varied outcome definitions, relatively small-scale samples, and infrequent inclusion of socioeconomic or treatment-related factors.

To address these gaps, we conducted a nationwide population-based study to (1) determine the six-month incidence of new persistent opioid use among opioid-naïve ICU survivors and (2) identify demographic, socioeconomic, clinical, and treatment-related predictors. While most large-scale studies to date have been conducted in North American settings, our use of a comprehensive South Korean cohort provides complementary evidence from an Asian healthcare system, broadening the global perspective on long-term opioid use after critical illness.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective cohort study used comprehensive, nationwide administrative health data and was reported in accordance with the Reporting of Studies Conducted Using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data for Pharmacoepidemiology (RECORD-PE) guidelines [10]. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (approval no. X‑2410‑930‑905) and from the NHIS (NHIS‑2025‑04‑1‑108), which granted data access. Because all personal identifiers were irreversibly anonymized and the dataset was originally compiled for reimbursement purposes, the requirement for informed consent was waived under Korean law, consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki (1975; revised 2008).

Data source

We used our data from the NHIS, South Korea’s universal single‑payer system. This database provides patient‑level longitudinal records encompassing diagnostic information coded by International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes (ICD‑10), procedural details, prescription histories, mortality outcomes, and demographic variables. Although the majority of care is delivered by private institutions, standardized reporting mandates and centralized oversight ensure uniformly high data integrity across the entire healthcare network [11].

Study population

We identified all adults admitted to any ICU in South Korea between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022. Patients with missing age or sex data, and those younger than 18 years, were excluded. To focus on true ICU survivors, we restricted the cohort to patients who survived to hospital discharge and remained alive for at least 180 days (6 months) thereafter. For patients with multiple ICU stays, only the first admission was retained to avoid bias when assessing pre-ICU opioid exposure. The final cohort comprised patients who survived to discharge and remained alive for at least 180 days thereafter.

New persistent opioid use among ICU survivors

We defined new persistent opioid use using a continuation-based approach, in which persistence required opioid prescriptions in two distinct post-discharge windows. Specifically, patients were classified as having persistent opioid use if they filled at least one outpatient opioid prescription between 1 and 90 days after hospital discharge and at least one additional prescription between 91 and 180 days after discharge [12]. Prescriptions were counted within a window if any part of the days-of-supply overlapped that window; when days-of-supply were unavailable, a dispensing date within the window was used. Inpatient opioid administrations were excluded to ensure that only community prescribing was captured.

To account for differences in analgesic strength [13], opioids were grouped into “less potent” (tramadol, dihydrocodeine—analgesic formulations only) and “potent” (fentanyl, morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, methadone). Codeine in all formulations and opioid antitussives (codeine/dihydrocodeine cough preparations) were excluded because these reflect cough treatment rather than analgesia. Patients receiving both potent and less-potent opioids in the late window were classified as potent. This framework captured both the persistence and the intensity of opioid exposure.

Study outcome

The primary outcome was the incidence of new persistent opioid use at six months following hospital discharge among the opioid-naïve survivor cohort, as well as the identification of demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical predictors associated with this outcome.

Covariates

First, demographic factors included patient age and sex, measured at the time of ICU admission. Second, socioeconomic indicators comprised employment status (employed vs. unemployed), residential area (urban—Seoul and other metropolitan regions—vs rural), and household income level categorized into quartiles, with an additional category for Medical Aid beneficiaries exempt from NHIS premiums. Third, baseline health burden and acute severity were captured using two approaches: (1) the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), derived from ICD-10 diagnostic codes recorded at hospital admission; and (2) binary indicators for acute organ dysfunction during the ICU stay—respiratory failure, acute renal failure, acute hepatic failure, shock, sepsis, coagulopathy/disseminated intravascular coagulation, and neurologic coma—each defined by validated ICD-10 algorithms (see Table S1 for details) [14]. We also included pre-ICU functional status assessed using a physician-graded disability scale (grades 1–3: severe disability; grades 4–6: mild-to-moderate; see Table S2). Finally, to address institutional and temporal variability, we incorporated hospital tier (A, B, or C, based on resource availability and service capacity), surgery-associated ICU admission, calendar year (2020, 2021, or 2022), and receipt of invasive therapies during the ICU stay, including mechanical ventilation, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), and cardiopulmonary resuscitation. In addition, we collected information on coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) diagnosis at ICU admission, ICU readmission defined as any repeat ICU admission occurring within six months after discharge from the index hospitalization (not including transfers or readmissions during the same hospitalization), and early opioid prescription (0–30 days post-discharge) as covariates, given their potential to confound the relationship between critical illness and persistent opioid use.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics for the cohort’s demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized as mean (standard deviation) for continuous variables and count (percentage) for categorical variables [15]. To categorize hospitals, we performed agglomerative hierarchical clustering on seven institutional variables—emergency and operating room bed counts, ICU bed capacity, numbers of physicians (total and specialists), nursing staff, and pharmacists—and assigned hospitals to three tiers (A–C), as detailed in Table S3. In the primary analysis, we fitted a multivariable logistic regression model including all predefined covariates to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for factors associated with new persistent opioid use among ICU survivors.

In addition, we performed separate multivariable logistic regression analyses for potent persistent opioid use and less-potent persistent opioid use (each versus non-use) to examine whether associated factors differed according to analgesic strength. As sensitivity analyses, we (1) repeated the regression across all ICU admissions rather than restricting to first admissions, and (2) repeated the models after excluding patients with cancer, patients with COVID-19, and both groups combined, and (3) repeated the models after excluding early opioid prescription (0–30 days post-discharge) from the predictors, given concerns about overlap with the outcome definition, to assess robustness against these potential sources of bias. Model calibration was evaluated with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and variance inflation factors (< 2.0) confirmed absence of multicollinearity. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), and two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

Between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2022, a total of 1,245,251 patients were admitted to an ICU in South Korea. After excluding patients who died within six months of hospital discharge (n = 332,431), those younger than 18 years (n = 108,002), patients with any opioid prescription in the 12 months preceding ICU admission (n = 123,662), and repeat ICU admissions (only the first admission retained; n = 113,896), the final study cohort comprised 567,260 opioid-naïve ICU survivors (Fig. 1). Among these, 23,945 persons developed new persistent opioid use within six months (4.2%). Of them, 22,643 (4.0%) were prescribed only less-potent opioids, while 1,643 (0.3%) received potent opioids. Predictors of new persistent opioid use

In the multivariable logistic regression (Table 2), several factors were strongly associated with new persistent opioid use at six months. Older age and female sex modestly increased risk (OR per year 1.01, 95% CI 1.01–1.02, P < 0.001; OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.09–1.16, P < 0.001). Socioeconomic vulnerability was evident, with Medical Aid beneficiaries having 30% higher odds compared with the lowest income quartile (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.23–1.38, P < 0.001). Clinically, malignancy (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01–1.09, P = 0.017) and metastatic solid tumors (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.15–1.35, P < 0.001) were significant predictors. Among ICU treatments, ECMO support was strongly associated with persistent use (OR 1.80, 95% CI 1.75–1.89, P < 0.001). CRRT also increased odds (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.11–1.37, P < 0.001).

Less potent and potent persistent opioid use

Predictors of persistent less-potent opioid use were largely consistent with those of the overall cohort (Table 3). Older age (OR 1.01, 95% CI 1.02–1.02, P < 0.001), female sex (OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.10–1.17, P < 0.001), low income (Medical Aid, OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.24–1.39, P < 0.001), and rural residence (OR 1.11, 95% CI 1.07–1.13, P < 0.001) were associated with increased risk. Unlike the main analysis, overall malignancy was inversely associated (OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.83–0.91, P < 0.001). ICU-level exposures such as ECMO (OR 1.20, P < 0.001) and CRRT (OR 1.23, P < 0.001) showed weaker but directionally similar associations, and early opioid prescription remained the strongest predictor (OR 19.4, 95% CI 18.9–20.0, P < 0.001).

Sensitivity analyses

Excluding ICU survivors with a diagnosis of COVID-19 at ICU admission (Table S4) or those with cancer (Table S5) yielded results consistent with the main analysis. In addition, repeating the regression across all ICU admissions rather than restricting to first admissions (Table S6) produced findings that closely mirrored the primary analysis. Finally, when early opioid prescription (0–30 days post-discharge) was removed from the predictor set (Table S7), the direction and magnitude of the associations remained largely unchanged, indicating that the observed predictors were robust even without this variable.

Discussion

In this large, nationwide cohort of 567,260 opioid-naïve ICU survivors, 4.2% developed new persistent opioid use by six months after discharge, consistent with prior ICU and postoperative cohort studies reporting rates between 2% and 6% [8, 9, 12, 16]. By applying a continuation-based definition aligned with prior pharmacoepidemiologic work [12], our incidence estimates are directly comparable across populations. While most large-scale studies have been conducted in North America, our findings from South Korea provide complementary evidence from an Asian healthcare system. This broader perspective helps assess generalizability and enriches the global evidence base.

Our study makes two contributions. First, we applied a continuation-based definition aligned with prior pharmacoepidemiologic research [12]. Second, by stratifying persistent use according to opioid potency, we reveal distinct trajectories for cancer versus non-cancer survivors—an approach rarely undertaken in ICU-based opioid research. Together, these features advance the global evidence base and enhance interpretability for clinical and policy audiences.

Our incidence estimate of 4.2% was somewhat higher than prior ICU studies, which generally report 2–4% [5, 8, 9]. Several factors may account for this difference. First, our continuation-based definition required opioid prescriptions in both early (1–90 days) and late (91–180 days) windows, which may capture persistence differently than refill-only definitions used elsewhere. Second, in South Korea, tramadol is widely prescribed as a reimbursed first-line analgesic [17], which likely increased rates of less-potent persistence compared with Western cohorts. Finally, our nationwide sample included a broad spectrum of ICU survivors, both medical and surgical, whereas prior studies often analyzed more narrowly defined or higher-risk populations (e.g., U.S. veterans). Our incidence may also reflect definitional variability, as alternative approaches such as refill-based or cumulative days-of-supply thresholds—often used to distinguish short-term supply from true persistence—tend to yield lower estimates, underscoring the importance of outcome definition.

Older age and female sex were associated with modestly higher odds of persistent opioid use, consistent with postoperative cohorts where advancing age correlates with slower pain recovery and comorbidity burden, and women report higher pain scores and are more frequently prescribed long-term opioids [5, 9]. Socioeconomic disadvantage, specifically Medical Aid enrollment, also independently predicted persistence, reflecting health inequities due to limited access to non-opioid modalities and reduced health literacy.

Among clinical and treatment‑related exposures, active malignancy emerged as a particularly strong predictor of potent persistent opioid use, whereas overall malignancy was not predictive of less‑potent use—suggesting that cancer-related prescribing drives the trajectory toward stronger opioids, whereas non‑cancer survivors follow different patterns. ECMO support and CRRT use were also strongly associated with persistence, reflecting the extreme physiologic stress and the intensive analgesic and sedative requirements required in these modalities. Analgosedation during ECMO is known to be particularly challenging, requiring high and often increasing dosages of potent agents to ensure patient‑ventilator synchrony and prevent circuit complications [18]. Notably, the consistent association of ECMO with persistent opioid use, observed across multiple sensitivity analyses, may further reflect long-term sequelae unique to ECMO survivors. Prior follow-up studies have shown that these patients frequently experience prolonged immobility, neuromuscular weakness, and chronic pain syndromes after discharge, which can increase reliance on opioid therapy [19, 20]. These mechanisms likely explain why ECMO remained a robust predictor of persistent opioid use in our study.

Interestingly, mechanical ventilation and respiratory failure were inversely associated with persistent opioid use, whereas prior literature has suggested that exposure during ventilation increases risk. One possible explanation is that in our cohort, many ventilated patients were surgical ICU admissions who required only short-term postoperative ventilation and experienced rapid opioid tapering thereafter. Moreover, patients with severe complications leading to ICU readmission or respiratory failure may be subject to closer prescribing oversight, non-opioid alternatives, or limitations in outpatient opioid access due to frailty or palliative trajectories. These dynamics may explain the protective associations we observed, contrasting with earlier assumptions.

The strongest predictor was early opioid prescription within 30 days of discharge, which increased the odds of persistence nearly twenty-fold. This finding supports the hypothesis that early post-discharge exposure is the critical transition point to chronic use. It aligns with prior evidence that initial prescribing patterns strongly determine long-term trajectories [5, 9]. Interventions targeting this early window—including tapering protocols, multimodal non-opioid analgesia, and structured patient education—may therefore have the greatest potential impact. Sensitivity analyses excluding cancer and COVID-19 patients, and including all ICU admissions, yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of our findings.

This study has several limitations. First, our reliance on administrative claims data meant that we captured prescription issuance but not actual opioid consumption or adherence, leaving a risk of exposure misclassification. Pain severity, patient-reported outcomes, and prescriber intent could not be assessed. Second, dosage and morphine-milligram equivalents were unavailable, limiting evaluation of dose–response relationships. In addition, we lacked detailed inpatient medication data such as cumulative opioid dose, duration of administration, and weight-adjusted dosing during ICU stay. Because these factors may influence long-term prescribing trajectories, our analysis was limited to outpatient prescriptions after discharge. Third, although we adjusted for a broad set of demographic, socioeconomic, clinical, and institutional factors, residual confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., in-hospital pain scores, prescriber preferences, rehabilitation access) remains possible. Fourth, while our continuation-based definition of persistent opioid use enhances comparability across studies, alternative definitions (e.g., cumulative days-of-supply thresholds) might yield different estimates. Fifth, we excluded inpatient administrations, which may have underestimated total exposure during index hospitalizations. Additionally, by requiring patients to survive at least six months after hospital discharge, our design may have introduced survivorship bias, as persistent opioid use could contribute to excess early mortality. Thus, our findings should be interpreted as applicable to ICU survivors rather than to the entire ICU population. Finally, as an observational cohort design, causality cannot be inferred between identified predictors and persistent opioid use. Future prospective studies incorporating clinical assessments, dose metrics, and patient-reported outcomes will be essential to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusions

In this nationwide cohort of more than half a million opioid-naïve ICU survivors, 4.2% developed new persistent opioid use within six months of discharge. Independent predictors included older age, female sex, socioeconomic disadvantage, malignancy (especially metastatic disease), and high-intensity ICU therapies such as ECMO and CRRT. Early post-discharge prescribing emerged as the strongest predictor. By adopting a standardized continuation-based definition and stratifying outcomes by potency, we refine global incidence estimates and demonstrate that cancer and non-cancer survivors follow distinct trajectories. These findings support potency-tailored stewardship strategies—early tapering, multimodal analgesia, patient education, and prescription monitoring—to balance adequate pain relief with prevention of long-term dependence.
