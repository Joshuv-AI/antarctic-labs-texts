IgG Subclass (IgG1‐4) and IgA Autoantibody Profiles Against Muscle‐Specific Kinase in a Greek Cohort

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsMuscle‐specific kinase myasthenia gravis (MuSK‐MG) is an autoimmune neuromuscular disorder predominantly mediated by IgG4 autoantibodies disrupting MuSK signaling. The contribution of other isotypes remains incompletely defined. We characterized the serological profile of a Greek cohort of MuSK‐MG patients.MethodsBaseline (n = 140) and longitudinal (available n = 99) samples from 140 patients, positive for anti‐MuSK by radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) were analyzed using a live cell‐based assay (L‐CBA) to detect total IgG, IgG1–4 subclasses, IgM, and IgA. Also, disease and healthy controls (n = 102) were included. Selected samples were additionally analyzed by flow cytometry. Clinical data were available for specific patients.ResultsOur analysis revealed 15 distinct immunoglobulin combinations and provided insights into the presence of IgA isotype. IgG4 was the most common subclass in 120/140 of patients, followed by IgG1 in 79/140, IgG3 in 59/140, and IgG2 in 42/140 patients, while IgM positivity was detected in selected patients. Anti‐MuSK IgA(1) immunoreactivity was detected in 54/140 patients, frequently co‐occurring with IgG, and in four patients at baseline with only‐IgA positivity. IgA was detected both near disease onset and at later stages, and persisted over time in rituximab‐treated patients. Controls tested largely negative for IgA, although low‐intensity signals were observed in six samples and were considered non‐specific. L‐CBA detected anti‐MuSK IgA more frequently than flow cytometry.DiscussionOur findings expand the serology of MuSK‐MG beyond IgG subclasses, identifying IgA as an additional component of the anti‐MuSK response. These results enhance further investigation into the clinical significance, pathogenic potential, and treatment‐associated fluctuations of anti‐MuSK IgA antibodies.Keywords: autoantibodies, CBA, IgA isotype, IgG subclasses, MuSK‐MG

Introduction

Muscle‐specific kinase Myasthenia Gravis (MuSK‐MG) is a rare but severe autoimmune neuromuscular disease caused by pathogenic IgG4 autoantibodies targeting MuSK, the key organizer of neuromuscular development and homeostasis [1, 2]. By sterically hindering the MuSK‐agrin/lipoprotein receptor‐related protein (LRP4) interaction [3, 4], these autoantibodies disrupt the downstream signaling that initiates the clustering of acetylcholine receptor (AChR), a critical process for neuromuscular transmission and muscle contraction, leading to fatigable skeletal muscle weakness [5, 6]. Although, MuSK‐MG is classified as an IgG4 autoimmune disease [7], antibodies of the IgG1‐3 subclasses also target MuSK, contributing to MuSK‐MG pathogenesis [3, 8, 9, 10, 11].

IgA is a key component of mucosal immunity, mainly as a dimer and a monomer in the circulatory system [12]. Unlike secretory IgA, in which IgA1 and IgA2 are present at equivalent levels [13], the role of circulating IgA (mainly IgA1) remains unclear [14, 15]. Nevertheless, the involvement of IgA is well‐documented in various autoimmune conditions, including IgA nephropathy [16], IgA vasculitis [17, 18], and rheumatoid arthritis [19]. Emerging evidence indicates that IgA autoantibodies may participate in AChR‐MG [20], although the potential pathogenic relevance has not yet been clearly defined.

Herein, we present data on the prevalence of anti‐MuSK IgG subclass profiles in a Greek MuSK‐positive cohort and provide novel evidence of IgA autoantibodies detected near disease onset and/or at later disease stages.

Methods

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consent

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Attikon University Hospital (protocol code B′ NEUR. EBD 280/17.5.21) and University of Patras (protocol code 6274). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Patients Sera and Data Collection

Patients with anti‐MuSK antibodies were identified at Tzartos NeuroDiagnostics (TND; Athens, Greece) between 2016 and 2025 through review of the diagnostic database generated during routine testing. Eligible patients were those with positive anti‐MuSK serostatus by radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA; RSR Ltd., Cardiff, UK) and available serum samples obtained with informed consent at the time of the diagnostic service. Patients were excluded if sample volume was insufficient. Disease controls were identified from the TND biorepository and included age‐ and sex‐matched individuals with other neuroimmune disorders; healthy controls were recruited from a volunteer cohort without autoimmune or neuromuscular disease. All controls had available serum samples and provided written informed consent. All samples were stored at—20°C. Demographic and available clinical data were extracted from medical records for some patients harboring anti‐MuSK IgA antibodies either at baseline level or longitudinally and for some patients harboring IgA antibodies with IgGs. MG severity was graded using the Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America (MGFA) clinical classification (class I–V) [21].

Subclasses and Isotype Screening by Live Cell‐Based Assay (L‐CBA)

HEK239T cells were transiently transfected [22] with a plasmid encoding full‐length human MuSK (pcDNA3.1‐MuSK‐myc‐His; provided by Prof. David Beeson, Oxford) [23] or pCMV6‐AQP4‐myc‐flag as control vector using jetPRIME kit transfection reagent (Polyplus jetPRIME, France). Briefly, MuSK‐transfected cells were incubated with sera (1:20 dilution in DMEM with 0.5% HEPES and 1% BSA) for 1 h, followed by washing steps, fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and incubations with secondary and tertiary antibodies for 1 h, all at room temperature (RT). Secondary antibodies included: goat anti‐human IgA‐AF‐488, mouse anti‐human IgG‐Fcγ‐AF568 and goat anti‐human IgM‐Fcμ‐A488 (Jackson, #109‐545‐011, #109‐165‐008, #109‐545‐043), mouse anti‐human IgG2‐4 and IgG1‐AF488 (Invitrogen, #05‐3500, #MH1031, #A10651, #A10631), all at 1:500 dilution. Tertiary antibodies included: rabbit anti‐goat IgG‐AF‐488 (Abcam, #ab15014), goat anti‐mouse IgG‐AF‐488 and IgG‐AF‐568 (Invitrogen, #A11001, #A11004), all at 1:750 dilution. Olympus CKX‐41 microscopy observation was performed under blinded conditions by 2 independent observers and images were captured by Infinity Analyze‐6.5 Lumenera software. The fluorescence intensity on cell membranes and the characteristic staining pattern were assessed according to the following semi‐quantitative scale system: – (negative) for the absence of fluorescence and no cell labeling, + (weak) for low intensity on a few cells and ++/+++ (moderate/strong) for high intensity and labeling of 50%–80% of cells per field.

Pre‐Adsorption

For pre‐adsorption studies, we followed an adapted version of the protocol reported by Huda et al. [24]. Briefly, sera from only‐IgA+ cases and double positive IgG4/IgA cases were adsorbed sequentially twice against total 9 × 106 live MuSK‐transfected HEK293T or untransfected HEK293T cells in solution for 45 min at 4 C° with gentle rotation. The adsorbed samples were tested by MuSK‐CBA to confirm adsorption and IgA binding was evaluated by fluorescence microscopy.

IgA Anti‐MuSK Antibody Detection by Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometric analysis of MuSK IgA was conducted accordingly [3], with minor modifications. Briefly, MuSK‐GFP‐transfected HEK293T cells (Origene, #RG2122 53) were incubated with sera (1:40 dilution) for 20 min, fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at 4°C and stained with anti‐IgA‐AF594 (Jackson, #109‐585‐011; 1:750 dilution). Samples were analyzed on a Cytoflex LX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter Brea, California, USA). The mCherry median fluorescence intensity (MFI) was measured and ΔMFI was calculated (MFI of GFP+ cells minus MFI of GFP− cells) to eliminate non‐specific binding. The cut‐off value was defined as the mean ΔMFI of four healthy controls plus four standard deviations.

Statistical Analysis

Data visualization plots and statistical analysis were performed using R statistical software (version 4.5.0). Signal intensity of IgA anti‐MuSK was analyzed using the Cochran‐Armitage trend test, while group positivity rates were compared by χ 2 tests. Concordance between assays was evaluated using Cohen's κ statistic, and paired detection rates were compared with McNemar's test. Any p‐values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Details showing which statistical test was performed in each experiment are reported in figure legends.

Results

Anti‐MuSK Subclass and Isotype CBA Analysis in Baseline Samples

Our cohort included 140 patients with anti‐MuSK antibodies at baseline. Among these, 28 patients had additional follow‐up samples, yielding 99 longitudinal samples and overall, 239 serum samples. Most longitudinal samples were re‐evaluated by RIPA to confirm sample integrity. The control cohort comprised 102 individuals, including 85 disease controls with other neuroimmune diseases and 17 healthy controls. From the analysis of all baseline samples (n = 140), 15 distinct serological profiles were identified (Figure 1). The most predominant subclass was IgG4, observed in 85.7% of all patients, including 18 positive only for IgG4. Moreover, IgG1 was detected in 56.4%, IgG3 in 42.1%, and IgG2 in 30%, all concomitant with IgG4. Anti‐MuSK IgA autoantibodies were present in 38.6%; 16 exhibited both IgA and IgG4, while the remaining patients were positive for IgA concomitant with IgG1‐3. However, 4 samples showed solely IgA positivity. Five patients were only total IgG positive when an anti‐IgG Fc secondary antibody was used (titers 0.6–6.2 nM), and 11 were seronegative for all tested antibodies. Subsequent analysis showed anti‐MuSK IgM positivity in three of the total IgG‐only cases (MuSK1, 5, 6, Table S1) and in 10 of the seronegative patients (titers 0.032–0.38 nM, with one outlier at 2 nM, Table S1), leaving only one patient as “true” CBA negative (2.9 nM) (MuSK2, Table S2).

Lastly, we screened a set of 102 control samples. None of them were positive for IgG anti‐MuSK antibodies. Moreover, anti‐MuSK IgA was absent in 96 controls; however, it was weakly detectable in 2 patients with myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody‐associated disease (MOGAD) and 4 healthy individuals. Notably, these samples were CBA‐weakly positive for anti‐MOG IgA, whereas two of the healthy controls demonstrated weak CBA‐based immunoreactivity against other unrelated antigens (Figure 2). The intensity of anti‐MuSK IgA signal was compared between all patients (n = 239) and control samples. Chi‐square analysis demonstrated a significantly higher overall positivity in patients [χ 2 = 28.21(df = 1), p < 0.0001], while the Cochran‐Armitage trend test showed a significant graded increase in intensity in patients compared with predominantly seronegative controls (Z = −8.09, p < 0.0001).

Longitudinal Isotype Dynamics of Anti‐MuSK Antibodies

To investigate changes in the MuSK autoantibody repertoire over time, we retrospectively analyzed longitudinal samples available only from 28/140 patients, collected annually on average between 2016 and 2024. Across 22 patients with available longitudinal samples, IgG4 exhibited the greatest serological stability, while RIPA exhibited remarkable fluctuations (data not shown). In contrast, IgG1‐3 subclasses demonstrated the highest degree of temporal dynamics among patients, as shown in Figure 3 and variations were also noted within individual levels, as it is represented by specific cases (Figure 3D–F). Furthermore, anti‐MuSK IgA antibodies showed interesting temporal dynamics. IgA either (i) rose early and persisted concomitantly with IgG (Figure 3D,E), (ii) appeared later during the disease course and persisted concomitantly with IgG (Figure 3B), (iii) was detected as an isolated IgA response without concomitant IgG in samples from patients with prior IgG positivity (Figure 3A), or (iv) appeared at intermediate time points and subsequently vanished (Figure 3A,F), inferring evidence of isotype‐specific heterogeneity in longitudinally collected samples.

Clinical Phenotypes of MuSK‐MG Patients Harboring Anti‐MuSK IgA Antibodies

We present clinical data from patients demonstrating anti‐MuSK IgA positivity during the disease course (Table 1). Retrospectively, four patients (P1, P3, P8, and P10, Table S2) demonstrated exclusive anti‐MuSK IgA‐positivity at cross‐sectional sampling, and P3 had additional samples during follow‐up. We included patients with longitudinal samples in whom IgA antibodies were detected either exclusively or in combination with IgG4 (P2, P4, P7, and P9). Also, we obtained clinical data from two MuSK‐MG patients who were IgA and IgG4 double seropositive but were never IgA‐only positive at any sampled time point (P5 and P6). Patients were predominantly female. At presentation, 6/10 patients had mild disease (MGFA Class I/II), while 4/10 had more severe involvement (Class III–V); notably, 9/10 were classified as MGFA subclass “b”, indicating generalized MG with bulbar and/or respiratory predominance. The majority exhibited a peak MGFA class of IVb and were treated with steroids either as monotherapy or in combinations with azathioprine, prednisolone, steroids, or/and rituximab. MGFA post‐intervention status was available for 9 patients, and the immunosuppressive treatment was effective in the majority of them, with only 2 demonstrating an MGFA score of IIb.

Anti‐MuSK IgA Binding Specificity and Co‐Localization With IgG4 on MuSK‐Expressing Cells

The scenario of a potential cross‐reactivity was resolved via adsorption‐based CBA in a few representative samples which confirmed anti‐MuSK IgA binding specificity (Figure S1). Moreover, IgG4/IgA double‐seropositive samples revealed a near‐perfect co‐localization on MuSK‐rich membrane regions (Figure 4C), but not on AQP4‐transfected control cells (Figure 4D), supporting target specificity and implying that IgA binding (Figure 4B) may occur in proximity to IgG4 binding sites (Figure 4A) on native MuSK protein, although this has not been directly demonstrated. Lastly, CBA demonstrated exclusive IgA1 anti‐MuSK immunoreactivity, with absence of IgA2 (data not shown).

Flow Cytometry‐Based Detection of Anti‐MuSK IgA and Methodological Comparison

We selected 61 samples from the total pool (n = 239), ensuring representative coverage of each serological profile, to further confirm the IgA presence by another method. The anti‐MuSK IgA immunoreactivity was also detected by flow cytometry (Figure S2). When cross‐referenced with CBA, substantial concordance was maintained between both assays (n = 14 double‐positive; n = 28 double‐negative) (Figure 5). However, overall agreement between them was moderate (Cohen's κ = 0.392) and McNemar's test revealed a significant difference in detection rates (χ 2 = 13.47), attributed to the higher number of CBA‐only positives (n = 18) and a single flow cytometry positive.

Discussion

We present findings showing anti‐MuSK IgA responses at both early and later disease stages and a potential association with clinical outcomes, expanding the known isotype profile of MuSK‐MG beyond IgG subclasses. The role of IgA in neuroimmunological diseases is an emerging topic, with recent studies involving IgA response in anti‐N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate receptor (NMDAR) encephalitis [25], multiple sclerosis [26] and CNS demyelinating disorders [27].

Our analysis confirmed that IgG4 was the most predominant subclass in our cohort, whereas IgG1–3 subclasses were detected less frequently, in line with previous reports [3, 10, 23]. IgG1 was the second most common subclass; in another study, it was detected in all MuSK‐MG patients [3], while Spagni et al. reported [11] IgG1 as the least frequent subclass. Moreover, IgG3 and IgG2 were least frequently present at baseline. At baseline, anti‐MuSK IgM analysis was focused only on a subset of patients (n = 10) who were RIPA positive but CBA negative for IgG and IgA. Three of these patients with exclusive IgM positivity were also CBA positive for total‐IgG. Anti‐MuSK IgM positivity has been reported in a large multinational study [22] and interestingly, 8% of patients harbored IgM concomitant with total IgG when Fc‐specific detection was utilized. As described by Huda et al., MuSK‐CBA detects IgM when anti‐IgG (H + L) but not anti‐IgG Fcγ secondary antibodies are used [24]. Although IgM antibodies may occur in MG [22, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32], their binding may be nonspecific and their clinical relevance remains uncertain [24, 33]. MuSK‐MG prevalence varies across countries and ethnic groups [34, 35] therefore, these findings may indicate a population‐specific heterogeneity in IgG and IgM distribution.

In our retrospective analysis, anti‐MuSK IgG4 antibodies predominated in baseline and cross‐sectional samples and showed the highest persistence longitudinally, supporting the concept that MuSK‐MG is mainly IgG4‐driven across disease stages [23]. In contrast, other IgG subclasses fluctuated over time, implying the view of less stable immune components of the anti‐MuSK response [7]. The variable dynamics of IgG1–3 over time may reflect subclass‐specific differences in assay sensitivity across phases of MG, when antibody levels fluctuate near detection threshold [36, 37]. Although data relative to therapy and sampling timepoints were unavailable, these patterns aligned with documented immunotherapy‐related B‐cell responses [7, 38, 39, 40]. Future studies will be essential to determine the clinical significance of these isotype/subclass dynamics.

IgA involvement in MG has attracted increasing interest in recent years. Vergoossen et al. [41] reported elevated total serum IgA levels in both MuSK‐ and AChR‐MG compared with controls, suggesting indirect IgA involvement in MG pathogenesis. Subsequently, Khani‐Habibabadi et al. [20] reported anti‐AChR IgA autoantibodies in 10% of MG patients, while Masi et al. [42] detected anti‐MuSK IgA—predominantly IgA1—in 10.3% of a MuSK‐MG cohort. Consistently, we detected a higher prevalence of IgA(1) anti‐MuSK antibodies by L‐CBA. IgA anti‐MuSK immunoreactivity was more frequent and intense in patients compared to controls; occasional low‐level reactivity in four MOGAD patients and two healthy individuals was weak and accompanied by anti‐IgA immunoreactivity to other non‐MuSK‐related antigens, similarly to [42] likely implying non‐specificity. Adsorption experiments confirmed anti‐MuSK IgA specificity, and the IgG4/IgA co‐localization indicated IgA binding in sites proximal to IgG4 binding epitopes on the native MuSK protein. Similarly, Masi et al. [42] proved partial overlapping between patient‐derived monoclonal anti‐MuSK IgA and IgG4 antibodies—inferring evidence of IgA association with disease‐specific autoimmunity.

We observed a marked female predominance among patients with anti‐MuSK IgA, consistent with epidemiologically MuSK‐MG [11, 43]. Clinically, most exhibited predominately facial, bulbar, and often respiratory symptoms, whereas pure ocular involvement was less frequent (8/10), aligning with the typical MuSK‐MG phenotype. Consistent with the recognized clinical course of MuSK‐MG [44], 60% of our cohort initially presented with mild disease (MGFA I/II); however, several patients progressed to more severe classes (IVb and V), requiring rescue therapy. Anti‐MuSK IgA showed heterogeneous longitudinal patterns, including early emergence alongside IgG and persistence over time. In chronic cases, IgA was longitudinally detected as a sole isotype with prior IgG presence, largely of IgG4 subclass, and this was observed in patients receiving rituximab treatment, which has been associated with depletion of CD20+ B‐cell precursors [7, 38, 39, 40] and clinical improvement. These sets of data parallel with Masi et al. [42] highlighting that in cases where IgA was coexisting with other IgGs, MuSK IgA seropositivity was persistent in all samples including longitudinal ones, implying relative resistance of IgA‐producing cells to therapeutic B‐cell depletion. Studies quantifying MuSK‐IgA before and after B‐cell–directed therapy are still lacking.

Flow cytometry has also been utilized for antibody profiling of IgG4‐mediated diseases (e.g., anti‐IgLON5 disease) [45] as well as in MuSK‐MG [3, 11] and AChR‐MG, where in the latter both IgG and IgA isotypes have been analyzed [20]. Herein, IgA anti‐MuSK antibodies were detected also by flow cytometry, indicating its method‐independent presence. While both assays showed near‐perfect negative concordance, CBA exhibited significantly higher positivity, necessitating validation to determine clinical relevance. These discrepancies align with a prior report [11] indicating no correlation between MuSK CBA end‐point titers and MuSK‐IgG levels measured by Fluorescence‐ Activated Cell sorting (FACS), a discrepancy likely driven by protocol variations.

Α few limitations should be acknowledged. The major limitation is the absence of functional studies to demonstrate the IgA isotype pathogenicity in vitro. However, Masi et al. showed that IgA can exert pathogenic effects on MuSK signaling and AChR clustering in a polyclonal way, whereas single IgA clones may exert neutral or protective effects against IgG4‐mediated pathology, suggesting a synergistic polyclonal pathological response [42]. In contrast, a single IgA clone activated MuSK signaling and promoted AChR clustering, neutralizing the harmful effects of IgG4 anti‐MuSK. Secondly, in patients with variable anti‐MuSK IgA patterns, sampling was not consistently available near disease onset, MG crisis or peak MGFA severity, limiting the ability to longitudinally track anti‐MuSK IgA levels in relation to MG progression. A third limitation is the different time points that RIPA, CBA and flow cytometry were performed, requiring additional serum freeze and thaw cycles. This could have caused a gradual degradation of the antibody titer, so both agreements and discordances between assays should be interpreted with caution. A final limitation is the lack of clinical data for the remaining patients, attenuating the ability to interpret how the observed isotype heterogeneity and longitudinal fluctuations could be associated with specific outcomes during disease course or treatment responses.

In conclusion, we provide evidence of a heterogenous autoantibody profile in a large Greek cohort with anti‐MuSK antibodies and demonstrate anti‐MuSK IgA response close to both disease onset and later stages. Our preliminary findings expand the known serological repertoire of MuSK‐MG. Elucidating how different isotypes and IgG subclasses are reshaped over time in MuSK‐MG may improve clinical management and support targeted therapies, whereas IgA responses may be linked to therapeutic response.

Although, full adsorption of IgG4 anti‐MuSK antibodies in the last serum sample was not observed (C), the immunoreactive signal still appeared reduced compared to the untransfected cell–adsorbed samples, therefore, the adsorption experiment was successful, validating further the IgA anti‐MuSK adsorption. White arrows indicate representative MuSK‐expressing cells characterized by positive immunoreactive signal.
