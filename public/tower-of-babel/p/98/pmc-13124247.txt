The First Reported Clinical Outcomes of a Next Generation Five‐Layer Foam Dressing for Exudate Management: A Retrospective, Real World, Cohort Analysis From the United States
Authors: Catherine Milne, Adam Tompkin, Mandy Spitzer, Sarah Megginson, Julie M. Murdoch, Kelly McFee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe management of wound exudate is a critical component of best practice, evidence‐based wound care. This analysis aimed to demonstrate the first clinical and performance outcomes of a newly designed, next generation five‐layer foam dressing for exudate management. A real‐world, retrospective cohort analysis was conducted at two healthcare organizations in the United States (US). Patients with acute or chronic wounds receiving a next generation five‐layer foam dressing were extracted from the organizations' Electronic Medical Record (EMR) systems. Utilizing descriptive statistics, the clinical outcomes and Healthcare Professional (HCP) reported dressing performance outcomes are reported. Twenty‐five patients with 28 acute or chronic wounds were evaluated during routine care, over a median treatment period of 42 days. Exudate levels improved, with high exudate decreasing from 60.7% to 39.3%, and favourable shifts in exudate type over time. Periwound condition improved, with maceration decreasing from 67.9% to 14.3%. Median wound area and volume decreased by 46.3% and 51.3%, respectively, with 17.9% of wounds achieving complete closure. HCP‐reported dressing performance exceeded expectations at 93%. This retrospective analysis demonstrates improvements in exudate levels, periwound status and wound status, alongside favourable HCP‐reported performance of a new next‐generation five‐layer foam dressing, during routine wound management.

Introduction

Wound exudate is a necessary component of wound healing. Production of exudate supports the healing process through maintaining a moist wound environment, delivering nutrients and transporting growth factors. However, insufficient or excessive exudate production can detrimentally affect wound healing outcomes. Excessive exudate production is a common and clinically significant challenge across acute and chronic wounds of all etiologies, and can lead to periwound maceration, skin breakdown and delayed wound epithelialization if not managed appropriately [1]. Furthermore, insufficient exudate production can result in a dry wound bed, reducing healing potential; therefore, a careful balance is required to enable optimized healing potential.

Wound management requires a structured approach to address the cause and impact of exudate production to both the wound and periwound. A systematic evaluation of wound aetiology, size, depth and tissue composition, as well as an evaluation of patient‐related factors such as lifestyle, nutritional status and relevant medical conditions, is critical, as these factors can significantly influence exudate levels. Selection of an appropriate wound dressing is vital, and should be made following clinical assessment of the patient's wound and clinical condition, while also considering the experiences and preferences of the patient. An effective dressing should provide sufficient absorbency to manage any excess exudate, while maintaining a moist wound environment [2]. Maintaining a good moisture balance minimizes patient discomfort before, during and after dressing changes [3].

Protection of the periwound skin is essential to prevent maceration and secondary skin damage. Wound closure can be delayed if adequate protection of the periwound is not provided. Prolonged contact with exudate weakens the skin barrier, making the skin more fragile and thus more likely to break down from friction, adhesives or minor trauma [4]. This can result in wound enlargement and/or cause new satellite areas of breakdown at the edges. Thus, continual monitoring and assessment of the wound and periwound condition are essential to ensure that exudate management remains appropriate as the wound condition evolves.

Exudate level is a key component to selecting the optimal dressing type and change frequency. Uncontrolled exudate can delay healing; thus, dressings should be changed if there is saturation, leakage, odour, increased pain, signs or symptoms of infection, loss of dressing integrity or maceration risk. However, minimizing dressing changes—when clinically appropriate—helps maintain an undisturbed wound healing environment by avoiding mechanical disruption of fragile granulation and epithelial tissue and reduces repeated exposure to external contaminants and temperature drops that can occur each time the wound is uncovered [5, 6]. Additionally, fewer dressing changes reduce the frequency of dressing change related pain and decreases the overall care burden, making wound management more tolerable and sustainable for both the patient and caregivers.

Foam dressings are advanced wound dressings designed to manage exudate while protecting the wound and surrounding periwound and skin. They are most often used on open partial or full‐thickness wounds requiring moisture management and their design and construction are intended to provide a combination of benefits to the wound: a silicone wound contact layer, an absorbent layer to lock away exudate and a semi‐permeable top layer to protect the wound from outside contamination while allowing proportional moisture vapour transmission.

The types of materials and the arrangement of the foam dressing layers can support wounds as they move through the stages of wound healing. For example, foam dressings have been shown to help manage excess moisture [4], promote a moist wound environment which can support the body's natural process of autolytic debridement [7], reduce the likelihood of sensitivity to adhesives [8, 9] and provide protection from repeated trauma to the wound or periwound which promotes undisturbed wound healing [6, 10, 11]. Additionally, prior research has demonstrated improved efficiency [12] when foam dressings are used due to increased dressing wear time [10, 11], reduced dressing change frequency [11, 13] and therefore cost effectiveness [13].

A newly developed, next generation five‐layer foam dressing has combined the design components of foam dressings intended to address the wound management requirements previously outlined. The dressing incorporates an enhanced soft silicone wound contact layer to balance adhesion and gentleness on the skin [14]. The perforated silicone then allows fluid transfer into an absorbent hydrocellular foam core [15]. An additional proprietary absorbent layer supports sustained absorption of exudate and retention of the bacteria contained within the fluid [16]. A breathable top film provides a protective barrier while permitting moisture vapour transmission, supporting maintenance of moisture balance over the wear time [17, 18, 19]. Finally, the outer layer provides discretion by limiting visible strike‐through while providing a newly‐designed visual cue to indicate that a dressing change could be considered [13, 18]. This feature is unique to other foams and may help avoid premature dressing removal, promoting increased efficiency. Decreasing dressing change may also offer clinical benefits, as it is understood that enhancing dressing wear time while maintaining a clinically stable wound and periwound can facilitate undisturbed wound healing [10, 11, 20]. The aim of this retrospective cohort analysis is to demonstrate the performance and clinical outcomes of a newly designed next generation five‐layer foam dressing during initial routine use across multiple wound types and etiologies.

Methods

A retrospective, noncomparative cohort analysis, evaluating the performance of a next generation, five‐layer foam dressing (ALLEVYN COMPLETE CARE Foam Dressing, Smith and Nephew, Hull, UK) was conducted in patients with acute and chronic wounds in postacute care settings including homecare, outpatient clinics and skilled nursing facilities. Two organizations in the United States evaluated this new foam dressing as part of real‐world, standard of care wound management. Treatment occurred over a 4‐month period, from September to December 2025. Nonidentifiable patient level data extraction from the electronic medical record (EMR) systems occurred in January 2026.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they received treatment with the next generation five‐layer foam dressing; ≥ 18 years of age at time of treatment and had an acute or chronic wound documented in the EMR. Patients with a documented allergy to any component of the next generation five‐layer foam dressing or its adhesive were excluded. Patients did not receive any assigned intervention: all treatment was routine care as per standard practice. The next generation five‐layer foam dressing was commercially available for use in the United States in accordance with applicable regulatory requirements, and used within its indications, as per the device Instructions for Use. Both organizations received a supply of the next generation five‐layer foam dressing from the legal manufacturer by way of a limited market release.

Nonidentifiable patient level data was extracted from the EMR systems on conclusion of wound treatment with the next generation five‐layer foam dressing. The nonidentifiable patient level EMR extraction was collated in a Microsoft Excel 365 file to allow for statistical analysis. Wound measurements were collected as part of routine clinical care and were consistently performed by trained healthcare professionals experienced in wound assessment at each participating organization. Length, width and depth were measured using standardized local clinical protocols and documented in the EMR. Wound area and volume were calculated using rectangular (length × width) and cubic (length × width × depth) approximations, respectively. Healthcare professional reported performance outcomes were captured on conclusion of dressing use, with performance rated across predefined domains relative to clinician expectation. Wound photography was also extracted from the EMR, which included the following stages of the use of the next generation five‐layer foam dressing, on application, in situ and at dressing changes. The dressings were changed according to standard organizational practices, or when deemed necessary in the judgement of the healthcare professional. All extractions of EMR nonidentifiable data and photography were conducted in alignment with the Institutional Review Board (IRB) waiver and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA).

Ethical Considerations

A waiver for the requirement of a favourable ethical opinion for the conduct of this real‐world, standard of care, retrospective cohort analysis was granted from the WCG IRB.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the reported data. Categorical and ordinal variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using number of observations, mean, median, standard deviation, minimum and maximum values and interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3). Wound area was calculated using a rectangular approximation (length × width), and wound volume using a cubic approximation (length × width × depth). All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2).

Results

Patient Demographics and Medical History

A total of 25 patients (female; n = 13 [52%]) with a total of 28 wounds were included in this analysis. One patient was treated for four wounds. Patients were included from two organizations in the United States, where treatment was provided in postacute care settings. The mean age of patients was 74.40 years (range, 25–100 years). Clinical Outcomes

The mean and median treatment duration were 42.8 days (SD 21.0) and 42.0 days (Q1 = 34.0; Q3 = 43.0), respectively, with a range of 7–105 days. The mean and median number of visits per wound were 6.8 (SD 2.9) and 6.5 (Q1 = 6.0; Q3 = 7.0), respectively (range 2–16 visits). The mean frequency of dressing changes was 3.13 per week (SD 1.31), with a median of 2.90 per week (Q1 = 2.33; Q3 = 3.50).

Periwound condition improved over time for 18 (64.3%) wounds: healthy periwound increased from 17.9% (n = 5) at treatment commencement to 78.6% (n = 22) at treatment discontinuation, maceration declined from 67.9% (n = 19) to 14.3% (n = 4). Nine (32.1%) remained in current state and one (3.6%) periwound was considered to have deteriorated (from indurated to erythematous). Figure 2 details the periwound status at treatment initiation and discontinuation.

Exudate levels demonstrated a net shift toward lower categories: high exudate decreased from 60.7% (n = 17 wounds) to 39.3% (n = 11 wounds), with 32.1% (n = 9 wounds) improving by ≥ 1 category, 57.1% (n = 16 wounds) unchanged. A proportion of wounds, 10.7% (n = 3 wounds) did report an increase in exudate volume over treatment duration, which can occur due to wound deterioration and result in more advanced wound management therapies being required. Overall, most improvements occurred among wounds that were high exudate at treatment initiation, suggesting that the next generation five‐layer foam dressing suitability managed exudate away from the wound bed while facilitating moist wound healing. Exudate type demonstrated favourable directional change over time. Serous exudate predominated at both treatment commencement and treatment discontinuation, while no wounds exhibited purulent or sanguinous exudate at treatment discontinuation, resulting in resolution over the treatment period (n = 4; 14.3%). At treatment commencement, the median wound area was 3.0 cm2 (Q1 = 1.1 cm2; Q3 = 21.25 cm2), and the median wound volume was 2.17 cm3 (Q1 = 0.74 cm3; Q3 = 12.36 cm3). Over the treatment period, the median absolute reduction in wound area was −0.93 cm2 (Q1 = −7.54 cm2; Q3 = 1.60 cm2), corresponding to a median percentage change reduction of −46.33% (Q1 = −84.67%; Q3 = 41.35%). Median wound volume similarly decreased, with an absolute change of −0.76 cm3 (Q1 = −4.68 cm3; Q3 = 1.24 cm3) and a median percentage change reduction of −51.31% (Q1 = −90.50%; Q3 = 108.27%).

Of the 28 wounds, 13 (46.4%) wounds improved over the duration of treatment, nine (32.1%) showed no change in wound status, and 6 (21.4%) showed deterioration. At treatment discontinuation five wounds (17.9%) had closed, defined as complete epithelization. HCP Reported Outcomes

At conclusion of treatment with the next generation five‐layer foam dressing, HCP‐reported performance outcomes were uniformly positive, with all assessed domains meeting or exceeding expectations in 100% of responses (n = 28). Overall dressing performance exceeded expectations in 93% (n = 26) of the wounds treated. Furthermore, similarly favorable ratings were given for wear time, exudate retention, change indication, ease of removal and ability to stay in place. Discussion

Effective management of wound exudate is central to supporting wound progression and protecting the periwound, particularly in patients with complex comorbidities and chronic wound etiologies. This real‐world retrospective cohort analysis, demonstrating the first reported use of a next generation five‐layer foam dressing, was associated with favorable clinical and HCP‐reported performance outcomes within a heterogeneous patient population with acute and chronic wounds. The findings reported address the predefined analysis aim by providing early clinical evidence on wound progression, exudate management and HCP‐reported dressing performance during routine, standard of care wound management within postacute treatment settings.

The patient population evaluated reflects a clinically complex cohort, characterized by advanced age, a high prevalence of cardiovascular disease, diabetes and other conditions known to impair wound healing, as well as a predominance of pressure injuries and chronic wounds of extended duration. Within this context, the observed directional improvements in wound status are clinically noteworthy. Nearly half of wounds improved over the study period, and a subset achieved complete closure despite relatively short treatment durations with the five‐layer foam dressing. Reductions in wound area and volume further support these findings; however, it is important to acknowledge that chronic wound healing is multifactorial, and wound size reduction alone does not guarantee healing in the absence of appropriate management of underlying etiologies such as ischemia or venous disease. Therefore, these observed changes in wound dimensions should be interpreted within the context of comprehensive wound and patient management.

One of the most consistent and clinically relevant findings was the improvement in periwound condition. A substantial reduction in periwound maceration, accompanied by an increase in wounds presenting with clinically healthy periwound skin, is particularly relevant given the well‐established relationship between uncontrolled exudate, periwound breakdown and delayed epithelial advancement. Although periwound outcomes are infrequently emphasized in clinical studies, these improvements represent an important indicator of effective exudate containment and local skin protection. This is especially pertinent as it is well established in the literature that moist wound healing enables the optimal environment for wound progression [21], where excess exudate is removed through the dressing moisture vapour transmission design ability [19], resulting in a stable state that permits moisture balance within the wound, periwound and surrounding skin health.

When assessing exudate management, it is important to consider both the volume and type, as each wound's lifecycle will produce variations in both. Within this analysis, assessment of exudate volume and type demonstrated a favorable overall shift during the treatment period. While not all wounds demonstrated improvement, the overall directional shift aligns with the intended purpose of advanced five‐layer foam dressings in managing moderate‐to‐high exudate while maintaining a moist wound environment conducive to wound progression and the ultimate goal of full re‐epithelization.

Wound outcomes are influenced by multiple interrelated factors, including wound bed preparation, debridement, compression therapy and offloading strategies, all of which were applied as part of standard care. Consequently, the outcomes demonstrated within this analysis are interpreted as associations observed during real‐world use of the dressing rather than a definitive treatment effect. Nevertheless, the consistency of directional improvements in exudate control, periwound condition and wound status provides early supportive evidence of the dressing's performance within comprehensive wound care pathways. The clinical observations are, however, supported by a plausible mechanistic rationale related to the multilayer design of advanced five‐layer foam dressings. These dressings are designed to facilitate fluid transfer away from the wound surface, absorb and retain exudate within the dressing structure and protect both the wound bed and surrounding skin while permitting moisture vapor transmission.

There is a dual modality of foam dressings; exudate management and pressure injury prevention, however this analysis focused solely on exudate management. When specifically assessing the mode of action of foam dressings, the literature is supportive of a multifactorial approach; maintaining moist environment, fluid absorption, wound homeostasis, peri‐wound skin protection and by extension protection from moisture associated skin damage [4, 21, 22, 23]. In the absence of these performance mechanisms, the status and progression of a wound can become inhibited, resulting in deterioration, stalling or adverse events such as infection or surgical intervention. As an example, it is well understood in clinical wound care practice that if moisture balance of the wound is not well maintained, this directly impacts progression. A wound that is too dry, there is a risk of eschar, tissue dehydration and desiccation formation in the wound bed. However, if a wound is too moist and exudate is not actively removed, the excessive moisture leaks into the peri‐wound skin, causing maceration and reduced cellular turnover leading to delayed granulation and inhibition of epithelization. A recently published cohort analysis, specifically reporting the clinical outcomes of the use of a five‐layer foam dressing, across multiple chronic wound types, demonstrated a statistically significant time to healing and lower dressing change frequency when compared to patients not receiving evidence‐based wound care. This study was observational; however, the cohort of patients treated with a five‐layer foam dressing integrated care bundle exceeded 16 000 patients, therefore exhibiting a positive trend in favour of the aforementioned multiple mechanisms of action enabling demonstration of measurable clinical outcomes [24].

Dressing change frequency in postacute settings is often driven by visit schedules rather than clinical necessity, which can lead to premature dressing removal. In this analysis, dressing change frequency remained clinically acceptable despite prevalent moderate‐to‐high exudate. Visual change indicators may support more objective decision‐making by signalling when a dressing change should be considered, helping reduce unnecessary changes, reinforce undisturbed wound healing and improve efficiency in postacute care settings. As this analysis was of routine, standard of care practice, dressing changes occurred as per the standard protocol within the two organizations. Future interventional clinical studies on the use of the new next generation five‐layer foam dressing can assess the impact of the in‐built dressing change indicator and look to determine how this technology can support nurse‐led decisions on dressing change requirements to aid undisturbed wound healing.

HCP‐reported performance outcomes complemented the clinical findings, with consistently positive ratings across all assessed domains, including exudate retention, wear time, ease of removal, ability to remain in place and overall performance. While subjective in nature and not a substitute for patient‐reported outcomes, these findings provide insight into the usability and integration of the dressing into routine practice. Ease of use and reliability are recognized facilitators of adoption for wound care technologies, particularly in real‐world care settings where efficiency and consistency are critical. It is therefore integral that this newly designed and optimized five‐layer foam dressing measures clinical efficiency but also captures HCP usability, as future evidence generation must report on multiple outcome measures to demonstrate value across wound care stakeholders.

Limitations

This analysis is limited by its retrospective, noncomparative design, lack of statistical powering and restriction to postacute care settings, which limit causal inference and generalizability. The heterogeneity of wound etiologies in this clinically complex patient population with multiple comorbidities reflects real‐world practice; however, this makes interpretation of dressing‐specific effects challenging. In addition, the cohort comprised 25 patients with 28 wounds, and the modest number of observations limits the robustness of statistical conclusions.

Outcomes may also have been influenced by documentation variability and unmeasured confounding factors, including concurrent therapies. Furthermore, the objective of this analysis was to demonstrate the performance of the dressing. The authors acknowledge that for future research it would be beneficial to also include analysis of prior treatments, to enable comparison of changes in outcomes before and after implementation of the next‐generation five‐layer foam dressing, especially as within this cohort the median wound duration was 92 days. Patient‐reported outcomes were not captured and represent an important area for future research.

Although wound measurements were conducted by trained clinicians using standardized clinical procedures, measurement error inherent to rectangular and cubic approximations cannot be excluded. However, the measurement approach reflects common practice in postacute care and is considered appropriate for evaluating directional change within wounds over time rather than precise absolute wound dimensions.

While this analysis is fully reflective of real‐world practices, these limitations highlight the need for future prospective, comparative, and multicentre studies, incorporating cost‐effectiveness analyses and patient‐reported outcome measures, to further substantiate the clinical, patient‐reported, health economic and care setting outcomes of this dressing.

Conclusion

This real‐world, retrospective cohort analysis reports the first clinical evidence for a newly designed next generation five‐layer foam dressing, demonstrating associated improvements in exudate levels, periwound status and wound status, alongside favourable HCP‐reported performance during routine wound management.
