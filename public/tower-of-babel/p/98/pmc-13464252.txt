Silent Migration of Retained Epicardial Pacing Wire to the Left Heart 24 Years After CABG
Authors: Haider Hussain Shah, Muhammad Adnan Zaman, Mohammad Omar Butt, Sidra Kalsoom, Mashaal Shakil Malik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRetained temporary epicardial pacing wires (TEPWs) can migrate after coronary artery bypass grafting; left heart migration is exceedingly rare.Case SummaryAn 81-year-old man presented 24 years after coronary artery bypass grafting. Routine echocardiography revealed a linear echogenic metallic structure traversing the right atrium, atrial septum, left atrium, and mitral valve into the left ventricle, confirmed by transesophageal echocardiography and computed tomography. He was asymptomatic with no infection or thromboembolism, and retrieval was deemed too high risk; thus, he was managed conservatively with anticoagulation (switched to dual antiplatelet therapy given intolerance) and surveillance every 6 months, remaining clinically stable at 1 year.DiscussionLeft-sided TEPW migration poses unique thromboembolic risks. Unlike prior cases, ours lacked infection, followed a novel transatrial trajectory, and represented the longest reported asymptomatic interval (24 years), underscoring conservative management in carefully selected patients.Take-Home MessagesLeft-sided TEPW migration can remain asymptomatic for decades. Conservative management with anticoagulation and serial surveillance is viable in the absence of infection, hemodynamic compromise, or further migration.

Case Presentation

An 81-year-old man presented to an outpatient cardiology clinic to establish care. He had a remote history of ischemic cardiomyopathy leading to coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) in 2002, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, gastroesophageal acid reflux, and hypothyroidism. His medications included aspirin 81 mg, clopidogrel 75 mg, atorvastatin 80 mg, lisinopril 10 mg, esomeprazole 20 mg, and levothyroxine 50 μg. The patient had a clinical frailty score of 2 and reported undergoing moderate-intensity exercise 5 times/wk without limitations. His last follow-up was 12 years ago, at which time a drug-eluting stent was placed in one of his 3 CABG grafts (saphenous vein graft–posterior descending artery).

Investigations

A routine transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 35% to 40%, grade 2 diastolic dysfunction, and mild aortic, mitral, and tricuspid regurgitation. Additionally, a linear, echogenic, nonmobile metallic structure was noted in multiple chambers of the heart, visible in the right atrium, traversing through the atrial septum into the left atrium, and through the mitral valve into the left ventricle. These findings were confirmed by transesophageal echocardiography and computed tomography (Figures 1 and 2, Videos 1 and 2). Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for an intracardiac echogenic linear structure included a permanent pacemaker lead, defibrillator lead, catheter fragment, intracardiac thrombus, or retained temporary epicardial pacing wire (TEPW). There was no history of prior pacemaker or any other device implantation, infective endocarditis, or cardiac reoperations. The clinical history of CABG, the absence of any permanent cardiac device implantation, and the metallic density on echocardiography and computed tomography scan together favored the diagnosis of a retained TEPW.

Management

No specific guidelines exist for the management of retained intracardiac TEPWs, which is understandable given the rarity of such occurrences. Given that the TEPW had been present for over 2 decades without any clinical sequelae, and that the risks of surgical or percutaneous retrieval were deemed to outweigh the potential benefits in an asymptomatic patient, a decision was made against retrieval of the wire. However, as a foreign body residing within the heart—spanning the right atrium, left atrium, and left ventricle—it was felt to increase thromboembolic risk; therefore, a decision was made to initiate therapeutic anticoagulation with apixaban, continue clopidogrel, and discontinue aspirin.

After multidisciplinary discussion, we determined that the risks of retrieving the wire outweighed the benefits. The parameters during follow-up that would necessitate removal of the wire include signs of infective endocarditis, development of clinical heart failure without any other identifiable provoking factor, further migration of the wire on serial echocardiograms to a point where it could cause damage, and development of cerebrovascular accidents.

Discussion

TEPWs provide temporary cardiac pacing support. They are suture-sized wires placed directly on the atrial and/or ventricular epicardium, with the proximal end brought out through the skin by tunneling through the mediastinum, exiting typically at the subxiphoid region or lower anterior chest wall. Their use carries a Class I recommendation for aortic valve surgery and atrial fibrillation surgery, and a Class IIa recommendation for isolated CABG.1 TEPWs are lightly anchored to the epicardium, allowing removal via gentle traction. In cases of resistance, the standard practice is to “withdraw the wire as far as safely possible” and to “cut it as close to the skin as possible,” as excessive force risks cardiac tamponade.

TEPW migration occurs in approximately 74% of patients.2 Of note, recent studies suggest that magnetic resonance imaging can be performed safely in these patients when following manufacturer guidelines.3 Retained TEPWs can migrate and cause a spectrum of complications ranging from local cutaneous abscess and fistula formation to major complications such as distant migration, direct perforation of cardiac musculature or vasculature, and infective endocarditis requiring reoperation, with significant associated morbidity and mortality.

The vast majority of reported TEPW migration cases involve the right side of the heart. Reported complications include diaphragmatic herniation, mediastinal hematoma, migration into the pulmonary artery, severe tricuspid regurgitation, bronchocutaneous fistula, lung consolidation, bowel obstruction, ventricular tachycardia and cardiac arrest, cutaneous nodule, bronchial foreign body, pelvic migration, neck migration, retroaortic abscess, prosthetic valve endocarditis, recurrent sternal infection, right atrial infective endocarditis, and cardiac arrest due to coronary artery compression. The mechanism of migration from the epicardium of the right atrium to the left side of the heart in our case is not entirely clear. Most published left-sided migration cases occurred in the context of infection, which may have contributed to tissue breakdown and direct perforation. Among the previously reported cases, migrated wires found within the left heart were eventually identified within the aorta or carotid arteries. Three features make our case particularly noteworthy. First, there was no clinical or laboratory evidence of infection. Second, the wire's trajectory was distinct from the aortic or carotid perforation pattern. Third, the patient had been asymptomatic throughout the entire 24-year interval between CABG and the incidental discovery of the migration.

Tipton et al10 described a case of a TEPW eroding into an ascending aortic aneurysm, discovered incidentally 13 years after CABG, which was managed conservatively with antithrombotic therapy; the authors suggested that surveillance could be considered for asymptomatic patients without additional operative indications. This precedent enabled us to formulate a tailored management strategy.

Follow-Up

Unfortunately, the patient did not tolerate apixaban, developing black stools after 4 days of therapy, and was therefore returned to his prior regimen of aspirin and clopidogrel. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic without evidence of thromboembolism, endocarditis, or hemodynamic compromise. Continued surveillance with periodic echocardiography every 6 months and ongoing clinical assessment are being maintained. Through shared decision-making, it was decided to continue monitoring the wire's position with no change in management plan, provided it continues to cause no harm.

Conclusions

This case underscores the importance of conservative management and close surveillance in carefully selected asymptomatic patients with a migrated retained TEPW. The principle of “less is more” is an evidence-based approach in health care, although it has not penetrated cardiology practice to the same extent as other medical subspecialties.
