Determinants of HPV vaccine hesitancy in South Asia: a systematic review
Authors: Zoha Imtiaz Malik, Abdul Momin Rizwan Ahmad, Sehar Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine hesitancy is a pronounced issue in low- and middle-income countries, including South Asia. The current systematic review focuses on examining vaccine hesitancy determinants, among South Asian countries. A comprehensive literature search was conducted across 2 data bases; PubMed and Scopus, using appropriate search strings, from the years 2018–2025. Articles were screened on the basis of a predefined inclusion criteria. After assessing for these criteria, a total of 29 articles were eligible for inclusion. The data extraction was carried out by 2 independent reviewers, who assessed the study for relevance, scientific rigor, and potential biases, while a third reviewer resolved discrepancies. Findings reported low HPV vaccine uptake across different South Asian countries. It was associated with lack of awareness regarding cervical cancer, HPV, and HPV vaccine, socio- cultural taboos, and religious beliefs, fear of ill effects, and inadequate healthcare guidance and recommendations. Limited access, high vaccination costs, and lack of integration of HPV vaccination within the national immunization programs, further contributed to decreased vaccine acceptance. A multi-pronged strategy, focusing on promoting community level awareness, health professional HPV vaccine training, culturally relevant health education, and policy support, is required to increase HPV vaccine uptake.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Human papillomavirus, HPV vaccine, South Asia, Knowledge, Vaccine hesitancy

INTRODUCTION

Vaccines are deemed as the most effective preventive tool for several infectious diseases in the modern world. They have emerged as the 1st line of defense against previously fatal diseases, and continue to contribute towards reducing the global morbidity and mortality burden [1]. However, vaccine hesitancy (VH) has greatly impacted the effectiveness and efficacy of vaccines. After a five times increase in measles worldwide in 2019, the World Health Organization (WHO) named vaccine hesitancy as one of the top 10 global health threats. VH has contributed to low coverage rates in European countries, as well as increasing incidence, morbidity, mortality, and costs, associated with preventable diseases, in American and European regions [2]. VH as defined by WHO includes factors related to confidence, complacency, and convenience in regards to vaccination. Vaccine-hesitant individuals may be hesitant or completely refuse certain or all vaccinations [3]. A number of factors, from socio-demographics to environmental and political, influence population groups’ uptake of vaccines. Male gender, lack of education, and belonging from a racial-ethnic minority, are all associated with increased VH. In contrast, knowledge about the importance of vaccines, positive information seeking behaviors, and trust in the health system and healthcare providers (HCPs), all are associated with higher vaccination rates [4]. Evidence has reported higher prevalence of VH among high-income countries, as compared to low-income regions, where vaccines tend to be widely accepted. A scoping review on the global coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccine hesitancy rates reported willingness rates as low as 6% in USA and as high as 97% in Nepal. However, these rates of vaccine willingness were derived from different population surveys, and not a single standardized instrument. Hence, the comparisons should be interpreted as a broader contextual reflection of vaccine hesitancy, while accounting for differences in study population, study design, and measurement approaches [5]. However, VH is not a new phenomenon and has been observed since the inception of vaccines in the 1800s. The high rates of VH are reflected in frequent infectious disease outbreaks in the past, as well as some recent ones, such as the 2019 measles outbreak in the United States, and COVID-19 pandemic globally [6].

Over the years, one vaccine that has gained attention in terms of VH is the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine, which has been included in the national immunization programs worldwide. The HPV vaccine is mainly administered to young girls aged 9–18 years, and sometimes to boys as well to prevent HPV induced cancers [7]. As of 2024, a total of 147 countries have incorporated the HPV vaccine in their national immunization programs, however, the percentage of girls receiving the first and last dose of the HPV vaccine is only 31% and 28%, respectively. Despite the cost being less than US $1, vaccine coverage rates remain alarmingly low worldwide [8]. Low-income countries have reported a steady increase in HPV vaccination rates, with 57 low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) increasing their HPV vaccination rates from 4% in 2019 to 16% in 2023. Till 2023, 38 countries have launched HPV vaccination programs and immunized 27.3 million young girls, resulting in the prevention of over 605,000 cervical cancer deaths [9]. Despite these promising statistics, the HPV vaccine has faced resistance from adolescents’ parents, even in developed countries like the US, where low coverage rates are associated with parents’ hindrance to get their children vaccinated. These hindrances have been evidenced to stem from the belief that vaccines do not work, are not effective against major illnesses like cancer, concern regarding vaccine safety, and mistrust over vaccine information available [10]. In contrast, in Asian and South Asian countries, the social stigma surrounding cervical cancer and HPV vaccination plays a greater role in VH among parents and the overall population. Additionally, Asian countries tend to be more sexually conservative and hence, health issues related to sexual and reproductive health are considered embarrassing and social taboos. This results in greater hesitancy towards their prevention and management as well, such as refusal to discuss or administer HPV vaccination [11]. Additionally, despite the South Asian region contributing to nearly half of the global cervical cancer cases and deaths, only Bhutan, Maldives, and Sri Lanka, have incorporated the vaccine in their national programs. This fact emerges despite the WHO naming HPV vaccination as one of the key components of the ‘triple-intervention strategy’ against cervical cancer in LMICs [12]. In addition to knowledge and social factors, physicians’ recommendation of the HPV vaccine also plays a major role in determining VH among parents. If the health care professionals are able to explain and reassure the parents and general population regarding the benefits of the HPV, the vaccination rates are likely to increase. Receiving information from a trusted healthcare professional can help address misinformation and break through social taboos, thereby lowering VH and improving vaccine compliance [13].

The HPV vaccine plays a vital role in preventing cervical cancer, as it provides protection against the HPV infection, especially HPV-16 and 18. Both these variants make up for 70% of the global cervical cancer cases, thereby highlighting the importance of the HPV vaccine. The vaccine works by producing strong antibodies against the HPV, via virus-like particles (VLPs) stimulation. These particles are made from the HPV L1 protein which is immunogenic and noninfectious, thereby making it safe to administer [14]. Early exposure to these VLPs and the subsequent immune blockade is crucial in preventing cervical cancer development. Additionally, when HPV infection does not occur, the E6 and E7 oncogenic genes are not expressed. These genes are important disruptors of the tumor suppressor pathways, and in their absence cervical cells are unable to transform into precancerous lesions which then leads to cervical cancer [15]. Recent global evidence has seen a marked decline in HPV and cervical cancer incidence in vaccinated population groups, emphasizing the positive association between HPV vaccine and cancer prevention. This data reinforces its inclusion in immunization programs and public health initiatives aimed at reducing HPV associated diseases burden and increasing vaccine uptake [16].

Given the crucial role HPV vaccine plays in prevention of cervical cancer, it is important to outline the factors that contribute to vaccine hesitancy, resulting in a widening gap between global targets and current coverage levels, particularly in South Asian countries, where cervical cancer burden remains high. Vaccine hesitancy has emerged as a complex and context-specific barrier, which influences vaccine uptake rates, even in regions with wide availability and access. A systematic review identifying the key determinants to HPV vaccine hesitancy is therefore, warranted in order to provide valuable insights for policymakers, healthcare professionals, and global health programs working to achieve higher HPV vaccination.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Search strategy

The search was carried out in 2 databases; PubMed and Scopus, with a 7-year time period, including articles published from 2018 to 2025. The studies published between 2018 and 2025 were included as they provided the most recent evidence regarding vaccine hesitancy determinants across South Asia. Additionally, they provide insight into how vaccination perception has changed since the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, this time period provides a reflection of the current immunization policies in South Asian countries. Moreover, the HPV vaccine was included in some South Asian countries’ immunization programs in these years such as Pakistan’s immunization program introduced the HPV vaccine in 2025. All of these factors influence vaccine hesitancy and acceptance rates and therefore, to accurately capture these factors studies from 2018–2025 were included. The keyword string used to search for articles included: ("HPV vaccine" OR "human papillomavirus vaccine" OR "human papilloma virus vaccine" OR "cervical cancer vaccine") AND ("vaccine hesitancy" OR "vaccine refusal" OR "vaccine acceptance" OR "vaccination attitudes" OR "vaccine uptake") AND (determinants OR factors OR barriers OR facilitators OR predictors OR attitudes) AND ("South Asia" OR "South Asian" OR "Indian subcontinent" OR "SAARC" OR "Afghanistan" OR "Bangladesh" OR "Bhutan" OR "India" OR "Maldives" OR "Nepal" OR "Pakistan" OR "Sri Lanka"). Additionally, various health organization reports including WHO and other South Asian region specific immunization reports were also reviewed to determine factors impacting HPV vaccination uptake in these countries. However, the findings summarized in this article were majorly derived from peer-review original research and review articles. The population groups in the included research studies comprised of women of reproductive ages, parents of adolescent girls, mothers, health care providers, undergraduate/college students, medical students, teachers, female health workers, and care givers of adolescent girls.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Studies published in the English language, from 2018–2025 were eligible to be included in the study. Included studies focused on the HPV and its vaccine knowledge, attitudes, practices, uptake, and the determinants of HPV vaccine hesitancy, in South Asia. Both observational, and exploratory study were included, however, interventional studies were excluded. Additionally, studies that did not report primary outcomes, conducted on non-relevant populations, or including hesitancy or determinants data on vaccines other than HPV, were not included.

Data extraction & quality assessment tools used

After identification of eligible studies by 2 independent reviewers, relevant data was extracted, which included study reference, study focus, design and settings, sample size and technique, and primary and secondary outcomes. Important methodological aspects such as various kinds of biases including selection, reporting, attrition, performance, detection, and other biases, were also assessed. For included cross-sectional and observational studies, the Cochrane Collaboration’s “Risk of Bias” framework was employed to evaluate the studies for biases. For meta-analysis and systematic reviews, included studies were assessed for biases arising from study selection, selective reporting, and heterogeneity of studies included. For narrative reviews, biases related to publication, and generalizability of findings. A third reviewer was consulted with in case of disagreements between the 2 primary reviewers.

Determinants of vaccine hesitancy

Despite being an effective strategy to prevent cervical cancer and other HPV related diseases, the HPV vaccine uptake remains poor in majority countries. These sub-optimal vaccination rates are attributed to HPV vaccine hesitancy, shaped by a number of determinants. Noreen et al. [17], found good knowledge levels to be one of the key influencing factors, that was positively associated with vaccine uptake. Kaundal et al. [18], identified vaccination safety, lack of awareness and accessibility, fear of side effects, and cost-effectiveness of the vaccine to be major determinants in the uptake of HPV vaccine hesitancy. Luwen et al. [19], termed lack of cervical cancer and HPV related health services, such as screening, timely diagnosis, and availability of vaccines, as the leading causes of cervical cancer deaths in developing countries. Coupled with lack of awareness regarding the disease and vaccination, results in greater hesitancy among the population in terms of HPV vaccination in countries like Pakistan and Afghanistan. Additionally, Rajkhowa et al. [20], found that HPV vaccine related misconceptions regarding its efficacy and safety, also negatively impact its acceptance. This misinformation can be provided either by healthcare professionals or prevail in social circles as conveyed via peers, parents, and community members. Khan et al. [21], found that parents of different ethnic backgrounds, including South Asian parents were hesitant to get their children vaccinated as it implied that the child was engaged in sexual activity. This highlights the social and religious beliefs that may influence parental attitudes and perceptions regarding the vaccine and act as determinants of VH.

Individual factors contributing to vaccine hesitancy

Vaccine safety

Concerns about vaccine safety are one of the leading factors contributing to VH. Kaundal et al. [18], found vaccination safety concerns to be associated with potential side effects of the vaccine, and to be more prevalent in the female population (p<0.001). Although, their study findings reported 90.2% female participants to have no vaccine side effects, still the prevalence of HPV vaccine related safety concerns was 7.4%. This difference can be attributed to perceived fears of vaccine safety, socio-cultural taboos, and lack of vaccine knowledge, and religious perceptions, all of which may negatively influence vaccine related decisions, even in the absence of harmful side effects. Additionally, the concerns were regarding sexual health issues in 6.7% of the participants [18]. However, a nationwide Bangladeshi study by Hawlader et al. [22], reported that safety concerns regarding the HPV vaccine were not significantly associated with VH. The study found only 0.79% of the respondents to consider vaccine safety concerns as perceived barriers to getting the vaccine. The authors attributed these findings with greater community engagement of local health workers with the general public, particularly with rural population groups. Another study by Chan et al. [23], exploring the barriers and facilitators of the HPV vaccine, in South Asian parents found mothers to be concerned about the vaccine’s safety in terms of sexual and reproductive health side effects. They voiced their questions regarding the effects the HPV vaccine would have on their daughter’s puberty, reproductive functions, and fertility status.

Rajkhowa et al. [24] found that social media users expressed safety concerns regarding the HPV vaccine in reference to the potential adverse events following immunization, mortality, fertility disruption, and lack of ethical vaccination practices. Social media users were also exposed to substantial misinformation such as positive association between the HPV vaccine and increased cancer risk, contributing to safety concerns. Another Indian study conducted by Shah et al. [25], found women’s fear of HPV vaccination associated with cancer, as they believed it increases the likelihood that they may get cancer. Majority of the females also did not want to get tested for HPV due to their fear of getting diagnosed with cervical cancer. However, a study by Shetty et al. [26], asked medical students regarding HPV vaccine safety, with majority of the participants being uncertain about its side effects. Although, 2 female students who got the vaccine expressed that they had no side effects or health issues. Additionally, an Indian infodemic analysis of HPV vaccine related Google trends by Mehra et al. [27], found a decline in vaccine safety related queries in the early 2010s when the HPV vaccination program was suspended, following safety and ethical concerns. This underscores how vaccine safety perceptions influence information seeking behaviors. Another study from Bihar, India by Krishna et al. [39] reported that students cited fear of side effects as a barrier to getting the vaccine. It was also tied to gaps in technical knowledge regarding the vaccine, which may distort the perception of vaccine safety [12].

Vaccine efficacy

Doubts regarding the HPV vaccine efficacy also contribute towards VH, particularly in South Asian countries, where general HPV awareness is limited. Kaundal et al. [18] highlighted how despite the proven efficacy of the vaccine, immunization coverage and acceptance remains low in India. Results of the study indicated insufficient knowledge about the vaccine’s efficacy, and concerns regarding its actual effectiveness as the leading contributors to not getting the HPV vaccine. Aggarwal et al. [28] conducted a study on HPV vaccination related knowledge, attitudes, and practices in Delhi based HCPs and found low uptake of the vaccine, despite high trust in its efficacy. Results reported 89.3% of medical doctors, 78.5% of nurses, and 71.5% paramedical staff claimed they had faith in the vaccine’s efficacy. However, only 11.6% doctors, 8.6% nurses, and 5.5% paramedical staff had actually received the vaccine. These results indicate that even in population groups where trust on vaccine’s efficacy is high, other barriers such as cost, access, and lack of knowledge, may negatively impact HPV vaccination uptake.

A study by Hawlader et al. [22], reported that majority of Bangladeshi parents (54.21%) agreed that the vaccine would be effective in protecting their daughter against cervical cancer. An ever greater proportion (64.71%) stated that they trust the vaccine’s efficacy due to existing research on its proven benefits against HPV infection and cervical cancer. This trust in vaccine efficacy was reported as an individual determinant of HPV vaccine acceptance in the study. A study by Chan et al. [23], exploring the determinants of HPV vaccination decisions reported that mothers expressed the desire to have more information regarding the vaccine’s efficacy in order to make informed vaccination decisions for their daughters. They wished to find out about the exact age range and the types of HPV strains the vaccine targeted. Rajkhowa et al. [24] discussed that vaccine efficacy is a key determinant for vaccine uptake among populations. Socio-cultural factors may impact misconceptions regarding vaccine efficacy and South Asian women have expressed desire to have evidence-based information on vaccine efficacy to improve its acceptance. This is particularly important as HPV and cervical cancer are stigmatized diseases, especially in South Asian countries, hence vaccine efficacy is vital for successful vaccination campaigns in these regions [12].

An exploratory study by Shah et al. [25], reported that female participants questioned the vaccine’s efficacy in various aspects. These included concerns regarding the range of protection it has to offer, and the age group till which it remains effective. Additionally, they believed if their primary HCPs recommended the HPV vaccine, it means it is effective in preventing cervical cancer. This underscores the importance of disseminating evidence-based information though trained healthcare professionals, as a significant factor in increasing public’s trust in HPV vaccine efficacy. Degarege et al. [29] stated that while India included the HPV vaccine in its national immunization program in 2015 after its National Technical Advisory Group assessed the vaccine’s efficacy, public acceptance of the vaccine still remains poor. National health education programs are needed to reduce the prevalent negative attitudes regarding vaccine’s low efficacy among parents and the general population.

Despite the discussed public apprehensions in terms of HPV vaccine efficacy, Bhattacharya et al. [30] reported that efficacy rates may reach 99%–100% for different HPV types. The overall efficacy levels of different HPV type vaccines was 95.8%, with complete protection against cervical intraepithelial neoplasia and genetic lesions. Younger recipients (aged 9 to 15 years) have the same antibody levels as older one (aged 16 to 26 years), with younger females exhibiting an even high antibody count 18 months post-vaccination. Efficacy related concerns and lack of knowledge are dominant even in the younger age groups, as evidenced by Swati et al. [31]’s study on college students. The results demonstrated that only about half (49%) of the participants knew of the vaccine’s high efficacy against cervical cancer and HPV infections. Additionally, 28.8% students reported that they do not believe HPV vaccine can prevent cervical cancer. These vaccine related perceptions then further negatively impact its uptake, even in younger population groups which may have greater benefits of receiving the vaccine.

Religious beliefs

Religious beliefs and practices are significant drivers in vaccine related decisions, particularly for stigmatized diseases such as HPV and cervical cancer. Khan et al. [32] conducted a study in Pakistan and found 2.6% of university students stating that they would not get vaccinated as their religion did not allow it. Luwen et al. [19], in their study on the understanding of HPV and its vaccine among Pakistani and Afghani women, included religion as a demographic variable. However, they failed to find any significant associations between religion and HPV and cervical cancer related knowledge, and willingness to get the HPV vaccine. It is to be noted that 95.3% of the participants were Muslim, which reflects the homogeneity of the sample and therefore, variations in HPV and vaccine related knowledge attributable to religion may not be adequately captured.

Another study by Atif et al. [33] reported that 15.9% of college students stated that they obtained their HPV related information from religious leaders and teachings. These sources are considered reliable for information on issues pertaining to sexual and reproductive health, and also underscore the impact religious leaders and their teachings may have on vaccine related decisions. This calls for engagement of these religious figures in awareness campaigns regarding HPV and cervical cancer. Njogu et al. [34] reported that religious leaders were a key stakeholder in a 2 dose HPV vaccine program targeted to 9–14 years old girls in Sikkim, India. Prior to their engagement with vaccine beneficiaries, the religious leaders also underwent trainings for effective public communication regarding the HPV vaccine. These religious leaders play an important role in shaping community beliefs, and building trust in the health system, and hence, must work in collaboration with HCPs.

An exploratory study by Chan et al. [23], found religious beliefs to be a major determinant of mothers’ decisions to get their daughter vaccinated. Muslim South Asian mothers stated that as their religion restricted pre-marital sexual activity, they did not feel the need to get their daughter vaccinated, because she would not be at risk of getting HPV infection. Therefore, religious teachings impacted the mothers’ perception of HPV risk in their daughters, which stresses that healthcare professionals must be trained to offer religiously relevant advice to the community. Another exploratory study by Shetty et al. [26] found religion to be a barrier towards cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccination. A nursing student, a part of the study, stated that women refuse to get pap smears due to religious reasons. The authors also reported that women’s reproductive health decisions are often subjected to religious and cultural norms, and therefore, religion acts as a barrier to getting the HPV vaccine. Degarege et al. [29] found religion to be significantly associated with parental norms related to getting their daughters vaccinated against HPV. The study also reported that the impact of religious beliefs on HPV vaccination depends on the area of residence (urban or rural). Those living in cities reportedly had higher awareness and healthcare accessibility and hence, their religion may have weaker impact on vaccination decisions. Rural residents are more likely to report taboos, and religious beliefs as reasons to not get the vaccine.

A scoping review by Kisa et al. [35], explored the impact that religion in Islamic countries, including three South Asian countries, namely Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan, had on vaccination beliefs and practices. The study found that despite no direct religious restriction for vaccines, religious reasons were commonly cited reasons for vaccination refusal. The authors also found that religion had a negative impact on HPV vaccination efforts, with parents’ religious perceptions contributing to their children’s vaccination status. However, the review also reported that majority of the general public were in favor of vaccines, particularly those with higher education such as teachers, thereby questioning the assumption that religion stands for anti-vaccination. Another study from Swati et al. [31] found a significant relationship between religion and HPV vaccine knowledge, with Muslims reporting higher awareness of the vaccine as compared to Hindus; which contradicts Kisa et al. [35]’s study which reported that Islam or other regions may act as barriers towards vaccine uptake.

Unawareness of cervical cancer, HPV, and HPV vaccine

Lack of knowledge about the HPV vaccine and cervical cancer are the leading causes of vaccine refusal. Kaundal et al. [18] reported that majority of their study participants gave unawareness regarding the vaccine as their reason for HPV vaccine refusal. Lack of vaccine awareness was also significantly associated with vaccination status of young girls, whereas awareness about the vaccine positively associated with vaccine uptake. Sultana et al. [36] focused on assessing the HPV vaccine awareness in parents and school teachers, as they are the key decision makers and influencers of their child’s vaccination. Risk awareness shapes the parents’ perception regarding vaccine acceptance, as cervical cancer knowledge is positively associated with preventive measures such as vaccination. Khan et al. [32] found no significant association between awareness of cervical cancer and vaccination, however, 80% of the participants stated that they would get vaccinated if the government gave them proper awareness. Additionally, HPV and cervical cancer awareness was positively associated with age, and field of study, as medical students were more aware. Furthermore, respondents also added that awareness regarding cervical cancer prevalence and mortality rates would be helpful in increasing vaccination uptake.

Another study by Luwen et al. [19] found poor knowledge and awareness levels among Pakistani and Afghani females, with less than 25% prevalence of cervical cancer, HPV and HPV vaccines awareness. Pakistani females particularly, were unaware of even the basic cervical cancer related facts, despite it being a leading cause of female mortality. It should also be mentioned that majority of these females expressed desire to know more about the vaccine. Similarly, Atif et al. [33]’s study from Pakistan, found 55.9% of the participants to have poor HPV vaccination knowledge scores, with only 16.4% aware of its availability in the country. The lack of awareness was reflected through very poor uptake of HPV vaccination among these students as a mere 2.3% had been vaccinated. Hawlader et al. [22] found that while cervical cancer awareness was high (80.15%), HPV related knowledge was lacking in the study sample (22.55%). They also reported that majority (50.26%) of the participants expressed that getting the HPV vaccine would also increase their awareness of other sexually transmitted diseases. Another exploratory study by Chan et al. [23] found that South Asian mothers’ knowledge of HPV significantly impacted their decisions to get their daughters vaccinated. A Pakistani mother stated that she was unaware that the HPV vaccine was available in the country. For some mothers it was their first time hearing about the HPV vaccine and cervical cancer. Rajkhowa et al. [12] also reported that because of significant parental involvement in vaccination decisions, awareness can help improve vaccination acceptance and uptake, particularly in South Asian countries, where lack of HPV vaccination knowledge is prevalent.

An example would be from Khan et al. [21]’s study which found that parents believed that the HPV vaccine was only for girls and there was no need for such a vaccine for individuals who were in a monogamous relationship. They also admitted that their resistance towards the vaccine stems from their lack of understanding and knowledge. The parents further proposed a youth based educational platform for awareness raising regarding cervical cancer and HPV vaccination. As reported by Kataria et al. [37], lack of awareness also impacts physicians’ prescription of the vaccine, while most are adequately aware of the vaccine’s benefits, some are still hesitant to recommend it due to uncertainties regarding its need, potential side effects, and age of administration. Building upon the aforementioned evidence, it is vital to incorporate HPV vaccination awareness across various sectors to increase knowledge of both the disease and preventive effects of HPV vaccination.

Community factors contributing to vaccine hesitancy

Socio-economic factors

Cost-effectiveness is one of the major contributing factors towards vaccine hesitancy, as unaffordability deters even willing individuals away from getting vaccinated. Kaundal et al. [18] reported that around 14.4% of their study participants were unable to get vaccinated due to high vaccination costs. Domestic production of vaccines and providing them at subsidized rates via government run immunization programs can help cut costs and promote wider acceptance. Free provision at government hospitals may increase vaccination rates at an even higher rate. Similarly, another study by Memon et al. [38], reported that a high percentage of physicians (70.9%) did not recommend the HPV vaccine due to high costs. This underscores how the cost of the vaccine is a major determinant of HPV vaccine hesitancy even in population groups that are well aware of its benefits. However, conflicting results were reported by Atif et al. [33], where 32.78% parents stated that they would vaccinate their daughters despite the high costs. This could be attributed to the positive impact of community health workers on increasing HPV vaccine uptake even in economically challenged population groups. A study by Chan et al. [23] found South Asian mothers unable to vaccinate their daughters due to high costs, and expressed that free or subsidized vaccines can help them get their daughters immunized against HPV. The study also mentioned cost as a high perceived barrier to HPV vaccination, which led to mothers stating that they would only get their daughters vaccinated if necessary. Furthermore, offering low cost or free vaccines immensely increased vaccine uptake in resource poor settings.

Rajkhowa et al. [24], in their geospatial mapping study in India, found public concerns on various social media platforms regarding the cost of HPV vaccines. A minority further voiced their desire to have a domestically produced low-cost vaccine that higher coverage and affordability. Social media posts also inquired the price of the available Gardasil vaccine, which reflects public interest in getting the HPV vaccination but cost acting as a barrier. Another study by Rajkhowa et al. [20] also reported high costs as a significant barrier towards HPV vaccination, and multiple doses of the vaccine further impose additional financial burden on socio-economically disadvantaged groups. Swati et al. [31] also reported that 22.7% of college students reported cost-related concerns as a reason for not getting vaccinated. Similarly, Krishna et al. [39] reported vaccination cost as a barrier in 5% medical and 9.48% non-medical students, as they expressed concerns over covering the price. Contrary to these findings, Mandal et al. [40] found that 75% of the nurses and female doctors denied that the HPV vaccine is costly, with 63% stating that they would take the vaccine if the cost was further reduced.

High HPV vaccination cost also acts as a barrier towards vaccine prescription among physicians, as reported by Kataria et al. [37]. The physicians in the study reported that they often did not recommend the vaccine to patients belonging to low income class as they would not afford it. Additionally, a lot of local pharmacies also did not have the vaccine due to high cost of purchase, resulting in limited availability. Shetty et al. [26], in their HPV vaccination study in medical students found similar results, with male students significantly reporting cost as barrier to vaccine uptake. They further added that despite their willingness to get the vaccine, they depend on their parents to make the decision as they will be the ones paying for it. The estimated cost of the vaccine as reported by the study, was 3,000 Indian rupees, which remains unaffordable for a large segment of the population.

As cost remains a prominent barrier towards HPV vaccination in LMICs, including South Asia, the WHO recommended a 2-dose strategy to reduce production and logistical costs. Dorji et al. [41] further reports that lack of sustainable funding channels further add to costing challenges, hindering vaccine production, delivery, and associated programmatic services. Bhattacharya et al. [30] argued that while the HPV vaccine costs $363 for each disability-adjusted life year (DALY) prevented, which is far less than the cost of HPV treatment for an unvaccinated individual, it is still unaffordable in LMICs such as India. Therefore, the integration of HPV vaccine in the national immunization program or domestic production of the vaccine, which would cost 200–300 Indian rupees, are the most viable strategies to increase its uptake. Furthermore, Agha et al. [42] reported that HPV vaccination was cost-effective in Bangladesh with only US $6.02 vaccination costs per vaccinated girl and US $39.57 per DALY averted. However, vaccination program and delivery costs remain a major challenge for up-scaling of vaccination interventions in resource poor setting.

Myths, taboos, and cultural constraints

Misinformation in the form of myths and cultural taboos may distort the public’s opinion regarding HPV vaccination and act as major barrier, especially in South Asian countries. Sultana et al. [36] reported that parents’ vaccination myths must be adequately addressed to promote positive vaccine related decisions for their children. These taboos and myths are mostly associated with sexual and reproductive health, thereby increasing vaccine hesitancy. Additionally, Atif et al. [33] stated that the culture of prohibition of pre-marital sexual relations contributes to the perception that HPV vaccination is unnecessary for unmarried girls. Njogu et al. [34] reported that a significant proportion India’s cervical cancer burden can be attributed to the social and cultural discrimination women face, particularly in terms of their reproductive and sexual health. The stigma surrounding these topics prevents them from seeking diagnosis, preventive, and treatment services, such as getting the HPV vaccine. Culturally appropriate awareness tools and communication is vital to overcome the social stigma associated with cervical cancer and HPV.

South Asian countries face a higher number of social and cultural barriers in terms of HPV vaccination, as compared to central Asia. As found in Chan et al. [23]’s exploratory study, Indian and Pakistani mothers believed their daughters would not engage in sexual activity prior to marriage, and therefore, do not need to get vaccinated against HPV. Furthermore, many South Asian mothers deemed it inappropriate to discuss HPV and cervical cancer with their daughters, as they were considered taboo topics in their culture. These findings further cement the need to incorporate cultural sensitivity in HPV vaccination and awareness campaigns.

Similarly, Rajkhowa et al. [12] stated that sociocultural beliefs and misconceptions may even hinder the healthcare professionals from recommending the HPV vaccine. Additionally, as fathers take upon the decision maker role in South Asian households, familial restrictions and perceived sexual promiscuity may hinder HPV vaccine uptake. Cultural factors also significantly influence the public’s perception and acceptance of health technology, such as newly developed vaccines. Ali et al. [43] added that socio-cultural factors acts as a barrier for healthcare workers in recommending the HPV vaccine. Doctors are reluctant due to the stigma surrounding vaccines and pre-marital sexual intimacy. Khan et al. [21] reported that while cultural beliefs were not significantly associated with HPV vaccine hesitancy in their study, parents felt uncomfortable about discussion on sexually transmitted diseases with school children. They stated that because school children were not sexually active they did not need to be exposed to such information about HPV and cervical cancer. Ratnasamy and Chagpar [44] also included cultural factors as a barrier towards utilization of reproductive and sexual health services among South Asian women. In countries like India, sex and HPV are considered taboo topics, as discussed by Shah et al. [25], who reported that women considered being unaware of such diseases a blessing. The participants further expressed that even close family members do not talk about such topics with each other, and do not discuss diagnosis and treatments. Social stigma and shame associated with these diseases, further make it difficult for them to openly talk about their concerns.

Similarly, another exploratory study by Shetty et al. [26] also found cultural issues to act as a barrier to getting the HPV vaccine, even in medical college students. Female students, particularly, worried about people, even close family members, perceiving them to be sexually active just for getting vaccinated. Additionally, parents worried that getting their daughters vaccinated at a young age would translate to them promoting sexual activity and promiscuity, resulting in their social defamation. A review study also found that cultural myths and beliefs played a significant role in preventing HPV vaccination, as claimed by 71% study participants. Additionally, males were more likely to be in favor of getting the vaccine, which can be attributed to the gender based social stigma and embarrassment females face in terms of sexual and reproductive health issues [35].

Vaccine stigma

HPV, cervical cancer, and HPV vaccine stigma are prevalent globally and pose practical barriers in increasing vaccine uptake. Rajkhowa et al. [24] reported that this stigmatization was also observed for other diseases such as monkey pox, and more so in South Asian countries. In India, the stigma is associated with women’s sexual health. Community awareness plans that reduce vaccination stigma is necessary to mitigate such concepts and promote acceptability of vaccine initiatives. It is also recommended to implement a community action plan that is similar to the one undertaken to improve public sensitivity towards other stigmatized diseases, such as HIV or monkey pox [12]. Khan et al. [21] added that because South Asian parents lacked appropriate understanding of the HPV disease and vaccine, it increased the stigma regarding sexually transmitted diseases. This resulted in them refusing to learn facts about the disease, leading to vaccine hesitancy. Community wide prevalence of myths and taboos resulting from lack of awareness further fuel HPV vaccine stigmatization. Similar to these findings, Ratnasamy et al. [44] also reported that people of Indian ancestry living in the US were more likely to be better educated on HPV and cervical cancer, and faced much less stigma in seeking preventive or treatment services for these diseases.

Shah et al. [25], in their study reported that majority of the South Asian women who are hesitant to receive the vaccine, state fear of judgment and vaccine stigma as the cause. They believe getting the vaccine would translate to them being sexually active or having the HIV virus. Interestingly, the women aged 27 to 45 years reflected greater stigma around the HPV vaccine as compared to the younger age group (18 to 26 years), underscoring the shift in HPV related stigma in recent years. Ali et al. [43] further added that vaccine associated stigma impacted HCPs, with many refusing to recommend the HPV vaccine due to its reputation in society. The issue is further exacerbated by their lack of training in terms of effective communication with patients regarding HPV, cervical cancer and its vaccination.

FUTURE RECOMMENDATIONS

HPV vaccine hesitancy remains a significant barrier in terms of cervical cancer, and other HPV infections prevention, particularly in the South Asian countries. Despite the availability of vaccines in these regions, their acceptance and uptake remains poor. This can be attributed to sociocultural beliefs, financial disparities, and systemic challenges. To overcome these impediments, it is necessary that individual, community, and policy level strategies be implemented.

Strengthening community level knowledge and awareness regarding cervical cancer, HPV, and its vaccination, should be a key priority. South Asian countries report a lack of adequate knowledge and awareness regarding these diseases and their preventive measures. Additionally, vaccination benefits need to be communicated in an efficient and effective manner that promotes vaccine acceptance. Vaccination campaigns should be tailored to the community’s cultural, religious, and educational backgrounds. Furthermore, incorporation of vaccine knowledge within school and adolescent education programs, can increase awareness among young individuals and parents alike. Involving community leaders, both political and religious, can help build trust among the community members with regards to the vaccine’s credibility and cultural appropriateness.

Health care providers play a crucial role in individuals’ health related decisions, and hence their attitudes towards the HPV vaccine must be enhanced. South Asian health professionals often hesitate to recommend the vaccine due to lack of awareness or perceived notions about it. There is a need for comprehensive training to educate them with accurate and evidence-based knowledge. Evidence suggests that healthcare professionals HPV vaccine endorsement has the most significant impact on vaccine acceptance. Therefore, empowering and educating these professionals is crucial in addressing HPV vaccine hesitancy.

Moreover, addressing cultural, social, and gender issues pertaining to HPV vaccination is crucial, in South Asian countries, where these factors greatly influence health related decisions. Lack of awareness regarding sexual and reproductive health issues stem from their cultural stigmatization. Therefore, culturally appropriate messages need to be developed and disseminated in a way that promotes the HPV vaccine as a preventive measure, rather than something related to sexual activity. This may help increase parental acceptance of the vaccine. Furthermore, involving mothers, female community teachers and female teachers in the HPV vaccination campaigns, can further increase acceptance and normalize discussion around these topics.

Social media and digital platforms must be engaged to counter misinformation present online regarding the disease and HPV vaccination, and should be employed to promote vaccine confidence among the population. Social media-based literacy interventions, involving online celebrities can increase people’s trust and their interest in the vaccine. Online information tracking systems can be used to monitor real-time information available on different digital platforms, and then to rapidly mitigate the impact of any misinformation present.

Finally, research regarding HPV and its vaccine must be enhanced to understand the determinants of the disease and vaccine hesitancy, and implement effective interventions to overcome barriers. Inter-regional collaboration among within South Asia can help develop more effective and wide-spread strategies to facilitate shared learning and political commitment to overcome the regional challenge of poor vaccine uptake. Through shared political will, community engagement, and inter-disciplinary collaboration, South Asian countries can increase HPV vaccine coverage and eliminate cervical cancer.

DISCUSSION

The current review validates the strikingly low HPV vaccine uptake in South Asian countries, despite the growing awareness and moderate favorable attitudes in the populations. These findings highlight the gap between knowledge and actual HPV vaccine practices, which can be attributed to misinformation, cultural beliefs, and healthcare system shortfalls. A comparative analysis of South Asian and global vaccine acceptance and hesitancy analysis show certain mutual, as well as context-specific barriers. Tobaiqy and MacLure [45] strategized 5 main approaches to enhance HPV vaccine uptake in their review. They specified different levels of interventions including, school level awareness with special emphasis on parental engagement, employing digital and multimedia to promote awareness, strengthening the role of health care professionals in vaccine recommendation, incorporating multi-sectoral approaches, with special attention to vulnerable populations, including young girls. The study also highlighted the role of barriers such as socio-cultural issues, cost-effectiveness, supply chain issues, and prevalent misinformation, in HPV hesitancy [45]. The HPV related findings from South Asian countries also report similar drivers and barriers in terms of vaccination, with cultural, religious, and sexual taboos playing an amplified role in limiting HPV vaccine uptake.

Contrasting findings are reported by central and middle-eastern Asian studies, such as a Chinese study on young female university students found vaccine hesitancy to be significantly associated with perceived safety concerns, and low likelihood of getting the disease. However, upon receiving health provider recommendations, they reported high level of willingness to get vaccinated [46]. Conversely, while South Asian studies also report the same barriers towards HPV vaccination, most of the hesitancy is driven by lack of appropriate knowledge regarding cervical cancer and HPV. It is further aggravated by cultural and social beliefs that may contradict medical information regarding the vaccine, making educational interventions less effective in the South Asian region as compared to other Asian countries. Similar socio-cultural barriers are also prevalent in Sub-Saharan Africa, with studies reporting additional barriers including, lack of healthcare workforce, logistical issues, social stigma, and misinformation. These combined barriers further aggravate low uptake of HPV vaccination in this region [47].

Latin America studies also report increased association of low HPV vaccine uptake and acceptability, with cultural barriers, knowledge deficit, and safety concerns. However, strategies including single dose HPV vaccine, school and educational institute-based vaccination, and health professional trainings in recommending the HPV vaccine, have been successful in various Latin American countries [48]. Findings from these studies indicate the ubiquitous nature of HPV vaccination barriers, while stressing upon contextually and culturally relevant interventions to promote vaccine acceptability and uptake. South Asian countries can adopt and tailor the above mentioned recommendations that have proven effective in tackling vaccine hesitancy in other parts of the world.

Widespread misinformation on various digital and social media platforms remains a global issue, in terms of various health topics, including cervical cancer and HPV vaccination. Recent evidence has highlighted how social media contributed to the false narrative regarding infertility linked with HPV vaccination. This limited the effectiveness of educational campaigns and instead promoted conspiracy theories, leading to low HPV vaccine uptake in these regions [49]. Additionally, gaps in cervical cancer and HPV research are also evident in South Asian countries, with a lack of longitudinal cohort studies, and an emphasis on cross-sectional findings. This limits the inferences and generalizability of results, leading to an inadequate understanding of the determinants of vaccine hesitancy [17]. Global HPV research data from other LMICs emphasizes the need to assess the effectiveness of introducing interventions that target vaccine coverage, distribution equity, and sustainability of immunization programs [50]. South Asian countries need to adopt these research strategies in order to develop a long-term understanding of vaccine hesitancy barriers and bring about viable behavior change.

To compliment HPV vaccine integration in immunization programs across South Asian countries, several promising strategies have emerged. Firstly, targeting 9–14 years old adolescent girls prior to sexual activity, through school based and routine immunization programs have been recommended by WHO and are currently in place in countries like Pakistan [51]. Secondly, a multi-stakeholder policy should be introduced and strengthened, along with community engagement. Involvement of local leaders, religious scholars, health workers, and educators can help address the socio-cultural, religious, and knowledge barriers towards HPV vaccination [52]. Thirdly, fostering sustainable partnerships with health organizations such as Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunization, can offer financial assistance for vaccination programs, particularly in resource poor countries. Lastly, development of regional technical guidance and monitoring frameworks, can help scale up HPV vaccination up to 90% till the year 2030.

CONCLUSION

The current systematic review examined determinants of HPV vaccine hesitancy across South Asian populations, gathering and analyzing data from both observational and exploratory studies. The findings of the review highlight lack of awareness regarding cervical cancer, HPV and HPV vaccination as the most commonly reported barriers to vaccine uptake. Furthermore, socio-cultural and religious beliefs greatly impact vaccine related decision making in South Asian countries, as HPV is linked with sexual promiscuity and considered a social taboo. Furthermore, doubts regarding the vaccine’s efficacy, and side effects, further hinder its administration, particularly among population groups with low education levels. Families also consider the impact of vaccination cost when taking vaccination decisions, as it is one the most commonly reported factors for vaccine hesitancy. Vaccination decisions being heavily influenced by parental dynamics and healthcare professionals providing limited information and hesitancy in recommending vaccination, also play a crucial role in decreased acceptance of the vaccine. While recent years have reported improving trends in HPV awareness and vaccine uptake, gaps in accessibility, availability, communication, and affordability, need to be largely addressed. It is essential to strengthen socio-culturally appropriate HPV and cervical cancer awareness campaigns targeted towards the community, continuously train healthcare professionals so they endorse and recommend the vaccine, and most importantly, include HPV vaccination as part of the national immunization program. These strategies will have a positive impact on increasing HPV vaccine uptake in South Asian countries.
