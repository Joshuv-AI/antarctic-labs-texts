Albumin and Ambulatory Status as Predictors of 5-Year Mortality After Low-Energy Femoral Neck Fractures
Authors: Sarah N. Powell, John Hunting, Granlee Nguyen, Aidan Gaertner, Elizabeth Lyden, Christopher Deans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Optimal management of low-energy femoral neck fractures (FNFs) in the elderly remains debated. Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is increasingly used despite evidence showing no clear advantage over hemiarthroplasty (HA) in the first 12 to 24 months and potentially higher costs and risks. Identifying objective predictors of outcomes may improve surgical decision making. This study evaluated factors associated with 1- and 5-year mortality after HA or THA.Methods:We conducted a retrospective review of patients older than 65 years who underwent HA or THA for low-energy FNFs at a tertiary center. Preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative variables were collected. The Fisher exact test assessed associations with 1- and 5-year mortality. Variables with P < 0.1 were entered into multivariable logistic regression using backward selection (P < 0.05 retention).Results:Of 496 patients reviewed, 427 met criteria (mean age 80.3 ± 8.9 years); 295 had HA and 132 had THA. At 5 years, 193 patients had died—166 in the HA group (56.6%) and 27 in the THA group (20.5%). One-year mortality was associated with preoperative ambulatory status, age, hemoglobin, and albumin. Five-year mortality was associated with ambulatory status, age, ASA score, albumin, and BMI. Noncommunity ambulators had 4.3 times higher odds of 5-year mortality (P < 0.001). Each 1 g/dL decrease in albumin raised 5-year mortality odds by 58%.Conclusion:Preoperative functional and nutritional status are strong predictors of mortality after FNF surgery. These findings support the development of an evidence-based index to guide HA vs. THA selection, aiming to improve outcomes, reduce complications, and promote cost-effective care.

Femoral neck fractures (FNFs) have long been a major focus of research and improvement among the orthopaedic community and physicians working in trauma, geriatrics, and internal medicine. This is largely due to the notable morbidity and mortality associated with FNF, which has been shown to be as high as 43% at 1-year postfracture.1-3 Currently, most displaced intracapsular FNFs in elderly individuals are treated with either total hip arthroplasty (THA) or hemiarthroplasty (HA). Surgical decision making is often a combination of national or societal guidelines with provider input and is generally based on prefracture ambulation status, cognitive status, and overall estimation of the health or frailty of the patient.1

Although there have been large-scale studies exploring the differences in outcomes between THA and HA, the results are heterogenous and it is difficult to draw conclusions about which operation definitively offers patients better outcomes.1,2,4-6 The HEALTH study, one of the largest randomized control trials to date, showed that patients undergoing THA had higher WOMAC scores at 2-year postfracture but that the difference of 6.37 points was below the established minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs) for the WOMAC score.5 However, most studies have found that THA and HA have similar clinical and patient-reported outcomes for the first 12 to 24 months postoperatively, and the theoretical advantages of THA do not come into effect until at least 2 years postoperatively.2 In addition, when THA is done for FNFs, there are several notable risks, including higher dislocation risk, longer surgical time, and higher blood loss compared with THA performed for osteoarthritis and HA for femoral neck fracture.2 Therefore, the patients who are most likely to benefit from THA as opposed to HA are those who are likely to live long enough to see the benefits; however, it is difficult preoperatively to ascertain which patients these may be. Therefore, some surgeons advocate for the utilization of biomarkers or scoring systems of frailty that can act as predictors of 1-5-year mortality risks to aid in this decision, such as albumin or frailty indices such as the modified 5-factor frailty index (mFI-5).1,3,7-20

The utility of preoperative albumin as a predictor of both short-term and long-term mortality is supported both in orthopaedic literature and among other specialties.7,21-24 Furthermore, hypoalbuminemia has been shown to be associated with measures of frailty, such as the 5-factor modified frailty index (mFI-5).25,26 Multiple studies have found that hypoalbuminemia is associated with increased risk of 30-day mortality in patients with hip fractures and longer term mortality.1,8,9,11,13-17,27

Similarly, studies have shown that preinjury mobility status is associated with morbidity and mortality after FNF. A study out of the Netherlands validated the Parker Mobility Score (PMS) as being strongly associated with discharge location after admission for FNF.28 In a study of patients older than 55 years with intracapsular hip fractures, Thomas et al1 found that limited outdoor mobility was an independent predictor of mortality at 5 years postoperatively (P < 0.001). Similarly, in a prospective study of 2,640 hip fractures, Stewart et al3 found that increased, preinjury mobility was predictive of 5-year survival.

Therefore, we aimed to assess which preoperative data points, including albumin, are associated with increased risk of postoperative mortality at 1 and 5 years after low-energy FNF. We additionally aimed to create a preoperative decision aid to help indicate patients with low-energy FNF for THA or HA. We hypothesized that low albumin as well as limited preoperative ambulatory status would be markedly associated with increased mortality after low-energy FNF.

Methods

After IRB approval (0562-23-EP), a retrospective chart review was undertaken from August 1, 2012, to August 1, 2023. All relevant encounters with an ICD-10 code corresponding to femoral neck fracture (ICD-10 S72.XX) with an associated surgery ID within Epic (citation) during that encounter corresponding to HA or THA were queried. This resulted in 496 patients within 505 different encounters. If the patient had duplicate encounters (i.e., bilateral FNF requiring surgery), only the first encounter was analyzed. These patient's charts were then reviewed by an orthopaedic surgery resident and trained medical students with a secondary review for accuracy by a singular orthopaedic surgery resident. Preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative data points were collected in a RedCap database (Table 1). Patients without complete data were not included in statistical analysis. For preoperative ambulatory status, patients were considered community ambulators if it was documented in the chart that they were independent in all activities of daily living (ADLs) and they regularly left their home. Community restricted ambulators were those patients who were independent with all household ADLs but only left the house occasionally. Ambulatory aid usage was extracted from prose in notes and from physical therapy notes on prior level of function. A patient was considered to have altered cognition if they had a diagnosis in the chart of Alzheimer or other notable cognitive decline. Osteoarthritis was based on Kellgren-Lawrence grading of preoperative radiographs and was considered present if Kellgren-Lawrence grade 2 or higher was noted on injury radiographs. If the patient had a history of cardiac stents or a diagnosis of heart failure, they were considered to have coronary artery disease/coronary heart failure (CAD/CHF). Albumin values were extracted from within 2 weeks of injury or on admission. Preoperative hemoglobin was defined as hemoglobin value the day before surgery or the day of surgery, whichever was available. The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Classification System was extracted from the preprocedural anesthesia note. Body mass index (BMI) was documented as BMI at admission or the value documented closest to the patient's time of admission. Mortality data were collected from the electronic health record (EHR) if present. If the EHR did not have a listed mortality event or recent encounter, publicly available data such as obituaries were used to establish dates of death for patients. If publicly available data were used for mortality data, it was required that both the patient's full name and date of birth matched for the data point to be used.

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, medians, ranges, counts, and percentages) were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients. The Fisher exact test was used to look at the association clinical characteristics with the outcomes (deceased at 1 year, deceased at 5 years). The independent sample t-test was used to compare continuous variables with the outcome variables. For the purposes of statistical analyses, preoperative ambulatory status was dichotomized into community (community consistently and community restricted) and noncommunity ambulators (i.e. home bound, transfers only, and nonambulatory).

Multivariable logistic regression was used to determine whether variables notable on univariate analysis remained independently predictive of outcomes after adjusting for other covariates in the model. A backward selection method was used to determine the best subset of variables associated with each of the outcomes. This method involved running a full model with all the predictors significant (at the P < 0.10 level) on univariate analysis first and then sequentially removing factors that were least associated with the outcome until only variables significant at the P < 0.05 level were included in the final model. This was done for both 1-year and 5-year mortality. The final logistic regression model includes an estimate of the concordance index (c-statistic or area under the curve) as a measure of the accuracy of the model prediction.

It was determined using the full-sample data set, and the C-statistic was used as the performance metric of the final model. Bootstrap validation was used to assess the predictive model's performance about optimism (a measure for overfitting). Optimism was calculated by first taking the difference between the performance of the original model and its performance when predictions were made on new data (i.e. bootstrap replicates) and calculating the mean of the differences. Then, the optimism-corrected value of the performance metric was determined as the difference between the original performance metric (i.e. C-statistic) and the estimated optimism. Five hundred bootstrap replicates were used for the validation.

All analysis was done using SAS, Version 9.4, and a P-value < 0.05, unless otherwise specified, was considered statistically significant.

Results

Four hundred twenty-seven patients met the inclusion criteria from our initial chart review. Of these 427 patients, 295 underwent HA and 132 underwent THA (Table 2). The average of this cohort at the time of surgery was 80.24 years old (SD 8.93 years). One hundred fifty patients were male and 274 were female patients in the overall cohort. Preoperatively, 272 patients (63.7%) were community ambulators, while 146 patients (34.2%) were restricted to ambulating within the home or being mobile for transfers only (15 patients did not have data on preoperative ambulatory status). The average BMI at time of injury was 24.80 (SD 5.34). The average preoperative albumin was 3.64 (SD 0.51). One hundred and four patients (24.4%) had died by 1 year postoperatively. By 5 years postoperatively, a total of 193 patients (45.2%) had died.

Four hundred twenty-one patients had complete data to 1 year postoperatively. By 1 year postoperatively, 104 patients had died. When these 104 patients were compared with the remaining 317 patients, there were a number of notable differences between the cohorts. A significantly greater number of patients who were decreased at 1 year postoperatively had had a HA performed (93 HA vs 11 THA, P < 0.001). A significant difference was observed in the patient's preoperative ambulatory status as well, with more patients who survived >1 year postoperatively being community ambulators (P < 0.001). In addition, there were statistical differences in ASA score, altered cognition, COPD, cancer diagnosis at the time of injury, preoperatively hemoglobin, albumin, and BMI at time of injury (Table 3). Based on the univariate analysis as described in the methods, preoperative ambulatory status (dichotomized into community vs noncommunity ambulator), altered cognition, COPD, cancer diagnosis at the time of injury, age, preoperatively albumin, preoperative hemoglobin, preoperative ASA score, and BMI at time of injury were included in the multivariate logistic regression. We found that each of these factors, except ASA score, was independently associated with 1-year mortality (Table 4). The odds of death for a noncommunity ambulator at 1 year after surgery was 3.53 times the odds of death at 1 year for a patient who was a community ambulator before their injury (P < 0.001). Preoperative albumin is statistically significantly associated with death at 1 year after adjusting for the other covariates in the model (P = 0.008); for a 1 g/dL decrease in albumin, the odds of death at 1 year postoperatively increased by 45% after accounting for other variables in the model.

Three hundred eleven patients (72.8%) had complete data available, including mortality data, out to 5 years postoperatively. At 5 years postoperatively, 193 patients had died. A significantly greater proportion of patients who were decreased at 5 years postoperatively had had a HA performed (166 HA [86.0%] vs 27 THA [14.0%], P < 0.001). Variables significantly associated with 5-year mortality included preoperative ambulatory status, ASA score, altered cognition, diagnosis coronary artery disease CAD/CHF, preoperative hemoglobin, COPD, age, preoperative albumin, ASA score, and BMI at time of injury (Table 5). Based on the univariate analysis as described in the Methods, these variables were included in a multivariate logistic regression. We found that preoperative ambulatory status, age, ASA score, preoperative albumin, and BMI at time of injury were significantly and independently associated with 5-year mortality (Table 6). The odds of death for a noncommunity ambulator at 5 years postoperatively was 4.35 times the odds of death at 1 year for a patient who was a community ambulator before their injury (P < 0.001). Preoperative albumin is statistically significantly associated with death at 5 years after adjusting for the other covariates in the model (P = 0.002); for a 1 g/dL decrease in albumin, the odds of death at 5 years postoperatively increased by 58% after accounting for other variables in the model.

Discussion

This study found several preoperative factors were associated with increased risks of mortality after FNF, with albumin and preoperative ambulatory status having the largest effect on 5-year mortality risk. This supports what has been previously reported in the literature.1,3 By contrast, Harrison et al14 did not find that preoperative ambulatory status was associated with increased odds of death after FNF. The different conclusion drawn by their study may be due to how ambulatory status was classified based on the number of ambulatory aids used or that they only followed patients after surgery for a maximum of 2.5 years.

We found that lower albumin was associated with increased risk of death at 5 years postoperatively. These results mirror what has been published previously in the literature, where hypoalbuminemia has been extensively associated with increased mortality after hip fractures.1,3,8,9,11-17,19,20 Although many studies have shown an association between hypoalbuminemia and mortality after FNF, fewer studies have shown that hypoalbuminemia continues to be a predictor factor of mortality at longer term follow-up. Thomas et al1 found that preoperative hypoalbuminemia was an independent predictor of mortality after FNF at up to 9 years postoperatively. However, their study put forth a cutoff of 42 g/L (4.2 g/dL) as optimal for identifying patient survival, which is a value within the normal limits of albumin values for most patients and is likely a much higher value than would be frequently seen in the hip fracture population.1

The cohort of patients studied in our retrospective review match the demographics represented in other studies regarding elderly FNF.1,8,11,27,29 At 1 year postoperatively, 24.3% of the cohort had died, which parallels 1-year mortality data in the literature, which ranges from 17 to 24%.2,5 At 5 years postoperatively, 45.8% of the cohort had died, which is similar to mortality metrics found in the literature for elderly FNF, with ranges reported in the literature from 65.6 to 75%.1,3

Although outcomes and factors associated with increased mortality after elderly FNF have been studied extensively, there are no objective preoperative risk stratification processes that have been published to assist in indicating patients for HA versus THA to date. A variety of indices exist to evaluate a patient's preoperative function and have been used to predict short-term morbidity and mortality. A study by Kim et al18 found that the mFI-5 was an independent predictor of the risk of complications after THA or HA for femoral neck fracture based on an American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement database study, but they did not find an association with mortality. Furthermore, there have been no published studies using mFI-5 to assist in indicating patients for THA or HA after FNF. Thomas et al1 discussed the possibility of using preoperative albumin levels to help indicate patients for THA vs HA, but as far as is known to the authors, this use has not been published on. The National Health Service (NHS) in the United Kingdom has published guidelines that recommend THA in patients with increased preoperative mobility, do not have comorbidities that make THA “unsuitable” for the patient, and are likely to live beyond 2 years postoperatively.30,31

In contrast to the aforementioned studies, we found that our equation using preoperative ambulatory status, albumin, BMI, and age created an accurate model for predicting 5-year mortality. Based on our retrospective data set, the C-statistic (i.e. area under the curve) was 0.777, and it did not decrease markedly after bootstrapping, again endorsing the validity of the model. Comparatively, Thomas et al1 found that their proposed albumin cutoff of 42 g/L had a C-statistic of 0.71, and in contrast to our equation, is based solely on one preoperative data point. In the study introducing the Sharma risk assessment score, they were able to demonstrate a postoperative difference in Harris Hip Scores between patients who received THA versus HA but were unable to otherwise demonstrate a difference in morbidities or mortalities between the groups.29 In addition, this risk scoring system was developed based on a single surgeon's input, incorporates data points such as socioeconomic status, and lacks retrospective or prospective validation of its utility in aiding in indicating THA or HA in an elderly FNF population.29 Regarding the NICE guidelines from the NHS, these guidelines lack incorporation of objective preoperative data points.30,31

Our study is not without limitations. One hundred sixteen patients were excluded from our 5-year mortality analysis because of missing data. The retrospective nature and being a single-institution study may limit the generalizability of the data. In addition, the use of the electronic health record and publicly available records (i.e., obituaries) to determine mortality dates of those events may mean that our data do not represent the accurate rate of mortality 5 years after FNF.

Conclusion

As the world's population continues to have increased life expectancies, FNF will only continue to increase in incidence. Given the increased cost and risks associated with THA in this population, efforts are being made to assist in deciding which patients would benefit most from THA over HA. We used a retrospective cohort study to evaluate which objective preoperative data points were most associated with 5-year mortality after FNF and found preoperative ambulatory status, preoperative albumin, BMI, and age to be markedly associated with 5-year mortality. Additional efforts will aim to create a preoperative decision assisting tool to help surgeons indicate elderly patients with FNF for THA or HA.
