Tea egg-inspired high mechanical strength hydrogel microneedle patch combined with tea polyphenol-magnesium nanoparticles promotes spinal cord injury repair
Authors: Lin Lin, Jingze Li, Guosong Han, Zhixiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRestoring motor function remains a primary goal in the treatment of spinal cord injury (SCI). However, the inflammatory microenvironment that develops after injury poses a significant barrier to effective neural repair. Addressing this challenge requires the development of bioactive scaffolds with potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, as well as reduced implantation-induced damage. In this study, we created a novel composite biomaterial scaffold with high mechanical strength and excellent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capabilities by dispersing in situ self-assembled tea polyphenol-magnesium nanoparticles (TPs-Mg NPs) into gelatin methacryloyl hydrogel (GelMA) microneedle patches (TPs-Mg MN). Our results demonstrate that TPs-Mg MN effectively modulates the inflammatory response by promoting macrophage polarization through suppression of the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby alleviating reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated oxidative damage. Following implantation in a rat SCI model, TPs-Mg MN significantly enhanced motor functional recovery. Behavioral analyses revealed that this recovery was achieved through multiple mechanisms, including reduced oxidative stress, inflammation, and scar formation at the injury site, as well as enhanced angiogenesis and neurogenesis within the spinal cord tissue. This study presents a multifunctional combinatorial strategy for mitigating ROS-induced oxidative stress, offering broad potential applications in SCI and other central nervous system disorders.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, Microneedle patch, Tea polyphenol-magnesium, Nerve repair

Introduction

Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) is a major cause of neurological dysfunction [1,2]. With the aging population and increasing vehicular activity, the incidence of accidental falls and traffic accidents has risen, leading to a rapid escalation in SCI cases globally. It is reported that approximately 500,000 individuals suffer from SCI annually worldwide [3,4]. Due to the limited self-repair capacity of spinal cord tissues and the complexity of pathological changes, there is currently no effective therapeutic strategy for SCI. Most clinically available treatments, including high-dose glucocorticoid shock therapy, are palliative and yield suboptimal outcomes in sensory and motor functional recovery, imposing substantial economic and societal burdens on patients and their families. The pathological progression following SCI involves multifaceted biological mechanisms and multi-stage pathological responses. A critical challenge in neuro-restoration lies in addressing the cascade of complex pathological processes triggered by SCI, encompassing primary mechanical injury and subsequent secondary events such as inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and neuronal apoptosis. The initial mechanical trauma directly damages neurons, glial cells, and vasculature. Secondary injury exacerbates the damage through sustained inflammation, blood-spinal cord barrier disruption, oxidative stress, and neurotoxic events, including vascular damage, edema, ionic imbalance, excitotoxicity, lipid peroxidation, free radical generation, calcium influx, cell death, axonal demyelination, and degeneration [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. These molecular and cellular alterations culminate in extensive neurodegeneration, disruption of spinal circuitry, and the formation of large cystic cavities. Subsequent inflammatory cell infiltration and axonal demyelination contribute to glial scar formation, which, along with cystic cavities, impedes axonal regeneration, further aggravating tissue destruction and suppressing neuro-regenerative capacity [9,10]. Collectively, these pathological changes underscore the urgent need to develop strategies that replenish lost neurons, reverse the hostile microenvironment, provide neurotrophic support, and mitigate neurotoxic effects of inflammation and oxidative stress. To address these challenges, researchers have explored diverse biomaterials and drug delivery systems to modulate the microenvironment and facilitate neural regeneration [[11], [12], [13]].

Advances in neuroscience and regenerative medicine have driven the evolution of multidisciplinary therapeutic strategies for SCI, including cell therapy, gene editing, tissue engineering, and advanced drug delivery technologies. Among these, optimizing the injury microenvironment by regulating secondary injury processes is pivotal for promoting neuro-regeneration and functional recovery. Interventions targeting inflammation and oxidative stress are widely recognized as critical pathways to improve therapeutic outcomes. In recent years, emerging therapies such as microneedle technology and nanomaterials have shown significant potential in enhancing functional recovery in SCI patients. Microneedles, as minimally invasive delivery platforms, enable efficient drug administration with minimal trauma, while nanomaterials exhibit exceptional biocompatibility and tunable functionalities for neuro-regeneration and microenvironment modulation. Hydrogel, extensively studied for SCI repair, can deliver bioactive compounds, peptides, or extracellular vesicles to regulate the post-SCI microenvironment [10,[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Compared to polymeric scaffolds, hydrogels better mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM), providing a conducive environment for cell growth and enabling higher cell encapsulation within their dense molecular networks [[18], [19], [20]]. Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), a photo-crosslinkable hydrogel, has emerged as a cornerstone material in regenerative medicine due to its superior biocompatibility, tunable mechanical properties, and three-dimensional cell-embedding capabilities. GelMA-based microneedle systems, integrating hydrogel biocompatibility with innovative drug delivery, hold groundbreaking potential in neuro-regeneration. However, standalone GelMA hydrogels exhibit limited anti-inflammatory and antioxidant capacities. Thus, combining GelMA with bioactive nanomaterials could significantly enhance its bioactivity and reparative efficacy.

Tea polyphenol (TP), natural antioxidants with robust free radical scavenging and anti-inflammatory properties, have garnered increasing attention. Recent studies demonstrate that tea polyphenols-magnesium nanoparticles (TPs-Mg NPs) synergistically enhance antioxidant performance and suppress neuroinflammation by modulating macrophage M1/M2 polarization, thereby accelerating tissue repair [21]. These nanoparticles exhibit remarkable advantages in mitigating oxidative stress, regulating inflammatory microenvironments, and promoting neuronal differentiation, highlighting their potential for SCI therapy. Nevertheless, the application of nanomaterial-based microneedle systems in SCI faces challenges, including precise spatiotemporal drug delivery, improving mechanical strength and biocompatibility of microneedles, and optimizing nanomaterial degradation to minimize toxicity. These issues demand urgent resolution. Natural polymer hydrogels possess biosafety and represent a class of irreplaceable biomaterials. However, their poor mechanical strength significantly limits their application as structural tissue engineering scaffolds. Inspired by the traditional Chinese snack, tea eggs, Wu et al. discovered that simply steeping protein gels in tea can enhance their rigidity [22]. Consequently, the mechanical properties of hydrogels are improved through hydrogen bonding interactions between tea polyphenols and polymers, while the coordination of single polyphenols with metal ions also enhances their bioavailability and the biosafety of metal nanoparticles.

Hence, this study proposes a novel hydrogel microneedle system integrating GelMA hydrogels with TPs-Mg NPs to synergistically alleviate inflammation, mitigate oxidative stress, and promote neuro-regeneration, thereby accelerating neural repair post-SCI. The findings will validate the multifunctional efficacy of this system in SCI models and lay a theoretical foundation for its clinical translation (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Synthesis and characterization of TPs-Mg NPs

TPs-Mg NPs with metal–polyphenol network was prepared by self-assembly under alkaline conditions. Specifically, 5.0 mL of 3 mM MgSO4 (Aladdin, China) was added to 25.0 mL of 0.5 mg mL−1 TPs (Aladdin, China) and reacted at 45 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 5 mL of 0.5 mM NaOH solution was added to the mixture, and the reduction reaction was carried out for 8 h. The resulting solution was then washed using a microfiltration membrane (MWCO: 3000 Da) to remove free ions, resulting in TPs-Mg nanoparticles. These nanoparticles can be stored for a long time at 4 °C. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL, Japan) was used to observe the nanoparticles and analyze their size distribution. Simultaneously, Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) was employed to analyze the particle size distribution of TPs-Mg NPs. The Zetasizer Nano (Anton Paar Litesizer 500, UK) was used to measure the zeta potentials of TPs, and TPs-Mg NPs. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet IS10, Thermo China) was utilized to characterize TPs-Mg, MgSO4 and TPs. X-ray diffraction spectroscopy (DX-2700, China) was employed to analyze TPs, TPs-Mg NPs and MgSO4 solutions.

Synthesis and characterization of TPs-Mg MN

To prepare the drug-loaded MN arrays, a suitable amount of TPs-Mg NPs was dispersed in 5 mL of GelMA solution (EFL, China) (50 mg/mL), to which 25 mg of LAP was subsequently added, ensuring that the final concentration of NPs was 10 μg/mL. The hydrogel precursor solution (300 μL) was then injected into a PDMS mold (MicroPoint Technologies, China). Following vacuum de-bubbling and multiple drying cycles, the drug-loaded MNs were formed via photo-crosslinking irradiation with 405 nm UV light. A PVA (Aladdin, China) (20% w/v) solution was applied to the bottom of the model and subjected to an additional two-step drying process. Finally, the drug-loaded MNs were demolded from the PDMS model for further use. Blank MN arrays were fabricated using the same procedure, except for the addition of TPs-Mg NPs.

The surface morphology and needle construction of the MNs were examined using JCM-7000 NeoScope SEM. The uniform elemental distribution of TPs-Mg MN was assessed via EDS using ZEISS GeminiSEM 300 for element mapping. Additionally, FITC-TPs-Mg was incorporated during MN fabrication. The fluorescence of the produced MNs was analyzed using a laser confocal microscope (Olympus FV1200, Japan). The mechanical strengths of Blank MN and TPs-Mg MN were determined using an electronic tensile testing machine (Instron 68FM-300, USA). The XPS analysis was performed using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo, USA).

Similarly, the content of TPs was measured using a UV spectrophotometer, and a release curve was plotted.

Biosafety evaluation

PC12 and raw 264.7 culture

Rat pheochromocytoma (PC12, CL-0481) cells and mouse-derived RAW 264.7 macrophage cells (CL-0190), acquired from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd, were respectively incubated in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (PS, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and high glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA) containing 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) PS at 37 °C in 5% CO2 atmosphere within a humidified cell incubator. The medium is replaced every three days to maintain optimal conditions for cell physiology and metabolism. Cells of optimal growth were seeded into well plates at a specified cell density and subjected to various conditions for further experiment.

Cell proliferation and viability

The cytocompatibility of drug was performed by evaluating the in vitro PC12 cell viability after treatment with various concentrations TPs-Mg NPs via using cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) assay on days 1, 2 and 3. Briefly, the PC12 cells with optimal growth condition were seeded into 96-well plates (1 × 103 cells/well) and then incubated overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2 atmosphere. Then the previous culture medium was replaced with culture medium containing different concentrations of TP-Mg NPs (final concentrations of 0, 0.5, 1, 5, 10 and 20 μg/mL, respectively). At different time points (24, 48, and 72 h), the culture mediums were withdrawn and replaced with CCK-8 solution (10% CCK-8 in serum free medium) for 2 h at 37 °C. Afterward, the medium was collected and the optical density (OD) values of the samples was measured at 450 nm via a microplate reader to assess proliferation. Cell morphology and adhesion

The cytoskeleton was stained with FITC-labeled-Phalloidin after 1 and 3 days of culture to observe cell adhesion. Briefly, PC12 cells with optimal growth condition were seeded on the 48-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 per well. After incubation with culture medium containing different concentrations of TPs-Mg NPs for 1 and 3 days, the morphology of the treated PC12 cells was assessed using light microscopy firstly (IX71, OLYMPUS, Japan). Likewise, the PC12 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Biosharp, China) after co-culture with culture medium for 24 and 72 h. Following 10 min of fixation, the PC12 cells were washed with PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X-100 (Biosharp, China). The PC12 cells were subsequently stained with FITC-Phalloidin (CA1620, Solarbio, China) for 30 min at 37 °C and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Biosharp, China) for 5 min. Finally, the stained cells were visualized by a microscope (IX71, OLYMPUS, Japan).

Live/dead staining

In addition, the viability of PC12 cells were assessed by Live/Dead cell staining kits (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) after co-culture with culture medium containing different concentrations of TPs-Mg NPs for 1 and 3 days. As described in the instructions for the live-dead staining kit, the PC12 cells were incubated with the prepared calcein-AM/Propidium Iodide (PI) solution for 30 min. Following this, the staining solution was aspirated and washed with buffer solution three times. Subsequently, the images of living/dead cells which live cells were labeled with green by calcein-AM and dead cells were labeled with red by PI were observed under a fluorescence microscope (IX71, OLYMPUS, Japan). Similarly, the effect of GelMA/TPs-Mg NPs MN on the viability of H2O2-stimulated PC12 cells was explored based on the above assay.

Cell apoptosis and necrosis assays

Apoptosis and necrosis of PC12 cells was evaluated via using flow cytometry. PC12 cells with optimal growth condition were inoculated in 3.5cm × 3.5 cm petri dishes and cultured in a humidified cell incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cell status was observed regularly, and when the cell density reached 60%, the culture medium containing different concentrations of TPs-Mg NPs were added for incubation for 48 h. Finally, the cells were treated according to the apoptosis kit instructions (Beyotime, China). Briefly, the treated PC12 cells were washed with PBS, and then PBS was added to gently blow to form a suspension. The PC12 cell suspension was collected and added to a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min before discarding the supernatant and collecting the cells. The PC12 cells were gently resuspended using 195 μl Annexin V-FITC conjugate from the apoptosis kit, followed by the addition of 5 μl Annexin V-FITC mix and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Finally, 10 μl propidium iodide staining solution (PI) was added and incubated for 5 min. During this incubation time, the cells can be shaken several times. Finally, the incubated PC12 cell suspension was centrifuged, the supernatant was discarded and resuspended with Annexin V-FITC conjugate, and subsequently stored in an ice bath. The assay is performed by flow cytometry. Ultimately, apoptotic or necrotic cells were statistically analyzed according to the instructions for the assay reagents.

In vitro assessment of macrophage polarization and western blotting

To assess the anti-inflammatory effects of TP-Mg MN in vitro, the macrophages (Raw 264.7) were seeded in well plates at a specified density. The growth medium, supplemented with a final concentration of 1 μg/mL LPS, was co-incubated with the Raw 264.7 cells for 12 h. Subsequently, growth medium containing different concentrations of the extracts of GelMA/TPs-Mg NPs MN were co-incubated with LPS-treated Raw 264.7 cells for 24 h. After this incubation period, the cells were then washed three times with PBS solution. Followed by fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked using 1% BSA solution at 4 °C for 1 h. The cells were incubated with M1 macrophage (maker for CD86) and M2 macrophage antibody (maker for CD206) dilutions overnight at 4 °C. This was followed by a 2-h incubation with secondary antibodies (Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit, Alexa fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit) at 4 °C. The cells were stained with DAPI solution for 5 min and finally photographed under a fluorescence microscope.

Initially, samples derived from RAW 264.7 cell culture was homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer fortified with phosphatase and protease inhibitor cocktails and incubated on ice for 30 min. The resulting homogenates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were retained. Protein concentrations were subsequently determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit. Standardized protein loads (20−40 μg) were mixed with SDS loading dye, thermally denatured at 95 °C for 300 s, and separated via SDS-PAGE on 10% gels. Following electrophoresis, proteins were electro-transferred onto PVDF membranes. To minimize nonspecific binding, for 1 h at room temperature, the PVDF was blocked using 5% nonfat dry milk in Trisbuffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST). Anti-CD86, anti-CD206, anti-p65, and anti-P-p65 were used to probethe blots overnight at 4 °C. The internal reference standard used for normalization was β-actin. The membranes were washed with TBST and then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. An enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (P0018S, Beyotime) was used to visualize protein bands, which were then recorded on a ChemiDoc XRS + imager (Bio-Rad). Lastly, target protein signals were adjusted before densitometric analysis was carried out using ImageJ software.

Antioxidant capacity test in vitro

The effects of different concentration extract on ROS formation in PC12 cells under oxidative stress conditions were labeled by DCFH-DA probe and detected using flow cytometry and immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, PC12 cells with optimal growth condition were inoculated in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 per well and cultured at 37 °C in 5% CO2 atmosphere within a humidified cell incubator for 24 h to make them adherent to the wall. When the PC12 cells reached 60% density, PC12 cells were treated with 200 μmol/L H2O2 solution for 2 h, then the extracts of GelMA/TPs-Mg MN contained various concentrations TPs-Mg NPs were added and treated for 8 h. The PC12 cells were digested using trypsin and the supernatant was discarded by centrifugation. Afterward, DCFH-DA solution diluted in serum-free medium was added and incubated in a 5% CO2 cell culture incubator at 37 °C for 20 min, during which the cells were shaken once. The suspension was then centrifuged and the PC12 cells were collected and washed by resuspension using serum-free medium to adequately remove the DCFH-DA that had not entered the cells. Finally, the cells were resuspended using serum-free medium and added to a flow-through tube for detection. For immunofluorescence staining, when serum-free medium was added to the diluted DCFH-DA solution, the cells were incubated for 20 min at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 cell culture incubator, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were washed with serum-free medium to remove the DCFH-DA that had not entered the cells, and finally observed and photographed under an inverted fluorescence microscope.

In vitro differentiation of neural stem cells

Neural stem cells (NSCs) were obtained from the spinal cords of neonatal SD rats within 24 h post-birth. The isolation process commenced by cooling the neonatal rats in an icebox, followed by a 5-min exposure to 75% ethanol for disinfection. After decapitating the rats and performing an incision to reveal the spine, spinal cord tissue was meticulously removed using forceps. Blood vessels were excised from the tissue, which was subsequently digested with Accutase at 37 °C. To terminate the digestion, a suitable volume of DMEM was added, after which the cells underwent centrifugation, were resuspended, and placed in NSC proliferation medium for further growth.

To evaluate the in vitro neurodifferentiation effect of TP-Mg MN, NSCs were inoculated in culture plates at the specified density. Incubate the growth medium containing a final concentration of 10 μg/mL LPS with NSCs for 12 h. Subsequently, the growth medium containing MN extracts of different groups was co-incubated with NSCs treated with LPS for 7 days. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS solution, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, permeated with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and sealed with 1% BSA solution at 4 °C for 1 h. The diluents of cell and neuron antibody (Tuj-1 labeled antibody) and astrocyte antibody (GFAP labeled antibody) were incubated overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with secondary antibodies (Cy3-labeled goat anti-rabbit antibody, Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit antibody) at 4 °C for 2 h. Finally, stain with DAPI solution for 5 min and observe under a fluorescence microscope.

Hemolysis assay

To evaluate the hemolytic effect of various components of the hydrogel extracts, a hemolysis assay was conducted. Whole rat blood was collected and treated with sodium citrate, then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the erythrocytes were washed three times with PBS, followed by dilution to a final concentration of 5% (v/v). Subsequently, different hydrogel extract groups were mixed with 1.0 mL of the 5% (v/v) erythrocyte suspension in 2 mL EP tubes and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and the absorbance of the resulting supernatant was measured at 540 nm. Hemolysis was quantified by comparing the absorbance values across the experimental groups. Deionized water served as the positive control, while PBS was used as the negative control. Additionally, a pure TPs-Mg solution, devoid of erythrocytes, was photographed and analyzed to eliminate any potential interference from its own color in the experiment.

In vivo experiments

SCI model construction and microneedle patch implantation

In this study, Female Sprague‒Dawley rats (weight = 200–250 g) obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Bengbu Medical University were randomly divided into four experimental groups, including the Sham group (n = 10), SCI group (n = 10), GelMA microneedle patch (MN) group (n = 10) and GelMA/TPs-Mg NPs microneedle patch (TPs-Mg MN) group (n = 10). Before surgery, all rats were kept 2 weeks of quarantine in a 12-h light/dark cycle environment. In brief, the rats were anesthetized with 1% v/v sodium pentobarbital (4.5 mL/kg) via intraperitoneal injection. Following the disinfection of the skin, a 2-cm midline incision was created. A laminectomy was subsequently conducted on the T8-T10 vertebral bodies utilizing a surgical blade. After exposing the T9 spinal cord, use a fixator to clamp the spinous process and strike the spinal cord with a 1.5 mm diameter impactor metal rod to establish a spinal cord contusion model. Depending on the categorization, the microneedle patch is positioned within the lesion site of the damaged spinal cord. After ensuring precise hemostasis, the muscle tissue and skin are meticulously sutured in a layered manner. Subsequently, the rat is transferred to a constant temperature box, maintained at 37 °C, to facilitate recovery. To mitigate the risk of wound infection, antibiotics are administered for a period of one week following the surgical procedure. Additionally, manual urination is conducted every 12 h post-operation until the urinary function reverts to its normal state.

Function analysis in SCI rats

The neurological outcomes were evaluated via using a Basso-Beattie-Bresnahan (BBB) score scale (scored from 0 to 21) weekly post-surgery until week 4. Before assessment, all post-surgery rat were placed on an open surface and allow them to walk freely for 5 min. After then, three independent observers recorded the walking and limb activity scores of the hind limbs for at least 5 min according to the BBB score table.

We analyzed the hind limb footprints and walking angles of the rats at week 4. Briefly, the forelimbs and hind limbs of the rats were marked with blue and red ink, respectively. A white paper was placed in a rectangular channel, and different groups of rats were positioned at the starting end, allowing them to move freely within the enclosure. This enabled the collection and analysis of hind limb footprints. To assess the hind limb angles, the rats from different groups were placed on a grid, and photographs were taken to document the positions of the hind limb joints during movement.

Finally, tissue samples of the spinal cord and gastrocnemius muscle of rats in each group at 4 weeks, as well as those of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs and kidneys at 4 weeks and 8 weeks were collected and photographed. Subsequently, the tissue repair and in vivo biocompatibility were analyzed through staining techniques.

Histological and immunohistochemistry analysis

After establishing the rat spinal cord injury model, perfusion and tissue collection were performed at different time points. All rats were deeply anesthetized and then perfused intracardially with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The spinal cord segment containing the injury site was dissected and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight. This was followed by dehydration in sequential 20% sucrose (48 h at 4 °C) and 30% sucrose solutions (72 h at 4 °C). The spinal cord tissue was then embedded in OCT (Optimal Cutting Temperature compound, Sakura Finetek, USA). The resulting frozen tissue blocks were sectioned into 8 μm thick slices using a cryostat (LEICA CM1900). To evaluate the lesion cavity area in spinal cord tissue, morphology of bladder tissue, changes in the gastrocnemius muscle of the hind limbs, and the in vivo biosafety of the tissue, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on spinal cord sections, gastrocnemius muscle, and organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys following standard procedures. Additionally, Nissl staining was conducted according to the established protocol.

To assess the levels of oxidative stress and inflammation in vivo, blood samples and spinal cord tissue were collected on day 7 after model establishment. The expression levels of TNF-α (mlbio, ml002095, Shanghai, China), IL-6 (mlbio, ml002293, Shanghai, China), and MDA (Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were measured using commercial kits.

For assessing nerve regeneration in vivo, spinal cord samples were analyzed using immunofluorescence staining. Briefly, tissue sections were washed with PBS buffer for 5 min, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 2 h. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After three washes with PBS, the tissue was incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at 4 °C. Finally, the sections were treated with DAPI solution for 5 min in the dark. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SEM, and statistical analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism 9.2.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Differences between groups were assessed by one-way and two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P values are represented as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Characterization of TPs-Mg NPs and MN

We constructed a complex of metallic magnesium and tea polyphenols in one step. The main reason for this might be that the catechol structure of tea polyphenols combines with metal ions under alkaline conditions. The environment forms stable cluster structures (Fig. 2A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 2B) show that nanoscale clusters are uniformly distributed in the reddish–brown solution, with a particle size distribution of 15.2 ± 2.43 nm (Fig. 2C). The evolution of the zeta potential further validated the success of TPs-Mg NPs. With the introduction of the strongly negatively charged TPs, the zeta potential of TPs-Mg NPs decreased to −19.77 mV. Comparatively, the zeta potentials of magnesium sulfate solution and TPs are −4.03 mV and −15.03 mV, respectively (Fig. 2D). To investigate the structural characteristics of TPs-Mg, its chemical composition and crystal structure were analyzed using FTIR and XRD. As shown in the FTIR spectra in Fig. 2E, The peaks at 1114 cm−1 and 1617 cm−1 are attributed to the C-O-C and C = C vibrational peaks in TPs, respectively. The absorption band at 3430 cm−1 corresponds to the O-H stretching vibration. Compared with TPs, the absorption peaks of TPs-Mg NPs at 2940 cm−1 can be attributed to the C-H stretching vibration absorption peaks. Furthermore, the XRD pattern in Fig. 2F showed that the diffraction peaks of TPs-Mg NPs at 34.3°, 47.9°, and 68.1°. This coincides with the JCPDS card (No. 35–0821) for Mg, signifying the successful binding of TPs and Mg. The survey spectrum confirmed the presence of C, O and Mg originating from the TP and MgSO4 (Fig. S1). These results collectively indicate the successful preparation of TPs-Mg NPs.

In this study, we chose GelMA MNs as the drug delivery vector. GelMA is a natural hydrogel derived from gelatin. This material has high biocompatibility and is widely used to support cell growth and simulate the natural extracellular matrix. MNs offers a promising strategy for sealing and protecting injured or damaged lesions, allowing therapeutic drugs to directly act on the injured site in a minimally invasive manner. The synthesis process of TPs-Mg MN, the silicone mold and the macroscopic morphology of MNs are shown in Fig. 2G. The resulting TPs-Mg MN was a square patch with 8 mm sides featuring 100 uniformly arranged conical MNs, and each MN patch measured 10 × 10. As seen in the SEM images, TPs-Mg MN exhibited a conical needle-like structure with a height of 600 μm, a base diameter of 300 μm, and a needle spacing of 300 μm (Fig. 2H). The elemental mapping of the needle body structure using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) revealed a uniform distribution of C, O and Mg, confirming the successful loading of TPs-Mg (Fig. 2I). To visualize the TPs-Mg distribution in the TPs-Mg MN, we performed a critical examination of TPs-MN labeling with FITC (green) and MN labeling with Rhodamine B dye (red). The distribution of TPs-Mg can be identified from the stereoscopic fluorescence images. The fluorescence imaging by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) clearly shows that the green fluorescence of the nanomedicine is uniformly distributed throughout the red fluorescence of MN (Fig. 2J).

Specifically, TPs-Mg MN demonstrates a distinctive sustained release profile. Magnesium ions (Mg2+) are released in an initial burst within the first 3 days, followed by a gradual and prolonged release over the course of 14 days, which helps maintain stable local concentrations of both TPs and Mg2+ (Fig. 2K). TPs also exhibit similar characteristics in their release from hydrogel microneedles (Fig. S2). Mechanical strength is a crucial parameter for the effective penetration of MN patches into the spinal cord tissue. Compression tests were conducted to measure their force-displacement curves to evaluate the mechanical properties of the various MN patch groups. Due to the abundant hydrogen bonds in TPs and TPs-Mg rapidly enhancing the stability of gelatin in GelMA hydrogels, the mechanical strength of the MN patch tips in each group all increased (Fig. 2L). The compressive force applied by MNs during full tip height deformation is 5.2 to 5.8 N per needle, which exceeds the minimum force required to penetrate the dura mater (0.08 N). This indicates that the mechanical strength of the TPs-Mg MN needle is sufficient to penetrate the dura mater and spinal cord tissue. To further prove this conclusion, MN was attached to the spinal cord tissue, and the patch tissue was lifted with forceps. The results indicated that the spinal cord tissue could resist its own gravity and remained firmly attached to the MN patch (Fig. S3).

Subsequently, we simulated the in vitro drug release of RhB-loaded MN patch. For continuous observation, the RhB-loaded MN patch was placed in a transparent agar hydrogel (2% w/v) and maintained under constant pressure. As shown in Fig. S4, the red-stained area within the hydrogel gradually expanded over time, indicating that the preloaded RhB was released slowly and continuously. As an emerging drug delivery system, microneedles can significantly improve permeability and demonstrate unique advantages. It indicates that microneedles create microchannels through a physical penetration mechanism, enabling not only the effective delivery of small-molecule drugs but also the transdermal delivery of large-molecule drugs. Furthermore, soluble microneedles can completely dissolve, facilitating precise drug delivery.

Highly hydrophilic structures typically exhibit significant water absorption and swelling capacities, which are advantageous for absorbing excess tissue fluid in SCI. TPs-Mg MN exhibited considerable swelling in PBS and water, with swelling ratios of 691% in H2O and 225% in PBS after 24 h (Fig. 2M). Furthermore, hydrophilic MNs may experience water loss due to evaporation during use, potentially affecting the TPs-Mg activity. Consequently, the water-loss activities of the MNs were investigated (Fig. 2N). The results showed that the water loss rates of MN and TPs-Mg MN treated at 30 °C for 12 h were 27% and 20.7% respectively. The differences between them might be due to the addition of TPs-Mg NPs. The degradation rate of MNs is a key factor influencing whether drugs can be released for a long time. Continuous, stable and long-term drug release is crucial for the repair of neurological function in rats with SCI. The degradation rate of TPs-Mg MN under physiological conditions was detected here (Fig. 2O). It was found that TPs-Mg MN presented a slow and stable degradation process, with a degradation rate of only 42.3% at 7 days. In vivo imaging system was used to detect the release, degradation and diffusion behaviors of drug-loaded microneedles at different time points in the body. There was still a small amount of drug residue in the fourth week. However, the fluorescent signal was confined to the implantation site, and by the eighth week, the signal had gradually disappeared from the rats' bodies, indicating the degradation of the hydrogel microneedles in vivo (Fig. S5). These results suggest that TPs-Mg MN helps to continuously release TPs and Mg2+ over the long term and thereby achieve the purpose of repairing SCI.

TPs-Mg NPs exhibit excellent histocompatibility

Determining the optimal drug concentration is essential to eliciting desired biological responses in cellular systems. To evaluate the biocompatibility of the drug, we conducted a series of direct and indirect assays. In our in vitro experiments, the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was employed to measure the proliferative capacity of cells treated with various concentrations TPs-Mg NPs. The absorbance values obtained from the CCK-8 assay provided a quantitative assessment of cellular proliferation, with higher absorbance values indicating increased cellular activity.

As shown in Fig. 3E, TPs-Mg NPs concentrations up to 10 μg/mL did not impair the robust proliferative capability of PC12 cells, suggesting minimal toxicity within this range. However, at a concentration of 20 μg/mL, significant inhibition of PC12 cell growth was observed by day 3, contrasting with the continued proliferation seen in groups treated with lower concentrations of TPs-Mg NPs (P < 0.05). These findings suggest that the higher concentration TPs-Mg NPs are detrimental to PC12 cell proliferation, likely disrupting normal cellular functions.

We also investigated cellular adhesion under the influence of various concentrations TPs-Mg NPs using light microscopy and nucleo-skeleton staining. As shown in Fig. 3A, during the first 24 h of culture, PC12 cells in the control group exhibited elongated, filamentous morphologies indicative of robust cellular plasticity. In contrast, the TPs-Mg NPs induced dose-dependent morphological alterations, with the most pronounced effects observed at a concentration of 20 μg/mL. At this threshold, PC12 cells displayed increased crumpling and a transition of cytoskeleton morphology from elongated to flattened or spherical configurations. Cells treated with lower concentrations of TPs-Mg NPs exhibited less pronounced deviations. By day 3, PC12 cells formed aggregates, indicative of marked proliferative activity. However, cells exposed to the highest concentrations of TPs-Mg NPs retained the morphological characteristics observed on day 1, suggesting persistent cytoskeletal disruption. Light microscopy images (Fig. 3A), corroborated by nucleo-skeleton staining (Fig. 3B), confirmed that lower concentrations of TPs-Mg NPs are well-tolerated, whereas higher concentrations impair cellular plasticity. The cytoskeleton's integrity and extensibility are critical for maintaining normal physiological functions, including extracellular matrix secretion and protein expression.

Live and dead cell staining provided an additional measure of biocompatibility. PC12 cells treated with varying concentrations TPs-Mg NPs were assessed for viability on days 1 and 3, with live cells stained green and dead cells stained red. As shown in Fig. 3C, no significant cell death was observed across treatment groups on day 1, and the live cell population remained stable. However, a marked decline in live cell counts and a concurrent increase in dead cells were observed in the 20 μg/mL TPs-Mg NPs group by day 3, consistent with the CCK-8 assay results. These findings affirm that a concentration of 20 μg/mL is deleterious to PC12 cell survival.

Establishing robust cytocompatibility is a critical prerequisite for the clinical application of pharmacological agents. To quantify the apoptotic and necrosis response of PC12 cells to varying concentrations TPs-Mg NPs, we performed flow cytometric analysis. As depicted in Fig. 3D, the control group exhibited minimal apoptosis and necrosis. In contrast, a dose-dependent increase in apoptosis was observed with escalating concentrations of TPs-Mg NPs, culminating in a significant apoptotic rate of 1.06% at 20 μg/mL (P < 0.01, Fig. 3F). This increase in apoptosis highlights the compromised biocompatibility of high concentrations TPs-Mg NPs, whereas lower concentrations appear to support normal physiological function in PC12 cells. Similar to the apoptosis results, the percentage of cell necrosis increased significantly with increasing concentration of TPs-Mg NPs, notably reaching 6.13% at 20 μg/mL (Fig. 3G). The increase in necrosis was not favorable to the normal physiological function of the cells.

Finally, to investigate the protective potential of TPs-Mg NPs against oxidative stress, we assessed their effects on PC12 cell survival under exposure to H2O2. As shown in Fig. 3H, cell viability decreased markedly with increasing H2O2 concentrations. At 200 μmol/L, H2O2 reduced PC12 cell viability by 50%, informing our selection of this concentration for subsequent experiments.

TPs-Mg MN facilitates NSC neurodifferentiation in vitro

Excessive inflammatory response after SCI has been shown to trigger the differentiation of NSCs into the astrocyte lineage, resulting in reduced neuronal differentiation. LPS was added to the NSCs medium to simulate the inflammatory state, and then co-cultured with the extracts of each group. Immunofluorescence staining showed that, compared with the LPS and blank MN groups, the number of Tuj-1 (neuronal marker) in LPS co-cultured NSCs treated with TPs-Mg MN extract increased (Fig. S6A and B), while the number of GFAP (astrocyte marker) decreased (Fig. S6C and D). All in all, these results indicate that TPs-Mg MN can reverse the inflammatory state and ultimately promote the transformation of neural stem cells into neurons.

TPs-Mg NPs effectively mitigates oxidative stress and inflammatory responses

Macrophages exhibit distinct phenotypes with varying functions, and the phenotypic transition from M1 to M2 macrophages plays a crucial role in tissue repair [23,24]. To explore whether TPs-Mg MN can exert anti-inflammatory effects by modulating macrophage phenotypes in vitro, we performed immunofluorescence staining experiments. As shown in Fig. 4A, LPS stimulation increased the fluorescence intensity of CD86, a marker specific to M1 macrophages, compared to the control group (MN), while the fluorescence intensity of CD206, a marker for M2 macrophages, decreased. This suggests that inflammatory stimuli promote M1 macrophage polarization and inhibit M2 macrophage expression, disrupting the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors, thereby delaying tissue repair. However, treatment with the extracts of TPs-Mg MN significantly reduced the fluorescence intensity of CD86 in comparison to the LPS group, with no significant difference observed between the extracts of high- and low-concentration TPs-Mg MN groups (Fig. 4H). In contrast, the extracts of TPs-Mg MN enhanced the fluorescence expression of CD206, promoting M2 macrophage polarization under inflammatory conditions. These findings indicate that LPS induces M1 macrophage polarization, while the extracts of TPs-Mg MN improve the inflammatory microenvironment, facilitating the shift from M1 to M2 macrophages. The results of detecting macrophage-specific markers CD86 and CD206 by Western blotting simultaneously also showed that TPs-Mg MN could promote the transformation of macrophages into the anti-inflammatory type. This suggests that the extracts of TPs-Mg MN, at certain concentrations, can promote macrophage polarization and exert anti-inflammatory effects, providing a foundation for subsequent experimental investigations. To better understand the molecular mechanism by which TPs-Mg MN affects the slightly environment of SCI, we used western blotting to detect the protein expression of the NF-κB signaling pathway. The results (Fig. S7D) and quantitative analyses (Fig. S7E) showed that the expression levels of p65 and p-p65 in the TPs-Mg MN group were lower than those in the LPS group, suggesting that the synergistic effect of tea polyphenols and magnesium ions inhibited the nuclear translocation of the NF-κB active subunit p65 and prevented it from activating the NF-κB signaling pathway by regulating target genes. Inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway leads to the transformation of macrophages from pro-inflammatory type to anti-inflammatory type (Fig. S7A–C). This indicates that TPs-Mg MN can effectively regulate the inflammatory microenvironment of in vivo, thereby promoting neural repair of SCI.

TPs-Mg MN improve the post-spinal inflammatory microenvironment in vivo

Secondary SCI involves complex microenvironmental changes, including extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells and neuronal death at the injury site. In the early stages of SCI, microglial cells rapidly activate, releasing cytotoxic substances and recruiting macrophages to the site of injury. The resultant cytokine storm, driven by an overproduction of inflammatory and chemotactic factors, exacerbates neuronal death, promotes tissue fibrosis, and forms dense scar tissue that obstructs neural repair [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Prior in vitro experiments have demonstrated that TPs-Mg NPs positively influence the polarization of RAW macrophages under simulated inflammatory conditions and mitigate oxidative stress caused by H2O2. Macrophages are critical mediators of inflammation and tissue repair, with the transition from pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages to anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages being essential for recovery. Based on the pathological microenvironment following SCI, we focus on the early use of TPs-Mg MN with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties to attenuate severe inflammatory responses and achieve neuroprotection.

As shown in Fig. S10, MDA activity significantly increased following spinal cord injury, whereas treatment with TPs Mg NPs reduced MDA activity, thereby improving the oxidative stress microenvironment after SCI. The severity of the inflammatory response was assessed by measuring levels of inflammatory cytokines. Following spinal cord injury, large amounts of pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α and IL-6) are produced, which are clearly detrimental to neural repair after SCI. However, after treatment with TPs Mg NPs, the levels of pro-inflammatory factors decreased significantly, effectively improving the microenvironment following SCI and providing a foundation for neural regeneration.

Elevated 8-OHdG levels after SCI indicate increased oxidative DNA damage in neurons, impairing cellular function and promoting apoptosis [29,30]. This oxidative damage exacerbates secondary injury progression and impairs functional recovery. Monitoring 8-OHdG levels can therefore assess the effectiveness of antioxidant therapies. Immunofluorescence staining showed that the expression of 8-OHdG at the injury site significantly increased at different time points after SCI, suggesting excessive ROS-induced oxidative stress leading to DNA damage (Fig. 7A and S11). While MN application did not reduce 8-OHdG expression, TPs-Mg MN group reversed this trend, indicating that TPs-Mg MN effectively reduced oxidative stress at the injury site, alleviating cellular dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis. INOS and CD163 were used as markers of M1 and M2 macrophages respectively to further detect the infiltration of macrophages at different time points after injury. As shown in Fig. 7B and S12A, abundant iNOS-positive M1 macrophages infiltrated the injury site, particularly in the SCI group, indicating significant macrophage recruitment during the early stages of injury.

Using CD163 to label M2 macrophages, we observed sparse CD163-positive M2 macrophage expression in the SCI group, where M1 macrophages rarely transitioned to M2 macrophages (Fig. 7D and S12B-E). Similarly, Chen et al. reported that bilayer multifunctional hydrogel scaffolds restored the hydration barrier of wound skin and modulated inflammation through macrophage polarization [32]. Inflammation regulation is critical for tissue repair, as macrophages secrete vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), essential for angiogenesis to supply oxygen and nutrients to regenerating tissue. Additionally, macrophages release various growth factors and cytokines that stimulate the proliferation and differentiation of endogenous stem and progenitor cells, further promoting tissue repair and regeneration.

TPs-Mg MN promote the migration of endogenous NSCs and nerve regeneration after SCI in vivo

The migration and differentiation of endogenous stem cells are essential for replacing lost neural cells at injury sites [14,[33], [34], [35]]. However, the pathological microenvironment following SCI is highly complex, with intense oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory responses severely limiting neural tissue repair. Studies have demonstrated that, in the natural SCI microenvironment, stem cells tend to differentiate into astrocytes rather than neurons [[36], [37], [38]]. Preliminary experiments revealed that TPs-Mg NPs exhibit significant anti-inflammatory properties. Building on macroscopic behavioral results, we employed tissue immunofluorescence staining to investigate the mechanisms underlying neural repair and reconstruction.

Nestin, a marker for NSCs, was used to observe the distribution of these cells at the injury site. Immunofluorescence results (Fig. 9A) showed that Nestin-positive NSCs were present across all groups at the injury site. In the sham group, these cells were evenly distributed throughout the spinal cord, reflecting their physiological expression under normal conditions. After SCI, the number of Nestin-positive NSCs increased significantly at the injury site in all groups, likely due to the recruitment of stem cells from the spinal cord ependymal zone. However, the expression of NSCs at the injury site did not show significant differences between groups in terms of fluorescence intensity. Evidence suggests that the inflammatory microenvironment post-injury promotes stem cell differentiation into astrocytes rather than neurons, while intense inflammation suppresses cell proliferation, adversely affecting tissue repair. Our results indicated that TPs-Mg MN exhibited anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects in vivo, improving the local microenvironment and enhancing the efficiency of stem cell differentiation into neurons. TPs-Mg NPs MN facilitate angiogenesis and axonal repair after SCI in rats in vivo

The inflammatory microenvironment at the injury site has multifaceted negative effects on the vascular system, primarily through the induction of endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT). This process transforms endothelial cells into fibroblast-like phenotypes, thereby exacerbating vascular degeneration [39,40]. Moreover, persistent hypoxia, resulting from vascular disruption and impaired blood flow, creates a vicious cycle that amplifies inflammation and hinders vascular repair. This hostile environment significantly limits vascular regeneration, impairs nutrient delivery and waste clearance, and ultimately reduces the capacity for neural repair. CD31, a specific marker for vascular endothelial cells, serves as an indicator of vascular density and integrity, aiding in the evaluation of vascular network damage and repair. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), one of the most critical growth factors in angiogenesis, reflects the activity of angiogenic signaling pathways following SCI. VEGF not only facilitates endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation but also inhibits apoptosis and enhances vascular stability. Blood vessels are not merely passive conduits for the transport of oxygen and nutrients to fulfill the metabolic demands of neurons. Beyond this essential function, the vasculature plays an active role in maintaining neuro-homeostasis by secreting a range of bioactive molecules, such as platelet-derived growth factors, which modulate neuronal activity and influence neurotransmitter release [[41], [42], [43]].

Using immunofluorescence staining, we examined the effects of different treatments on vascular regeneration at the injury site. Fig. 10A highlights variations in CD31 and VEGF expression across experimental groups. In the sham group, stable expression of CD31 and VEGF in spinal cord tissue reflects a homeostatic vascular network, ensuring efficient oxygen and nutrient delivery. Following SCI, the expression of CD31 and VEGF markedly declined at the injury site, indicating vascular damage and dysfunction driven by the inflammatory microenvironment. VEGF reduction further exacerbates local hypoxia, impairing the tissue's intrinsic repair mechanisms. In the MN group, CD31 and VEGF expression levels showed no significant improvement compared to the SCI group, underscoring the limited efficacy of scaffold materials alone in addressing the multifactorial challenges of SCI, such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and extracellular matrix degradation. However, the combined therapy involving TPs-Mg MN yielded significantly better outcomes than other treatments. This enhancement can be attributed to the synergistic effects of the therapy: TPs-Mg NPs improved the local microenvironment by promoting endothelial cell survival and upregulating VEGF expression, while MN provided structural support conducive to neural regeneration. Together, these factors established a robust physiological foundation for spinal cord functional recovery.

To further assess the impact of TPs-Mg MN on axonal regeneration and synaptic communication, immunofluorescence staining was conducted using specific markers NF-200 and Syn [[44], [45], [46]]. The results (Fig. 10B) revealed significantly higher fluorescence intensity of NF-200 and Syn in the TPs-Mg MN group compared to the SCI and MN groups. In the SCI group, the inflammatory microenvironment limited NF-200 and Syn expression, which was predominantly localized to the periphery of the injury site, failing to form a central “relay station” for signal transmission. While the MN group showed a modest increase in NF-200 and Syn expression relative to the SCI group, the improvement lacked statistical significance. Thus, although MN did not substantially modify the SCI environment, they functioned as an effective carrier, amplifying the physiological benefits of TPs-Mg NPs.

In vivo evaluation of histocompatibility

To evaluate the potential cytotoxic effects of TPs-Mg MN implanted in the spinal cord, histological staining was performed on the major organs of each experimental group at 4 weeks and 8 weeks, with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining results (Fig. 11A and Fig. S13) revealing no significant pathological changes or toxic manifestations in major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, across the Sham, SCI, MN, and TPs-Mg MN groups. Specifically, myocardial fibers in all groups were arranged normally, without evidence of inflammatory cell infiltration, necrosis, or fibrosis; liver tissue displayed orderly hepatic cord distribution and clear lobular structures, with no signs of degeneration, necrosis, or inflammatory infiltration; spleen histology showed intact lymphoid follicles with well-defined red and white pulp boundaries, devoid of congestion or fibrosis; lung sections exhibited normal alveolar structures without interstitial thickening or inflammatory infiltration; and renal histology demonstrated intact glomerular and tubular structures, without dilation, necrosis, or interstitial fibrosis. These observations indicate that MN and TPs-Mg MN treatments did not induce detectable toxicity in major organs, and histological comparisons revealed no significant differences between the treatment groups and the Sham or SCI groups. The results of rat serology also demonstrated good histocompatibility (Fig. 11B). The results highlight the excellent biocompatibility and safety profile of MN and TPs-Mg MN, providing critical preliminary toxicological evidence for their therapeutic potential while laying the groundwork for exploring the added therapeutic advantages of TPs-Mg MN over MN alone. Collectively, these findings contribute to the development of low-toxicity, high-efficacy therapeutic agents and offer valuable insights into constructing safe and effective treatment platforms.

Conclusion

In this study, the TPs-Mg MN appears to be a potential therapeutic strategy for SCI repair. Through the synergistic effects of TPs-Mg NPs, this composite scaffold may help mitigate oxidative stress, modulate inflammatory responses, and support neuronal regeneration. The results suggest improvements in motor function, tissue repair, and neuroprotection, indicating its possible utility as a multifunctional platform for SCI. Furthermore, the TPs-Mg MN shows favorable biocompatibility and low toxicity in this study, which may be relevant for future translational research. This study provides a preliminary basis for further investigations into regenerative medicine for SCI.
