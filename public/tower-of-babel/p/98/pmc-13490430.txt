Microplastic contamination in tadpoles (Anura) in the Brazilian Amazon
Authors: Fabrielle Barbosa de Araújo, Maria Luiza Cunha e Souza-Ferreira, Winston Asafe Santos da Rocha Sepaul, André Mourão Batista, Anderson Zanardi Freitas, Niklaus Ursus Wetter, Rosildo Santos Paiva, Verônica Regina Lobato de Oliveira-Bahia, Rossineide Martins da Rocha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The potential risks posed by microplastics (MPs) to anuran tadpoles remain poorly studied in the Brazilian Amazon region. Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyze MP contamination in Scinax x-signatus tadpoles at two developmental stages, as well as to characterize and quantify MPs. In addition, we evaluated MP concentrations in surface water and their relationship with environmental characteristics. The study was conducted at the Gunma Ecological Park, Santa Bárbara, Pará, Brazil, across five sampling sites during April 2025. We identified MP contamination in both surface water and tadpoles from all sampled sites. Environmental characteristics showed no direct relationship with MP contamination in tadpoles. However, tadpoles in the pre-metamorphic stage exhibited higher levels of MP contamination than those in the pro-metamorphic stage. A correlation was observed between tadpole body weight (g) and MP concentration, indicating that MP ingestion decreased as body weight increased. This study is the first to report in situ MP contamination in anuran tadpoles from the Amazon region, demonstrating the susceptibility of these organisms to contamination by this pollutant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44742-8.

Introduction

Currently plastic consumption has grown exponentially, with more than 430 million tons produced annually1,2. Due to its low cost, versatility, and high durability, plastic is widely used in various applications, including food packaging, household appliances, medical instruments, and civil construction3,4. This increase in production, combined with improper disposal, has become a significant environmental concern, as the degradation of these materials results in the formation of smaller plastic particles5,6.

Microplastics (MPs) are polymers that range in size from 5 mm to 1 μm and exhibit a wide variety of shapes and sizes7. They may originate from different sources: primary MPs are intentionally manufactured at these sizes by industries, whereas secondary MPs result from the fragmentation of larger plastics due to physical, chemical, and biological processes such as photo-oxidation, mechanical stress, and environmental weathering8,9.

MPs are easily dispersed in the environment, reaching both aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems10,11. Freshwater ecosystems are considered major pathways for MPs entering marine environments12. Although research on MPs in freshwater systems has increased substantially in recent years, important knowledge gaps persist, particularly regarding small lentic water bodies in tropical regions and studies assessing in situ contamination of resident species13,14. These temporary environments harbor a high diversity of organisms that may be vulnerable to MP exposure due to the widespread distribution and persistence of this pollutant, potentially affecting organisms ranging from small invertebrates15 to large mammals16,17.

Despite recent advances in research conducted in the Amazon region, studies on MPs have primarily focused on analyses of water, sediments, invertebrates, and fish18, leaving a knowledge gap regarding how other organisms are affected. Most research on MPs in Amazonian fauna demonstrates the presence of this contaminant in the digestive systems of animals, suggesting its ubiquity in the region and highlighting the need to expand studies on anurans and tadpoles in the Amazon18,19.

Belonging to the class Amphibia, anurans are frequently used as model organisms in ecotoxicological studies due to their sensitivity to environmental changes20, making them excellent bioindicators and biomonitors of environmental quality. Anurans play an essential role in connecting aquatic and terrestrial habitats due to their biphasic life cycle21. Freshwater habitats (rivers and ponds) serve as nurseries where most anuran species deposit their eggs, which may be directly or indirectly contaminated by MPs, especially in areas close to urban centers22,23. During the early stages of development (pre-metamorphosis), anuran tadpoles exhibit intense feeding activity, making them particularly vulnerable to the ingestion of MPs present in the water24.

Studies indicate that tadpole ecomorphotypes significantly influence diet composition25, as they are associated with the use of specific microhabitats that differ in food availability26. Tadpoles that inhabit the water column are classified as nektonic and typically feed by filtering suspended material from the water27,28. These organisms play crucial ecological roles within their microhabitats27, contributing to the control of algal biomass and decomposing organisms, which may occasionally be mistaken for MPs due to similarities in shape and size24,29. By occupying a microhabitat directly exposed to MP fluxes, nektonic tadpoles become a priority group for investigating the ingestion of this contaminant.

MPs can accumulate in tadpole organs through direct exposure via the respiratory system (gills and integument) as well as through feeding30,31, causing morphological, mutagenic, and cytotoxic alterations that compromise tadpole health and development32. Evidence in the literature indicates that the quantity and characteristics of ingested MPs, such as particle size, may vary according to developmental stage and feeding behavior14,33, suggesting that the occurrence of MPs in tadpoles is shaped by the interaction between environmental conditions and intrinsic biological traits14.

In this context, Scinax x-signatus34, belonging to the family Hylidae35, was selected as the study model. This species is widely distributed throughout South America and occurs both at forest edges and in urban environments36. The tadpoles of S. x-signatus are classified as nektonic, with an elliptical body in dorsal view and a triangular-depressed body in lateral view. They have lateral eyes and an anteroventral oral apparatus, with ventrolateral marginal papillae and a 2(2)/3(1) dental formula. The dorsal fin originates at the mid-body37,38. Their diet consists mainly of algae, protozoa, fungi, and eggs, with other items consumed in smaller amounts39.

Considering the ecological importance of tadpoles and their vulnerability to MPs contamination, as well as the scarcity of studies on this topic in the Amazon region, this study aimed to investigate MP contamination in S. x-signatus tadpoles at two developmental stages (pre- and pro-metamorphosis), in order to characterize and quantify the particles. Additionally, we evaluated MPs concentrations in the surface water of the sampled water bodies and analyzed their relationship with environmental variables. We specifically tested whether the amount of MPs differs between the two larval developmental stages. This study contributes to filling knowledge gaps regarding MP contamination in anuran larvae in the Amazon, highlighting the susceptibility of nektonic organisms inhabiting small freshwater bodies to the exposure and ingestion of this pollutant.

Results

Quality control and contamination assessment

Particles observed in contamination control samples consisted of transparent, blue, black, and pink fibers, corresponding to less than 1% per sample. Only fibers were recorded in tadpole samples, including 12 transparent, two blue, and one black fiber, while water samples contained five transparent fibers and one pink fiber. Therefore, contamination was considered negligible and was not subtracted from the results of the present study.

Physical characterization and contamination by MPs in the environment

Based on physical and environmental characteristics, five distinct sampling sites were identified. All sites corresponded to natural water bodies formed by the accumulation of rainwater in soil depressions. These environments are characterized as temporary ponds that play an important ecological role as habitats for aquatic invertebrates and other associated organisms, as well as serving as breeding sites and larval development areas for anurans27.

Sampling site 1 (S1) was narrow and elongated, presenting the smallest width among sampled sites, with low vegetation along the margins and some leaf litter within the water body (Figure S2a). Sampling site 2 (S2) exhibited the highest forest canopy openness compared to the other sites, approximately 96.03%, and contained small grasses within the water body (Figure S2b). Sampling site 3 (S3) was the smallest in terms of length and depth and was surrounded by dense vegetation (Figure S2c). Sampling site 4 (S4) presented the second smallest size, sparse vegetation both around and within the water body, and the second highest canopy openness (Figure S2d). Finally, Sampling site 5 (S5) was the largest in length and depth, exhibited the lowest canopy openness, and was similar to S1 in terms of vegetation characteristics (Table 1) (Figure S2e).

Microplastics in tadpoles at different stages of development

A total of 100 tadpoles were analyzed, comprising 50 individuals at the pre-metamorphic stage and 50 at the pro-metamorphic stage. All sampled tadpoles showed contamination by microplastics (MPs), totaling 933 particles (9.32 ± 7.3 MP/tadpole). Of these, 565 particles (11.3 ± 8 MP/tadpole) were recorded in individuals at the pre-metamorphic stage and 368 particles (7.3 ± 6 MP/tadpole) at the pro-metamorphic stage (Fig. 3a). Total MP concentration differed significantly between developmental stages (W = 1621.5; p < 0.01), indicating higher contamination in pre-metamorphic tadpoles.

Fibers were the predominant morphotype (87.8%) (Fig. 2d) regardless of site or developmental stage, followed by fragments (12%) and a negligible proportion of spheres (0.2%) (Table 2). MPs colors included transparent, blue (Fig. 2ef), black, green, red, pink, and yellow (Table 2), in decreasing order of abundance, with sizes ranging from 10.75 μm to 6066.7 μm, and no significant difference in ingested MP size between stages of development (W = 92.471; p = 0.3736).

Discussion

Our study was the first to record contamination by MPs in anuran tadpoles in situ in the Amazon region. MPs were detected at all sampled sites, indicating widespread contamination across the study area. Based on water analyses, the overall MP concentration was 0.231 ± 0.101 MPs/mL, demonstrating considerably higher contamination levels compared to previous studies. For example, surface waters from small water bodies in different functional zones of the Greater Hangzhou Bay, China, showed concentrations of 1.27 ± 0.306 MPs/L33, while a study conducted in Turkey investigating MPs in tadpoles and adults of Pelophylax spp. reported concentrations ranging from 0 to 7.20 ± 1.06 MPs/L41. The substantially higher concentrations observed in the present study may be associated with local anthropogenic pressures. Although the area is characterized by low population density, the presence of small local communities performing daily domestic activities, combined with the absence of basic sanitation infrastructure, likely contributes to the continuous input of MPs into adjacent water bodies through domestic effluents and improper waste disposal42,43.

Fibers and fragments were the predominant forms found in the water samples, mainly in transparent, blue, and black colors. This pattern is consistent with the literature, which reports the predominance of these morphologies in studies involving different groups of amphibians, including anurans, suggesting a recurring trend in the composition of MPs in freshwater environments44,45. These results are similar to those reported in a literature review study on plastic pollution conducted in the Amazon Basin, which described a predominance of blue and transparent fibers and fragments originating from sanitary sewage and fishing activities42. Although contamination of rivers and estuaries in this region is well documented, small water bodies are the most numerous freshwater environments globally and are subject to all types of threats that affect larger water bodies46. However, small water bodies are still poorly studied and may be even more contaminated by MPs. The presence of MPs (fibers and fragments) in the present study may be associated both with long-distance terrestrial runoff and with the discharge of domestic effluents from the small local community, originating from clothes washing, which releases millions of fibers per wash47,48.

The polymers identified in the present study are used in various industrial applications due to their low production cost and are often modified with additives (e.g., Bisphenol A)49,50. Among the most abundant polymers in our samples, polyester (17%) is widely used in clothing manufacturing as a synthetic fiber and is a concerning material due to its high durability, which hinders its degradation in the environment and allows bioaccumulation in terrestrial51 and aquatic organisms52, including tadpoles, as demonstrated in the present study. However, we did not assess the physiological effects of this accumulation on these animals.

In S. x-signatus from the present study, contamination ranged from 2.3 to 1083 MP/g, values higher than those observed in tadpoles of Rana limnochari, which ranged from 0 to 168.48 MP/g52 and in tadpoles of Pelophylax ridibundus, which recorded values between 302.62 and 306.69 MP/g53. It is noteworthy that the contamination observed in this study also exceeds the values reported for adult anurans, such as Ranidae species (Pelophylax spp.), which presented an average of 3.17 MPs per individual44, indicating a considerably lower load than the mean recorded for the tadpoles in the present study (9.32 ± 7.3 MPs/tadpole). Furthermore, the values reported here exceed those recorded in other freshwater organisms, such as shrimp54 and fish11, suggesting that tadpoles may represent one of the groups most exposed to MP contamination in these environments.

At site S1, which had the lowest MP concentrations, tadpoles showed higher contamination, whereas at sites S2 and S3, which had higher MP concentrations, tadpoles were less contaminated. This result may be explained by ecological and behavioral factors of these animals, as they are non-selective in their foraging strategy55, and ingest plastic particles along with food present in the water column. Although our results did not reveal a significant relationship between water contamination and tadpole contamination, these organisms may still be ingesting MPs suspended in the water column, reflecting the association between microhabitat and the nektonic lifestyle of the species14.

A considerable percentage of dyes was identified by Raman spectroscopy (23%) in tadpole samples from the present study, most of which are non-biodegradable, mutagenic, carcinogenic, and hazardous to aquatic life56,57. Indigo blue dye, detected in our samples, is used to color cotton fibers (such as denim fabrics) and has been frequently reported in studies on MP contamination58,59. The combination of polymers with pigments such as copper-based phthalocyanines (Mortoperm Blue) was also observed, all of which may act as stressors and represent an additional source of toxicity in aquatic environments for tadpoles60.

We observed that all tadpoles ingested MPs regardless of food resource availability at the different sampled sites. In addition, biomass availability did not significantly influence the number of MPs per individual, indicating that MP ingestion was not associated with local food resource variation. The available biomass identified at the studied sites corroborates studies on tadpole feeding ecology, which is mainly composed of algae, eggs, microcrustaceans, and nematodes39,61. Food intake occurs randomly, reflecting the availability and distribution of resources in the microhabitats they use26, S. x-signatus are filter-feeding animals that filter large volumes of water to obtain food, consequently increasing the probability of consuming MPs62. Therefore, these individuals are more susceptible to ingesting a wide variety of non-food particles, such as MPs, which are in the same size range as plankton and can be easily mistaken for it29.

In the present study, we did not observe a significant relationship between tadpole body size and MP concentration in water bodies. This result may be related to the growth dilution effect, in which a proportional increase in tadpole body size relative to MP concentration results in a lower amount of contaminant per individual63. This result was confirmed by the correlation between tadpole weight and MP concentration, in which higher tadpole weight was associated with lower MP quantities, corroborating results found in tadpoles of Rana limnochari and Microhyla ornata by Hu et al. (2022)33.

In addition, we observed that progression in developmental stage and increased body mass of tadpoles is associated with a significant reduction in the number of MPs per individual, indicating a decrease in MP quantity at later stages. A factor that may be associated with higher MP concentrations during the pre-metamorphic phase is the increased feeding intensity of tadpoles, as during this stage they need to accumulate sufficient energy and body mass to undergo metamorphosis28. With increased food intake and their mode of resource acquisition in the water column, tadpoles become more vulnerable to the accidental ingestion of plastic particles dispersed in the water39,61.

Thus, we verified that small freshwater bodies located near an ecological reserve under anthropogenic influence from a small local community are contaminated by MPs, as are the tadpoles. During pre-metamorphosis, tadpoles ingest more MPs due to intense feeding activity required to undergo metamorphosis. We also observed a correlation between body mass, developmental stage progression, and MP quantity, in which larger tadpoles in the pro-metamorphic stage are less contaminated by MPs.

Methods

Sampling area

Sampling was conducted at the Gunma Ecological Park (PEG), located in the municipality of Santa Bárbara, northeastern Pará State, Brazil (01° 13 ’00.86” S; 48° 17’ 41.18” W) (Figure S1). PEG is subject to anthropogenic activity due to a small surrounding community and covers an area of approximately 540 ha, with a predominant vegetation of dense ombrophilous forest on firm ground, as well as secondary forests, igapó forests, and floodplains, under a humid tropical climate64.

Sampling was carried out in April 2025, corresponding to the Amazonian rainy season and the reproductive period of most anuran species in the region. After mapping the study area, five sampling points (Figure S1) were randomly selected, corresponding to water bodies containing tadpoles at different developmental stages, maintaining a minimum distance of 50 m between sampling points. All points were georeferenced using GPS (SIRGAS 2000), photographed (Figure S2), and characterized in terms of abiotic factors, including temperature (°C) and pH, measured using a multiparameter meter (ITT-32430). In addition, length (m), width (m), and mean depth (cm) were measured, and forest canopy openness (%) was estimated using a densiometer65. Surface area (m2) was estimated by multiplying the maximum length by the maximum width of each water body (Table 1).

Sample collection

To obtain water samples, 250 mL of surface water (0–10 cm) was collected at four distinct points within each water body using glass bottles with aluminum caps, totaling 1 L of sample per water body (adapted from Hu et al., 2022)33. For phytoplankton density analysis, 50 mL of surface water was collected from each sampling point and fixed with 4% formalin. In the laboratory, a 5 mL aliquot from each sample was transferred to a Utermöhl chamber, stained with Rose Bengal dye, and analyzed by scanning the entire chamber using an inverted microscope (Nikon Eclipse TS100). Organisms were quantified and identified to the lowest possible taxonomic level and subsequently used to calculate organisms per liter in order to estimate the available biomass at each site (adapted from Paiva et al., 2006)66.

For biological samples, 100 S. x-signatus tadpoles were collected, with 20 individuals per water body (10 at pre-metamorphosis and 10 at pro-metamorphosis), using stainless steel sieves. Tadpoles were then placed in glass containers with aluminum caps previously washed with bi-filtered distilled water. In the field, tadpoles were anesthetized and euthanized using topical lidocaine (0.2%), weighed to determine total mass (g) using a digital balance (precision 0.001 g), and measured for body length (body excluding tail) (mm) using a digital caliper (precision 0.05 mm).

Developmental stages were classified according to the morphological characteristics described by Gosner (1960)37: stages 25–30 (pre-metamorphosis) and 31–41 (pro-metamorphosis). After sorting, all tadpoles were rinsed with bi-filtered distilled water, individually wrapped in aluminum foil envelopes, stored in glass containers, and transported to the Multidisciplinary Laboratory of Animal Morphophysiology at the Federal University of Pará. Until processing, samples were stored in a freezer at an average temperature of −18 °C.

Sample processing

MP extraction from water samples was adapted from Tatlı et al. (2025)41. At each sampling site, four independent water replicates of 250 mL were collected, totaling 1 L of water per site. Each 250 mL sample was sieved separately using a stainless steel sieve with a 75 μm mesh. The retained material was transferred to a glass beaker containing 150 mL of 30% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and incubated at 50 °C for 48 h to digest organic matter. After digestion, samples were filtered onto ester membrane filters with 0.2 μm pore size and stored in glass Petri dishes for subsequent analysis.

MP extraction from tadpoles was adapted from Balestrieri et al. (2022)24. Tadpoles were placed in 10 mL vials containing H₂O₂ (20%), covered with aluminum foil, and incubated at 50 °C for a maximum of 72 h. After complete digestion, samples were filtered and stored following the same procedure described for water samples.

Analysis and characterization of microplastics

Visual analysis and particle characterization followed the method described by4. Each filter was analyzed using a systematic scanning approach covering the entire filter area with the aid of a Leica M205C stereomicroscope at 3.2x magnification, coupled to a Flexacam C3 camera and equipped with LAS X software for image acquisition and morphometric measurements. Particles were described according to color (blue, red, black, pink, green, and transparent), shape (fiber, fragment, and sphere), and size.

For sample validation, 12% of the particles recorded in both tadpoles and water were subjected to Raman spectroscopy, providing a representative subsample to estimate contamination proportions while balancing analytical feasibility given the large volume of material. In addition, particles were randomly selected within the main morphotypes identified, ensuring that the subsample reflected the full diversity observed. Raman spectroscopy was used to identify polymer types and possible additives67, using a LabRAM HR Evolution system (HORIBA) equipped with lasers of different wavelengths (473 nm, 532 nm, 633 nm, and 785 nm) and a 50× long-range objective (NA = 0.7).

The resistance of each material was tested at different laser power levels to obtain the best spectral signal without damaging the material. An initial spectrometer position was used to observe peaks in the polymer identification (fingerprint) region around 1600 cm⁻¹. For final spectral acquisition, the spectral range from 200 cm⁻¹ to 3200 cm⁻¹ was used, allowing observation of the entire polymer fingerprint region and the C–H stretching region.

Baseline correction and noise filtering were applied using tools within the micro-Raman software (Labspec) or custom Matlab® code. Spectral compounds were identified using the KnowItAll® database, which provides a Hit Quality Index (HQI) for each spectral comparison. HQI values were recorded and are presented in Fig. 2 for transparency. However, polymer identification was not based solely on a fixed numerical threshold. Instead, assignments were validated through point-by-point spectral comparison, prioritizing the matching of key diagnostic peaks (characteristic functional groups), since overall similarity scores may be influenced by baseline noise or matrix interference.

Quality control criteria

To minimize potential cross-contamination, measures recommended by68, were adopted and adapted throughout all stages of the study, including collection, sorting, processing, and sample analysis. The distilled water used was filtered twice through ester membrane filters with 0.2 μm pore size and applied both for equipment cleaning and solution preparation (H₂O₂). In addition, all solutions used, including H₂O₂ and 70% ethanol, were also filtered. All materials employed were made of glass or aluminum, and 100% cotton lab coats were worn to avoid the release of synthetic fibers and sample contamination.

Personnel movement within the laboratory was restricted during filtration processing and visual analysis of samples. Additionally, containers filled with bi-filtered water were individually exposed during collection, sorting, and processing, serving as atmospheric contamination controls for each stage; these controls were subsequently filtered and analyzed in the same manner as the other samples. If particles were detected in these controls, the corresponding number was subtracted from the MP quantification of processed samples, considering particle characteristics such as color and shape68.

To ensure reliability in MP identification, inclusion and exclusion criteria proposed by69 were applied. Suspected particles were subjected to the hot needle test, in which contact with a heated tip causes softening and adhesion of the material, a characteristic response of synthetic polymers70. Particles resembling undigested animal remains (e.g., wings or antennae) were not counted in the MP analysis, nor were non-shiny particles or segmented fibers. All identified particles were compared in terms of morphotype and coloration with descriptions available in the literature that confirmed polymer type71,72.

Data analysis

To assess whether MP concentrations differed among sampled water bodies, data were initially tested for normality and homoscedasticity using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. As assumptions were met, data were analyzed using one-way Analysis of Variance (one-way ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test. To evaluate whether the mean number of MPs (absolute counts) in tadpoles differed among localities, data that did not meet assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were analyzed using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni-adjusted p-values. For the same dataset, differences in MP concentration (absolute counts) between developmental stages (pre-metamorphosis and post-metamorphosis) were evaluated using the non-parametric Wilcoxon test. Data analyzed using non-parametric tests are presented as median (Md) and interquartile range (IQR). Additionally, to assess the effects of locality and developmental stage on the MP contamination index weighted by mass (number of MPs/mass (g)), data were log-transformed and, after meeting assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity, analyzed using two-way ANOVA with interaction between predictors; when significant differences among group means were detected, Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was applied. Canopy openness data met parametric assumptions and were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test for multiple comparisons among sampling points. To evaluate the effects of environmental variables, biomass availability, locality, developmental stage, mass (g), and length (mm) on the number of MPs per individual, data were analyzed using a Generalized Linear Model (GLM). Data distribution and multicollinearity were first assessed using Spearman’s correlation test. Several models were tested, and the Gamma distribution family showed the best fit based on residual analysis. Model selection followed the criterion of the lowest AIC value. All analyses were performed in the R environment (R version 4.4.2, 2025-10-31 ucrt) using the packages “rstatix”73 and “vegan”74. A significance level of 5% was adopted for all statistical analyses.
