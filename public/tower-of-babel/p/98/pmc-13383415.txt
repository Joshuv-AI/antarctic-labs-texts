Design, development and preliminary usability evaluation of “PhysioMaster”: adaptive gamified assessment tool for neurorehabilitation in undergraduate physiotherapy students

Background

Stroke is one of the leading causes of motor and physical disabilities worldwide, significantly impacting patients' quality of life. This condition affects an individual's motor function and can result in severe cognitive and emotional impairments. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 15 million people worldwide experience a stroke each year, and 5 million are left with varying degrees of disability [1]. Despite the importance of therapeutic interventions for stroke patients, teaching the required physiotherapy concepts and skills to students remains challenging, especially within specialized courses such as "Physiotherapy in neurological patients. Current teaching methods, which are often theory-based and non-interactive, typically fail to address the diverse educational needs of students. Factors such as redundancy and limited opportunities for practical skill acquisition reduce learning quality. Crucially, traditional approaches struggle to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and the complex clinical reasoning required to manage neurological patients, where decision-making must be both rapid and precise [2].

In recent years, leveraging modern educational technologies has emerged as a promising approach to overcoming these limitations. Previous research in health professions education has demonstrated that digital interventions ranging from mobile-based quizzes to virtual patient simulations can significantly reduce the 'theory–practice gap' by providing a risk-free environment for skill acquisition. One such strategy is gamification, defined as the application of game elements in non-game contexts [3, 7]. Gamification enhances the learning experience by integrating components such as point scoring, challenges, immediate feedback, leveling, competition, and leaderboards. These features make learning more engaging and effective. Additionally, numerous studies have shown that gamification enhances motivation, encourages active participation, and improves learning outcomes [8, 11].

Although there is growing evidence that gamification is beneficial in medical education by boosting motivation and improving knowledge acquisition [12, 16], its application in physiotherapy, and particularly in the field of neurorehabilitation, remains largely unexplored. Despite the proliferation of generic gamified platforms, there remains a critical shortage of domain-specific tools tailored to the intricate and evolving clinical scenarios of neurorehabilitation. Unlike general tools, neurorehabilitation requires a dynamic pedagogical bridge that allows students to navigate complex neurological assessments repeatedly. This lack of specialized digital frameworks hinders the transition from rote memorization to mastery-based clinical competence. To address this gap, the present study is positioned as an early-stage educational technology development study with a formative evaluation. It aims to design and develop "PhysioMaster" a gamified educational system for the undergraduate course ‘Physiotherapy in Neurological Patients’ and to perform a preliminary usability evaluation to refine its features.

Methods

Design and setting

A formative evaluation framework was employed, focusing on the iterative development and refinement of the tool based on expert and user feedback. The stakeholders included experts in physiotherapy, medical education, medical informatics, and undergraduate students. The study was conducted between 2023 and 2024 at Mashhad University of Medical Sciences in Iran. All participating experts were faculty members, and student participants were selected from the university's undergraduate physiotherapy program.

Theoretical and pedagogical framework

The development of PhysioMaster was guided by a structured pedagogical framework to ensure that gamification elements served specific instructional goals. The following theories were integrated into the system’s design:

The principles of mastery learning were operationalized through the implementation of immediate feedback and scoring systems that are directly linked to predefined competency thresholds. This approach ensures that students achieve a specific level of clinical understanding before they are permitted to advance to more complex modules, thereby preventing the accumulation of knowledge gaps in foundational neurorehabilitation concepts [17, 18].

The core innovation of the application lies in the integration of retrieval practice and the spacing effect, which are facilitated through an advanced adaptive reassessment mechanism. Specifically, a weighted error-tracking algorithm was utilized to identify individual knowledge gaps within clinical scenarios. By systematically reintroducing these specific challenges at optimal intervals, the system promotes active recall and enhances long-term memory retention of complex diagnostic procedures [19, 20].

Study phases

The design and development process were implemented in five phases:

Exploratory literature review

To establish a robust evidence-based foundation for PhysioMaster, a systematic exploratory review was conducted. This phase followed a predefined protocol based on the PICo framework (Population: physiotherapy students/professionals; Interest: gamification/GBL; Context: physiotherapy education). A comprehensive search was performed across PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science using structured Boolean strings. Studies were screened against specific inclusion criteria focusing on digital GBL tools and pedagogical outcomes. Methodological quality was independently assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) to ensure the reliability of the identified features. The core findings from this review—specifically regarding gamification elements (e.g., feedback, scoring) and implementation challenges—directly informed the design and development phases of the PhysioMaster application. This approach ensured that the platform aligns with evidence-based pedagogical principles and addresses the practical requirements of the field.

Needs analysis

Participants were selected using a purposive sampling strategy, including four physiotherapy faculty members and seven undergraduate students to ensure a diverse range of perspectives. Two focus group sessions, each lasting approximately 60 to 90 min, were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide. These sessions were moderated by F.K., a researcher who facilitated the discussions to ensure balanced participation, while the overall qualitative process was overseen by S.A., an expert in qualitative methodology. Data were analysed using a thematic analysis approach, where audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and subjected to a multi-stage coding process to identify recurring themes. To capture the divergence in perspectives, a constant comparative method was employed during the analysis to systematically contrast the pedagogical expectations of faculty members against the perceived learning barriers of students.

The team identified distinct viewpoints by comparing the independent coding sets from both participant groups, which revealed a significant gap between the clinical complexity demanded by experts and the level of instructional support required by students. These requirements were then translated into the technical specifications of the PhysioMaster system. For instance, the identified need for interactive clinical reasoning was mapped to the development of the 'Scenario' module, while the requirement for sustained engagement informed the design of the 'Leaderboard' and scoring system. By synthesizing these differing viewpoints through the expert guidance of S.A., the application's final architecture was refined to ensure it maintained high academic standards while remaining accessible to undergraduate learners.

Content development and evaluation

The educational content was developed and divided into text-based and video-based content. Videos were produced in the clinical studio at Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, and used a simulated patient. Finally, the content was reviewed by four physiotherapists and seven students to evaluate its alignment with learning objectives, clarity, and efficacy. A 5-point Likert scale (ranging from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree) was used for this evaluation. To ensure content validity, the agreement among reviewers was assessed. Only items that achieved a consensus of over 75% in the "Agree" and "Strongly Agree" categories were retained, ensuring that the educational material was formally approved for clarity and pedagogical alignment before being integrated into the system.

System development and evaluation

This final phase consisted of the system 's design, functional evaluation, usability testing, and data analysis.

System design

Based on the prior stages, the elements of the gamified system were identified. The system was designed to deliver course content while leveraging gamification techniques to enhance engagement. Two main panels were featured: an admin panel for educators and a student panel. A prototype was designed using Figma and then passed over to the technical team for development.

Functional evaluation

Following development, the system was used by the research team and selected students, and their experiences and challenges were documented. The results were analyzed, and a report of strengths, weaknesses, and recommendations for improvement was generated.

To ensure transparency, PhysioMaster was developed using a structured logic where the adaptive reassessment is governed by a weighted error-tracking algorithm. This logic prioritizes re-introducing clinical scenarios that received 'incorrect' or 'partially correct' status in the practice module within the next two assessment cycles. The development workflow followed an Agile-inspired methodology, transitioning from Figma prototypes to a functional React Native build, ensuring that pedagogical requirements directly dictated technical specifications.

Usability testing

A Think-Aloud evaluation was conducted to identify usability barriers and refine the user interface. Seven final-year physiotherapy students (four females, three males; aged 23–33) were recruited. This sample size was justified based on Nielsen’s usability standards, which suggest that five to eight participants are sufficient to identify over 80% of usability issues in formative evaluations [25]. While this sample size is primarily intended for identifying user interface barriers in a formative phase rather than establishing broad statistical generalizability, it provided sufficient qualitative saturation to ensure the tool's functional readiness for educational use.

All participants were digitally literate and had completed the theoretical neurorehabilitation course, ensuring the necessary clinical background. Each session lasted 45–60 min and was conducted in a quiet environment. Under the moderation of an HCI expert, participants performed seven standardized tasks: (1) account registration, (2) content navigation, (3) scenario interaction, (4) completing graded practice sessions, (5) completing a course exam, (6) community chat interaction, and (7) leaderboard interpretation.

Data analysis

The sessions were recorded and analyzed. Usability issues were identified and categorized based on Van Haak’s framework [26] into four types: layout, terminology, data entry, and comprehensiveness. The frequency and severity of each problem were then discussed with the technical team to prioritize resolutions.

Results

Educational needs and design requirements

The exploratory literature review identified key gamification elements essential for medical education apps, including immediate feedback, progress tracking, leaderboards, and leveled challenges. Furthermore, the review highlighted that integrating clinical scenarios and multimedia content (videos and animations) significantly supports the learning of practical skills in physiotherapy. These identified elements formed the foundational requirements for the design and development of the PhysioMaster system.

The "Physiotherapy in neurological patients" course includes both theoretical and practical parts. The practical part focuses on skill acquisition in patient positioning, neurological assessment, and rehabilitation interventions. Feedback from past student assessments highlighted significant challenges, particularly in practical skills and specific theoretical topics. The thematic analysis of focus group discussions further revealed three primary requirements: the need for interactive clinical case simulations, a centralized repository for high-quality instructional videos, and a competitive scoring system to maintain motivation. These qualitative findings directly informed the structural design of PhysioMaster, specifically leading to the creation of the Scenario and Leaderboard modules. The evaluation by physiotherapy experts (n = 4) confirmed the high relevance and necessity of the selected topics. Regarding the logical organization of topics, a consensus was reached with 50% (n = 2) rating it as 'Strongly Agree' and 50% (n = 2) as 'Agree'.

Implemented system architecture and features

PhysioMaster was developed as a Progressive Web App (PWA) using a React Native build, ensuring cross-platform responsiveness. The system architecture comprises two primary interfaces: an Educator Panel for content management, question bank creation, and real-time student progress tracking (Fig. 4), and a Student Panel focused on gamified learning (Fig. 5).

Key functionalities integrated into the student experience include structured educational modules, gamified practice sessions with immediate feedback, and timed exams. A core feature of the architecture is the adaptive mastery learning system, which utilizes a weighted error-tracking algorithm to reintroduce missed questions, thereby addressing individual knowledge gaps. Engagement is further supported through social features like a community chatroom and a dynamic leaderboard system.

Usability evaluation

The Think-Aloud sessions revealed eight key usability barriers. Most significant were comprehensive problems (e.g., lack of progress bars and search functions) which hindered navigation efficiency. Data entry and layout issues, while less critical, were identified as factors that could increase cognitive load. These findings, categorized using Van Haak’s framework, provided the basis for the final UI refinement.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide preliminary evidence that PhysioMaster is a functional and usable tool, specifically designed for neurorehabilitation physiotherapy education. Key results indicate that while the core pedagogical logic is highly relevant, the evaluation revealed significant differences between expert and student perceptions, particularly regarding content complexity and time sufficiency. This underscores the study's significance in identifying the cognitive gap between faculty expectations and student experiences within a digital neurorehabilitation environment.

The study's findings suggest that gamification offers a robust framework for designing educational content intended to support the quality of learning. Theoretically, the architecture of PhysioMaster is grounded in Self-Determination Theory (SDT), where mechanics such as points, leaderboards, and avatars are strategically implemented to satisfy the psychological needs for competence and autonomy. By aligning these elements with SDT constructs, the application aims to facilitate intrinsic motivation, ensuring that students remain engaged with complex medical curricula [27].

In recent years, various gamification-based apps have been developed for medical education. For example, apps such as Kahoot! known for their competitive and user-friendly design, have been used in physiotherapy education and have positively influenced student engagement and content retention [28]. While Kahoot! excels at group-based, synchronous competition, our pedagogical approach shifts the focus to individualized mastery, inspired by the principles in highly successful apps like Duolingo. A notable divergence was observed in the response distribution between experts and students. While experts clustered around strong agreement, student feedback was more varied, specifically regarding the simplicity and time sufficiency of the content. This suggests that certain clinical scenarios, despite being logically structured by experts, may impose a higher cognitive load on undergraduate learners than anticipated. From the perspective of Self-Determination Theory (SDT), this highlights the need for better 'scaffolding' by breaking down complex neurological assessments into smaller units to prevent learner frustration and maintain a sense of competence.

It is essential to clarify that during the initial evaluation, PhysioMaster was positioned as an advanced functional prototype rather than a finalized product. It functions as an adaptive gamified assessment and mastery tool rather than an immersive, narrative-driven game-based learning environment. Unlike generic platforms that often treat learning as a linear progression, our design introduces a non-linear feedback loop. This distinction allows the application to function as a longitudinal training companion by specifically tailoring mechanics to the iterative nature of clinical reasoning.

The practical implication of this 'error-driven re-assessment' is the creation of a safe environment for clinical trial-and-error. In traditional neurorehabilitation training, errors in patient assessment can have real-world consequences. PhysioMaster allows students to bridge this gap by refining their decision-making logic through repeated exposure to failed scenarios, thereby shifting the educational focus from simple recall to the development of clinical intuition. Our system achieves this through an adaptive algorithm where questions answered incorrectly during practice sessions are strategically reintroduced in subsequent exams. This 'error-driven re-assessment' ensures a deeper, personalized learning path. This approach aligns with evidence [29] suggesting that active cognitive engagement is significantly amplified when digital environments provide transdisciplinary thinking opportunities. Furthermore, integrating these elements within specialized fields like neurorehabilitation [30] supports the transition from theoretical knowledge to clinical reasoning. Also, in designing PhysioMaster, efforts were made to incorporate clinical scenarios, staged structures, and challenges to provide an experience beyond simple quizzes and to create a simulated environment conducive to interactive learning. Furthermore, this system was specifically tailored to local educational context and based on Iran's official physiotherapy curriculum and educational requirements. However, it is crucial to recognize the potential downsides of competitive gamification, particularly the leaderboard system. While rankings can drive engagement for high-performers, they may inadvertently create 'leaderboard demotivation' or increased anxiety for students who struggle with the content. To align theory with practice, our findings suggest that while competition drives some, a 'cooperative mode' or 'private mastery tracking' might be more effective for students struggling with complex neurological concepts. This implies that the 'one-size-fits-all' gamification approach should be replaced by flexible pedagogical settings that educators can tailor to the psychological profile of their cohort. This personalization is consistent with Self-Determination Theory, as it supports student autonomy by allowing for different engagement styles. By prioritizing individualized mastery over pure competition, the system helps maintain a balance between challenge and skill, preventing cognitive overload and ensuring that the gamified mechanics directly serve the pedagogical objective of clinical competence.

A critical finding of the formative evaluation was the identification of several core technical challenges during the prototype stage, such as server connectivity issues and scoring system defects. While these issues initially affected stability, their discovery was a deliberate outcome of our formative evaluation strategy. Rather than presenting a finalized product at that stage, we used these 'real-world' failures as a roadmap for complete technical refinement. By acknowledging these vulnerabilities at an advanced prototype stage, we were able to systematically resolve all critical and high-severity bugs before the final implementation. This iterative process ensured that the stable release of PhysioMaster achieved the necessary reliability for educational deployment. From a theory-to-practice perspective, the resolution of scoring defects and server issues is not merely a technical fix but a pedagogical necessity. According to Self-Determination Theory, 'competence' is a core driver of motivation; if a system fails to record points accurately, it directly undermines the student's sense of achievement and can lead to rapid disengagement. Ensuring technical stability in the final version was, therefore, the fundamental step in translating gamification theory into a reliable clinical training tool.

Previous studies have demonstrated that diversity in content delivery, including videos, images, and texts, can enhance the effectiveness of educational processes. Integrating these diverse formats supports various learning profiles and has the potential to improve academic performance in health sciences [31] aligning with the need for modern training tools in physical education [32]. Thus, PhysioMaster allows educators to upload various media types and benefits from personal profiles and performance tracking systems, facilitating active monitoring of student learning progress. While the present study focused on usability, the inclusion of gamification elements such as immediate feedback and scoring was based on previous literature [21, 33, 35] which suggests these features can increase motivation and learning effectiveness. Future research is needed to confirm if these outcomes are achieved with PhysioMaster. Immediate feedback after each question and final performance evaluation at the end of each section help continuous learning improvement and understanding of complex concepts. Compared to similar apps, PhysioMaster utilizes common gamification elements such as scoring and goal-setting while adapting itself to clinical scenarios in a way that aligns with the unique requirements of physiotherapy education, which are essential for practical training and clinical reasoning.

Another strength of this study is the participatory design approach, which involved active stakeholders, including faculty members and students, to ensure the final product aligned with real user needs. Moreover, conducting both functional and usability evaluations in the early stages of development facilitated the timely identification and resolution of potential problems, providing a useful model for future educational technology designs.

Reflecting on the design architecture, a primary advantage of PhysioMaster is its high pedagogical fidelity, ensuring that game mechanics do not overshadow clinical logic. However, a limitation is the current reliance on a fixed-rule algorithm for reassessment, which lacks the fluidity of AI-driven paths that could adjust difficulty based on real-time cognitive load. Additionally, the focus on individual mastery might overlook the development of collaborative decision-making skills essential in multi-disciplinary teams. Nevertheless, this study has certain constraints that should be considered. The small sample size (n = 7) in usability testing limits the generalizability of the findings to a broader population. Furthermore, as the primary objective was to optimize design and functionality, the actual educational effectiveness and learning outcomes of the system were not directly assessed. High usability scores, while promising, do not inherently guarantee improved clinical competence; therefore, future interventional studies with larger cohorts are needed to evaluate the system’s impact on learning outcomes compared to traditional methods. Finally, while this study focused on neurorehabilitation, investigating the potential of this model in other physiotherapy domains remains a subject for future research.

Novelty and scientific contribution

While standard gamification elements are widely used in educational systems, PhysioMaster introduces a unique integration of domain-specific adaptive reassessment that is tailored for neurorehabilitation education. Unlike generic apps, the innovation lies in a pedagogical logic where the system identifies individual knowledge gaps in complex clinical scenarios and dynamically reintroduces those specific challenges in future sessions. This ensures a transition from mere rote memorization to mastery-based clinical reasoning. By bridging the gap between theoretical neurological assessment and practical intervention through an iterative feedback loop, PhysioMaster represents a meaningful advancement over traditional, linear gamified systems.

Conclusion

This study presents the design, development, and preliminary evaluation of PhysioMaster, a domain-specific gamified application for neurorehabilitation education. The formative evaluation suggests that the tool is technically feasible and exhibits promising usability among a small cohort of undergraduate physiotherapy students. However, as this was a preliminary study with a limited sample size, the findings should be interpreted as an initial proof-of-concept rather than a definitive measure of educational impact. While the application is grounded in pedagogical principles, its actual effect on motivation and learning outcomes was not directly measured. Therefore, high usability scores at this stage do not imply guaranteed clinical competence. Future research with larger, controlled cohorts is necessary to rigorously evaluate the pedagogical effectiveness of PhysioMaster. At present, these findings establish a preliminary framework for exploring how gamified tools might serve as a supplementary resource in physiotherapy curricula.
