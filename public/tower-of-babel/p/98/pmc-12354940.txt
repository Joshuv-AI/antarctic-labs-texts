Exploring Clinicians' Suggestions for Addressing Discrimination Towards Children and Youth With Disabilities With Multiple Minoritized Identities
Authors: Sally Lindsay, Janice Phonepraseuth, Nicole Thomson, Jennifer N. Stinson, Sharon Smile
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundClinicians can help address the discrimination that children and youth with disabilities often encounter. However, they commonly report lacking training and experience in addressing multiple forms of discrimination, such as ableism and racism. A lack of knowledge could lead to stigma and inequities within healthcare. This study explores clinicians' suggestions for addressing multiple forms of discrimination among children and youth with disabilities.MethodsThis qualitative study involved in‐depth interviews with a purposive sample of 15 paediatric rehabilitation clinicians and community service providers working with disabled youth who have multiple minoritized identities. We applied an inductive thematic analysis to the interview transcripts.ResultsOur findings highlighted the following four themes: (1) disability awareness and anti‐ableism training and education; (2) enhancing inclusive programming, services and policies; (3) building connections to supports and resources; and (4) advocacy and incorporating lived experience perspectives.ConclusionThe results underscore the need for clinicians to engage in more training and to facilitate access to resources for multiply minoritized youth with disabilities. Dedicated funding, resources and commitment at organizational, systems and policy levels are needed to address discrimination.

Although policies exist to address discrimination towards people with disabilities, they continue to experience multiple forms of discrimination, such as ableism and racism (Fuentes et al. 2024). Ableism refers to disability‐related discrimination, including exploitative or abusive conduct against people with disabilities that stigmatizes or creates intimidating or offensive environments (Harder et al. 2019). Common types of ableism include inaccessible environments, physical barriers, negative attitudes, stigma and harassment (Lindsay, Fuentes, et al. 2023). Ableism and other forms of discrimination, such as racism, racist ableism and gender‐based discrimination, can lead to adverse health and social outcomes (Daley et al. 2018; Lloyd et al. 2019). Given that people with disabilities make up 22% of the global population, addressing the multiple forms of discrimination they experience is vital (Return on Disability Group 2024). The United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (Article 8) states that a change of perceptions is needed to address discrimination, prejudice and stereotypes experienced by people with disabilities, while promoting greater awareness of their abilities (United Nations 2006).

Until recently, the intersection of disability with other minoritized identities (e.g., race, gender) has largely been neglected, especially among children and youth with disabilities (Fuentes et al. 2024). Many existing models of disability overlook other aspects of identity and tend to categorize people with disabilities as one similar group (Brinkman et al. 2023). Of the limited recent research on this topic, it shows that multiply minoritized youth with disabilities experience higher rates of bullying, lower levels of community belonging and life satisfaction, and inequitable access to services and resources (Ali et al. 2001; Gage et al. 2021; Lindsay et al. 2022; Lindsay, Patel, et al. 2023). Intersectionality theory is relevant to this work because it focuses on understanding how multiple social identities, such as disability and race, intersect at the micro level of individual experiences to reflect interlocking systems of privilege and oppression (e.g., racism and ableism) at the organizational and societal level (Crenshaw 1991). This theory highlights how people who belong to more than one marginalized group may experience multiple forms of discrimination (Fuentes et al. 2024; Shaw et al. 2012). Critical race theory also emphasizes the need to examine how race overlaps with other social identities when discussing racism and focuses on intersectionality (Delgado and Stefancic 2023). Aligning with the goals of this study, critical race theory encourages learning from the experiences and perspectives of minoritized individuals and encourages activism to improve the circumstances of marginalized groups (Delgado and Stefancic 2023).

Recent systematic reviews exploring discrimination among people with disabilities highlight the urgent need for more effective strategies to minimize discrimination and enhance social inclusion. Exploring this topic is salient given the prevalence of discrimination towards children and youth with disabilities, especially among those who belong to minoritized groups (Fuentes et al. 2024; Lindsay and Dain 2024; Lindsay, Patel, et al. 2023; Lindsay et al. 2024). Clinicians and service providers can address the discrimination that their clients with disabilities encounter; however, several studies report that they lack training and knowledge about how to help (Chapman et al. 2013; Lindsay and Cancelliere 2018; Oslin et al. 2024).

Existing research on addressing discrimination among patients with disabilities mainly focuses on adults and often does not consider intersecting identities, especially the multiple forms of discrimination that minoritized children and youth with disabilities experience. Our study aimed to address an important literature gap by exploring clinicians' suggestions for addressing discrimination among multiply minoritized children and youth with disabilities. This is salient because children and youth, especially those with disabilities, often encounter higher rates of discrimination compared to adults (Harder et al. 2019). Our objective was to explore clinicians' recommendations for addressing discrimination that multiply minoritized children and youth with disabilities encounter.

Methods

Design and Sample

In‐depth individual interviews were conducted using a qualitative descriptive design (Colorafi and Evans 2016; Doyle et al. 2020). This approach explores multiple phenomena and their various aspects. We received ethics approval from our institutional Research Ethics Board (REB‐0533). The team used a purposive sample to recruit participants through invitation letters and advertisements sent to various relevant community disability organizations and a paediatric rehabilitation hospital in a large urban, multicultural Canadian city. A research co‐ordinator screened participants through a phone call or Zoom meeting using the following inclusion criteria: a clinician (i.e., service provider and educator) (1) with at least 1 year of experience working with children/youth with disabilities or a decision‐maker (i.e., role affecting policies or practices for children or youth with disabilities) and (2) who is able to communicate in English. The research team had no prior relationships or conflicts of interest with the participants. Participants who met the inclusion criteria read the study information letter and signed an e‐consent form via REDCap software.

Data Collection and Analysis

Two racially minoritized female interviewers (with academic backgrounds in sociology and rehabilitation sciences) conducted interviews using a semistructured guide. The interview guide was informed by systematic reviews conducted by our team, which was piloted with a paediatric clinician before conducting all interviews. Questions asked about suggestions for addressing discrimination (i.e., ableism, gender‐based discrimination and racism), removing or reducing barriers, required resources, and priorities for change (see Data S1).

Researchers conducted interviews through Zoom from January to August 2024, which lasted up to 72 min (average of 41 min). Interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed through Zoom, deidentified and verified for accuracy. Our research question guided the inductive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006). Two researchers read all the transcripts several times and each developed initial codes, while noting patterns around addressing discrimination. Next, we discussed, compared and revised the codes until we reached consensus on the final coding framework, which was applied to all transcripts in NVivo (version 12). The team agreed that thematic and code saturation were reached in this data (Hennink et al. 2017). We selected relevant quotes reflective of each theme and sub‐theme (Braun and Clarke 2006).

We optimized the trustworthiness of the findings by drawing on descriptive participant accounts and peer debriefing among the research team (Rolfe 2006). Team discussions about the codes and analysis helped address the credibility of the results. We documented decisions made during the analysis and team discussions (Rolfe 2006). Additionally, we used a consistent interview guide for all participants. We practised reflexivity and considered how our positions may have impacted the development of the themes (Rolfe 2006). Finally, we followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines (Tong et al. 2007).

Results

Participant Characteristics

Our sample involved 15 clinicians, including 13 women, 1 man and 1 nonbinary individual (see Table 1). Participants identified as belonging to the following racial categories: White, South Asian, East Asian, Black and Latin American, and one did not specify. Five participants had a disability, and three were parents of a child with a disability. Participants' occupations included community service providers, social workers, community‐based coordinators, occupational therapists, speech language pathologists, educational assistants and a nurse, all with experience in paediatric rehabilitation or community‐based organizations.

Theme 1. Disability Awareness and Anti‐ableism Training and Education

Need for Training

Most participants described their lack of knowledge and experience working with minoritized children with disabilities. They identified the need for specific disability awareness and anti‐ableism training and education. Clinicians provided suggestions regarding the context, timing and content of anti‐ableism training. A clinician shared: ‘people don't know what ableism is, and about discrimination amongst individuals with a disability … education can go a long way to helping people understand what some of these kids have challenges with and how we can help them’ (#5). Others emphasized the importance of additional and more effective anti‐ableism training in healthcare, education, employment and community‐based programming. Some clinicians witnessed racism and ableism in their practice and recognized the importance of more specific training on intersectional identities. For instance, one clinician recalled: ‘ have worked with racialized groups, with children with disabilities as well … if you had 2 children, that one was non‐racialized with a disability and another one racialized with the disability, I have seen them get treated differently and wondered if it was because of their race’ (#2).

Context and Content of Training

Participants described the context and content for disability awareness and anti‐ableism trainings. Clinicians recommended education on disabilities and other minoritized identities to help reduce multiple forms of discrimination; that education should start early on and continue throughout their career. Participants described that healthcare providers, educators and other service providers should receive appropriate training for working with minoritized disabled youth. A clinician described:

‘EDI training is often not child specific … they're not factoring in that children are living within the cultures of their parents … there could be different considerations to make about how they think about the way they're being treated by others that don't apply to adults who have lived with it for so long’ (#7).

Participants' recommendations included intersectional needs (e.g., language, race and culture), cultural sensitivity and antibullying strategies in the content of disability awareness and anti‐ableism training. A participant explained, ‘having more examples on how to provide care for these populations … would help to make me feel more confident’ (#10). Another clinician shared, ‘another piece to that education to help understand, if you have a cultural background, how to address that along with the disability piece’ (#5).

Twelve participants explained the importance of confronting biases and assumptions about multiply minoritized people with disabilities. They suggested having conversations, asking questions, and developing greater self‐awareness of internalized assumptions. To illustrate, a clinician described:

‘Intersectionality is super important to any approach … there could be better inclusion around race and gender diversity … Educate staff in a way that is inclusive to those individuals … when you have marginalized identities intersect, you're going to have layered barriers’ (#3).

Some providers recommended offering additional and more specific cultural sensitivity training focusing on its intersection with disability. A clinician explained: ‘we have families from all different backgrounds … depending on what the culture has taught them about a child with a disability. Service providers need to be prepared for that and be able to have sensitive conversations with family members’ (#7).

Another aspect of disability awareness and anti‐ableism training involves showcasing the abilities of people with disabilities. A clinician explained:

‘There needs to be more recognition that just because someone is disabled does not mean they're incapable. There's still such a stigma that someone who is disabled is incapable and that is making it a lot harder to get access to something we take for granted. We need policy makers to see that’ (#15).

Some clinicians received training on disability awareness and inclusion, but it mainly targeted adults. They emphasized the need for specific training for those working with children with disabilities. A clinician shared: ‘there's a lack of awareness about inclusivity. One of the biggest barriers is changing attitudes regarding stigma and negative stereotyping and how this affects how people are perceived and treated’ (#12). Inclusive training helped clinicians address ableism and encouraged them to increase their knowledge and awareness about disabilities.

Theme 2. Enhancing Inclusive Programming, Services and Policies

Thirteen participants highlighted the importance of improving programmes, services and policies for minoritized youth with disabilities.

Funding

Clinicians suggested additional funding for community organizations providing programs, interpreters/translation and transportation to services for youth with disabilities. A clinician said, ‘funding for attending appointments, like arranging accessible vehicles’ (#8). Further, a community service provider highlighted the need to address language barriers that youth with multiple minoritized identities often face.

Clinicians recommended incorporating more inclusive design principles when creating specific programs or events for multiply minoritized youth. An occupational therapist suggested ‘better inclusion around gender diversity and race and clearer indication of inclusive spaces by including some sort of visual image or something to indicate it's an inclusive space’ (#3). A social worker recommended specific programming for minorized groups: ‘designing things specifically for Black youth with disabilities and Indigenous youth disabilities and LGBTQ+ and females’ (#9). Another clinician highlighted: ‘more programs especially in the community. Either specific organizations that deal with that population or include more programs. They start from the funding. We don't have the programs we can refer people to because we don't have funding’ (#11).

Remove Barriers to Enable Equitable Access to Services

Another aspect of inclusive programming involves removing barriers to enable minoritized youth with disabilities to access resources equitably. A community service provider shared, ‘healthcare should be more accessible … educational institutions need to be more accessible for people with disabilities and added intersectionality of race, gender, color’ (#8). A clinician observed inequities in the services they offered to minoritized groups and identified the need to remove barriers:

‘In communities where there are more children in a racialized community. They don't seem to have the same amount of services, and the quality is slightly different. Some of the staffing in communities where it's children who are racialized, they're not as good as the ones in white communities’ (#2).

Clinicians suggested that inclusive programming should involve creating inclusive spaces and specific programming for minoritized children and youth with disabilities.

Theme 3. Building Connections to Supports and Resources

Clinicians recommended helping youth enhance their knowledge and awareness of services by assisting youth's connection with supports and services. Additionally, they advocated for offering specific supports and resources for multiply minoritized youth. A clinician described the importance of connecting youth to resources: ‘more access to resources, including funding and availability and awareness of the resources’ (#15). Some clinicians reported that minoritized youth, such as newcomer or immigrant youth with disabilities, do not know about potential resources or how to access them. They emphasized the importance of helping youth build connections to facilitate access to services.

‘Community networking. There should be a networking of the service providers and sharing … have interagency meetings … more networking with help people get connected with the resources … make it more accessible, make it easier for them … someone should be there to help them navigate, to help facilitate that kind of social support and connection into the community’. (#11).

Another clinician shared: ‘getting to know what's out there is half the battle for families … we could up our resources for families … a lot of families are very isolated and particularly in racialized communities’ (#2). A community‐based coordinator recommended that minoritized individuals need better access to meaningful and relevant resources. This would allow youth to share their concerns with others, which could help address some of the negative impacts of discrimination. A clinician highlighted the importance of culturally appropriate resources: ‘the youth need to see themselves in the resources … it shouldn't be a picture of a white kid doing white kid things. It should be a picture of someone that looks really close to them. It's important to have that representation and having those resources tailored to their own cultural practices’ (#9).

Theme 4. Advocacy and Incorporating Lived Experience Perspectives

Most clinicians emphasized the importance of advocacy (at individual, organizational and policy levels) and incorporating lived experience perspectives. A clinician shared: “the promotion of self‐advocacy is important … for understanding their culture and what they practice and being able to support them … the advocacy piece comes into play a little more in terms of special needs … that can also be said in terms of differences in languages as well” (#6). Others highlighted: “we need to remove the onus on the individual with disability to have to advocate for their needs” (#12). At an organization level, participants shared: “if we're talking about policy and practice there has to be more advocacy and political will … we need to empower families and empower the kids … empower some of the youth to become advocates … advocacy skills are so important” (#15).

Most clinicians emphasized the importance of incorporating lived experience perspectives to address discrimination and create inclusive programming for multiply minoritized youth with disabilities. A clinician explained:

“You need to utilize the voices of youth with disabilities, particularly ensuring the group of youth come from a diverse background to ensure that those intersectionalities are accounted for … if we're going to pour money into something let's make sure we're doing it right the first time.” (#9).

Others noted the importance of connecting youth with others who share similar lived experiences: “building in mentorship with people with lived experience … more people with lived experience in leadership roles … changing the whole dynamic of who the experts are” (#14). At an organizational level, a clinician suggested: “including lived experience when you're developing policies … and practices and making sure their input is considered … communicate and listen to what the needs are” (#15). Another clinician recommended having more lived experience perspectives from healthcare staff: “we need representation from people from varied backgrounds serving people coming into the doors … when you have somebody from a similar background also in a servicing role its very useful in making one feel at home, more welcomed because it showcases inclusion” (#8).

Discussion

This study examined clinicians' and community service providers' recommendations for how they can address discrimination towards multiply minoritized children and youth with disabilities. Clinicians need more disability awareness and anti‐ableism training and education, as our findings emphasized. Further training is important because when people lack knowledge, they often make quick judgements and rely on inaccurate societal narratives, which can lead to stigma and discrimination (Chapman et al. 2013; Lindsay and Cancelliere 2018; Oslin et al. 2024). Service providers are capable of promoting inclusivity, avoiding discrimination, and developing better strategies for working with multiply minoritized youth with disabilities by regularly engaging in disability and diversity training (Houtrow et al. 2022; Sassu and Volkmar 2023). Such training and interventions can improve attitudes towards people with disabilities by enabling individuals to confront their biases on an ongoing basis (Calma‐Roddin 2025; Lindsay and Edwards 2013; Oslin et al. 2024). Clinicians could develop greater self‐awareness and understanding of how to address internal biases through conversations about disabilities and other minoritized identities (Lindsay et al. 2014; Moola et al. 2023). Some researchers suggest that racial oppression models apply to anti‐ableism training and bias reduction work (Madden and Cruz 2021; Oslin et al. 2024). Such models prioritize antiracism strategies that can help implement change by identifying habits that perpetuate stigma and racist and/or ableist ideals (Oslin et al. 2024). Other research shows that enhancing disability awareness also involves engagement with the disability community, participation in workshops, implementing universal design, and accountability for the inclusion of multiply minoritized individuals (Whalen et al. 2024). Some research suggests that integrating training into clinical practice may be worthwhile to ensure optimal uptake (Steinert et al. 2006). We recognize the difficulties of changing attitudes and behaviours through knowledge of disability alone (Soper et al. 2025). Soper et al. (2025) argue that disability awareness programs should be complemented with positive feelings about disability (e.g., encouraging empathy, challenging prejudice and valuing difference, strategies for interacting and advocacy), while being mindful of how positive feelings may unintentionally lead to negative ones (e.g., pity) that can harm people with disabilities.

Our findings highlight the importance of enhancing inclusive programming, services and policies for children and youth with multiple minoritized identities. Our results align with Kattari et al's (2016) study, which emphasizes building inclusive and respectful environments to improve the health outcomes of marginalized groups. For example, they described the importance of having supportive and inclusive health care providers and our findings emphasized inclusive programming and policies to improve the wellbeing of children and youth with disabilities (Kattari et al. 2016). Clinicians need the support of healthcare leaders and decision‐makers to obtain essential funding and resources to offer appropriate care to clients with multiple minoritized identities. We must provide inclusive psychologically safe spaces to reduce discrimination (Clark 2020; Siad and Rabi 2021).

Our results demonstrated the importance of assisting minoritized youth with disabilities in building connections to supports and resources, which play an important role in addressing discrimination (Lindsay and Cao 2025). Sufficient funding should be provided to youth with disabilities so they can access relevant supports and resources, such as funds for transportation or social participation. Other research shows that disability allyship, healthcare partnerships, and the incorporation of disability equity within rehabilitation education and practice can help address ableism (Feldner et al. 2022).

Our findings highlight the importance of advocacy at several levels (individual, organization, policy) in addition to incorporating youth's lived experience perspectives into designing services to address health equity (Farrer et al. 2015). Culturally sensitive care models and patient advocacy can create awareness of one's own values, biases, and power differences (Lindsay et al. 2014; Yan and Yuk‐Lin 2005). Some researchers argue that effective advocacy should include persistent efforts to raise awareness and understand the essential role of the social determinants of health (e.g., minoritized identities such as disability, race, etc.) (Farrer et al. 2015). Institutions and policies must commit to addressing discrimination towards multiply minoritized groups (Bockers et al. 2017; Lindsay et al. 2019).

Other research demonstrates that sharing the lived experiences of people with disabilities teaches others about their perspectives, thereby enhancing disability awareness and reducing discrimination (Calma‐Roddin 2025; Moola et al. 2023; Rotenberg et al. 2022; Young et al. 2023). Engaging youth in such work also allows them to build critical skills like leadership, social justice, and advocacy (Young et al. 2023).

Limitations and future directions

Our sample was recruited from one location; our findings may not represent clinicians from other locations. Additionally, we unintentionally overrepresented women in our sample. Nevertheless, this reflects the gender composition of paediatric rehabilitation clinicians (Khanam et al. 2022). Future studies should recruit additional perspectives of racially minoritized, male, and gender diverse clinicians. Over half our sample had lived experience of disability in addition to their clinical experience, which may have influenced their perspectives. Researchers could examine specific types of disabilities, gender identities, and/or racially minoritized groups in future studies. Doing so could provide a more nuanced understanding of how multiple forms of discrimination (i.e., ableism, racism, gender‐based discrimination) impact the well‐being and outcomes of young people with disabilities. Researchers should explore additional minoritized identities, such as social class and geographic location. Finally, further research should assess the institutional, societal, and political changes that could help to reduce discriminatory attitudes and behaviours towards people with multiple minoritized identities.

Conclusion

Clinicians suggested ways to address discrimination towards multiply minoritized youth with disabilities in our qualitative study. Our findings highlight the importance of additional and specific disability awareness and anti‐ableism training for educators, healthcare providers, and other community service providers. The results indicated that healthcare leaders and decision‐makers should enhance inclusive community programs, services, and policies to reduce discrimination. Clinicians also reported that building connections to relevant supports and resources could benefit youth. Our findings showed how advocacy at several levels is needed, along with incorporating lived experience perspectives into programs, services, and policies. Organizations need dedicated funding and resources to address discrimination among children and youth with disabilities.
