A surgical technique for tape-free lateral decubitus positioning in lateral lumbar interbody fusion using a novel mechanical patient positioner
Authors: Mitchell K Ng, Paul Mastrokostas, Mohamed Said, Rajendra Singh, Rohan Rajan, Ezekiel Kopilak, Ahmed K Emara, Afshin E Razi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable lateral decubitus positioning is critical for safe and effective lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF); however, traditional patient stabilization with adhesive taping can be time-consuming, inconsistent, and associated with skin shear or breakdown, particularly in high body mass index (BMI) or elderly patients. In this technical report, we describe the use of a mechanical patient positioning system as a tape-free method of stabilization designed to provide counter-pressure at the iliac crest, thoracic cage, and/or greater trochanter while allowing for fine adjustments in alignment. The system is mounted directly to the operating table and uses adjustable support arms with anatomically contoured pads to improve control of pelvic tilt, trunk rotation, and coronal balance during positioning Conceptually, this approach enables controlled, reproducible support at fixed bony landmarks and may facilitate positioning optimization during fluoroscopic imaging. Compared with traditional taping techniques, this system is intended to reduce reliance on variable manually applied tension and instead provide adjustable mechanical support. Potential advantages include improved positioning consistency, decreased dependence on skin-based traction, and the ability to make intraoperative adjustments after draping. In addition, the technique may streamline operating room (OR) workflow by reducing repeated repositioning maneuvers and minimizing setup variability between surgical teams. The ability to fine-tune patient alignment after preparation may be particularly useful in multilevel LLIF procedures and in patients with increased soft-tissue mobility. This report aims to: (I) outline the rationale for mechanical positioning in LLIF; (II) describe the technical application of a table-mounted positioning system; and (III) discuss its potential advantages and limitations relative to conventional taping methods. No quantitative outcome data are presented, and further study is required to evaluate clinical impact.Keywords: Novel technique, patient positioning, lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF)

Introduction

Lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) has become an important minimally invasive option with a wide range of indications including correction of coronal alignment, and achieving indirect decompression through a retroperitoneal approach (1). Of note, when performed in the lateral decubitus position, successful procedural execution depends heavily on achieving a perfect patient positioning, as small deviations in rotation or pelvic tilt can directly affect fluoroscopic quality, orthogonality of retractors, and ultimately the surgeon’s operative trajectory across the disc space (1,2). For this reason, most existing LLIF surgical technique guides emphasize the need for meticulous positioning, and describe elaborate adhesive taping strategies across the chest, pelvis, and lower extremities to both counteract patient drift while maintaining appropriate intraoperative exposure (3).

Despite its widespread use, adhesive taping has several well-recognized limitations. Effective taping requires coordinated effort among operating room (OR) personnel and varies considerably across institutions and surgical teams (4). In certain patients [e.g., those with higher body mass index (BMI) and/or significant soft-tissue mobility], aggressive multi-wrap taping may still loosen over the course of a long procedure, altering the working corridor or necessitating re-leveling of fluoroscopy (5). In addition to routinely wasting several rolls of adhesive tape per case, the high shear forces generated by tensioned tape can also pose risks to patients with fragile/elderly skin (6,7).

As lateral approaches have expanded to include multi-level constructs and single-position workflows, and patients with overall greater complexity, the tolerance for positioning error has narrowed. It is commonly believed that the core of the case and the majority of operative time of a lateral interbody fusion involves patient positioning, even more so than actual surgical skin-to-close time. Despite these implications, patient positioning for LLIF remains one of the least standardized components of the procedure, with techniques varying widely across institutions and/or operative teams. This variability underscores the need for positioning strategies that are reproducible, adjustable, and less dependent on skin-based traction.

Mechanical table patient positioners, commonly used for retractor support or limb control in other surgical disciplines, offer a promising alternative to traditional adhesive taping. By providing rigid, adjustable counter-pressure at key anatomic landmarks, these devices may enable more precise control of pelvic and thoracolumbar alignment while eliminating potential skin-related drawbacks of tape. Early experience suggests that mechanical positioning may also enhance fluoroscopic consistency and streamline the overall workflow of LLIF. The purpose of this review is to: (I) outline the rationale for using a table-mounted patient positioners for lateral decubitus stabilization; (II) describe specific preoperative techniques in detail; and (III) highlight the potential advantages of this approach compared with conventional taping. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://aoj.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/aoj-2026-1-0010/rc).

Preoperative preparations and requirements

Achieving and maintaining a stable lateral decubitus position has long been recognized as a prerequisite for safe and efficient LLIF. Standard positioning commonly relies on adhesive taping applied in multiple directions to counteract the natural tendency of the torso and pelvis to rotate/drift intraoperatively (Figure 1) (2).

Image demonstrating traditional setup and patient positioning prior to lateral interbody fusion lateral approach with adhesive tape.

Variations of these taping strategies (e.g., circumferential chest wraps, hip/iliac crest traction bands, and figure-of-eight thigh configurations) have been adopted across various institutions, but all these techniques remain heavily dependent on the experience of the OR team (3). Furthermore, in patients with higher BMI and/or reduced skin integrity, securing necessary tension without causing shear or blistering may be challenging, and tape may loosen prolonged surgical time or patient perspiration (6). These limitations are even more pronounced in single-position lateral workflows and multilevel LLIF procedures, where small changes in pelvic rotation or shoulder tilt can degrade fluoroscopic alignment and alter the working corridor. When considering overall spine trends towards more minimally invasive lateral techniques, the need for a more reproducible, adjustable, and skin-sparing method of stabilization has become increasingly apparent.

Importantly, the continued reliance on adhesive taping reflects familiarity rather than actual consistency across diverse clinical scenarios. While ultimately effective in certain routine cases, taping strategies may be less reliable in prolonged procedures or cases requiring fine intraoperative correction of alignment. Furthermore, and perhaps even more importantly, as lateral lumbar surgery emphasizes efficiency/reproducibility, positioning methods depending on a non-systematic and variable technique may represent a limitation to current operative setups.

Mechanical positioning system and required equipment

Patient positioning is also key to lateral approaches to spine, especially in the context of minimally invasive techniques that rely on accurate fluoroscopy and small working corridors. Traditional methods for patient positioning (e.g., extensive adhesive taping, foam wedges, and bean-bag supports) are commonly used but are limited by technique variability, inconsistency between OR teams, and overall inherent challenges of relying on soft tissue/skin traction for stabilization. To address these shortcomings, a new mechanical system (FISSO Body Support Line, FISSO USA, Zurich, Switzerland) has been developed that offers a more structured and reproducible approach to lateral decubitus positioning (8). These patient positioners consists of 4 table-mounted support arms paired with a set of 5–7 anatomically contoured pads designed to stabilize specific regions of the body, including the thoracic cage, lumbar spine, pelvis, and extremities (8).

Step by step description

Each support arm contains a series of locking joints that allow the surgeon to tailor the direction and magnitude of counter-pressure with a level of precision not achievable with tape or foam. Once secured to the operating table, the arms can be adjusted to apply gentle but firm support against reliable bony landmarks such as the iliac crest or greater trochanter. The accompanying pads, shaped to match regional anatomy, help distribute force evenly. Because the arms can be fine-tuned after the patient has been prepped and draped, they provide a quick and reproducible solution for precise positioning where small adjustments in rotation, pelvic tilt, or shoulder position are necessary to optimize fluoroscopic alignment (9).

Application of the patient positioner system

After the patient is placed in the lateral decubitus position on a radiolucent table, the Body Support Product Line is deployed prior to prepping/draping to assist in patient positioning and stabilization during the case. Four table-mounted mechanical arms are secured to the rails, and anatomically contoured pads are selected based on body habitus. The thoracic pad is positioned against the rib cage to control shoulder/trunk rotation, while a pelvic/iliac crest pad stabilizes the pelvis and prevents anterior or posterior drift during retractor manipulation (Figure 2A). A trochanteric pad can be applied to fine-tune coronal alignment or to support patients with significant soft-tissue mobility. Additional pads may be used to support the downside thigh or shoulder to further optimize pressure distribution and nerve protection (Figure 2B).

Image demonstrating novel mechanical patient positioning prior to lateral interbody fusion lateral approach for (A) upper torso and (B) lower extremities.

While exact numerical angles are not required, placing the patient in mild hip flexion (e.g., ~15 degrees) helps reduce tension on the psoas and femoral nerve, contributing to a more neutral and stable pelvic orientation (10). Similarly, moderate flexion of the knee (~20–25 degrees) minimizes stretch through the hamstring complex and reduces the risk of neurapraxia involving the sciatic or peroneal nerves at the fibular head (11). For these reasons, the hip/knee should be viewed not in terms of precise measurement, but as a functional safety range to promotes muscular relaxation and reduces traction on neural structures.

Once the appropriate contact points are chosen, each mechanical arm is adjusted individually to apply firm, controlled counter-pressure against fixed bony landmarks. The multi-joint design of the system allows the surgeon to dial in rotation, tilt, and translation with far greater precision than is possible with current methods (e.g., taping, foam bolsters, or bean bags). Small fine-tune adjustments to pelvic tilt/shoulder position can be made rapidly in response to fluoroscopic feedback, with changes remaining stable even after a patient has been prepped/draped. This feature is particularly advantageous for LLIF/Extreme Lateral Interbody Fusion (XLIF) when achieving true orthogonal anterior-posterior (AP)/lateral imaging requires fine-tune adjustments (12). To complete the setup, the ipsilateral and contralateral arms are supported with either standard or vertical-column armboards, ensuring that upper-extremity positioning does not interfere with the operating corridor or compromise brachial plexus safety (13) (Figure 3).

Summary of remaining surgical steps

Once positioning is finalized, the rest of the procedure proceeds according to standard LLIF technique. Fluoroscopy is used to localize the disc space, followed by a small flank incision, with blunt dissection through the abdominal wall muscles (external oblique, internal oblique transversus abdominus) to access the retroperitoneal space (4). After manual palpation of the psoas, dilators are advanced to the lateral annulus under neuromonitoring to help stay clear of the lumbar plexus, with expandable retractor positioned perfectly orthogonally to the disc (14). After confirming alignment on AP/lateral imaging, an annulotomy is performed, the disc is removed (discectomy), and endplates are prepared appropriately. Trials are inserted to restore appropriate disc height and overall lordotic alignment, and final implant placed. The retractor is withdrawn, and the wound is closed in layers with particular focus on closing the transversus abdominus or external oblique muscles. Throughout, the patient positioner remains in place, reducing need for repositioning and providing a stable platform for accurate fluoroscopy.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

At the conclusion of the case, the table-mounted supports and armboards are removed in a controlled fashion, and the patient is assessed for any evidence of positioning-related pressure injury or nerve irritation. Particular attention should be directed to the shoulder girdle, rib cage, iliac crest, greater trochanter, fibular head, and dependent soft-tissue contact points. A routine postoperative neurologic examination should be performed, and any new upper- or lower-extremity sensory or motor symptoms should be documented. Beyond these positioning-specific checks, postoperative care follows standard LLIF protocols, including pain management, mobilization, wound surveillance, and monitoring for approach-related complications.

Tips and pearls

Several practical considerations may improve use of the mechanical positioning system. Pads should be applied against stable bony landmarks rather than soft tissue alone in order to maximize reproducibility and minimize drift. Before final locking of the support arms, orthogonal anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopic images should be confirmed, as even small changes in pelvic tilt, trunk rotation, or shoulder position may alter the working corridor. Mild hip flexion and moderate knee flexion may help reduce neural tension and improve pelvic neutrality. Surgeons should avoid excessive focal compression at the thoracic cage, iliac crest, and greater trochanter, and upper-extremity support should be positioned carefully to maintain brachial plexus safety while preserving access to the surgical field.

Discussion

The increasing adoption of LLIF has highlighted the importance of conducting reproducible and secure patient positioning. Traditional stabilization methods (e.g., most commonly adhesive taping, foam bolsters, and bean bags) have limitations that become most evident during longer cases/multilevel exposures (Figure 4). While familiar to most operating teams, these methods depend heavily on skin traction and provider technique, introducing variability each case. In contrast, a mechanical patient positioner provides direct support against fixed bony landmarks, allowing easy control of rotation, pelvic alignment, and with overall far greater precision. The contoured pads also distribute pressure more evenly than standard/narrow tape edges or firm foam blocks, reducing risk of any patient skin injury. From a workflow efficiency standpoint, the positioner shortens setup by eliminating multi-layer taping and reduces need for intraoperative adjustments. Overall, these differences support a shift toward mechanical stabilization as a more reproducible and controlled method for LLIF positioning. These techniques depend on skin traction and manual tensioning, which are susceptible to loosening, soft-tissue shift, and/or patient habitus. In contrast, a table-mounted mechanical support system offers a controlled method of achieving/maintaining desired alignment intraoperatively. To this end, the Body Support Product Line described in this review aims to combine rigid articulating arms with anatomically contoured pads to distribute load evenly across the thoracic, lumbar, pelvic, and extremity regions.

Although the Body Support system is the primary focus of this article, lateral lumbar procedures also require dependable positioning of the upper extremities (9). FISSO’s armboard options serve this purpose and are included here for completeness. These armboards help maintain a clean operative field and reduce strain on the shoulder girdle, complementing the trunk and pelvic support provided by the Body Support arms.

Viewed as a unified system, the Body Support Product Line addresses several priorities that are increasingly important in minimally invasive spine surgery. First, by reducing or eliminating the need for extensive taping and multiple foam supports, the system streamlines patient positioning and can shorten the time required to obtain a stable lateral position. Second, the anatomically shaped pads offer improved protection of the skin and underlying soft tissues by promoting more even pressure distribution and reducing the risk of pressure-related neuropathies or skin breakdown. Third, the mechanical nature of the system enhances consistency across cases: once the arms are locked, the patient’s position remains stable, regardless of habitus, perspiration, or the duration of the procedure. In this way, the Body Support Product Line represents a shift toward more predictable and standardized positioning methods for lateral lumbar spine surgery.

One of the clearest advantages of a mechanical positioner is the degree of precision they enable. Small changes in pelvic rotation, shoulder alignment, or lumbar tilt can influence fluoroscopic orthogonality and retractor trajectory. Because support arms can be adjusted after the draping a patient, surgeons can correct subtle deviations that would otherwise require re-taping or partial repositioning. This capability is particularly valuable in LLIF and XLIF, where the working corridor is narrow and sensitive to rotational error (Figure 5).

Improved consistency translates into predictable fluoroscopic imaging, decreasing need for adjustments and overall radiation exposure to the operating team. Patient safety represents another important consideration. By shifting support away from skin-based traction towards cushioned pads, this novel system reduces risk of pressure-related complications. Skin breakdown, shear injury, and positioning-related neuropathies are well-described concerns in high-BMI patients, elderly patients/those with fragile skin, and cases requiring prolonged retraction (9). More uniform pressure distribution may mitigate these risks in common sites of focal compression (e.g., shoulder girdle, iliac crest region, and greater trochanter).

The potential time savings associated with this system should also be appreciated. Manual taping and traditional positioning up to date often require repeated adjustments, as the operative team works to balance rotation/stability for fluoroscopic alignment. The mechanical positioner streamlines this process, by anchoring directly to the table and permitting easy fine-tuning.

Although this paper focuses on its use in lateral lumbar approaches, the system’s utility likely extends beyond LLIF. Precise stabilization is equally important in anterior-to-psoas (ATP) approaches, prone lateral techniques, thoracolumbar corpectomy via a lateral corridor, and even transthoracic lateral exposures (11,15,16). Outside of lateral surgery, the ability to apply adjustable, rigid support has potential benefits for thoracic osteotomies where coronal balance must be maintained during instrumentation, and complex deformity corrections that require stable pelvic anchoring (11,16). Beyond spine surgery, similar positioning principles are relevant in lateral hip arthroplasty, femoral nailing, and pelvic acetabular fixation (17,18).

Beyond technical execution, the adoption of mechanical positioning systems may have broader implications for operative workflow/standardization. More reproducible positioning may facilitate consistent fluoroscopic alignment, reduce setup variability, and support evolving lateral strategies that involve multi-level constructs. In training environments, shifting the reliance of positioning precision from staff experience to adjustable hardware may potentially help shorten the learning curve. Although further comparative studies are warranted, these considerations suggest that positioning systems may influence not just surgical exposure, but also efficiency/scalability of lateral lumbar procedures.

Despite these advantages, there are several important considerations. Mechanical arms introduce new hardware into the operative field, and as with all novel products operating teams must gain familiarity with this device (19). Additional studies are warranted to more fully quantify potential benefits, including comparing objective measurements of setup time, fluoroscopic alignment, patient comfort, and rates of skin/nerve-related complications (9). This study has several important limitations. First, this report represents a technical description of a positioning approach and does not include quantitative outcome data; therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding clinical superiority over traditional positioning methods. Specifically, objective measures such as setup time, fluoroscopy time, radiation exposure, and complication rates were not collected or analyzed.

Second, the proposed advantages of this system are based on conceptual rationale and early experience rather than prospective or comparative evaluation. As such, these observations may be subject to user-dependent variability and may not be generalizable across different surgeons, OR teams, or institutional workflows.

Third, implementation of a mechanical positioning system introduces additional equipment into the operative field and may require an initial learning curve for surgical staff. Variability in familiarity with the device could influence workflow efficiency and perceived benefit.

Fourth, cost considerations, including device acquisition, maintenance, and availability of compatible OR infrastructure, were not formally evaluated and may impact adoption across practice settings.

Finally, this report may be subject to potential bias inherent to descriptive technical studies. Although efforts were made to present a balanced perspective, further independent validation is necessary.

Future studies should include prospective and comparative analyses evaluating objective intraoperative metrics, patient safety outcomes, and cost-effectiveness to better define the role of mechanical positioning systems in lateral lumbar spine surgery

Nevertheless, overall this novel mechanical patient positioner provides an easy and reliable approach to lateral decubitus stabilization. As indications for lateral approaches continue to expand, systems supporting fine-tuned mechanical control will likely play an increasingly important role in optimizing surgical exposure and patient safety.

Conclusions

The introduction of this novel table-mounted mechanical positioner offers a reliable method of achieving lateral decubitus alignment during LLIF/XLIF, addressing traditional shortcomings associated with adhesive taping. By providing stable support at key bony landmarks and allowing for precise adjustments after draping relative to traditional methods, this system ultimately enhances overall workflow. As minimally invasive surgery (MIS) approaches continue to expand, adoption of more standardized positioning tools may play an increasingly important role in improving operative reliability and efficiency.
