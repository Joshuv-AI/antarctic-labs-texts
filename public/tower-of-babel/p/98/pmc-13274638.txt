Theranostics and molecular imaging are taking off: current situation, requirements and how to train more physicians to fill the gap. A perspective from three different continents
Authors: Patrick Veit-Haibach, Winnie Lam, Rebecca K S Wong, Gary J R Cook
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe field of radiopharmaceuticals and therapies is currently rapidly increasing. However, there is no consensus about how to address the expected shortage of qualified personnel to deliver such therapies. This commentary gives an overview of the current situation in different exemplary jurisdictions and also tries to show possible paths forward and how to prepare the upcoming physician’s generation for these radiopharmaceutical therapies.Keywords: theranostic, radiopharmaceutical, physician training

Introduction

The field of radiopharmaceuticals promises to be the next major pillar in cancer care, inspired by its core tenets of non-invasive imaging for patient selection, biological targeting and propelled by its favourable toxicity profile, impressive molecular responses and clinical outcomes. The clinical need for currently approved indications is projected to result in several hundred thousand incremental cycles in treatment volume globally, not to mention the active research trials which are currently underway.1 While the initially predicted numbers may not have been seen in clinical reality due to several factors, it is undebated that we are on a clear trajectory of increased use of theranostics in many indications, and with that, the need for cross-trained professionals is on the rise as well.2

There is an increasing supply of radiopharmaceuticals, especially for therapy, and there is likely a sufficient number of PET systems available in most jurisdictions for evaluation/qualification of such therapies. Also, there are currently significant investments being made into the supply chain of radiopharmaceuticals as well as new molecules. What is significantly lacking, however, are medical specialists who are sufficiently trained to identify those patients, who are familiar with the diagnostic and therapeutic radiopharmaceutical landscape and who are able to provide the respective therapies to patients.

In this commentary, we provide an exemplary overview from different continents about the training and staffing situation as well as try to point out possible avenues to overcome potential staffing shortages in theranostics.

North American perspective

Human resources that is required to support the care of patients requiring Theranostics will rapidly become the rate limiting step. Active planning together with a pragmatic debate on how existing departments, professional scopes of practice and regulatory frameworks need to be revised to ensure that health care systems are equipped for efficient growth and innovation is urgently needed.

In Canada, the most responsible physician for the use of radiopharmaceuticals in the care of cancer patients are generally nuclear medicine or radiation oncology physicians, with strong collaborations with other oncology disciplines (eg, urology, medical oncology, endocrinology) based on local expertise. Additionally, there is close collaboration with health professionals such as technologists, radiopharmacists, medical physicist, radiation safety officers among others. From a regulatory perspective, the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission, CNSC provides licenses to institutions and facilities (licensee) for the administration of radiopharmaceuticals for clinical care and research. It is the role of the licensee to ensure appropriate training is provided to everyone involved. The radiation safety officers provide training and approvals to provide care to all staff involved on safe handling of each radioisotope. Thus, application of radiopharmaceuticals still needs to be done in designated areas such as the Nuclear Medicine Department, however the physician itself only need to designated/trained by the authorized institution. It is now confined a specific group of physicians in general.

Competency by design framework for Canadian residency training, has been implemented in Nuclear medicine (2020) and Radiation oncology (2019) Royal College Residency Training Programs in Canada. It prepares the trainee through assessment of competencies acquired for supporting quality clinical care, as well as skills required for innovation of life-long learning. It incorporates continuing professional development, maintenance of competence and advanced expertise post certification while in the final year of training, before transitioning into professional practice. To that end, residents have the opportunity to develop enhanced skills including Theranostics where it aligns with their career planning and aspirations. Definitions for Nuclear Medicine and Radiation Oncology training intersects in several areas including the use of radiation in cancer care with different and complementary competencies. More in-depth expertise can be pursued ie through fellowships offering increased breadth and depth to prepare the nuclear oncologists of the future. For example, a two- year fellowship program at University of Toronto since 2020 is designed to provide the trainee through equal time spent in Molecular Imaging/Nuclear Medicine and Medical Oncology with a focus on developing expertise in multiple competencies including but not limited to in-depth understanding of indications, alternatives, decision making, patient care, toxicity management, radiation safety, radiopharmaceutical production, quality assurance, individualized dosimetry, applications of molecular imaging in radiation oncology, research methods and leadership skills. Additional transdisciplinary training such as participation in ‘Strategic Training in Transdisciplinary Radiation Science’ for the 21st century3 is also available. In Canada as well as in other jurisdictions, there is additionally the opportunity to develop and establish an APC (Advanced Practice Certificate) through the respective College for physicians, which renders the structure somewhat similar to an abbreviated fellowship. An APC offers the possibility for several, different medical specialties to gain insight and sufficient competence to at least properly select and identify patients for Theranostics treatment and possibly to deliver the treatment itself as well. Similar enriched curricula are being gradually incorporated into accredited training programs for technologists and medical physicists to prepare the workforce that is needed, capable for supporting innovation and to keep pace with the clinical demand.

During the ‘classical’ Molecular Imaging/Nuclear Medicine track in North America, theranostics is naturally part of the curriculum and fully trained nuclear medicine physicians are capable to interpret the respective PET and SPECT images and deliver the therapies. However, having some training crossover into Radiation Medicine and Medical Oncology will also help to get better clinical insight into the advances of other therapies and will improve the therapy selection for patients. While trained specialists have remained somewhat stable in Canada over the years, specifically in the US, Nuclear Medicine training programs have been on the decline for multiple years and only recently have increased in number again to train physicians for the increasing demand in theranostics. In addition, selected training programmes have now opened up their intake to train crossover specialists as well, that is, Nuclear Medicine Departments have agreed to train Radiation Oncologists in theranostics. These efforts for cross-specialist/cross-discipline training are partly and understandably met with reluctance. However, pragmatically, if the current predictions about the volumes of needed treatment cycles (not even accounting for new indications) become reality in the near future, none of the medical disciplines alone would have enough specialists available for adequate patient care and therapy delivery.4

Singapore/Asian perspective

In Singapore, the first ‘Theranostics’ service was the measurement of radioiodine (I-131) uptake by means of a gamma probe scintillation detector system and a rectilinear scanner in the 1960s. When Singapore General Hospital was re-developed in 1980, the medical applications of all unsealed radioactive materials were put under the charge of a Department of Nuclear Medicine by merging the nuclear medicine section of the Department of Therapeutic Radiology with the radioimmunoassay section of the Department of Pathology. Over the years, an increasing relevance to patient care has ensured that the specialty has become more prominent and integral to holistic health care. This evolution can be attributed to two factors. The first is the rapid advances in technology and equipment that assist in diagnostic nuclear medicine imaging. The second is the tremendous inroads made in the field of radiopharmaceuticals, providing a wide range of different radiotracers for imaging many biological functions at a molecular level. There have also been many innovations in radiopharmaceuticals for radionuclide therapy. This has come about due to the intense research done by dedicated scientists and nuclear medicine specialists.

Currently, there is a great demand for specialists in this discipline, as evidenced by the number of new doctors opting for this field and the fact that it is one of the specialties offered under the local residency program.

Consequently, the Nuclear Medicine Society (Singapore) was officially registered in 2010. This has allowed the educational and research platforms to reach out to the scientific community.

Currently the training quota is determined by the government (Ministry of Health). Application to nuclear medicine senior residency (2.5-year programme) is competitive. Applicants are coming from internal medicine route (3-year residency) and diagnostic radiology route (4-year residency before dual certification).

The field of nuclear medicine is about the tracer principle, with morphological imaging mostly used as an adjunct. Dosimetry in Nuclear Medicine is also different, this time compared to external beam radiation therapy, as more and more theranostics is introduced into clinical practice.

While some people are attracted to diagnostic radiology because of its limited patient interaction—in theranostics, patient interaction is crucial. There is however significant cross-disciplinary work such as radiosynovectomy (radiosynoviorthesis) and selective internal radiation therapy, where there is extensive collaboration with Interventional Radiology.

Singapore has witnessed significant advancements in theranostic development, driven by research collaborations, technological innovations, and a supportive healthcare ecosystem. Singapore’s regulatory framework and emphasis on clinical trials have fostered a conducive environment for the development and commercialization of theranostic products. Other advances in nuclear medicine and molecular imaging in Asia in general are developing partly inconsistently, heavily influenced by geopolitical issues and supply chain and economics considerations, which are amplified by the short half-life of many radionuclides.

United Kingdom perspective

In the UK nuclear medicine imaging services are delivered by nuclear medicine physicians, radionuclide radiologists or dual-accredited radiologist/nuclear medicine consultants. Each department requires at least one consultant to be sufficiently trained in nuclear medicine to hold an Administration of Radioactive Substances Advisory Committee (ARSAC) licence from the Department of Health to cover the specific radiopharmaceuticals used in the department. Other colleagues in the department can work under delegation from the ARSAC licence holder.

Although there are non-radiology alternatives, the main training route in the UK is now combined with radiology. To gain a certificate of completion of training (CCT) in both nuclear medicine and radiology, there is first a requirement of 2 years of core medical training and passing the membership of the Royal College of Physicians (MRCP) exam in internal medicine (or equivalent surgical or paediatric exams) before entering training. Combined training then includes 3 years focused mainly on radiology (passing the fellow of the Royal College of Radiologists (FRCR) exam) and 3 years of mainly nuclear medicine to include a Nuclear Medicine diploma or MSc. Radionuclide radiologists can complete radiology training with 1-2 additional years of nuclear medicine training but may not have sufficient training in all aspects, particularly therapy, to obtain ARSAC licences.

While these combined training routes have become more popular, the majority of trainees are primarily interested in molecular imaging rather than therapy, given the development of hybrid modalities that have become the norm in many departments. There remains a shortage of clinicians able to deliver radionuclide therapies. This is partly offset by clinical (radiation) oncologists being able to be trained in radionuclide therapy techniques to obtain ARSAC licences for specific therapies, somewhat similar to what is described above in North America. Despite the exciting developments in nuclear medicine, there remains a relative shortage of molecular imaging or molecular therapy clinical academics, where posts are usually split approximately 50:50 between university research and education and clinical practice in an NHS hospital. However, advances in molecular imaging equipment, for example, PET/MRI and total-body PET/CT, and therapy, for example, [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617, [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-TATE, have generated a small resurgence in nuclear medicine clinicians wanting to formally have an academic component to their work.

There is a perception among old-school nuclear medicine physicians that there is a slow creep away from the origins of nuclear medicine as a method to measure pathophysiology and molecular dysfunction in disease, to be dominated by the morphological imaging aspects of hybrid imaging aided by a radioactive ‘contrast agent’ (eg, cancer vs not cancer) without a deeper understanding of what the underlying biology and physiology of radiopharmaceuticals can reveal (eg, FDG for glucose metabolism, DOTA-TATE or PSMA for receptor expression). This needs to be addressed in future curricula so that this knowledge is actually used for clinical care as well as being passed on to future trainees.

In Europe, nuclear medicine often remains a separate speciality from radiology, maintaining a single-speciality focus in its practise. There are undoubtedly advantages and disadvantages to the various routes of training required to practice nuclear medicine. The good news is that the recent exciting developments in hybrid imaging and effective radionuclide therapies have generated more interest from young doctors deciding on career paths and an increase and increased enthusiasm for delivering a nuclear medicine clinical service irrespective of combined or single training routes.

Conclusion

As outlined, there are significant differences internationally in terms of access and training requirements for nuclear medicine/molecular imaging as well as theranostics.

Ultimately, a new sub-specialisation of a ‘nuclear oncologist’ might emerge from the current restructuring of the international radiotheranostic landscape (somewhat similar to the earlier nuclear medicine physicians coming out of internal medicine), as it has been discussed on several international meetings already. This, however, might need somewhat streamlined training programmes and consensus between the multiple partaking professional societies. Finally, the need for such specialists will be heavily influenced by future demand and reimbursement of such therapies.
