Comparative analysis of pachymetry and assessment of corneal topography in pediatric congenital corneal opacities: A study of AS-OCT and UBM imaging modalities
Authors: Sonam Yangzes, Deeksha Sharma, Anchal Thakur, Jitender Jinagal, Sushmita Kaushik, Chintan Malhotra, Amit Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to assess the interchangeability of pachymetry measurements obtained by anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) and ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) in children with congenital corneal opacity (CCO). In this single-centre, comparative case series, children with unilateral or bilateral CCO who underwent examination under anesthesia (EUA) were evaluated using both AS-OCT and UBM. Pachymetry values from both modalities were compared using Bland–Altman analysis, and topography maps from AS-OCT were examined. A total of 54 eyes from 34 children (16 males, 18 females) were included. The mean age at first EUA was 263.93 ± 348.98 days (range: 11 days to 4 years). Mean pachymetry was 785.83 ± 23 μm with UBM and 719.80 ± 247 μm with AS-OCT, showing a statistically significant mean difference of 66.04 ± 133.55 μm (P = 0.0006). UBM consistently yielded higher measurements, and Bland–Altman analysis indicated poor agreement between the two devices. The findings suggest that AS-OCT and UBM are not interchangeable for pachymetry assessment in CCO. However, AS-OCT, being non-contact and usable without sedation, provides practical advantages for longitudinal follow-up and also offers topographic imaging, making it a useful tool in pediatric corneal evaluation.

Accurate corneal thickness measurement is crucial for intraocular pressure assessment, as pachymetry significantly influences tonometric readings. In cases of congenital corneal opacity (CCO) with anterior segment dysgenesis (ASD), altered corneal biomechanics can lead to unreliable pressure measurements. Ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) and anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) are both valuable imaging tools for examining ASD in opaque corneas, each capable of providing pachymetry measurements.[1234] While both modalities offer pachymetric data, AS-OCT has the additional advantage of providing corneal topography measurements in these abnormal and opaque corneas. However, UBM, being a contact procedure, can be challenging to perform in very young children, necessitating either examination under anesthesia (EUA) or sedation. This requirement poses logistical difficulties, especially in children with systemic or syndromic comorbidities, which can delay early evaluation and treatment. The swept-source (SS) technology of the Casia AS-OCT SS-1000 (Tomey, Nagoya, Japan) provides rapid, high-resolution imaging of pediatric eyes without sedation.[567] This is one of the most extensive comparative studies of pachymetry with SS-AS-OCT versus UBM for CCO and aims to determine whether pachymetric readings from these two imaging modalities are interchangeable. Moreover, this paper gives an insight into the corneal topography of CCOs.

Materials and Methods

We conducted a retrospective review of children under 5 years of age presenting with CCO at the Pediatric Cornea Clinic, Advanced Eye Centre, PGI Chandigarh, India, between January 2022 and January 2024. This study followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the institutional review board.

Children with unilateral or bilateral congenital corneal opacities were included in the study if their opacities were severe enough to obstruct standard anterior segment evaluation. Patients with a history of ocular trauma, previous surgery, keratitis, or primary congenital glaucoma were excluded. Parental consent was obtained for every participant, and all AS-OCT and UBM imaging was performed by a single examiner (SY). Central corneal thickness (CCT) was evaluated uniformly across all eyes, irrespective of the morphology or density of the opacity, as only cases with central corneal opacity were included.

For the AS-OCT (Casia Tomey SS-1000, Tomey, Nagoya, Japan), topical proparacaine 1% was instilled and punctal occlusion applied. A pediatric speculum was then placed, and the child was held upright on the guardian’s lap. The machine captures 360° widefield radial scans (16 mm scan range; A/B scan: 512; B/C scan: 128) in approximately 10 seconds per eye. From the two-dimensional images, CCT and other anterior segment structures were identified, and pachymetry was noted for each eye. Although exact localization of the same spot on both imaging modalities is challenging, especially in the presence of corneal opacity, an approximately similar location was selected on UBM by overlapping it with the corresponding AS-OCT image. The calipers of the UBM system were then used to measure corneal thickness at this location, and care was taken to align it as closely as possible with the AS-OCT measurement site. The in-built software of AS-OCT shows the central corneal location based on the scleral spur to scleral spur diameter in the horizontal plane, and it also provides CCT [Fig. 1a]. Unlike AS-OCT, UBM lacks integrated software to precisely identify and mark specific corneal sites; hence, visual correlation was used to ensure maximal alignment between the two imaging. Corneal topography measurements were analyzed using Casia AS-OCT in cases whenever topographic data could be captured [Fig. 1].

Representative AS-OCT image (a) showing the CCT measured by the machine based on scleral spur-to scleral spur (STS) diameter. (b) showing the UBM CCT taken at the same site as that of AS-OCT. (c and d) showing AS-OCT CCT and UBM CCT in an eye with anterior segment dysgenesis

UBM (Aviso S, Quantel Medical, Clermont-Ferrand, France) using a 50-MHz probe was performed subsequent to AS-OCT, typically within a week, and generally under anesthesia. The transducer was immersed in a fluid bath to maintain perpendicular alignment with the cornea and ensure optimal imaging. Horizontal meridian images from UBM were directly compared with those from the AS-OCT [Fig. 1]. Phenotypic diagnosis was made based on EUA findings. Ultrasound pachymetry was performed whenever possible, and digital photographs were used to document the clinical findings. The detailed procedure of image acquisition is mentioned in our previously published literature, and the same method was used in this study as well.[8]

Statistical analysis

Data were processed using SPSS version 26 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL). All values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). We used paired Student’s t-tests to compare the mean CCT between UBM and AS-OCT. Bland–Altman analysis evaluated limits of agreement and interchangeability. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.[9]

Results

Demographic data

Fifty-four eyes of 34 patients (16 males, 18 females) underwent both AS-OCT and UBM at the time of EUA. Their mean age at first EUA was 288.72 ± 348.98 days (range: 11 days–4 years). A single examiner (SY) performed all measurements. UBM Pachymetry was 785.83 ± 23 μm, while the AS-OCT Pachymetry was 719.80 ± 248 μm [Table 1]. UBM readings surpassed those from AS-OCT in nearly all cases except five eyes. The mean difference of 66.04 ± 133.55 μm was statistically significant (P = 0.0006), and Bland–Altman analysis [Fig. 3] indicated no agreement between the two imaging modalities.[9]

Additional keratometry findings

Out of 54 eyes, 39 (72.22%) provided adequate image quality on Casia AS-OCT for corneal topography. AS-OCT was not able to capture keratometry values in 15 eyes. The mean steep keratometry (Ks) was 59.77 ± 20.77 D, and the mean flat keratometry (Kf) was 45.40 ± 14.30 D. (Table 1 details the pachymetry and keratometry findings.) Discussion

The landscape of anterior segment imaging has undergone remarkable evolution in recent years, particularly in the context of pediatric ophthalmology. Anterior segment OCT has transformed our approach to diagnostics and preoperative evaluation of anterior segment diseases, offering resolution 10–25 times higher than high-frequency ultrasound.[810] Our study provides crucial insights into the comparative utility of AS-OCT and UBM in evaluating congenital corneal opacities and pachymetry, revealing both the strengths and limitations of each modality.

The significant difference we observed between UBM and AS-OCT measurements in CCO cases (74.39 ± 140.64 µm) presents an intriguing contrast to previous studies that demonstrated good correlation in normal corneas.[11] This disparity likely stems from fundamental differences in the physical principles underlying each technology. AS-OCT, utilizing light-based measurements, interacts with opaque tissue differently than UBM’s sound-based measurements, as demonstrated by Pierro et al.[2] in their comparative analysis of different pachymetry methods. In our view, while these imaging technologies may yield similar measurements in clear corneas, the presence of corneal opacity and ASD appears to fundamentally alter their performance. Based on our observations, we suggest that the structural alterations in these congenital conditions affect each imaging modality differently, leading to the measurement variations we encountered. This interpretation helps explain why measurements that typically align well in normal corneas can diverge significantly in pathological cases.

Our findings align partially with Garcia et al.’s[12] observations regarding the relationship between opacity density and measurement variations.[12] However, our study revealed consistently higher UBM readings compared to AS-OCT, contrasting with their findings in dense opacities. This difference might be attributed to our unique patient population – pediatric cases with ASD – compared to their adult cohort with simpler corneal opacities.

The SS technology employed in our study represents a significant advancement over time-domain OCT used in previous research. This evolution in technology has been well-documented by Chansangpetch et al.,[13] who found considerable proportional bias between SS and time-domain OCT measurements. Casia AS-OCT SS-1000’s ability to capture high-resolution, 360-degree corneal images within seconds proves particularly valuable in pediatric cases where patient cooperation is limited, a benefit previously noted by Cauduro et al.[5] in their pediatric applications of AS-OCT.

We encountered specific limitations with AS-OCT, particularly in cases with retrocorneal membranes where penetration beyond the opacity was restricted, a limitation also observed by Pavlin et al.[14] in their comparative imaging study. UBM, while requiring contact and often sedation, demonstrated superior penetration in dense opacities and provided better visualization of deeper anterior segment structures, confirming findings by Kim et al.[15] and Nischal et al.[16] in their studies of sclerocornea and congenital corneal opacification.

One of the most significant challenges we encountered was ensuring accurate CCT measurements in cases with variable degrees of ASD, a challenge also noted by Zhou et al.[17] in their imaging study of opaque corneas. The distorted sulcus anatomy in these cases complicated the standardization of measurement locations, an issue particularly relevant in pediatric cases, as highlighted by Majander et al.[7] in their AS-OCT study of congenital corneal opacities. Furthermore, it is worth noting that corneal hydration during EUA might result in slightly elevated pachymetric readings compared to non-contact AS-OCT measurements. Even though the average time for a UBM examination is less than 2 minutes, it is crucial to recognize the potential limitations associated with pachymetry data measurement. It is important to note that in the above-mentioned studies on opaque corneas, the population consisted of adults without ASD, and OCT was conducted using time-domain OCT instead of SS-OCT. Pachymetry measurements were obtained using both AS-OCT and UBM; they were performed by a single examiner to minimize variability. However, intra- and inter-observer reproducibility was not formally assessed. Given that AS-OCT is relatively operator-independent while UBM requires manual caliper placement, the absence of reproducibility metrics may affect generalizability.

Our study analyzed corneal topography in cases of CCO, highlighting a significant advancement as keratometry of opaque corneas has not been studied in detail. Using SS-AS-OCT, we successfully captured keratometric readings in 72.22% of cases, revealing a mean steep keratometry (Ks) of 59.77 ± 20.77 D and flat keratometry (Kf) of 45.40 ± 14.30 D. These findings provide valuable insights into the curvature of opaque corneas, which are typically challenging to evaluate with conventional keratometers. However, the values differ significantly from normal pediatric values reported by Friling et al.[18] in their study of newborn keratometry. In our study, topographic data could not be obtained in 27.78% of cases due to marked anatomical distortion associated with conditions such as anterior staphyloma, sclerocornea, and large corneal dermoids. These structural abnormalities hindered proper image alignment and signal capture, thereby limiting AS-OCT’s ability to generate reliable curvature maps. In our experience, neither the density of corneal opacity nor the age of the child significantly affected the acquisition of keratometry data. Notably, infants demonstrated a lesser tendency for movement compared to toddlers, which facilitated smoother alignment and reduced motion artifacts during AS-OCT imaging.

The lack of interchangeability between AS-OCT and UBM measurements emphasizes the need for consistent use of a single modality in longitudinal monitoring, a conclusion supported by Khurana et al.[19] in their high-speed OCT study of corneal opacities. Although UBM requires sedation, it remains an essential imaging modality in specific clinical scenarios. Its superior tissue penetration enables detailed visualization of retrocorneal structures, posterior corneal curvature, and iridocorneal adhesions—features that may be inadequately visualized with AS-OCT due to optical shadowing or media opacity. Consequently, UBM is particularly advantageous in cases of dense corneal opacities or complex ASD, where comprehensive structural assessment is critical for diagnostic and surgical planning. The complementary nature of these technologies suggests that optimal evaluation might require both modalities in complex cases, particularly in situations where detailed structural information is crucial for surgical planning, as demonstrated by Chen et al.[20] in their study of Peters’ and Rieger’s anomalies in infants. The findings of this study have important clinical implications for follow-up and surgical planning in children with congenital corneal opacities. AS-OCT, being a non-contact modality that does not require sedation, is ideal for repeated use in outpatient settings, enabling consistent longitudinal monitoring, particularly in the postoperative period. In addition to pachymetric assessment, AS-OCT provides valuable topographic information even in opaque pediatric corneas, offering further insight into corneal curvature and aiding comprehensive preoperative evaluation. Across all phenotypes—including Peters’ anomaly, sclerocornea, anterior staphyloma, and others—UBM consistently measured thicker CCT than AS-OCT; subgroup numbers were too small for meaningful statistical comparison, but the direction of the discrepancy was uniform.

Conclusion

While AS-OCT and UBM demonstrate correlation in most cases, their pachymetric values are not interchangeable in eyes with CCO. Consequently, for reliable longitudinal follow-up, clinicians should adhere to a single imaging modality. AS-OCT offers distinct advantages as a non-contact modality, particularly suited for preoperative planning, postoperative monitoring, and prognostication in pediatric patients, thereby enabling more informed parental counseling. Its ability to provide reproducible pachymetric data along with valuable topographic insights enhances its clinical relevance in longitudinal care. Nonetheless, UBM remains indispensable in select scenarios requiring deeper tissue penetration. The complementary strengths of both imaging modalities affirm their collective importance in the comprehensive assessment of congenital corneal opacities.
