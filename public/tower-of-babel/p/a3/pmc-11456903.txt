Bezold's abscess in tuberculous otitis media: An extremely rare case report and literature review
Authors: Anton Budhi Darmawan, Bella Jovita Darmayan, Hidayat Sulistyo
Published: 2024

Introduction

Bezold's abscess is a rare complication of chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM) since the advent of antibiotics [1]. However, not all CSOM cases can be successfully treated with standard antibiotic treatments [2]. Otitis media may also result from rare bacterial infections, such as mycobacterium tuberculosis [3,4]. Only 0.05–0.9 % of chronic otitis media is caused by tuberculosis [4]. According to World Health Organization (WHO) surveys, extrapulmonary tuberculosis comprises about 15–20 % of all tuberculosis cases [2] with the ear being involved in <1 % of these cases [5]. Due to the difficulty in diagnosing one of the rarest types of tuberculosis [5,6], its diagnosis is frequently delayed [3]. It is often misdiagnosed because its clinical symptoms are very similar with other chronic middle ear conditions [3]. Delays in receiving adequate treatment may increase the risk of complications, including abscess formation [4]. We report a case of Bezold's abscess as complication of primary tuberculous chronic suppurative otitis media. This work is reported by following the surgical case report (SCARE) guidelines [7].

Case presentation

A 21-year-old male presented to our department with right-sided neck swelling persisting for four days, accompanied by fever and difficulty opening his mouth. He had a history of persistent purulent discharge in both ears for six months, which had not responded to topical antibiotics. The patient also reported severe hearing impairment, otalgia, and tinnitus in both ears. There were no signs of cough, dyspnea, or partial nerve palsy. On physical examination, the neck swelling was red, soft, fluctuant, warm, and painful (Fig. 1). Oto-endoscopic examination of the right ear revealed cholesteatoma, with no multiple perforations observed (Fig. 2). The patient had severe mixed hearing loss in both ears. Non-contrast Computed Tomography (CT) scan of the bilateral temporal bones and soft tissues showed opacification in the right middle ear cavity and mastoid antrum with a breach at the mastoid tip, but no evidence of intracranial extension (Fig. 3). The CT scan of the left ear indicated destruction of the ossicles. Radiological examination of the lungs was within normal limits. The initial diagnosis was Bezold's abscess secondary to CSOM in the right ear and CSOM in the left ear.

The patient was administered intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics and underwent an emergency cutaneous incision and drainage to address the abscess (Fig. 4). Approximately 10 ml of thick, foul-smelling pus was drained from the abscess. Due to technical issues and a previously scheduled mastoidectomy that could not be postponed, we were required to defer the mastoidectomy for this case. Consequently, only the life-saving procedure of incision and drainage was performed.

Intraoperative image of cutaneous incision and drainage performed to treat Bezold's abscess. Approximately 10 ml of thick, foul-smelling pus was drained from the abscess.

Postoperatively, the patient received daily wound care and a course of broad-spectrum oral antibiotics. The wound healed completely within two weeks. After this period, the patient underwent a canal wall-down mastoidectomy by an otologist. A post-aural incision was made, and the temporalis fascia was harvested. During the procedure, cholesteatoma was observed, and the ossicles were found to be destructed and covered with granulation tissue, with pale granulation tissue extending from the mastoid cavity to the tympanic cavity. All granulation tissue was meticulously removed, and a sample was sent for histopathological examination.

Post-aural dressing was applied for seven days, and sutures were removed on the seventh postoperative day. Histopathological examination revealed epithelioid cells, Langhans giant cells, and caseous necrosis (Fig. 5). The definitive diagnosis was Tuberculous Otitis Media (TOM) with bilateral CSOM. Following these results, the patient was initiated on Antitubercular Therapy (ATT) category 1. At the six-month follow-up, the graft demonstrated good growth, and the mastoid cavity exhibited satisfactory healing, with no evidence of aural exudate or recurrent granulation tissue formation (Fig. 6). Surgery for the left ear had been previously scheduled, but it was cancelled due to the improvement in ear discharge after the initiation of ATT.

Discussion

Mycobacterium tuberculosis is the causative agent of tuberculosis, a chronic granulomatous disease prevalent in developing countries. While tuberculosis primarily affects the lungs, it can also involve various body parts, including the ears [2]. The ear constitutes less than 1 % of all tuberculosis cases [5,6]. Furthermore, M. tuberculosis represents a rare etiology of ear infections, including otitis media. Tuberculosis accounts for only 0.05–0.9 % of chronic otitis media cases, contributing to diagnostic challenges [4].

The classical triad of TOM typically includes painless, foul-smelling ear discharge, multiple tympanic membrane perforations, and ipsilateral facial nerve paralysis. Additional findings often include unilateral complaints, conductive hearing loss, and abundant pale granulation tissue on physical examination. In our patient, we did not observe multiple tympanic membrane perforations or facial nerve paralysis. This observation aligns with recent studies indicating that these classic features are now rarely seen [3,5,6]. Currently, the most common clinical presentations include foul-smelling otorrhea with hearing loss and persistent ear discharge that does not respond to standard antimicrobial treatments, which is consistent with our case [4,6]. Our patient experienced bilateral discomfort, likely due to middle ear infection via the eustachian tube pathogenesis [3]. Other potential mechanisms include hematogenous spread from other tuberculosis foci and direct invasion through tympanic perforation [3,4,6].

Tuberculosis is primarily transmitted through airborne droplets, where the bacteria are phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages, leading to granuloma formation in the lungs. The infection can disseminate via the hematogenous route, spreading from lymph nodes to the bloodstream and creating new infection sites throughout the body, including extrapulmonary locations. In the ears, TB can occur through hematogenous spread, direct extension through the Eustachian tube, or invasion via a perforated tympanic membrane, resulting in chronic granulomatous inflammation and ear symptoms such as persistent discharge and hearing loss [8]. Our patient also exhibited severe mixed hearing loss, attributed to chronic ear infection and ossicle destruction observed during surgery. Conductive hearing loss is typically seen in the early stages of the disease. Intraoperative findings included pale granulation tissue, and histopathological examination revealed Langhans multinucleated giant cells, confirming the presence of M. tuberculosis [3].

Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) can be utilized to diagnose tuberculosis, with the ability to detect M. tuberculosis in 28.6 % of cases with a negative Acid-Fast Bacilli (AFB) smear [9]. The rarity of this condition and its resemblance to other CSOM infections complicate diagnosis, which is often made peri- or post-operatively [5]. Diagnostic delays may result in postponed management and heightened risk of severe CSOM complications [4]. Complications can also arise due to antimicrobial resistance, patient non-compliance, and delayed diagnosis [4,10].

In developing countries, CSOM remains the most prevalent ear disease. CSOM can lead to serious complications, including deep neck abscesses and brain abscesses. Although the advent of newer antibiotics has significantly reduced the incidence of complications, Bezold's abscess still accounts for approximately 1.3 % of CSOM cases. Common clinical manifestations of Bezold's abscess include fever, otalgia, neck swelling, ear discharge, restricted cervical mobility, and hearing loss [10,11]. This condition arises when an infection penetrates the mastoid cortex medial to the sternocleidomastoid attachment and posterior to the digastric muscles [12]. CT scans are highly effective for detecting Bezold's abscess, as they allow for the identification of pus collections in the cervical region and mastoid involvement [10,12]. In our case, a CT scan was utilized for both diagnosis and as a surgical guide.

Before surgery, the patient was administered intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics, given that infections are often polymicrobial in nature. Initial antibiotic therapy with broad coverage is recommended to reduce the risk of progression to severe complications [13]. The patient underwent a cutaneous incision and drainage procedure to evacuate the abscess, aligning with findings from previous studies that highlight prompt drainage as a critical component in the management of Bezold's abscess [1]. Two weeks post-drainage, the patient underwent Canal Wall Down (CWD) mastoidectomy as the definitive treatment for CSOM with complications, including abscess formation [14,15].

The decision to perform a CWD mastoidectomy, rather than a Canal Wall Up (CWU) procedure, was made to ensure optimal access for complete cholesteatoma removal and to reduce the rates of recurrence and the need for revision surgery. While CWU procedures are generally associated with better hearing outcomes, this option was less suitable for our patient due to pre-existing severe hearing loss and ossicular chain destruction. Additionally, hearing outcomes in CWU procedures are often influenced by factors such as the condition of the middle ear mucosa and the integrity of the stapes superstructure, which can have a greater impact on hearing than canal wall preservation, further supporting the choice of CWD in this case [16,17].

Surgery is required in TOM when complications arise. Surgical intervention is performed for functional reconstruction, removal of bony sequestrum, and management of complications. The standard treatment for TOM is ATT category 1, which is administered for a minimum of six months. ATT Category 1 includes rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for the initial two months, followed by rifampicin and isoniazid for the remaining four months [3]. In advanced cases, ATT may be extended to 6–9 months. Some literature also mentions chemotherapy as part of TOM management, though further research is needed to evaluate its efficacy [3,6].

Conclusion

TOM is challenging to diagnose. Tuberculous infections should be suspected whenever the optimal regimen fails to meet the expected outcomes. Diagnosis plays an important role in avoiding delayed treatment in TOM that can lead to severe complications such as Bezold's abscess.
