Protocol to assess the cytotoxicity of autologous human skin immune cells against senescent fibroblasts
Authors: Tatsuya Hasegawa, Tomonori Oka, Shadmehr Demehri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmune clearance of senescent cells contributes to their control in aging tissues; however, it remains unclear how immunity against senescent cells is regulated in humans. Here, we provide a protocol to measure the cytotoxicity of autologous human skin immune cells against senescent dermal fibroblasts. We describe the ex vivo co-culture experiments using normal and senescent fibroblasts together with autologous immune cells isolated from the human skin. This protocol is applicable to understanding the mechanism of immune surveillance and clearance of senescent cells in humans.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Hasegawa et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Cell culture, Cell isolation, Health Sciences, Immunology

Before you begin

Senescent cells are thought to be eliminated by the immune system to prevent their accumulation in tissues; however, how immunity against senescent cells is regulated in humans remains unclear. One reason is that it is technically challenging to prepare both immune cells and senescent cells from the same donor, especially from peripheral tissues, in order to avoid the risk of allogeneic immunologic rejection, and to utilize them in functional assays using co-culture systems. To overcome this limitation, we have developed an experimental system.

The protocol below describes the assessment of the cytotoxicity of autologous human skin immune cells against senescent fibroblasts. This will lead to a better understanding of the immune surveillance and clearance of senescent fibroblasts in human skin. First, this step allows for the isolation of both dermal fibroblasts and immune cells from the same fresh human skin sample. Next, a co-culture system is established, consisting of normal and replication-induced senescent dermal fibroblasts together with autologous immune cells isolated from the same skin sample. Finally, an immunostaining assay is performed to assess apoptosis of senescent fibroblasts mediated by the autologous immune cells.

Innovation

This protocol represents a pioneering system for enhancing the understanding of the mechanisms underlying the immune-mediated clearance of senescent cells in human skin. In the fields of immunology, dermatology, and gerontology, it is crucial to recognize the significant differences between mice and humans. These differences include immune responses, structural characteristics of the skin, and the phenotype of senescent cells.2,3 Given these disparities, it is essential to deepen our understanding using human models. To address this challenge, an advanced protocol has been developed in which autologous immune cells and senescent fibroblasts are isolated and co-cultured from the skin of the same donor. This approach enables the execution of functional experiments that more accurately represent human biology, providing significant insights into the immune-mediated clearance mechanisms of senescent cells in human skin.

Expected outcomes

This protocol enables functional investigation of immune surveillance and clearance mechanisms targeting senescent fibroblasts. It provides a useful platform for elucidating how the immune system recognizes and eliminates senescent cells in human skin.

In addition to studying immune responses to senescent fibroblasts, this system is expected to be applicable for investigating immune interactions with disease-specific human dermal fibroblasts, such as those associated with skin cancer and fibrosis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The number of cleaved caspase-3-positive apoptotic cells and vimentin-positive cells is manually counted. Cleaved caspase-3-positive cells are normalized to the total number of vimentin-positive fibroblasts in each field. This quantification is performed across ten randomly selected fields (100× magnification) per well, and the average value is subsequently calculated.

Limitations

In our experimental system, the immune cells isolated from human skin have limited proliferative potential, which makes them difficult to use in functional experiments that require relatively large numbers of skin immune cells.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low yield of isolated dermal fibroblasts (Step 11).

Potential solution

If the cell number is low in Step 11, we note that the enzymatic digestion of the dermal piece may be problematic. We recommend pipetting up and down to facilitate tissue digestion with a plastic pipette in Step 9.

Problem 2

Slow proliferation rate of cultured dermal fibroblasts (Step 14).

Potential solution

It is recommended that the seeded fibroblasts are collected once and reseeded at a higher cell density (5–6 × 103 cells/cm2) into 75 cm2 cell culture flasks. Smaller culture flasks, such as a 25 cm2 cell culture flask, can also be used.

Problem 3

Insufficient induction of cellular senescence in dermal fibroblasts (Step 18).

Potential solution

If the cell growth rate does not decrease significantly, this suggests that cellular senescence has not been sufficiently induced. The expected growth rate for normal (non-senescent) fibroblasts is characterized by a doubling time of 5–7 days, measured from seeding a specific number of cells until 80% confluency is reached in the flask. In contrast, senescent fibroblasts fail to reach 80% confluency in the flask even after at least two weeks.

Further culturing and additional passages are recommended until a marked reduction in growth rate is observed. Senescent fibroblasts typically exhibit an enlarged, flattened morphology.

Problem 4

Low yield of isolated skin immune cells (Step 32).

Potential solution

As mentioned in Step 24, it is important to finely mince the skin sample. The sample should be minced until the skin is in very small pieces (less than 1 mm). Additionally, we recommend pipetting cell solution up and down to facilitate digestion with a plastic pipette before filtering the cells through a strainer in Step 27. Note that the yield of isolated skin immune cells is also donor dependent.

Problem 5

Poor cytotoxic activity of skin immune cells against senescent fibroblasts (Step 34).

Potential solution

Regarding the status of fibroblasts, it is recommended to ensure that cellular senescence is sufficiently induced (See problem 3).

Regarding the condition of immune cells, particularly T cells, the quality of the human skin sample may be a contributing factor. This may be influenced by factors such as the freshness of the sample and the composition of immune cells in the donor’s skin (Step 32).

Problem 6

Observation of the excessive cytotoxic effects of immune cells on normal (non-senescent) fibroblasts (Step 34).

Potential solution

As noted in Step 33, we recommend using fibroblasts that have undergone five to eight passages as normal (non-senescent) fibroblasts. Additionally, a higher cell seeding density (5–10×103 cells/well) is recommended at the start of co-culture experiments, as excessively low seeding densities may compromise cell viability and make it difficult to use the cells as normal controls.
