Explainable machine learning framework for predicting cobalt ion removal by natural hematite
Authors: Arwa Saad, Abdelmoty M Ahmed, Mohamed Shaban, Ahmed A Elngar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCobalt contamination in aquatic systems presents considerable environmental and public health concerns. A hybrid artificial intelligence framework was developed for accurate modeling, and this study investigated natural hematite (α-Fe2O3) as an economical and sustainable adsorbent for the extraction of cobalt ions (Co2+). A maximum removal effectiveness of 89.7% was achieved at 100 ppm during batch adsorption tests conducted under various operational conditions, including contact time (10–120 min), adsorbent dosage (0.01–0.10 g), initial cobalt concentration (100–1000 ppm), and temperature (34–90 °C). Statistical analyses indicated that temperature and contact time were the most influential parameters (p < 0.01). Among the evaluated models, the contrastive learning XGBoost approach demonstrated superior predictive accuracy, achieving R2 = 0.987 ± 0.0137 (95% CI: 0.9748–0.9845), RMSE = 1.8676 ± 0.8509 (95% CI: 1.2590–3.4763), MAE = 0.0500 ± 0.0200 (95% CI: 0.0100–0.0300), and MAPE = 1.6753 ± 0.5802 (95% CI: 1.2602–2.0904). Introduction

Water contamination by heavy metals remains one of the most pressing global environmental challenges, particularly in regions undergoing rapid industrialization. Among these pollutants, cobalt ions (Co2+) are of particular concern due to their high toxicity, environmental persistence, and strong potential for bioaccumulation. Anthropogenic activities such as battery manufacturing, electroplating, pigment production, and mining are the primary sources of cobalt discharge, posing significant ecological and public health risks. Consequently, the efficient remediation of cobalt from aquatic systems has become both an environmental necessity and a regulatory priority1.

Adsorption has emerged as a widely adopted, cost-effective, and scalable technique for heavy metal removal. Natural hematite (α-Fe2O3), a readily available iron oxide mineral, has attracted considerable attention as a promising adsorbent owing to its favorable surface characteristics and strong affinity for metal binding. Nonetheless, conventional optimization of adsorption parameters, including contact time, adsorbent dosage, initial concentration, and temperature, is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and often impractical for real-time deployment in dynamic industrial systems2.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has demonstrated strong potential in environmental remediation by enabling predictive modeling and process optimization. ML models can efficiently learn from experimental datasets, minimize the need for exhaustive trial-and-error testing, and uncover hidden patterns to support process design and decision-making. However, the practical application of ML in adsorption studies remains limited by two key challenges: (i) insufficient interpretability, which reduces trust and applicability in real-world contexts, and (ii) inadequate validation across diverse operating conditions, particularly for cobalt remediation3,4.

Several research studies in the field of sustainable water resources have been conducted, including the work. In5, soil heavy metal contamination remains a critical issue due to challenges in mapping spatial distribution and identifying high-risk areas. To address this, a hybrid machine learning approach was developed, integrating random forest (RF), fuzzy c-means clustering, and inverse distance weighted interpolation, utilizing 577 soil samples. The random forest model outperformed multiple linear regression in predicting the concentrations of metal ions, achieving R2 values between 0.76 and 0.86. The key influencing factors varied for each metal, with industrial activity, soil pH, and population density playing significant roles.

In5 an improved Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN) was developed to predict heavy metal content in soil, overcoming the challenges of nonlinearities and delays in traditional prediction methods. The enhancements significantly boosted prediction accuracy, with cadmium increasing from 89.56 to 89.85% and lead from 79.44 to 81.81%.

In6 proposed a hybrid model integrating data decomposition and machine learning (SVM and PSO-BP-NN) to predict cobalt ion concentrations in zinc hydrometallurgy, achieving high accuracy with an R2 of 0.833 and significant reductions in RMSE and MAPE. However, the study is limited by its use of a small, proprietary dataset, computational intensity that may hinder real-time applications, and a focus solely on cobalt ions without addressing other potential contaminants or uncertainty quantification in predictions.

In7 carbonaceous materials have been widely utilized as adsorbents for heavy metals, but determining their adsorption capacity remains a time-consuming and energy-intensive process. To overcome this challenge, three tree-based machine learning techniques, random forest, gradient boosting decision tree, and extreme gradient boosting, were developed to predict the adsorption capacity of eight heavy metals on activated carbons, biochar, and carbon nanotubes. Based on 3,674 data points extracted from 151 journal articles, the gradient boosting decision tree demonstrated the best performance for the combined dataset, achieving an R2 of 0.9707 and an RMSE of 0.1420.

In8, machine learning was used to simulate the adsorption of heavy metals by clay minerals, analyzing the interactions of metal ions with montmorillonite and kaolinite. Six machine learning techniques, Random Forest, Extremely Randomized Trees, Gradient Boosting Decision Tree, Extreme Gradient Boosting, Light Gradient Boosting, and Category Boosting, were applied for feature engineering. The pH50-Box method, combined with Extreme Gradient Boosting, was identified as the optimal approach, achieving the highest predictive performance with an R2 of 0.977 and an RMSE of 4.158%.

In9, conducted a large-scale study using visible and near-infrared (VNIR) spectroscopy combined with eight ML algorithms, including XGBoost, to classify cobalt contamination in over 18,000 top-soil samples. Their optimized XGBoost model achieved the highest predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.904). This study illustrates the potential of ML, particularly XGBoost, for national-scale environmental assessments involving cobalt.

In10, applied artificial neural networks (ANNs) to model the leaching behavior of cobalt and copper from oxidized ores under varying process conditions. Using a 5–10–2 ANN architecture with backpropagation, they achieved 95.4% cobalt recovery with high predictive performance (R2 = 0.97). Their work highlights the role of machine learning in optimizing hydrometallurgical processes for cobalt recovery from ores, complementing more common adsorption-focused studies.

Despite these advances, existing studies present notable limitations, as shown in Table 1. Many focus on metals other than cobalt, employ computationally intensive models that limit scalability, or lack explainability, an essential feature for translating predictive models into practical environmental applications. Furthermore, very few studies have systematically integrated adsorption experiments with explainable machine learning to optimize the cobalt ion.

To address these gaps, this study proposes a comprehensive and practical AI-driven framework for the prediction and optimization of cobalt ion removal from water using natural hematite as an adsorbent. Our methodology integrates laboratory experimentation with a hybrid machine learning pipeline that combines contrastive learning, a powerful representation learning technique with XGBoost (R2 = 0.9966) to improve prediction robustness and accuracy.

The objective of this study is to integrate adsorption experiments using natural hematite (α-Fe2O3) with an explainable hybrid artificial intelligence framework to provide statistically validated and interpretable predictions of cobalt ion (Co2+) removal efficiency. Batch adsorption experiments were performed under varying operational conditions, and the resulting dataset was analyzed using contrastive learning XGBoost as the primary technique and TabNet for comparison. Statistical evaluation through 95% confidence intervals and p-values confirmed the superior performance of the proposed hybrid framework (R2 = 0.987, MAPE < 2%), demonstrating both robustness and accuracy. Beyond predictive performance, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) were employed to interpret model outputs, consistently identifying temperature and contact time as the most influential parameters with statistical significance at the 95% confidence level.

The contributions of this work are threefold: (i) systematic evaluation of natural hematite as a sustainable adsorbent for cobalt removal, (ii) application of advanced machine learning to reduce experimental workload and enhance predictive reliability, and (iii) demonstration of an interpretable hybrid framework that effectively links laboratory adsorption experiments with explainable AI analysis.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: section “Materials and methods” presents the materials, experimental design, and machine learning methods used for cobalt ion removal modeling. Section “Exploratory data analysis” reports experimental adsorption results, predictive model performance, statistical analysis, and interpretability using SHAP values. Section “Result” discusses comparative evaluation with state-of-the-art studies and highlights the advantages of the proposed framework. Finally, section “Conclusion” concludes the study, emphasizing the main findings, practical implications, and directions for future research.

Materials

Natural hematite ore (sourced from Saudi Arabia) is used as an adsorbent. Subsequently, three samples of hematite were milled for 2 h, 4 h, and 6 h, respectively, at a constant speed of 1000 rpm. Finally, all three milled samples were subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 120 °C for 6 h.

A stock solution of cobalt (II) ions (Co2+) was prepared by dissolving cobalt acetate tetrahydrate (CH3COO2Co∙4H2O, 249.08 g/mol, Merck) in deionized water. Working solutions with initial cobalt concentrations ranging from 1000 − 100 ppm (parts per million) were prepared for adsorption studies.

Adsorption experiments

Adsorption experiments were conducted to evaluate the removal of Co (II) ions using natural hematite. Preliminary experiments were conducted to determine the optimal contact time, varying it from 1 min to 360 min. The results indicated that a contact time of 120 min (2 h) was sufficient to achieve equilibrium.

Three series of adsorption experiments were conducted using hematite under varying conditions. The temperature varied between 34 °C and 90 °C, with 34 °C selected as the standard experimental temperature to reflect the room temperature during the period when the experiments were conducted.

The adsorbent dosage changed from 0.01 g to 0.1 g to assess adsorption efficiency at different levels of adsorbent availability. A dosage of 0.05 g, the midpoint of this range, was chosen as the constant value for specific experiments to provide a balanced evaluation of adsorption capacity.

The initial Co (II) ion concentration ranged from 100 ppm to 1000 ppm, representing a wide spectrum of contamination levels. 100 ppm simulated lower contamination, while 1000 ppm reflected highly polluted wastewater. This range allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of adsorption performance under diverse conditions, ensuring the robustness of the results. A constant contact time of 120 min and continuous agitation were maintained throughout the experiments to ensure consistency and reliable data collection.

The ranges of the experimental parameters were selected based on previous adsorption studies and practical considerations. Contact time (10–120 min) was chosen to capture adsorption kinetics up to equilibrium, which typically occurs within 2 h in heavy metal adsorption systems11. Adsorbent dosage (0.01–0.10 g) represented a typical low-to-high range while avoiding particle agglomeration12. Initial cobalt concentrations (100–1000 ppm) reflected both moderately contaminated water and highly polluted wastewater, consistent with reported industrial effluents3. Temperature (34–90 °C) was selected to cover ambient laboratory conditions and to study the effect of elevated temperature on adsorption performance8.

The removal efficiency of Co (II) ions using natural hematite was determined by analyzing the change in absorbance at the characteristic wavelength of cobalt ions. The concentration of Co (II) ions before and after adsorption was quantified using a double-beam spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer Lambda 990 UV/Vis/NIR, PerkinElmer Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Exploratory data analysis

The experimental dataset comprised 1,219 data points, each corresponding to a unique adsorption run conducted under a specific combination of four independent variables: contact time (10–120 min), catalyst dose (0.01–0.1 g), initial cobalt ion concentration (100–1000 ppm), and temperature (34–90 °C). All features were numerical, and no missing values were detected. Each removal efficiency value was obtained from a single measurement under controlled laboratory conditions without replication or averaging. The dataset was compiled from three experimental series, each using a differently milled natural hematite sample to evaluate adsorption behavior. Although a full factorial design was not employed, the parameters were systematically varied to ensure diverse coverage of the experimental space. The number of unique values for each feature confirmed the richness and variety of the dataset.

Explainable machine learning framework proposed

This study introduces a comprehensive hybrid machine learning framework designed to predict the removal efficiency of heavy metals in water treatment processes. The proposed framework in Fig. 5 for predicting cobalt ion (Co²⁺) removal efficiency consists of seven sequential stages. Stage 1 involves the use of a comprehensive experimental dataset. In Stage 2, the process of adsorption is conducted. In Stage 3, relevant features were selected for modeling, with the four experimental parameters identified as the key input variables, and the removal efficiency as the target output. In Stage 4, it involved preprocessing and dataset splitting. In Stage 5, supervised machine learning techniques and optimization were trained and evaluated using the prepared dataset. In Stage 6, model evaluation, among the tested models, XGBoost demonstrated the highest predictive performance and was selected as the optimal model. Finally, Stage 7 involves using the trained model to predict removal efficiency under various input conditions.

Explainable machine learning framework proposed.

Contrastive learning

To improve how features are represented using basic tabular data, this study uses contrastive learning, which teaches a neural encoder to tell the difference between sample pairs that are similar and those that are not based on how well they remove something. Positive pairs (similar efficiency) are brought closer together in the embedding space, while negative pairs are moved further apart using a contrastive loss function. XGBoost on contrastive embeddings

After the contrastive learning phase, the trained encoder was used on the whole dataset to create small, useful embeddings that represent the main aspects of the adsorption process. These embeddings were used as input features for an Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) regressor to predict the removal efficiency of cobalt ions. XGBoost was selected for its superior performance on tabular data, robustness to multicollinearity, and ability to model complex feature interactions. Furthermore, its scalability and regularization capabilities make it well-suited for predictive modeling in scientific domains. Effect of contact time

The adsorption efficiency vs. (a) contact time, (b) adsorbent dosage, (c) temperature, and (d) metal ion concentration.

These results indicate that the bowl milling process significantly enhances the adsorbent’s ability to capture metal ions, due to improved surface properties and increased active sites20,21. However, after a certain duration, no significant changes in removal efficiency were observed, suggesting the system had reached adsorption equilibrium.

Effect of adsorbent dosage

Figure 7(b) investigates the effect of adsorbent dosage on the removal efficiency of metal ions. The adsorption performance was evaluated by varying the adsorbent dosage from 0.02 g to 0.10 g while keeping the contact time at 2 h, temperature at 34 °C, and initial metal ion concentration at 1000 ppm. The removal efficiency increased with increasing adsorbent dosage, reaching a maximum of approximately 81% at 0.09 g, as shown in Fig. 7(b). This improvement can be attributed to the increased availability of active adsorption sites as the dosage increases. However, beyond 0.09 g, a slight decline in removal efficiency due to the agglomeration of excess adsorbent particles, which reduces the effective surface area available for adsorption12,22.

Effect of temperature

Figure 7(c) shows the effect of temperature on the removal efficiency of metal ions. The adsorption performance was evaluated over a temperature range of 34 °C to 90 °C while keeping the contact time at 2 h, adsorbent dosage at 0.05 g, and initial metal ion concentration at 1000 ppm. As shown in Fig. 7(c), the removal efficiency decreases with increasing temperature. Initially, at 34 °C, the removal efficiency is around 75%, but as the temperature rises, it gradually declines, reaching below 30% at 90 °C11. This trend shows that the adsorption process is exothermic, meaning that higher temperatures reduce the adsorbent’s ability to capture metal ions3,23.

Effect of metal ion concentration

Figure 7(d) illustrates the effect of metal ion concentration on removal efficiency (%) at room temperature (34 °C) with a contact time of 2 h and an adsorbent dosage of 0.05 g. From Fig. 7(d), it is evident that h, the removal efficiency, is highest at 100 ppm, reaching 89.7%. However, as the metal ion concentration increases, the removal efficiency gradually decreases, reaching 79.8% at 1000 ppm. At lower concentrations, the adsorbent provides sufficient active sites for effective ion removal, whereas at higher concentrations, the availability of active sites becomes a limiting factor, reducing the overall efficiency24–26.

The analysis revealed that contact time had a strong positive effect on removal efficiency, which increased steadily up to 120 min, beyond which equilibrium was reached. This indicates that 120 min is sufficient to achieve optimal cobalt adsorption. In contrast, temperature demonstrated a clear inverse relationship with removal efficiency, decreasing significantly as the temperature rose, consistent with the exothermic nature of the process. Regarding adsorbent dosage, increasing from 0.02 g to 0.09 g improved efficiency due to more available active sites, though a slight decline beyond 0.09 g was observed—likely due to particle agglomeration reducing surface area. Initial Co2+ concentration showed that removal was highest (89.7%) at 100 ppm and gradually declined to 79.8% at 1000 ppm, suggesting that adsorption sites became saturated at higher contaminant levels.

The SVR model provided competitive, yet inferior results compared with XGB, with higher error values (RMSE = 3.1182, MAE = 0.3000, R2 = 0.9656) and wider confidence intervals, suggesting less consistent performance. While its training time (0.13 s) was shorter, this gain in efficiency was offset by reduced predictive robustness. By contrast, the ANN (MLP) model showed the weakest performance (RMSE = 4.6617, MAE = 0.5000, MAPE = 5.2094, R2 = 0.9268), accompanied by broad confidence intervals that reflect variability and lower reliability. Moreover, its training time (0.92 s) was higher than both SVR and XGB, indicating limited efficiency. TabNet has moderate results (RMSE = 3.1587, MAE = 0.3500, R2 = 0.9326, MAPE = 5.2672) but required the longest training time (2.96 s). Despite its architectural sophistication, the model failed to surpass the predictive accuracy or robustness of XGB. In summary, the results demonstrate that XGB with Contrastive provides the most accurate, stable, and computationally efficient framework. The combination of contrastive learning with gradient boosting proves particularly effective in modeling the nonlinear adsorption dynamics of cobalt ion removal.

Framework interpretability

Explainability method (SHAP)

To enhance framework interpretability, SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) was employed to analyze the contribution of each input feature to the prediction made by the XGBoost model. Although the contrastive encoder was first used to obtain embeddings, SHAP was used on an XGBoost model that was trained directly with the original, scaled features (contact time, catalyst dose, metal ion, and temperature). This alternative approach allowed for a more intuitive interpretation of the model in terms of the raw experimental parameters. The SHAP analysis was conducted after training, using the Tree Explainer provided by the SHAP library. The output offered a detailed understanding of how each feature value influenced the model’s output for removing cobalt ions, revealing both global feature importance and local decision behavior for individual predictions.

The SHAP summary plot in Fig. 12 revealed that temperature and contact time were the most influential features in predicting removal efficiency, followed by metal ion concentration and catalyst dose. High temperature values tended to negatively impact the model’s prediction, while moderate contact time and catalyst dose values showed positive contributions. The SHAP values showed how changes in each feature affected predictions compared to the average, helping to identify important factors that influence adsorption performance. This interpretability not only confirms the physical trends observed in the experimental data but also builds trust in the decision-making of the machine learning technique. The integration of SHAP into the framework underscores the utility of explainable AI (XAI) tools for supporting data-driven environmental decision-making.

Explaining framework predictions with SHAP values

The SHAP waterfall plot provides a detailed explanation of how individual input features contributed to the model’s prediction for a specific sample31. Figure 13 summarizes the SHAP analysis of the XGBoost model, showing the relative contribution of each parameter. Temperature and contact time were the most influential features, followed by cobalt concentration and dosage. The negative impact of increasing temperature and the positive role of contact time are in agreement with the experimental results, reinforcing the reliability of the model’s interpretability. The combination of experimental observation and explainable AI ensures that the framework is not only accurate but also transparent in identifying the driving factors behind adsorption performance. In this instance, the base value (i.e., the average model prediction across all features) was 61.892, while the final predicted output was 82.654. The input features’ additive contributions explain this difference.

Although temperature had a negative raw value (–0.828), it contributed significantly positively to the prediction, adding + 16.02. Similarly, Metal Ion, with a value of − 0.121, contributed + 2.10. These results indicate that the model has learned a pattern where lower temperature and heavy metals are associated with higher target outputs. In addition, contact time (value = 0.473) positively influenced the prediction, contributing + 3.29. In contrast, Catalyst Dose (value = 0.852) had a minor negative impact, reducing the prediction by − 0.65. This analysis shows that a feature’s value and the model’s ability to associate it with the target variable during training both affect its influence on prediction.

The adsorption experiments in this study achieved a maximum removal efficiency of approximately 89.7% at 100 ppm under optimized conditions. These findings are consistent with recent reports in the literature. For instance, achieved up to 98.6% removal of Pb2+ using g-C4N3 nanosheets32, while33 reported cobalt removal efficiencies in the range of 80–88% through process-intensified adsorption systems. Similarly34, demonstrated rejection efficiencies of 91–93% for oily wastewater using PPSU/TiO₂ nanocomposite membranes. In comparison, the natural hematite-based system developed in this work exhibits competitive performance, particularly considering its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and environmental sustainability. Moreover, the integration of machine learning models enhances this approach by enabling accurate prediction and optimization across diverse experimental conditions, a dimension that has received limited attention in prior adsorption studies.

This demonstrates that while hematite is a simple material, its performance combined with AI modeling is competitive with more complex nanomaterials. Recent studies have also highlighted the effectiveness of advanced materials and process intensification strategies in enhancing cobalt remediation. For instance, inorganic–organic hybrid adsorbents have shown considerable adsorption capacities for cobalt ions32, while modified polymeric membranes have achieved pollutant rejection efficiencies exceeding 90%34. In addition, optimized reactor designs and process modeling approaches have been proposed to further improve treatment efficiency33. Although these approaches demonstrate promising performance, they often require complex synthesis routes, high costs, or specialized operational conditions, which limit their scalability for practical applications.

Conclusion

This study evaluated a hybrid explainable machine learning framework for predictive optimization and examined natural hematite (α-Fe₂O₃) as a sustainable and economical adsorbent for the removal of cobalt ions (Co2+). Under carefully monitored laboratory conditions, batch adsorption experiments reached a maximum removal efficiency of 89.7%. The contrastive learning XGBoost model outperformed TabNet and showed high predictive accuracy (R2 = 0.9966, RMSE = 1.08); the most important parameters, according to SHAP analysis, were temperature and contact time. Statistical analysis, such as mean ± standard deviation, 95% CIs, and pairwise significance testing (p < 0.05), supported the reported metrics. Although encouraging, these findings are still only applicable to artificial laboratory tests and a small range of operating conditions. The long-term regeneration capacity, cost-effectiveness, and suitability for complex real wastewater systems have yet to be confirmed. Therefore, future research should concentrate on evaluating the reusability of hematite, testing the framework with industrial wastewater, and broadening the parameter space (e.g., pH, coexisting ions). Confirming the practical viability of combining explainable artificial intelligence with natural mineral adsorption for sustainable water treatment will require larger datasets and scalability studies.
