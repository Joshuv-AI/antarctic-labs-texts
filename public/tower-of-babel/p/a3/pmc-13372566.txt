Postoperative Ischemic Bronchitis Progressing to Bronchopleural Fistula After Robotic Lobectomy: A First Report
Authors: Rahaf A. Jereisat, Nawras Ibrahim, Majd Alnas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPostoperative ischemic bronchitis (POIB) is a known precursor to bronchopleural fistula (BPF) after lung resection with systematic lymphadenectomy, occurring in 2.5%–3.2% of cases, yet has only been reported following open thoracotomy. We report a case of POIB progressing to BPF following robotic lobectomy. A 51‐year‐old male underwent robotic‐assisted right lower lobectomy with systematic lymph node dissection for stage IB lung adenocarcinoma. Three weeks postoperatively, he developed dyspnea and hemoptysis. Diagnostic bronchoscopy revealed transmural necrosis of the right mainstem bronchus (a 2.5 × 4 cm defect with grade 4–5 ischemic changes) which required surgical repair. Follow‐up bronchoscopy confirmed complete healing. As robotic lobectomy enables more extensive lymphadenectomy than conventional approaches, pulmonologists should recognize POIB as a potential complication. Systematic postoperative bronchoscopy between days 7 and 12 should be considered in high‐risk patients, as 80% of POIB cases are asymptomatic and early detection may prevent progression to BPF.

Introduction

Bronchopleural fistula (BPF) is a rare and potentially life‐threatening complication, occurring approximately 0.44%–1.2% after lobectomies with mortality rates of 14%–25%, and frequently presents with pneumothorax and empyema [1, 2]. Established risk factors include male sex, right lower lobectomy, and extensive mediastinal lymph node dissection [1, 3]. Postoperative ischemic bronchitis (POIB) results from disruption of bronchial arterial supply during lymphadenectomy, occurs in 2.5%–3.2% of patients and is a known precursor to BPF usually around 8–10 days post op; 10% of POIB cases progress to BPF versus 0.4% without ischemic changes [4, 5].

Robotic lobectomy achieves superior lymph node harvest compared to video‐assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). The RVlob randomized trial demonstrated higher lymph node yield (11 vs. 10 nodes, p = 0.02) and more stations examined (6 vs. 5, p < 0.001) with robotic approaches [6]. However, POIB has only been described after open thoracotomy, and the implications of more aggressive robotic lymphadenectomy for bronchial blood supply have not been explored [4, 5].

We report the first case of extensive mainstem bronchial ischemic necrosis following robotic lobectomy presenting as delayed BPF, with a literature review of POIB and its bronchoscopic evaluation.

Case Report

A 51‐year‐old male former smoker (20 pack‐years, quit 15 years prior) with COPD presented with an incidental 19 mm right lower lobe nodule on lung cancer screening CT. Robotic bronchoscopy with transbronchial biopsy confirmed moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma (TTF‐1 positive). PET‐CT showed no mediastinal uptake or distant metastases.

He underwent robotic‐assisted right lower lobectomy with systematic lymph node dissection, harvesting 23 nodes from stations 4R, 7, 8, 9, 11, and 12. Final pathology confirmed pT2aN0M0 (stage IB) adenocarcinoma with visceral pleural invasion and negative margins. He was discharged on postoperative day (POD) 2. On POD 14, he presented with orthopnea; chest radiograph showed right hydropneumothorax (Figure 1E,F). On POD 21, he developed worsening dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, and hemoptysis with tachycardia and leukocytosis (16.9 × 103/μL). CT demonstrated moderate right hydropneumothorax (Figure 1G,H). Chest tube placement drained 800 mL of bloody exudative fluid.

Diagnostic bronchoscopy revealed extensive mucosal ulceration and transmural necrosis of the posterior membranous wall of the right mainstem bronchus, extending from near the carina to the bronchus intermedius; a 2.5 × 4 cm defect (Figure 2B). The right middle lobe bronchus demonstrated > 90% narrowing. The left tracheobronchial tree was normal (Figure 2A). Tissue biopsy confirmed ischemic necrosis (Figure 2C,D).

Using the Ludwig classification, findings were graded 4–5 (transmural necrosis with perforation) [7]. Endoscopic closure was contraindicated according to established criteria (Table 2; large defect size far exceeding the 8 mm threshold), non‐viable tissue, and central mainstem location which required surgical referral [8].

The patient underwent thoracotomy with repair using acellular dermal matrix, bovine pericardial patch, and intercostal muscle pedicle flap reinforcement. The patient was extubated the same day and discharged after 2 weeks on room air. Surveillance bronchoscopy at 6 weeks confirmed complete healing with patent airways and intact mucosa.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of bronchial ischemic necrosis following robotic lobectomy. Several features distinguish this case; the ischemic necrosis involved the proximal mainstem bronchus rather than the bronchial stump, where POIB most commonly occurs [5]. The defect size (2.5 × 4 cm) and severity (grade 4–5) far exceeded typical POIB lesions. The delayed presentation at 3 weeks is characteristic of ischemia‐related rather than mechanical BPF [1]. The mechanism is well‐established; subcarinal lymph node dissection disrupts intercostobronchial arteries that supply the airways via the tracheal proper sheath [5]. The 23‐node harvest in this case (including complete subcarinal dissection) likely caused more extensive proximal vascular disruption than typically seen with open approaches. As robotic lobectomy consistently achieves greater lymph node yields than VATS [6], with meta‐analyses confirming superior nodal staging [9, 10], this complication may become increasingly relevant.

Most importantly, this case highlights the value of systematic postoperative bronchoscopy. Benhamed et al. demonstrated that routine bronchoscopy between POD 7–12 detects POIB before BPF progression, with 80% of cases being asymptomatic [4]. Early detection allows intervention with hyperbaric oxygen therapy, which achieves 82% success when initiated early [11]. In this case, symptoms appeared on POD 14 but bronchoscopy was delayed until POD 21 when frank BPF had developed. Earlier surveillance might have detected grade 2–3 changes amenable to conservative management.

We recommend maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients presenting with delayed respiratory symptoms undergoing robotic lobectomy with extensive lymphadenectomy who have additional risk factors for POIB.
