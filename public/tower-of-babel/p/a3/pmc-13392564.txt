KLF16 promotes immune escape in hepatocellular carcinoma cells by upregulating PD-L1 expression via PRKDC
Authors: Shujia Kong, Chen Zhao, Fangyi Lu, Yanwen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•KLF16 is highly expressed in liver cancer samples and is associated with immunity.•KLF16 upregulates PD-L1 expression by promoting PRKDC transcription.•KLF16 knockdown enhances T-cell antitumor immunity.•KLF16 knockdown enhances the therapeutic efficacy of combined immune checkpoint blockade.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a clinically common primary malignant tumor of the liver and is the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. Early-stage HCC patients can be cured through surgical resection, liver transplantation, or local interventional therapy, whereas advanced HCC primarily relies on systemic treatment and often has a poor prognosis [2]. In recent years, with in-depth research on the tumor immune microenvironment and the interactions between immune cells and cancer cells, immunotherapy has become an important treatment strategy following surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [3]. Programmed death receptor 1 (PD-1) is a common immunosuppressive factor on the surface of T cells, and programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) is overexpressed on the surface of HCC cells. PD-L1 binds to PD-1, inhibiting T-cell proliferation and activation and leading to T-cell inactivation, ultimately resulting in immune escape and treatment failure [4]. Currently, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as PD-L1/PD-1-targeted drugs, are used in the clinical treatment of HCC [5,6]. However, only a small number of patients benefit from them, and the occurrence of drug resistance remains a significant challenge, limiting their application in HCC [7]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop ICI sensitization therapies to improve the prognosis of patients with HCC.

Krüppel-like factors (KLFs) belong to the zinc finger transcription factor family and can bind to GT- or GC-rich regions of target genes to regulate transcription as activators or repressors, playing crucial roles in embryogenesis, development, and cancer progression [8]. KLF16 is located on chromosome 19 and is involved in various cellular activities, such as proliferation, metabolism, and death [9]. In recent years, the role of KLF16 in various cancers has been investigated, revealing heterogeneity. Mi et al. [10] demonstrated that KLF16 positively regulates SMAD6 to promote the proliferation and migration of pancreatic cancer cells. Bang et al. [11] revealed that high expression of KLF16 is associated with poor prognosis in patients with breast cancer and that knocking down its expression can inhibit cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. In stomach adenocarcinoma, KLF16 may be involved in immune evasion processes, and silencing its expression can suppress cancer cell proliferation [12]. Recent studies have revealed that KLF16 can increase PD-L1 expression, thereby promoting the progression and immune evasion of HCC [13]. However, the mechanisms underlying the role of KLF16 in PD-L1-driven immune evasion in HCC remain incompletely understood.

The protein kinase DNA-activated catalytic polypeptide gene (PRKDC) encodes the catalytic subunit of DNA-dependent protein kinase (DNA-PK), a serine/threonine protein kinase that plays a critical role in the repair of DNA double-strand breaks [14]. Beyond its role in DNA-damage repair, PRKDC has been increasingly recognized as a pro-tumorigenic factor in multiple cancers. In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), inhibiting PRKDC can enhance the infiltration of M1 macrophages (M1-polarized macrophages exhibit cytotoxicity against various LUAD cell lines), thereby triggering apoptosis in LUAD cells [15]. In osteosarcoma, PRKDC overexpression confers docetaxel resistance and contributes to chemotherapy failure [16]. Additionally, as a serine/threonine protein kinase, DNA-PK phosphorylates specific substrates—for example, the autoimmune regulator AIRE—and thereby controls their transcriptional activity [17]. Recent studies have revealed that DNA-PK directly phosphorylates PD-L1 at threonine-20 and threonine-22, stabilizing PD-L1 protein and thereby driving immune escape in gastric cancer cells and promote tumor development [18]. However, whether DNA-PK in HCC drives immune escape by stabilizing PD-L1 expression requires further investigation. Furthermore, exploring whether the transcriptional regulator KLF16 can influence immune escape in HCC through the regulation of PD-L1 stability through PRKDC is important.

In summary, this study aimed to investigate the role of KLF16 in immune escape in HCC and elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample collection

This work used residual surgical specimens; it was an observational sample-collection study and did not constitute a clinical trial. A total of 16 paired HCC and matched adjacent non-tumor liver tissues were collected from patients who underwent surgical resection at Yunnan Cancer Hospital. None of the patients had received radiotherapy or chemotherapy prior to surgery. Written informed consent was obtained from every participant before specimen collection, and the protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Yunnan Cancer Hospital (approval number: KYLX2024–107) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Cell culture, transduction, and treatment

The mouse hepatocellular carcinoma cell line Hepa1–6 (CL-0105) and the human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T (CL-0005) were purchased from Procell, China. They were cultured in DMEM (with sodium pyruvate) high-glucose medium (11,995,040; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (A5669701; Gibco, USA) and 1% (v/v) penicillin‒streptomycin (abs9244; Absin, China) under standard conditions at 37 °C and 5% (v/v) CO2. For cell transduction, when the cells reached 70% confluence, they were seeded in medium containing KLF16 knockdown (sh-KLF16) or knockdown control (sh-NC), PRKDC overexpression (OE-PRKDC), KLF16 overexpression (OE-KLF16), or overexpression control (OE-NC) lentivirus (MOI=20; Hanbio, China) and incubated for 48 h. The transduction efficiency was then assessed via Western blotting. To investigate the effect of KLF16 on antitumor immune responses, Hepa1–6 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL recombinant mouse interferon-γ protein (IFN-γ; IF005; Sigma‒Aldrich, China) for 24 h to activate immune responses.

Animal experiments

All animal-related experiments in this study were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Kunming Medical University (approval number: kmmu20240079). Five-week-old male BALB/c nude mice and BALB/c mice (weight 16–18 g) were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. After one week of adaptive feeding, the mice were randomly grouped, and an HCC tumor-bearing model was established following previous methods [19]. Briefly, Hepa1–6 cells were resuspended in Matrigel solution to prepare a cell suspension at a concentration of 5 × 107 cells/mL. Subsequently, 100 μL of the cell suspension was subcutaneously injected into the right back of BALB/c mice and BALB/c nude mice to establish an HCC tumor-bearing model. For the sh-KLF16 group, BALB/c mice and BALB/c nude mice were injected with Hepa1–6 cells with KLF16 knockdown as described above. Additionally, to investigate the tumor-suppressive effect of KLF16 knockdown combined with immune checkpoint therapy, following the method of Engeland et al. [20], BALB/c mice were intraperitoneally injected with 100 μg PD-1 monoclonal antibody (PD-1 mAb, 16–9985–82, Invitrogen, USA) and CTLA-4 monoclonal antibody (CTLA-4 mAb, 16–1521–82, Invitrogen, USA) every 3 days starting 3 days after injection of Hepa1–6 or KLF16-knockdown Hepa1–6 cells. Each experimental group included no fewer than 5 mice. The tumor volume was measured every 4 days after cell injection and calculated using the formula 1/2 × length × width2. All mice were euthanized 15 days after cell injection, and the tumor tissues were dissected, weighed, photographed, and used for subsequent studies. All tumor-growth, immune-infiltration and combination-therapy experiments described in this manuscript were performed in mice.

RT‒qPCR was performed using SupRealQ Purple Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q412–02; Vazyme, China). β-actin was used as the internal reference, and the experimental results were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Western blot

Total protein from clinical tissues, Hepa1–6 cells, and tumor tissues from mice was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (HY-K1001, MCE, USA) supplemented with 1% (v/v) protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor. The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein concentration assay kit (PC0020; Solarbio, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS‒PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (88,518; Thermo Fisher, USA). After blocking with 5% (w/v) skim milk in TBST, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against KLF16 (1:1000; PA5–103,504; Invitrogen, USA), PD-L1 (1:1000; ab213480; Abcam, UK), DNA-PK (1:1000; PA5–86,134; Invitrogen, USA), and β-actin (1:1000; MA5–15,739; Invitrogen, USA). The next day, the membranes were washed three times and treated with the corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The protein bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence reagent (36223ES10; Yeasen, China) and semiquantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence

After the Hepa1–6 cell culture was complete, the cells were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-PD-L1 antibody (1:100; ab213480; Abcam, UK). The next day, the cells were incubated with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, followed by observation, imaging, and statistical analysis of the mean fluorescence intensity using a fluorescence microscope to characterize protein expression levels.

Luciferase reporter gene assay

The pGL3 and pRL-TK plasmids were purchased from Promega (USA), and the PD-L1 promoter sequence was cloned and inserted into pGL3 to construct the pGL3-PD-L1 plasmid. HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. The following day, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (L3000075; Invitrogen, USA) was used to cotransfect pGL3-PD-L1, pRL-TK, and KLF16 interference (si-KLF16) or control interference (si-NC) plasmids (Fulengen, China) into the cells. After 24 h, a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (HY-K1013, MCE, USA) was used to measure luciferase activity (firefly luciferase relative activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity).

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissues from BALB/c nude mice and BALB/c mice were fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm) were cut from the tissue wax blocks. After dewaxing and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed using 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Then, the sections were treated with 3% (w/v) H2O2 for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After being washed with PBS three times, the sections were blocked with 10% (v/v) goat serum for 30 min. The sections were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody against Ki67 (1:100; ab16667; Abcam, UK), followed by treatment with the corresponding secondary antibody. Color development was carried out using a DAB chromogenic kit (G1212–200T; Servicebio, China). Finally, the sections were re-stained with hematoxylin for 3 min. Microscopy was used to capture images and quantify the relative protein expression levels.

Flow cytometry

The abundance of CD8+ T cells in tumor-bearing HCC tissues was detected by flow cytometry. Briefly, the tumor tissues were collected and cut into small pieces using sterile ophthalmic surgical scissors. Then, the tumor-bearing tissues were treated with 0.25% trypsin to prepare a single-cell suspension. The tumor pieces were digested in a 37 °C water bath shaker for 30–60 min until the tissue is completely dispersed into single-cell suspension. Then, the cell suspension was passed through a 70 μm cell strainer. Erythrocytes were lysed with ACK lysis buffer (A1049201, Gibco, USA) for 5 min at room temperature, and the resulting single-cell suspension was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL in FACS buffer. First, the cells were treated with Fc-blocking agent (156,604, Biolegend, USA) at 4 °C for 15 min to prevent non-specific antibody binding. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with anti-CD8 (1:100; 11–0081–82; eBioscience, USA), anti-CD45 (1:100; 12–0451–82; eBioscience, USA), anti-IFN-γ (1:100; 12–7311–82; eBioscience, USA), or anti-GzmB (1:100; 12–8898–82; eBioscience, USA) antibodies at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. For intracellular staining of IFN-γ and GzmB, the cells were treated using the eBioscience™ cell fixation and permeabilization buffer kit (eBioscience, USA) prior to staining. Finally, the cells were loaded onto a flow cytometer for analysis. Viable lymphocytes were gated based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC). FSC reflects cell size, while SSC reflects cell complexity or granularity. By gating on the two-dimensional plot of FSC and SSC, debris and dead cells can be excluded, and the viable cell population can be retained.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are expressed as the mean ± SD. Statistical analysis of the experimental data was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.1 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The t-test was used to compare data between two groups, while one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare data among multiple groups, with Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. An p value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

KLF16 is highly expressed in liver cancer samples and is associated with immunity

Previous studies have shown that KLF16 plays a role in the progression of various cancers [10,11,21] and that KLF16 may be involved in the immune escape process of cancer cells [12]. Therefore, we validated the expression of KLF16 in HCC and its correlation with immunity. The expression of KLF16 was detected by RT‒qPCR and Western blotting, and the mRNA (Fig. 1A) and protein (Fig. 1B) expression levels of KLF16 were significantly elevated in clinical human-derived HCC tissues. Single-gene pathway enrichment analysis of KLF16 revealed that the T-cell receptor signaling pathway was significantly enriched (Fig. 1C), suggesting that KLF16 may be involved in immune processes in HCC. To explore the role of KLF16 in HCC immune responses, we knocked down KLF16 expression in Hepa1–6 cells. Western blot results revealed that compared with that in the sh-NC group, the expression level of KLF16 in the sh-KLF16 group was significantly lower (Fig. 1D), indicating the successful generation of Hepa1–6 cells with KLF16 knockdown. RT‒qPCR revealed that KLF16 knockdown inhibited the mRNA expression of TNFSF9 (Fig. 1E), IL7R (Fig. 1F), ZP3 (Fig. 1G), LCK (Fig. 1H), NR4A3 (Fig. 1I), and promoted IL6R mRNA level (Fig. 1J) in Hepa1–6 cells. INF-γ is the main antitumor effector factor produced by CD8+ T cells [22]. We treated Hepa1–6 cells with INF-γ to simulate the in vivo immune microenvironment and found that the expression of KLF16 and PD-L1 was significantly upregulated after IFN-γ treatment (Fig. 1K). These results indicate that KLF16 is upregulated in HCC and is associated with immunity.

KLF16 enhances PD-L1 expression in liver cancer cells

To subsequently investigate the impact of KLF16 on PD-L1 expression in HCC, we overexpressed KLF16 in Hepa1–6 cells. Western blot analysis of KLF16 expression revealed that the KLF16 expression level in the OE-KLF16 group was significantly greater than that in the OE-NC group (Fig. 2A), indicating the successful generation of Hepa1–6 cells overexpressing KLF16. Second, KLF16 overexpression promoted PD-L1 expression in Hepa1–6 cells (Fig. 2B). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that PD-L1 expression in Hepa1–6 cells was significantly upregulated after KLF16 overexpression (Fig. 2C). Conversely, knockdown of KLF16 in Hepa1–6 cells with or without IFN-γ treatment led to a significant reduction in PD-L1 expression levels (Fig. 2D–E). KLF16 upregulates PD-L1 expression by promoting PRKDC transcription

Next, we investigated whether KLF16 directly targets PD-L1 to regulate its expression. Through luciferase reporter gene assays, we found that transfection with KLF16 interference plasmids did not affect luciferase activity (Fig. 3A), indicating that KLF16 does not regulate PD-L1 expression by binding to PD-L1. To explore the mediators through which KLF16 regulates PD-L1 expression, we obtained genes that are differentially expressed in liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) from the TCGA database and proteins related to “immunity” from the UniProt database and further acquired KLF16 ChIP-Seq data in HCC cells (HepG2) from the Cistrome database. Venn diagram analysis of the three datasets revealed 64 overlapping genes, among which PRKDC ranked first on the basis of binding scores (Fig. 3B), and PRKDC expression was significantly upregulated in HCC (Fig. 3C). Using the UCSC database, we found that KLF16 binds to the promoter region of PRKDC (Fig. 3D). Additionally, we observed that knocking down KLF16 in Hepa1–6 cells suppressed PRKDC mRNA and DNA-PK protein expression, whereas KLF16 overexpression produced the opposite effects (Fig. 3E–F), suggesting that KLF16 may regulate PD-L1 expression via PRKDC. Notably, DNA-PK, the protein encoded by PRKDC, has been previously shown to stabilize PD-L1 expression by catalyzing PD-L1 phosphorylation at dual sites, T20 and T22 [18]. Therefore, to investigate whether KLF16 promotes PD-L1 expression in HCC through PRKDC, we overexpressed PRKDC in Hepa1–6 cells. Western blot analysis revealed that DNA-PK expression levels were significantly greater in the OE-PRKDC group than in the OE-NC group (Fig. 3G), confirming the successful generation of the PRKDC-overexpressing Hepa1–6 cells. Furthermore, we found that PD-L1 expression was partially upregulated in KLF16-knockdown Hepa1–6 cells after PRKDC overexpression (Fig. 3H–I). Knockdown of KLF16 enhances T-cell antitumor immunity

Next, we investigated the impact of KLF16 on T-cell-driven antitumor immunity in vivo. We found that knockdown of KLF16 inhibited tumor tissue growth in BALB/c nude mice (Fig. 4A–C) and reduced Ki67 expression in tumor tissues (Fig. 4D). The knockdown of KLF16 also suppressed tumor growth in BALB/c mice (Fig. 4E–G) and decreased Ki67 expression in tumor tissues (Fig. 4H). The inhibitory effect of KLF16 knockdown on tumor growth was more pronounced in immunocompetent mice than in nude mice, suggesting that the differential tumor-suppressive effects of KLF16 may stem from variations in immune surveillance mechanisms between the animal models. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that KLF16 knockdown upregulated CD8+ T-cell levels in the tumor tissues of BALB/c mice (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, the proportions of GzmB-positive (Fig. 4J) and IFN-γ-positive (Fig. 4K) CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues significantly increased after KLF16 knockdown. Additionally, KLF16 knockdown suppressed the expression of KLF16 and PD-L1 in mouse tumor tissues (Fig. 4L). Knockdown of KLF16 enhances the therapeutic efficacy of combined immune checkpoint blockade

In addition, in addition to PD-1, cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) on the surface of T cells is a common immune checkpoint that can bind to CD80 on the surface of antigen-presenting cells to inhibit T-cell activation [23], and combination therapy with a PD-1 mAb and a CTLA-4 mAb has already been applied in clinical HCC treatment [24]. Therefore, we investigated the effect of KLF16 knockdown on the therapeutic efficacy of combined PD-1 mAb and CTLA-4 mAb treatment in BALB/c mice. We found that KLF16 knockdown further suppressed tumor growth in PD-1 mAb- and CTLA-4 mAb-treated mice (Fig. 5A–C). Immunohistochemical staining revealed that the expression level of Ki67 in tumor tissues from mice treated with PD-1 mAb and CTLA-4 mAb was significantly reduced after KLF16 knockdown (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, KLF16 knockdown enhanced the effects of the PD-1 mAb and CTLA-4 mAb, leading to significant increases in CD8+ T-cell infiltration (Fig. 5E) and the proportions of GzmB-positive (Fig. 5F) and IFN-γ-positive (Fig. 5G) CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues. Discussion

Currently, immunotherapy centered on ICIs benefits only a small proportion of patients with HCC, with no significant improvement in overall survival [7]. This study revealed that KLF16 is upregulated in HCC, and the results of GSEA suggest that its function involves the T-cell immune receptor pathway. Knockdown of KLF16 can inhibit PRKDC transcription to downregulate PD-L1 expression, thereby enhancing T-cell antitumor immunity and improving the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 mAbs and CTLA-4 mAbs. These findings indicate that KLF16 may be a potential therapeutic target for immune evasion in HCC.

In recent years, the role of KLF16 in cancer has been extensively studied. Previous research has shown that high levels of KLF16 are associated with poor prognosis in patients with breast cancer and that knocking down KLF16 expression can inhibit the proliferation of cancer cells both in vitro and in vivo [11]. In gastric cancer, the overexpression of KLF16 is linked to shorter disease-free survival and overall survival in patients, and knocking down KLF16 can suppress the proliferation and metastasis of cancer cells by inhibiting the activation of the Notch signaling pathway [25]. In prostate cancer, KLF16 also promotes cancer cell growth by activating the MYC signaling pathway [26]. In HCC, KLF16 facilitates tumor development by activating glycolysis [27]. Consistent with previous findings, this study revealed that KLF16 is upregulated in HCC and that knocking down KLF16 inhibits the growth of tumor-bearing tissues, indicating that KLF16 is a risk factor for HCC progression. Additionally, KLF16 is involved in tumor-related immune responses. Xue et al. [12] demonstrated that KLF16 is an immune evasion signature (IES) gene. The risk score of the IES is negatively correlated with immune infiltration (including that of CD8+ T cells and dendritic cells), suggesting that KLF16 may participate in tumor immune evasion. Chen et al. [13] reported that KLF16 promotes immune escape in HCC by upregulating PD-L1 expression through the transcriptional activation of C12orf49, thereby accelerating the progression of HCC. The results of the bioinformatics analysis in this study indicate that KLF16 is involved in the T-cell receptor signaling pathway. The overexpression of KLF16 in vitro increased PD-L1 expression in Hepa1–6 cells, whereas the knockdown of KLF16 in Hepa1–6 cells with or without IFN-γ treatment suppressed PD-L1 expression. The inhibitory effect of KLF16 knockdown on HCC in immunocompetent mice is far stronger than that in nude mice. Moreover, KLF16 knockdown promoted CD8+ T-cell infiltration in tumor-bearing tissues and inhibited PD-L1 expression. Furthermore, combining KLF16 knockdown with anti-PD-1 mAb and anti-CTLA-4 mAb treatment further suppressed the growth of tumor-bearing tissues in mice and enriched CD8+ T cells within the tumors. In alignment with prior research, our study demonstrated that KLF16 promotes immune escape in HCC by upregulating PD-L1 expression and that knocking down KLF16 expression enhances the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 mAb and CTLA-4 mAb blockade.

In addition, we found that KLF16 does not directly bind to the PD-L1 promoter to promote PD-L1 expression, suggesting that KLF16 is not a transcriptional regulator of PD-L1 and that its regulation of PD-L1 expression depends on other molecules. Through bioinformatics analysis, we discovered that KLF16 may bind to the PRKDC promoter. Studies have confirmed that PRKDC can act as an oncogene to promote tumor progression. In breast cancer, PRKDC can promote cancer cell metastasis by activating epithelial‒mesenchymal transition [28]. Melanoma patients with low PRKDC expression have longer survival, and PRKDC knockout combined with anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 mAb treatment significantly increases the survival rate of tumor-bearing mice [29]. PRKDC expression is also significantly upregulated in HCC, and high expression of PRKDC can inhibit immune cell activation, thereby exacerbating the poor prognosis of HCC patients [30]. In this study, KLF16 knockdown inhibited the transcription of PRKDC and the expression of DNA-PK, a protein encoded by PRKDC, in Hepa1–6 cells, whereas KLF16 overexpression had the opposite effect, indicating that KLF16 is a transcriptional regulator of PRKDC in HCC. Furthermore, existing evidence suggests that PRKDC gene mutations in the Chinese population with lung cancer may be associated with upregulated PD-L1 expression [31]. A study by Miao et al. [18] revealed that DNA-PK can promote immune escape of gastric cancer cells by stabilizing PD-L1, and exert a pro-tumor effect. This study revealed that PRKDC overexpression attenuates the inhibitory effect of KLF16 knockdown on PD-L1 expression, demonstrating that KLF16 upregulates PD-L1 expression in HCC by promoting PRKDC transcription.

In summary, our study demonstrated that KLF16 promotes PD-L1 expression by upregulating DNA-PK through the promotion of PRKDC transcription, thereby leading to immune escape in HCC. Knockdown of KLF16 inhibits tumor growth and enhances the efficacy of PD-1 and CTLA-4 mAb blockade therapy, suggesting that KLF16 may be a potential therapeutic target for treating HCC. However, this study has several limitations. The safety and efficacy of KLF16 knockdown combined with PD-1 and CTLA-4 blockade therapy have not been evaluated in preclinical studies. Additionally, whether any drugs can resist immune escape in HCC through the inhibition of KLF16 remains unclear. These will be the key issues to be addressed in our subsequent research.
