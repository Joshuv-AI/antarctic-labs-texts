Gas Concentration Errors in Vitreoretinal Surgery: Insights from a Model of Gas Mass Transfer Dynamics
Authors: Luke Michaels, Mariantonia Ferrara, Joseph van Batenburg-Sherwood, Mario R. Romano, Jean-Yves Guillemaut, Tom H. Williamson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionConcentration mix errors of fluorinated gases commonly used in vitreoretinal surgery can lead to catastrophic elevations in intraocular pressure (IOP). The timing and optimal management of this complication is poorly understood. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of controlled venting on IOP elevation following simulated gas mix errors.MethodsA validated mass transfer model of intraocular gas dynamics was used to simulate the properties of “isovolumetric” gas concentrations of 20% SF6, 16% C2F6, and 12% C3F8, along with the most common concentration mix errors: double concentration, 10/3 (3.33×) concentration, and 100% gas. Simulations were performed on eyes with vitreous cavity volumes of 4.0 mL, 7.2 mL, and 10.0 mL (representing hyperopic to highly myopic eyes). The model calculated gas volume and IOP over time, both before and after controlled venting events to normalise IOP.ResultsAll gas concentration errors produced complete gas fill and rapid IOP elevations. Only double concentration errors generated IOPs potentially manageable with medical therapy (peak 60 mm Hg for 40% SF6, 75 mm Hg for 32% C2F6, 61 mm Hg for 24% C3F8). Simulated 10/3 (3.33×) and 100% concentrations resulted in extreme IOP elevations incompatible with ocular perfusion. Controlled gas venting on day 1 normalised IOP for SF6 but repeated venting procedures were required for C2F6 and C3F8 due to continued gas re-expansion. Eyes with larger vitreous cavities experienced prolonged IOP elevation and delayed equilibration after venting.ConclusionGas concentration mix errors produce rapid and often unsalvageable IOP rises. While limited SF6 over-concentrations may be temporarily stabilised with medical therapy and controlled venting, most scenarios, especially involving C2F6 or C3F8, require urgent surgical complete gas exchange.

Introduction

Sulphur hexafluoride (SF6), hexafluoroethane (C2F6), and perfluoropropane (C3F8) are routinely used in vitreoretinal surgery as intraocular tamponade agents [1, 2]. Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) has been shown to potentially cause intraocular pressure (IOP) elevations in the immediate, early, and late postoperative period and vitrectomised eyes are at higher risk of ocular hypertension and glaucoma when compared to fellow eyes [3, 4]. Although multiple mechanisms have been proposed, postoperative IOP elevation is often attributed to intraocular gas expansion [5]. Clinically, postoperative gas expansions usually result in modest IOP elevation during the first few hours/days after vitrectomy but do not commonly cause significant ocular perfusion alterations and injury [6].

However, there have been occasional reports of catastrophic IOP elevations causing optic nerve damage, retinal ischaemia, and irreversible vision loss [7, 8]. Where the IOP exceeds 40–45 mm Hg, the mechanisms by which the retina autoregulates perfusion have been predicted to begin to fail, resulting in venule collapse and retinal ischaemia through increased venous resistance [9]. The initial gas type and concentration influence the physics of gas bubble expansion; therefore, errors in gas concentration mix are the most likely cause of postoperative uncontrolled IOP elevation [6]. The impact of errors in gas concentration mix on IOP remains poorly understood, with uncertainties surrounding optimal timing and volume of gas venting required to return IOP to an acceptable level. Furthermore, it is unclear whether management strategies for correcting gas concentration errors for one tamponade can be applied to others.

The understanding of gas pharmacokinetics and of the effects of venting procedures is essential to mitigate risks of gas concentration errors [7]. A model that accurately predicts the mass transfer dynamics of tamponade gases commonly used in vitreoretinal surgery has previously been produced, and key assumptions of the model are summarised in Table 1 [6]. The model predicts the expansions and persistence of intraocular gases in eyes of varying axial lengths as well as the effect on IOP according to the expected dynamics of a healthy eye [6]. In this study, the aforementioned model was interrogated to evaluate the impact of controlled venting on IOP normalisation in cases of gas mix error with SF6, C2F6 and C3F8 in eye models of different volumes.

Methods

Modelling experiments were performed on simulated eyes with vitreous cavity volumes of 4.0 mL, 7.2 mL, and 10.0 mL, representing hyperopic to highly myopic eyes. An 80% gas fill was selected as a realistic fill that would be expected to be achieved intraoperatively [10].

Parameters were adjusted to simulate commonly used “isovolumetric” gas concentrations: 20% SF6, 16% C2F6, 12% C3F8 and their effect on gas volume and IOP elevation over time. Parameters were then adjusted to simulate the effect of the commonest gas concentration mix errors: double concentration – to simulate error in conversion when using 50 mL syringe; 10/3 concentration (3.33×) – to simulate error in conversion when using a 30 mL syringe; 100% concentration.

The results of the above parameter adjustments and graphs showing volume (mL)-time (days) and IOP (mm Hg)-time (days) were generated according to the model code. The model was then run to simulate controlled venting events where gas was evacuated to a target IOP of 15 mm Hg for each of the above gas concentration mix error scenarios on day 1. The gas volume (mL) and IOP (mm Hg) over time (days) were graphed according to the model output to show the effect of gas venting and subsequent gas expansion after venting. On days 2 and 3, where IOP exceeded 20 mm Hg, a further venting event was simulated up to a maximum of 3 venting events. Venting was also simulated in non-mistake scenarios wherever IOP exceeded 20 mm Hg for comparison. Venting time points were simulated on days 1, 2, and 3 in keeping with common clinical practice with a patient presenting to clinic for daily review and IOP measurement.

Results

The graphs of simulated gas volume (mL) and IOP (mm Hg) for the commonest gas concentration mix errors are shown in Figure 1 along with simulations of venting events to control IOP. The results demonstrate that gas concentration mix errors result in 100% gas fill and raised IOP (to ≥60 mm Hg) for all gases and gas concentration mix errors. Only double concentration errors produced IOPs that could potentially be controlled with medical management, with peak IOP for 40% SF6, 32% C2F6, and 24% C3F8 of 60 mm Hg, 75 mm Hg, and 61 mm Hg, respectively.

Discussion

This study provides novel and valuable insights into the consequences of venting procedures in cases of gas concentration mix errors using a validated model of tamponade gas dynamics [6]. Our results demonstrate that the commonest concentration mix errors can produce rapid and substantial IOP elevations. Only double concentration mix errors are potentially salvageable as the other simulated mix errors produced extremely high IOP rises above 100 mm Hg that are incompatible with continued retinal perfusion [9]. Where a gas concentration mix error has occurred, medical therapy may provide adequate IOP control when a low-concentration, short-acting tamponade has been used. However, medical management is not always successful and surgical evacuation of gas, either by venting or by performing a complete gas exchange with the correct gas concentration, may be required [8].

Although venting is a known procedure for the management of gas mix errors, the changes in gas dynamics and, thus, the impact on IOP have not yet been fully elucidated. In this regard, our model also allowed the simulation of venting intervention for the various gas concentration mix error scenarios.

To perform controlled venting, the authors recommend utilising the following technique: a bag of balanced salt solution (BSS) is attached to a drip stand at 20 cm above patient eye level as shown in Figure 4. Noncompliant tubing should be connected to the bag of BSS and to a small gauge needle that is used to pierce the vitreous cavity using a sterile aseptic technique as per local protocols. The expanded gas is allowed to bubble through the tubing and exchange with BSS until a state of equilibrium is achieved. The hydrostatic pressure of 1 centimetre of water is approximately equal to 0.735 mm Hg and therefore placing the bag of BSS at 20 cm above the patient’s eye will result in a post equilibrium IOP of around 15 mm Hg. Venting just with a needle and syringe is not recommended, as uncontrolled rapid venting of gas can lead to rapid hypotony and risks suprachoroidal haemorrhage. Furthermore, it would not be possible to accurately measure the percentage volume of gas that has been removed from the eye to accurately determine an endpoint. The above methodology of controlled venting can be applied in an emergency situation in most healthcare setting where needles, tubing and BSS are available and does not require the immediate availability of an emergency ophthalmic operating theatre, replacement gaseous tamponade, vitreoretinal specialist expertise, or dedicated equipment. The procedure can be performed with the patient in a supine or upright position.

Controlled venting of intraocular gas. Fluid is connected to noncompliant tubing and attached to a small gauge venting needle that allows for controlled venting of gas to a target IOP. Target IOP is determined by the centimetre pressure of the fluid and its height above eye level.

Venting of a sufficient gas volume to a physiological IOP may be effective in limited overconcentration errors of SF6 and C2F6, but for highly expansile gases such as C3F8, the volume of gas that would need to be vented to offset the expansile potential of the gas would likely result in insufficient volume to produce an effective tamponade, which can be critical in cases of retinal detachment where retinopexy has not yet had enough time to become established. In our model, a single venting demonstrated efficacy exclusively in SF6-filled eyes. Notably, the simulation reveals that the associated IOP spike is abrupt, invariably reaching its peak within the initial 24 h. Consequently, venting must be undertaken within this critical early period to minimise the risk of deleterious IOP elevation.

All other explored scenarios required serial venting to maintain safe IOP levels. The reason for recurrent gas expansion in these scenarios is the continued diffusion of nitrogen into the gas bubble even after decompression, leading to gas bubble re-expansion until equilibrium is reached. As shown by the model, the implication is that a single decompression is inadequate to effectively control IOP and repeated venting or complete gas exchange may be required to adequately control a gas concentration mix error. In addition, the model shows that eyes with larger vitreous cavity volumes experience prolonged phases of raised IOP with delayed equilibration after venting. This corroborates with previous clinical findings that increased axial lengths are associated with prolonged intraocular gas persistence and is particularly relevant to the global increasing incidence of myopia and retinal detachment [11–15].

In light of the complexity of the clinical scenario of a gas mix error and the need of intensive patient care and monitoring, it may be suggested that the optimal solution is to urgently take the patient back to theatre and perform a complete gas exchange. Using a PPV setup with valved ports and an air infusion line allows for a closed system where gas can be vented and replaced with air at incrementally reduced pressures until a target IOP is achieved, and subsequently, the air can be exchanged with gas of the correct concentration as would be done routinely at the end of PPV surgery.

It is worth pointing out that gas concentration mix errors are an entirely preventable cause of postoperative complications after vitreoretinal surgery. Recognised strategies for risk reduction and gas concentration error prevention include the following [10]:1.Clear labelling of gas syringes to prevent cross use.2.Standardised dilution protocols with double verification by 2 scrub team members.3.Record keeping of dilution calculations for traceability.4.Use of prefilled, manufacturer prepared gas mixtures that avoid the need for manual dilution.

We acknowledge that the use of a theoretical model has intrinsic limitations. However, while this model does not represent a true physiological environment and the complexity of real life conditions (such as temperature, partial pressure gradients, altitude, and wound sealing), it has been validated against published literature in humans providing reliable estimates of the scale and direction of IOP change following gas expansion [6]. Furthermore, the advantage of using this model lies in allowing the systematic exploration of potentially hazardous scenarios of gas concentration mix errors that would be unethical to reproduce clinically.

Conclusions

Gas concentration mix errors are a preventable complication of vitreoretinal surgery that produce rapid and often unsalvageable IOP elevations. Controlled venting can temporarily stabilise and control some errors, but in the majority of cases, urgent surgical intervention is required with complete gas exchange with a gas of the correct concentration.
