A case of congenital total intestinal atresia in a neonate: a case report
Authors: Hindeya Hailu Hagos, Yemane Leake Gebremichael, Birhanu Kassie Reta, Tiegist Bahta Woldu, Hailemariam Kahsay Kidanu, Gebreyohans Tesfay Kidanu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntestinal atresia is the complete occlusion of the intestinal lumen at various parts of the intestine. It has the potential to cause intestinal failure. Two mechanisms are suggested: failure of recanalization and/or accidental injury. This case report aims to discuss an exceptionally rare case of congenital total intestinal atresia.Case presentationA 1-day-old male neonate, Tigrian ethnic of Ethiopia born at term, was brought to our hospital with the complaint of repeated vomiting and abdominal distension soon after birth. On examination, he had stable vital signs and a minimally distended abdomen. Abdominal imaging showed features of intestinal obstruction. With a preoperative impression of jejunal atresia, he underwent exploratory laparotomy. The findings were a type IIIa jejunal atresia in addition to an atretic intestine starting from half of the jejunum to the rectum, along with a distended stomach, duodenum, and blind-end jejunum. Decompressive jejunostomy was performed.ConclusionTotal intestinal atresia is an extremely rare and fatal condition in our setting. Future investigations are required to examine early prenatal detection for effective intervention and prevention.Keywords: Atresia, Neonate, Total intestinal atresia, Case report

Introduction

Intestinal atresia refers to the complete occlusion of the intestinal lumen [1]. The epidemiology of intestinal atresia is elusive. It is one of the causes of acute abdominal distension and emergency presentation during the neonatal age. It occurs in any part of the intestine. It has the potential to cause intestinal failure, a devastating condition that makes patients dependent on supplemental parenteral nutrition [2, 3]. The most common types of intestinal atresia include, but are not limited to, isolated duodenal, jejunal, ileal, jejuno-ileal, and colonic atresia [1, 4–6], each with a variable epidemiological occurrence across various countries [1, 6–8]. The mechanisms giving rise to intestinal atresia are thought to be owing to failure of recanalization during embryogenesis of the fetal intestines and disruption of vascular supply in late pregnancy, especially in jejuno-ileal and colonic atresia [1, 5, 7, 8]. Genetic predisposition is also implicated in some cases of atresia, particularly in multiple intestinal atresia [9]. Clinical presentations are those of intestinal obstruction [1]. Total intestinal atresia (TIA) is an extremely rare condition, considered an incredibly unusual and unique case, with few case reports. We present a case of a 1-day-old male neonate found to have total intestinal atresia intraoperatively. The epidemiology of TIA is elusive, with only a few cases reported. This case report is prepared in line with the Surgical CAse REport (SCARE) criteria [10].

Case presentation

History and physical examination

A 1-day-old male neonate, an Ethiopian of Tigrayan ethnicity, weighing 3000 gm, born at term gestation to a para II mother with an uncomplicated pregnancy, was brought to our Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) with repeated bilious vomiting and progressive abdominal distention. He had failed to breastfeed since birth and had not passed meconium. The mother had regular, uneventful antenatal care at a nearby hospital. The mother, who was referred from another hospital, reported that she had undergone an obstetric ultrasound scan, which was found to be normal, with no evidence of excess fluid. There was no relevant family history or consanguinity. On examination, he had no dysmorphic features; his vital signs and anthropometric measurements were within normal limits. He exhibited minimal, slightly tense abdominal distention, more pronounced in the proximal abdomen, but without visible peristalsis. He had a normal anorectal examination; the thermometer tip was admitted approximately 2 cm with resistance to further advancement, and there was no meconium staining. Primitive reflexes were intact except for depressed suckling. Other systems examinations were unremarkable.

Diagnostic workup

Routine laboratory tests, including blood glucose and full blood count, were within the normal range. Other blood chemistry results were unremarkable. A plain abdominopelvic x-ray showed dilation of the stomach, duodenum, and proximal jejunum (triple bubble sign) with distal absence of gas shadow (Fig. 1). Abdominal ultrasound showed a gaseous abdomen, a dilated stomach and proximal bowel without distal peristaltic movement, consistent with small bowel obstruction (SBO) secondary to jejunal atresia. Other visceral structures appeared normal.

Erect plain abdominal x ray showing dilated stomach duodenum and proximal jejunum (triple bubble sign)

Treatment and follow-up

A nasogastric (NG) tube was passed for abdominal decompression and contained bile-stained content. He received a bolus of 10 ml/kg of normal saline, was kept “nil per os” (NPO), and then put on maintenance fluids. He was started on empiric first-line antibiotics for sepsis.

Intraoperative findings

After resuscitation, with a preoperative diagnosis of SBO secondary to jejunal atresia, he underwent exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperative findings included a distended stomach, duodenum, and blind-ended jejunum. In addition, a type IIIa jejunal atresia was found approximately 30 cm from the duodenojejunal junction, with no distal lumen from the midpoint of the jejunum to the rectum. Multiple enterotomies were performed, and the patency of the intestinal lumen was tested but found to be obliterated. Otherwise, abdominal structures were in normal anatomic positions; the mesentery appeared normal, and there were no signs of malrotation or volvulus (Fig. 2). Management was challenging; however, decompressive jejunostomy was ultimately performed. The patient received routine postoperative care until death at 4 days of age, with sepsis as the probable cause.

Discussion

Intestinal atresia is the complete occlusion of the intestinal lumen and it can occur in any part of the intestine [1]. It has the potential to cause intestinal failure [2, 11], primarily by causing short bowel syndrome [11]. The Pediatric Intestinal Failure Consortium reported that atresia accounts for 27/272 (10%) of infants with intestinal failure [3]. The most common forms of intestinal atresia include, but are not limited to, isolated duodenal, jejunal, ileal, jejuno-ileal, and colonic atresia, each with a variable epidemiological occurrence across the world [1]. Herein, we report on a 1-day-old male neonate diagnosed with total intestinal atresia intraoperatively. To the best of our knowledge, this is the fifth reported case of this condition in the literature. Similarly, Aggarwal and associates reported on a 1-day-old male neonate diagnosed with total intestinal atresia, with intraoperative findings of type IIIa jejunal atresia seen at 10 cm and 15 cm from the duodenojejunal (DJ) flexure and an atretic bowel starting from the jejunum, the whole ileum, and colon to the rectum [5]. This patient has very similar findings to this report, except the distance from the DJ flexure in our case is longer, approximately 30 cm, and the atretic part extends to the colon. Likewise, G. Morris-Stiff and colleagues reported a case of total intestinal atresia, where only the duodenum and 10 cm of jejunum were distended; otherwise, a complete absence of most of the small intestine and large bowel was documented [12]. R. Samha et al. reported on a neonate with total intestinal atresia extending from the duodenum to the rectum [13]. Furthermore, Nonnemacher et al. reported a 3-day-old neonate who was confirmed to have total intestinal atresia (TIA) intraoperatively [14].

The clinical presentations of intestinal atresia are those of intestinal obstruction, including repeated vomiting of bilious or nonbilious contents, failure to pass meconium, progressive abdominal distension, and jaundice [1, 4, 5]. Except for jaundice, this patient presented with these manifestations. The diagnosis of intestinal atresia can be made prenatally by ultrasonography in most patients [1, 5]. Postnatal diagnosis is mainly obtained through abdominal x-ray showing features of intestinal obstruction, such as multiple air-fluid levels, double or triple bubbles in the case of duodenal and jejunoileal atresia, respectively [1, 4, 5]. Ultrasonography can also suggest the level of obstruction [15]. Other diagnostic modalities include contrast-based imaging studies, which were not performed in our patient owing to unavailability at our hospital [13, 14]. However, diagnosing total intestinal atresia using these diagnostic modalities is challenging. Thus, several authors have described surgical exploration, confirming the diagnosis of TIA [5, 13, 14]. As with reported cases, the diagnosis in our patient was made intraoperatively. Postsurgery, histopathologic examination helps differentiate whether it is caused by an ischemic insult, i.e., vascular disruption or failure of recanalization [5].

Even though the exact mechanism of intestinal atresia is controversial, two broadly accepted theories of its pathogenesis exist [1, 4, 5, 16]. The first hypothesizes that intestinal atresia results from a failure of recanalization of the intestinal lumen following obliteration during epithelial proliferation. This stage is characterized by the presence of bile, squamous cells, and lanugo distal to the obstruction site. This indicates that the insult resulting in the atretic segment occurred after intestinal luminal development, when some contents had already passed distal to the obstruction site [1, 4, 5, 16]. While this theory better explains isolated atresia, particularly duodenal atresia, our patient fits this theory, considering the finding of an atretic and fibrinous cord structure from the jejunum to the rectum [5]. The second theory involves a vascular accident, evidenced by mesentery hypoplasia, mesenteric defects, gut ischemia, or absent mesenteric segments [1, 4, 5]. Atresia also appears in association with other intrauterine vascular insults, such as intussusception, midgut volvulus, thromboembolic occlusions, transmesenteric internal hernias, and bowel incarceration or snaring in an omphalocele or gastroschisis. These associations have contributed to the widespread acceptance of this hypothesis [1, 4, 5, 12]. In this case, mesenteric examination appeared normal. It is unclear in cases of total intestinal atresia if intrauterine vascular accidents are the etiology, as is the case in an isolated atresia [14]. Moreover, there was nothing to suggest intrauterine vascular disruption when comes to our case.

Extraintestinal anomalies occur in 25–35% of patients with intestinal atresia, with a higher incidence associated with jejunal atresia compared with ileal atresia; this incidence reaches > 50% in duodenal atresia (DA) [1]. Our patient’s systemic examination, including echocardiography, abdominal ultrasound of visceral structures (excluding the intestine), transfontanelle ultrasound, and musculoskeletal examination, was unremarkable.

The management of total intestinal atresia is generally entirely supportive. It particularly is challenging in resource-constrained countries such as Ethiopia, where parenteral nutrition is unavailable. Moreover, to date, there is no surgical or medical cure for TIA [14]. Management depends on the patient’s condition and includes supportive treatments such as nasogastric (NG) tube decompression, correction of dehydration and electrolyte imbalances, parenteral nutritional support (when available), and broad-spectrum antibiotics in case of sepsis [5]. Our patient received all these supportive measures except parenteral nutrition, which was unavailable in our setting.

Surgical management of intestinal atresias generally involves resection of the atretic segment and anastomosis. However, managing total intestinal atresia (TIA) remains challenging, ranging from no intervention to rarely, ostomy creation. A review of three patients by Samha et al. reported that in two cases of TIA, no intervention was performed; the intestine was repositioned and closed. In one patient, an end duodenostomy was performed [13]. Similarly, Nonnemacher et al. placed a gastrostomy tube for decompression [14]. Surgical options in our case were challenging; ultimately, a decompressive end jejunostomy was performed, followed by routine postoperative care.

Over the past decades, survival rates for intestinal atresia after surgery have significantly improved. This is largely owing to a better understanding of etiological factors and impaired intestinal function, as well as refinements in pediatric anesthesia, operative techniques, and preoperative and postoperative care, especially in nutritional support [17, 18]. However, the prognosis for total intestinal atresia (TIA) remains challenging, particularly in resource-limited settings where parenteral nutrition is unavailable. In a review by R. Samha et al., two of three cases died within days of surgery, and the outcome of one patient was unknown as the parents took the child home after 2 postoperative days [13]. In addition, Nonnemacher et al. reported a case in which the family compassionately withdrew care, and the patient passed away 3 days later at home [14]. Despite optimized postoperative care, our patient died within 72 h (4 days of age) postoperatively, with sepsis as the probable cause of death. This case report has an important limitation: a pathological examination was not performed, which would have provided a deeper understanding of the condition. This was owing to the unavailability of such services in our hospital, partly resulting from the near collapse of the health infrastructure in the Tigray region following the war between the Tigray region and the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia, which erupted in November 2020, and the subsequent siege that followed [19, 20].

Conclusion

Total intestinal atresia is an extremely rare condition with a poor prognosis, especially in resource-constrained settings. Further investigation is needed to improve early prenatal detection and prediction for effective intervention and prevention, thereby to improve outcome. We report this case owing to its rarity.
