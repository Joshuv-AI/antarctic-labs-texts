A self-supervised GNN–Transformer framework for weak microseismic signal identification
Authors: Mingwei Liu, Zhigang Deng, Yunpeng Li, Zhijia Zhang, Tianyu Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWeak microseismic signals generated during hydraulic fracturing contain critical information on fracture initiation and propagation, serving as a key basis for evaluating fracturing effectiveness and ensuring mining safety. However, in deep coal mining environments, strong mechanical disturbances, electromagnetic interference, and complex non-stationary noise lead to low signal-to-noise ratios and weak signal energy, posing significant challenges to traditional identification methods. To address these issues, a self-supervised GNN–Transformer framework (SS-GNN-MSTF-Transformer) is proposed for robust weak microseismic signal recognition. First, a multi-scale spatiotemporal feature extraction module (MSTF) is developed by integrating Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) with multi-scale convolution, enabling joint modeling of global and local time–frequency characteristics. Then, a Transformer encoder incorporating Rotary Position Encoding (RoPE) and multi-scale attention is employed to capture both long- and short-range temporal dependencies. Meanwhile, an adaptive graph neural network (GNN) is introduced to learn spatial correlations among monitoring stations in a data-driven manner, facilitating effective multi-station information fusion. Furthermore, a self-supervised learning strategy combining contrastive learning, masked time–frequency prediction, and graph structure recovery is designed to enhance feature representation under limited labeled data conditions. Experimental results based on real microseismic monitoring datasets demonstrate that the proposed method achieves an average accuracy and F1-score of 0.9343 under five-fold cross-validation, outperforming conventional methods such as SVM, KNN, RF, and BPNN. In addition, engineering validation on two independent field datasets shows that the model achieves F1-scores of 0.9341 and 0.9216, respectively, with improvements of 7.17% and 6.79% compared with the best traditional method (RF), confirming its strong generalization ability under different geological conditions and noise environments. The model also maintains an inference latency of approximately 14.9 ms per sample, demonstrating its feasibility for real-time monitoring applications. Overall, the proposed framework significantly improves the accuracy, robustness, and generalization ability of weak microseismic signal identification, providing a reliable and efficient solution for hydraulic fracturing monitoring, fracture propagation analysis, and safety assessment in deep mining environments.Keywords: Rock formation hydraulic fracturing, Weak microseismic signals, Intelligent recognition, SS-GNN-MSTF-TransformerSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Coal is one of the earliest fossil fuels to be widely utilized and continues to play a vital role in industrial production, power generation, and other sectors1,2. According to the Statistical Review of World Energy (2024), global coal production has reached a record high of 179 EJ, while coal consumption has exceeded 164 EJ for the first time3. Although the substitution effect of coal is gradually strengthening with the advancement of new energy technologies, coal will continue to occupy a significant position in the global energy consumption structure for a considerable period4,5. As mining depth continues to increase, dynamic hazards such as rock bursts have become increasingly prominent, posing a serious threat to mine safety. Therefore, to improve the efficiency of coal resource development and ensure the safety of deep mining, hydraulic fracturing technology has been widely applied for stress relief in coal bodies and for the prevention and control of rock bursts6. By inducing the formation of a complex fracture network within the coal-rock mass, this technology effectively reduces stress concentration, releases accumulated elastic energy, and mitigates the risk of rock burst occurrence. During the hydraulic fracturing process, continuous injection of high-pressure fluid disrupts the original stress equilibrium of the coal-rock mass, leading to structural instability in rock layers and promoting fracture initiation and propagation. As fractures develop and energy is released, the accumulated elastic energy propagates through the surrounding medium in the form of elastic waves, thereby generating microseismic signals7. These signals provide real-time insights into fracture initiation and propagation, making them essential for evaluating fracturing effectiveness and analyzing fracture evolution characteristics8. By strategically deploying sensor arrays on the surface or underground to monitor microseismic signals in real time, and by integrating source localization and source mechanism inversion analysis, it is possible to effectively reconstruct the spatial morphology and propagation characteristics of fractures, thereby providing a scientific basis for optimizing fracturing parameters and evaluating the effectiveness of rock burst prevention and control9. However, weak microseismic signals in coal mine monitoring data are characterized by low energy, strong non-stationarity, and complex spatial correlations. Traditional methods still exhibit significant limitations in feature representation and generalization capability, making it imperative to develop advanced intelligent recognition models specifically tailored for weak microseismic signal identification10.

Microseismic signals are a class of non-stationary signals characterized by strong randomness and pronounced time-varying properties; the observed signals are typically composed of a superposition of true source signals and various types of background noise11. Accurate identification of microseismic signals is fundamental to the stable operation of monitoring systems and the reliability of disaster early warning12. However, under the complex environmental conditions of underground coal mines, microseismic events often occur as low-magnitude events13, with magnitudes typically below ML 0. These signals exhibit low signal-to-noise ratios, often below 2 dB, along with complex waveform structures and dispersed energy distributions, which further complicate signal identification. Meanwhile, underground operations are affected by a wide range of interference sources, including vibrations from mining equipment14, blasting activities15, electromagnetic interference16, and water flow noise17. These interference signals exhibit high similarity to genuine microseismic signals in both the time and frequency domains, increasing the likelihood of misclassification and missed detection. Under the combined influence of strong noise backgrounds and weak signal conditions18, traditional methods based on manual feature extraction or single-scale analysis struggle to effectively distinguish microseismic signals, posing significant challenges to the intelligent and reliable operation of coal mine microseismic monitoring systems. To address these challenges, extensive research has been conducted worldwide on noise suppression for nonlinear and non-stationary microseismic signals. Currently, commonly used noise reduction methods mainly include bandpass filtering19, wavelet threshold-based denoising20, and Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD)21 and its variants. Bandpass filtering suppresses noise by presetting specific frequency bands; however, due to the strong non-stationarity of microseismic signals, its effectiveness in complex environments remains limited. Wavelet threshold-based methods improve the processing capability for non-stationary signals through multiscale decomposition; however, their performance is highly dependent on manually selected global thresholds. Hard-thresholding often leads to the loss of signal details, whereas soft-thresholding may result in excessive smoothing. EMD and its improved variants, such as Collective Empirical Mode Decomposition (CEEMD)22 and Complementary Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition (CEEMDAN)23, alleviate the issue of mode mixing to some extent through adaptive decomposition. Nevertheless, during the signal reconstruction stage, statistical measures such as cross-correlation coefficients and information entropy are still required to select intrinsic mode functions (IMFs)24. Overall, these time–frequency domain-based denoising methods generally face challenges in threshold or criterion selection in practical applications, thereby limiting their stability and effectiveness in complex signal environments25.

In recent years, with the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence technologies, data-driven deep learning methods have gradually emerged as a dominant research direction in the field of signal processing. Owing to their multi-layer nonlinear architectures, deep neural networks are capable of automatically extracting high-level features from data and establishing complex nonlinear mapping relationships26, thereby demonstrating significant advantages in handling nonlinear and non-stationary signals. At present, such methods have been widely applied in the field of seismic data denoising and have achieved promising results. For instance, Liu et al.27 introduced a three-dimensional denoising convolutional neural network (DnCNN) for seismic data processing, which effectively suppresses random noise by learning the feature differences between noise and useful signals through deep convolutional structures. Dodda et al.28 proposed an attention-based wavelet convolutional neural network (AWUN), which leverages the multiscale properties of wavelet transforms and the feature selection capability of attention mechanisms to not only perform effective noise reduction but also reconstruct missing signals, significantly improving data recovery quality. Ji et al.29 developed a seismic signal denoising method that integrates Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) with the MobileNetV2 neural network. By employing a deep neural network to learn and optimize SVD decomposition results, this method avoids the difficulty of rank determination in traditional SVD approaches, thereby enhancing denoising stability and accuracy. Inspired by the success of deep learning in seismic signal denoising, researchers have increasingly introduced neural network-based approaches into the field of microseismic signal processing to improve noise suppression performance in complex environments. For example, Zhang et al.30 proposed a dual-convolutional neural network guided by a low-rank structure extraction module to recover microseismic signals masked by strong noise. This method incorporates synthetic data, field microseismic data, and natural image data for training, thereby improving model generalization and robustness. Ma et al.10 applied deep learning techniques to microseismic event identification in coal mines by extracting features such as amplitude, dominant frequency, and energy, and constructing classification models based on Support Vector Machines (SVM), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks. Their results indicate that the LSTM model achieves higher prediction accuracy compared with traditional machine learning methods and significantly enhances the identification capability of coal-rock fracture events. Furthermore, Zhang et al.31 proposed a rockburst risk prediction model integrating a convolutional neural network (CNN), a modified long short-term memory network (MoLSTM), and an attention mechanism, with hyperparameters optimized using the sparrow search algorithm (SSA). Using the advance distance of the working face as the prediction sequence and validating with microseismic monitoring data, the model achieved a prediction accuracy of 93.62%, significantly outperforming conventional approaches. However, compared with conventional seismic or general microseismic signals, microseismic signals induced by hydraulic fracturing of rock formations exhibit a more complex generation mechanism. Rock masses typically contain fractures, joints, and bedding structures at multiple scales, exhibiting strong heterogeneity and anisotropy32. During hydraulic fracturing, high-pressure fluid injection induces rock failure and promotes continuous fracture propagation, generating microseismic signals with pronounced nonlinearity and strong non-stationarity33. These signals exhibit complex variations in spectral structure, energy distribution, and time-domain waveforms, which differ significantly from conventional seismic or general rock microseismic signals. Moreover, the complex downhole monitoring environment introduces additional interference from mechanical vibrations, electromagnetic disturbances, and environmental noise, further increasing the difficulty of extracting and accurately identifying weak microseismic signals34.

In summary, this study proposes an intelligent framework for weak microseismic signal identification during hydraulic fracturing by integrating self-supervised learning, graph neural networks, and a multi-scale spatiotemporal Transformer. Specifically, a multi-scale spatiotemporal decomposition (MSTF) module is designed to extract both short-time Fourier transform (STFT) spectral features and multi-scale convolutional representations, enabling effective characterization of the non-stationary properties of microseismic signals. An improved Transformer encoder is then developed to model complex temporal dependencies through positional encoding and a multi-scale attention mechanism. Meanwhile, an adaptive graph neural network is introduced to learn latent spatial correlations among monitoring stations in a data-driven manner, facilitating efficient multi-station information fusion. Furthermore, a self-supervised learning strategy is constructed by jointly incorporating contrastive learning, masked time–frequency prediction, and graph structure recovery tasks, aiming to enhance feature representation capability under limited labeled data conditions. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the methodology and model architecture in detail. Section 3 presents the experimental setup and data description. Section 4 discusses the results and comparative analysis. Finally, Sect. 5 concludes the study and outlines future research directions.

Theoretical foundation

Overview of the overall architecture

The proposed MSTF-Transformer-GNN-SS model consists of four core components: MSTF, Transformer, GNN, and a self-supervised (SS) learning strategy. The MSTF module first extracts multi-scale time–frequency features from raw signals by combining Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) with learnable wavelet decomposition, forming a unified representation. Based on this, a time–frequency Transformer encoder is employed for sequence modeling, integrating multi-scale attention and Rotary Position Encoding (RoPE) to capture dependencies across different temporal scales. To further model spatial relationships, an adaptive Graph Neural Network (GNN) is introduced to learn latent topological structures among monitoring stations and aggregate multi-node information via a Graph Attention Network (GAT). During training, a self-supervised learning strategy is adopted, where the model is first pre-trained on large-scale unlabeled data and then fine-tuned with limited labeled samples. Specifically, three auxiliary tasks—contrastive learning, masked spatiotemporal prediction, and graph structure recovery—are designed to enhance representation learning and improve generalization performance. First, the original time series is processed through a multiscale time-frequency decomposition module, which uses two parallel paths—the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) and learnable wavelet decomposition—to extract time-frequency features at multiple resolutions. These features are then fused into a unified time-frequency representation matrix HTF ∈ RB×N×T′×D, where T′ is the number of time-frequency frames and D is the feature dimension. Second, the spatio-temporal features are fed into a spatio-temporal Transformer encoder after undergoing node-level reorganization. This encoder employs a multi-scale attention mechanism with Rotational Position Encoding (RoPE), which computes self-attention in parallel across different temporal resolutions to fully capture long- and short-range dependencies in the spatio-temporal domain. The encoded features are then compressed along the temporal dimension via adaptive average pooling, yielding a compact feature vector hi ∈ RD for each node. The node-level representations are then fed into an adaptive Graph Neural Network (GNN) module. A data-driven graph structure learning mechanism is first used to infer latent topological relationships and construct a sparse adjacency matrix. Based on this learned structure, a multi-layer Graph Attention Network (GAT) propagates information to capture spatial dependencies and enhance node representations. For downstream tasks, the model adopts task-specific output heads. In classification tasks, global graph-level average pooling is followed by a fully connected classifier, while in forecasting tasks, future time steps are directly predicted via a feedforward network. During training, a self-supervised pretraining strategy is employed, where the model is jointly optimized through three auxiliary tasks: contrastive learning, masked spatiotemporal prediction, and graph structure recovery.

Time-frequency Transformer encoder

After obtaining multi-scale spatiotemporal representations, it is necessary to perform sequence modeling to capture dependencies across different time steps. To this end, this study employs a Transformer encoder based on the Pre-Norm architecture for feature modeling and introduces two key enhancements to the standard Transformer framework: Rotary Position Encoding (RoPE) and a multi-scale attention mechanism. These improvements effectively enhance the model’s ability to capture temporal structural information and model both short-term and long-term dependencies within the sequence.

Transformer encoder architecture

This study adopts a Pre-Norm architecture, in which Layer Normalization (LayerNorm) is applied prior to attention computation. Compared with the conventional Post-Norm structure, this design alleviates the vanishing gradient problem during deep network training to a certain extent, thereby improving training stability. The feed-forward network (FFN) adopts a two-layer fully connected architecture, with the hidden dimension expanded to 256 and the Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) employed as the activation function. In addition, a dropout rate of 0.2 is applied to enhance model regularization and reduce the risk of overfitting. The overall encoder is composed of three stacked Transformer layers, ensuring sufficient model expressiveness while effectively controlling parameter scale and computational complexity.

Adaptive graph neural network module

Signals recorded at different stations in a microseismic monitoring system exhibit inherent spatial correlations, reflecting the propagation characteristics of seismic events. While traditional methods rely on predefined physical topologies, such correlations are influenced not only by inter-station distance but also by geological structures and propagation media. To address this, we design an adaptive Graph Neural Network (GNN) module to learn data-driven functional connectivity among stations.

Multi-layer attention network

Based on the learned graph structure, this study employs a Graph Attention Network (GAT) for information propagation and feature aggregation40. By introducing an attention mechanism, GAT assigns differentiated weights to different neighboring nodes, thereby enabling adaptive aggregation of neighborhood information and enhancing the ability to model spatial dependencies. This study stacks two layers of GAT networks, with each layer configured with four multi-head attention heads, and maintains the intermediate feature dimension at 128 dimensions. Additionally, LayerNorm and residual connections are introduced after each GAT layer to enhance the stability of the model training process and facilitate effective gradient propagation within the deep network.

Masked time-frequency prediction

Drawing inspiration from the concept of Masked Language Models (MLM) in the field of natural language processing, we formulated the masked time-frequency prediction task as one of the self-supervised learning objectives43. Specifically, 35% of the time-frequency blocks in the input time-frequency representation are randomly selected for masking and replaced with a learnable mask token. The model must predict and reconstruct the masked time-frequency features based on the unmasked contextual information. In this task, the reconstruction target is the original value of the STFT amplitude spectrum. The decoder architecture employs a two-layer fully connected network, with an output dimension matching the number of frequency bins. Reconstruction error is measured using mean squared error (MSE), and the loss is computed only at the masked positions.

Graph reconstruction

The adjacency relationships learned by the graph structure module can reflect semantic similarity among nodes. To further enhance graph representation learning, we introduce a link prediction auxiliary task. The model predicts the existence of edges between node pairs based on their learned representations. Positive samples are derived from the Top-K inferred edges, while negative samples are randomly sampled node pairs44. An edge predictor concatenates the features of each node pair and feeds them into a two-layer fully connected network to estimate connection probabilities. The model is optimized using binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss.

Joint optimization objective

The three self-supervised tasks are integrated into a unified training objective via a weighted sum, comprising contrastive learning, masked spatiotemporal prediction, and graph structure recovery losses with weights of 1.0, 0.5, and 0.3, respectively. This configuration reflects both task importance and learning difficulty: contrastive learning serves as the primary objective to enhance global representations, while the other two act as auxiliary tasks to improve spatiotemporal and structural modeling, providing complementary constraints during training.

Data preprocessing

During the data preprocessing stage, each waveform is first normalized using the root mean square (RMS) method to standardize amplitude scales across different recordings, ensuring that waveform amplitudes are within a comparable range. For stations that did not record a given event, zero padding is applied, and a corresponding mask matrix is used to indicate valid stations, allowing the model to distinguish between real observations and padded values during training. The preprocessed results are illustrated in Fig. 4. Analysis of Fig. 4 indicates that after RMS normalization and zero padding of missing stations, the waveform amplitudes across different stations are generally within a comparable range, primarily distributed between [− 1.8, 1.8], demonstrating that the normalization effectively eliminates amplitude disparities among recordings and ensures comparability across stations. The upper row in Fig. 4 represents microseismic event samples, where multiple stations exhibit pronounced vibration responses and energy enhancements within similar time intervals, with waveforms showing clear periodic oscillatory patterns. This cross-station synchronous variation reflects the propagation of microseismic waves through the coal medium and their reception at different stations; however, due to spatial location and propagation path differences, some amplitude and phase variations remain among stations. In contrast, the lower row depicts noise samples, which generally exhibit random fluctuations. Most stations show no significant energy bursts or sustained oscillatory structures, and temporal variations across stations lack consistency, exhibiting strong randomness and dispersion. Occasionally, certain stations display localized fluctuations at specific times, but these changes do not occur synchronously across multiple stations. Overall, microseismic signals differ significantly from noise in both temporal structure and spatial correlation, providing a critical basis for constructing an intelligent identification model for weak microseismic signals induced by hydraulic fracturing.

Experimental conditions and parameters

The model is trained using a two-stage strategy to fully leverage the advantages of self-supervised learning and supervised fine-tuning. In the first stage, self-supervised pretraining is performed on all training samples, including the labeled data used in the subsequent fine-tuning phase as well as any additional unlabeled data. The model is optimized using a multi-task self-supervised loss for 50 epochs with a learning rate of 5 × 10⁻⁴, employing the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of 1 × 10⁻⁴. A cosine annealing learning rate scheduler (CosineAnnealingLR) gradually decreases the learning rate from the initial value to a minimum of 1 × 10⁻⁶. No classification labels are used during this stage; instead, the model learns robust spatiotemporal representations through self-supervised tasks. In the second stage, supervised fine-tuning is conducted on the labeled dataset after loading the pretrained weights. The classification head is fine-tuned for 100 epochs with a reduced learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴, while all other hyperparameters remain consistent with the pretraining stage. To address class imbalance, a weighted cross-entropy loss is applied, with weights computed as the inverse of class sample counts (microseismic: 1.19, noise: 1.0). Early stopping is employed, terminating training if the validation F1-score does not improve for 20 consecutive epochs to prevent overfitting. Gradient clipping (max_norm = 1.0) is applied during training to prevent gradient explosion, and data augmentation continues to be used during fine-tuning to enhance model generalization. The batch size is set to 16, enabling stable training on an 8 GB GPU. All experiments are implemented in PyTorch 2.0 and executed on an NVIDIA RTX 4080 GPU (8 GB memory), with the random seed fixed at 42 to ensure reproducibility. To comprehensively evaluate model performance, a five-fold cross-validation scheme is adopted, ensuring the stability and reliability of the results. Selection of evaluation indicators

In the task of intelligent identification of weak microseismic signals from hydraulic fracturing in this study, four evaluation metrics are commonly employed to assess model classification performance: accuracy (Acc), precision (P), recall (R), and F1-score. As shown in Fig. 8, removing any component leads to a noticeable degradation in model performance, demonstrating the effectiveness of each module. First, removing the MSTF module results in the most significant performance drop (F1-score decreases from 0.9343 to 0.9011), indicating that multi-scale time–frequency feature extraction plays a crucial role in capturing the non-stationary characteristics of weak microseismic signals. Second, replacing the Transformer encoder with a simple temporal modeling structure also leads to a clear decline in performance (F1 = 0.9126), confirming that the multi-scale attention mechanism is essential for modeling long-range temporal dependencies. Overall, the full model consistently achieves the best performance, demonstrating that the integration of MSTF, Transformer, GNN, and self-supervised learning leads to complementary improvements and superior identification capability.

Generalization validation

In hydraulic fracturing permeability enhancement engineering, the intelligent identification of weak microseismic signals is of great significance for evaluating fracturing effectiveness, optimizing construction parameters, and ensuring operational safety. However, due to the complexity of on-site monitoring environments, mechanical vibrations, electromagnetic interference, and environmental noise are often superimposed with effective microseismic events, making the identification of weak signals highly challenging. To evaluate the applicability of the SS-GNN-MSTF-Transformer model in real engineering scenarios, this section selects two sets of field microseismic monitoring data from Xinjiang and Northeast China for analysis, and compares the results with traditional methods such as Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), and Backpropagation Neural Network (BPNN), in order to further verify the recognition performance of the proposed model on engineering data from different regions.

The datasets used in this study are derived from actual hydraulic fracturing monitoring processes in mining areas in Xinjiang and Northeast China. Significant differences exist between the two regions in terms of burial depth, surrounding rock structure, noise composition, and station deployment patterns. Specifically, the Xinjiang dataset is characterized by strong and persistent mechanical interference, where weak events are more likely to be submerged in background noise. In contrast, the Northeast dataset better reflects the influence of local geological structures and station geometry on waveform propagation characteristics. Therefore, conducting comparative analysis based on these two real-world datasets can objectively evaluate the model’s identification capability under different engineering conditions.

Discussion

This study addresses the challenge of accurately identifying weak microseismic signals during hydraulic fracturing, which are often poorly recognized by traditional methods, by proposing an intelligent identification approach that integrates self-supervised learning, graph neural networks, and a multi-scale time-frequency Transformer. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves an average accuracy (Acc) and F1-score of 0.9343 ± 0.0142 under five-fold cross-validation, indicating high stability and reliability in the weak microseismic signal identification task. Compared with methods relying solely on single-domain time- or frequency-based features, the multi-scale time-frequency decomposition module (MSTF) in this study simultaneously extracts STFT spectral features and multi-scale convolutional features, enabling the model to comprehensively capture the non-stationary characteristics and multi-scale energy distribution patterns of microseismic signals. Additionally, the Transformer encoder employs a multi-scale attention mechanism to effectively capture both long- and short-term temporal dependencies in the signals, significantly enhancing the model’s ability to represent complex waveform structures. Meanwhile, the adaptive graph neural network module automatically learns latent spatial correlations among monitoring stations in a data-driven manner, overcoming the limitations of traditional approaches that rely on fixed physical topologies and enabling more effective multi-station information fusion. During the self-supervised pretraining stage, tasks such as contrastive learning, masked time-frequency prediction, and graph structure recovery leverage large amounts of unlabeled data to improve the model’s representation capability, resulting in faster convergence and higher classification accuracy during fine-tuning. Nevertheless, this study has certain limitations. The experimental dataset is relatively limited in scale and primarily sourced from a single mining site, leaving the model’s generalization across diverse geological conditions to be further validated. Future work could incorporate larger, multi-mine microseismic monitoring datasets and integrate additional information such as source location or fracture propagation, enabling joint analysis of weak microseismic signal identification and dynamic fracturing process evaluation, thereby further enhancing the practical applicability of the model in engineering operations.
