Loneliness and social isolation in transitions to adverse health conditions and mortality: an analysis of data from the UK Biobank study
Authors: Kaisla Komulainen, Ripsa Niemi, Mai Gutvilig, Laura Cachón Alonso, Christian Hakulinen, Marko Elovainio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse health conditions may serve as intermediate states linking loneliness and social isolation with excess mortality. We examined whether loneliness and social isolation are primarily associated with the risk of developing an adverse health condition or with prognosis after diagnosis.MethodsA cohort of 236 879 individuals from the UK Biobank was followed from baseline in 2006–2010 until death or administrative end of follow-up in 2022. The incidence of three broad categories of adverse health conditions, (1) neoplasms, (2) endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases and (3) circulatory diseases, was recorded through self-report or register-based data. Progressive multistate Markov proportional hazards models were used to examine the associations of loneliness and social isolation with transitions from a healthy state to adverse health conditions and mortality, while adjusting for confounding.ResultsLoneliness and social isolation were associated with all three transitions: from a healthy state to adverse health conditions (except for neoplasms), from a healthy state to mortality and from adverse health conditions to mortality. In transitions to adverse health conditions, the HRs were larger for loneliness (HR range 1.12–1.17 for loneliness, 1.05–1.08 for social isolation). In subsequent transitions to mortality, the HRs were larger for social isolation (1.05–1.13 for loneliness, 1.28–1.42 for social isolation).ConclusionWhile both loneliness and social isolation were associated with the onset and prognosis of adverse health conditions, our findings highlight the associations of social isolation, in particular, with mortality.Keywords: MORBIDITY, MORTALITY, COHORT STUDIES

Introduction

Contemporary societal shifts towards digital interaction and individualism highlight the importance of understanding mechanisms through which social disconnection, loneliness and social isolation may affect health and longevity.1 Although related in complex, bidirectional ways, loneliness and social isolation are conceptually distinct; while social isolation means objectively few social contacts and infrequent social interaction, loneliness refers to subjective distress arising from perceived social isolation, when the needs for preferred and actual social contact do not meet.2 3 Loneliness and social isolation are associated with excess mortality4 and several adverse health conditions, such as cardiovascular disease,5 6 diabetes7 8 and certain cancers.9 10 Furthermore, previous studies suggest a worse prognosis among individuals with adverse health conditions who lack social connections.11 12 A study using data from the UK Biobank reported that social isolation, but not loneliness, was associated with higher mortality after incident stroke and myocardial infarction.6 In Finnish register data, living alone was linked to higher mortality among individuals diagnosed with cancer.9 In another UK Biobank study, social isolation was associated with all-cause and cardiovascular disease mortality among individuals with type 2 diabetes, while loneliness was linked to non-fatal cardiovascular outcomes.13

Adverse health conditions may serve as intermediate states linking loneliness and social isolation to mortality. However, it is not known whether loneliness and social isolation are primarily associated with the risk of an adverse health condition, or the risk of death among individuals with an existing adverse health condition. The few studies that considered social connections in transitions from a healthy state to adverse health and subsequently to death were limited to old adults, small samples and self-reported health conditions, yielding mixed findings.14,16 Understanding the role of loneliness and social isolation in the temporal progression from a healthy state to mortality is crucial for guiding prevention and intervention efforts.

We used multistate models to examine the associations of loneliness and social isolation with the transitions from a healthy state to adverse health conditions and mortality among >230 000 initially healthy individuals from the UK Biobank with register-based data on morbidity and mortality. We included adverse health conditions from three primary ICD-10 diagnostic categories previously linked to loneliness and social isolation: (1) neoplasms, (2) endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases and (3) circulatory diseases.5,10 These conditions represent key broad categories of chronic, non-communicable diseases occurring across a wide age range, which contribute to the global disease burden and mortality.17 18 Associations with loneliness and social isolation were examined separately to understand their potentially distinct roles in progressing from a healthy state to adverse health conditions and mortality.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort of 502 506 UK volunteer participants aged 37–73 years at baseline. Baseline data were collected in 2006–2010 in 22 research assessment centres across the UK. We excluded 376 individuals due to withdrawal of consent, 20 622 due to missing data on either loneliness or social isolation at baseline, and 6 due to error in original data (ie, date of death preceding the date of a diagnosis of an adverse health condition). To mitigate reverse causation, 244 623 prevalent cases, that is, those diagnosed with the adverse health conditions of interest at or prior to baseline were further excluded, yielding a sample size of 236 879.

Procedures

Loneliness and social isolation

Loneliness was assessed with a single question ‘Do you often feel lonely?’ (0, no vs 1, yes) at baseline. A person was defined lonely if they gave a positive response. Single-item loneliness measures are highly correlated with the UCLA loneliness scale, a standard multi-item measure of loneliness used in population surveys. Social isolation was assessed with three questions at baseline: (1) ‘Including yourself, how many people live in your household?’ (1 point for living alone); (2) ‘How often do you visit friends or family or have them visit you?’ (1 point for less than one visit per month) and (3) ‘Which of the following (leisure or social activities) do you engage in once a week or more often?’ (1 point for not participating in any social activities at least weekly). The sum of the responses to these questions resulted in a scale ranging 0–3. Persons with 2 or 3 points were categorised as socially isolated, resulting in the final dichotomous variable.19 20

Adverse health conditions

Adverse health conditions were based on the first occurrence of (1) neoplasms, (2) endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases and (3) circulatory diseases, recorded through either self-report at any assessment centre, inpatient hospital data, primary care data, cancer register or death record data.21 The full list of adverse health conditions by ICD-10 diagnostic categories assessed is given in online supplemental table S1. Date of onset of each adverse health condition was defined as the date of the first recorded diagnosis in question.

Mortality

Mortality data until the administrative end of follow-up were retrieved from the national death registry data from NHS England in England and Wales and from the NHS Central Register, part of the National Records of Scotland, in Scotland.

Covariates

We included baseline covariates that we hypothesised may confound the associations of loneliness and social isolation with the transitions from a healthy state to an adverse health condition and death. Data on sex (male vs female) and age were obtained from local NHS Primary Care Trust registers at study recruitment. Ethnicity (white vs non-white), education (low (no secondary education), intermediate (secondary education) or high (university degree)), employment status (employed, retired or other) and prior psychological distress (ever vs never visited a doctor (GP) or a psychiatrist for nerves, anxiety, tension or depression) were self-reported by participants at baseline. In addition, we included residential socioeconomic deprivation assessed with the Townsend deprivation index categorised into tertiles (least, average and most deprived) prior to the study entry, assigned to participants based on their residential postcodes. Missing values for confounders were coded as a separate category.

Statistical analysis

Eligible individuals were followed from their baseline assessment until the date of death or administrative end of follow-up, whichever came first. Due to the availability of data on adverse health conditions in the UK Biobank, the administrative end of follow-up was 20 December 2022 for analyses of endocrine, nutritional and metabolic diseases and circulatory diseases, and 2 June 2022 for analyses of neoplasms. The transition rates of interest were between three states (healthy, morbidity, ie, adverse health condition and mortality). The associations of loneliness and social isolation with the transitions (A) from a healthy state to an adverse health condition, (B) from a healthy state to mortality without the adverse health condition and (C) from the adverse health condition to mortality were estimated with progressive multistate Markov proportional hazards models.22 Separate models with the same transition structure were conducted for both loneliness and social isolation as well as for the three broad categories of adverse health conditions and specific diagnostic categories. The three transitions could occur at any time during the follow-up. Backward transitions were not allowed; individuals who transitioned to an adverse health condition were considered to remain in that state until transitioning to death or the end of follow-up. To improve precision, the baseline hazards of the transitions B and C were assumed to be proportional.22 All estimates were adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, educational attainment, employment status, neighbourhood deprivation and prior psychological distress. We did not correct for multiple comparisons, as the individual null hypotheses we tested in our analyses do not form a joint null hypothesis.23

As sensitivity analyses, we repeated the analyses with a less restrictive exclusion criterion, only excluding those with a prior diagnosis of the specific adverse health condition of interest at or prior to baseline in each analysis. We also estimated models where the exposure was co-occurring loneliness and social isolation, with the reference including participants with either only loneliness, only isolation or neither, to assess whether the joint exposure would show greater associations with the transitions to morbidity and mortality. Additionally, we conducted sex-stratified models across the three broad categories of adverse health conditions to evaluate potential effect modification by sex. The analyses were conducted using STATA V.18 and R V.4.4.0. The analysis code is available at https://github.com/ripsaniemi/multistate_loneliness_isolation.

In models adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, education, employment status, neighbourhood deprivation and psychological distress, both loneliness and social isolation were associated with the transition from a healthy state to a diagnosis within the broad category of endocrine, nutritional or metabolic diseases (HR=1.17, 95% CI 1.14 to 1.20 for loneliness, HR=1.08, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.12 for social isolation) and circulatory diseases (HR=1.12, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.14 for loneliness, HR=1.05, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.07 for social isolation), but not with the transition from a healthy state to a diagnosis within the broad category of neoplasms (HR=0.99, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.02 for loneliness, HR=0.97, 95% CI 0.93 to 1.00 for social isolation) (transition A). The HRs for associations with diagnoses within the broad categories of endocrine, nutritional or metabolic diseases and circulatory diseases were slightly larger for loneliness than for social isolation (figures 3–4).

The diagnosis-specific associations were largely similar to those observed for broad diagnostic categories, with the exception of neoplasms. While both loneliness and social isolation were associated with a smaller risk of melanoma or other malignant neoplasm of skin (C43–C44), social isolation, but not loneliness, was additionally associated with a greater risk of malignant neoplasms of lip, oral cavity and pharynx (C00–C14), digestive organs (C15–C26), respiratory and intrathoracic organs (C30–C39), urinary tract (C64–C68), and female genital organs (C51–C58), and a smaller risk of neoplasms of male genital organs (C60–C63) (figure 2).

Both loneliness and social isolation were associated with the transition from a healthy state to mortality (ie, without the adverse health condition; transition B), with larger HRs consistently observed for social isolation and mortality (HRs ranged from 1.29 to 1.58), compared with loneliness and mortality (HRs ranged from 1.14 to 1.32) (figures 2–4).

There was evidence of excess mortality among individuals with adverse health conditions reporting loneliness or social isolation at baseline (transition C). While both loneliness and social isolation were associated with a greater risk of a transition from the broad categories of neoplasms and circulatory diseases to mortality, only social isolation was associated with mortality after a diagnosis of an endocrine, nutritional or metabolic disease. The excess mortality was larger for social isolation compared with loneliness: the HRs in relation to baseline loneliness versus social isolation were 1.12 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.20) versus 1.38 (95% CI 1.28 to 1.50) after a diagnosis of a neoplasm (figure 2), 1.05 (95% CI 0.98 to 1.12) versus 1.28 (95% CI 1.18 to 1.38) after an endocrine, nutritional or metabolic disease (figure 3), and 1.13 (95% CI 1.07 to 1.20) vs 1.42 (95% CI 1.33 to 1.51) after a circulatory disease (figure 4). In the diagnosis-specific analyses, this pattern was evident especially when examining transitions from circulatory diseases to mortality.

Graphical inspection of the Schoenfeld residuals suggested the proportional hazards assumption was not consistently met across the analyses (online supplemental figures S1–64); in these cases, we interpreted the HRs as weighted average ratios over the time period.24 The proportional baseline hazards assumption generally held across transitions B and C, although some deviations emerged in diagnosis-specific analyses (online supplemental figures S65–128). The transitions from morbidity to mortality were independent of the time spent in the previous immediate state (the Markov assumption; online supplemental tables S5 and S6).22

Results from sensitivity analyses with analysis-specific exclusion of prevalent cases of adverse health conditions of interest yielded the same main conclusions as the primary analysis (online supplemental figures S129–131, online supplemental table S10); for descriptive characteristics and incidence rates, see online supplemental tables S7–9). As expected, the larger sample sizes resulted in narrower CIs in the diagnosis-specific analyses; in contrast to the primary analysis, the majority of the diagnosis-specific associations of loneliness and social isolation with transitions from morbidity to mortality (transition C) were statistically significant. With few exceptions, the HRs for transitions from morbidity to mortality were consistently larger for social isolation compared with loneliness (online supplemental figures S129–131, online supplemental table S10).

Associations of co-occurring loneliness and social isolation were generally similar in magnitude and overall pattern to those from the primary analysis in which loneliness and social isolation were analysed separately. As expected, given the lower prevalence of co-occurring loneliness and social isolation, the estimates, especially from the diagnosis-specific analyses, were less precise (online supplemental figures S132–134, online supplemental table S11). In analyses stratified by sex, the general pattern of associations was consistent with the primary analysis, with slightly larger HRs for both loneliness and social isolation observed in transitions to mortality (transitions B and C) among males compared with females. Also, there was evidence of effect modification by sex in the association of social isolation with the transition from a healthy state to the broad categories of adverse health conditions (transition A): social isolation was associated with a greater risk of endocrine, nutritional or metabolic diseases and circulatory diseases only among females, and with a smaller risk of neoplasms among males (online supplemental table S12).

Discussion

In this study, among 236 879 individuals from the UK Biobank, we assessed the associations of loneliness and social isolation with transitions from a healthy state between ages 38 and 73 to common adverse health conditions and mortality. Both loneliness and social isolation were associated with transitions to endocrine, nutritional or metabolic diseases and circulatory diseases, with slightly larger HRs observed for loneliness compared with social isolation. Neither loneliness nor social isolation was associated with a transition to a diagnosis within the broad category of neoplasms. In diagnosis-specific analyses, however, social isolation was associated with either a higher or lower risk of the majority of common malignant neoplasms, whereas the associations of loneliness with neoplasms were smaller and more often non-significant. Furthermore, the HRs associated with social isolation were larger for transitions from adverse health conditions to mortality as well as from a healthy state directly to mortality (ie, not through the adverse health condition examined). These findings suggest that loneliness and social isolation are linked with both the onset and prognosis of adverse health conditions, although the magnitude of these associations differs.

To our knowledge, our study is the largest and most comprehensive investigation on the role of loneliness and social isolation in transitions from a healthy state to an adverse health condition and mortality. While previous studies have observed consistent associations of loneliness and social isolation with both adverse health conditions5,1020 25 26 and greater mortality,4 the few studies that explicitly examined the role of social relationships in transitions to adverse health and mortality reported mixed findings.14,16 In our study, with over 230 000 participants from the UK Biobank, we observed associations of loneliness and social isolation with transitions to both adverse health conditions and mortality. With the exception of neoplasms, the HRs for transitions to adverse health conditions were slightly larger for loneliness than for social isolation. In contrast, the HRs for transitions to mortality were larger for social isolation. This observation extends the evidence from previous studies that reported a worse prognosis of diabetes, myocardial infarction, stroke and cancer among individuals with smaller social networks and less social support.69 11,13

Related evidence on social inequalities in mortality suggested that inequalities exist in the transition from a healthy state to adverse health conditions, but not in the transition from adverse health conditions to mortality.27 In contrast, our findings suggest that loneliness and social isolation are associated with both transitions, yet with somewhat different effect sizes. This observation is consistent with the idea that loneliness and social isolation only partially overlap; in addition, the associations with morbidity and mortality were generally similar in magnitude when loneliness and social isolation co-occurred, compared with when any of the two were present. Apart from isolation, which can be a preferred social condition, loneliness refers to subjective distress arising from discrepancy between preferred and actual social contacts. Loneliness in particular has been hypothesised to elicit a heightened stress response, and chronic psychosocial stress has been implicated in the pathogenesis of cardiometabolic conditions.28 29 Social isolation, in turn, may entail pragmatic lack of structural social support or other psychosocial resources, which can affect the capacity and motivation to access and maintain treatment contact or sustain healthy behaviours.30 31 A previous study in the Whitehall II highlighted the role of behavioural factors in shaping mortality after cardiovascular disease or multimorbidity.32 In addition to the greater associations with transitions to mortality, we observed that social isolation was associated with a higher rate of cancers with a strong behavioural component, such as neoplasms of respiratory or intrathoracic organs largely driven by smoking, or neoplasms of female genital organs, for which early detection often depends on attending screenings. Our findings are consistent with a hypothesis that social isolation in particular may be associated with health and mortality through behavioural pathways.

Due to the low incidence of some adverse health conditions of interest, we had limited statistical power to conduct diagnosis-specific analyses by sex across all transitions. However, the associations of loneliness and social isolation with transitions to morbidity and mortality across the broad categories of adverse health conditions were generally consistent across sexes. Similar to a few previous studies,19 33 we observed slightly greater HRs for both loneliness and social isolation in transitions to mortality among males. We also saw evidence of effect modification by sex in the associations of social isolation with transitions from a healthy state to an adverse health condition: social isolation was associated with a greater risk of endocrine, nutritional or metabolic diseases and circulatory diseases only among females, and with a smaller risk of neoplasms among males. These findings are consistent with previous reports of sex differences in the association between living alone and cancer incidence,9 for instance. Explanations remain hypothetical but could involve gendered differences in treatment-seeking behaviours or cultural expectations around experiences of social isolation. Further research is needed to replicate and further assess the potential sex differences in how social disconnection is associated with health outcomes.

The main strength of our study was the large sample that allowed us to provide comprehensive estimates for transitions to a wide array of adverse health conditions and mortality. We obtained information on adverse health conditions and mortality from national health registers, thus avoiding bias from merely self-reported health conditions and yielding almost complete follow-up for all eligible participants. While our approach allowed an analysis across a wide range of adverse health conditions, modelling each adverse health condition as an independent state does not reflect the complexity of clinical reality, where disease progression often involves causally linked health conditions and the development of multimorbidity.34 Further work is needed to assess the role of social connection in such cascading patterns of disease.

Some other limitations should be noted. First, adverse health conditions can plausibly affect social relationships, resulting in reverse causation. To mitigate reverse causation, we excluded participants with a recorded diagnosis of the adverse health condition of interest at or before baseline. However, we lacked information on undiagnosed subclinical or early-stage conditions and cannot rule out the possibility that such unrecorded health issues led to social disconnection rather than the reverse. The concern of reverse causation is partially alleviated by our finding that the time to morbidity was not associated with the transition from morbidity to mortality, suggesting that the adverse health conditions conferred similar mortality risks regardless of their timing. While this does not rule out the possibility that early transitions from a healthy state to an adverse health condition reflect undiagnosed baseline morbidity, it mitigates the concern that such cases would disproportionately drive the associations of loneliness and social isolation with subsequent mortality. Second, while the proportional hazards models allowed for outcome-wide comparisons, the proportionality assumption was not consistently met across the three transitions and diagnosis-specific analyses. Thus, our effect estimates do not capture the time dependence of such associations and should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Third, our sample was limited in terms of ethnic diversity, and further research is needed to evaluate the generalisability of our findings to other populations. In addition, although risk factor–disease associations from the UK Biobank have been shown to align with findings from representative UK populations,35 the volunteer-based sampling population of the UK Biobank can compromise external validity.36 Fourth, despite adjusting for several potential confounders, there could be other confounders. For instance, due to the overlap of marital status with our measure of social isolation, we did not adjust for marital status at baseline; this decision may yet allow for confounding. While we hypothesised that lifestyle factors (eg, smoking, physical activity, alcohol consumption) would be intermediates (ie, on the causal pathway from social disconnection to adverse health conditions and mortality) rather than confounders, the associations can be bidirectional,20 and alternative causal hypotheses are possible. As we cannot rule out the possibility of unmeasured or residual confounding, nor dismiss evidence suggesting that loneliness might be a surrogate marker rather than a causal risk factor for health,37 our findings do not have a causal interpretation. Finally, loneliness and social isolation are conceptually distinct, but they likely influence each other in a complex, bidirectional manner. There was limited overlap between loneliness and social isolation at baseline in our sample, and the associations were broadly similar in magnitude when loneliness and social isolation co-occurred compared with when at least one was present. However, future research with longitudinal data on loneliness and social isolation is needed to systematically disentangle the direct and indirect pathways linking loneliness, social isolation and health outcomes.

Our analysis suggests that loneliness and social isolation are associated with both the onset and prognosis of adverse health conditions. While related, loneliness and social isolation are distinct; our findings highlight the associations of social isolation, in particular, with mortality. Further reseach is needed to disentangle the non-overlapping pathways through which loneliness and social isolation may generate differences in health and longevity to better understand optimal targets for primary and secondary prevention.
