Nurse-led menstrual hygiene management package to improve menstrual health practices among university students: Randomised waitlist-controlled protocol study
Authors: Ranjita Devi, Barkha Devi, Rubi Dey, Shraddha Deokota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Despite the cost, disposal issues, risk of infections and environmental burden, sanitary pads continue to be the most used method of menstrual hygiene among young women in India. Menstrual cups are a safer, cost-effective and environmentally friendly option, but acceptance is very low because of myths, cultural taboos and absence of organised education. There has been no randomised trial in Sikkim to assess a menstrual cup awareness and use promotion intervention. This study is designed to assess the effect of a Nurse-Led Menstrual Hygiene Management Package (NL-MHMP) on awareness, perception and sustained use of menstrual cups among university students.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A randomised active wait list-controlled trial will be conducted among 140 undergraduate female students (18–25 years) using proportionate stratified sampling technique from government and private universities in Gangtok, Sikkim and simple randomisation will be used to allocate participants into intervention and wait list groups. The NL-MHMP includes a 15-minute teaching session, audiovisual demonstration, graphical hand-out, clarification session, and the distribution of two ISO-certified menstrual cups with digital follow-up for six menstrual cycles. The primary outcome is sustained adoption of menstrual cup use among sanitary pad users. Secondary outcomes include changes in awareness, attitude, acceptability, adaptability, misconceptions, satisfaction, and preference toward menstrual cups.CONCLUSION:This nurse-led, digitally supported intervention offers a structured and scalable approach to improving menstrual health literacy and promoting sustainable menstrual hygiene practices among young women in Sikkim. Findings may inform university-based health programs and contribute to evidence-based policy initiatives supporting eco-friendly menstrual products.TRIAL REGISTRATION:The study is registered on 19/06/2025 in the Clinical Trial Registry India with trial ID: CTRI/2025/06/089117, prospectively registered.

Introduction

Menstruation is a natural physiological process that marks the onset of a woman’s reproductive life and is experienced monthly by millions of women worldwide.[1] Although it is a normal biological phenomenon, menstruation continues to be surrounded by fear, embarrassment, misconceptions, and inadequate guidance, particularly among adolescents and young women in low and middle-income countries.[2] Social taboos, cultural beliefs, and limited opportunities for structured education often restrict open discussion on menstrual health, negatively influencing knowledge, attitudes, and menstrual hygiene practices.[3]

The United Nations defines appropriate menstrual hygiene management (MHM) as the use of clean materials to absorb menstrual blood, the ability to change them in privacy as often as required, access to water and soap for personal hygiene, and facilities for safe disposal of used menstrual products.[2] Proper MHM is essential for safeguarding physical and reproductive health, maintaining dignity, supporting educational participation, and improving overall quality of life.[4] Consequently, menstrual hygiene promotion has been recognised as an important public health priority and is closely linked to global health promotion, gender equity, and sustainable development goals.[5]

Recent studies published have highlighted persistent gaps in menstrual hygiene knowledge and practices among adolescents and women of reproductive age. A cross-sectional study conducted among teens and reproductive-age women reported inadequate menstrual hygiene practices and emphasised the need for targeted awareness and educational interventions to improve menstrual health outcomes.[6] In addition, a recently published study protocol demonstrated the potential of innovative educational strategies, such as gamification, in improving pubertal and menstrual health education among adolescent girls, underscoring the importance of structured, theory-driven interventions in menstrual health promotion.[7]

Despite increasing awareness initiatives, disposable sanitary pads remain the most commonly used menstrual hygiene product. However, concerns related to recurring cost, disposal challenges, environmental burden, and sustainability have raised questions about their long-term suitability.[8] Menstrual cups have emerged as a safe, reusable, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly alternative to disposable menstrual products.[9] Nevertheless, awareness, acceptance, and consistent use of menstrual cups remain low, largely due to myths related to vaginal insertion, fear of discomfort, concerns regarding virginity and fertility, and the absence of organised, evidence-based education.[101112]

University students constitute an important target group for menstrual health promotion, as this period represents a transition toward independent health-related decision-making.[13] However, studies indicate that even among university-going women, misconceptions and negative perceptions regarding menstrual cup use persist.[1415] Nurses play a pivotal role in health education and are considered credible, approachable, and culturally sensitive sources of information. Nurse-led menstrual hygiene management packages that integrate structured education, practical demonstration, myth clarification, and follow-up support may therefore be effective in improving menstrual cup awareness and informed choice.[16]

Although several studies have explored menstrual hygiene practices and perceptions toward menstrual cups, most available evidence is descriptive or cross-sectional and lacks rigorous evaluation of structured educational interventions.[1718] There is a paucity of randomised controlled trials assessing nurse-led interventions focusing specifically on menstrual cup awareness and use, particularly among university students. To address this gap, the present randomised waitlist-controlled study protocol has been designed to evaluate a Nurse-Led Menstrual Hygiene Management Package targeting menstrual cup awareness and use. By prioritising awareness as the primary outcome and examining related behavioural and experiential factors, this protocol aims to generate evidence relevant to menstrual health promotion, nursing practice, and health education policy.[192021] Specific goals include:

To evaluate the impact of the Nurse-Led Menstrual Hygiene Management Package (NL-MHMP) on attitude, acceptability, adaptability, misconceptions, satisfaction, and preference toward menstrual cup use;To assess the sustainability and continuation of menstrual cup use across six consecutive menstrual cycles and beyond;To examine associations between socio-demographic and menstrual characteristics and key outcome measures; andTo determine the feasibility, content validity, and participant acceptability of the NL-MHMP as a group-based educational intervention.

Materials and Methods

Study setting and design

This study is designed as a quantitative, university-based randomised controlled trial with a waitlist control design. The study will be conducted in selected government and private universities located in the Gangtok district of Sikkim. To ensure balanced representation of institutions and academic disciplines, a proportionate stratified sampling approach will be applied at the institutional level. The participating universities collectively enroll an estimated 8000–10,000 female undergraduate students, which is sufficient to recruit the targeted sample size of 140 participants.

The trial follows a superiority design, aiming to evaluate the effectiveness of a Nurse-Led Menstrual Hygiene Management Package (NL-MHMP) in improving menstrual cup awareness and related behavioural outcomes among female undergraduate students. After baseline assessment, eligible participants will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group or the waitlist control group using a computer-generated randomisation sequence. Participants in the waitlist control group will continue their usual menstrual hygiene practices during the study period and will receive the complete intervention after completion of final outcome assessments.

Study participants and sampling

The study population will include female undergraduate students aged 18–25 years enrolled in the selected universities. Eligible participants must be menstruating, currently using disposable sanitary pads, and have no prior experience with menstrual cup use. Students who meet the eligibility criteria and provide written informed consent will be considered for inclusion in the study.

A proportionate stratified sampling technique will be used to select participants from both government and private universities, as well as across different academic courses, to ensure representativeness. Within each institution, participants will be screened for eligibility and enrolled until the required sample size of 140 participants is achieved. Following enrolment, participants will be stratified by institution and randomised into either the intervention group (n = 70) or the waitlist control group (n = 70) in a 1:1 allocation ratio.

This sampling strategy is intended to minimise selection bias, enhance internal validity, and ensure feasibility within the university setting while allowing for balanced representation of the study population. The reporting will adhere to CONSORT guidelines for non-pharmacological trials.[22] and describe components of the intervention in detail as per the requirements of STRICTA.[23] The timeline of enrolment, interventions, and assessments is described according to SPIRIT 2025 in Table 1.[24]

Intervention

The University-based randomised controlled trial with Nurse-Led Menstrual Hygiene Management Package (NL-MHMP) is designed to increase knowledge, dispel myths, and facilitate safe use of the menstrual cup among female university students. The intervention draws on evidence-based menstrual health education models and incorporates expert review. Groups of students will attend the sessions and will receive support through monthly digital follow-up during the duration of the intervention.

The NL-MHMP is a 15-minute PowerPoint presentation that the nurse uses to teach about menstruation anatomy, advantages of the menstrual cup, hygiene, usage, and troubleshooting. The following will be an audio-visual presentation on insertion, removal, folding methods, and cleaning, along with a graphical user guide for home practice. After an initial orientation which dispels myths and addresses common fears, the participants are given two ISO-certified menstrual cups to be used for six menstrual cycles. There is a brief question and answer session to clarify any questions from individuals.

On a monthly basis, they will be sent digital reminders and reinforcement through WhatsApp or phone calls to track usage, help address challenges, and empower them as they learn to use the menstrual cup. This continued support promotes continuity and persistence of practice during the study period. Participants may discontinue or modify use in cases of persistent discomfort, suspected urogenital infection, safety concerns, or upon personal request. Those who discontinue the intervention will continue outcome assessments unless they withdraw entirely.

Adherence Monitoring: Compliance with the NL-MHMP will be assessed through monthly digital check-in via WhatsApp or phone call, in which participants will report on menstrual cup use, challenges, and levels of comfort. Participants will also keep short usage logs. Attendance at the orientation and completion of ≥80% of monthly follow-up contacts will be considered sufficient adherence. Any problems that arise will be dealt with individual guidance provided by the nurse investigator. Study outcomes

The main outcome of this trial is awareness change in the use of a menstrual cup from baseline to end of six menstrual cycles assessed by a validated awareness questionnaire. Secondary outcomes are differences in attitude, acceptability, feasibility, misconceptions, satisfaction and preference toward menstrual cups versus safety pads. Menstrual cup sustainability of use for six cycles will also be measured. Baseline and monthly follow-ups for all outcomes will be conducted using standardised, self-administered instruments. Additional analyses will also investigate the relationship between socio-demographic variables and the key behavioural outcomes, as well as participants’ satisfaction with the NL-MHMP. These assessments will evaluate both the short-term and the long-term impacts of the intervention.

Trial Oversight and Data Management: This randomised waitlist-controlled trial will be overseen by the Principal Investigator with support from institutional authorities. Study monitoring will include periodic review of recruitment, informed consent, protocol adherence, and data completeness. As this is a low-risk behavioural study, no Data Safety Monitoring Board is required; instead, monthly internal reviews will assess intervention fidelity, outcome completion, and safety. Access to study data will be restricted to authorised personnel. All data will be coded, de-identified, and securely stored. Data quality will be routinely monitored, and missing data will be addressed using appropriate methods, including complete-case or imputation analyses. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to ensure robustness of findings.

Data analysis

Data will be analysed in IBM SPSS version 23. A per-protocol analysis will be performed, considering only participants who finish the intervention and provide outcome data for all six menstrual cycles. Descriptive statistics will be used to report the baseline demographics and outcomes. Categorical variables (frequency and percentage) and continuous variables (awareness, attitude, acceptability, adaptability, misconception, satisfaction and preference scores) will be analysed with means, standard deviations, medians, and ranges.

Baseline equivalence between the intervention group and the waitlist control group will be analysed by independent t-tests for continuous variables and Chi-square or exact (fisher) for categorical variables. Indicators of adherence, such as attendance at the initial session, completion of monthly follow-ups, and logs of use of the menstrual cup, will also be reported.

Data collection procedure

Data will be collected using standardised, self-administered questionnaires at baseline and at monthly follow-up points for six consecutive menstrual cycles. Assessments will be conducted through supervised in-person sessions and reinforced via digital follow-up to ensure completeness and consistency of data.

Study duration

Safety events will be reviewed biweekly, and any serious adverse event will be reported to the Institutional Ethics Committee within 24 hours.[192029]

Discussion

The present study protocol is grounded in the recognition that menstrual hygiene management is not merely a matter of product use but a complex interaction of knowledge, attitudes, sociocultural norms, access to credible information, and sustained behavioural support.[12728] Although menstruation is a universal biological process, the persistence of stigma, misinformation, and silence around menstrual health continues to undermine informed decision-making among young women, even within educated populations such as university students.[183031] This protocol responds to an important gap in menstrual health research by proposing a structured, nurse-led intervention that moves beyond descriptive assessment to systematically evaluate a theory-informed strategy for improving menstrual health practices.[121632]

National and international literature consistently highlights suboptimal menstrual hygiene practices and limited awareness of sustainable menstrual products, particularly menstrual cups.[28] Studies from India and other low- and middle-income countries report low adoption of menstrual cups despite evidence supporting their safety, cost-effectiveness, and environmental benefits.[1011] These findings are commonly attributed to deeply rooted cultural beliefs, fear of vaginal insertion, concerns related to virginity and fertility, and the absence of guided education.[91219] International studies have demonstrated acceptable safety and usability of menstrual cups; however, many focus on short-term acceptability and lack structured educational frameworks or extended follow-up to assess sustained behavioural change.[517] By incorporating longitudinal follow-up across six menstrual cycles, the present protocol addresses a major limitation of existing literature, the inability to evaluate adaptation, learning curves, and continuation of use over time.[163233]

The choice of a nurse-led menstrual hygiene management package is a key conceptual strength of this study. Nurses are central to health promotion and are widely regarded as trusted, accessible, and culturally sensitive health professionals.[18] Their role extends beyond information delivery to include counselling, myth clarification, skill demonstration, and ongoing support elements that are particularly important for interventions involving intimate health behaviours.[6] A nurse-led approach aligns closely with contemporary models of preventive healthcare and health education, emphasising empowerment, informed choice, and sustained behavioural change rather than one-time information dissemination.[34] From a health education perspective, the structured and manualised nature of the NL-MHMP enhances reproducibility and scalability, allowing integration into university health services, community health nursing programs, and broader public health initiatives.[323536]

Methodologically, this protocol contributes to the literature by employing a randomised waitlist-controlled design, balancing scientific rigour with ethical considerations. Ensuring that participants in the waitlist control group receive the intervention after completion of outcome assessments maintains ethical equity while preserving internal validity. Proportionate stratified sampling across government and private universities improves representativeness, while the inclusion of multiple outcome domains; awareness, attitudes, acceptability, adaptability, misconceptions, satisfaction, and sustained use reflects a comprehensive understanding of menstrual health practices as multidimensional constructs.[133738]

Several limitations should be acknowledged at the protocol stage. As a behavioural intervention, blinding of participants and intervention providers is not feasible, which may introduce expectancy or performance bias. Reliance on self-reported outcome measures may also be influenced by recall or social desirability bias.[39] Additionally, conducting the study in a university setting may limit generalisability to non-student populations or women from different sociocultural backgrounds.[14] While digital follow-up enhances feasibility and continuity, variability in access, privacy, and digital literacy may influence participant engagement.[61333]

Despite these limitations, the potential implications of this study are considerable. If the intervention demonstrates feasibility and positive trends, it may inform the development of standardised nurse-led menstrual health education modules within academic institutions and nursing practice.[1833] The findings may also contribute to policy discussions on promoting sustainable menstrual hygiene products as part of environmental health and women’s health promotion strategies. Future research could expand this work by incorporating mixed-methods designs to capture experiential perspectives, adapting the intervention for community or school-based settings, or evaluating long-term implementation and scalability outcomes.[63540]

In summary, this study protocol addresses a critical and underexplored area in menstrual health promotion by integrating nursing leadership, structured education, and longitudinal behavioural assessment. By focusing on menstrual cup awareness and use within a randomised framework, the proposed research has the potential to generate evidence that advances nursing practice, strengthens health education strategies, and supports sustainable and dignified menstrual health management.[833]
