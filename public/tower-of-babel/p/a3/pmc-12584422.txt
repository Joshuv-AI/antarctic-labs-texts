Cost-effectiveness of screening for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chunyan Song, Yuwei Xie, Zhiqiang Feng, Zhen Xue, Demosthenes Panagiotakos, Xiaoning Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimWe aimed to synthesize the current evidence on cost-effectiveness of screening for Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) and to meta-analyse the incremental cost effectiveness ratios (ICER) to quantify the economic value of different COPD screening strategies on COPD development.MethodsWe searched for publications in MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, Web of Science and Cochrane Library databases, until December 2024, without any restriction of countries or article type. ICER were retrieved and meta-analysis based on DerSimonian and Laird method was applied to pool ICER across studies.ResultsOf 1,108 originally retrieved papers, 12 articles fulfilled all search criteria which encompassed in total, 1,082,021 participants. Random effects meta-analysis revealed that the pooled ICER obtained from studies that used questionnaires for COPD screening against studies that did not perform any screening was $899.5 (95%CI [-1,903.9, 3,702.9]). The pooled ICER obtained from studies that used spirometry for COPD screening against studies that did not perform any screening was $521.3 (95%CI [116.0, 926.7]). When the analysis was based on studies that evaluated ICERs of the combination of spirometry testing plus questionnaires against no screening for COPD it was observed that the pooled ICER $15,305.8 (95%CI [5,364.2, 25,247.4]).ConclusionDespite variations in screening methods, the findings from this meta-analysis suggest that implementing COPD screening in primary care is cost-effective. It is recommended that policymakers prioritize screening for high-risk groups such as smokers and the elderly, and choose appropriate tools based on local economic conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24797-4.Keywords: Cost-effectiveness, Screening, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Meta-analysis

Introduction

According to the latest data from the World Health Organization (WHO), Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is the fourth leading cause of death globally,, causing 3.5 million deaths in 2021, approximately 5% of all global deaths [1]. Projections indicate that the number of COPD cases is expected to rise substantially, with estimates suggesting an increase of 112 million cases by 2050, bringing the total to approximately 592 million cases. This anticipated rise underscores the urgent need for enhanced preventive measures, early detection, and effective management strategies to mitigate the growing burden of COPD globally [2].

Screening for COPD is crucial for early detection and intervention, as the disease is often asymptomatic in its early stages. Early diagnosis and treatment can slow the pulmonary function decline, reduce the incidence of acute exacerbation of COPD, and thereby improve the patient’s quality of life, which are of great significance for COPD treatment [3]. However, evidence from randomized controlled trials supporting this view remains limited at present, particularly from large-scale studies, and more studies are urgently needed [4]. – [5] In addition, many individuals remain undiagnosed until significant lung damage has occurred [6]. – [7] A survey conducted in primary healthcare facilities in Poland showed that among 1,960 eligible participants, approximately 9.3% were diagnosed with COPD, while only 18.6% of these COPD patients had been previously diagnosed [8]. Similarly, a population-based study conducted in an Asian country found that only 24% of screen-detected individuals with COPD had ever been told of their diagnosis [9], highlighting the global under-recognition of COPD. The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) recommends spirometry as the diagnostic gold standard for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [10]– [11]; in addition, tools such as the COPD Assessment Test (CAT), modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale, peak flow measurements (though less precise than spirometry), and imaging techniques (particularly for unexplained respiratory symptoms) can be used to evaluate symptoms, exposure history, and structural changes in the lungs [10, 12].

While the clinical value of COPD screening is increasingly recognized, gaps in research on its cost-effectiveness have undermined its scalability and impact. First, studies on cost-effectiveness are fragmented and of inconsistent quality [13]. – [14] Second, there is a lack of comprehensive evidence across different regions [15], screening methods, and study populations, particularly in asymptomatic populations [16]. Finally, there is a dearth of integrated evidence on the long-term cost-effectiveness outcomes of screening [17].

The aim of the present systematic review was to synthesize the evidence on cost-effectiveness of screening for COPD, integrating cost-effectiveness data of COPD screening across different regions, populations, screening methods, and study durations (short-term, medium-to-long-term), and to draw insights on how to make current screening programs more cost-effective. A meta-analysis of incremental cost effectiveness ratios (ICER) was also applied to quantify the economic value of different COPD screening strategies on COPD development. Scientifically valuable and policy-relevant, this study provides an evidence base for designing more efficient COPD screening and management strategies. By identifying unmet clinical and economic needs, this study contributes to a fuller understanding of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and its public health impact, providing critical insights into optimizing resource allocation and improving the efficiency of COPD screening programs worldwide.

Materials and methods

We followed a pre-specified protocol registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024507159). Our report follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [18].

Search strategy

The search strategy was designed to identify all relevant studies on the cost-effectiveness of COPD screening. Articles in 4 electronic database including MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, Web of Science and Cochrane Library, were searched. All publications in English language, until December 2024 were searched without any restriction of countries or article type. The electronic database search was supplemented by a manual search of the reference lists of included articles. The Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms in MEDLINE (Ovid), EMTREE terms in EMBASE (Ovid), and keyword search terms for “chronic obstructive pulmonary disease”, “COPD”, “cost-effectiveness” and “economic evaluation” were used in all databases. The process followed the PRISMA guidelines, and the complete search protocol is available in Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

The study selection criteria were developed according to the PICOS (Participants, Interventions, Comparators, Outcomes, Study types) framework as recommended by the Cochrane Handbook [19].

Inclusion criteria: (1) Population: Studies involving individuals of any age, including at least a subgroup without a prior diagnosis of COPD; (2) Intervention: Any active COPD-screening strategy: questionnaires, portable spirometry, micro spirometry, or any combination thereof; (3) Comparator: No screening or usual care; (4) Outcomes: Studies reporting the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) or those in which ICER values could be derived from reported data or through correspondence with authors; (5) Study types: Full economic evaluations based on primary trial data, observational cohorts, or decision-analytic models.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Systematic reviews/protocols, conference/poster abstracts, or not economic evaluation studies; (2) Duplicate publications or multiple reports from the same study that do not provide additional usable data; (3) Studies restricted to patients with an established COPD diagnosis; (4) Interventions aimed solely at COPD treatment, management, or rehabilitation rather than screening; (5) Studies for which ICER could not be estimated based on available information or after contacting the authors.

Study selection

Studies that enrolled patients of any age and included at least a subset of patients free of COPD at baseline were included. Studies that only included patients already diagnosed with COPD were excluded. All studies that compared any screening questionnaires, or portable spirometer screening, or micro spirometry, or no screening were included.

Data extraction

Titles and abstracts of studies retrieved using the search strategy and those from additional sources were screened independently by two authors to identify studies that potentially meet the inclusion criteria outlined above. The full text of these potentially eligible studies was retrieved and independently assessed for eligibility by two authors, which were blinded to each other’s decisions. Any disagreement between the two review authors over the eligibility of studies was resolved through discussion with a third author.

Quality assessment

The Consensus on Health Economic Criteria list (CHEC-list) developed to assess methodological quality of economic evaluations was applied [20]. This tool contains 19 items of the same weight. Two authors independently assigned a value of “Yes” or “No” for each of the 19 items. Disagreements between the authors over the risk of bias in particular studies were resolved by discussion, with involvement of a third author where necessary.

Data analysis

ICERs were adjusted for inflation to 2023 value with the consumer Price Index and then converted to international dollars with purchasing power parity (PPP) conversion rates. We will also compare ICERs values to one gross domestic product (GDP) per-capita for 2023 from the World Bank. The Der Simonian and Laird’s random-effects model with standard error adjustment was used to meta-analyse the data. The delta method was used to approximate the standard error of the retrieved ICERs [21]. Egger’s regression test and funnel plots were applied to assess potential publication bias. Heterogeneity across studies was examined by the Cochran Q, I2 statistic (I2 threshold value of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicate low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively). The source of heterogeneity was investigated using stratified meta-analysis. The trim-and-fill method was also used to detect and adjust the results for any potential publication bias by reporting the probable impact of missing studies (i.e., observed vs. imputed) on the combined estimates. Meta-analysis was performed using STATA statistical software (StataCorp. Stata: Release 17. Statistical Software. College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Search results

The initial search yielded 1,108 articles, of which 157 Duplicates were removed. After screening by title and abstract, 38 articles were selected for full-text review. Of those, 12 articles were found eligible and included in the final review [6, 17]22– [31] (see Table 1). Articles were excluded for the following reasons: not economic evaluation studies (n = 15), systematic reviews/protocols (n = 4), conference abstracts (n = 1), not available results (n = 6). Among the included articles, ten had scores exceeding 16 points, one scored 15, and one scored 11. The overall methodological quality was moderate to high (see Table 2). Research characteristics

Of the 12 articles selected, five were referring to studies set in China, one in Canada, three in the UK, one in Brazil, one in Sweden, and one in USA; of them, six were from a High-Income Country (HIC) and six were from an Upper-Middle-Income Country (UMIC). Participants were from the general population, of both sexes, aged 40 and older (average age of the participants ranged from 51.0 to 65.21 years), including people with morbidities, and residents attending community health centers. The sample size of the studies ranged from 238 thousand to 1 million participants (see Table 1).

Methods of screening

The types of COPD screening evaluated in the selected studies were mostly symptom and risk factor-based questionnaires or spirometry screening. The questionnaires included the COPD Diagnostic Questionnaire (CDQ), the COPD Assessment in Primary Care To Identify Undiagnosed Respiratory Disease and Exacerbation Risk (CAPTURE), the COPD Screening Questionnaire (COPD-SQ) and the Chinese Symptom-Based Questionnaire (C-SBQ) (see Tables 3 and 4).

Modelling

Five studies applied modeling approach, including the decision tree, the Markov model and a previously validated discrete event. Five studies used Quality Adjusted Life-Years (QALY) as the measure of effectiveness, one study used both Life Years Saved (LYS) and QALY, and six studies used the number of cases detected as the measure of effectiveness (see Table 3).

Nine studies assessed evaluation periods ranging from several months to decades, with a minimum Duration of nine months and a maximum of 50 years. Among these, six studies explicitly stated their perspective: five adopted the viewpoint of the healthcare payer, while one considered the perspective of the health service. Additionally, six studies specified the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold, with two referencing GDP per capita, two using the cost per QALY, and one based on the per capita disposable income of urban residents nationwide. In terms of the discount rate, nine studies illustrated discount rates, seven of which were all 3.5% and the others were 3%, 1.5%. Sensitivity analyses were taken in all eight studies, seven of which were the univariate sensitivity analysis, and the other was the multivariate sensitivity analysis, as shown in Tables 3 and 4.

Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios

The 12 included articles comprised 52 independent cost-effectiveness analyses (see Table 5). The retrieved data were normalized for per capita testing costs and ICERs. The results showed that in terms of per capita testing costs, the studies conducted in China ranged from $2.7 to $121.7 per capita, those in Brazil from $1.9 to $3.4, those in the United Kingdom from $425.9 to $740.9, and those in Sweden from $3.1 to $23.2. In terms of ICER, studies conducted in China ranged from $−131.5 to $13694.7, in Brazil from $91.9 to $632.9, in the UK from $−48.8 to $3,226.5, in Canada from $19,084.6 to $55,143.3, in Sweden from $355.1, and in the US from $30,420.0 (see Fig. 2).

Cost-effectiveness data of the 52 studies

Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios of screening for COPD by countries’ gross domestic product per capita

Based on the principle adapted from WHO’s Commission on Macroeconomic and Health pervasive threshold, interventions with an ICER lower than 1×GDP per capita are considered “very cost-effective”, and those with an ICER lower than 3×GDP per capita are deemed “cost-effective”. Conversely, if the ICER is equal to or exceeds 3×GDP per capita, the intervention is regarded as “not cost-effective” [32–34].

When comparing ICERs with the willingness-to-pay threshold of 1×GDP per capita, the results show that all studies were below this threshold (thus classified as “very cost-effective”), except for one study in the UK (where all patients received combined CDQ and lung function screening every three years, with an ICER of $55,143.34) and one study in China (which adopted one-time screening using either a one-step or two-step method, with ICERs of $13,694.69 and $12,573.94 respectively), whose ICERs were slightly above their respective thresholds.

When ICERs were compared with the willingness-to-pay threshold of 3×GDP per capita, all studies were far below this threshold and therefore considered “cost-effective”.

Meta-analysis of ICER on COPD screening methods

A meta-analysis of ICER on COPD screening methods used in the studied projects (i.e., questionnaire, spirometry or combination), was performed. Random effects modelling revealed that the pooled ICER obtained from 13 individual studies included in the retrieved articles that used questionnaires for COPD screening against the studies that did not perform any screening [25, 27, 28, 30] was $899.5 (95%CI [−1,903.9, 3,702.9]) (Fig. 3, a). The leave-one-out approach to evaluate the sensitivity of the analysis revealed that the pooled effect estimates of ICER varied from $412.0 when the study by Mighua Du et al., was omitted to $1,925.8 when the study by Shuli Qu et al., was omitted. The level of heterogeneity was low (I² ≈ 0%). No publication bias was detected (Egger’s test Z = 0.31, p-value = 0.760; trim-and-fill method: observed (12 studies) vs. imputed (7 studies) 899.499 vs. 899.450).

Forest-plot of a meta-analysis of studies evaluated ICERs between (a) questionnaire vs no screening, (b) spirometry vs no screening, (c) spirometry plus questionnaire screening versus no screening, on COPD outcome

Then, the analysis was focused on eight studies comparing spirometry against no screening [22, 27, 28]. It was observed that the pooled ICER obtained from studies that used questionnaires for COPD screening against the studies that did not perform any screening was $521.3 (95%CI [116.0, 926.7]) (Fig. 3, b). The leave-one-out approach to evaluate the sensitivity of the analysis revealed that the pooled effect estimates of ICER varied from $521.4 when the study by Zihan Pan et al., was omitted to $3,967.3 when the study by Shuli Qu et al., was omitted. The level of heterogeneity was low (I² ≈ 0%); no indication for publication bias was observed (Egger’s test Z = 0.09, p-value = 0.924; trim-and-fill method: observed (8 studies) vs. imputed (4 studies) 521.348 vs. 521.377).

When the analysis was based on the 10 studies that evaluated ICERs of the combination of spirometry testing plus questionnaires against no screening for COPD [6, 17, 26, 27, 30] it was observed that the pooled ICER was $15,305.8 (95%CI [5,364.2, 25,247.4]) (Fig. 3, c). The leave-one-out approach to evaluate the sensitivity of the analysis revealed that the pooled effect estimates of ICER varied from $15,033.4 when the study by Kate M et al., was omitted to $16,313.5 when the study by Quiushi Chen et al., was omitted. The level of heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 55%). No indication for publication bias was observed (Egger’s test Z = 0.32, p-value = 0.747; trim-and-fill method: observed (12 studies) vs. imputed (0 studies)).

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to synthesize the evidence on cost-effectiveness of screening for COPD. This study provides new evidence for the cost-effectiveness analysis of COPD screening. The results show that different screening strategies, including separate questionnaire screening, spirometry testing, and the combination of both methods, have varying cost-effectiveness results. Our findings suggest that combined approaches may offer greater diagnostic accuracy but at a higher cost, whereas single-method strategies may be more affordable but less comprehensive. These variations underscore the importance of context-specific evaluations when implementing screening programs. Overall, the synthesis highlights both the potential benefits and trade-offs that policymakers must consider in adopting COPD screening strategies.

In studies comparing questionnaire screening to no screening, the pooled ICER was $899.5 (95% CI [−1,903.9, 3,702.9]). Although the wide confidence interval suggests some heterogeneity in the results, the sensitivity analysis revealed that excluding specific studies (such as those by Qu Shuli et al. [28].) significantly reduced the heterogeneity level, indicating that screening benefits are greatly influenced by population characteristics and geographic regions. In studies comparing spirometry to no screening, the pooled ICER was $521.3 (95% CI [116.0, 926.7]), which indicates that spirometry is also a cost-effective method for COPD screening. The study by Qu Shuli et al. showed that portable spirometry screening was more cost-effective than questionnaire screening, with an ICER of $5,026 per QALY compared to questionnaire screening and $1,766 per QALY compared to no screening [28]. This highlights the importance of using objective measures like spirometry to improve the accuracy of COPD diagnosis. Meanwhile, subgroup analysis showed that low heterogeneity and no publication bias indicate that spirometry screening is a reliable and cost-effective approach across different studies. The analysis of combined screening using questionnaires and spirometry revealed a higher pooled ICER of $15,305.8 (95% CI [5,364.2, 25,247.4]), suggesting that this combined approach may be less cost-effective compared to individual screening methods. Subgroup analysis results showed that the moderate heterogeneity may be attributed to different combinations of screening tools and the populations studied. For example, the study by Kate M. Johnson et al. showed that screening patients aged ≥ 40 years for CDQ every 3 years was cost-effective, with an ICER of $19,632 per case detected compared to no screening [27]. This indicates that the combined approach may be more suitable for specific high-risk populations, such as older individuals with a history of smoking.

However, differences in healthcare systems and national willingness-to-pay thresholds may play a crucial role in shaping the generalizability of cost-effectiveness findings. Healthcare systems vary widely in terms of financing mechanisms, reimbursement policies, resource availability, and priority-setting processes, all of which influence both the costs and outcomes of interventions. Similarly, national WTP thresholds reflect local economic conditions, societal values, and budgetary constraints, meaning that an intervention deemed cost-effective in one country may not be considered so in another. These variations limit the direct transferability of results across settings and highlight the importance of contextualizing findings within specific national frameworks. Expanding the discussion on these issues can provide greater clarity on the applicability of evidence, guide policymakers in interpreting results more appropriately, and emphasize the need for country-specific evaluations where substantial differences exist.

The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) recommendations provide widely accepted international standards for the diagnosis, management, and economic evaluation of COPD interventions [10, 11]. They emphasize the importance of cost-effectiveness analyses that incorporate QALYs, discounting, and appropriate perspectives, ensuring that resource allocation decisions are both clinically relevant and economically sound. Comparing our synthesis with the GOLD framework highlights the alignment of included studies with recognized global best practices. The GOLD recommendations also stress the need for evaluating long-term outcomes and the impact of interventions on patients’ quality of life, which directly relates to the use of QALY-based analyses in our synthesis [10]. Furthermore, they advocate for considering both direct healthcare costs and broader societal costs, reinforcing the value of adopting multiple analytical perspectives. By situating our findings within the context of GOLD, the relevance and applicability of the evidence to international policy and practice are further strengthened.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of the present synthesis of study’s findings is that the included studies used QALY estimation tools, discount rates, and analytical perspectives. This consistency enhances the comparability of results across different contexts and interventions. Moreover, it strengthens the robustness of the conclusions by ensuring alignment with established standards in health economic evaluations. However, due to limited information available from the selected studies, we could not explore potential clinical sources of heterogeneity (e.g., differences in populations, screening methods, or model assumptions) and discuss them accordingly. It should be underlined that the cost-effectiveness of COPD screening may also be influenced by factors such as the prevalence of COPD in the target population, the accuracy of screening tools, and the availability of healthcare resources. For instance, screening programs may be more cost-effective in populations with a higher prevalence of COPD, as the likelihood of detecting true cases is higher. Additionally, the implementation of screening programs should consider the availability of follow-up care and treatment options to ensure that patients diagnosed with COPD receive appropriate management. Moreover, heterogeneity in willingness-to-pay thresholds, model structures, and underlying economic contexts can significantly affect the validity of pooling incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) across studies. Variations in WTP thresholds reflect differences in healthcare system priorities, resource constraints, and societal preferences, while discrepancies in model structures—such as time horizons, discounting approaches, or included cost components—can introduce methodological inconsistencies. Additionally, diverse economic contexts, including differences in price levels, healthcare delivery systems, and disease burden, further complicate comparability. To address these challenges and strengthen the robustness of conclusions, it is advisable to perform sensitivity analyses or subgroup analyses. These approaches can help identify the influence of key assumptions, assess the stability of pooled results, and provide more context-specific insights for policy and decision-making.

Conclusion

Despite variations in screening methods, the overall data from this meta-analysis suggest that implementing COPD screening in primary care seems cost-effective. Both questionnaire-based screening and spirometry-based screening have demonstrated cost-effectiveness, while the combined approach of spirometry testing plus questionnaires may be less cost-effective but still valuable for specific high-risk populations. Future research on COPD screening should incorporate indirect costs, patient-centered outcomes, and the long-term effects of implementation to better inform policy and practice. Evaluating these dimensions will provide a more comprehensive understanding of real-world value and sustainability. Importantly, equity considerations must remain central, ensuring that screening strategies are accessible and beneficial to resource-limited and underserved populations.
