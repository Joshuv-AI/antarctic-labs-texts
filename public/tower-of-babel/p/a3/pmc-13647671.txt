Divergent dogfish: morphological correlates of vertebral column bending mechanics in two species of distantly related sharks
Authors: Marianne E Porter, Candido Diaz, Harald Kryvi, John H Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDogfish sharks from two different Orders — Squalus acanthias and Mustelus canis — offer the opportunity to compare whether known differences in vertebral morphology correlate with differences in the dynamic mechanical properties of the vertebral column. Histology of a caudal intervertebral joint in both species, combined with morphometrics of the entire column, provide the morphological information. Dynamic bending tests of ten-vertebrae sections of the vertebral column from the head to the base of the caudal fin are conducted by varying the amplitude of lateral curvature and bending frequency in a factorial design. Using multivariate modeling, this question is addressed: How do species, anatomical region, and vertebral column morphology correlate with the dynamic mechanical properties of the vertebral column in bending?ResultsVertebral column morphology differs in the ratio of precaudal to caudal vertebrae, the form of the intracentral canal, and the lateral position of the intervertebral ligaments. Six quantitative features of the vertebral column — dimensions and ratios of the centra and the intervertebral joints — vary between species. The dynamic mechanical properties, in turn, differ between species at three structural levels, the intact vertebral column, the cross-sectional level, and as apparent material properties. The two species have similar mechanical behavior: a strong response to changes in curvature and almost no response to changes in bending frequency; in both species, the loss modulus increases dramatically at the highest bending frequency. In spite of those similarities, most of the morphological and mechanical features are different. In terms of morphological correlates, the diameter and length of the centrum significantly and robustly predict the apparent material properties of storage and loss moduli, with coefficients of determination above 0.50 in all multivariate models for those properties.ConclusionsThe increase in the loss modulus at higher bending frequencies is consistent with the hypothesis that the vertebral column of dogfish sharks functions as a spring-brake mechanism during swimming. The mechanical properties are predicted, to a large degree, by variation in the morphology of the centrum within and between species, suggesting a possible link between vertebral morphology and bending mechanics, with morphological differences between species indicative of possible evolutionary trajectories for the alteration of the mechanical behavior of the vertebral column.Keywords: Centra, Vertebral column, Stiffness, Storage modulus, Loss modulus, Spring, Brake

Background

Although “dogfish” in the sense of being small adults with a fusiform body shape, the smooth dogfish, Mustelus canis, and the spiny dogfish, Squalus acanthias, are members of different Orders, the Carcharhiniformes and the Squaliformes, respectively. These Orders have diverged in the structure of their vertebral centra: Species of the Carcharhiniformes, at the late embryo stage, have well-calcified intermedialia and lamellae — structures that develop separately from but are connected to the medial double cones (corpus calcarean) — while species of the Squaliformes lack lamellae [1]. Structural differences matter mechanically: In Carcharhiniformes and Lamniformes, differences in the internal structure of centra correlate with differences in their compressive stiffness and toughness [2]. Centra are linked by ligaments that form intervertebral joints, and the morphometrics of the joints and centra vary by region within species and among different shark species; interspecific differences predict differences in body curvature during turning [3]. In biomimetic models of shark vertebral columns, differences in the axial length of the intervertebral joints control the apparent material stiffness of the column in lateral bending [4]. In turn, bending stiffness of the biomimetic vertebral column determines the swimming performance of self-propelled biorobotic fish [4, 5]. Taking advantage of the known differences in the structure of the centra of these divergent dogfish, we asked: How do species, anatomical region, and vertebral column morphology correlate with the dynamic mechanical properties of the vertebral column in bending?

In addition to the ordinal-level divergence in the structure of their centra, we chose these two species of dogfish for several reasons. The similarities in their external morphologies and ecologies are striking. At sexual maturity M. canis is between 95 and 100 cm total length, while S. acanthias is between 69 and 100 cm [6]. Both species have fusiform body shapes, recumbent heterocercal caudal fins, and two dorsal fins (but S. acanthias lacks an anal fin; see our Fig. 2). Their ranges overlap in the temperate and sub-tropical coastal waters of the western North and South Atlantic, including habitats that are demersal and distributions that overlap in depth between 50 and 200 m; while spiny dogfish eat fishes and squid, both species eat crustaceans and are migratory [7]. Thus we find it interesting, from an evolutionary perspective, to compare two species whose external, behavioral, and ecological similarities belie different vertebral column morphologies, and, we therefore predict, differences in the bending mechanics of the vertebral column.

This study builds on an approach developed to correlate variation in the body’s regional bending stiffness with its variation in regional vertebral column morphology in two members of the Carcharhiniformes: Blacktip sharks, Carcharinus limbatus, and a hammerhead species known as a bonnethead, Sphryna tiburo [4]. In a functional morphology framework, vertebrae and the intervertebral joints contribute to the mechanical behavior of the whole vertebral column [8]. Intraspecific regional variation in morphological features — like the axial length of the intervertebral joint and the centrum — are correlated with regional variation in the vertebral column’s bending stiffness [4].

Bending stiffness is a complex mechanical property underlying the flexibility of fish bodies [9] and the variable force transmission properties of shark vertebral columns [10]. Bending stiffness, the column’s resistance to an imposed lateral force, can be measured as spring stiffness, k (Nm− 1), a simple proportionality constant between the force, F (N), and the resulting displacement of the column, y (m). Using techniques common in biomechanics [10], the spring stiffness can be decomposed into its length-specific flexural stiffness, EI (Nm2), and its apparent material stiffness, the complex modulus, E* (Nm− 2). The complex modulus, in turn, can be reduced to storage modulus, E’, and loss modulus, E”, each representing, respectively, the composite material’s contribution to spring-like and brake-like mechanical behavior. These properties are “dynamic” in the sense that they vary with changes in the frequency and curvature imposed on the isolated vertebral column by the mechanical testing device to which it is connected [10]. Frequency and curvature change as a shark varies its swimming speed and acceleration. Thus, a vertebral column bending as part of a swimming, undulating body does not have a single bending stiffness. Instead, it has many: As the behavior of a shark changes, so too, does the stiffness of its vertebral column, sometimes acting as a spring and sometimes acting as an energy-dampening brake [10].

Sharks swim by undulating their bodies, creating traveling waves of bending that are, at any one place along the body, controlled variation in the magnitude and time rate of change of the curvature of the body [11]. While the nervous system has an obvious role in adjusting swimming maneuvers and hence behavior, a dynamical systems perspective [12] reminds us that body flexibility is controlled by two components acting in parallel and in series: (1) neural activation driving muscle contraction [13] and (2) dynamic viscoelastic properties of the body that determine stiffness and damping [14]. Thus, a key to understanding how sharks — or any fishes— swim is to measure the dynamic viscoelastic properties of the vertebral column.

Our previous work in the Atlantic spiny dogfish, S. acanthias shows that the viscoelastic properties of one region of the vertebral column change as swimming motion changes, varying continuously from elastic-dominated properties associated with springs to viscous-dominated properties associated with energy damping [10]. In terms of functional morphology, the mechanical properties of the vertebral column the Pacific spiny dogfish, S. suckleyi, arise from the morphological deformation of both intervertebral joints and the calcified vertebrae, both of which undergo significant strains during swimming [15]. Under physiologically relevant strains in a number of species, the compressive stiffness of isolated vertebrae varies as a function of vertebral shape, developmental age, axial position, and species [2]. But we lack a comparative understanding of the functional morphology of the whole vertebral column — vertebrae and their intervertebral ligaments from head to tail. By comparing two divergent species of dogfish, this work addresses those gaps in our understanding.

Methods

Specimens

All procedures involving live animals were carried out in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of animals in research. All experimental procedures were approved by the Vassar College Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol 10-05B). Animals were humanely euthanized using an overdose of MS-222 (500 mg L− 1 in buffered seawater) prior to tissue collection, in accordance with AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 Revised Version). Five live individuals each of Squalus acanthias (Squalidea: Squaliformes) and Mustelus canis (Triakidea: Carcharhiniformes) were obtained from a commercial supplier, the Marine Biology Laboratory (Woods Hole, Massachusetts). They were transported in aerated and temperature-controlled aquaria to Vassar College, Poughkeepsie, New York. In the laboratory, animals were euthanized one at time and immediately placed into the experimental workflow: Vertebral columns were excised posterior to the chondrocranium and anterior to the caudal fin. During dissection, we recorded sex, total length, trunk length, trunk vertebral number, and the total number of vertebrae for every shark (Fig. 1A; Table 1). Excised vertebral columns were divided into eight body segments of ten centra and nine intervertebral joints; these segments, we call a “bending segment” because that is the structure that was tested mechanically. Bending segments were stored in elasmobranch Ringers [16] at 22 °C until testing, which followed within 30 to 90 min, thus ensuring that tissues were fresh. Each body segment from all ten individuals was tested, providing nearly complete coverage of the vertebral column in the trunk and caudal regions. Please note that vertebrae beyond 80, which were primarily in the caudal fin, were not tested; this was a practical decision, since vertebrae in the caudal fin were too small to be gripped with our testing apparatus.

Morphometrics and histology

After mechanical testing, we used manual calipers to collect morphometric data from each bending segment: centra height, centra length, intervertebral joint length, and location of the transition from trunk to caudal vertebrae; segment width at the widest point, and length of the segment. The calipers had an accuracy of ± 0.1 mm; imprecision of the measurements was at most ± 6% range about the mean, as determined by taking repeated measurements of the same feature.

For histology we followed the general protocols for vertebrate tissues outlined in Kryvi et al., ([17] and [18]). Briefly, for each species, a caudal body segment from one individual was preserved in a phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solution with 10% freshly made formaldehyde, 25% glutaraldehyde, 20 ml 0.2 M cacodylate buffer and 60 ml PBS [19]. Samples were held in preservative for 72 h at 5 °C. The preserved samples were then stored in a PBS solution, with pH adjusted to 7.35, at 5 °C until processing. Vertebral columns were then decalcified in a solution of formic acid to allow sectioning. For thin section light microscopy, specimens were embedded in Technovit 7100 (Heraeus Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany). Thin sections were cut on a standard LKB microtome. Thick sections were cut with a scalpel. Tissues were stained with methylene blue and toluidine blue stains.

Mechanical testing

We subjected each body segment to a suite of 60 tests to measure the regional differences in mechanical properties along the length of the vertebral column. We used a custom designed and engineered rig to translate the uniaxial motion of our material testing system into bending loads imposed by end loading [4, 10, 15]. Mechanical tests were conducted on a MTS Tytron 250, whose calibration was company-certified annually, using a 50 N single-axis load cell. During testing, segments were submerged in an environmental chamber filled with 22 °C elasmobranch Ringers [16], allowing us to maintain tissue hydration and temperature throughout the suite of tests. The length of the moment arm, r, on the bending rig was held constant at 0.059 m. We tested each segment at five frequencies, f, (0.01, 0.1, 0.25, 0.50, and 1.00 Hz) and four actuator displacement amplitudes, x0 (1, 5, 10, and 15 mm). As x0 changes, so, too, does the amplitude of the segment’s curvature, κ0 (m− 1), which is also dependent on the length of the body segment, L (m), and the moment arm, r (m) of the actuator (Fig. 1 and [10]):

Moduli and mechanical properties

In addition to the linear motion of the actuator, x, the MTS recorded the corresponding force produced on the load cell, F (N), at any point in time. The instantaneous bending moment, M (Nm), is the product of F and r. For a given set of inputs, the flexural stiffness, EI (Nm2), was calculated as the ratio of the amplitude of the resulting bending moment, M0 (Nm), and the amplitude of curvature of the segment, κ0. For a given set of inputs, the spring stiffness, k (Nm− 1), was calculated as the ratio of 3 EI to L3.

The second moment of area, I (m4), was calculated in two ways for the vertebral column undergoing lateral bending: the I based on the total cross-section shape, Itotal, and the I based on just the shape of the centra, Icentra (for equations, see Fig. 5A). Our intention was to assess the consequences of these two different measures on the calculation of the bending moduli. Previous studies assume that only the ligaments at the intervertebral joint, connecting adjacent centra, contribute to flexural stiffness. That assumption is appropriate for the caudal vertebrae, which lack lateral processes. But in the trunk vertebrae, lateral processes extend much wider than the centrum and may amplify EI by increasing I. Because the formula for Itotal is based on a triangular shape, when the width, w, and height, h, of the cross-section converge on the diameter of the centrum, d, the weighted value of the cross-sectional area (weighted by the square of its distance from the neutral axis of bending, presumed here to be the mid-sagittal plane) drops Itotal below that of Icentra. This happens when h equals d and w equals ~ 1.33d, near the transition from the trunk to the caudal vertebrae, when the lateral processes have shrunk and the area of the cross-section is dominated by the circular centrum. At that point we set Itotal = Icentra to avoid calculating impossibly small values of Itotal.

Statistical analysis of morphology and mechanical tests

Three categories of statistical tests were conducted: (1) morphological, (2) mechanical, and (3) predicting mechanical properties from morphology. For morphological and mechanical analysis, linear mixed-effect models were conducted using the lmerTest library [20] in R version 4.3.1 [21]. For predicting mechanical properties from morphology, stepwise linear regressions with AIC selection criterion were conducted using the lm() and step() functions from base R. The critical value, α, is 0.05 throughout.

Results

Morphology

Meristics

While the samples of five individuals in each species do not vary significantly in fork length (Table 1), their vertebral columns differ significantly in the number of trunk, caudal, and total vertebrae (Fig. 1A; Table 1). S. acanthias had more trunk vertebrae and M. canis had more caudal vertebrae. M. canis also had significantly more vertebrae overall. They also differ in proportion, with S. acanthias having more trunk than caudal vertebrae and M. canis having the opposite pattern. Please note that these counts do not include the many vertebrae that are found in the caudal fin, since we did not measure the mechanical properties of that section.

Morphometrics

When the vertebral column was partitioned into bending segments (Fig. 1A), the blocks of ten vertebrae used in bending tests, the segment morphology of the two species differed statistically (Table 2) only in the diameter of the segment’s first centrum, with M. canis having vertebrae of greater diameter (mean of 8.1 v. 6.7 mm). The diameter also varied significantly with position along the body, the interaction of species and position, and fork length. The segments’ width varied significantly only with position along the body; segment height did not vary significantly with any factor; segment length varied significantly with position and fork length. Neither calculation of the second moment of area varied significantly with any factor.

Histology

Intervertebral structures differ between species at the intracentral canal (ICC) (Fig. 2C, D). The ICC separates adjacent intervertebral capsules (IVC) by a fibrous plug in M. canis, but by only a thin membrane in S. acanthias. Horizontal and transverse sections through the ICC at the same scale show differences in size and composition (Fig. 2E, F). Close-ups of the ICC contrast the occluded canal of M. canis with the large canal of S. acanthias (Fig. 2G, H). The ICC membrane of S. acanthias is easily pierced by a small wire, as shown in the inset in transverse perspective (Fig. 2H).

The patterns of mineralization in the vertebrae of these two species also differ (Fig. 3). In these horizontal sections, the calcified cone that forms the ICC is much larger in S. acanthias. While the calcified cone extends in S. acanthias to form the intervertebral joint, in M. canis the joint appears to be formed by middle zone cartilage, which we presume, because of the reduced calcification, to be of lower material stiffness. The differences in the formation of the joint are correlated with differences in the location of the intervertebral ligaments (Fig. 4A, B), with the fibers attached medially to the adjacent centra in M. canis and the fibers attached laterally to adjacent centra in S. acanthias. In this qualitative screening, we detect no differences in the size and shape of the vacuolated chordocytes that occupy the intervertebral capsule nor in the structure of the mineralized regions of the cartilage of the centra.

Mechanical properties

In separate linear mixed-effect models for Icentrum and Itotal, neither varied significantly by species, segment, or species-segment interaction (Table 2; Fig. 5B). But Icentrum and Itotal are different by definition (see equations, Fig. 5A); by our convention (see Methods) they converge when Itotal < Icentrum. This convergence happens in segment 5 at the transition from the trunk region, where vertebrae have lateral processes, to the caudal region, where they do not. The identity of Icentrum and Itotal at this point removes variance from this part of the data set and is likely the cause of the lack of any statistical differences between the two.

Of the nine morphological features measured at the level of the bending segments (Table 2) and the level of centra (Table 3), six varied significantly by species, position, the species x position interaction, and/or fork length (Fig. 6). Because they varied, we used them as candidate variables to predict mechanical properties (see below).

Discussion

In two species of divergent dogfish, we addressed this question: How do the dynamic mechanical properties of the vertebral column vary by species, anatomical region, and vertebral column morphology? While similar in size, appearance, and ecological niche, the two distantly related dogfish —Squalus acanthias (Order Squaliformes) and Mustelis canis (Order Carcharhiniformes) — show significant interspecific differences in the dynamic mechanical properties of their vertebral columns (Table 4), differences that are, in turn, correlated with variation in anatomical region (Figs. 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11) and vertebral column morphology (Table 5). We discuss these findings in the context of the mechanical behavior of the vertebral column during swimming. Using mechanical and engineering principles, we speculate on the causal connections between morphology and function in order to generate or reconsider testable hypotheses. Taken in this light, this work offers ways to consider biomechanically plausible evolutionary trajectories of the vertebral columns of sharks.

Species differences and possible evolutionary implications

The two species differ in terms of the regional organization of their vertebrae (Fig. 1), M. canis has a short trunk — the region where vertebrae have only neural arches — and a long caudal region — where vertebrae have both neural and hemal arches. In contrast, S. acanthias has a long trunk and a short caudal region. The two species also differ in terms of the size of some but not all aspects of their vertebrae and intervertebral ligaments (Fig. 6; Tables 2 and 3). The diameter of the centrum is greater in M. canis, while the intervertebral joint is longer in S. acanthias, which also has a greater ratio of the joint to the length of the centrum, increasing from the trunk to the caudal region. Since the diameter of the centrum is the basis for the second moment of area, I — which measures the contribution of the cross-sectional shape and distribution of material to flexural stiffness, EI — it was surprising that I did not differ statistically between the species.

Using histology, we verified the reported differences [1] in the microscopic structure of the caudal vertebrae (Fig. 2): M. canis has a closed intracentral canal (ICC) while the ICC of S. acanthias are open, with chambers of adjacent intervertebral capsules separated by a flexible septum of connective tissue. In addition to possessing a patent ICC, the caudal vertebral centra of S. acanthias have a robust calcified medial cone; in contrast, those of M. canis are small (Fig. 3). As Ridewood and MacBride [1] notes, these features are characteristic not just of these genera but also of the higher-level taxa that they represent.

In adult chondrichthyans, a centrum is a unitary structure developed from a composite of up to three separate layers [22]. Three layers, their developmental interactions with the adjacent notochord, and variable levels of calcification throughout provide the combinatorial opportunities that produce the variety of vertebrae seen within anatomical regions within an individual and among living and extinct taxa [23]. Using multiple phylogenetic techniques, Criswell [23] reconstructed the hypothetical common ancestor of chondrichthyans as having a vertebral column without centra; later, ring centra and whole centra evolved independently multiple times. When whole centra evolve, as they have in most of the living species of chondrichthyan, they are characterized by the double-cone structure, the corpus calcarean recognized by the early anatomists (for review, see [1]).

What selection pressures might possibly have driven the evolutionary origin of centra? In behaviorally autonomous fish-like robots, selection for enhanced foraging and predatory avoidance increased the number of ring centra in the propulsive tail [24]. As the number of centra increase in a biomimetic vertebral column, they cause the apparent storage modulus, E’, a measure of material stiffness, to increase when the column is bent; with increasing E’, steady swimming speed and peak acceleration of a self-propelled robotic swimmer also increase [4]. While these biorobotic studies demonstrate a causal relation between the number of ring centra, the stiffness of the vertebral column, swimming speed, and behavior, they don’t address the functional impact of differences in centra structure that we see between S. acanthias and M. canis.

The evidence for the vertebral column as a variable spring-brake

The double cone of centra has been hypothesized to determine, in part, the stiffness of the centra themselves, with cone shape and degree of mineralization changing their mechanical behavior as a spring [2, 25, 26]. If this were true, then we would expect the greater degree of mineralization in the double cones of caudal vertebrae of S. acanthias, relative to that in M. canis ((Fig. 3), to increase the stiffness of the whole column. We find support for this effect of increased mineralization in the higher E’ (Fig. 9) found in the caudal region of S. acanthias. Keeping in mind that E’ is the apparent material stiffness, with the impact of shape and size removed, it is interesting to note that when the effects of size and shape are present, the caudal centra of S. acanthias are less stiff in terms of spring stiffness, k, and flexural stiffness, EI (Fig. 7) and offer less energy return during bending than those of M. canis (Fig. 11). This may be explained by the shape of the cones, with S. acanthias having relatively shorter centra, as shown by the much greater ratio of the length of the intervertebral joint to the length of the centrum (Fig. 6D).

For a centrum to act as spring, storing and returning the force and energy of its deformation, it must change shape; that reconfiguration strains the material of the structure, rearranging it reversibly on a molecular level. Too stiff and it will function as postulated by Ridewood and MacBride ([1], p. 325): “The centra and the arches of the vertebrae [of] are fairly rigid, movements of the vertebral column as whole are permitted solely by the flexibility of the intervertebral ligaments and the tracts of white fibrous tissue that occur between the several plates of cartilage that constitute the neural and hemal arches.” But vertebrae are not rigid in S. acanthias: Significant strains in both the intervertebral joints and the vertebrae have been measured during swimming and in vitro bending (Porter et al., 2010).

The idea that centra act as springs is complicated by the fact that the intact vertebral column does not only behave mechanically as a spring. Using non-linear viscoelastic analysis, we have shown that the caudal region of the vertebral column of S. acanthias, bent under physiological loading conditions, has a range of continuously changing mechanical behaviors, from spring to brake [10]. When acting as a spring, the vertebral column stores and returns the energy required to bend the column, which is characterized by the apparent storage modulus, E’. When acting as a brake, which is characterized by the apparent loss modulus, E”, it absorbs and does not return that energy, serving as an energy-dissipating damper. By measuring E” throughout the vertebral column, we find that in both species, brake-like damping behavior is greatest at the higher bending frequencies and in the caudal region (Fig. 10). By calculating the ratio of E” to E’ as tan δ, it can be seen that the energy dissipation effect at 1.0 Hz is occurring throughout the column for both species. Given that bending frequency increases with increases in swimming speed, this suggests that the vertebral column is operating simultaneously as a spring and a brake at higher speeds.

The functional implications of this dynamic spring-brake mechanical behavior were not considered explicitly in classic computational models of fish swimming (for example, [27]), but damping and its effects on possible resonance of the body have been considered in the dynamic mechanical behavior of hagfish (Myxine glutinosa) notochords [28] and in more recent computational fluid models of swimming fish [29, 30]. In a physical model, increased damping of an isolated flapping tail improves, relative to tails with less damping, the magnitude of thrust generation and extends that high performance over a wide range of tailbeat frequencies [31]. Thus, the increased damping of vertebral columns of dogfish with increasing bending frequency may amplify and extend thrust generation over a range of swimming speeds.

The morphological correlates of the spring-brake behavior of the vertebral column

Considering the spring-brake’s underlying vertebral morphology, the double cone and intervertebral ligaments, as discussed above, are good candidates for spring-like mechanical behavior. But it’s less obvious what might serve as an energy-dissipating damper. The material of the centra itself has the potential for viscous damping — the water content of vertebrae ranges from a mean of approximately 25 to 53% among seven species of elasmobranch [32]. In addition, vertebral structures may serve a structural role as a viscous fluid dashpot. What does such a dashpot or damper look like? We conjecture that the septum of the ICC in S. acanthias (Fig. 2) serves as a damper, flexing during bending as the fluid within the intervertebral capsule flows as the joint reconfigures. If this is correct, then we would expect the size- and shape-independent measure of damping, the loss modulus, E”, to be greater in S. acanthias. We find this to be the case (Fig. 10). Note that this is a simple correlation, with a ceterus paribus assumption. Experiments or modeling of the size, shape, and stiffness of the ICC would be required to test its causal role in viscous damping.

One way to test whether morphological differences between species and regions of the body might possibly cause mechanical differences is to measure their correlations in a multivariate model: If variations in the morphological features are uncorrelated with variations in the mechanical properties, then we would reject any causal role of those features. Those that are significantly correlated are considered “predictors” of the mechanical property in the sense that they are either directly involved in the underlying mechanism or are correlated with morphology and materials properties.

To test for significant correlations, we generated a set of candidate morphological features. Because we had two primary factors in our statistical models for morphology — species and axial position — morphological features that varied significantly by one of those factors or their interaction were selected as potential candidates for predicting mechanical properties (Tables 2, 3). Six of the nine features were thus selected as candidates (Fig. 6). Because of our proposals about the importance of the structure of the centra in determining the storage and loss moduli, E’ and E” (see previous section), it is noteworthy that only three of the six candidate features — diameter and length of the centra and the width of the bending segment — predict storage and loss moduli (Table 5). Of those three features, only the diameter of the centra varies significantly between species (Fig. 6A). Given that the second moments of area, Ic and It, derived from measures of diameter (see Fig. 5A), do not vary by species (Table 2), it is likely that centrum diameter — rather than structural features like second moment that are based on beam theory — is directly and causally related to E’ and E”.

Because E’ and E” are size- and shape-independent mechanical properties, it came as a surprise that they were predicted by any morphological feature. Because they are apparent material properties, E’ and E” should — by engineering theory — be invariant with respect to morphological structure. But since E’ and E” are predicted by centrum diameter and length, it may be that those mechanical properties are a function of variation in mineralization and arrangement of that material within and between vertebral columns (Figs. 2 and 3). Differences in mineralization of the vertebrae of a range of shark species has been shown to correlated with differences in mechanical properties [32].

Moreover, and counter to expectations, coefficients in the multiple regression model for E’ and E” were negative for centrum diameter (Table 5): As centra increase in size both their storage and loss moduli decrease. In terms of the storage modulus, E’, this means that larger diameter centra, compared to those with smaller diameters, either have less mineralization and/or the biconic walls of the centrum have a different angle (Fig. 2), relative to the long axis, that shifts strains under axial loads from mostly parallel to perpendicular to those conic surfaces. This would change the loads on the cartilage from primarily compressive to primarily bending, and thus reduce the force-displacement response of the centrum and the apparent E’. For the apparent loss modulus, E”, the decrease with increasing centrum diameter may simply be explained by the fact that the intracentral canal (ICC), which we propose as a potential damper (see above), does not grow with increasing diameter, since centra grow by adding new material laterally.

Limitations

We caution that the correlation of mineralization, ligament attachment position, and intracentral canal (Figs. 2, 3 and 4) with species differences in mechanical properties (Figs. 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11) relies on the microscopic morphology of only one position in the caudal section of one individual of each species. While those data are consistent with previous work in these genera (for review, see [1]), we lack information about variation in those morphological features as a function of individual and anatomical position. While individual and regional variation are included in the morphometric data (Figs. 5 and 6), the correlations provided by the multivariate models (Table 5) can only be used to predict mechanical properties, not explain their structural and material causes.

When considering the function of the vertebral column during swimming, we are doing so without understanding the role of the other tissues of the body — muscles and skin most notably — in the mechanics of bending and thrust generation. Computational fluid dynamic models show the complexity of specifying the information needed about all of these physiological systems [29]. Experiments on whole, swimming animals are needed to adequately test the spring-brake hypothesis.

It is possible that both mineralization and structural differences play a causal role in the variable spring-brake mechanical behavior of the vertebral column seen between species of dogfish and within individual anatomical regions. But we lack the information to test that hypothesis. We examined internal morphology of the vertebral column in only one location: the caudal region. We did not measure mineralization of whole centra or their separate parts. Morphological correlates may predict but they do not explain. We look to future studies for testing of this hypothesis.

Conclusions

In two divergent species of dogfish shark, the dynamic mechanical properties of the vertebral column differ: they vary by anatomical region and are correlated with vertebral column morphology. The diameter and length of the centrum are robust predictors of the storage and loss moduli, with multiple regression models accounting for more than 50% of the variance in the mechanical property. These correlates indicate but do not demonstrate a morphological basis for mechanical behavior of the vertebral column as a spring-brake mechanism during swimming.
