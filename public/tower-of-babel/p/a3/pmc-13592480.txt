Complementary predictive value of electromagnetic source imaging and hemodynamic responses in epilepsy surgery: A quantitative spatial analysis
Authors: Chifaou Abdallah, Edouard Delaire, Arielle Dascal, Aura González, Zhengchen Cai, Tanguy Hedrich, Hassan Khajehpour, Satoru Ikemoto, Masataka Tanaka, Boris C. Bernhardt, Eliane Kobayashi, Jean Gotman, Birgit Frauscher, Christophe Grova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAccurate presurgical evaluation should assess not only the location but also the spatial extent of epileptic activity relative to the surgical cavity, a practical approximation of the epileptogenic zone. We evaluated how electromagnetic source imaging (EMSI) and hemodynamic responses to epileptic discharges relate to the surgical cavity and postoperative outcome, and whether multimodal concordance improves surgical prediction.MethodsConsecutive patients with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy who underwent electroencephalography/magnetoencephalography (EEG/MEG), and EEG/functional magnetic resonance imaging (EEG/fMRI) followed by surgery and ≥1‐year postoperative follow‐up were included. EMSI was performed using maximum entropy on the mean. The most significant fMRI cluster was extracted. All results were projected onto a common cortical surface. Spatial concordance with the surgical cavity was quantified using spatial overlap (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]), distance localization error (DLE), and spatial dispersion (SD). Predictive performance was evaluated using a combined spatial criterion (AUC >0.70 and DLE = 0 mm).ResultsTwenty‐five patients were included. EEG/MEG, EEG/fMRI, and multimodal analyses were contributive in 23, 14, and 12 patients, respectively. In the EEG/MEG group (n = 23), patients with good outcome showed higher spatial overlap (mean ± standard deviation, AUC: 0.70 ± 0.14 vs 0.56 ± 0.18; p = 0.04), shorter DLE (4.83 ± 6.80 mm vs 17.98 ± 14.39 mm; p = 0.02), and a trend toward lower SD (24.45 ± 17.07 mm vs 34.21 ± 14.22 mm; p = 0.08) than patients with poor outcome. EEG/fMRI (n = 14) exhibited similar trends without statistical significance. In predicting surgical outcome, EMSI achieved a negative predictive value of 62% and an accuracy of 61%, whereas EEG/fMRI reached 83% and 86%, respectively. When both modalities were considered (n = 12), all patients with concordant resected findings achieved a good outcome, whereas 8 of 10 patients with discordant results did not.SignificanceEEG/MEG and EEG/fMRI showed complementary profiles. Resection of EEG/MEG localizations were more frequently associated with good outcome, whereas unresected EEG/fMRI responses predicted poor outcome. Concordant and resected multimodal findings were associated with good outcome, supporting a quantitative multimodal approach in presurgical evaluation.

Key points Larger electromagnetic source imaging (EMSI) spatial overlap with the cavity, lower distance localization error, and lower spatial dispersion were associated with good surgical outcome.Electroencephalography/functional magnetic resonance imaging (EEG/fMRI) demonstrated higher negative predictive value, as unresected localizations were often linked to poor surgical outcome.When both modalities were concordant and resected, all patients achieved a good surgical outcome, highlighting their complementary predictive value.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy surgery is an established treatment for drug‐resistant focal epilepsy, with surgical success depending on accurate localization and resection of the presumed epileptogenic zone (p‐EZ). 1 , 2 The p‐EZ, however, is not a focal point but a spatially distributed cortical region capable of generating seizures. 3 Defining both its location and spatial extent remains a major challenge in presurgical evaluation, particularly in patients with non‐lesional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or discordant clinical and electrophysiological findings. 4 In this context, non‐invasive functional imaging techniques have become increasingly valuable for estimating cortical regions involved in epileptogenesis. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11

Simultaneous electroencephalography/magnetoencephalography (EEG/MEG) and EEG/functional MRI (EEG/fMRI) provide complementary information on epileptic activity. 10 , 11 EEG/fMRI offers whole brain coverage and high spatial resolution maps of blood oxygen level–dependent (BOLD) changes linked to epileptic discharges, 8 , 12 , 13 whereas MEG directly measures magnetic fields generated by these discharges, offering high temporal and good spatial resolution but low sensitivity to deep sources. 9 , 10 , 14 When combined with EEG, electromagnetic source imaging (EMSI) improves source localization accuracy by integrating both electrophysiological signals into a unified source model. 15 , 16

Both modalities have yielded promising results in identifying candidate regions for surgical resection, with several studies demonstrating good concordance between the most significant fMRI response or EMSI findings and intracerebral EEG findings. 7 , 10 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Moreover, previous studies have reported that resection of the most significant fMRI response cluster is generally associated with good outcome, although not sufficient to ensure seizure freedom (with negative predictive values ranging from 0.63 to 100%). 19 , 21 For MSI, reported predictive values are similarly variable, with negative predictive values between 40 and 65% and positive predictive values between 60 and 83%, 9 , 20 likely reflecting methodological and cohort differences. Most previous studies have relied on qualitative evaluations or single‐point distance metrics between the localization peak (EEG/fMRI responses or EEG/MEG sources) and the surgical cavity, 9 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 overlooking the spatial extent of the p‐EZ. Given that incomplete resection of the p‐EZ may lead to persistent seizures, 3 , 4 assessing spatial overlap and dispersion relative to the surgical cavity may provide a more clinically meaningful evaluation.

In the context of epilepsy surgery, when both modalities are available, their respective predictive value for postoperative outcome remains unclear. Should surgical planning prioritize regions identified by EEG/MEG, EEG/fMRI or by both modalities? Addressing this question requires analyzing both modalities in the same space. However, few studies have done so, 10 , 23 , 24 and most relied on qualitative or semi‐quantitative comparisons without a common anatomic reference, leaving their predictive performance unclear.

In this study, using the same space, we aimed to (1) quantify the spatial relationship between EMSI, the most significant fMRI response, and the surgical cavity; (2) assess their predictive value for postoperative seizure outcome; and (3) determine whether multimodal concordance improves prediction.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and patient's selection

We retrospectively identified patients with drug‐resistant unifocal epilepsy from the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) MEG database, who underwent both simultaneous EEG/MEG and EEG/fMRI as part of their presurgical evaluation between 2008 and 2019. Patients were included if they subsequently underwent open resective surgery and had at least 1 year of postoperative follow‐up. All patients had available preoperative and postoperative imaging. The study was approved by the institutional ethics board, and written informed consent was obtained. Of note, surgical planning was based on the overall electroclinical hypothesis integrating clinical, electrophysiological, and imaging data, including stereo‐electroencephalography when indicated, whereas EEG/MEG and EEG/fMRI results were considered at the discretion of the treating physicians. EEG/MEG acquisition and analysis

Simultaneous EEG/MEG recordings, processing, and analysis were performed as described in previous studies. 10 , 25 , 26 Recordings were acquired using a 275‐gradiometer CTF MEG system (MISL, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada) and a 56‐channel EEG cap (Easy‐Cap, Herrsching, Germany). 25 , 26 Each session lasted ~1 h, divided into 10 runs of 6 min each.

Interictal epileptic discharges (IEDs) were visually marked on MEG by a board‐certified epileptologist (C.A.), independently of EEG/fMRI results. The two runs containing the largest number of IEDs (or one run if the number exceeded 100 IEDs) were selected for analysis. 10

Source localization was performed using the coherent maximum entropy on the mean (cMEM) method, which is sensitive to the spatial extent of the underlying generators. 27 , 28 , 29 This framework enables a fusion approach for EMSI by combining EEG and MEG data into a single, unified source model. 15 , 16 EMSI was computed for each single marked IED, and a consensus map was generated by applying hierarchical clustering across all source maps, followed by averaging the maps within the cluster containing the largest number of IEDs. 10 This procedure improves the reliability of EMSI results across different IED types. 15 For patients with fewer than 10 IEDs, EMSI was computed using the averaged IED to balance consensus map reliability and source localizations signal‐to‐noise ratio. 10 The final consensus EMSI maps were analyzed at the first peak visible on either EEG or MEG data, since the first signal has been shown to have the highest concordance with intracerebral findings. 30

EEG/fMRI acquisition and analysis

Simultaneous EEG/fMRI recordings, processing, and analysis were performed as described in previous studies. 17 , 21 Data were recorded using a 3 T MRI scanner with 25 MRI‐compatible EEG electrodes and were collected in runs of 6 min each, for a total of 6 to 15 runs per patient, acquired with patients at rest. Cleaned EEG data were reviewed by epileptologists (S.I. and M.T.), and IEDs were visually identified and grouped according to their morphology/shape and spatial distribution.

Functional MRI data were analyzed using an event‐related design implemented in fMRIstat. 31 Each IED type was modeled as a separate regressor in a general linear model, using the onset and duration of all marked IEDs convolved with four predefined hemodynamic response functions peaking 3, 5, 7, and 9 s after the spike onset. 32 A statistical t‐map was generated for each IED type with noise modeled by nuisance regressors (e.g., motion and global averaged signal in the brain). A combined t‐map was then created by taking, at each voxel, the highest absolute t‐value from the 4 t‐maps created with each hemodynamic response function. This combined t‐map from each IED type was first thresholded (t > 3.1) and corrected for multiple testing using a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05. 17 For subsequent analyses, only the fMRI cluster exhibiting the highest absolute t‐value (either activation: positive t or deactivation: negative t), corresponding to the most significant cluster, was considered for comparison with the surgical cavity. This cluster has been shown to best correlate with intracerebral findings (spike‐onset and seizure‐onset zones) 10 , 17 , 18 and to have surgical predictive value. 19 , 21 Although IED grouping in EEG/fMRI was based on scalp morphology and distribution, EMSI retained the largest source–space cluster after hierarchical clustering of individual IED maps. While these approaches may not isolate exactly the same IED subtype, both were intended to capture the dominant IED population in each patient.

Postoperative imaging (MRI or computed tomography [CT]), the anatomic MRI acquired during EEG/fMRI, and the corresponding fMRI t‐map were co‐registered to the 3T anatomic MRI used for EMSI processing. For each patient, the surgical cavity was manually delineated in volume space using MRIcron 33 and smoothed using 5 mm kernel in Matlab (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) to account for potential co‐registration inaccuracies and postoperative brain distortion. 34 For fMRI analysis, in order to obtain a spatially contiguous pattern suitable for surface‐based interpolation and spatial comparison, we applied a voxel wise threshold of |t| > 3.1. Both the selected fMRI cluster of interest and the binary surgical cavity mask were projected onto the reference cortical surface. This projection followed a previously described method based on voronoï cells, which define anatomically informed interpolation kernels respecting sulco‐gyral morphology. 35 For each cortical vertex, the resulting fMRI response was averaged across the voxels comprising the corresponding voronoï cells.

To quantitatively assess the spatial agreement between EMSI, the most significant fMRI cluster, and the surgical cavity considered as the gold‐standard together with surgical outcome, we computed three spatial metrics. (1) Area under the curve (AUC): the AUC of a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was derived by applying various thresholds on the EMSI maps, 36 using the surgical cavity voxels as reference. To reduce sampling bias caused by the large number of true‐negative voxels outside the surgical mask, AUC values were computed as the mean of two complementary measures: AUC_close (negative voxels, i.e., outside the cavity but near the cavity) and AUC_far (negative voxels distant from the cavity). Both measures were calculated using an equal number of voxels inside and outside the surgical cavity, with external voxels selected based on their proximity to the cavity boundary (30–35 mm). 10 , 36 Values above 0.70 were considered indicative of good spatial overlap. 10 (2) Distance localization error (DLE): the minimum Euclidean distance (in mm) between the maximum of each modality's map to the nearest voxel of the surgical cavity was calculated. 37 When the maximum localization was within the cavity, DLE = 0 mm. (3) Spatial dispersion (SD): spatial dispersion quantified the spatial spread (in mm) of each modality's response relative to the surgical cavity. It was calculated as the root mean square of the distance between each voxel and the closest resected voxel, weighted by the energy (squared value) of the source localization map at that voxel. 29 Because no statistical threshold can be directly applied to EMSI maps, these spatial metrics were computed without any threshold, as described previously. 14

Additional spatial metrics were estimated at the voxel‐level for each patient, precision (proportion of correctly localized voxels in the surgical cavity) and recall (proportion of the surgical cavity correctly identified by EEG/MEG or EEG/fMRI). For these analyses, voxels within the cavity were considered as ground truth, independently of postsurgical outcome. Precision and recall were derived from the same voxel‐wise overlap framework used for AUC computation (AUC_close and AUC_far). EMSI voxels were classified as active at 30% of the maximal EMSI value, whereas fMRI voxels were considered active when showing non‐zero values after interpolation onto the cortical surface.

Statistical analysis

We conducted non‐parametric tests. Patients were classified into good (Engel class I) and poor (Engel class II–IV) surgical outcome groups. Differences between outcome groups for AUC, DLE, and SD were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The rank biserial correlation (rb) was used to represent effect size (ranges from −1 to + 1), with absolute cutoff values of 0.1–0.3, 0.3–0.5, and >0.5 interpreted as small, moderate, and large effects, respectively. Associations between spatial metrics were examined using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient ρ, with absolute values of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 interpreted as small, moderate, and strong correlations, respectively. Analyses were restricted to patients with contributive findings for the corresponding modality, defined as >5 IEDs for EMSI and a significant fMRI response for EEG/fMRI.

To assess the ability of each modality in predicting postoperative outcome at the patient‐level, patients were classified as true positive (TP), false positive (FP), true negative (TN), or false negative (FN) based on the spatial relationship between imaging results and surgical outcome, using the following two criteria: Combined spatial threshold: good spatial overlap (AUC > 0.70) 10 and resection of the maximum response (DLE = 0 mm) 37 ;Distance‐only threshold: DLE = 0 mm, consistent with prior studies relying only on the resection status of the maximum response. 9 , 19 , 21

A case was considered TP when the criterion was met in a patient with good outcome; all other combinations were classified as FP, TN, FN, accordingly. For example, for the combined spatial threshold (AUC > 0.70 and DLE = 0 mm), and using EEG/MEG, TP was defined as a patient with AUC > 0.70, DLE = 0 mm, and good outcome; FP met the same spatial criterion but had poor outcome; TN did not meet the spatial criterion (AUC ≤ 0.70 or DLE > 0 mm) and had poor outcome; and FN did not meet the spatial criterion but achieved a good outcome. For each modality and condition, we then computed sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV).

Finally, to determine whether spatial agreement between electrophysiological and hemodynamic responses improved predictive accuracy compared to individual modalities, analyses were restricted to patients with contributive results from both modalities, which were defined as concordant when both localizations were resected (DLE = 0) and discordant otherwise. All analyses were performed using the JASP statistical software (Version 0.95.1), 38 with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Twenty‐five patients (mean age of 27 ± 9 years, 12 female) with drug‐resistant focal epilepsy were included. Ten patients (40%) achieved a good surgical outcome, whereas 15 (60%) had persistent seizures at their last follow‐up. The cohort included both temporal (n = 9) and extratemporal (n = 16, including 4 patients with a presumed temporal plus epilepsy) cases. MRI was negative in 10 patients (40%). Table 1 summarizes the patient clinical and electrophysiological characteristics. A total of 1137 IEDs (range 9–215) were marked in EEG/MEG, of which 816 were included in the main cluster of the consensus map (cluster with the largest number of IEDs). In EEG/fMRI, a total of 2864 IEDs (range 3–897) were analyzed. Twenty‐three patients (92%) had EMSI results (>5 IEDs), whereas EEG/fMRI resulted in significant statistical fMRI responses in 14 patients (56%). Precisely, among the 25 included patients, 5 had no IED recorded during EEG/fMRI, and 6 showed recorded IEDs but no significant fMRI cluster. For EEG/MEG, two patients had fewer than five recorded IEDs. Consequently, 12 patients had contributive results from both modalities.

Spatial performance metrics relative to the surgical cavity

In the EEG/MEG group (n = 23), patients with good surgical outcome showed significantly higher AUC (mean ± standard deviation: 0.70 ± 0.14 vs 0.56 ± 0.18; p = 0.04, (r (rb)) = −0.51), shorter DLE (4.83 ± 6.80 mm vs 17.98 ± 14.39 mm; p = 0.02, (r (rb)) = 0.57), and lower but not significant SD (24.45 ± 17.07 mm vs 34.21 ± 14.22 mm; p = 0.08, (r (rb)) = 0.43) compared with patients with poor outcome (Figure 3).

Patients with good surgical outcome showed significantly higher precision (0.77 ± 0.15 vs 0.36 ± 0.39; p = 0.02, (r (rb)) = −0.55) compared to those with poor outcome (Figure S1). No statistically significant difference was found when considering the recall (Figure S1).

In the EEG/fMRI group (n = 14), similar but not statistically significant differences were observed between outcome groups across the three spatial metrics (AUC: 0.68 ± 0.18 vs 0.58 ± 0.22; p = 0.73, (r (rb)) = −0.15, DLE: 11.16 ± 17.14 mm vs. 13.55 ± 18.37 mm; p = 0.62, (r (rb)) = 0.20, and SD: 27.07 ± 16.85 mm vs 30.82 ± 27.40 mm; p = 0.84, (r (rb)) = −0.10; Figure S2). No statistically significant differences were found between good and poor outcome groups when considering precision and recall metrics (Figure S1).

Regarding the correlation between these spatial metrics, for EEG/MEG, we found that AUC correlated negatively with DLE (ρ = −0.64, p < 0.001) and with SD (ρ = −0.57, p = 0.005), whereas DLE correlated positively with SD (ρ = 0.40, p = 0.05; Figure 4). Similarly, for EEG/fMRI (Figure 4), AUC correlated negatively with DLE (ρ = −0.68, p = 0.006) and with SD (ρ = −0.60, p = 0.02), whereas DLE and SD showed a positive trend of correlation (ρ = 0.49, p = 0.07).

Surgical predictive performance

Using the combined threshold of AUC > 0.70 and DLE = 0 mm to assess the ability of each modality in predicting postoperative outcome at the patient‐level, EMSI achieved a PPV of 57% [95% confidence interval [CI]: 25%–84%], NPV of 62% [39%–82%], and an accuracy of 61% [41%–78%]. EEG/fMRI showed a PPV of 100%, NPV of 83% [55%–95%], and an accuracy of 86% [60%–96%].

When considering DLE = 0 mm alone, EMSI predictive values increased with a PPV of 67% [35%–88%], NPV of 71% [45%–88%], and an accuracy of 70% [49%–86%]. Results from EEG/fMRI remained the same (Table S1).

Of the 12 patients with contributive results from both modalities, two showed spatially concordant findings, with the maximum localization of both EMSI and the most significant fMRI cluster located within the surgical cavity; both patients achieved good outcome (Figure 5, P#16). The remaining 10 patients displayed discordant localizations between modalities. Among them, only two patients achieved good outcome, one with the EMSI localization resected (DLE = 0 mm), whereas the maximum fMRI response was relatively distant from the cavity (DLE = 36.16 mm; Figure 5, P#15), and the other one with both EMSI and fMRI results near the resection margin (DLE = 8.50 mm for fMRI and DLE = 18.10 mm for EMSI). Among the eight patients with poor outcome (Figure 5, P#18), none had the maximum fMRI response inside the surgical cavity, whereas the EMSI maximum was resected in three of them.

DISCUSSION

This study quantitatively assessed how EMSI primary source and EEG/fMRI most significant fMRI cluster relate to the surgical cavity and seizure outcome in drug‐resistant focal epilepsy. For EMSI results, patients with good outcome showed greater spatial overlap, shorter distance to the resection cavity, and a trend toward lower spatial dispersion around the cavity compared with those with poor outcome. Although EEG/fMRI metrics did not differ significantly between outcome groups, similar trends were observed (Figure S2), and, importantly, not resecting the most significant fMRI clusters was strongly predictive of poor outcome. When both modalities were available, patients with concordant and resected localizations (n = 2) achieved a good outcome, supporting the complementary value of integrating source localization and hemodynamic information in the presurgical workup.

Our EEG/fMRI findings are consistent with previous large‐scale studies (70 and 84 patients) demonstrating that when the peak of the most significant fMRI cluster was not resected, the likelihood of achieving a good outcome was low, especially in cases where the difference in absolute t‐values between the most significant and the next cluster was large, indicating high‐confidence results. 19 , 21 In our cohort, 10 of 12 patients (83%) whose peak voxel of the most significant fMRI cluster was located outside the surgical cavity had poor outcome. Among the two remaining patients, one did not have a high confidence result (Figure S3), whereas the other exhibited a high confidence result with a DLE of 8.5 mm. Under our strict criterion (DLE = 0 mm), this latter case was classified as non‐resected; however, using a broader 2 cm distance criterion, including both complete and partial concordance with the surgical cavity, as in a previous study, 21 the same patient would have been considered resected. This interpretation aligns with prior results showing that this cluster often co‐localizes with the seizure‐onset zone, the main clinical proxy of the p‐EZ, identified by intracerebral EEG. 10 , 18

Regarding EEG/MEG, our results showed a significant association at the voxel level between resection of the EMSI cluster with the largest number of IEDs and good outcome. This finding aligns with prior results showing that removal of the source‐imaging localization is associated with good outcome. 9 , 20 , 39 , 40 In a large MEG study of 1000 cases (149 underwent epilepsy surgery and had a follow‐up of at least 1 year postoperatively), the authors reported a PPV of 83%, NPV of 65%, and a sensitivity of 66% for complete MEG resection in predicting outcome, using dipole modeling for source localization. 9 In another EEG/MEG study of 141 patients (57 operated) comparing multiple software packages and different standard source imaging approaches, the authors found a PPV between 60% and 83%, NPV between 42% and 49%, and an accuracy ranging from 44% to 57%, depending on the localization method/software used. 20 In our study, using the same proximity‐based criterion (DLE = 0 mm), we obtained a PPV of 67%, NPV of 71%, sensitivity of 60%, and an accuracy of 70% (Table S1). Some variabilities in predictive performance across studies are expected, as differences in patients electroclinical presentation, in software, source localization methods, and the epileptologist performing IED marking have all been shown to influence outcomes. 20 , 39

Of interest, when combining DLE with AUC to account for both proximity and spatial overlap, the overall predictive performance slightly decreased (PPV from 67% to 57%, NPV from 71% to 62%, and accuracy from 70% to 61%). This reduction may reflect the fact that the p‐EZ may not be a homogeneous entity but a spatially distributed network where certain nodes may be more critical for seizure generation. 25 , 41 , 42 Therefore, resection of these key nodes may be sufficient to achieve surgical success, even if parts of the broader generator remain. Thus, although a good spatial overlap between EMSI localizations and the surgical cavity differentiated good from poor outcomes at the group level in our study, extensive overlap may not be necessary for individual success. Our threshold (AUC > 0.70) might therefore have been a little bit conservative. Future studies with larger datasets are needed to refine optimal spatial thresholds and investigate the network properties of the p‐EZ.

Another relevant point is related to the voxel level spatial analyses. In a previous source imaging study of 29 patients, recall and precision values were moderate (0.6) in those with good outcome and slightly lower (0.4–0.5) in those with poor outcome, without significant group differences. 22 In our cohort (n = 23), recall, which represents the overlap between EMSI localization and surgical cavity normalized by surgical cavity, was similar between groups (0.24 in good vs 0.20 in poor outcome, Figure S1), whereas precision, which indicates the overlap between EMSI localization and surgical cavity normalized by EMSI map, differed significantly (0.77 vs 0.36m Figure S1). This discrepancy may be explained by methodological differences, as cMEM may produce more focal maps 14 than the source imaging method used previously. 22 Consequently, with cMEM, a lower sensitivity but higher precision (along with a lower rate of FP) would be expected when comparing to the surgical cavity. These results suggest that EMSI effectively captures part of the p‐EZ (approximated by the surgical cavity). Of note, the significantly higher AUC values observed in patients with good outcome indicate that MEM‐derived estimates of spatial extent remain clinically informative and provide complementary information beyond simple peak localization.

However, it is important to mention that the resection cavity generally exceeds the true EZ and may not represent the ground truth, particularly in patients with poor outcome. Surgical success depends more on the proportion of accurately localized epileptogenic tissue (precision) than on the total spatial overlap with the surgical cavity.

Of interest, when applying the AUC metric, which integrates both precision and recall into a continuous, surface‐based measure rather than a voxel‐wise binary classification (i.e., precision and recall), we were able to distinguish good and poor outcomes. This indicates that AUC captures more anatomically meaningful and clinically relevant information than simple overlap metrics that are threshold dependent. It highlights that not only the extent but also the intensity and spatial organization of abnormal activity identified by EMSI contain clinically relevant information. 43 In line with this, correlations among spatial metrics further support the structured organization of epileptogenic activity. Higher AUC values were associated with shorter DLE and lower SD, whereas DLE correlated positively with SD. These relationships indicate that regions closer to the surgical cavity tended to be more spatially compact and more strongly overlapping with the surgical cavity. In a recent study involving MEG and intracranial EEG simultaneously acquired, we further confirmed the performance of cMEM in recovering the generators of IEDs with their underlying spatial extents, resulting in similar AUC values. 44

EMSI was contributive in a larger proportion of patients than EEG/fMRI (23/25 vs 14/25), likely because EEG/fMRI requires both sufficient interictal activity during scanning and a statistically significant hemodynamic response after correction for multiple comparisons. In contrast, EMSI can be applied more broadly when sufficient IEDs (>5) are recorded. Although this may suggest greater practical applicability of EMSI, the two modalities appear complementary rather than interchangeable, as EEG/fMRI may still provide additional value, particularly for deep generators, 12 , 13 where EEG/MEG source imaging can be more challenging. 44

Finally, combining these modalities yielded the highest predictive accuracy. All patients with concordant and resected EMSI and EEG/fMRI localizations achieved good outcome, whereas discordant results were associated with poorer outcome (8/10 patients with discordant results had poor outcome). These findings are consistent with a prior multimodal study showing that resection of concordant regions was associated with good outcome. 23 This complementary value reflects their underlying physiology: EMSI captures rapid neuronal discharges with high temporal precision, primarily reflecting the zone generating the most frequent IEDs but also the seizure‐onset zone, 5 , 7 , 10 , 30 whereas EEG/fMRI detects slower, spatially distributed hemodynamic changes in brain regions with highest oxygen consumption, typically corresponding to the spike‐onset and seizure‐onset zones. 12 , 17 , 18

The strengths of this study include the integration of multimodal data on a common cortical surface, allowing direct spatial comparison across modalities, and the use of quantitative, continuous metrics that extend beyond categorical classifications. However, this study also has limitations. The sample size was relatively small. In our center, EEG/MEG and EEG/fMRI are performed primarily in complex cases; therefore, the clinical yield of each modality across all presurgical patients is unknown. This was particularly true for patients with contributive results from both modalities, which further reduced statistical power. In addition, multimodal concordance analyses were restricted to patients with contributive findings from both modalities. Therefore, the predictive performance of concordant vs discordant results applies only to this selected subgroup and may overestimate the clinical utility of multimodal concordance in an intent‐to‐treat perspective. Only the most significant fMRI cluster was analyzed, potentially omitting clinically relevant information from other clusters. However, this cluster has been shown to best correlate with intracerebral findings and surgical outcome. 8 , 17 , 18 , 21 Similarly, for EMSI, only the cluster with the largest number of IEDs was included. 10 Although this approach has been shown to yield more reliable and physiologically consistent results, 15 , 16 it may have excluded relevant information from smaller clusters. At the individual level, the interpretation of AUC should be made with caution, as our results are derived from group‐level analyses. AUC reflects the proportion of the EMSI, EEG/fMRI results contained within the surgical cavity and may be clinically informative. To mitigate the effects of postoperative brain deformation and cavity shrinkage, the resection cavity was smoothed with a 5‐mm kernel and projected onto the preoperative cortical surface. A strong correlation between cavity size measured in the original volume space and after projection onto the cortical surface was found, supporting the validity of our surface‐based approach (Figure S4). Higher AUC values, particularly when associated with high precision and short distances to the resection, suggest that the identified region is well captured by the planned surgery, whereas lower AUC values may indicate partial overlap or extension beyond it. Finally, although this is a multimodal study, additional features, such as connectivity metrics, 22 , 25 , 43 seizures, 22 , 45 , 46 and gamma spikes 47 , 48 , 49 were not explored. Future studies with larger cohorts may assess whether integrating these features in addition to our approach can improve the predictive performance in epilepsy surgery.

CONCLUSION

This study quantitatively shows that EEG/MEG and EEG/fMRI provide complementary spatial information relevant to presurgical evaluation in drug‐resistant focal epilepsy. By assessing overlap, proximity, and dispersion relative to the surgical cavity, we gained a more nuanced understanding of how EEG/MEG and EEG/fMRI localizations relate to surgical outcome. Notably, when both modalities were spatially concordant and resected, patients tended to achieve surgical success, whereas discordant results were associated with poorer prognosis.
