Infant social withdrawal with parents and strangers—The role of parental sensitivity and depression
Authors: Maja Rudling, Anne Christine Stuart, Johanne Smith‐Nielsen, Ida Egmose, Katrine Røhder, Mette Skovgaard Væver
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfant social withdrawal is an early indicator of infant distress linked to adverse socio‐emotional development. We hypothesized that parental sensitivity moderates an association between parental depression (PPD) and infant social withdrawal. We examined these associations cross‐sectionally in 471 families in which mothers screened positive for PPD, assessing infant social withdrawal (mean infant age 3.6 months) during interactions with mothers, fathers/co‐parents and a stranger. No significant association was found between parental PPD symptom severity and infant social withdrawal in interaction with either the parents or the stranger. Instead, parental sensitivity was negatively associated with infant social withdrawal in mother–infant interactions and less consistently in father/co‐parent–infant interactions, though not in interactions with the stranger. Thus, in families at risk for PPD, infant withdrawal may be more closely related to parental sensitivity than parental depressive symptoms. Moreover, the influence of parental sensitivity on infant social withdrawal may not extend beyond parent–infant interactions.

INTRODUCTION

Infants' social interaction with their caregivers is a major driver of social, emotional and cognitive development early in life (Feldman, 2007; Markova et al., 2019). However, infants will not constantly engage in social interaction. Instead, typical early social behaviour is characterized by the infant initiating intermittent breaks and shortly withdrawing from social interactions (Beebe et al., 1997; Braarud et al., 2013; Guedeney, 1997). Such infant social withdrawal can take the form of turning their head away, closing their eyes, engaging less in eye contact and smiling and vocalizing less (Guedeney et al., 2013; Guedeney & Fermanian, 2001). It is hypothesized that short instances of social withdrawal are important, early self‐regulatory behaviours (Guedeney et al., 2013; Mäntymaa et al., 2008). If these social withdrawal behaviours become prolonged and increase in frequency, however, they can be a sign of infant distress which may affect both socio‐emotional and cognitive development negatively (Guedeney et al., 2012, 2013, 2014; Milne et al., 2009; Rubin et al., 2009; Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2019). According to attachment theory, such sustained withdrawal may reflect a protective strategy of the infant, following attempts at protests, as a way of detaching from a caregiver that cannot provide the care and emotional bond that the infant needs (Ainsworth & Bowlby, 1991; Dollberg et al., 2006). Indeed, infant social withdrawal has been found to be more prevalent in children showing insecure attachment (Costa & Figueiredo, 2013). Furthermore, attachment theory proposes that attachment is built from early interactions between caregivers and infants, and that the quality of the caregiving is reflected in infant attachment behaviour (Madigan et al., 2024). Parental depression is in turn a caregiving factor that has tentatively been linked to both insecure attachment (Madigan et al., 2023; Śliwerski et al., 2020) and infant social withdrawal (Dollberg et al., 2006; Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2019; Stuart et al., 2022). Although parental depression is a heterogeneous condition (Apter et al., 2012; Lovejoy et al., 2000; Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2016), for some depressed parents the interaction with their child is characterized by being overly intrusive or detached, thus disrupting the parent–child relationship (Tronick & Reck, 2009). A proposed mitigating factor between infant social withdrawal and parental depression is therefore the parent's ability to be attentive and respond appropriately to their infant's needs, termed parental sensitivity (Goodman & Gotlib, 1999; Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2019). In this study, we tested this by examining the interplay between infant social withdrawal, postpartum depression (PPD), and parental sensitivity across different interaction contexts. Clarifying when and with whom infants exhibit social withdrawal helps us understand whether the behaviour signals a specific relational difficulty or a broader behavioural pattern that extends beyond the caregiver relationship (Goodman & Gotlib, 1999).

Parental sensitivity is characterized by the parent being attentive to child cues of emotional and/or physical needs and responding promptly and appropriately to these cues (Ainsworth et al., 2015). Sensitive parenting has consistently been linked to secure infant‐parent attachment (De Wolff & van Ijzendoorn, 1997; Madigan et al., 2024; Verhage et al., 2016), and positive socio‐emotional development more generally (Kok et al., 2013; Leerkes et al., 2009; Raby et al., 2015). Specifically in relation to infant social withdrawal, infants with more social withdrawal tend to have less sensitive mothers (Dollberg et al., 2006; Puura et al., 2013). Many studies on parent–child interaction focus on the mother, but the few studies on father–infant interaction have generally found fathers to be similarly sensitive in their behaviour towards their infant as mothers (Deneault et al., 2022). Despite this, there seem to be some differences in how mothers and fathers typically interact with their infants, where mothers more often engage in caregiving‐focused behaviours than fathers (Cabrera et al., 2014). To our knowledge, only one study has investigated infant social withdrawal in relation to paternal sensitivity (Tissot et al., 2025). In that study, higher levels of sensitive parenting in fathers were associated with less infant social withdrawal in interaction with the father, alike to the previous studies on mother–infant interaction. Interestingly, they found that the link between maternal sensitivity and infant social withdrawal was inconsistent and smaller than the one for fathers. Although difficult to interpret in and of itself, this result may pinpoint the need to study parent–infant interaction in all caregivers to understand the relationships between parenting and infant behaviours, as differences in parenting roles may shape how sensitivity relates to infant withdrawal. These previous studies have examined the link between parental sensitivity and infant social withdrawal in interactions with the parents, but less is known about the influence of parental sensitivity on infant social withdrawal with a stranger. While social withdrawal in interaction with a caregiver that exhibits low sensitivity can be seen as an adaptive strategy for an infant coping with disruptions of the caregiving relationship and attachment bond (Ainsworth & Bowlby, 1991; Goodman & Gotlib, 1999), it is not evident that the influence of parental sensitivity would extend to social withdrawal in interactions with individuals outside of the immediate caregiving context. Therefore, an infant exhibiting social withdrawal when interacting with a stranger as a consequence of caregiving style may reflect a more generalized atypicality in social behaviour, possibly shaped by more sustained or severe caregiving stressors (Field et al., 1988; Goodman & Gotlib, 1999; Matthey et al., 2013; Weinberg & Tronick, 1996). It is therefore important to examine the relationship between parental sensitivity and infant social withdrawal not only in interactions with the parents but also with a stranger.

Parental mental health issues, and particularly postpartum depression (PPD), have been found to negatively impact parental sensitivity (Bernard et al., 2018). Occurring within the first year after childbirth or late during pregnancy, PPD is a disorder characterized by intense feelings of sadness, hopelessness, anxiety and/or irritability, difficulty bonding with the baby and less interest in activities previously enjoyed (American Psychiatric Association, 2022; Gavin et al., 2005; Radoš et al., 2024). While the prevalence of PPD for mothers is approximately 15%–20% (Gavin et al., 2005; Zacher Kjeldsen et al., 2025), it is also common in fathers with an estimated prevalence of 4%–25% (Goodman, 2004; Stadtlander, 2015). Paternal depression is more common in families where the mother also has PPD and often debuts around 3–6 months postpartum, in contrast to maternal PPD, which is most common around the time of birth (Goodman, 2004; Stadtlander, 2015). A meta‐analysis including 48 studies showed a significant association between maternal depressive symptoms and sensitivity within the child's first year of life (Bernard et al., 2018). The authors suggest that this may be due to depressed mothers being less receptive to the infant's cues and that they struggle with regulating their own emotions and reactivity. However, not all studies find an association. For example, in a study on 165 mother–infant dyads, Kaplan et al. (2015) found no significant association between maternal depressive symptoms and sensitivity. Likewise, in a previous study based on the same sample (N = 419) as the current study, no association was found between clinical and subclinical depression and maternal sensitivity (Stuart et al., 2023). When it comes to fathers with PPD, the research is still sparse; one study found that depressed fathers did not differ in how they interact with their child compared to non‐depressed fathers (Field et al., 1999), whereas other studies found that having a father with PPD affects children similarly to having a mother with PPD (Lewis & Lamb, 2003; Parfitt et al., 2013; Ramchandani & Psychogiou, 2009). In summary, despite having a potential negative impact on parenting quality, parental PPD does not seem to inevitably do so (Frankel et al., 2024).

Previous research has found indications that infants of parents with PPD are more likely to exhibit socially withdrawn behaviours, with results showing a link between maternal PPD severity and infant social withdrawal behaviour in interactions with their mother (Dollberg et al., 2006; Field et al., 1990; Puura et al., 2013), and with a stranger (Braarud et al., 2013; Mäntymaa et al., 2008; Matthey et al., 2005; Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2019; Stuart et al., 2022). However, the evidence is inconclusive. For example, Matthey et al. (2005) did not find a direct link between infant social withdrawal at 4–13 months of age and the mothers' concurrent PPD symptom severity. Instead, if the mother retrospectively responded yes to having felt more irritable, anxious, sad or depressed since the birth, possibly reflecting a more long‐term depressive mood, this was linked to their infant's social withdrawal behaviour. Other studies have found maternal PPD to not be a significant predictor of infant social withdrawal (Guedeney et al., 2012; Molteno et al., 2014). There are also notable contradictions in the research regarding at which ages that PPD may have an influence on infant social withdrawal. For example, Braarud et al. (2013) reported that although concurrent maternal depression was associated with full‐term infants' social withdrawal at 6 and 9 months of age, it was not at 3 months of age. In their premature cohort, maternal PPD only explained concurrent infant social withdrawal at 6 months. In contrast, in a large population‐based study, Stuart et al. (2022) found that the mother's PPD scores were associated with infant social withdrawal at infant ages 2–3 months, but not at 4–7 months or 8–12 months. Thus, despite several studies on the link between maternal PPD and infant social withdrawal, findings vary widely in whether PPD relates to infant withdrawal and at which ages, suggesting that additional factors may impact this association. Very few studies have looked at fathers' mental health in relation to infant social withdrawal (Mäntymaa et al., 2008; Stuart et al., 2022). Stuart et al. (2022) found no links between paternal PPD and infant social withdrawal at any age between 2 and 12 months. Mäntymaa et al. (2008) similarly found no significant association between formally assessed postpartum depressive symptoms in fathers and infant social withdrawal behaviours between the ages of 4 and 18 months. However, similarly to the study by Matthey et al. (2005), when the fathers were asked to rate their mental health during their child's first year, the ones who rated it as poor were significantly more likely to have infants who were socially withdrawn.

Taken together, existing research shows a link between sustained infant social withdrawal and parental sensitivity, whereas findings for parental depression, particularly concurrent PPD, are mixed across studies and may depend on factors such as symptom chronicity, timing and variability across families. This heterogeneity may indicate important moderating effects, shaping when and for whom parental depression is linked to infant social withdrawal. It has been suggested that the quality of parent–infant interaction and parental sensitivity may be such a moderator (Campbell et al., 2004; Dollberg et al., 2006; Frankel et al., 2024; Goodman & Gotlib, 1999; Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2019). Thus, in this cross‐sectional study we test whether sensitivity moderates an association between parental depression and sustained infant social withdrawal during the first year of life, in families identified at high risk of maternal PPD. Specifically, as motivated by attachment theory, infants may be most at risk for sustained social withdrawal when parental depression is accompanied by lower levels of sensitivity. If so, this knowledge would help us better understand which families run higher risk of adverse infant socio‐emotional development due to parental depression, which may have implications for targeting of directed support to the families most in need of it. Furthermore, we examined the relationship among these factors separately for infant social withdrawal exhibited during interaction with a stranger, the mother and the father/co‐parent. Co‐parent is here defined as a primary caregiver or parent that does not identify as a father. Clinically, withdrawal that generalizes to strangers may index broader risk, beyond a single caregiver relationship. Further understanding of differentiation of interaction partners may inform both screening (who to assess, in what context) and intervention targets (sensitivity promotion vs. broader regulatory support). Furthermore, while brief, self‐initiated pauses are normative and may support self‐regulation in overstimulating interactions, our measures target elevated or sustained withdrawal signals that exceed such adaptive breaks. Thus, the hypotheses and outcomes below address clinically relevant withdrawal rather than normative micro‐regulation.

The hypotheses tested in this study were: (1) Infant social withdrawal during interaction with strangers, their mothers and their fathers/co‐parents is positively associated. This hypothesis addresses whether infant withdrawal reflects interaction‐partner‐specific behaviour or a broader pattern across interaction contexts; (2) Maternal PPD symptom severity is positively associated with infant social withdrawal during interaction with strangers and with their mothers. This hypothesis targets the mixed evidence on whether concurrent maternal PPD symptoms relate to infant withdrawal; (3) Maternal sensitivity moderates the relationship between maternal PPD symptom severity and infant social withdrawal during interaction with strangers and with their mothers, in a way where higher PPD symptom severity scores combined with lower sensitivity scores are associated with higher social withdrawal scores. Given the inconsistent evidence on PPD–withdrawal associations, this hypothesis tests whether parental sensitivity helps determine when, and for whom, PPD symptoms relate to infant withdrawal; (4) There is an association between paternal/co‐parental PPD, paternal/co‐parental sensitivity and infant social withdrawal during interaction with their fathers/co‐parents. This exploratory hypothesis investigates the limited and inconsistent evidence on fathers/co‐parents, addressing whether associations seen in mother–infant interaction extend to other primary caregivers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants and procedure

This pre‐registered cross‐sectional study (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/RNCQD) draws on data from the Copenhagen Infant Mental Health Program (CIMHP; Væver et al., 2016), conducted 2015–2019. Families were recruited through the public health programme in Copenhagen, where health visitors visit families with newborns during the first year postpartum. Families were eligible for recruitment if the mother scored ≥ 10 on the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS; Cox et al., 1987) at the health visit. This was a study‐specific threshold selected to identify mothers in the upper range of postpartum depressive symptoms and at elevated risk for PPD (see Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2018). Furthermore, mothers had to be ≥ 18 years old and fluent in Danish, and the infant < 1 year old. In a subset of families, the father or co‐parent was present during data collection and was included if they were fluent in Danish or English. Families were excluded if the infant had a severe medical condition, or if the mother presented with psychiatric conditions other than PPD. The final sample comprised 471 mother–infant dyads, and 278 fathers/co‐parents (see Table 1).

Data collection was conducted by a psychologist from the research team who visited the families in their home. During this visit, the psychologist collected the EPDS. ADBB, and CIB scores, which were the measures used in the analyses. Considerations were taken of the infant's needs and mood, with possibilities for breaks when needed. Therefore, the exact order of assessments and the length of the visit could differ between families. No compensation for participation was received by the families.

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the University of Copenhagen ethics committee (Approval: 2015–10). All parents provided written informed consent.

Measures

The Alarm Distress Baby Scale (ADBB)

Sustained infant social withdrawal during interaction with a stranger was measured with the ADBB (Guedeney & Fermanian, 2001). This is an observational instrument comprising eight items assessing atypical infant social behaviours: facial expression, eye contact, activity level, self‐stimulation, vocalizations, responsiveness, ability to engage socially and ability to attract and maintain observer attention. Each item is rated 0–4. A score of 0 reflects typical social engagement, and 4 indicates severe social withdrawal, characterized by absence of social behaviours despite sustained efforts by the observer to initiate social interaction during a 10‐min observation. The total scores range 0–32, with higher scores indicating elevated social withdrawal. The project psychologist could use the entire home visit to inform scoring. Reliability scoring was based on a 10‐min video‐recorded interaction, and inter‐rater reliability was assessed on 20% of the CIMHP sample by comparing psychologist scores with those of an independent coder. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = .79) indicated good reliability.

The coding interactive behaviour (CIB)

Infant social withdrawal during parent interaction and parental sensitivity were assessed during the home visit using the CIB (Feldman, 1998). CIB is a global rating scale of parent–child interaction assessed with video recordings of 5 min mother–infant and 3 min father/co‐parent–infant free‐play sessions, which has previously been found to be sufficient time for coding interaction quality (Beebe et al., 2008). Parents were instructed to play with their infants as they usually do, which could include toys. The CIB includes 33 items rated 1–5, in increments of 0.5; higher scores indicate greater frequency and quality of the behaviour. For this study, we focused on two items: Acknowledging (parental sensitivity) and Withdrawal (infant social withdrawal). Acknowledging is scored based on the levels of the parent's acknowledgements and responses to the child's signals through vocalization, gaze, facial expression or body movement. Withdrawal is scored based on how much the infant appears detached from the social interaction by not initiating or responding to bids of interaction, appearing closed off and exhibiting low levels of affect. Six independent coders, blind to maternal diagnostic status and unfamiliar with the families, coded the full CIMHP sample. Inter‐rater reliability was excellent: intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) of maternal Acknowledging = .94, mother–infant Withdrawal = .92, paternal/co‐parental Acknowledging = .92 and father/co‐parent–infant Withdrawal = .93; percentage agreement of maternal Acknowledging = 93.8%, mother–infant Withdrawal = 94.6%, paternal/co‐parental Acknowledging = 89.2% and father/co‐parent–infant Withdrawal = 91.9%. When data from both parents was included in the study, they were rated by independent coders.

The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)

Maternal and paternal/co‐parental PPD symptom severity was measured with the EPDS (Cox et al., 1987; Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2018). The scale includes 10 items rating mood and feelings of the past 7 days on a 4‐point Likert scale (0‐3). Total scores range 0–30 with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Chronbach's alpha for mothers and fathers (.882 and .840) indicated high reliability.

Control variables

Potential covariates included parents' age, education (classified according to the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) and grouped into three levels; 1–3, 4–7 and 8–9, corresponding to 9–12, 14–16 and > 17 years of schooling), employment (dichotomous: ‘yes’ if employed or enrolled in full‐time studies), cohabitation (dichotomous: ‘yes’ if parents lived together), infant sex and age, and prematurity (dichotomous: gestational age < 37 weeks). These measures were obtained through surveys.

Analysis

The hypotheses and analysis plan were pre‐registered in the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/RNCQD). The registration occurred after data collection but before any analyses reported here. Analyses were conducted in R (v4.5.0, RStudio v25.05.0) using custom scripts and in Jamovi (v2.6.26). Significance tests were two‐tailed with α = .05. Normality was assessed via skewness, kurtosis and visual inspection (QQ‐plots, histograms). To address non‐normality, bias‐corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrapped confidence intervals (1000 samples) was applied throughout. Families were excluded from the current study if data were missing for the mothers' EPDS (0%), ADBB (157 families, 24%) and CIB scores (167 families, 26%). The most common causes for missing data were timing constraints during the home visit (e.g., insufficient time to complete all assessments) and technical issues with the video recordings. Missingness was evaluated using Little's test in combination with analyses of associations between missingness indicators and observed variables. These analyses suggested the data were consistent with a missing at random assumption. Given the adequate final sample size for the planned analyses, complete‐case analyses were conducted in accordance with the analysis plan.

Hypothesis 1 was tested using Pearson's correlations between ADBB and CIB Withdrawal scores with the mother and the father/co‐parent. Hypotheses 2 and 3 were examined with hierarchical linear regression models. The dependent variables were the ADBB score for infant social withdrawal during interactions with a stranger and the CIB Withdrawal score for interactions with the mother. Predictors were added stepwise: (1) covariates; (2) EPDS; (3) maternal CIB Acknowledging; (4) EPDS * maternal CIB Acknowledging interaction term. Hypothesis 2 was tested at the second step in both models. Hypothesis 4 was exploratory and first evaluated by correlating paternal/co‐parental CIB Withdrawal, CIB Acknowledging and EPDS. Significant associations led to a hierarchical regression analysis structured as for hypotheses 2 and 3. Interaction variables were mean centred. Associations between potential control variables and the dependent variables were assessed prior to inclusion in the regression models (Pearson's correlation or one‐way ANOVA). Infant age correlated with ADBB (r(465) = 0.12, p = .010, 95%BCa CI [.03, .21]), maternal CIB Withdrawal (r(465) = −0.19, p < .001, 95%BCa CI [−.24, −.13]) and paternal/co‐parental CIB Withdrawal (r(273) = −0.20, p = .001, 95%BCa CI [−.26, −.14]). Maternal age correlated with ADBB (r(465) = 0.11, p = .021, 95%BCa CI [.01, .23]). No other correlations were significant. Thus, maternal and infant age were included as covariates in the respective analyses, and maternal EPDS was added to father/co‐parent models to account for possible sampling bias.

To aid interpretation, exploratory Bayesian correlations (not pre‐registered) were conducted in Jamovi using default settings and uniform priors. The Bayes factor (BF10) quantify the relative predictive performance of the alternative hypothesis against the null hypothesis, providing insight into non‐significant results.

A small but significant negative partial correlation emerged between the father's/co‐parent's sensitivity (CIB Acknowledging score) and CIB Withdrawal scores (r(273) = −.16, p = .007, 95%BCa CI [−.298, −.031]). This result remained significant even after correcting for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni corrections (p = .021). However, in the subsequent hierarchical regression analysis, sensitivity was no longer significant, likely due to reduced sample size (n = 197) from missing EPDS data for fathers/co‐parents (n = 197). The father's/co‐parent's EPDS score and its interaction with sensitivity was also non‐significant (Table S2).

To further interpret the non‐significant results, we conducted Bayesian correlation analyses between the three outcome variables (withdrawal with the mother, father/co‐parent and stranger) and the main predictors (maternal and paternal/co‐parental PPD symptom severity and sensitivity). The only correlation with robust evidence in favour of the alternative hypothesis was between the mothers' sensitivity scores and infant withdrawal (r = −0.34, BF10 > 1011, indicating extreme evidence). For paternal/co‐parental sensitivity and infant social withdrawal, evidence was weak (r = −0.16, BF10 = 2.62), indicating that the data provide only anecdotal support and do not robustly favour either hypothesis. All other correlations had a BF10 < ±1, indicating no evidence for or against the alternative hypothesis. The results thus aligned with the frequentist analyses (Table S3).

DISCUSSION

The main aim of this study was to investigate whether parental sensitivity moderates an association between PPD severity and sustained infant social withdrawal in interactions with the mother, father/co‐parent and a stranger. However, we found no significant associations between parental PPD symptom severity and infant social withdrawal with either interaction partner, and these null results were supported by Bayesian analyses. Instead, maternal sensitivity, observed during mother–infant interaction, was negatively associated with infant social withdrawal in the same context. Thus, the infants were more likely to display social withdrawal behaviours with mothers who showed lower levels of sensitivity towards them. Tentatively, this may have been the case also for paternal/co‐parental sensitivity during father/co‐parent–infant interactions, but for infant interactions with a stranger, we found no associations with maternal sensitivity. For neither interaction partner was there any indication of an interaction that would suggest a moderating effect.

Given the substantial body of research linking maternal depression to infant social withdrawal (Braarud et al., 2013; Dollberg et al., 2006; Field et al., 1999; Mäntymaa et al., 2008; Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2019; Stuart et al., 2022; Væver et al., 2020), our results are somewhat surprising. Importantly though, not all previous studies found significant associations between PPD symptom severity and infant social withdrawal, particularly in early infancy (Braarud et al., 2013; Guedeney et al., 2012; Mäntymaa et al., 2008; Matthey et al., 2005; Molteno et al., 2014). Furthermore, for both mothers (Matthey et al., 2005) and fathers (Mäntymaa et al., 2008), there has been indications that retrospectively asking parents about their mental health during the first year postpartum may be more sensitive for identifying cases where parental mental health affects infant social withdrawal than concurrent testing of parental PPD. Although both preliminary and difficult to interpret, this may reflect that in some cases, parental mental health concerns need to persist over time to influence infant social withdrawal. In the current study, infants were on average 3.6 months old (with 89% being below 6 months) when infant social withdrawal and parental PPD was assessed, and infant age emerged as a significant covariate for infant social withdrawal with all three interaction partners. Taken together, this may suggest that parental depression is less impactful on infant social withdrawal over shorter time periods, such as the first months of the infant's life. This would be in line with the conclusions of Goodman and Gotlib (1999), who found that the timing and chronicity of maternal depression moderated the severity and course of infant adverse development. However, other studies have found a significant association between concurrent EPDS scores and infant social withdrawal already when infants are 2–4 months, which would go against this interpretation (Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2019; Stuart et al., 2022). In addition, because previous studies has found differing results based on how parental depression was measured (concurrently with EPDS vs. retroactively; (Mäntymaa et al., 2008; Matthey et al., 2005)), it is also possible that we may have gotten different results with other measurements. In any case, our results point to a complexity in the relationship between parental depression and infant socio‐emotional well‐being. Because postpartum depression is a heterogeneous condition that often coincide with other difficulties (Apter et al., 2012; Lovejoy et al., 2000; Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2016), it is likely that other factors, like aspects of the infants themselves, the parents, the caregiving environment, the social support system, etc., have at least as large of an impact on infant socio‐emotional development as parental depression (Mäntymaa et al., 2008; Molteno et al., 2014; Smith‐Nielsen et al., 2016).

Our results highlight the importance of parental sensitivity in parent–child interaction and infant socio‐emotional behaviours, at least for the mother. This is in line with the findings of both Dollberg et al. (2006) and Puura et al. (2013). For the father/co‐parent, partial correlations suggested a similar association, even after correcting for multiple comparisons. However, this was not confirmed by the regression analysis, possibly due to reduced statistical power and the Bayesian analysis suggested anecdotal levels of evidence in favour of such an association. These results are contrary to the recent findings of Tissot et al. (2025), where maternal sensitivity was less conclusively linked to infant social withdrawal than paternal sensitivity. Despite being difficult to interpret, both findings point to maternal and paternal/co‐parental sensitivity possibly having differential effects on infant social withdrawal behaviours. Related to the potential differences between mothers and fathers/co‐parents, we found that infant social withdrawal during interactions with a stranger and with the father/co‐parent were significantly correlated, albeit with a small effect size and not robust for correction for multiple comparisons, whereas withdrawal with the mother was not associated with either. Possibly, this reflects the separate roles that mothers and fathers/co‐parents typically play in the first months of life, with mothers generally being the primary caregiver in this sample. Consistent with attachment theory, infants may engage more deeply in regulatory and attachment‐related behaviours during mother–infant interactions, making withdrawal more detectable and more closely tied to maternal sensitivity. This result thus underscores the need to better understand the dynamics of parental sensitivity and infant social withdrawal in relation to all primary caregivers.

To our knowledge, no previous studies have examined associations between parental sensitivity and infant social withdrawal with a stranger. In contrast to interaction with the parents, we found no association between parental sensitivity and infant social withdrawal during interactions with a stranger in either the pre‐registered frequentist or the Bayesian analyses. This could suggest that infant withdrawal as a response to how their parents interact with them is specific to that parent–infant relationship. If so, the influence of parental sensitivity on infant social withdrawal would not necessarily extend to interactions with individuals outside of the caregiving context. Alternatively, because the parents in the current study exhibited relatively high levels of sensitivity, another interpretation is that caregiving quality needs to be more negatively impacted before it leads to infant social withdrawal behaviours being generalized also to strangers. Although not tested here, it is important to recognize the potential bidirectional influence between infant social withdrawal and parental sensitivity, as less social responsivity in the infant could lead to more intrusive and less sensitive behaviours in the parents (Frankel et al., 2024; Neece et al., 2012). Thus, alike to the link to postpartum depression, the association between parental sensitivity and infant social withdrawal is complex and there is a need to further understand the mechanisms involved to adequately give recommendations to health professionals and parents.

The findings of this study have some practical implications. Firstly, PPD symptom severity was not significantly associated with either maternal or paternal/co‐parental sensitivity, thus demonstrating that low parental sensitivity is not an inevitable consequence of PPD. Based on this, health care professionals who meet parents with PPD or at risk for PPD should provide a nuanced picture of the consequences of PPD on the infants. As parents who experience PPD may worry about how their mental health is affecting their parenting ability and in turn their child's development, this may provide some comfort. Secondly, the finding that infant social withdrawal may differ between interactions with the mothers, co‐parents and strangers early in development highlights the importance of considering that infants may not show social withdrawal across all relationships just because they do it in one. This may be important knowledge for health care professionals assessing infant social withdrawal as a general sign of distress. Thirdly, given the association between infant age and social withdrawal across all interaction partners, it may be important to assess social withdrawal at several time points during infancy, rather than relying solely on evaluations at one time point (e.g., 2 months of age). Otherwise, we may risk missing instances of social withdrawal that has not yet become a generalized pattern of interaction.

The conclusions drawn from this study should be considered in light of important limitations. Firstly, the population of Copenhagen is generally well educated, with high socio‐economic status, which is also reflected in our sample. Danish parents are typically well‐informed about parenting and the importance of being sensitive to their child's needs. These factors may potentially limit the generalizability of our results. Secondly, when postpartum mental health concerns or child welfare issues are identified, support is available through both the healthcare system and social services to help mitigate potential negative effects. As our sample was recruited based on such warning signs of maternal PPD, health care support could have mitigated EPDS associations and this should be systematically assessed in future work. However, the data underlying the present analyses were obtained at the baseline assessment, which was conducted shortly after the first positive PPD screening by health visitors. Based on our experience during data collection, mothers had not yet been referred to or initiated treatment at the time of our home visits. Thirdly, sensitivity was assessed in a low‐stress, free‐play interaction for 5 minutes with mothers and 3 minutes with fathers/co‐parents. This may not fully capture how depressed parents interact with their infants under more stressful conditions. The differences in interaction length between mothers and fathers/co‐parents may also have contributed to weaker effects in the latter group (although the shorter time period has been found sufficient for coding interaction quality; Beebe et al., 2008). In addition, both sensitivity and social withdrawal in interaction with the parents was rated from the same interaction situation, which may have introduced shared‐method bias. Although the associations between parental sensitivity and infant withdrawal had differing strength between mothers and fathers/co‐parents, thus suggesting that this explanation alone is unlikely to fully account for the findings, these are important considerations. Relatedly, infant social withdrawal was assessed differently in interaction with the parents (CIB) and the stranger (ADBB) and therefore capture diverse aspects of the withdrawal behaviours. This may have exaggerated differences in interaction patterns within these relational contexts. Furthermore, the macro‐level interaction coding of this study may not capture the dynamic nature of fine‐grained contingencies between infant and parent behaviour. Future studies should aim to replicate the findings using assessments from different interaction contexts, with different measures and on different coding timescales to better disentangle these effects. Lastly, the broad age range of infants included in the current study may limit our conclusions. Although age was included as a covariate in the analyses, it is difficult to draw conclusions about how age may have influenced our results, especially in relation to the timing and chronicity of parental depression. We therefore propose longitudinal studies to investigate how parental depression and sensitivity is related to infant social withdrawal longitudinally.

CONCLUSION

Contrary to expectations, PPD symptoms severity was not significantly related to infant social withdrawal in interaction with either parents or strangers. Instead, parental sensitivity emerged as a meaningful predictor, particularly in mother–infant interaction, as infants displayed more withdrawal behaviours when their mothers were less sensitive during interaction. Evidence for a similar association with paternal/co‐parental sensitivity was weaker, as even though the correlation was significant, regression analyses did not support sensitivity as a predictor, and the Bayesian analysis indicated only anecdotal evidence. No link was found between maternal sensitivity and infant social withdrawal when interacting with a stranger. The diverging findings across mothers, fathers/co‐parents and strangers underscore the importance of considering caregiver‐specific interaction contexts when interpreting infant social withdrawal.

The findings of this study suggest that the quality of parent–infant interaction may play a more influential role in shaping early socio‐emotional development than parental depression, particularly in well‐resourced families. They highlight the need to broaden the focus beyond parental depression to capture the complex interplay of factors affecting infant development and well‐being. Accordingly, interventions for families at risk of adverse child outcomes may benefit from emphasizing the promotion of parental sensitivity alongside efforts to address parental mental health. Future research should examine these dynamics in more diverse populations and across interaction contexts with varying stress levels, as well as over extended developmental periods. Doing so could help clarify how parental sensitivity and depression jointly contribute to infant social behaviour and development.
