Meta-integration of qualitative research on the clinical work experiences of emergency department nurses
Authors: Liwen Huang, Mengjiao Li, Yuping Ning, Yan Zhou, Yinghua Cao, Ping Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA growing body of evidence indicates that emergency department nurses are frequently exposed to high-intensity work-related stress, complex emotional demands, and occupational hazards in their clinical practice. These factors significantly influence their job satisfaction and retention intention. This meta-integrated qualitative study aimed to systematically examine the clinical work experiences of emergency department nurses, with a focus on identifying stressors, coping mechanisms, and emotional dynamics, in order to provide an evidence-based foundation for optimizing the emergency nursing work environment.MethodsA qualitative systematic review was conducted. Electronic searches were performed across the databases CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Scopus, OVID, and SinoMed (CBM), covering all records from inception to 10 February 2025. Eligible studies focusing on the clinical work experiences of emergency department nurses were selected. Study quality was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. Data were synthesized using a meta-integration approach. The review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.ResultsA total of 18 studies were included, yielding 40 individual findings that were classified into 8 thematic categories. These were further synthesized into three overarching integrative themes: (1) the Organizational–Clinical Stress Ecosystem, (2) Resilience-Building Mechanisms, and (3) the Duality of Professional Identity.ConclusionHospital administrators should prioritize the physical and psychological well-being of emergency department nurses. It is essential to assist nurses in adjusting to their work conditions by reducing night shift frequency, enhancing staffing ratios, and reinforcing diverse forms of support. Furthermore, providing more opportunities for skill development and professional training that integrates theoretical knowledge with clinical practice is recommended. Practical and evidence-informed management policies should be implemented to improve the sustainability of emergency nursing workforce retention.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04491-5.Keywords: Emergency nursing, Nurses, Clinical experience, Qualitative research, Meta-integration

Introduction

Emergency department nurses are required to demonstrate a high level of clinical competence, comprehensive medical knowledge, advanced nursing skills, resilience under pressure, a strong sense of responsibility, and focused attention [1]. The emergency department serves not only as the frontline for epidemic control and in-hospital inclusion and exclusion cremergency care, but also as a pivotal node within the diagnostic and treatment system for critically ill patients. Emergency nurses constitute a core component of the emergency medical team and play a vital role in delivering time-sensitive interventions. In contrast to nurses working in general wards, emergency department nurses are exposed to a distinctive “three-highs” working environment: high-intensity clinical decision-making demands (managing 2–3 diagnostic or therapeutic decisions per minute), high-frequency emotional labour (averaging 1.2 episodes of emotional escalation from patients or family members per hour), and high-density interdisciplinary collaboration (interfacing with approximately seven distinct professional teams per shift). This unique pattern of clinical exposure renders emergency nursing one of the most psychologically demanding and stress-prone specialties in the healthcare system. In China, the professional experience of emergency nurses is marked by a “paradoxical complexity” -a simultaneous coexistence of high professional demands and structural inefficiencies. Research evidence indicates that the average daily patient volume managed by emergency nurses in tertiary hospitals exceeds the internationally recommended standard by approximately 2.3 times [2], and the duration of a typical night shift often surpasses 12 h to recent survey data [3, 4], 84.15% of emergency department nurses in China reported experiencing verbal violence within the past three months. Among these, 80.49% encountered verbal abuse, with a significant proportion escalating to physical threats or assault. This persistent physical and psychological exhaustion has contributed to a burnout prevalence exceeding 40% among emergency nurses nationwide [5, 6]. In contrast, emergency nurses in the United States have increased their direct patient care time to 62% of their working hours by adopting the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) five-level triage system and a standardized nurse-to-patient ratio of 1:3 [7]. Similarly, in Switzerland, the implementation of the “buffer nurse” system—dedicated to managing physician–patient communication—has reduced the incidence of workplace violence to below 8% [8]. Conversely, emergency department nurses in China report poorer work experiences, elevated stress levels, and higher turnover rates. The adverse effects of suboptimal work conditions appear to exhibit a multi-level cascading impact across individual, team, and organizational dimensions. At the individual level, 27% of emergency nurses in China exhibit symptoms of depression, and 41.5% report insomnia—both rates significantly exceeding those observed in the general population [9]. At the organizational level, a high turnover intention of 70.8% has led to escalating human resource training costs [10]. Most critically, at the patient safety level, nurses’ emotional exhaustion is significantly and positively associated with increased rates of medication administration errors. Emotional exhaustion directly contributes to operational mistakes by impairing attention and diminishing work engagement [14]. This triadic impact—spanning nurse, organization, and patient—warrants systematic deconstruction and targeted intervention.

Currently, existing qualitative studies on emergency nursing exhibit notable theoretical and methodological limitations. For example, several studies relied primarily on single-site samples from tertiary hospitals and predominantly used semi-structured interviews without methodological triangulation or explicit reporting of theoretical saturation [11–13]. First, most samples are predominantly drawn from tertiary hospitals, resulting in significant data gaps and delays in the development of culturally adapted assessment tools for primary care settings. Second, methodological rigor is often lacking: the majority of studies rely solely on semi-structured interviews, without employing multi-method triangulation techniques such as direct observation. Additionally, theoretical saturation is frequently underreported or incompletely documented, which undermines the credibility and transferability of the findings. To address these gaps, the present study adopted Sandelowski and Barroso’s qualitative meta-integration approach to systematically analyze the lived clinical experiences of emergency department nurses [14]. This study aimed to systematically synthesize qualitative evidence to identify stress-generating factors influencing emergency department nurses’clinical work experiences, as well as the coping mechanisms and psychological responses associated with these stressors.

Data and methods

Research design

This study employed a meta-synthesis approach to systematically review qualitative research. Grounded in a comprehensive understanding of the philosophical foundations and methodological principles of meta-synthesis, we conducted iterative readings of the included studies to extract thematic elements and latent meanings. Themes with overlapping or similar connotations were subsequently synthesized, categorized, and integrated into newly constructed conceptual findings. This synthesis enabled the generation of more profound and contextually grounded explanations of specific phenomena, thereby enhancing the explanatory power and persuasiveness of the final conclusions [15]. Specifically, this study adopted Sandelowski and Barroso’s qualitative meta-integration framework, which involves three sequential stages: (1) extraction and classification of interpretive findings from primary qualitative studies, (2) integration of recurring concepts into thematic categories through constant comparison, and (3) synthesis of these categories into higher-order analytical themes that capture the essence of the phenomenon across contexts. This approach emphasizes interpretive depth and conceptual integration rather than simple aggregation of findings, thereby enhancing the theoretical coherence of the synthesis. The review process followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was reported in accordance with the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) statement [16, 17]. The study protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO.

Data source

Computerized searches were conducted across the following databases: Knowledge.com, Wanfang, Wikipedia, PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL (via EBSCO), Cochrane Library, SinoMed (CBM), and OVID. The search aimed to identify qualitative studies on the clinical work experiences of nurses in emergency departments. The retrieval period covered all available records from the inception of each database up to February 10, 2025. A comprehensive search strategy combining Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms was employed. The key search terms included: “emergency medicine,” “emergency medical technicians,” “nurses,” “nursing staff”,“cognition*,” “perception*,” “attitude*,” “feeling*,” “experience*,” “view*,” “thought*”, “qualitative research,” “grounded theory,” “phenomenolog*,” “ethnograph*,” “hermeneutic*,” “thematic analysis,” “content analysis,” “qualitative studies,” “historical study”,“work*,” “career*”.Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary File S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria Studies were included if they met the following criteria:①Population: emergency department nurses, including emergency intensive care unit (EICU) nurses.②Phenomenon of interest: The lived clinical experiences and psychological perceptions of Emergency Intensive Care Nurse or EICU or emergency nurses. ③Setting: Work-related locations such as nurses’ offices, lounges, duty rooms, and demonstration areas. ④Study design: Qualitative research designs, including descriptive studies, phenomenological research, ethnographic studies, and grounded theory approaches.

Exclusion Criteria Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria:①Non-Chinese and non-English language publications. ②Study population did not involve emergency or Emergency Intensive Care Nurse. ③Duplicate publications or studies with incomplete data. ④ Conference proceedings, abstracts, or review articles.

Literature selection and data extraction

The retrieved studies were independently screened by two researchers. Data extraction and verification were conducted collaboratively, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third researcher. The extracted information included the follow in elements: author(s), year of publication, country of origin, research methodology, study population, phenomenon of interest, and main findings.

Quality assessment of included studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently evaluated by two researchers using the 2016 version of the Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Centre for Evidence-Based Health Care in Australia [18]. The checklist comprises ten items, each assessed using one of the following responses: “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable.” Based on the appraisal results, the quality of each study was categorized into three levels: Level A: All criteria were met, indicating a low risk of bias. Level B: Most criteria were met, indicating a moderate risk of bias. Level C: Few or no criteria were met, indicating a high risk of bias. In cases of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion between the two reviewers, with a third researcher consulted for adjudication when necessary. Following the appraisal, all 18 included studies were rated as Level A or B in quality. The detailed results of the quality assessment are presented in Table 1.

Synthesis and analysis of data

Guided by a comprehensive understanding of the philosophical foundations underlying various qualitative research methodologies, the extracted findings included thematic patterns, latent meanings, and categorized constructs. Following multiple iterative readings and comparative analysis of the included studies, conceptually similar findings were grouped into newly defined categories. These categories were then synthesized into higher-order analytical themes, which are presented as the final synthesized findings of this review.

Results

Literature selection steps and results

In this study, 2085 relevant articles were initially identified. Following a rigorous screening process, 18 qualitative studies were ultimately included in the final analysis [13, 19–35]. Four of these studies did not specify whether approval from an ethics committee had been obtained [21, 23, 27, 31]. The included literature originated from the following countries: China (n = 11) [13, 19–25, 27, 30, 31], Australia (n = 2) [26, 34], England (n = 1) [29], Thailand (n = 1) [28], Finland (n = 1) [33], Canada (n = 1) [35],and Saudi Arabia (n = 1) [32] research methodologies employed included phenomenological approaches (n = 10), descriptive qualitative studies (n = 6), and studies with unspecified qualitative designs (n = 2). Most of the included studies were published within the past 10 years and were original peer-reviewed articles (in Table 2). Through iterative reading and thematic comparison, the researchers extracted 40 primary findings, which were clustered into 8 thematic categories. These categories were further synthesized into 3 overarching analytical findings. The literature screening process and results are presented in Fig. 1.

Meta-integration results

Through repeated reading and in-depth analysis of the included studies, a total of 40 distinct primary findings were extracted. These findings were subsequently organized into 8 newly constructed sub-themes, which were further synthesized into 3 overarching integrative themes.

Integrated theme 1: Organizational–clinical stress ecosystem

This theme highlights the multi-layered sources of occupational stress encountered by emergency department nurses, resembling an ecosystemic distribution across three key dimensions: environmental threats, emotional burden, and structural resource shortages. The stressors identified span physical workplace conditions, high-acuity patient demands, and persistent understaffing. This ecosystem model of stress is supported across multiple studies, demonstrating cross-cultural and cross-situational consistency.

Sub-theme: Clinical operational stress

Clinical operational stress refers to the anxiety and fear experienced by emergency department nurses during clinical procedures, primarily due to insufficient professional competence and a highly complex work environment. For newly employed or early-career nurses, limited clinical experience and underdeveloped procedural skills often trigger persistent anxiety, which has been identified as a contributing factor to the high turnover rate among junior nursing staff. In parallel, the complexity of emergency care, combined with inadequate staffing and insufficient hands-on training, undermines nurses’ confidence in performing critical interventions. Many nurses report fear of making operational errors during emergency procedures, particularly those that may result in patient harm or death, further intensifying emotional strain and decreasing job retention.

Emergency department nurses were found to lack the professional skills required to perform emergency procedures, resulting in increased anxiety and unease. All the training, the lecture is textbook knowledge, no clinical cases. Insufficient clinical experience and less training, even the ventilator and defibrillator were not used [19] .

Critically ill patients presenting in night shift emergency and unfamiliar with knowledge when treating them [19] .

There is a lot that I don’t know about child rearing, and I even question my professionalism a little bit [21] .

There is no support from the organization to improve the skills or potential of the staff. And the hospital does not provide any support in terms of not improving clinical professionalism and very worried about their competence [28] .

Sub-theme: Emotional and moral burden

Emotional and moral burden refers to the emotional exhaustion experienced by emergency department nurses as a result of sustained empathic engagement and frequent exposure to ethical dilemmas. One major source of this burden is the strained nurse–patient relationship, wherein patients and their families often display mistrust toward healthcare professionals. Difficulties in communication and low levels of cooperation exacerbate emotional labor demands, rendering nurses vulnerable to psychological fatigue. Additionally, during end-of-life scenarios or the deterioration of critically ill patients, nurses often internalize the emotional suffering of patients and their families, resulting in deep empathic distress that can be difficult to regulate in the short term. Compounding these challenges are institutional issues such as irrational shift scheduling, inequitable resource allocation, and significant discrepancies in compensation among staff. These systemic stressors contribute to a diminished sense of social identity and professional value among nurses.

Families lack medical-related knowledge but are very legally aware and distrust healthcare professionals, while influencing other family members to seek counseling. They think we are “babysitters” and look down on nurses [19].

It makes me very sad when we are not able to keep the statement after trying to resuscitate the patient [22].

Children who cannot be resuscitated remind me of my own children, and I still can’t shake that sadness after work [27].

Nurses work hard and are underpaid compared to other medical professionals [19].

Emergency work is more intense and labor-intensive, and it can be mentally overwhelming to be tense for a long period of time. We definitely have to call the emergency ladder when we are particularly busy [20].

You can’t imagine the intensity of the work and emergencies that we are exposed to on a daily basis here. It requires communication, resolution and coordination. There will be a lot of controversy and we are under a lot of pressure [30].

Our scheduling is erratic and there is often overtime [34].

Sub-theme: Organizational stressors

In addition to individual-level operational challenges, emergency nurses face broader organizational stressors arising from institutional management and workplace systems. Organizational stressors refer to systemic pressures arising from deficiencies in institutional management and workplace policies. One of the most commonly reported issues is the prevalence of prolonged and irregular high-intensity night shifts, which hinder nurses’ability to achieve adequate rest and recovery. Over time, these patterns contribute to physical deterioration and a variety of adverse physiological responses. In addition, structural limitations—such as restricted career advancement pathways, lack of access to continuing education opportunities, and inadequate institutional support for professional development—have led to widespread dissatisfaction among emergency department nurses. Compounding these frustrations is a perceived imbalance between workload and compensation, which has contributed to demotivation, emotional disengagement, and a reduced sense of organizational commitment.

APNs are scheduled to work night shifts too frequently leading to fatigue accumulation [20].

Most of the emergency care is practiced in two shifts and double shifts, which is extremely intense and requires a high level of vigilance to prevent emergencies and to be on call [24].

Integrative theme 2: Resilience construction mechanisms

Emergency department nurses have developed a variety of resilience-building mechanisms to preserve their psychological well-being and sustain clinical performance amidst high-intensity work environments. These mechanisms encompass team-based support systems, cognitive restructuring techniques, and work–life balance practices. While the core elements of resilience appear consistent across different cultural contexts, the specific strategies and their implementation demonstrate notable contextual variations.

Sub-theme: Team support networks

The concept of the “emergency family” is a cross-culturally consistent phenomenon in emergency nursing, wherein nurses rely on team-based support networks to mitigate occupational stress. These networks manifest in diverse forms depending on the cultural context. First, nurses benefit from emotional and technical guidance through senior–junior mentorship and mutual assistance among peers. Second, collaborative teamwork with physicians—especially during rapid and successful interventions—provides a sense of shared accomplishment and professional validation. Third, external support from family members, supervisors, and close friends fosters internal motivation and emotional resilience. These multidimensional support mechanisms not only enhance interprofessional collaboration but also promote work reciprocity. According to participant reports, such team dynamics may improve the clinical efficiency of less experienced nurses by up to 30%, while simultaneously reducing perceived work pressure and emotional exhaustion.

Emergency nurses consistently emphasized the emotional and professional support derived from their team environments. One nurse shared, “During resuscitation, the supervisor stood behind me to guide my actions. I felt I was not alone“ [31]. Mentorship from senior staff not only built clinical competence but also instilled psychological safety, as another participant reflected, “Now, he even guides us through resuscitations, which gives us the confidence and backbone to work without fear” [19].

Peer support extended beyond technical guidance. One respondent noted, “When the workload is too intense, they are always willing to help me” [23]. Emotional reassurance from colleagues and superiors was also critical: “Even though I’m not as capable as I used to be, my colleagues and supervisors are very accommodating” [21].

Effective collaboration with physicians further enhanced job satisfaction and efficiency. As one participant explained, “My colleague and I work seamlessly—he records, and I resuscitate the patient. It makes us more effective” [26].

Team bonding rituals, such as humor and shared coping language, also played a role in resilience: “We call difficult families ‘VIP patients.’ Once the case is handled, we all smile—and the stress is gone” [32].

Sub-theme: Cognitive reframing strategies

Cognitive reframing strategies refer to the psychological processes by which emergency department nurses reinterpret and manage occupational stress through mental adjustment. These strategies serve as key mechanisms for building inner resilience and maintaining professional function in high-pressure environments. One dimension of reframing involves deriving a sense of professional pride and self-worth from clinical successes, particularly in life-saving interventions such as resuscitation. Accumulated experience and technical mastery foster feelings of accomplishment and reinforce nurses’ commitment to their roles. Another critical aspect is continuous self-directed learning. Nurses emphasized the importance of regularly updating their clinical knowledge and cognitive frameworks through education and professional development, which enhances their confidence and operational efficiency. Additionally, emotional regulation techniques—such as “occupational emotional detachment”—were highlighted as tools for establishing psychological boundaries. These techniques help mitigate the emotional toll of patient suffering and reduce the risk of emotional contagion, allowing nurses to remain functional and focused in emotionally charged scenarios.

Emergency nurses frequently cited moments of clinical success as catalysts for transforming emotional strain into professional confidence. One participant shared, “After the successful resuscitation, I was sure that I could cope with any emergencies” [31], while another stated, “I felt immensely proud of that moment” after saving a critically ill child [19]. Similarly, completing medical records independently was described as a milestone in professional maturity: “I felt that I had grown up and accomplished something meaningful“ [13].

Learning also played a key role in cognitive restructuring. As one nurse observed, “Emergency medicine lets me see and learn more. otherwise I’d miss the chance to grow professionally” [25]. Continuous education was seen as empowering, with one respondent asserting, “If I have the opportunity, I’ll continue my undergraduate degree—it’s okay to work harder” [22].

To maintain psychological stability, many nurses reported intentional strategies to separate work from life. One stated, “In my off hours, I try not to think about the patients. it refreshes me” [28]. Another noted, “You have to learn to separate life from work to relax“ [30]. Emotional rewards were also cited as balancing factors: “Watching patients get better. makes me deeply happy inside” [31].

Though some acknowledged maladaptive responses such as smoking or overeating, positive strategies like “prayer, rest, family time, and exercise” were most commonly adopted [32].

Sub-theme: Work–life balance practices

Work–life balance practices refer to strategies that help maintain a dynamic equilibrium between professional responsibilities and personal life. Cross-cultural differences play a significant role in shaping how nurses navigate this balance. In Eastern cultures, particularly in East Asia, work–life balance is often anchored in family-centered expectations. While family support is a critical resource, it may also lead to internal conflict and emotional strain. Nurses frequently report feelings of guilt stemming from their inability to care adequately for children or fulfill family obligations due to demanding clinical schedules. In contrast, Western cultures—such as those in Europe and North America—tend to emphasize individualized regulation and the preservation of personal boundaries. Nurses in these contexts often benefit from institutional policies that support flexible scheduling, access to psychological counseling, and legal protections for work-hour limits. These mechanisms help mitigate burnout and support sustained professional engagement.

Work–life balance practices among emergency nurses reflect not only individual preference but also broader cultural orientations. In East Asian contexts, family-based support structures are both a source of strength and strain. One nurse shared, “Both of my days off are spent with my children. my parents support me by caring for them when I’m at work“ [20]. However, role conflict and guilt are common: “I often feel guilty toward my child because I’m so frequently absent or inattentive” [21], and “Being suddenly called back to work causes misunderstandings. my husband ends up doing all the housework“ [23].

In contrast, nurses in Western cultural settings emphasized individualized coping and emotional boundary-setting. One participant stated, “After work, I just rest. my family understands me“ [28], while another described a ritual of decompression through music and social drinking [28]. Others engaged in structured self-care: “Prayer, exercise, therapy—these are the most common coping mechanisms” [32].

Some even emphasized the symbolic defense of personal space: “My doorbell is muted after hours—it’s my sacred right to rest” [34]. Emotional detachment was also cited as a strategy: “We protect ourselves emotionally through professional detachment to avoid internalizing patients’ suffering” [33].

Integrated theme 3: Professional duality

The professional experience of emergency department nurses is defined by a distinct duality. On one hand, the immediate sense of accomplishment following successful resuscitations reinforces a strong perception of professional value and purpose. On the other hand, sustained exposure to high-stakes, high-pressure environments contributes to chronic physical fatigue and emotional exhaustion. This paradoxical tension—between short-term gratification and long-term depletion—emerged consistently across cultural contexts and study settings. Nurses described a cycle of acute professional fulfillment followed by gradual erosion of psychological well-being. The persistence of this duality underscores the urgent need for systemic interventions that can support both performance sustainability and workforce retention.

Sub-theme: Immediate success and chronic exhaustion

This sub-theme describes the coexistence of peak clinical success and long-term occupational fatigue—referred to in the literature as the “adrenaline paradox” [36]. Emergency department nurses routinely navigate a cyclical “peak–trough” dynamic, in which moments of high-intensity triumph, such as successful resuscitations, are followed by prolonged periods of psychological and physical exhaustion. The “peak” experience is most prominently observed during successful life-saving interventions, where nurses are able to rescue critically ill patients from the brink of death. These moments generate a profound sense of professional meaning, accomplishment, and moral purpose. However, such acute highs are often unsustainable and may be accompanied by cumulative fatigue, especially when emotional recovery and institutional support are lacking. Over time, this recurring psycho-physiological pattern contributes to the development of chronic stress and occupational burnout.

The adrenaline-driven duality of emergency nursing was consistently highlighted across participants. Successful interventions often generated intense emotional highs. As one nurse described, “I felt immensely proud… only happiness and fulfillment remained” after rescuing a child in critical condition [19]. Others emphasized that independent procedures, such as complex punctures or solo resuscitations, enhanced both confidence and professional identity: “My self-esteem soared… I felt capable of handling any challenge” [23].

Despite these psychological rewards, the physical toll was pronounced. Nurses described acute fatigue and somatic distress following prolonged resuscitations. One participant shared, “Even though we were physically exhausted, we felt deeply satisfied psychologically” [25], while another stated, “After resuscitation, I was sore all over… completely fatigued“ [28]. Objective physiological indicators mirrored these experiences: “Baseline cortisol levels in senior nurses were 2.3 times higher than in newcomers” [35].

Sub-theme: Systemic improvement claims

Systemic improvement claims refer to structural reform demands articulated by emergency department (ED) nurses aimed at optimizing their clinical work environment. These demands are grounded in considerations of both individual well-being and patient safety, reflecting recurring themes across cultural and institutional contexts. A frequently cited priority is the preservation of physiological rhythms via standardized shift scheduling (e.g., 3 × 8-hour rotations), which has been associated with reductions in medical error rates and occupational fatigue. Nurses also emphasized the importance of providing specific accommodations for targeted groups—for example, establishing lactation rooms to facilitate smoother reintegration for postpartum nurses, thereby enhancing overall workforce efficiency. Furthermore, implementing trauma-informed management strategies—such as narrative debriefing and emotional processing sessions—was considered essential for addressing secondary trauma and moral injury. These cross-contextual recommendations reflect a shared professional consensus regarding the urgent need to reform the acute care environment to support both clinical performance and psychological resilience.

Emergency nurses consistently expressed the need for institutional changes to support well-being and performance. Many emphasized shifts reform, especially the benefits of the 3 × 8-hour model. As one participant explained, “The workload feels manageable… when I get at least 6 hours of uninterrupted sleep“ [20].

Support for postpartum nurses was another key area. “Breast-pumping time is not counted as absence… and we have a mother-and-baby room in the department” [21], one nurse noted, highlighting the role of spatial and temporal accommodations in easing workforce reintegration.

Trauma-informed management was also widely endorsed. Monthly narrative debriefing sessions were viewed as essential emotional outlets: “It makes the burden much lighter” [28]. Brief structured breaks after emotionally intense events were equally valued: “We are given a 15-minute break to avoid carrying emotional residue into the next task” [34].

Discussion

This study employed a meta-integration methodology to synthesize qualitative evidence regarding the clinical work experiences of emergency department nurses. Three overarching themes were identified across the included studies: the organizational–clinical stress ecosystem, mechanisms of resilience construction, and professional duality. The findings indicate that the challenges encountered by emergency department nurses, together with their associated coping strategies, constitute a complex and multidimensional interplay. This interplay not only underscores the substantial influence of institutional structures and workplace conditions on nurses’ mental health and professional trajectories, but also emphasizes the moderating role of cultural context in shaping these experiences.

Occupational stress ecosystem: multidimensional challenges in a high-pressure environment

Comprehensive qualitative and quantitative evidence indicates that emergency department (ED) nurses face multidimensional stressors that collectively form a typical occupational stress ecosystem. These include clinical operational stress, emotional and moral burdens, and systemic organizational stressors. Clinical operational stress is primarily driven by a combination of insufficient procedural competence and the complexity of the emergency care environment. Early-career ED nurses are particularly vulnerable to anxiety and fear when managing critically ill patients due to limited clinical experience and inadequate training in high-acuity interventions, consistent with the findings of Maria et al. [37] Emotional and moral burdens arise from persistent emotional labor, strained nurse–patient relationships, and psychological responses such as grief and self-blame when confronted with patient deterioration or death [38].

Organizational stressors manifest through long, irregular night shifts, frequent overtime, limited promotion pathways, and deficient management structures. Previous studies have confirmed that ED nurses frequently encounter overwhelming workloads, high labor intensity, skill gaps, and inadequate occupational protection [13, 27, 39, 40]. For instance, Ai et al. [41] that most ED nurses worked more than six night shifts per month, each exceeding eight hours, and this was significantly correlated with a burnout rate of over 60%. As the front line of hospital operations, the emergency department is characterized by urgent, complex cases that demand rapid decision-making and frequent emotional engagement. The superimposition of high-stakes clinical decision-making with continuous emotional labor is a primary driver of the occupational stress ecosystem. These stressors are exacerbated by insufficient staffing, outdated equipment, and constrained institutional support [2]. Structural flaws in nursing management—such as inadequate promotion systems and unstructured professional development pathways—further limit nurses’ long-term career growth [42].

To mitigate this, hospitals should prioritize strategic resource allocation to emergency care units. This includes increasing staffing ratios during night shifts, reducing scheduling frequency, and enhancing financial and institutional support. Such measures may promote greater work–life balance, improve physical health, and support family functioning [43, 44]. In parallel, a standardized nurse training model integrating theoretical instruction with practical simulation should be developed. Peer mentorship programs should be promoted to reduce skill disparities and enhance team performance. Additionally, internal psychosocial support mechanisms—such as counseling services involving nutrition, psychology, and psychiatry departments—should be institutionalized to address staff burnout and reinforce emotional resilience [45].

Resilience construction mechanisms: multiple strategies for coping with stress

Emergency department nurses adopt a range of resilience-building strategies to sustain psychological well-being and clinical performance under extreme occupational demands. These strategies encompass team-based support networks [46], cognitive reframing techniques [47], and culturally influenced work–life balance practices. Team-based support is a central resource in nurses’ resilience frameworks. Mentorship from senior nurses, peer collaboration, and tacit interprofessional coordination with physicians not only enhance clinical efficiency but also reinforce emotional stability and psychological endurance [48]. In terms of cognitive strategies, many nurses draw upon professional pride and emotional self-regulation to construct internal defenses. Targeted training in emotion management and adaptive thinking contributes to maintaining a positive mindset during high-stakes situations. Work–life balance practices vary significantly across cultural contexts. In East Asian healthcare systems, resilience often stems from family-centered support systems, although these may also introduce role conflict and guilt related to caregiving duties. Conversely, in Western settings, work–life balance is typically maintained through individualized boundary-setting, flexible scheduling, and access to psychological counseling. While existing literature has primarily emphasized the critical role of peer and team support in stress mitigation [49, 50], our synthesis reveals that familial and societal support systems are equally vital. Emotional support from family members and material security provided by society contribute holistically to nurses’ ability to cope with stress. Many participants described their families as the strongest psychological anchor, enabling them to remain focused and energized at work. To reinforce these resilience mechanisms, hospitals should construct comprehensive support frameworks. These include strengthening interdisciplinary teamwork, encouraging experienced nurses to serve as mentors, and cultivating a psychologically safe work environment. Institutions should also offer accessible mental health services, regular emotional resilience training, and structured psychological counseling. Moreover, efforts to accommodate personal and family needs—such as flexible shift scheduling and the establishment of mother–baby rooms—can alleviate work–life conflict and promote sustainable engagement.

Occupational value duality: the dual experience of achievement and depletion

The professional experience of emergency department nurses is marked by a paradoxical duality: the immediate sense of achievement following clinical success coexists with chronic physical and psychological depletion. Successful resuscitations and critical interventions often provide nurses with a profound sense of professional meaning and value. However, prolonged exposure to high-acuity, fast-paced, and emotionally taxing environments contributes to cumulative fatigue, burnout, and diminished psychological well-being. This “achievement–exhaustion paradox” was consistently observed across studies in diverse cultural settings. While the “peak” moments of successful patient recovery reinforce nurses’ identity and motivation, the recurring “troughs” of long-term stress create a unique psychophysiological cycle. Over time, this cycle may affect not only performance but also long-term retention and professional satisfaction. Notably, several studies report that 68.3% of emergency nurses and 85% of intensive care nurses experience positive changes in professional identity through coping and adaptive growth [51, 52]. This suggests that occupational adversity may also facilitate identity consolidation, if appropriately supported. To address this duality, interventions must target both the individual and institutional levels. Nurses should be encouraged to engage in self-care practices and monitor their own mental and physical health, particularly during emotionally intense clinical periods. Concurrently, healthcare institutions should improve nursing management systems, including the establishment of fair and transparent promotion pathways and diversified career development opportunities. Encouraging nurse involvement in technological innovation and evidence-based clinical improvement may also enhance professional fulfillment and long-term engagement. Furthermore, communication between frontline nurses and management must be strengthened. Hospitals should actively listen to staff voices and implement personalized management strategies tailored to nurses’ needs and expectations. Such responsiveness can reinforce nurses’ sense of belonging, increase morale, and reduce turnover intentions.

In summary, the clinical work experience of emergency department nurses is shaped by a dynamic interaction of stress, resilience, and professional identity. This meta-integrated synthesis highlights the urgent need for institutional reform and structured psychosocial support to ensure nurse well-being, promote clinical effectiveness, and enhance workforce retention. Future interventions should adopt a multidimensional perspective that incorporates cultural context, individual coping strategies, and systemic organizational improvements.

Conclusion and implications

Conclusion

This meta-synthesis of 18 qualitative studies elucidates the complex, multidimensional nature of the clinical work experience of emergency department (ED) nurses. Three overarching integrative themes emerged, capturing the core aspects of this experience:1.Organizational–Clinical Stress Ecosystem, in which environmental threats, emotional burdens, and resource shortages interact to create a pervasive source of occupational strain;2.Resilience-Building Mechanisms, encompassing team support, cognitive reframing, and culturally mediated work-life strategies that nurses employ to maintain well-being; 3.Professional Duality, reflecting the paradoxical coexistence of profound fulfillment derived from clinical achievements with chronic physical and psychological depletion.

The principal inference from these findings is that the sustainability of the ED nursing workforce depends on a delicate balance between systemic, often institutional, stressors inherent to the ED environment and the personal, interpersonal, and organizational resources available to mitigate them. Although the identified resilience mechanisms are robust, they are frequently mobilized reactively in response to a stress ecosystem that demands proactive, systemic reform. Therefore, promoting high-quality emergency care and ensuring nurse retention require interventions that simultaneously target the multiple layers of occupational stress and strengthen the structural and social supports upon which nurses rely.

Implications for clinical practice and management

The findings of this review suggest several concrete implications for nursing management and clinical practice:

Mitigating the Stress Ecosystem: Hospital administrators should prioritize addressing the identified stress ecosystem. Strategies include implementing evidence-based staffing models to reduce nurse-to-patient ratios, redesigning shift schedules (e.g., minimizing consecutive night shifts) to protect circadian rhythms and recovery time, and establishing clear protocols and targeted training to enhance procedural confidence, particularly among junior nurses.

Structuring Resilience Support: Informal resilience mechanisms reported by nurses should be formalized into institutional support structures. Recommended actions include creating structured peer-mentorship and clinical debriefing programs to strengthen team support networks, providing accessible mental health resources and training in emotional regulation skills, and ensuring workplace policies (e.g., access to lactation rooms and flexible scheduling options) actively facilitate work-life integration.

Acknowledging and Leveraging Professional Duality: Management strategies should recognize the dual nature of ED nursing. To sustain the positive aspects of professional identity, institutions should create avenues for recognizing clinical achievements and facilitating professional development. Simultaneously, to address physical and psychological exhaustion, organizations should implement regular burnout monitoring and ensure mandatory rest periods following highly stressful incidents.

Future research

To build upon the findings of this synthesis, future research should:1.Employ longitudinal mixed-methods designs to better understand the temporal dynamics among stress, resilience, and outcomes such as burnout or staff retention.2.Actively investigate the experiences of under-represented cohorts in the current literature, including nurses in primary care emergency settings, male ED nurses, and senior nursing staff.3.Develop and evaluate context-specific interventions, such as testing the efficacy of tailored shift patterns or resilience training programs, guided by robust theoretical frameworks to support implementation and assessment.
