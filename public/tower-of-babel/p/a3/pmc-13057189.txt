Ant colony optimization approach for sustainable end-milling with minimum quantity nano-green lubrication
Authors: Mustafa Abdullah, A C Umamaheshwer Rao, T Ramachandran, Swetarani Biswal, Shivam P Chaudhary, Ripendeep Singh, Akanksha Mishra, Vikasdeep Singh Mann, Abhijit Bhowmik, Yalew Tamene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the emergence of environmentally conscious production requirements, machining industries increasingly employ sustainable lubrication strategies to minimize the drawbacks associated with conventional flood cooling. Minimum quantity lubrication (MQL), in which a fine mist of lubricant is directed into the tool–workpiece interface, has gained significant traction owing to its ability to reduce fluid consumption while delivering enhanced lubrication. In this context, the present study examines the feasibility of alumina-reinforced palm oil as a nano-green lubricant under MQL conditions during end-milling. Alumina nanoparticles (0–1.4%) were dispersed into palm oil, and the optimum concentration (0.8%) was selected after thermo-physical characterization. Twenty-seven end-milling trials on Inconel 690 were subsequently conducted using the optimum nano-lubricant. Machining responses were analyzed using main effect plots, empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF), and analysis of variance (ANOVA). Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was used to construct predictive mathematical models. To determine optimum machining conditions, the present work employs Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) in place of conventional optimization schemes, where the RSM-derived expressions served as objective functions for the ACO-based search mechanism. The ACO-generated solution corresponds to cutting speed = 118.348 m/min, feed rate = 0.18 mm/tooth, and depth of cut = 0.7 mm. Validation experiments confirmed that the deviation between predicted and measured outputs remained below 3%, demonstrating that the RSM–ACO framework provides a robust, reliable optimization strategy for sustainable nano-lubricated machining.Keywords: Minimum quantity lubrication (MQL), Nano-green lubricants, End-milling, Sustainable machining, Response surface methodology (RSM), Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Nickel-based superalloys have long been acknowledged by engineers for their exceptional capability to retain mechanical and metallurgical properties under elevated temperature conditions, making them indispensable in critical engineering domains such as gas turbines, aero-engine components, thermal power plants, and marine propulsion systems1–4. Their superior resistance to oxidation, creep, corrosion, and thermal deformation ensures safe high-temperature operation; however, these same properties significantly constrain their machinability, presenting challenges such as high cutting forces, severe tool wear, excessive heat generation, and poor surface integrity during metal-cutting processes5–8. As a result, recent developments in machining research have emphasized the need for advanced cooling, lubrication, and optimization strategies to enhance machinability while simultaneously fulfilling industrial sustainability mandates9–11.

In addition to lubrication efficiency, the sustainability perspective of modern machining extends beyond fluid consumption and encompasses energy efficiency, waste reduction, and lifecycle environmental impact. Nickel-based superalloys such as Inconel 690 demand intensive machining energy due to their high strength and strain-hardening behavior, which directly increases power consumption and tool replacement frequency. Therefore, lubrication strategies that simultaneously reduce cutting forces and temperature can significantly lower specific cutting energy and enhance overall process sustainability. Recent investigations into eco-benign MQL-assisted machining of superalloys have demonstrated that hybrid nano-MQL environments not only improve tribological performance but also reduce environmental burden compared to conventional flood cooling systems11,12. This dual benefit of ecological compatibility and machining performance enhancement has motivated continued exploration of bio-based nano-lubricants as viable alternatives for advanced manufacturing applications13–17.

Among the most practical and economical approaches to machinability enhancement is the application of cutting fluids. Conventional flood lubrication, although effective in reducing friction and temperature, has come under scrutiny due to high consumption volumes, associated environmental hazards, occupational health concerns, and disposal-related costs18–21. In the last decade, the industrial shift toward sustainability—driven by ISO 14,000 directives emphasizing resource conservation, waste minimization, and eco-friendly manufacturing—has accelerated the exploration of alternative lubrication methods22–24. Consequently, minimum quantity lubrication (MQL) has emerged as a highly promising lubrication strategy. MQL employs a precise mist of lubricant delivered directly to the cutting zone, drastically reducing fluid consumption by up to 90–95% while still offering significant improvements in frictional behavior and thermal control. Numerous studies have highlighted the efficacy of MQL in turning, milling, and grinding applications, particularly when machining difficult-to-cut alloys25–28.

The choice of lubricant is central to the success of MQL. Vegetable oils have demonstrated superior lubricity due to their long-chain fatty acid structures, strong molecular polarity, and affinity for metal surfaces. However, concerns related to thermal degradation, oxidation, and viscosity breakdown at high temperatures restrict their direct adoption in high-performance machining environments29. To overcome these limitations, researchers have introduced nanoparticle reinforcement to formulate nano-green lubricants. Several studies have validated the potential of nanofluids in enhancing lubrication properties, increasing heat dissipation rates, modifying viscosity behavior, and forming protective tribo-films on machined surfaces30. Zhang et al.31 evaluated MoS₂-doped vegetable oils and reported substantial reductions in surface roughness and grinding forces owing to the formation of a stable lubricating film. Jia et al.32 observed significant improvements in grinding energy and surface finish using nano-enhanced castor and soybean oils. Singh et al.33 demonstrated improved lubrication performance of graphene-reinforced oils during machining of Ti-based alloys, attributing the improvements to enhanced film formation and reduced adhesion wear. A comprehensive review of hybrid nano-green lubricants by Gajrani et al.34 further emphasized that nanoparticles enhance thermal conductivity, rheological stability, and wettability when dispersed in vegetable oils. However, the concentration of nanoparticles must be optimized to minimize agglomeration and sedimentation, which negatively affect lubrication performance. Therefore, research into optimal nanoparticle selection, concentration, and dispersion techniques remains an important area of sustainable machining studies.

Parallel to lubrication advancements, the machining research community has made substantial progress in modeling and optimization of machining processes. Machining responses such as surface roughness, tool wear, cutting forces, and temperature are influenced by complex and nonlinear interactions between cutting parameters, lubrication mechanisms, and material properties. Conventional empirical or analytical models often prove inadequate in capturing such interactions, motivating the integration of computational intelligence and evolutionary optimization tools35,36. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) has been widely used for establishing empirical models and understanding parameter interactions. Numerous studies have combined RSM with intelligent optimization algorithms to achieve optimal machining conditions. Adalarasan et al.37 employed RSM–desirability analysis for laser machining optimization, while Laouissi et al.38 compared ANN and RSM predictive efficiency before integrating their models with a genetic algorithm (GA) for final optimization. Coppel et al.39 explored multiple evolutionary algorithms for predictive control of micro-milling operations and reported varying accuracy levels among algorithms depending on machining context.

While algorithms such as PSO, TLBO, and GA have been extensively explored, emerging trends in machining optimization have turned toward ant colony–based algorithms (ACOs), which offer distinct advantages in solving nonlinear, multimodal optimization problems40,41. ACO is inspired by the foraging behavior of real ants, which rely on pheromone-laying and pheromone-following mechanisms to discover shorter paths between food sources and their nest. In computational contexts, artificial ants traverse a solution space by probabilistically selecting paths based on pheromone trails and heuristic desirability, reinforcing promising regions of the search space through repeated exploration. The inherent features of ACO—such as distributed computation, adaptive learning, positive feedback amplification, and efficient exploration–exploitation balance—make it particularly well suited for machining optimization, where multiple objectives must be simultaneously minimized or maximized under nonlinear constraints. Unlike certain swarm-based algorithms that may prematurely converge or rely heavily on velocity–position updates, ACO utilizes probabilistic transitions, allowing more controlled diversification during early iterations and intensified exploitation in later stages. This behavior facilitates stable convergence in machining tasks involving multiple machining responses, interacting variables, and complex parameter sensitivities42,43.

Given these advantages, the present study employs an RSM–ACO optimization framework to determine the optimal end-milling parameters for machining Inconel 690 under minimum quantity nano-green lubrication. The study begins with the synthesis of alumina-reinforced palm oil nanofluids, followed by a detailed evaluation of their thermo-physical properties. Comparative machining trials under dry, palm oil MQL, and nano-green MQL are conducted, after which the best-performing nano-lubricant is selected. Twenty-seven milling trials are executed using the Taguchi L27 design, and the resulting data are analyzed statistically using ECDF and ANOVA. RSM-based models are then formulated for surface roughness (Ra), resultant cutting force (Fr), and temperature (T). These models serve as objective functions in an ACO-based optimization routine designed to identify the machining parameters that collectively minimize all responses. Thus, the present work contributes to the growing area of sustainable machining by demonstrating the combined potential of nano-green lubrication and ACO-driven optimization. By employing ACO as an alternative evolutionary optimization mechanism, the study demonstrates the feasibility of using nature-inspired algorithms to achieve high-performance and environmentally responsible machining conditions. The integration of nano-green lubrication strategies with computational intelligence provides a robust pathway for the development of advanced manufacturing systems aligned with global sustainability goals.

Although several studies have explored nano-MQL machining and evolutionary optimization independently, limited research has systematically integrated nano-green lubrication with an Ant Colony Optimization framework for multi-response milling of Inconel 690. Existing works primarily employ GA, PSO, or desirability-based techniques. The present study addresses this gap by combining experimentally validated nano-green lubrication with an RSM–ACO hybrid optimization model, thereby introducing a novel decision-support framework for sustainable machining of nickel-based superalloys.

Materials and methods

The following section presents a comprehensive description of the experimental workflow, beginning with the synthesis and characterization of nano-green lubricants and followed by the execution of MQL-assisted end-milling operations. All details related to the preparation technique, machining setup, measurement devices, and design of experiments have been retained exactly as in the uploaded manuscript, with enhanced clarity and refined technical articulation.

Experimental endeavor

Nanofluid preparation and characterization

The preparation of nano-green lubricants was carried out using alumina nanoparticles of average particle size 40 nm dispersed into high-purity palm oil (99.85%). Alumina was selected owing to its superior thermal conductivity, chemical inertness, high melting point, and excellent compatibility with vegetable oil molecules, which collectively enhance the thermo-physical behavior of the base lubricant during high-temperature machining. To systematically investigate the role of nanoparticle concentration on lubricant performance, the volume fraction of alumina was varied from 0 to 1.4%, ensuring that the chosen range adequately captured the transition from effective reinforcement to potential agglomeration-induced deterioration.

Nanofluid synthesis followed the well-established two-step preparation technique44, adopted widely in nanofluid research due to its effectiveness in ensuring the uniform dispersion of solid nanoparticles into liquid media. In the first step, measured quantities of alumina nanoparticles were gradually introduced into palm oil while being subjected to mechanical agitation using a digital magnetic stirrer (Abdos®, MS-H280-Pro, India). This pre-mixing stage allows the nanoparticles to be initially wetted and separated, reducing the likelihood of cluster formation. The stirring parameters—speed, temperature, and duration—were optimized based on preliminary trials to avoid excessive shear that could affect the chemical stability of the oil. In the second step, the mixture was subjected to probe-type ultrasonication (Johnson Plastosonic, ITB-32D4A76D, India), a critical phase responsible for breaking down micro-scale agglomerates and ensuring homogenous nanoparticle dispersion. Ultrasonication parameters such as amplitude, duty cycle, and exposure time were selected to balance dispersion efficiency with thermal safety, preventing degradation of the oil due to localized heating. The use of a probe sonicator, as opposed to a bath sonicator, ensured high-intensity acoustic cavitation, resulting in finer dispersion and improved suspension stability. To mitigate oxidation-related degradation—a known limitation of vegetable oils exposed to fluctuating temperatures—tocopherol (Vitamin E) was incorporated as a natural antioxidant. Its addition prevents the cleavage of unsaturated bonds in palm oil molecules. Additionally, a small proportion (0.3%) of polysorbate-80 was mixed into the nanofluid as a biodegradable surfactant. This compound reduces interfacial tension between nanoparticles and the oil medium, thereby enhancing long-term dispersion stability and lowering the probability of re-agglomeration during machining operations. Following synthesis, the nanofluids underwent detailed thermo-physical characterization. The thermal conductivity was measured using a UVSAR SYSTRONICS-304 thermal conductivity meter, which operates based on transient hot-wire principles to deliver accurate measurements even for highly viscous fluids. The dynamic viscosity was recorded using a Brookfield DV2T viscometer, ensuring controlled shear-rate measurements across a range of temperatures representative of machining conditions. These measurements were essential for identifying the nanoparticle loading that provides maximum benefit without compromising fluid flow characteristics within the MQL nozzle. Machining operation

Machining experiments were performed on Inconel 690 plates measuring 50 mm × 20 mm × 10 mm using a CNC milling machine. Tables 1 and 2 present the chemical composition and physical–mechanical properties of the alloy, highlighting its high strength, corrosion resistance, and limited thermal conductivity — factors that contribute to its challenging machinability. To maintain experimental consistency, an uncoated solid carbide end-mill was employed for all trials. The optimal nanoparticle concentration identified through thermo-physical analysis (0.8% alumina) was adopted for subsequent machining trials. This nano-green lubricant was delivered via an external MQL system (Model: KRS-MQL-2/PS/FS/T, India), whose specifications are listed in Table 3. The MQL system was operated under controlled laboratory ambient conditions (maintained at 27 ± 2 °C). No external heating mechanism was applied to the lubricant. The compressed air temperature remained within the operational limits specified in Table 3, ensuring stable atomization performance throughout the experiments. The MQL parameters — including flow rate, air pressure, and nozzle orientation — were derived from established literature and held constant throughout the study to ensure that only machining parameters influenced the results. The MQL nozzle was positioned at a distance of 25 mm from the cutting zone and oriented at an inclination angle of 45° relative to the tool axis. The spray was directed precisely toward the tool–chip interface to ensure effective penetration of the atomized nano-lubricant into the primary shear zone. The nozzle position was fixed throughout all experimental trials to maintain consistent lubrication conditions. A Taguchi L27 orthogonal array45 was chosen to design the experiments, considering three primary machining parameters: cutting speed (Vc), feed rate (f), and depth of cut (ap), each set at three levels. The numerical values and coding of these parameters are summarized in Table 4. The machining responses of interest — average surface roughness, resultant cutting force, and maximum cutting temperature — were measured using highly sensitive instrumentation. A 3D surface profilometer (Taylor Hobson, CCI MP, India) was used for Ra evaluation, while a piezoelectric dynamometer (Kistler, 9272B, India) recorded force components in three orthogonal directions. Cutting temperature was monitored using an infrared thermal camera (Thermo Pro, TP8). Finally, scanning electron microscopy (ZEISS Sigma 300) facilitated a microstructural examination of machined surfaces to assess defect morphology and nanoparticle deposition. Modeling and optimization

The machining responses obtained from the experimental trials—surface roughness, resultant cutting force, and maximum temperature—exhibited nonlinear dependence on the input variables. To capture these interactions and generate a predictive framework capable of navigating multidimensional parameter spaces, a hybrid modeling–optimization strategy was adopted. This approach combines RSM for empirical model development with ACO as a nature-inspired algorithm for determining optimum machining parameters under nano-green MQL conditions.

RSM-based metamodeling

RSM is employed as a statistical tool to model the relationship between machining parameters and the corresponding output responses. The purpose of using RSM is to develop analytical expressions that can later be integrated within an optimization framework. To assess the adequacy of each developed model, several statistical parameters such as the normalized root mean square error (NRMSE), coefficient of determination (R²), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), Theil’s inequality coefficient (U₂), and Kullback–Leibler divergence (KL–N) are typically considered. These indices measure how well the regression model captures the underlying trends in the data without incorporating any experimental outcomes here. The validated RSM equations serve as surrogate models for the optimization phase, allowing the algorithm to evaluate candidate solutions without requiring additional experiments.

Ant colony optimization framework

ACO is a metaheuristic optimization algorithm inspired by the foraging behavior of real ant colonies. In nature, ants communicate indirectly by depositing a chemical substance known as pheromone along the paths they traverse49. Over time, routes associated with richer pheromone concentrations become more attractive, enabling the colony to collectively converge toward the shortest or most efficient path between their nest and a food source. This self-organized and distributed problem-solving capability forms the basis of ACO’s computational framework. In the context of numerical optimization, ACO models each decision variable as a node or discrete state within a probabilistic search space50. Artificial ants explore this space by constructing solutions incrementally, selecting parameter values according to a transition probability that combines pheromone intensity and heuristic desirability. The pheromone factor encapsulates accumulated learning from previous iterations, whereas the heuristic factor generally reflects problem-specific information such as the inverse of the objective function or the gradient direction51. Higher values of α favor exploitation of known good solutions, while larger β values encourage exploration guided by problem-specific heuristics.

Once all ants have constructed candidate solutions for an iteration, the quality of each solution is evaluated using the combined objective function (COF). Solutions that produce lower COF values are considered superior. Pheromone trails are then updated based on two opposing mechanisms: evaporation and reinforcement. Early iterations favor diversity of search, while later iterations emphasize intensification around promising regions. The algorithm terminates when the maximum number of iterations is reached or when successive improvements in the COF fall below a predefined tolerance threshold, indicating convergence. This adaptive learning behavior enables ACO to navigate nonlinear, multidimensional machining optimization landscapes efficiently, making it particularly suitable for identifying optimal cutting conditions in end-milling operations where complex interactions exist between lubrication mechanism, thermal behavior, and material removal. Results and discussion

Thermo-physical behaviors of nano-green lubricants

The thermo-physical characterization of the prepared nano-green lubricants is essential for understanding their suitability in machining environments where frictional heat generation and tool–workpiece interaction dominate the cutting mechanics. In the present study, palm oil was selected as the base fluid due to its high lubricity, biodegradability, and natural film-forming ability, consistent with earlier reports advocating vegetable oils for sustainable machining52. However, the inherent thermal instability and oxidation tendency of plant-based oils under high cutting temperatures have been widely documented. To overcome this limitation, alumina nanoparticles were dispersed into the base oil, as alumina possesses high thermal conductivity, chemical inertness, and mechanical stability, making it one of the most frequently studied metal-oxide additives in nanolubricants53. The synthesized nanofluids were subjected to systematic thermo-physical analysis to determine how nanoparticle concentration influenced the lubricant’s heat transfer potential and flow characteristics.

Thermal conductivity measurements revealed a monotonic increase with rising nanoparticle concentration up to 0.8%, beyond which the enhancement plateaued. This improvement can be scientifically attributed to phonon-mediated energy transport, Brownian motion-induced micro-convection, and the formation of thermally conductive percolation networks within the liquid medium54. Similar observations were reported by Gajrani et al.34, who noted that nano-reinforced bio-lubricants achieved more efficient heat dissipation due to the presence of thermally active nanoparticles. In the present case, the rise in thermal conductivity suggests that the alumina particles effectively facilitated heat removal from the cutting zone, which is critical for machining nickel-based alloys known for low thermal diffusivity and severe heat concentration1. A graphical representation of thermal conductivity variation with nanoparticle concentration (Fig. 4 (a)) clearly demonstrates the steepest conductivity gain between 0.4% and 0.8%, confirming this concentration range as thermally advantageous.

Thermal conductivity and dynamic viscosity variation with nanoparticle concentration.

Dynamic viscosity exhibited a proportional increase with nanoparticle loading (Fig. 4(b)), indicating higher internal resistance to flow and improved hydrodynamic film strength. Controlled viscosity growth is beneficial because it sustains a stable lubricating layer at the tool–chip interface, reducing asperity contact and adhesive wear. However, excessively viscous lubricants may impair atomization efficiency in MQL nozzles, leading to uneven droplet dispersion and localized starvation. The observed viscosity trend in this study aligns with temperature-dependent fluid behavior reported by Zhang et al.31, where vegetable-oil-based nanofluids demonstrated viscosity reduction with rising temperature, yet maintained higher absolute viscosity than their non-reinforced counterparts. The intermediate viscosity achieved at 0.8% alumina concentration suggests an optimal balance between sprayability and lubrication film integrity, making it the most suitable composition for subsequent machining experiments.

The overall thermo-physical evaluation confirms that alumina reinforcement significantly enhances both heat dissipation capacity and load-carrying ability of palm oil without compromising fluid stability. Stability assessments indicated no visible sedimentation for several weeks, affirming the effectiveness of ultrasonication and surfactant addition as dispersion-stabilizing techniques, in agreement with findings presented by Singh et al.33. When linked to machining requirements, improved thermal conductivity contributes to reduced cutting temperature, while increased viscosity supports lower frictional forces and improved surface finish. These interdependent performance characteristics justify the selection of the 0.8% alumina nano-green lubricant for further investigation under MQL-assisted end-milling, as it provides a balanced thermo-physical profile conducive to sustainable machining applications.

Comparative machining behaviour

The machining response of Inconel 690 under different lubrication environments—dry cutting, pure palm oil MQL, and alumina-based nano-green MQL—was evaluated to understand how lubrication mechanisms influence surface finish, cutting forces, and thermal behaviour. The results consistently indicated that the nano-green lubricant produced superior machining performance across all measured parameters. This improvement is closely related to the lubricant’s enhanced thermo-physical properties, which directly influence tribological interactions, chip formation processes, and tool–workpiece heat transfer during milling. Under dry machining, inadequate lubrication leads to pronounced adhesion, increased frictional stress, and elevated temperatures, all of which degrade surface integrity and raise cutting forces. These observations align with earlier work demonstrating that machining nickel-based alloys without lubrication tends to intensify tool–workpiece friction due to the alloy’s low thermal conductivity and high hot hardness2,3. A comparative plot of machining responses under the three lubrication conditions is shown in Fig. 5, illustrating the superior stability of the nano-green lubricant.

Comparison of machining responses under different lubrication conditions.

Surface roughness showed the most notable improvement under the nano-green MQL condition. The improved lubrication film resulting from higher viscosity and enhanced thermal conductivity at 0.8% alumina concentration facilitated smoother tool–workpiece interaction. The nanoparticles acted as micro-ball bearings, reducing ploughing action and suppressing built-up edge formation—mechanisms also reported by Singh et al.33 and Gajrani et al.34 for other vegetable-oil-based nanofluids. Figure 6 presents a comparative SEM analysis of the machined surfaces obtained under dry milling, vegetable oil–assisted MQL milling, and nanoparticle-assisted MQL milling conditions. The surface produced during dry milling (Fig. 6a) exhibits pronounced morphological damage characterized by deep pits, material pull-out regions, severe ploughing marks, and adhered wear debris. These defects arise due to intense friction and elevated cutting temperatures in the absence of effective lubrication, leading to dominant adhesive and abrasive wear mechanisms. In contrast, the surface machined under vegetable oil–assisted MQL (Fig. 6b) shows noticeable improvement, with relatively smoother and more uniform feed marks. However, localized micro-adhesive debris are still evident, indicating partial mitigation of friction and thermal effects through the formation of a lubricating oil film, though its stability remains limited under severe cutting conditions.

SEM images of machined surfaces under different lubrication environments.

A significant enhancement in surface integrity is observed for the nanoparticle-assisted MQL condition (Fig. 6c), where the machined surface displays highly uniform and continuous feed marks with negligible surface defects. The absence of deep pits, pull-out zones, and adhered debris confirms the effective suppression of adhesion and plastic deformation mechanisms. This improvement is attributed to the synergistic action of nanoparticles, which enhance the load-carrying capacity of the lubricant, promote stable tribo-film formation, and facilitate rolling, polishing, and mending effects at the tool–workpiece interface. Consequently, nanoparticle-assisted MQL milling demonstrates superior control over friction and heat generation, resulting in the smoothest surface finish and the most favorable wear characteristics among the investigated machining environments.

Resultant cutting force also demonstrated a significant reduction under nano-green MQL. The reduction is a consequence of both improved lubrication and thermal softening of the workpiece surface layer. As nanoparticles strengthen the load-carrying capacity of the lubricant film, they help maintain separation between interacting surfaces, thereby reducing friction-induced resistance. Palm oil MQL, although beneficial, provided a thinner and less stable lubricating film compared to the nanofluid, particularly at higher temperatures. Dry machining consistently produced the highest forces because friction was dominant and chip flow was restricted. The observed trends are consistent with tribological studies that highlight nanoparticle-assisted lubrication as an effective strategy for reducing contact stresses and energy consumption in machining15,16.

Cutting temperature exhibited a similar trend, with nano-green MQL displaying the lowest values. The superior heat removal capability of the nanofluid can be attributed to its enhanced thermal conductivity and greater spreading ability over the hot cutting zone. These properties facilitate rapid dissipation of frictional heat away from the cutting edges, which is crucial for machining superalloys known for maintaining strength at elevated temperatures1. Pure palm oil MQL provided moderate cooling but was less effective than the nanofluid, while dry machining resulted in substantial temperature rise due to the absence of any cooling medium. Reduced temperature under nano-lubricated conditions not only improves dimensional accuracy but also minimizes thermal damage to both tool and workpiece, supporting findings from earlier sustainable machining studies12,17.

Overall, the comparative analysis demonstrates that the alumina-based nano-green MQL environment significantly enhances machining efficiency by synergistically improving lubrication, cooling, and tribological behaviour. These improvements manifest through reduced friction, lower heat generation, improved chip evacuation, and more stable cutting mechanics. Thus, the nano-green lubricant shows clear potential as a sustainable alternative to traditional cutting fluids, particularly in machining advanced engineering materials such as Inconel 690.

Main effect plot analysis

The MEPs in Fig. 7 illustrates the direct influence of each machining parameter on the measured responses. For Ra, cutting speed demonstrated an inverse relationship with surface roughness. Higher speeds promoted thermal softening at the tool–workpiece interface, reducing ploughing and resulting in finer surface textures. This behavior aligns with typical machining responses observed for high-strength alloys, where elevated speeds facilitate smoother chip formation due to reduced shear energy2,3. The feed rate, however, exhibited a strong positive effect: increasing feed values led to progressively higher Ra. This is attributed to the geometric constraints of milling, where larger feed per tooth causes increased scallop height and more pronounced tool marks. Depth of cut influenced Ra moderately; higher depths created greater chip load and potential vibration, which can deteriorate surface finish.

For Fr (Fig. 8), feed rate showed the largest impact, as expected in milling operations where chip thickness directly determines the cutting force. At higher feeds, the tool encounters increased resistance due to larger material removal per tooth. Depth of cut also significantly influenced force generation because enlarging the engagement zone increases shear area and load on the cutting edge. Cutting speed exhibited a slight decreasing trend in force values, largely due to thermal softening at higher speeds, which reduces the material’s apparent shear strength. This trend is consistent with the findings of earlier studies that evaluated nano-lubricated milling and its effects on force reduction mechanisms.

Cutting temperature, shown in Fig. 9, was dominated by cutting speed. Increasing speed raises the rate of frictional heat generation and chip flow velocity, both of which elevate the thermal load in the cutting zone. Feed rate contributed to temperature increase when thicker chips were produced, trapping more heat near the cutting edge. Depth of cut also played an influential role by expanding the plastic deformation region and increasing heat storage within the material. Overall, the MEPs provide a clear visual representation of how each parameter influences the thermo-mechanical behavior during machining.

Empirical Cumulative Distribution Analysis

ECDF analysis was conducted to evaluate the statistical distribution characteristics of Ra, Fr, and T. The ECDF curves presented in Fig. 10 show that the plotted experimental data points closely follow the theoretical normal distribution line, indicating that the measured responses exhibit strong normality. This near-linear alignment confirms that the variations in machining responses arise from systematic parameter effects rather than random anomalies or outliers. For Ra, the clustering of data points around the normal probability line shows uniform response behavior across all 27 experimental trials. Similarly, the Fr data demonstrate symmetrical distribution with minimal deviations, validating the stability of cutting force measurements. The cutting temperature values also display excellent agreement with the normal model, reflecting consistent thermal behavior under each machining condition. The normality confirmed through ECDF analysis strengthens the reliability of the experimental dataset, making the responses suitable for regression modeling. Such stability is crucial for ensuring that the prediction models developed in later sections are based on statistically sound and unbiased data. Comparable statistical conformity has been reported in machining studies involving hybrid and nano-lubricants, reinforcing the relevance of ECDF as a diagnostic tool.

Analysis of Variance

ANOVA was performed to quantify the statistical significance of machining parameters and to determine their percentage contributions to Ra, Fr, and T (Fig. 11). Which reveal that feed rate is the most influential parameter governing Ra and Fr. This is consistent with theoretical milling mechanics, where feed directly dictates chip formation, surface interaction, and mechanical loading. High feed rates impose larger cutting forces and result in thicker chips, leading to increased surface irregularities and higher energy consumption. Cutting speed emerged as the primary contributor to cutting temperature, accounting for the majority of variance in T. The dominance of speed in heat generation is well established, as increased tool–chip sliding velocity naturally generates higher frictional heat. Depth of cut played a secondary but statistically significant role in all three responses by modifying the material removal zone and altering the stress distribution at the cutting interface. The F-values for the dominant factors were substantially higher than the critical F-table values, and the corresponding p-values were below 0.05, confirming statistical significance at the 95% confidence level. These results align with prior machining literature, which consistently identifies feed rate as a critical parameter for mechanical responses and speed as the governing factor for thermal responses in milling high-temperature alloys. The ANOVA findings thus provide a clear hierarchy of parameter influence, offering essential guidance for subsequent modeling and optimization efforts.

Results of ACO

To determine a unified optimal machining condition for Ra, Fr, and T, it was essential to construct a COF. Each individual machining response was first transformed into a normalized satisfaction metric, ensuring that all three responses contributed proportionately to the overall evaluation. These satisfaction values were then integrated using a multiplicative aggregation operator to formulate a single COF representing the collective machining performance. The primary goal of the optimization routine was to minimize this COF so that all responses could be simultaneously improved under the nano-green MQL environment.

In the present study, the COF was optimized using the ACO algorithm. The ACO framework imitates the pheromone-based learning strategy of real ant colonies; therefore, its optimization capability heavily depends on pheromone updating, evaporation control, and heuristic guidance. The algorithm was implemented in MATLAB, where each artificial ant represented a candidate machining condition defined by the three machining variables. During the iterative search process, ants moved through the multidimensional parametric space based on pheromone intensities and heuristic visibility. Better-performing solutions deposited stronger pheromone trails, directing subsequent ants toward promising regions of the search space. Meanwhile, pheromone evaporation prevented premature stagnation and maintained diversity among the explored solutions. Extensive trial-and-error runs were conducted to select the most suitable ACO hyperparameters for this machining problem. Parameters such as the number of ants, pheromone influence factor (α), heuristic influence factor (β), pheromone evaporation rate (ρ), and the maximum number of iterations were tuned to ensure stable and consistent convergence. The final optimized configuration of the ACO routine is summarized in Table 6. These tuned parameters ensured a balanced trade-off between exploration (searching new solution paths) and exploitation (refining the best solutions).

It is important to distinguish the functional roles of RSM and ACO within the proposed framework. RSM demonstrated strong predictive capability for individual responses (Ra, Fr, and T), as confirmed by high R² values (0.89–0.91). However, RSM alone does not inherently perform simultaneous multi-response optimization without desirability integration. ACO, when coupled with the RSM-derived equations, effectively minimized the combined objective function and produced a unified optimal parameter set. Therefore, RSM performed better in predictive modeling of individual responses, while ACO demonstrated superior capability in global multi-response optimization.
