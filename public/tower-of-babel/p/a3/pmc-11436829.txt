Interplay of the Indian summer monsoon and intermonsoon precipitation in Sri Lanka due to ITCZ migration during the last 80000 years
Authors: P N Ranasinghe, K A Hughen, T U T Wijewardhana, J D Ortiz, A K I U Kapuge, Kalpani M Ratnayake, N U Nanayakkara, A L T Hewawasam, Y P S Siriwardana, S Kodithuwakku
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecipitation variations in the tropical Indian Ocean region result from changes in the Indian Summer Monsoon (ISM), as well as convective and cyclonic rainfall. The relative roles of such forcing can be elucidated by constructing robust paleoclimate records, which help to better predict future variability in precipitation due to rising greenhouse gases. This study was carried out using a suite of paleoclimate proxies on a combination of marine and terrestrial sediment cores from Sri Lanka. The sites receive differing amounts of precipitation from the ISM versus convective and cyclone-driven intermonsoon rainfall and allow differentiation of precipitation sources through time. The constructed 80000-year-long precipitation record for the ISM-dominant regions of Sri Lanka shows strong sensitivity to orbital scale insolation variations as well as to millennial-scale events in the North Atlantic region. This precipitation response to external and internal forcings is a result of the combined effects of ISM and Intermonsoon intensity either of which dominated periodically. The strong positive correlation between different periods in the precipitation record and different monthly insolation curves shows that changes in solar insolation due to precession decide the dominant rainfall mechanism in the region, suggesting the ISM dominated after 13 ka (MIS1) while the first intermonsoon dominated between 29 and 13 ka (MIS2) and the second intermonsoon dominated period between 77 and 40 ka.Subject terms: Palaeoceanography, Palaeoclimate, Climate sciences, Ocean sciences

Background

The Asian monsoon, with its Indian and East Asian monsoon branches, is the main source of precipitation for about 60% of the world's population in southern and eastern Asia1. Therefore, it drives agriculture and livelihood for most of the world's population. Although South Asia is primarily influenced by the summer monsoon regime, convective intermonsoonal (pre and post monsoons) rainfall driven by variations in the position of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), as well as cyclonic rainfall, also significantly contributes to the annual precipitation total2. Some regions in continental countries like India, Pakistan, and China, and equatorial islands such as Borneo, and Sri Lanka receive considerable rain from the pre and post monsoons3–6. Generally, pre-monsoon (First intermonsoon) rainfall occurs between March and May in the Asian monsoon region, while post-monsoon (Second intermonsoon) occurs between September and December. However, its duration and intensity vary depending on the region. Through its effect on the hydrological cycle, the climate has affected human evolution, migration, and civilization. Precipitation changes have also significantly influenced agriculture in the ancient kingdoms of Sri Lanka during the historical period7. In modern times, drought conditions and warming are removing the buffer to water scarcity provided by mountain snowpack and high-elevation mountain ice in Asia8. As has happened in the past, precipitation changes in the future will determine the water supply and groundwater recharge for the vast population of Asia.

Although decades of observational and model-based studies have recognized that ISM intensity and onset depend on many factors, such as solar radiation9–12, inter-hemispheric heat transport10,13, and high-latitude glacial and interglacial boundary conditions11, a significant debate exists over the primary drivers of orbital and millennial-scale monsoon variability10,14–17. Studies show that orbital scale variability of obliquity and precession affects the Indian monsoon by changing the meridional gradients of solar insolation and surface temperature16,18. On millennial timescales, forcings that shift the ITCZ, such as North Atlantic overturning circulation, volcanism, and solar activity, also affect the monsoon19. Currently, temporal and spatial discrepancies exist between Asian monsoon records due to age uncertainties, leads, and lags of different terrestrial and marine proxies, as well as regional geographical variability11,20–24. Therefore, new palaeoclimatic records may play an important role in understanding the regional characteristics of the different monsoon subsystems, as well as the internal feedback processes within the climate system itself19. Regional changes have also been observed in intermonsoon rainfall, independent of summer or winter monsoons. Intensification of intermonsoon rainfall in western India and Pakistan5,25,26, weakening of ISM rainfall, and long-term declining rainfall in some areas in Sri Lanka have been reported27,28. Hence, understanding the past behavior of both ISM and intermonsoon precipitation is critical to predicting future changes in response to anthropogenic global warming and minimizing impacts on agriculture and communities.

The annually reversing ISM and winter monsoons (IWM) exchange heat between the equatorial Indian Ocean and the continental Indian land mass. Sri Lanka receives rainfall from both the ISM and IWM. During the May-September period, the southwestern part of the island receives ISM rains due to the orographic effect from the central mountains, while the northeastern region, north-central plains, and northeastern mountain slopes become dry in the rain shadow of the central mountains. The opposite happens during the December–February IWM period29,30. The first intermonsoon rainfall occurs throughout the country between March and April while the second intermonsoon prevails between October and November period29,30. During intermonsoons, precipitation formed by local convection (thunderstorms) formed as a result of day and night differential heating of the land and ocean (on-shore breezes), positioning of ITCZ during its northward (April) and southward (October) migration, and tropical cyclones and depressions which are dominant during second intermonsoon (post monsoonal)31–33. Local convection is interrupted by the monsoon winds in the particular monsoon region31,32,34,35. Sri Lanka receives about 60% of the total rainfall during the ISM and second Intermonsoon periods36. Because Sri Lanka is strategically situated at the southern end of the Indian subcontinent, precipitation records from the country can differentiate between the ISM and IWM, along with the first and second intermonsoons. Paleo-precipitation records are very limited in Sri Lanka, and high-resolution, long-term records are currently absent37,38. Capitalizing on this geographic setting, this study reconstructs high-resolution precipitation records for the ISM-dominant southwestern region during the last 80 ka using well-correlated marine and terrestrial sediment records.

A 25 m sediment core from an isolated sedimentary basin in Ratnapura (RP) provides a terrestrial precipitation record for the 40–80 ka period. The age model was produced by correlating the red-green contrast of the RP core colour reflectance index record, a*, with the NGRIP Greenland δ18O record, supported by AMS 14C dates (Supplements 1 and 2). Precipitation records for the last ~ 30 ka are provided by a terrestrial sediment core from the Gawarathenna Plain of Frogmore area (FM: 2.04 m) in Maskeliya, and marine cores from the continental slope off Colombo (CMB: 1.76 m) and continental shelf off Negombo (NG: 3.90 m), western Sri Lanka (Fig. 1).

Late Pleistocene precipitation variability

A suite of color reflectance and chemical composition-based proxies were used to reconstruct late Pleistocene precipitation variability in the summer monsoonal region of Sri Lanka. Color reflectance of sediment is used to identify its mineral composition and to interpret the climatic conditions that prevailed during sediment deposition39–42. The indices a* and b* denote the red (+ a*)-green (− a*) and yellow (+ b*)-blue (− b*) chromaticities, respectively. In this location, the red color of sediment is mainly controlled by the iron input to sediment40,43,44. Fe is one of the three most abundant minerals in the saprolite profiles of the wet zone of Sri Lanka, underlaid by granulite metamorphic terrain rich in Fe-bearing hypersthene, hornblende, and garnets45. Enhanced erosion and leaching can release Fe from the regolith to the adjacent basins during periods of intensified precipitation. The southwestern region of the island, including the Gawaratenna plain in the Kelani River basin and the Ratnapura basin, is underlain by granulite-grade rocks with Fe-bearing minerals above. Therefore, the release of Fe into the studied terrestrial sedimentary basins is a precipitation-driven process that increases sediment redness (a*). The first varimax-rotated principal component of elemental compositional data in the terrestrial cores RP and FM (XRF-VPC1TERR) has high loadings for Fe and Mn (Supplement 3), indicating that it represents terrigenous erosion by precipitation. XRF-VPC1TERR of RP and FM show significant positive correlations with a* records of RP (r = 0.18, p < 0.01) and FM (r = 0.41, p < 0.01), further confirming that a* represents precipitation-driven Fe discharge. The a* values in the marine NG core, extracted close to the coastline and Kelani river plume, also captured terrestrial flux as its dominant signal because it positively correlates (r = 0.5, p < 0.00) with XRF-VPC1NG in the NG core, which has high loadings for Al, Si, Ti, and Mn (Supplement 3), representing the terrigenous erosional flux. Similarly, XRF-VPC1CMB has high loadings with Fe, Ti, and Si representing terrestrial flux. The colour reflectance lightness (L*) record in the CMB core extracted from the continental slope about 36 km off the coast positively correlates with (− a*) (greeness) and XRF-VPC3CMB of the core, with high loadings for Sr (r = 0.7, p < 0.05 and r = 0.5, p < 0.00 respectively) (Supplement 3). This area exhibits high chlorophyll-a during the summer monsoonal months46. L* is used as an indicator of marine biogenic productivity, and thus, we infer that L*, (− a*) and XRF-VPC3CMB represent the monsoon-driven export productivity of CaCO347. The close match in down core variability of independent reflectance and chemical composition-based proxies in both terrestrial and marine sediment cores excludes the possibility of significant influence from other non-monsoon related oceanic processes including sea level changes.

The high-resolution (Sampling interval ~ 13 yr) proxy records from the RP core provide a robust precipitation record. Increased a* during warm marine isotope stages MIS1, MIS3, and MIS5 shows enhanced Fe supply during warm intervals (Fig. 2). XRF-VPC1TERR of elemental compositional data, which correlates with Fe, also increased during these warm intervals (Fig. 2). Precipitation was generally higher during warm MIS1, MIS3, and MIS5 isotope stages despite millennial scale perturbations due to Dansgaard-Oeschger (DO) events (Fig. 2). Both RP a* (r = 0.5, p = 0.00) and RP XRF-VPC1TERR (0.2, p = 0.00) positively correlate with NGRP δ18O records validating the enhanced precipitation hypothesis during warm MIS and interstadial phases of DO events. The a* and XRF-VPC1TERR records of the RP core exhibit increased values during the warm interstadials of DO events 11–20, indicating increased Fe in sediment and increased precipitation (Fig. 3). The data clearly show the precipitation response to even short DO events such as 18, 15, and 13 (Fig. 3). Significant precipitation variability can be observed within some longer events, such as DO 20 and 14 (Fig. 3). However, the timing of the precipitation response to North Atlantic events cannot be independently constrained because the age model of the RP core was mainly based on correlation to NGRIP data48 (Supplement 1). Increased precipitation from the Indian summer monsoon during the interstadials of DO events has been identified in ISM and EAM records constructed using speleothems in northern India21, China15,49 and from Socotra Island50. Sedimentary records from the Arabian Sea indicate the strengthening and weakening of the ISM during the interstadial and stadial phases of DO cycles47. However, the RP record is unique because it exhibits contrasting variability between stadials and interstadials and captures even short-duration events.

Precipitation changes during the LGM and Holocene

Colour reflectance data and XRF-VPCs of the terrestrial FM, and marine CMB and NG sediment cores, show summer monsoon precipitation variations during the last 30 ka (Fig. 4). Although there are small offsets in timing between events recorded in the CMB and FM sediment cores, they are within the uncertainties of the age models. The interstadials of DO events 4 and 2 are represented by short peaks at ~ 29 and ~ 23 ka in the FM a* and XRF-VPC1TERR records (Fig. 4). They indicate enhanced precipitation during these intervals. The CMB L* record illustrates a short peak at interstadial DO 2, indicating possible enhanced productivity due to the intensified monsoon (Fig. 4). Correlating with regional and local monsoon records10,15,37,48,52 the FM a* and XRF-VPC1TERR and XRF VPC1 CMB records indicate that precipitation was low during the last glacial maximum (LGM, 26–19 ka), except during the warm interval of DO 2 (Fig. 4). The CMB core L* record also shows low productivity during the LGM.

At the end of the LGM, precipitation and productivity gradually increased from ~18 ka to 8 ka BP. However, there was a clear pause in the monsoon-driven precipitation, and productivity increased at around ~16-15 ka in the CMB core. A pause in precipitation increase was also recorded at ~ 16 ka in the FM sediment core (Fig. 4). Heinrich event 1 (H1) in the North Atlantic is recorded between 17.1 and 14.3 ka53. Therefore, the pause in the increase of precipitation and productivity may represent the monsoon response to the cold H1 stadial event in the North Atlantic. The H1 stadial paused the post-LGM ISM precipitation increase at around 16 ka in Mawmluh Cave, Meghalaya52,54, Hulu Cave51, Arabian Sea47, Yellow River delta55, and Huguang Maar Lake56 as well. The Cariaco basin in the tropical Atlantic also records H1 as a period of weak precipitation and a southward shift in the ITCZ57. During the subsequent Bølling–Allerød (B-A) warming period, precipitation in FM, CMB, and productivity in the western continental slope recovered, but no significant peak can be identified (Fig. 4). The regional records cited above also show a recovery from the precipitation minima that occurred during H1 and an increasing precipitation trend during the B-A that followed. The Antarctic cold reversal (ACR) (14.7–13 ka), which coincides with the BA event, shows contrasting signals between the south pole and high northern latitudes58. Reduction of moisture supply due to cooling the southern Indian Ocean during the ACR may be responsible for the lack of significant intensification in ISM related precipitation during the B-A. Although both the FM and CMB cores show a clear decrease in precipitation after the B-A event (11–13 ka), export productivity does not show a clear decrease (Fig. 4). Within the age uncertainties of the CMB and FM cores, this decrease in precipitation correlates well with the Younger Dryas cooling event. The NG XRF–VPC1NG (Si, Ti, Mn) and a* records indicate reduced terrigenous flux immediately after 10 ka. When considering the age uncertainty (± ~ 470 yrs), this low terrigenous flux at the bottom of the records may represent the end of the Younger Dryas cold event, during which rainfall would have been weakened. The FM, CMB, and NG cores show increased precipitation from 10 to 5 ka. L* of the NG core shows that export productivity also increased during this period, indicating the strengthening of the monsoon associated with this increased precipitation. Peak precipitation is reported between 10 and 8 ka, coincident with the northern hemisphere hypsythermal or climactic optimum. The summer monsoon records from Hoti and Quanf caves in Oman22, Sri Lanka37, east Asia48, the Arabian Sea8,59, and southeast Asia60 also record maximum summer monsoon precipitation from 10 to 8 ka. An abrupt decrease in precipitation is shown in the NG records and FM XRF-VPC1TERR at ~ 8.0 ka. This decrease coincides with the 8.2 cooling event, recorded in northern high-latitude areas61 and a decreased precipitation event in the Oman caves22. However, the FM reflectance record does not exhibit a significant precipitation drop during the 8.2 event. Similarly, some Indian subcontinental terrestrial sediment records do not show this event, although most marine and speleothem records do. The FM and NG records show that precipitation decreased significantly after ~ 5 ka. A pollen-based study at the Horton Plains upper montane rainforest of Sri Lanka, which is situated a few tens of kilometers east of the Frogmore site, also reported increasing aridity after 8 ka and arid conditions from 5.5 to 3.5 ka37. A widespread decrease in monsoonal precipitation during the late Holocene was common in many parts of Asia, including the core monsoon zone (CMZ) of peninsular India62, northeast India63, Oman22, east Asia51,49, Arabian sea11,47,64, and southwest Asia60. A southward shift of the ITCZ due to the gradual decrease of solar insolation in the northern hemisphere could have resulted in decreased precipitation in the region. A significant precipitation decrease was observed in both the FM and NG records around ~ 4 ka. This would have caused widespread droughts because this rapid precipitation decrease occurred after a gradual decrease in precipitation over the previous 4000 years. Within age uncertainties, this arid period coincides with the 4.2 ka dry event that was recorded in many parts of peninsular India65–68.

What caused precipitation changes?

When we analyze the frequencies and phase relationships of solar insolation variability with the RP and FM precipitation records, a significant shift in the precipitation source can be identified. The precipitation and insolation relationship during the last 80 ka is summarised in Supplement 4. At present, the Ratnapura region receives about 37% of its annual rainfall during the inter-monsoonal months (1463 mm from Mar–Apr to Oct–Nov) and about 50% during the summer monsoon period (1973 mm)32. So, it is important to understand whether the increased precipitation during the warm MIS periods could be due to an enhanced ISM only, or to both ISM and intermonsoon enhancement. During warm MIS periods, which coincide with the precession cycle of northern hemisphere solar insolation, increased boreal summer solar heating enhanced the thermal contrast between north and south, and migration of the ITCZ northward intensified the ISM10,21,69–71. Convective rainfall associated with ITCZ migration also shows similar variability elsewhere in the tropics72,73. Precipitation maxima during the warmer MIS1, 3, and 5 periods, therefore, could be due to the strengthening of both ISM and intermonsoon convective precipitation. Convective rainfall received in Borneo due to the ITCZ migration is in phase with equatorial (0°) October insolation variability, whereas EASM rainfall variability in Hulu Cave coincides with 33°N Jun-Aug variability51,74 (Fig. 3). When we analyze the precipitation records from the RP and FM cores, a change in the phasing solar insolation curve can be identified, suggesting a change in the dominant rainfall period for the area. It is clear that precipitation is in-phase (r = 0.54, p = 0.001) with 0° October insolation during 77–40 ka (MIS3-5), identifying the second intermonsoonal rainfall as the dominant rainfall mechanism during this interval (Fig. 5). This could be due to increased cyclonic activity from warm SSTs, and the influence of extreme ITCZ migration at this time. However, between 29 and 13 ka (MIS2), the precipitation record goes out of phase with the 0° October insolation curve and coincides with 0° April insolation, suggesting enhancement of the first intermonsoon (r = 0.76, p = 0.001) (Fig. 5). This shift in timing is too great to be explained by uncertainty in the age model. During the cold LGM period (MIS2), decreased cyclonic activity due to colder ocean temperatures could have made the first intermonsoon the dominant rainfall source. The a* record is in phase (r = 0.94, p < 0.00) with 30°N insolation after 13 ka (Fig. 5). This result indicates that the ISM became the dominant rainfall mechanism during the Holocene as a result of increasing solar insolation due to the occurrence of perihelion at the time of the northern hemisphere summer solstice. Such a result indicates that changes in solar insolation due to precession decide the dominant rainfall mechanism in the region rather than a constant ISM domination. As a result, the highest rainfall season of the year has changed during this last 80 ka period. Although different rainfall mechanisms dominate during each season, convection can occur throughout the year. Similar modern variations have been observed due to global climate change. A decreasing trend in ISM and the first intermonsoon rainfall in the wet zone of Sri Lanka where this study was mainly focused, and an islandwide increase in rainfall by extreme events, mostly due to depression and tropical cyclones during the second intermonsoon have been observed in the last few decades30,31,75,76. The last 30 years' climatology of the sites shown in Fig. 1B also provides evidence for this current trend.

Such changes in intermonsoon rainfall due to ITCZ variability may explain the observed sensitivity of the Sri Lankan records to millennial-scale events relative to other records in the region. In addition to solar insolation variability, changes in temperature gradients during millennial-scale climate events such as DO events also help to move the ITCZ northward, resulting in warmer boreal extratropics14,77. It was observed that convective rainfall in the Cariaco Basin and at Socotra island was directly affected by the migration of the ITCZ during stadial and interstadial DO events due to these northern hemisphere temperature changes56,78,79. The ISM also increases during such northward ITCZ movements14. The correlation of the increasing trend of summer monsoonal rainfall in the southwestern wet zone of Sri Lanka80 and the northward shift of the ITCZ since 200281 is a modern analog for DO interstadial ISM rainfall in Sri Lanka and varying ITCZ position. Increased precipitation around Sri Lanka has been observed at modern extreme northward ITCZ shifts during austral spring (Sep-Nov, i.e., second intermonsoon82). Therefore, strengthening second intermonsoon rainfall due to the northward shift of the ITCZ could be the main contributing factor to increased rainfall for DO interstadial events during the 40–77 ka period. Increased ISM rainfall during DO interstadials is indicated in Hulu Cave and the northern Indian speleothem and Arabian Sea records10,51,52,78. Therefore, this combined effect of monsoon and intermonsoon rainfall changes driven by variations in ITCZ position may enhance the variability between the stadial and interstadial phases of the DO events, making them distinct in the RP record in comparison to many other northern Indian terrestrial and marine-based records. Given the projections of high-latitude anthropogenic warming in the future, despite the decrease in precession-driven modern June and October insolation, these results suggest that not only ISM but also intermonsoon rainfall will increase and that the timing of the latter may be shifted toward the second intermonsoon.

Methods

Sediment sampling

This study was based on two marine and two terrestrial sediment cores recovered from southwestern Sri Lanka, which receive rainfall mainly from the Indian summer monsoon and ITCZ migration during the first and second intermonsoon. Two marine cores (Fig. 1A) were obtained beyond the continental slope off Colombo (CMB: 6° 50.233′ N, 79° 32.776′ E) and on the continental shelf off Negombo (NG: 7°.7.5117 N, 79°.45.7964 E). The Colombo coring site is situated on a low-angle continental slope with undisturbed, inclined sediment strata extending from the shelf, and a 1.76 m long sediment core (CMB) was extracted at a water depth of 1122 m. This area receives siliciclastic terrestrial sediment from the shelf and biogenic material from abundant phytoplankton during the summer monsoonal high productivity period. A 3.9 m long sediment core (NG) was extracted from ~ 50 m water depth at the Negombo site, which receives terrestrial sediment discharge from the Kelani River, draining from the wet zone.

Terrestrial sediment cores were collected from the Ratnapura sedimentary basin and Frogmore peneplain. The Ratnapura sediment core (RP) was extracted from a small isolated basin surrounded by small hillocks. A 40.22 m long continuous terrestrial core was obtained using a hammer coring method. Because gem mining has been carried out in the area for decades, the top 17 m is disturbed. The exact undisturbed boundary was identified by careful observation of sedimentological properties. The Frogmore Peneplain is a grassland situated in the upper catchment of the Kelani River at an elevation of about 1830 m. It is covered with C4 grass species such as Chrysopogon zeylanicus and Garnotia exaristata. A 2.04 m long sediment core (FM) was extracted by hammer coring.

The Ratnapura, Frogmore and Negombo areas belong to the wet zone of Sri Lanka. Monthly climatology data (100 yrs–1881–1981) from nearby stations (Ratnapura, Nuwara Eliya, Colombo) show that all these areas received > 200 mm mean monthly rainfall from April–Dec from ISM and both intermonsoons. The mean monthly 100 yr precipitation variability among all three areas shows a significant correlation (Pearson r > .5 at sig. 1-tailed P = 0.05), justifying the combining data from the 03 locations for this study. Climatology of the last three decades shows an increase in 2nd intermonsoon and a decrease in ISM (Fig. 1B).

Chemical composition and color measurements

All the cores were split and carefully logged. Along the center of the long axis of all the cores, colour reflectance (360–740 nm) was measured at 1 cm resolution using a Minolta 2600D colour spectrophotometer, and elemental composition was measured at the same resolution using a Bruker handheld X-ray fluorescence spectrophotometer (XRF). Standard quality control methods were adapted for measuring color reflectance and XRF. Chemical composition of representative sections of sediment cores was measured three times during 03 three-month period to check the influence of water content, and no significant change in data variability was observed over time. The close similarity of the down-core variability of independent color reflectance and XRF data in marine cores also suggests that there is a minimum influence of grain size and water content on XRF data.

Statistical analysis

Terrestrial XRF data were normalized and combined, considering the common lithological characteristics of the Ratnpaura and Frogmore areas. However, the Negombo and Colombo sites were treated separately due to the differences in oceanographic conditions. Single point peaks > 3σ were removed. Varimax-rotated principal component analysis (VPCA) was performed using the SPSS statistical software package after examining the suitability of the data sets using Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin Measure of Sampling Adequacy and Bartlett's Test of Sphericity. Components having eigenvalues > 1 were extracted. Solar insolation data at 0° Oct, 0° Apr, and 30°N June were used to understand the relationship between insolation and precipitation, considering intermonsoonal and ISM rainfall and the timing and location of influencing precessional solar insolation bands74,83–85. DSR a* data were interpolated to 100 yr resolution (Interp a*), and Pearson product correlations were calculated between Interp a* and 0° Oct, 0° Apr, and 30°N June insolation for 40 to 80 ka, 12.6–29.3 ka, and 0–12.6 ka periods separately to understand the linear relationship between insolation and precipitation.

Age models

A charcoal sample in RP (RP 1063) extracted from 1063 cm depth yielded a 14C age of 41434 ± 3041 cal yrs BP. Six bulk organic sediment samples obtained at 1628 cm, 1740 cm, 2273 cm, 3035 cm, 3383 cm, and 3721 cm depths from the RP core yielded ages > 45 ka by AMS 14C dating (Supplement 1). Therefore, the a* data measured at 1 cm resolution were correlated with NGRIP (GICC05) ice core δ18O data48 to construct an age model that was fixed to the topmost 14C age of RP1063. The 25 m long, 1 cm resolution a* record shows a substantial peak-to-peak match with the NGRIP δ18O record, yielding an age model spanning from 43 to 77 ka (Supplement 2). Therefore, this sediment core produces high-resolution records for colour reflectance a* (resolution 13 yrs), elemental composition, organic matter, and δ13C (65 yrs). Micro-mollusk samples picked from the CMB (four samples) and NG (four samples) sediment cores were dated using AMS 14C or gas bench methods, while bulk sediment samples from the Frogmore core (4 samples) were dated using AMS 14C to construct age models (Supplement 1). AMS 14C and gas ion source dating were completed at the DirectAMS and NOSAMS laboratories. Calibrated radiocarbon age models were constructed for FM, CMB, and NG cores using the R-based WinBacon 2.2 software86 (Supplement 2).
