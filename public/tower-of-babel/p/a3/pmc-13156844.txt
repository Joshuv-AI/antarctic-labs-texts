Provider and caregiver perspectives on vision services for childhood cancer survivors: a qualitative study
Authors: Asha Parvathaneni, Luke Zabotka, Jacob Strelnikov, Allison J. L’Hotta, Matthew J. Ehrhardt, Maria Montoya, Kyathi Thallapureddy, Robert J. Hayashi, Margaret Reynolds, Sara Malone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study qualitatively explored barriers and facilitators to accessing supportive vision services for childhood cancer survivors with vision deficits and identified current service-delivery contexts from the perspectives of community professionals and caregivers.MethodsParticipants included caregivers and professionals who completed semi-structured interviews. Provider questions focused on patient identification and assessment, follow-up protocols, and available resources, while caregiver questions addressed navigating survivorship care, academic needs, and goals for their children. Transcripts were thematically analyzed following inductive and deductive coding.ResultsSix caregivers and 10 professionals completed interviews. Thematic analysis resulted in three contexts for barriers and facilitators: school settings, community organizations, and healthcare settings. Obstacles children and their families may face when navigating the care system include service eligibility, financial barriers, transportation barriers, and geographical barriers. Caregivers described their care navigation experience through the same three contexts.ConclusionChildhood cancer survivors with retinoblastoma face substantial barriers to vision-related support across school, community, and clinical settings. This includes limited access to specialists, inequitable access to resources, and fragmented referral pathways. This study highlights the need for care coordination among healthcare providers, educators, and community agencies to improve functional, academic, and quality-of-life outcomes for this population.Implications for cancer survivorsThe high survival rate of retinoblastoma leaves a population with vision deficits, yet there remains a lack of integration of interventions for this population to address such deficits. Understanding barriers to vision care in survivorship can inform where to implement future interventions to increase this population’s health service utilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14455-9.

Introduction

Modern advancements in retinoblastoma treatment have dramatically improved survival rates [1]. While retinoblastoma, a cancer of the eye, is relatively rare, impacting only 1 in 18,000 live births per year, survival rates in the United States are estimated to be 95–97% [2, 3]. Increased survival necessitates a need to address long-term secondary health outcomes, as childhood cancer survivors represent a growing population [4, 5]. The Children’s Oncology Group (COG) and other organizations have emphasized the importance of screening for and treating secondary morbidities in all childhood cancer survivors [6, 7].

Visual impairment is a prominent secondary morbidity among retinoblastoma survivors, comparable to that seen in other head and neck cancers [8, 9]. Approximately 22% of all childhood cancer survivors develop auditory or visual deficits within a decade of treatment, with vision loss particularly prevalent after head or ocular therapies [10, 11]. These deficits often remain unrecognized, despite their significant impact on quality of life and long-term socioeconomic outcomes, including increased rates of poverty and unemployment [10–13]. Among children with retinoblastoma, up to 10% have bilateral visual acuity worse than 20/40, and nearly one-quarter experience academic difficulties [14]. Objective testing reveals universal abnormalities in saccades and contrast sensitivity [2], and studies document associated deficits in reading, visuomotor integration, and activities of daily living, alongside reduced eye-related quality of life [15–18]. Occupational therapy (OT) can improve visuomotor and functional outcomes, and educational legislation such as the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) mandates individualized supports for children with visual or developmental challenges [17]. Despite the opportunity for visual supports through services such as OT, the uptake of these services remains unknown. Additionally, barriers to accessing such care remain substantial, highlighting a critical gap in follow-up and the need for systematic vision rehabilitation in this population [18–20].

Although many resources are accessible across different settings, obstacles to care remain. There is insufficient evidence for effective interventions that improve awareness of vision care needs during retinoblastoma survivorship years and that address barriers to care [21]. Barriers to cancer survivorship care have been studied broadly, yet a gap remains in integrating care across school and healthcare settings [22–24]. Even with the known prevalence of visual deficits among childhood cancer survivors, there are not established pathways to support these children and their families as they navigate various care systems across urban and rural settings in a high-income country [23, 25, 26].

To begin addressing this gap, the current study aims to describe how caregivers and service professionals perceive the resources and supports available to childhood cancer survivors with vision loss. Specifically, this qualitative study characterizes how children’s needs are identified, how they access care, and how schools and health settings work together to provide survivorship care. As survival rates improve, more individuals require follow-up survivorship care, and this study informs how to improve care and account for the specific vulnerabilities of this group of patients.

Methods

We conducted a semi-structured interview study with caregivers and practitioners around children with vision needs. Qualitative methods were selected to answer questions about the experiences of various individuals within the setting and to understand why barriers might exist. Individual interviews were chosen to capture personal experiences and potential sensitivities in discussions of children, families, and professionals navigating vision services. We adopted the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research in the description of the methods and the presentation of results [27]. The study received IRB approval at Washington University in St. Louis, and all participants provided consent prior to participating in the study procedures.

Setting, participants, and recruitment

This study was conducted at a midwestern pediatric medical center, with 200 new cancer diagnoses per year, approximately 10 of which are retinoblastoma. The study sample includes children from across the entire state and region, reflecting the medical center’s catchment area. Eligible study participants included two groups: (1) community professionals that provide vision-related services to children, including occupational therapists (OTs), teachers for the visually impaired (TVIs), or other vision program specialists, and (2) caregivers for children previously diagnosed with either retinoblastoma or neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) who completed active treatment. NF1 was included due to similarities in visual needs and potential secondary outcomes of the disease, and patients were purposively sampled for those who had history of vision impairment associated with the diagnosis. All participants were English-speaking.

Caregivers were recruited by phone from participants in a larger study in which children ages 5–16 participated in a reading evaluation. Vision professionals were recruited through snowball sampling, beginning with a local school district providing services for these patients. Informed consent to an in-person or virtual interview was obtained for all participants.

Data collection

A trained interviewer with no involvement in the patient care or with any professional relationship with caregivers or community professionals conducted all semi-structured interviews. Interviews lasted 30–60 min and were audio-recorded. All recordings were transcribed using secure software. A study team member reviewed and corrected each transcription prior to coding. Participants were given the option to complete the interview over the phone, virtually, or in a private clinic room. All professionals chose to complete interviews over Zoom, while all but one caregiver interview was conducted in person. All participants received $50 at the completion of the interview.

The interview guide was developed by the research team, which included experts in cancer survivorship, occupational therapy, and vision care. The guide was reviewed with the advisory board, which included professionals who care for children with vision impairment in a variety of settings. Broadly, the interviews explored caregiver (Additional File 1) and professional experiences (Additional File 2) with vision assessments, accessing follow-up care, referral pathways, and available resources. Caregiver interviews covered rehabilitation and the challenges encountered in accessing care. Questions explored the experience of caregiving for a child with a cancer diagnosis, navigating survivorship care, academic performance, goals for their children, and perceived vision needs. Provider interviews included questions about the processes for identifying children with vision needs, subsequent follow-up protocols for receiving services, perceived facilitators and barriers to care, and the delivery of necessary services.

Data analysis

We conducted thematic content analysis, using both inductive and deductive coding approaches. We used a combined inductive and deductive approach to balance the emergence of novel insights with the systematic examination of themes informed by existing literature and theoretical frameworks. Deductive codes included: vision assessment protocols in and outside school settings, instances of follow-up care, and communication across care settings. The research team (LZ, SM, AL) read transcripts and met to iteratively develop and refine the codebook. Provider and caregiver transcripts were independently coded in NVivo Version 14 by two team members. The coding team met to resolve outstanding conflicts, which were discussed and resolved through team consensus to 100% agreement across all data. The codebook was edited inductively to further classify certain resources and processes, such as individualized education program (IEP) assessments and assistive technologies used in and out of the classroom. Codes were double-coded to indicate the context that was being described for resources, intervention, etc. – this included provider, family/patient, or organization/system levels where appropriate. The research team held a final thematic emergence meeting with a community advisory board to review the developed themes and discuss the findings’ implications. This board was established to support this line of survivorship work and consists of school service professionals, community vision professionals, healthcare clinical team members, and childhood cancer survivors and their caregivers.

Results

Participant demographics

Six caregivers and 10 professionals participated in semi-structured interviews. Caregivers were majority white, non-Hispanic, and held commercial, Medicaid, or Tricare insurance policies. All professionals worked in a major metropolitan region and held a graduate degree. Their level of experience providing services to children with visual impairment ranged from early to mid-career professionals with 5–10 years of experience to late-career professionals with 20 or more years of experience.

Our analysis identified a range of barriers (Table 1) to accessing vision care emerging across three key settings: schools, community agencies, and health care settings. These settings were consistently referenced by participants as the central places shaping children’s ability to receive timely and appropriate vision services, and resources (Fig. 1). To capture the distinct challenges and opportunities within each domain, results are presented thematically by setting, highlighting obstacles to care and strategies or resources that promote access and utilization. While some determinants are similar (e.g. transportation needs, availability), they were described differently depending on the setting in which they were accessed or used. For example, caregivers and educators both described the need for advocacy. However, the act of parental advocacy—maintaining lines of communication with a child’s teachers and medical professionals—was described in a very different manner than educators discussed their role in advocating for students.

School and extra-curricular settings

Professionals identified school-based services as a crucial location in the care continuum, but resources available to assist students varied by the primary work location. Some professionals indicated that students in a school system with dedicated special education services have better access to school-based services than those in rural or underfunded districts. One professional commented, “We are very lucky to be a standalone school district funded by a standalone tax base… we’re lucky enough that [low vision] can be corrected for a lot of our students.” Similarly, students who present with “legal blindness” (i.e. vision acuity less than 20/200) may be referred to dedicated schools for the blind. These dedicated schools require a referral based on a series of functional vision assessments, which typically begin with an eye report from an ophthalmologist within the healthcare system.

Yet, survivors of retinoblastoma or other cancers that affect vision may not require such intensive care approaches. Multiple caregivers reported that their children, post-treatment for Retinoblastoma, have a high quality of life and can participate in school activities, although adaptation is sometimes necessary. Additionally, they described participation in extra-curricular activities that occur both at school and within the community. One caregiver, when referring to participating in outdoor sports with her daughter, stated, “We get to play, like, baseball, or catching ball, or something like that. And I don’t see that being a problem for her because she’s, you know, she adapts to it. But more than anything, I think she’s fine.” Regarding driving and other physical activities, a caregiver mentioned the following regarding her child: “He drives. He has no issues. … Who knew when he started actually losing his vision? Because when you’re a baby, you don’t know how to tell somebody. So, he’s literally lived his whole life with vision in just one eye. But he’s great driver. I mean, he can do everything.” Notably, school professionals have noticed that the service needs of retinoblastoma survivors are not well identified, leading to services not being provided. One mentions, “There are other teachers at school [who] maybe don’t see [retinoblastoma survivorship] as a big deal. And probably don’t recognize and appreciate all that is going on visually and how hard that can be. Like we have a student who I think is 20/60, and he’s the Braille reader. But just because of the fatigue and only having one eye…Just mass amounts of fatigue and headaches, and braille, it’s just easier.” Service allocation depends on access to a series of evaluations conducted by qualified professionals. Yet, participants in that role reported that the lack of qualified professionals makes it difficult to initiate the evaluations needed to begin the process. One describes a critical shortage of staff to perform necessary assessments: “There is a national and international shortage of teachers of the blind and visually impaired.” Another mentioned that, even given educational grants for educators to achieve specialization in vision impairment educational services, the shortage of eligible professionals has grown over time: “You can get your Master’s pretty much paid for because of the federal grants out there because there’s such a shortage, and that was 20 years ago. And there’s even more of a shortage now.”

School systems lacking robust special-education services were described as not having appropriate staff support to perform initial evaluations or provide services, often relying instead on contracted service professionals through community organizations and non-profits. Participants reported occasions when staff shortages led to an inability to provide all services that they believed would be useful for students, including having time to develop paper-based assignments of appropriate print size or guide students in using assistive technologies. Additionally, some staff report having to cover multiple schools and having extra barriers associated with time shortage due to the need to travel between physical spaces.

From the caregiver perspective, most reported advocating for classroom accommodations for their child. One described general education teachers often being “unconcerned” regarding her son’s education needs. However, she reported that her student had vision needs regardless of his ability to perform in the standard classroom setting. When asked about what advice they would give to families navigating the school system with a child who has survived a vision-impacting cancer, they frequently recommend good communication with the school and direct contact with the professional completing the IEP for the student, recognizing that this is a place where caregivers are able to influence student access to services. Caregivers described tensions when certain decisions about their lives, such as moving to a quieter, rural area, resulted in reduced access to qualified professionals to perform vision evaluations and provide services. Children who have consistent access to school vision professionals also have greater access to other financial resources and classroom materials. Additionally, strong relationships between school staff and caregivers result in a shared increase in knowledge of students’ needs and potential resources to best support them.

Community agencies

Other organizations, including non-profit organizations, community agencies, and informal networks, provide services to students and caregivers, but strategic navigation among them is required. One staff member discussed how their organization has unique collaborations with numerous other geographically diverse organizations. Organizations each provide varying services, some dedicated to specific age ranges or regions, particular programming, or transitional services. Coordinating employees, outreach specialists, and contracted vision staff in each organization plays a vital role in bridging gaps to available resources. These specialists have industry knowledge of shared resources across an informal network of professionals in each system a child navigates. One staff member noted that staff experience working with various agencies is often dependent on experience: “Sometimes, if you have a seasoned specialist, they are familiar with the organizations to get resources through, but a newer person might not understand. Or, if a kid comes to our program…We can present the [resources] to them, and say, ‘hey, you can get these resources through this particular organization, but we can’t make them.’” Access to different service professionals varies by location and community availability, with one organization even having an ophthalmologist available to perform eye evaluations. Once connected to organizations outside of healthcare and school, families can receive a variety of technologies that may assist with classroom learning, such as magnification devices. When provided through such organizations, the assistive devices can be used in various settings, not limited to location as in the school setting.

To meet certain needs, caregivers reported navigating these organizations after learning about them in the healthcare setting. Professionals in the healthcare setting, such as occupational therapists who provide outpatient services, often refer to these organizations for additional support, but follow-through on referrals still depends on parental advocacy. After hearing about an organization that provides occupational therapy, one caregiver mentioned, “I believe I contacted them first, but they required a referral.” Caregivers noted that they also used informal networks through social media groups for survivors of retinoblastoma to connect with other families who have used existing resources. Many caregivers reported using social media groups as a primary source of networking.

Provider participants mentioned that greater communication across settings is needed for organizations to form a more cohesive network of care. Specifically, professionals identified understanding how to improve cross-sector notifications and transitions as an opportunity for improvement, such as in other sectors: “There is an agency called [redacted]. If a child is born in any Missouri hospital with suspected hearing loss, they get a referral for early hearing detection. We would love that same referral [for vision] and a couple of states have been able to implement it.” Consistent with the described need for high-quality communication across teams when providing children’s vision services, organizations with more multidisciplinary service professionals were better able to support caregivers with navigating resources. However, awareness remained that a single resource for comprehensive service provision is still lacking.

Moreover, from a provider’s perspective, transportation to and from service organizations was frequently cited as a barrier to families receiving care. Even in the cases where students meet qualifications for transportation assistance, it was described as challenging to use. To address this need, one provider described an example of a mobile clinic for rural access, which would require a shared understanding of availability and awareness of the clinic. Some professionals described distress due to the awareness of these barriers without the ability to intervene or help families navigate access to community settings.

Healthcare setting

When discussing interaction with the healthcare setting, most caregivers primarily reflected on their experience navigating it during diagnosis and active treatment. Several parallels to themes during survivorship emerged, including their description of complexities in booking appointments, financial barriers, and insurance. When asked specifically about care during survivorship, caregivers described attending annual visits to late effects clinics. Participants emphasized the importance of attending these follow-up appointments, with caregivers reporting few obstacles. Notably, it was frequently mentioned that, beyond follow-up appointments at the late effects clinic, their children do not need formal health services, despite reporting frequent issues with depth perception. Reflecting on this, a caregiver described: “Maybe OT could have helped. There’s still depth perception problems.” However, some caregivers, even after receiving a referral for OT, reported that the service was not delivered in a health setting. Instead, families reported receiving OT from external community organizations or through the school. One occupational therapist described the opportunity for occupational therapy to provide services, including care for survivors in clinics, during home visits, and within schools to assist with classroom accommodations. Again, staff emphasized how these transitions and services require frequent communication.

Discussion

This study revealed important barriers to accessing vision care in three contexts: the school, across community agencies, and in the clinic. Patients faced significant barriers in obtaining functional visual assessments, and parents described patients’ needs for assistance in the school setting due to noticeable deficits after treatment. The study underscores the difficulties patients and families face in navigating resources and obtaining care, even after needs have been identified, highlighting critical gaps in coordinated support that must be addressed to improve outcomes.

Caregivers and professionals highlighted substantial challenges related to visual impairment, access to vision-specific educational resources, and inequities in service availability. The lack of TVIs, limited OT access, and complex referral pathways emerged as major impediments to adequate school support. Survivors frequently experienced difficulties in academic performance and independence in daily activities, underscoring the need for early functional visual assessments and tailored accommodations. Despite these challenges, caregivers expressed overall satisfaction with the medical care received and emphasized the importance of continued multidisciplinary and community-based support throughout survivorship.

Our findings that patients need adaptation and vision resources align closely with previous literature describing decreased reading performance, slower reading speeds, and lower critical print size among school-age retinoblastoma survivors [15]. Prior studies have similarly associated systemic chemotherapy with cognitive impairment and increased need for individualized educational plans [14, 28]. Consistent with earlier work, the present study reinforces the role of social determinants of health, such as financial constraints, limited transportation, and educational inequities in influencing survivors’ access to care [29–33]. Collectively, these findings extend prior research by underscoring the interplay among clinical care, educational systems, and community organizations in shaping survivor outcomes. Specifically, there will need to be standardized coordination and communication to align the potentially fragmented systems between healthcare, school, and community contexts.

Several limitations should be noted. First, retinoblastoma is a rare disease, but we believe our approach to recruitment and qualitative analysis provides insights that could inform other areas of vision screening and treatment. Second, the study was conducted within specific school districts and regional healthcare systems, potentially constraining the applicability of findings to other educational or healthcare settings. Third, caregiver reports may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. This was mitigated by interviews being conducted by a team member not affiliated with the care team. Finally, variations in treatment modality and timing among participants may have contributed to heterogeneity in functional and cognitive outcomes.

Notwithstanding these limitations, this study underscores the urgent need for system-level interventions to improve care coordination and equity in educational access for childhood cancer survivors. Strengthening multidisciplinary communication among healthcare professionals, educators, and families; increasing funding for TVIs and adaptive technologies; and simplifying referral pathways could significantly enhance both academic performance and quality of life. Future research should explore implementation strategies for integrated care models and longitudinally assess their impact on functional, psychosocial, and educational outcomes. By addressing gaps in service access and communication, the survivorship care continuum can more effectively support the long-term development and independence of retinoblastoma survivors.
