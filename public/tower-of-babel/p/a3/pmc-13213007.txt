Novel Raman signature evidences an unknown cyanobacterial pigment
Authors: Yannick J Lara, Alexandre Lambion, Emmanuelle J Javaux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrom the first botanical characterization of cyanobacteria about two centuries ago to contemporary studies, only two categories of extracellular UV-screening pigments have been considered: scytonemin which is very well described and gloeocapsin, which remains enigmatic. In this study, we use Raman microspectroscopy to compare the extracellular EPS-associated pigment produced by the strain Chamaesiphon polonicus SAG 32.87 with gloeocapsins, scytonemins and other natural and synthetic pigments. Chemometrics analyses of Raman data reveal the existence of a third and new category of cyanobacterial extracellular pigment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46521-x.Keywords: Microbial pigments, Cyanobacteria, Exopolymeric substance (EPS), Raman microspectroscopySubject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

From the emergence of their oxygenic photosynthetic ancestor – probably during the early Precambrian1– cyanobacteria colonized a wide range of habitats that include rock surfaces, intertidal zones, open waters, equatorial deserts and polar ecosystems among others2,3. Due to the absence of a protective ozone layer in the early atmosphere, they may have developed different strategies to survive lethal UV radiations4,5. One of these strategies consists in the biosynthesis of molecules that contain UV-absorbing function(s) and that are stored in the cell or exported through the cell wall and condensed in the extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) envelope.

Scytonemin and gloeocapsin are the only cyanobacterial extracellular (UV-screening) pigments reported so far. Both molecules represent promising biosignature candidates for the search of early traces of cyanobacteria, if they cannot be formed abiotically and are unique to this clade5,6. Indeed, their preservation in fossil cell-like or sheath-like shape or other morphological traces of life in a suitable environmental context would permit to accurately identify microfossils even when they have simple morphologies. Indeed, the evidence of their occurrence in the fossil record when associated with cell-like or sheath-like shape or other morphological traces of life and preserved in a suitable environmental context permit to accurately identify microfossils even when they have simple morphologies5,7.

Across the cyanobacterial phylogeny, the biofilm-forming genus Chamaesiphon (Gomontiellales)8 was first described by Alexander Braun in 18649. It is characterized by a thin sheath (also called pseudovagina) that can be slightly lamellate and colorless or yellowish or brownish, in which a mother cell divides into smaller cells called exocytes that are expelled by the sheath apex10. Colonies of the Chamaesiphon genus are abundant in mountain rivers where they form conspicuous colorful biofilms on rocky surfaces, but can also be found as epiphytes on algae, and as biofilms on rocky surfaces from rainforest streams11,12, from the Antarctic continent13,14, to the arctic biosphere15. Except for one strain (C. incrustans N°1036), 16S rRNA gene sequences phylogeny analysis suggests that all strains of the genus had a common ancestor16. The production of UV-screening pigment by Chamaesiphon spp. was investigated by UV-HPLC in three morphospecies sampled in the Alps from two shaded and exposed sites with different geochemistries and altitudes17. Absorption spectra analyses suggested the presence of scytonemin in C. starmachii and C. geilteri whereas the identification of the pigment produced by C. polonicus was more enigmatic and somehow seemed more related to a carotenoid17. More recently, Velichko and colleagues18 characterized a new strain isolated from a dark crimson (brownish-red) forest spring biofilm. While characterizing their new isolates, the authors highlighted a complementary chromatic adaptation under low light leading to the dark crimson phenotype observed in that environment and which is mostly related to the cell phycobilisome composition18.

Raman spectroscopy is a non-destructive technique that allows the characterization of molecules based on their vibrational behavior under monochromatic excitation19. High resolution Raman spectroscopy performed at the micron scale allows to differentiate microbial pigments at a cellular level5,20–24 and to characterize their cellular distribution. Recently, while investigating microbial epilithic and endolithic communities from polish gypsum, Edwards and colleagues25 were able to isolate the Raman signal of scytonin for the first time, enabling the extension of scytonemin family signatures, showing that Raman spectroscopy is a useful method to study the still poorly known molecular diversity of cyanobacterial UV-screening pigments.

In this study, we used a Raman microspectroscopy approach to investigate the Raman signature of the extracellular pigment produced by C. polonicus SAG 32.87. Its comparison with the Raman signatures of cyanobacterial, microbial and artificially produced pigments strongly suggests the existence of an unknown family of cyanobacterial extracellular pigments, strengthening the importance for further exploration of the diverse molecular strategies developed by cyanobacteria for photoprotection. In addition to the interest for fundamental and applied microbiology, such pigments might also provide new and potentially unique taxonomic signatures useful in paleobiology and astrobiology.

Results

Morphological characterization of Chamaesiphon polonicus SAG 32.87

The cyanobacterial strain SAG 32.87 morphology corresponds to the description of Chamaesiphon polonicus10. Cells are spherical, oblong and sometimes appear with a pear or skittle-shape (Fig. 1c,f). They are solitary, pale blue-green and may contain large granules (Fig. 1f). Cells may be surrounded by a mucilaginous concentric, firm, pale orange to brown-colored multilayered envelope called pseudovagina or EPS sheath (Fig. 1). Exocytes released by division of the mother cell are observed (Fig. 1c). The orange-brownish pigment is never observed inside the cells, but in the EPS sheath.

Characterization of extracellular pigment using both single point and Raman mapping

To characterize the Raman signature of the enigmatic pigment produced by Chamaesiphon polonicus SAG 32.87, we first performed single point Raman microspectroscopy on in vivo cells with EPS sheath and EDTA treated cell-free EPS sheath using 514 nm light laser (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S1). Fifteen Raman bands were observed and assigned based on literature (Table 1). Bands with strongest intensities were observed at 1740 cm− 1 assigned to C=O stretching vibration that can be found for cholesterol ester or triglycerides, at 1636 cm− 1 potentially assigned to C–CH, CNH bending or C=C stretching vibration, and at 644–648 cm− 1 potentially assigned to a ring deformation. Two of the strongest and one medium strong band were specific to the main skeletal features of carotenoids ν1,ν2 and ν3, respectively observed at 1519 cm− 1 assigned to C=C stretching, 1155 cm− 1 C–C stretching and 1004 cm− 1 C–CH bending. Four bands with medium strong intensities were potentially interpreted as a C–H bending or indole ring vibration at 1490 cm− 1, as quadrant C–N–C at 1407 cm− 1, C–C ring or indole ring at 1330 cm− 1, as C–H bending at 970 cm− 1. Additional less intense bands were observed at 1550 cm− 1 that can be assigned to potential C–CH aromatic ring stretch, and at 1435 cm− 1 and 1225 cm− 1, 1195 cm− 1, which vibrations may be related to C–N bonds.

Comparison of the Raman signature of the enigmatic pigment with known natural and synthetic pigments signatures using multivariate statistics

Raman signatures of twelve natural and two synthetic pigments that are or can be produced in the environment by microorganisms were investigated using a green laser (514 nm for coumarin, gloeocapsin, pyocyanin, quercetin, scytonemin and violacein), an orange laser (633 nm for anthraquinone) or a red laser (785 nm for 2-hydromethyl anthraquinone, emodin, emodin-8-glucoside, methyl-amino-anthraquinone, and parietin) when the Raman fluorescence background was too intense to observe actual Raman bands (Fig. 4). Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and hierarchical cluster analyses were performed to classify pigments based on their spectral similarities (Fig. 5a). The NMDS shows that C. polonicus SAG 32.87 pigment signature does not cluster with any other pigments. Generalized additive model fitting generated higher restricted maximum likelihood (REML) scores for the complexity computed index (REML = 58.3), the molecular weight (REML = 51.1) and the topological polar surface area (REML = 46.7) (Supplementary Table S1). In contrast, significant Pearson correlation coefficients were obtained between NMDS axis 1 and topological polar surface area (R2 = 0.83, p-value = 0.002), molecular weight (R2 = 0.80, p-value = 0.003), heavy atom count (R2= 0.79, p-value = 0.003), number of OH function (R2= 0.78, p-value = 0.004), number of C–O bonds (R2 = 0.75, p-value = 0.006), and complexity computed index (R2 = 0.71, p-value = 0.01) (Supplementary Table S2). No significant correlation was found for the NMDS axis 2. Similarities between four pairs of compounds are observed: anthraquinone and 2-hydroxy-methyl-antrhaquinone share nine bands (± 6 cm− 1), emodin and methylamino-anthraquinone share eight bands (± 6 cm− 1), scytonemin signals from Nostoc and S. informe share more than nine bands (± 1 cm− 1), and gloeocapsins from G. alpina and P. nigrescens share seven bands (± 6 cm− 1) (Tables 2 and 3).

Anthraquinone derivatives are organized along the NMDS-1 axis from the simplest form, the anthraquinone, to the most structurally complex form, the emodin-8-glucoside. Gloeocapsins share the same ordinates intervals as the anthraquinone derivatives. Some of the gloeocapsin bands are also common to one of the other analyzed anthraquinone derivatives. All Hierarchical Cluster Analysis (HCA) resulting dendrograms, regardless of the distance index and the clustering methods, grouped the SAG 32.87 Raman signature with the quercetin signature relatively far from the signatures of the other pigments (Fig. 5b,c; Supplementary Fig. S3). Quercetin and SAG 32.87 pigment share five bands (640–648 cm− 1, 1220–1225 cm− 1, 1330 cm− 1, 1400–1407 cm− 1, 1435 cm− 1) which may explain the clustering of the two Raman signatures.

Discussion

To our knowledge, the Raman signatures of C. polonicus extracellular pigment were not characterized before this study. The present analysis shows that the pigment is not related to any of the other prokaryotic pigments elucidated so far and suggests the existence of a third class of cyanobacterial extracellular pigment beside scytonemin (including scytonin) and gloeocapsin.

Although the orange color of the targeted pigment (Fig. 1) as well as the published absorption spectrum suggest that it may belong to the carotenoid group17, the general profile of the Raman spectrum (Fig. 2) obtained during this study seems to exclude this hypothesis.

The overall signature bands suggest some aromaticity of the compound, and several bands seem to be related to the presence of at least one nitrogen atom in the molecular composition of the pigment (Table 1). Besides pigments, EPS envelopes are mainly composed of polysaccharides as well as other compounds such as various proteins, DNA, metals and lipids. This nanoscale molecular heterogeneity may imply strong interactions like hydrogen bonds between the different compounds which may be related to the broadening of Raman bands (Fig. 2). In this study, the spot size of Raman analyses (700 nm) does not allow to isolate molecular species but represents a large surface which may contain various molecules. In addition, a potential amorphous state of the pigment may also explain broadened Raman bands. Four of the bands were only related to a carbon-nitrogen interaction suggesting the presence of a carbon ring containing a nitrogen atom such as an indole group. Comparison of bands wavenumbers with those of already elucidated microbial pigments shows little resemblance to N-containing cyanobacterial compound scytonemin and scytonin, violacein from the Pseudomonadota Chromobacterium violaceum or prodigiosin from Serratia marescens (Table 2; Fig. 4). Interestingly, the targeted compound exhibits a strong band at 1740 cm− 1 that is possibly related to the C=O stretching vibration of ester groups of cholesteryls26, or of a ketone group, both allowing the association of an aromatic backbone together with a side chain. Reported pigment esters in microbes include carotenoid-retinol derivatives, such as astaxanthins or the flexirubin pigments27–29. Their Raman spectra are recognizable by the dominance of ν C=C and ν C–C stretching distinctive of carotenoids29, which is not the case here.

UV-screening pigments are also synthetized by fungi. Orange rubropunctatin and monascorubin, red and orange red sequoiamonascin fungal pigments are produced by Aspergillus parasiticus, Monascus spp., Penicillium spp. or Talaromyces purpureogenus for example30,31. These pigments are polyketides that share a pyrano-quinone bicylic core specific to azaphilone pigments32. Among these compounds, nitrogenated azaphilones can be generated during cultivation in presence of endogenous ammonia or exogenous amino compounds, and some may harbor a side chain linked by a ketone function such as the rubropunctatin33. Cyanobacteria are known to produce secondary metabolites via the polyketide synthase pathway, therefore the azaphilone nature of the target pigment could be speculated. However, to our knowledge, azaphilone biosynthesis has never been reported in cyanobacteria so far or any other bacteria. Future analyses shall include Raman characterization of this class of molecules.

The NMDS classification analysis (Fig. 5a) was performed to visualize similarities between the strain SAG 32.87 novel pigment signature, some reference purified and in vivo bacterial UV-screening pigments and anthraquinone derivative naturally synthetized by lichens or artificially obtained, as well as the two aromatic compounds coumarin and quercetin synthetized by plants. The novel pigment does not seem to cluster with any of the microbial pigments. Despite the limited number of fully characterized molecules present in our dataset, the first NMDS axis exhibited significant relationships with chemical and physical properties of the purified compounds. The best GAM (REML score = 58.3) fit obtained for complexity computed index can be illustrated by the distribution of anthraquinone derivative horizontally arranged along the NMDS-1 axis from the simple anthraquinone to the most structurally complex form, the emodin-8-glucoside (Fig. 5a). Scytonemins from EPS envelopes of Nostoc sp. and S. informe have close location and seem not to agree with GAM fit as shown by a complexity index of purified scytonemin of 1290 (Fig. 5a).

Gloeocapsins are grouped together and quite distant from the other compounds which may be explained by the large width of the bands between 1000 and 1600 cm− 1 potentially evidencing the amorphous nature of the extracted gloeocapsins.

Chemometrics, more precisely hierarchical cluster analysis, has been successfully used previously to differentiate anthocyanins, carotenoid, and flavonols34. Dendrograms resulting from our hierarchical cluster analyses are separating gloeocapsin from the rest of the compounds (Fig. 5b,c). All topologies are regrouping the SAG 32.87 Raman signature with the signature of quercetin. Quercetin has been reported once in chloroform extracts of Anabaena aequalis35, and other flavonoids are known to be produced by cyanobacteria36 but to our knowledge were never described as an extracellular protective pigment.

To date, microbial pigments are known to absorb photonic radiations to perform photosynthesis or other phototrophic processes, to perform photosensitivity reactions, to protect from lethal radiations, to prevent from virulence or predation, or to prevent from oxidative stress37. In the case of C. polonicus, the extracellular distribution of the pigment obviously excludes a photosynthetic role. In nature, Chamaesiphon spp. are found in both streams and eulittoral zone of lakes forming conspicuous biofilms exposed to desiccation, high light and UV-radiations stresses. Therefore, a likely hypothesis is that the extracellular pigment found in C. polonicus SAG 32.87 sheath may play a role in light absorption or UV- screening as in the case of the gloeocapsin and scytonemin pigments5,20,38. The UV-screening properties of environmental C. polonicus extracellular pigment were questioned due to its carotenoid-like absorption spectrum with a maximum at 470 nm and the absence of absorption in the UVA-range17. However, the strong absorbance at 322 nm of SAG 32.87 pigmented cell-free sheath (Supplementary Fig. S4) does suggest a UV protective role of at least one molecule contained in the sheath or by the association of polysaccharide matrix and potential lipopolysaccharide, protein or another molecule that may be present in the sheath. Moreover, it was previously shown that C. polonicus was more resistant to UV exposition than freshwater planktonic taxa and could survive up to 4 h irradiation during laboratory experiments39. Therefore, it is possible that the compound present in C. polonicus SAG 32.87 extracellular envelope/sheath differs from the one isolated from the Isar Spring specimen17 and does play a UV-protective role.

Flavonoids are known to have allelopathic, antibiotic and anti-predatory activities.

Further chromatographic, spectroscopic, mass spectrometry, and NMR analysis will allow to better hypothesize on the ecological role of this enigmatic compound.

Pigments have also critical implications in life evolution because of their role in light harvesting for photosynthesis or phototrophy or to prevent from damages provoked by extreme radiations exposure. Their great diversity in different clades of prokaryotes40 suggests their early emergence and important functions. UV-screening pigments are widespread in the tree of life, although not yet reported in archaea41 in contrast to fungi or cyanobacteria.

The Proterozoic fossil record of cyanobacteria includes abundant microfossils with an identification ranging from possible, to probable, to unambiguous cyanobacteria7,42. Among the unambiguous taxa, the endolithic Eoenthophysalis belcherensis shows the accumulation of organic matter in some regions of its fossilized EPS envelopes resembling scytonemin localization in its modern counterparts43. Previous work has demonstrated that porphyrins derived from cyanobacterial chlorophylls can be preserved within fossil cells for more than 1 billion years (Ga)44 or in 1.1 billion year old fossil oil45, as well as carotenoids derived from anoxygenic photosynthetic bacteria and or cyanobacteria in up to 1.73 Ga bitumen46. Therefore, it is possible that molecular remains of UV-screening pigments may be preserved in the rock record but this remains to be evidenced.

The presence or resemblance of microfossils to Chamaesiphon genera in the geological record is quite rare and may remain ambiguous. Populations of epibionts on possible cyanobacterial filaments harboring morphological resemblance with Chamaesiphon have been described in Mesoproterozoic silicified stromatolites and tentatively identified based on their asymmetric, linearly oriented cell division47. Evidencing the molecular signatures of the pigment specific to this taxon in such a microfossil would remove ambiguities towards its biological affinity.

So far, Raman signatures of UV-screening pigments in the fossil record included carotenoid signatures obtained from various ancient samples of different ages, in halite brine inclusions of 9 Ka to 1.44 Ma borehole cores from three sites in California48, in a Miocene Fe-Rich fossil vents49, in fossil photosynthetic mats from an East Antarctic lake50. Scytonemin signature was recovered from 4530-year-old filaments isolated from an Antarctic lacustrine fossil mat50.

Other features such as the preservation of thylakoid membranes51 or pigments within fossil cells and their particular division pattern and morphology44 are important diagnostic criteria as well but not the topic of this work focused on extracellular UV-screening pigments. The unambiguous interpretation of microfossils would then allow to compute time calibrated phylogenetic trees with higher resolution, providing new insights on the evolution of cyanobacteria, on the evolution of bacteria and chloroplast, on the evolution of their pigment biosynthetic gene clusters as well as genes from the same family5.

In contrast to carotenoids, UV-screening pigments are not necessarily widely distributed in the tree of life and may represent a potential diagnostic taxonomic signature for modern and fossil samples, such as scytonemin for cyanobacteria and possibly gloeocapsin.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified the Raman signature of a yet undescribed extracellular pigment produced the cyanobacterium Chamaesiphon polonicus SAG 32.87. Bands related to the pigment differ from gloeocaspin and scytonemin derivatives. The pigment spectrum was differentiated from fourteen other compounds (including scytonemin and gloeocapsin) using chemometrics analyses. These results suggest the occurrence of a third category of extracellular pigment in the cyanobacterial phylum.

The discovery of this potential third class of extracellular cyanobacterial pigment shows the relevance of Raman signatures as diagnostic tools for probing and discovering new microbial pigments for future ecological, evolutionary and paleobiological studies.

Methods

Cell culture and preparation of cyanobacterial sheath and of pigment standards

The strain Chamaesiphon polonicus SAG 32.87 was purchased at the University of Göttingen culture collection of algae (SAG). SAG 32.87 was grown in BG11 liquid medium at 20 °C under constant illumination for 3 months using a hyperspectral LED bar which covered 400–900 nm from the company EFFILUX (Les Ulis, France).

Emptied sheaths were obtained from grinding dried biomass from old cultures. Sheaths were washed in EDTA to expel potential remaining cells out of the sheath as described by Lepot and colleagues50. Cells and purified sheaths were placed onto glass slides used for analyses.

Ten commercial references of natural and synthetic pigments for which chemical and physical computed properties are available, listed in Supplementary Table S3, were purchased at Merck Life Science BV (Belgium). Two scytonemin spectra were obtained from the analysis of the extracellular sheath associated pigment of Stigonema informe filaments from the specimen CBFS-A34 of the CBFS herbarium of the Botany department of the University of South Bohemia, and from extracellular sheath of an environmental colony of Nostoc commune collected in La Chartreuse park (N50° 37′ 49.468″, E5° 35′ 47.972″) Grivegnée, Belgium.

Raman single spectrum acquisitions and high-resolution Raman spatial analysis

The samples were deposited on ZnSe windows. Raman investigations targeting SAG 32.87 pigments were performed using a Renishaw inVia Raman spectrometer coupled to a Leica DM 2500 confocal microscope equipped with a 100X objective and connected to a RL514C100 green laser source of 514 nm (maximum power = 45 W), with a 1800 L/mm grating and an air cooled (− 70°) 1040 × 256-pixel CCD array detector at the Early Life Traces & Evolution-Astrobiology Lab at ULiege. Single spectrum acquisitions were performed with 514 nm confocal laser with an exposure time of 1 s, 10 accumulations for the pigment before EDTA treatment at a laser power of 5 mW on the sample, and 20 accumulations after EDTA treatment at a laser power of 0.6 mW on the sample.

Raman maps were acquired using the Edge Stream HR mode, at objective 100× with 10% of the maximum power of the 514 nm laser beam, and an exposure time of 2s, and a spot size of 700 nm. Spectra were acquired using Renishaw software WIRE 5.1. The acquired spectral range was from 100 to 2000 cm− 1, with a spectral resolution of 1 cm− 1. Spectral band assignments were determined based on reference listed in Table 1.

Classification analyses

Spectra were processed using R package hyperSpec (version 0.100.2). Baselines were subtracted using polynomial function using least square fitting approach. The conserved spectral region was from 350 to 2000 cm− 1. After normalization by subtracting the minimum intensity and subsequently dividing by the maximum intensity for each spectrum using function normalize01 from hyperspec R package, an Euclidean distance matrix was computed between the fifteen spectra to perform a non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) using metaMDS function from vegan package (version 2.6-10). Chemical and physical computed properties were fitted on the ordination using generalized additive modelling (GAM)52 implemented the function ordisurf as described in53. Best GAM is characterized by higher restricted maximum likelihood (REML) values. The function cor.test from R package stats (version 3.6.2) was used to compute Pearson correlation coefficients and their p-value between chemical properties and computed properties retrieved from PubChem database (molecular weight, complexity index computed used as a proxy estimating how complex is the molecular structure using Bertz/Hendrickson/Ihlenfeldt formula54,55, heavy atom count, topological polar surface area in square Angström) and numbers of atom-atom chemical bonds with NMDS ordinates for elucidated compounds. Distances matrices for hierarchical clustering analyses was calculated with absolute distances, Euclidean distances, and Minkowski distances. Dendrograms were computed using dendrogram function from the R package stats (version 3.6.2) with Ward56, UPGMA and WPGMA methods. Bootstraps and p-value percentage were computed with 10 000 iterations with the function pvclust from pvclust R package.
