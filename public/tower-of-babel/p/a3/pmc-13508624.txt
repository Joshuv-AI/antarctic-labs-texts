Unique Human Intestinal Tuft Cell Features Revealed by Pluripotent Stem Cell-Derived Mini-Guts
Authors: Masaya Tsukamoto, Tomoyuki Kawasaki, Kenji Ishiwata, Hideaki Morita, Akihiro Umezawa, Hidenori Akutsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsIntestinal tuft cells are rare chemosensory epithelial cells critical for mucosal immunity. However, the molecular features of human intestinal tuft cells remain incompletely defined due to limited access to human tissue and known species-specific differences from murine models. We sought to establish a human tuft cell–enriched intestinal organoid system derived from pluripotent stem cells.MethodsHuman pluripotent stem cell–derived intestinal organoids containing epithelial, stromal, and neural components were subjected to Notch pathway inhibition and T helper 2 cytokine stimulation to promote tuft cell differentiation. Induced tuft cells were analyzed by immunofluorescence, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), and single-cell RNA sequencing, followed by integrated transcriptomic comparison with primary human small intestinal tuft cells.ResultsTuft cell–enriched organoids were reproducibly generated from 3 independent human pluripotent stem cell lines. Integrated single-cell transcriptomic analyses demonstrated strong concordance between in vitro-derived and primary human tuft cells. The organoid-derived tuft cells exhibited distinct molecular features, including low and non-enriched interleukin-25 expression and predominant DCLK2 expression, distinguishing them from murine tuft cells. Under defined experimental conditions, murine-reported regulatory mechanisms, including bone morphogenetic protein-dependent negative feedback and goblet cell–mediated lateral inhibition, were not recapitulated.ConclusionWe establish a human pluripotent stem cell–derived intestinal organoid model that enables enrichment and molecular characterization of human tuft cells. This platform provides a human-specific system for elucidating molecular programs governing tuft cell differentiation and epithelial–immune signaling.

Introduction

The intestinal epithelium functions in close coordination with underlying stromal, immune, muscular, and neural compartments to maintain intestinal homeostasis.1 Among epithelial lineages, tuft cells represent a rare chemosensory population that orchestrates type 2 immune responses.2 In murine models, tuft cells detect helminths through chemosensory pathways, including succinate sensing, leading to interleukin (IL)-25 secretion and activation of type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s).3,4 ILC2-derived cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13 drive a feed-forward circuit promoting goblet and tuft cell expansion. In addition, murine tuft cells secrete acetylcholine, which promotes chloride and fluid secretion from epithelial cells into the intestinal lumen.5, 6, 7 Together, these coordinated responses contribute to the “weep and sweep” response for parasite clearance.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10

Although these regulatory circuits are well established in mice, including bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)-dependent negative feedback,11 their conservation in humans has not been experimentally validated. Given documented species-specific differences in epithelial signaling and immune regulation, direct extrapolation from murine models may be insufficient to define human tuft cell biology.

Progress in addressing this question has been limited by the availability of human experimental systems that recapitulate the multicellular intestinal niche. Although human tuft cells can be induced in conventional epithelial-only organoids,12 such systems do not incorporate stromal or neural components that may influence epithelial fate decisions. We previously established human pluripotent stem cell (PSC)–derived intestinal organoids (“mini-guts”) comprising epithelial, mesenchymal, smooth muscle, and rudimentary enteric neural components, thereby recapitulating selected features of the human intestinal microenvironment.13

Here, we leveraged this PSC-derived human intestinal organoid platform, integrated with primary human single-cell transcriptomic datasets, to directly interrogate murine-defined regulatory circuits. Our analyses identified human-specific molecular programs and revealed divergence from established murine feedback mechanisms, thereby redefining the regulatory framework of human intestinal tuft cells.

Materials and Methods

Pluripotent Stem Cell Culture

Three previously established human PSC lines were used in this study: 2 human embryonic stem cell (ESC) lines (SEES-3 and SEES-5) and one human induced PSC (iPSC) line (Edom-iPSC), derived from menstrual blood using a Sendai virus vector. Cells were maintained under feeder-free conditions using StemFlex medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on vitronectin-coated plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Ethics Statement

All experiments involving human PSCs were approved by the institutional review board of the National Center for Child Health and Development (NCCHD), Japan. The human iPSC lines used in this study were previously established from healthy donor cells under approved iPSC research protocols (approval nos. #146 and #518). The use of human iPSC- and ES cell-based experimental systems for gastrointestinal differentiation studies was additionally approved under the project entitled “Studies on Differentiation Across the Gastrointestinal Tract from Human ES Cells” (approval no. NCCHD-ES#232). Written informed consent was obtained from all cell donors in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All human materials were used in compliance with the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects issued by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare and the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology of Japan.

Intestinal Organoid Differentiation

Intestinal organoids were generated using a defined xenogeneic-free differentiation medium. The basal medium, designated as XF(−), consisted of 85% knockout Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium, 15% knockOut Serum Replacement XF (XF-KSR; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mmol/L GlutaMAX-I, penicillin–streptomycin, 1× non-essential amino acids (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and sodium pyruvate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). This basal medium was supplemented with 50 μg/mL L-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany), 10 ng/mL heregulin-1β (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), 200 ng/mL recombinant human insulin-like growth factor-1 (Sigma-Aldrich), and 20 ng/mL human basic fibroblast growth factor (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Undifferentiated hPSCs were plated onto a cell-patterning glass substrate (CytoGraph, Dai Nippon Printing, Tokyo, Japan). The differentiation medium was replaced every 3–4 days. Floating intestinal organoids were collected and maintained in suspension culture dishes (Corning, New York, NY, USA) in the differentiation medium.

Tuft Cell Induction

Intestinal organoids at day 90–150 post-differentiation were subjected to tuft cell induction. Organoids were washed with XF(−) medium and cultured in an induction medium consisting of XF(−) supplemented with 5 μM Notch signal inhibitor N-[N-(3,5-difluorophenacetyl)-L-alanyl]-S-phenylglycine tert-butyl ester (DAPT, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), 40 ng/mL IL-13 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 400 ng/mL IL-4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The medium was replenished every 2–3 days. Organoids were harvested for analysis after 7 days of induction.

Immunofluorescence Analysis

Organoids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (Thermo Fisher Scientific), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Tissue sections were blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin for 30 minutes at 27 °C, followed by incubation with primary antibodies for 14–16 hours at 4 °C. After washing, sections were incubated with the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were acquired using a confocal laser microscope (LSM900; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Quantitative PCR

Intestinal organoids were mechanically fragmented and enzymatically digested with dispase and collagenase type I for 1 hour at 37 °C. Total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Micro kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and genomic DNA removal was performed using DNase (Qiagen). Reverse transcription was performed on 400 ng of total RNA using SuperScriptTM IV VILO Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a QuantStudio 7 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primer sequences are listed in Table.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

Intestinal organoids were cultured in 2 mL of XF(−) basal medium for 24 hours at 37 °C. Culture supernatants were collected, and mucin-2 and IL-25 secretion levels were measured using a mucin-2 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (ab282871; Abcam) or IL-25 ELISA kit (ab272200; Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Single Cell RNA Sequencing

Organoids were digested into single-cell suspensions using dispase, collagenase type I, and TrypLE Express (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Droplet-based single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3 (Rev C; 10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Library quality and fragment size were assessed using a Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sequencing was conducted on a NovaSeq X Plus system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using 150-base pair paired-end reads, targeting a read depth of 30,000 reads per cell.

Data Processing, Data Analysis, and Visualization

Raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) were aligned to the GRCh38 (hg38) human reference genome. The Cell Ranger pipeline (10x Genomics) was used to generate digital gene expression matrices. Downstream analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.0) primarily utilizing the Seurat package. Low-quality cells were filtered based on sample-specific thresholds for each organoid. Cells with low gene counts or high mitochondrial transcript ratios were excluded. The minimum number of genes detected per barcode was set to 700, 1,000, and 850 for the 3 organoids, respectively, while the maximum proportion of mitochondrial UMIs per barcode was set at 60%, 40%, and 40%, respectively. Data were normalized and scaled using the SCTransform and ScaleData functions. Doublets were identified and removed using DoubleFinder. Dimensionality reduction was performed using Principal Component Analysis, and batch effects were corrected. Unsupervised clustering was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions and visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection.

Cell Type Annotation

Epithelial lineage cells were identified as EPCAM- and CDH1-positive. Subpopulations were annotated based on canonical markers: enterocytes (FABP1), transient-amplifying (TA) cells (MKI67, TOP2A), goblet cells (MUC2, SPINK4), and enteroendocrine cells (CHGA, NEUROD1). Cells expressing tuft cell hallmark genes (POU2F3, FYB1, SH2D6, HCK, DCLK2, and PTGS1) were annotated as tuft cells. Mesenchymal lineage cells were identified with VIM and COL1A1 expression. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the FindMarkers function.

For comparative analysis, a publicly available human small intestine (SI) scRNA-seq dataset (GSE185224) was obtained. The h5ad files were converted into Seurat objects for analysis. Due to significant batch effects observed in the original dataset, samples from “Donor 2” were excluded from downstream analysis. DEGs were identified within the POU2F3-expressing tuft cell cluster.

Assessment of Similarities Between In Vivo and In Vitro Tuft Cells

Tuft cell marker genes for both in vivo and in vitro datasets were identified using the FindMarkers function with a false discovery rate cutoff of <0.05. The intersection of these gene sets was determined to assess model fidelity. Gene ontology and Reactome pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the total DEGs for in vivo human SI tuft cells and on the subset of in vitro mini-gut tuft cell DEGs that were shared with the in vivo signature.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For pairwise comparisons, statistical significance was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. For comparisons among multiple groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons was used. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: ∗P < .05.

To establish an experimental platform that more closely recapitulates the human intestinal microenvironment, we generated human intestinal organoids (“mini-guts”) from 1 human iPSC line (Edom-iPSC)16 and 2 human ESC lines, SEES-3 and SEES-5.17 Because these mini-guts contain epithelial components together with stromal and neural elements, we hypothesized that they would support the complex intercellular signaling required for human tuft cell differentiation (Figure 1B).

We next examined the effects of IL-4 and IL-13, T helper 2 (Th2) cytokines that drive tuft cell hyperplasia in murine models through an ILC2-mediated circuit.8,9 In contrast to murine studies, in which IL-13 alone can be sufficient to induce tuft cell expansion, treatment with IL-4 or IL-13 alone did not produce a statistically significant increase in the number of pEGFR-positive tuft cells in human mini-guts, despite a modest upward trend (Figure 1C and D). Because Notch inhibition biases intestinal epithelial progenitors toward secretory lineages, we hypothesized that it may enhance the response to Th2 cytokines. Consistent with this possibility, DAPT treatment in combination with IL-4, or with both IL-4 and IL-13, significantly increased the number of pEGFR-positive tuft cells compared with untreated controls (Figure 1C and D). DAPT-containing conditions similarly increased pEGFR-positive tuft cells in ESC-derived mini-guts (Figure 1E and F). These findings indicate that Notch inhibition enhances tuft cell differentiation in response to Th2 cytokines in human PSC-derived mini-guts under our experimental conditions.

Transmission electron microscopy confirmed that induced cells exhibited ultrastructural features characteristic of mature tuft cells,18 including lateral spinules and prominently elongated apical microvilli (Figure 1G). Tuft cell induction was accompanied by a concomitant increase in goblet cell numbers (approximately 3.0-fold, P < .05) in both iPSC- and ESC-derived mini-guts (Figure 1H and I). Consistently, ELISAs revealed elevated mucin-2 levels in culture supernatants (approximately 4.0-fold increase, P < .05) (Figure 1J), indicating a coordinated expansion of secretory lineages. Together, these findings demonstrate that the combined action of Th2 cytokines and Notch signaling inhibition activates a type 2 immune–associated secretory program in human PSC-derived intestinal mini-guts.

Human Intestinal Tuft Cells Exhibit a Distinct Gene Expression Signature

Having established a tuft cell–enriched organoid model, we next examined the extent to which in vitro-derived tuft cells recapitulate the transcriptional features of native human intestinal tuft cells. To this end, we reanalyzed a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset of the human SI19 (Figure 2A). In contrast to a previous report suggesting heterogeneity within the human tuft cell population,12 our analysis under defined clustering parameters did not resolve major transcriptionally distinct subpopulations among these cells. The apparent lack of tuft cell subclusters in the primary tissue dataset should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect differences in tissue origin, sequencing depth, or analytical workflows and parameter choices, as well as the limited number of tuft cells captured in the dataset. Instead, human tuft cells displayed robust, uniform expression of POU2F3 (Figure 2B–D), a lineage-defining regulator of airway tuft cells.20,21 In addition, we identified FYB1, SH2D6,19 and HCK,12 as consistently expressed components of the human intestinal tuft cell gene signature (Figure 2B–D).Figure 2Transcriptional landscape of human tuft cells in vivo. We next assessed the conservation of the succinate-sensing pathway. While the succinate receptor SUCNR1 was robustly expressed in human tuft cells,22 IL25 transcripts were detected at very low levels and were not enriched in the tuft cell cluster (Figure 2C and D). This apparent dissociation between the chemosensory receptor and its canonical downstream effector in mice suggests that the utilization of downstream effectors differs between human and murine tuft cells. Furthermore, analysis of the doublecortin-like kinase family revealed a distinct isoform usage in human tuft cells. Although DCLK1 is widely regarded as a canonical tuft cell marker in mice,23 its expression was limited to a small subset of human tuft cells. In contrast, DCLK2 was consistently and selectively expressed across the human tuft cell population (Figure 2B–D). These findings identify DCLK2, rather than DCLK1, as a predominant and selective marker of human intestinal tuft cells and highlight fundamental differences in tuft-cell molecular identity between humans and mice. The expression patterns of cyclooxygenase (COX)-1 and COX-2 in human SI tuft cells have remained controversial.14,24,25 In our analysis, PTGS1 transcripts (COX-1) were preferentially enriched in human SI tuft cells, whereas PTGS2 (COX-2) transcripts were not significantly detected within the tuft cell cluster (Figure 2B–D), indicating that human SI tuft cells predominantly express COX-1.

To further characterize the functional properties of human SI tuft cells, we performed gene ontology enrichment analysis. Consistent with previous reports,4,26 this analysis revealed enrichment of pathways involved in leukocyte interactions, implicating tuft cells in modulating immune responses (Figure 2E). In addition, pathways associated with actin filament organization were significantly enriched (Figure 2E), consistent with the prominent microvilli and lateral spinules that define tuft cell morphology.18,27

Pathway enrichment analysis indicated activation of IL-related signaling pathways in tuft cells (Figure 2F), consistent with the established role of Th2-associated cytokines in tuft cell biology28 and with our experimental findings (Figure 1D). Members of the RHO GTPase family, which regulate actin cytoskeletal dynamics, were also prominently represented. Reflecting their established role in host defense,3,4 pathways associated with parasitic infection, including Leishmania infection, were significantly enriched (Figure 2F). Although pathway annotations for parasite-specific responses are limited in current databases, the observed enrichment consists of a role for human tuft cells in sensing and responding to eukaryotic pathogens.

Beyond immune-associated pathways, we observed strong enrichment of G protein–coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling and downstream phosphatidylinositol (PI) metabolites (Figure 2F), consistent with a broader chemosensory function. Notably, gene sets annotated as “platelet activation” were enriched; however, canonical platelet-specific receptors, such as P2Y1, were not detected, indicating that this signal likely reflects shared calcium-dependent signaling modules rather than platelet-related functions. In line with this interpretation, we identified expression of the sweet taste receptor TAS1R3 and its downstream signaling components, including GNAT3, GNB1, and GNG13 (Figure 2G). Downstream GPCR effectors, including PIK3R5, PIK3CG, PLCG2, and small GTPase (VAV1, RAC2, RHOG), were also enriched (Figure 2H), together with genes involved in calcium flux regulation (PLCG2, ITPR2, CALM2, TRPM5) (Figure 2I).

Collectively, these analyses define a core molecular signature of human intestinal tuft cells, comprising POU2F3, FYB1, SH2D6, HCK, SUCNR1, DCLK2, and PTGS1. These genes were subsequently used as primary markers for the identification and characterization of tuft cells within human PSC-derived intestinal mini-guts. In contrast, IL25 was intentionally excluded from this marker panel, not because of technical detection limits but to reflect the distinct regulatory biology of the human intestinal epithelium.

We next assessed the expression of tuft cell-associated markers by immunostaining and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Immunostaining demonstrated the localization of FYB1, POU2F3, HCK, and DCLK2 in both human small intestinal tissue and organoids (Figure 3A). qPCR showed significant upregulation of FYB1 and SH2D6 in tuft cell-enriched organoids derived from both iPSCs and ESCs (Figure 3B and C). To characterize cellular heterogeneity, we performed scRNA-seq on the organoids. Cells were classified into epithelial and mesenchymal populations (Figure 4A and B). Among the mesenchymal populations, we identified a cluster characterized by expression of PDGFRA, F3, and DLL1 (Figure 4C), consistent with subepithelial niche-like cells described in developing human intestinal tissue.29 Epithelial cells were further resolved into 6 clusters, including TA cells, epithelium, enterocyte, goblet cell, enteroendocrine cell, and tuft cell (Figure 4A and B). Tuft cell cluster exhibited distinct expressions of hallmark genes, including POU2F3, FYB1, SH2D6, HCK, SUCNR1, DCLK2, and PTGS1 (Figure 4D and E). Importantly, organoid-derived tuft cells recapitulated key aspects of the cytokine expression profile observed in vivo. We also identified a shared transcriptional regulator between tuft cells and microfold (M) cells. SPIB, which encodes the Spi-B transcription factor (SPI-B), emerged as one of the most highly upregulated genes in organoid-derived tuft cells (Figure 4D). Although SPI-B is a master regulator of M cell differentiation,30 other canonical M cell markers were not detected (Figure 4F). This selective expression pattern was corroborated by analysis of human SI tuft cells (Figure 4G), indicating that SPIB was expressed in human intestinal tuft cells and suggesting a shared developmental node with the M cell lineage, independent of complete M cell differentiation.

Comparative analysis of DEGs revealed that organoid-derived tuft cells shared approximately 45% of significantly upregulated genes under matched analytical thresholds (Figure 4H). Given the sterile, simplified culture conditions of the organoid system, which lack immune and microbial inputs, this level of transcriptomic concordance indicates the conservation of core elements of tuft cell identity. Genes uniquely enriched in the in vitro population may reflect adaptation to culture conditions rather than fundamental lineage divergence.

Despite the absence of immune effectors in the culture medium, functional enrichment analysis indicated that organoid-derived tuft cells retain intrinsic molecular programs associated with responses to parasitic infection (Figure 2E and 4J). In addition, enrichment of arachidonic acid metabolism pathways suggested a capacity for eicosanoid synthesis (Figure 4I and J), consistent with previously reported observations in murine tuft cells.31, 32, 33 With respect to sensory transduction, while broad G protein–related terms varied, specific components of GPCR signaling, PI-mediated pathways, and calcium flux were robustly enriched (Figure 4I–K).

Taken together, these data demonstrate that the human PSC-derived mini-gut model reproduces key molecular features and sensory-associated programs of human intestinal tuft cells, supporting its utility as a physiologically relevant platform for investigating human-specific tuft cell biology.

Human Tuft Cell Hyperplasia Operates Independently of Murine-Canonical Bone Morphogenetic Protein Feedback Loops

In the murine intestine, BMP signaling functions as a negative feedback mechanism that constrains IL-13–driven tuft cell hyperplasia.11 We therefore examined whether a similar regulatory circuit limits tuft cell expansion in the human intestinal epithelium. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that expression of BMP signaling–associated genes, including BMP2, BMP8, ID1, and ID3 was not upregulated following tuft cell induction in both iPSC- and ESC-derived mini-guts (Figure 5A and B). To functionally assess the role of BMP signaling, we treated organoids with the BMP pathway inhibitor DMH1. In contrast to murine models, in which DMH1 enhances tuft cell hyperplasia, DMH1 treatment did not significantly alter tuft cell abundance in the human mini-gut system (Figure 5C and D). We next investigated the contribution of lateral inhibition, another canonical mechanism that restricts secretory lineage density in the intestine. We confirmed that the molecular components required for lateral inhibition were present34: MUC2+ goblet cells expressed high levels of ATOH1 and the Notch ligands DLL1 and DLL4, both in vivo (Figure 5E) and in vitro (Figure 5F). According to the classical lateral inhibition model, these goblet cell-derived ligands would be expected to suppress tuft cell differentiation in adjacent epithelial cells. However, spatial analysis did not support this prediction. Tuft cell frequency did not significantly differ between regions adjacent to goblet cells and non-adjacent regions (Figure 5G), indicating that tuft cell induction in the human epithelium is not measurably constrained by Notch-mediated lateral inhibition.

Together, these results indicate that human tuft cell hyperplasia proceeds without detectable engagement of 2 regulatory mechanisms established in murine models—BMP-dependent negative feedback and Notch-mediated lateral inhibition—under the conditions tested. Instead, human tuft cell expansion is driven by Th2 cytokine stimulation combined with Notch inhibition, without evidence of these canonical constraints, permitting accumulation of secretory epithelial cells within the human intestinal epithelium.

Discussion

Tuft cells have emerged as key sentinels of the intestinal epithelium, coordinating type 2 immune responses. However, a predominant reliance on murine models has left essential gaps in our understanding of human-specific tuft cell signaling and regulatory mechanisms. In this study, we addressed this limitation by establishing a tuft cell–enriched human PSC –derived intestinal organoid model. Using this platform, we demonstrate that several canonical paradigms derived from mouse studies are not fully recapitulated in human tuft cell biology and instead reveal evidence of a human-specific regulatory framework.

A central finding of our study is that human tuft cell differentiation requires the concurrent presence of Th2-associated cytokines and inhibition of Notch signaling. This strict combinatorial requirement contrasts with murine systems, in which IL-13 alone is often sufficient to drive tuft cell hyperplasia. Given that human ILC2s secrete both IL-4 and IL-13,35 this requirement suggests that tuft cell differentiation in humans is tightly gated and may be protected against inadvertent activation by isolated cytokine fluctuations. Such a mechanism may indicate a higher activation threshold for epithelial immune remodeling in the human intestine.

During preparation of this manuscript, a study reported that human tuft cell differentiation can be induced in adult stem cell-derived organoids (enteroids) by Wnt signaling and Th2 cytokines.12 In that system, tuft cells displayed stem cell-like properties and contributed to epithelial regeneration. In contrast, the tuft cell population in our PSC-derived mini-guts did not show clear proliferative features but showed transcriptional enrichment of immune-related pathways, suggesting a distinct transcriptional state. These differences may reflect differences in culture conditions and cellular composition, as enteroids are epithelial-only cultures, whereas our organoids include mesenchymal and neural components.13 Notably, the presence of DLL1-expressing mesenchymal cells raises the possibility that mesenchyme-derived Notch signaling modulates responsiveness to Th2 cytokines and may limit tuft cell differentiation in the absence of Notch inhibition. Because these datasets were generated under different experimental and analytical conditions, direct comparison should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, these observations are consistent with the possibility that human tuft cells exhibit heterogeneous states, analogous to those described in mice, in which tuft-1 cells represent a precursor-like state and tuft-2 cells correspond to chemosensory and immune effector cells.36

In addition, our data further reveal divergence in downstream effector programs between humans and mice. Although expression of the succinate receptor SUCNR1 is conserved, human tuft cells exhibited low and non-enriched expression of IL25, suggesting a relative uncoupling of chemosensory input from the canonical murine effector pathway. In mice, the succinate–IL-25 axis is central to the “weep and sweep” response.8,9 The limited IL-25 induction observed in human tuft cells, even under strong differentiation conditions, suggests that alternative effector mechanisms mediate tuft cell–immune communication in the human intestine. The enrichment of PTGS1 and arachidonic acid metabolism–related pathways raises the possibility that lipid mediators, such as prostaglandins or leukotrienes, serve as functional substitutes for IL-25 in humans. This interpretation underscores the need for caution when extrapolating IL-25–centered therapeutic strategies from murine models to human intestinal disease.

Another defining feature of human tuft cells uncovered in this study is the preferential expression of DCLK2 rather than the canonical murine marker DCLK1. Both proteins share conserved doublecortin-like and kinase domains essential for cytoskeletal dynamics,37 yet are known to exhibit distinct functional properties in other tissues.33 Given the extensive cytoskeletal remodeling required to maintain the characteristic tuft cell morphology,38 we propose that DCLK2 may contribute to the regulation of microvillar architecture in human tuft cells. Consistent with this interpretation, murine nasal tuft-like microvillar cells have been reported to express very low levels of DCLK1,39 whereas DCLK1 expression in the nasal epithelium may also mark other epithelial cell populations. These findings highlight the regional and contextual variability of DCLK1 expression and caution against its use as a universal tuft cell marker. In our human intestinal mini-gut model, DCLK2 may therefore provide a useful complementary marker for identifying tuft cells.

Our study also challenges the assumption that negative feedback mechanisms governing tuft cell density are conserved across species. In murine models, BMP signaling11 and Notch-mediated lateral inhibition40 act to constrain tuft cell expansion. In contrast, we found that neither BMP pathway activity nor lateral inhibition limited tuft cell hyperplasia in human organoids. These observations suggest that, once the differentiation threshold is met, human tuft cell expansion proceeds without engagement of these canonical inhibitory circuits. This regulatory architecture may permit a more rapid or extensive epithelial response to appropriate immune cues in the human intestine.

Finally, we identify SPIB as a shared transcriptional signature between human tuft cells and M cells, independent of complete M cell differentiation. In mice, the vomeronasal receptor Vmn2r26 on tuft cells links chemosensory activation to Spib induction through calcium signaling, leading to the regulation of tuft cell number.33,41 In the absence of this receptor in humans, our data support an alternative sensory axis involving TAS1R3 and downstream G protein– and calcium-dependent signaling components.42, 43, 44 We therefore propose a potential TAS1R3–calcium–SPIB pathway as a human-specific sensory–transcriptional axis in tuft cells, positioning SPIB as a candidate regulator of the human tuft cell lineage rather than an exclusive M cell marker.

Single-cell transcriptomic comparisons between human small intestinal tissue and tuft cell–enriched organoids further emphasize species-specific differences in DCLK family usage and effector programs. Whereas murine tuft cells utilize DCLK1 to regulate COX-2 expression and prostaglandin E2 production,45 human tuft cells preferentially express DCLK2, pointing to divergent regulatory logic at the level of paralog selection. Elucidating the functional consequences of this switch will be an important direction for future studies.

Several limitations of this work should be acknowledged. In particular, the absence of immune cells in the organoid system precludes direct validation of epithelial–immune communication loops. Nevertheless, the human mini-gut model partially recapitulates the multicellular intestinal niche by incorporating epithelial, mesenchymal, smooth muscle, and rudimentary enteric neural components. In addition, its outward-oriented apical surface enables direct access to the luminal compartment. Given that tuft cells function as chemosensory cells that sense luminal stimuli and interact with surrounding cell populations, the mini-gut model provides a tractable platform for investigating cellular interactions relevant to human tuft cell biology.

Conclusion

This study redefines the molecular and regulatory features of human intestinal tuft cells. By moving beyond murine-centered frameworks, we identify key features of human-specific tuft cell biology characterized by DCLK2 expression, limited reliance on IL-25, and a regulatory architecture that appears to operate without detectable engagement of these canonical inhibitory circuits under the conditions tested. These findings provide a refined conceptual basis for understanding human tuft cell function and offer a framework for future translational studies targeting intestinal immune regulation.
