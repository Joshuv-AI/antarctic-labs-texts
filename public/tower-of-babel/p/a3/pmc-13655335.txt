Significance of peritoneal washing cytology as a predictive factor for the long-term benefit of secondary debulking surgery in ovarian cancer
Authors: Hiroaki Nagano, Akimasa Ichinoe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the significance of peritoneal washing cytology (PWC) as a predictive factor of the long-term benefits of secondary debulking surgery (SDS) for ovarian cancer (OC).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the clinical records of patients with OC who underwent SDS for intraperitoneal recurrence (IPR). Various clinical data points were investigated, including the residual tumor size at SDS, PWC results at SDS, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS).ResultsBetween 2007 and 2022, 207 patients with ovarian, fallopian, or peritoneal cancers were treated at our institution. IPR occurred in 49 patients, 20 of whom underwent SDS. This study included 18 patients, excluding 2 patients with squamous cell carcinoma. Complete resection of the SDS was achieved in 15 patients. PWC was negative in 12 patients but positive in 6; ascites was not observed in any patient. IPR occurred in all PWC-positive patients after SDS, although 3 patients had no residual tumors. In contrast, only 4 of the 12 PWC-negative patients (all without residual tumors) experienced IPR during follow-up (median: 78 months), 2 of whom had secondary IPR at SDS. The association between PWC and IPR after SDS was significant (p=0.009, Fisher’s exact test). The PFS and OS after SDS were significantly higher in the PWC-negative group than in the PWC-positive group.ConclusionPWC is a significant predictor of the long-term benefit of SDS in patients with OC and IPR.

INTRODUCTION

Ovarian cancer (OC) is the 8th leading cause of cancer death worldwide [1]. Many patients are at an advanced stage, and over two-thirds of patients with advanced disease experience relapse after initial therapy. Chemotherapy is the mainstay treatment for tumor recurrence; however, recurrent tumors eventually acquire chemoresistance, leading to death in most cases.

In this context, the therapeutic significance of secondary debulking surgery (SDS) for recurrent OC has been debated. Several randomized controlled trials have investigated the therapeutic significance of SDS. Although the conclusions are not entirely consistent, SDS is generally considered to offer therapeutic value to some patients [234].

The detection of malignant cells using peritoneal washing cytology (PWC) provides important prognostic information because their presence is commonly associated with a more advanced disease stage and indicates a poorer prognosis [56]. PWC is a requisite procedure at initial surgery and should be routinely performed for optimal patient staging. Several studies have investigated the prognostic value of PWC in patients who underwent interval debulking surgery (IDS) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy for OC [7891011].

The most prevalent site of recurrence after initial therapy is the intraperitoneal cavity, although it can also metastasize to the lymph nodes or distant organs such as the liver, lungs, and brain [812]. Therefore, many candidates for SDS are considered to have intraperitoneal recurrence (IPR). It is important to determine whether PWC at SDS affects the prognosis of patients with recurrence. However, to the best of our knowledge, no data are available on the prognostic value of PWC at SDS in patients with OC relapse.

This study evaluated the prognostic value of PWC at SDS by retrospectively reviewing clinical records at the Tokyo Women’s Medical University Adachi Medical Center, limiting participants to patients with OC who underwent SDS for IPR.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Clinical records of patients with OC who underwent SDS for IPR were retrospectively reviewed. This study included a small number of patients with secondary IPR who were treated with chemotherapy during the first IPR. We investigated various clinical data points, including patients’ age at SDS, International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage, histopathological types, types of initial surgery (primary debulking surgery [PDS] or IDS), residual tumor status after primary surgery (categorized as complete or optimal [<1 cm]), platinum-free interval (PFI) which is defined as the time from the end of the last platinum-based chemotherapy to the first disease progression, timing of SDS (either at first or secondary relapse), chemotherapy administered prior to SDS, an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) score before SDS, presence of ascites, PWC at SDS, residual tumor size at SDS, treatment post-SDS, occurrence of IPR post-SDS, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS). The rationale for selecting SDS for each patient was also reviewed from clinical records. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tokyo Women’s Medical University (approval No. 2025-0032) and patient notifications were implemented using an opt-out approach.

Intraoperative PWC was performed immediately after the abdominal cavity was opened and recurrent sites were confirmed, before tumor manipulation to avoid iatrogenic cell dissemination. If there was sufficient ascitic fluid, it was aspirated; otherwise, approximately 50 mL of warm saline was instilled onto the peritoneal surface adjacent to the recurrent sites, and the fluid was aspirated from cavities such as the subdiaphragmatic area or pelvic region. The fluids were centrifuged, and the cell pellets were microscopically evaluated by certified screeners and pathologists using Papanicolaou, Giemsa, and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining techniques. Peritoneal cytology was deemed positive if cancer cells were detected microscopically following standardized criteria. All patients underwent preoperative and follow-up computed tomography (CT) assessments.

Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Fisher’s exact test, and differences in continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon test. PFS was defined as the time from the date of SDS to disease progression or all-cause mortality, whichever occurred first. OS was defined as the time from the date of SDS to all-cause mortality. Additionally, we estimated the IPR-specific PFS (IPR-PFS), defined as the period from the date of SDS to disease progression within the abdominal cavity. Survival estimates were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences were analyzed using the log-rank test. Statistical differences were considered significant when the 2-sided p-value was <0.05. JMP-Pro version 17.0.0 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) was used for all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

Between 2007 and 2022, 207 patients with ovarian, fallopian, or peritoneal cancer were treated at our institute. Among them, 68 experienced recurrence, with 49 (72.1%) experiencing IPR. Of the 19 patients without IPR, 4 underwent SDS. The majority of other metastatic sites were lymph node metastases (22 patients), followed by lung metastases (5 patients) and liver metastases (4 patients). Among the patients with IPR, SDS was performed in 20 patients, of whom 18 were included in this study. Two patients with squamous cell carcinoma were excluded because of its rarity in OC and its markedly different biological characteristics from those of other histological types. All eligible patients who underwent SDS for IPR received PWC (Table 1).

The selection of patients for SDS and determination of resectability based on CT imaging were ultimately left to the physician’s judgment. Patients with diffusely disseminated intraperitoneal metastases, including carcinomatous peritonitis or massive ascites, were excluded, as these conditions typically present challenges for complete surgical resection. Additionally, patients with extraperitoneal metastases (of the 29 patients with IPR who did not undergo SDS, 9 had simultaneous extraperitoneal metastases such as lymph node metastases), as well as elderly patients deemed unsuitable for surgery due to comorbidities, were also excluded from consideration for SDS. Only patients who provided informed consent and met the inclusion criteria underwent SDS.

Patients’ ages ranged from 40 to 77 years, with a median age of 67 years. Histological tumor types included high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) in 16 patients and clear cell carcinoma in 2. FIGO staging revealed that 3 patients had stage IC, 2 had stage IIB, and the remaining had stage III or higher. In the initial surgery (either PDS or IDS), 15 patients achieved complete resection, whereas 3 had residual tumors <1 cm in diameter. Three patients had secondary IPR. Ten patients received at least 1 cycle of chemotherapy before undergoing SDS.

At SDS, the PS score was 0 in 15 patients and 1 in 3 patients, and ascites was not observed in any of the patients. During SDS, complete resection was achieved in 15 patients (83.3%), while PWC were negative in 12 patients (66.7%) and positive in 6. Rapid intraoperative PWC evaluation was not performed in any case. After SDS, chemotherapy was administered to 16 patients, radiation therapy to 1 patient (patient 12), and 1 patient was placed under observation without any additional treatment (patient 6). In terms of the chemotherapy regimens, among the ten PWC-negative patients, 8 were treated with platinum-based regimens, one received a platinum-based regimen followed by a poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor, and one was treated solely with a PARP inhibitor. Among the 6 PWC-positive patients, 4 were treated with platinum-based regimens and 2 received weekly paclitaxel. Eleven patients experienced recurrence after SDS, 1 of whom developed brain metastases (patient 8), while another exhibited both IPR and lymph node metastases (patient 9). The remaining 9 patients experienced only IPR. IPR occurred in all PWC-positive patients after SDS during the follow-up period (median, 78 months; range, 13–146 months), although 3 of them had no residual tumors (patients 14, 15, and 16). In contrast, only 4 of the 12 PWC-negative patients (all without residual tumor) experienced IPR, 2 of whom had secondary IPR at SDS (patients 11 and 12). Notably, both PWC-negative patients with clear cell carcinoma remained free of IPR in the long term (patients 4 and 7).

PWC status was significantly correlated with IPR post-SDS (p=0.009). The association between PWC and PS at SDS and post-SDS residual tumors was significant (both p=0.025; Table 2). Among the HGSC patients, the PWC-negative group showed a statistically significant trend toward a longer PFI (Wilcoxon test, p=0.026) (Fig. 1).

Subgroup analyses limited to patients with HGSC showed that these results were consistent with those of the overall group. Specifically, the median PFS was 56 months for the PWC-negative group compared to 8.5 months for the PWC-positive group (p=0.001), and the median OS was NR for the PWC-negative group versus 17 months for the PWC-positive group (p<0.001). The difference in OS based on the residual tumor status after surgery was also evident. The median OS for the non-residual tumor group was 89 months (95% CI=40 months to N/A) compared with 16 months (95% CI=12 to 16 months) for the residual tumor group (p<0.001) (Fig. 3B).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the prognostic value of PWC for SDS. Our data indicate that patients with IPR are often PWC-negative and that their prognosis can be improved by undergoing SDS.

The Arbeitsgemeinschaft Gynakologische Onkologie (AGO) group in Germany, led by Harter et al. [13], conducted a randomized controlled trial to investigate the therapeutic role of SDS in recurrent OC [2]. They compared the OS between patients with first recurrence who experienced relapse >6 months after the initial treatment, assessing one group treated with chemotherapy alone and another group receiving SDS and chemotherapy. The criteria for patient selection included AGO score positivity, defined as fulfilling 3 conditions: complete resection at the primary surgery, an ECOG PS score of 0, and ascites of 500 mL or less. This criterion was designed to identify patients in whom complete resection may be achievable, as only complete resection was associated with any long-term benefit in recurrent OC [1314]. The complete resection rate in the SDS group was high (75.5%), and the OS was significantly extended compared with that in the control group, confirming its therapeutic significance.

Similarly, a trial conducted by Shi et al. [3] in China showed a significant increase in PFS. No significant difference was observed in OS in the intention-to-treat population; however, analysis after crossover adjustment indicated a significant extension in the SDS group [15]. They employed a more complex method for predicting complete resection by combining the International Model score with PET/CT imaging as part of the patient selection criteria.

In contrast, a trial by Coleman et al. (GOG-0213, Gynecologic Oncology Group study) [4] found no increase in OS in the SDS group. In their study, the prediction of complete resection was left to the discretion of the investigators during patient selection. The complete resection rate was 63%, and even among these patients, no therapeutic benefit was observed in the SDS group. While the AGO study limited patients to those with a PS of 0, the GOG study allowed for a PS score of up to 2 on a 4-point scale. However, the reason for these contradictory results is difficult to determine.

Similar to the GOG study, the prediction of resectability in this study was left to the discretion of the physician based on imaging and other factors. This approach may introduce subjectivity into the selection process, particularly in the interpretation of CT scans. Such subjectivity could lead to a selection bias, potentially influencing the observed outcomes. Nevertheless, when the AGO score was retrospectively applied, the positivity rate was 72.2%, 13 of the 18 cases were classified as positive, and complete resection was achieved in 15 patients (83.3%). These results suggest that the potential selection bias was minimized in this study, enhancing the reliability of our findings.

On the other hand, as for PWC, several studies have investigated the prognostic value of PWC in patients undergoing IDS following neoadjuvant chemotherapy as initial therapy for OC. Many of these studies have indicated that positive PWC at IDS is a significant factor for poor prognosis [891011]. In contrast, in the reviewed studies and other literature related to SDS, no mention of PWC was found in patients undergoing SDS. This may be due to the preconceived notion that they are inherently positive in IPR cases. However, our data indicated that 12 of 18 (66.7%) patients tested negative, suggesting the presence of negative cases.

Furthermore, PWC results correlated with the residual disease status and were significantly associated with IPR post-SDS, PFS, and OS. Notably, even in cases of complete resection, a positive PWC result was associated with poor prognosis.

In terms of the correlation between PWC and the status of residual disease post-SDS, there are insufficient cases to perform a multivariate analysis in this study because logistic regression requires a minimum of at least 10 events per predictor variable to ensure stable estimates; nevertheless, our findings allow us to draw several conclusions: a negative PWC result is associated with a higher likelihood of complete resection and a better prognosis. Conversely, even when complete resection is achieved, a positive PWC result indicates a higher IPR risk. Although we cannot definitively determine the independent prognostic value of PWC, it is likely that PWC reflects the underlying tumor burden rather than serving as a purely independent prognostic marker.

Moreover, there are several other potential confounding factors when assessing PWC as a prognostic marker. The PWC-negative group showed a significant trend toward longer PFI in HGSC cases. While the relationship between PFI and PWC findings offers valuable insights, it also suggests that PFI may act as a confounding prognostic factor. Post-SDS treatment may also influence survival outcomes, although we did not find definitive differences between the 2 groups in this small cohort.

Nevertheless, given that our data indicated a high percentage of IPR in OC recurrence both before and after SDS, we believe that the findings related to PWC have a significant impact on the prognosis of patients with recurrent OC.

It is impossible to predict the results of PWC prior to SDS. However, while we have not yet implemented rapid intraoperative diagnosis, it has the potential to permit timely confirmation of the results. This approach could help guide prognostic predictions and treatment strategy determination, and potentially serve as a valuable clinical tool. Our study highlights the limitations of SDS in PWC-positive patients. However, given the limited number of cases examined, it is premature to make definitive recommendations for avoiding SDS in PWC-positive patients. At this stage, we believe that our findings should be regarded as references for prognostic predictions and postoperative therapeutic choices, rather than strict guidelines.

It is expected that the introduction of new therapies, including PARP inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and antibody-drug conjugates, along with the practice of personalized medicine based on comprehensive genomic profiling, will significantly expand treatment options for recurrent OC. Therefore, we believe that it is necessary to further explore how physicians incorporate PWC results into their postoperative management plans.

The limitations of this study were inherent to its design, as it was a retrospective analysis involving a small number of cases at a single institution. The variability in the years of treatment, patient backgrounds, and treatment protocols further underscores the need for caution when interpreting these findings. Given the limited sample size and exploratory nature of the analysis, it was challenging to definitively calculate parameters such as the minimum detectable effect size within the current context. Nevertheless, this study lays the groundwork for future research in this area and underscores the need for larger and more comprehensive studies to better address these statistical considerations. Future studies should focus on verifying these results by using a more sophisticated approach in a multicenter setting.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the prognostic value of PWC for SDS. Patients with IPR are often PWC-negative, and their prognosis can be improved by undergoing SDS. Conversely, PWC-positive patients are less likely to be suitable candidates for SDS even if they achieve complete resection. Thus, PWC is a significant predictor of the long-term benefits of SDS in patients with OC and IPR.
