High-performance chitosan/blue-green algae (Cyanobacteria) nanocomposite for rapid and optimized removal of organic pollutant from wastewater
Authors: Ahmed E Alprol, Mohamed Ashour, Sameerah I Al-Saeedi, Abdallah Tageldein Mansour, Maymounah N Alharthi, Ehab El-Haroun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work explores the synthesis and utilization of a green composite material based on chitosan and blue-green algae biomass for the effective removal of Acid Red 35, a recalcitrant anionic dye, from aqueous solutions. Chitosan–Blue-Green Algae (Ch-BGA) nanocomposite was prepared by cross-linking chitosan with glutaraldehyde and subsequent BGA biomass incorporation, which resulted in gel-like nanoparticles (16.3–19.6 nm). FTIR characterization confirmed the presence of the key functional groups (O-H, N-H, C = O) involved in adsorption. Measurement of the zeta potential revealed a positive surface charge (+ 27.8 mV) that facilitates electrostatic attraction for the anionic AR35 dye. SEM imaging revealed a rough, irregular surface morphology conducive to adsorption and EDX confirmed the main C, O, N composition. Optimum dye removal (> 96%) was achieved at acidic pH (pH 3). Adsorption equilibrium was reached in approximately 150 min. The Langmuir isotherm model best fitted the equilibrium data (R² = 0.999), indicating monolayer adsorption with a maximum adsorption capacity (qm) of 38.2 mg/g. Kinetic studies showed that the pseudo-second-order model accurately described the adsorption process (R² = 0.970), suggesting that chemisorption could be involved alongside physisorption, although intraparticle diffusion was also suggested as a factor. Thermodynamic calculation showed an exothermic process and a spontaneous (ΔG° = -7.603 kJ mol− 1) and entropy-reducing (ΔS° = -0.1736 Jmol K− 1) process, characteristic of physisorption. Definitive Screening Design (DSD) was effectively applied for optimization, and initial dye concentration and contact time were determined to be the most influential variables. DSD modeling predicted maximum 97–98% removal rates at optimized conditions (e.g., pH 3, 0.1 g dosage).Keywords: Anionic dye, Blue-green algae (Cyanobacteria), Definitive screening design (DSD), Eco-friendly remediation, Wastewater treatment, OptimizationSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Industrial activities have significantly contributed to the global environmental burden, with the release of various pollutants into air, water, and soil. Among these pollutants, industrial dyes pose a substantial threat due to their widespread use and persistence in the environment. A high degree of acid red dyes which are classified as industrial dyes are frequently used in textiles, paper, leather and beautifying industries due to their bright color and stability. AR35 exhibits high chemical stability and resistance to biodegradation, making it ideal for long-term applications. Its water-soluble nature facilitates its use in various industrial processes. Unfortunately, the improper disposal and discharge of these dyes into the marine environment through industrial wastewater have raised serious concerns about their impact on marine organisms and the ecosystem as a whole3. Bioaccumulation of these dyes have been associated with long-term health risks such as genotoxicity and carcinogenicity4. Furthermore, the persistence and mobility of acid red dyes in the marine environment further exacerbate bioaccumulation of these dyes in higher trophic levels5.

In recent years, the escalating global concerns regarding environmental pollution have spurred significant research efforts toward the development of efficient and sustainable technologies for wastewater treatment6. For this objective, a variety of techniques have been investigated, comprising chemical, biological, and physical procedures7. These techniques include ion exchange, electrochemical processes, photodegradation, membrane technology, coagulation-flocculation, reverse osmosis, irradiation, chemical oxidation, ozonation, bioremediation using microbes, and adsorption8,9. Among these methods, the process of adsorption represents an appealing approach that involves removing contaminant molecules from their surroundings and transferring them to the surface of a matrix. Adsorption has some advantages over other separation and purification methods (e.g., membrane technology, phytoremediation, ion exchange, and electrochemical separation)10 for instance simplicity of use, low cost, and high efficiency11. Given its wide range of applications and capacity to function under scaling settings, the adsorption process has demonstrated to be a viable treatment method for the elimination of synthetic organic dyes from wastewater12.

Among these, the use of natural, sustainable, and eco-friendly materials such as chitosan and blue-green algae (cyanobacteria), has gained considerable attention due to their remarkable adsorption and biosorption capacity. The second most prevalent biopolymer in nature is chitosan, a biopolymer made from chitin that has shown promise as an adsorbent for dye removal13. Chitosan possesses numerous amino and hydroxyl functional groups that provide a high affinity for anionic dyes like Acid Red 35 (AR35) through electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and surface complexation14. In addition, blue-green algae, a group of photosynthetic microorganisms primarily composed of cyanobacteria, have also shown great potential for dye removal15. Functional groups like carboxyl, amino and hydroxyl groups are abundant on the cell surfaces of cyanobacteria, facilitating the uptake and binding of organic molecules, including dyes16. Moreover, the metabolic activities of blue-green algae may lead to the degradation or transformation of dyes into less harmful products, enhancing the overall remediation efficiency17. Therefore, combining Ch/BGA into a composite is appealing as both are natural, biocompatible, and biodegradable materials that may display complementary activities. A composite of this kind would be highly suitable for applications where sustainability and biocompatibility are essential, such as biomedical applications, agriculture, and wastewater treatment18. This synergistic effect makes the composite a promising candidate for a broad range of uses ranging from wound healing, food packaging, and water purification applications19.

Furthermore, blue-green algae are rich in nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, and essential micronutrients. Combining them with chitosan in a composite provides a slow-release mechanism for these nutrients, making it beneficial for agricultural applications as biofertilizers and soil conditioners20. It is worth noting that several factors impact the adsorption and biosorption capacities of Ch/BGA, including pH, temperature, dye concentration, contact time, and surface properties of the adsorbents21. In the context of experimental design and optimization, Definitive Screening Design (DSD) serves as an efficient tool for exploring the influence of multiple factors on a specific response22. This approach finds broad application across numerous fields, such as industrial process optimization, product development, and scientific research. The core principle of a Definitive Screening Design (DSD) is its capacity to strike a balance between efficiency and thoroughness in exploring the effects of multiple factors on outcomes23. Through the careful selection of a subset of factors and levels, a Definitive Screening Design (DSD) enables researchers to efficiently investigate primary effects and significant interactions among factors, all while reducing the number of experimental runs needed.

The Definitive Screening Design (DSD), a powerful statistical tool, facilitates the concurrent evaluation of multiple factors and their interactions, aiding in the identification of optimal conditions for effective adsorption24. It is widely acknowledged that one of the most effective tools for optimizing degradation processes is the Design of Experimentation (DOE). This approach uses statistical screening techniques to separate the irrelevant variables from the significant ones and then apply higher sensitivity experimental designs (level designs) to study the significant variables11. The use of DSD in experimental research is, however, very limited. Therefore, in order to validate the methodology’s utility, more validation studies must be conducted25. Thus, the selection of chitosan and cyanobacterial biomass as the core of our biosorbent was driven by a charge-complementarity and multifunctional-site design strategy tailored to Acid Red 35 (AR35), an anionic azo dye bearing sulfonate groups. Chitosan is a renewable, cationic biopolymer whose amine functionalities become protonated in acidic media, enabling strong electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding with anionic dyes; its abundant –NH2/–OH groups also allow secondary interactions that stabilize sorption at the solid–liquid interface26. In parallel, cyanobacterial cell envelopes present a dense mosaic of carboxyl, hydroxyl, and amino/phosphate groups that contribute additional binding domains and textural heterogeneity, while the biomass is locally abundant and readily sourced (Burullus Lake), aligning with low-cost, circular-bioeconomy goals. To translate this rationale into a healthy material, we cross-linked chitosan with glutaraldehyde and immobilized the cyanobacterial biomass within the formed network, yielding gel-like nanoparticles (≈ 16.3–19.6 nm) with rough, irregular surfaces that increase accessible area and adsorption microenvironments27. The composite exhibits a positively charged surface (ζ = +27.8 mV), deliberately engineered to favor electrostatic capture of AR35 under acidic conditions; under these conditions the material achieves > 96% dye removal, validating the charge-complementarity design. Novelty and new concepts. (i) We implement a dual-mechanism biosorbent design that couples cationic chitosan domains with multi-site cyanobacterial functional groups in a single cross-linked, handleable gel an approach that simultaneously enhances affinity (electrostatic/H-bonding) and microtexture-driven uptake. (ii) We integrate Definitive Screening Design (DSD) with adsorption modeling to identify the most influential operational factors and map their interactions efficiently—an optimization route still rarely applied in dye adsorption studies—thereby moving beyond one-factor-at-a-time tuning. (iii) We target the recalcitrant AR35 specifically and demonstrate near-quantitative removal at low dosage and acidic pH, linking material properties (ζ-potential, FTIR-identified groups, SEM texture) to performance through isotherm/kinetic analysis (Langmuir, PSO, intraparticle diffusion).

Materials and methods

Preparation of CH/BGA composite

Blue-green algae (Cyanobacteria) was collected from Burullus Lake (Egypt), dried, then crushed into pieces (no larger than 150 mm). Acid Red 35 (AR35) dye (C20H16N4O9S2Na2) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (purity 95%; C.I. 18065). Acetic acid and chitosan (with 90% deacetylation) were bought from Egyptian chemical company. A mixture of chitosan (2 g) was dissolved in a 100 mL solution of 2% acetic acid to create a nanocomposite solution. The mixture was ultrasonicated for thirteen minutes to promote the dissolution. After fully dissolving the chitosan, 2 mL of distilled water were added to the solution. Next, glutaraldehyde drops were introduced at room temperature to cross-link the chitosan. Following this, 3 g of blue-green algae were incorporated into the cross-linked mixture, which was stirred for 1 h to facilitate hydrogel formation. This process yielded a gel-like material, not a powder. The measured sizes range from approximately 16.3 nm to 19.6 nm.

Batch adsorption examinations

A solution of AR35 dye at a concentration of 1000 ppm was made by dissolving 1.0 g of the dye in one Liter of distilled water, and then further diluting it to create calibration standards and for conducting adsorption tests. The sorption capacity and kinetic properties of chitosan and blue-green algae were evaluated through batch adsorption studies. In these studies, 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks were used, each containing 50 mL of AR35 dye solution prepared at different concentrations. Various amounts of Ch/BGA composite were added to the solutions. The pH of each sample was adjusted within the experimental range (pH 3–10) using 0.1 M HCl or NaOH to ensure precise control. The flasks were then placed in a thermostatic water bath set at 20 °C and shaken continuously at 160 rpm for 180 min to maintain uniform mixing and temperature. Samples were collected at specific time intervals 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, and 210 min for analysis. To study the effect of adsorbent dosage, varying amounts of Ch/BGA (ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 g) were tested, while keeping all other experimental conditions constant as described above. This approach allowed a systematic evaluation of adsorption efficiency under controlled parameters. In batch experiments investigating the effects of pH changes, the optimum adsorption time and dye concentration were maintained. Following the target contact time, the flasks were taken off of the orbital shaker, and the concentration of any remaining dyes was measured by spectrophotometric analysis after the supernatant had been filtered throughout a 0.47 μm membrane filter. Using spectrophotometry at λmax = 505 nm, the concentration of AR35 dye was determined. Batch adsorption tests were conducted with strict controls and repeated twice to ensure reliability and consistency. Batch adsorption tests were conducted with strict controls and repeated in triplicate (n = 3) under identical conditions to ensure reliability and consistency. The results presented in figures and tables are the mean values of these parallel runs, and standard deviations were calculated for all measurements. In cases where error bars are not visible in the graphs, they are smaller than the plotting symbols. This approach minimizes random error and ensures the statistical robustness of the experimental data. Isotherm and kinetic studies

The initial concentration of AR35 dye solutions was varied from 10 to 100 mg L− 1 to 200 mg L− 1, at pH 3, 0.3 g and 180 min to assess equilibrium adsorption capacity. The equilibrium adsorption data were fitted to the Langmuir, Freundlich, Tempkin, and Harkins–Jura isotherm models to investigate adsorption behavior. Adsorption kinetics were monitored over a range of contact times from 0 to 180 min at 10 mg L− 1 of dye concentration, pH 3 and 0.3 g to capture equilibrium behavior. Adsorption kinetics were modeled using pseudo-first-order (PFO), pseudo-second-order (PSO), and intraparticle diffusion models. Correspondingly, thermodynamic parameters and temperature effect was investigated at 10 mg L− 1 of dye concentration: pH 3, 0.3 g, 180 min and temperature range at 20 °C, 30 °C, 40 °C, 50 °C, and 60 °C. Analyzing thermodynamic factors, including entropy change (∆S°), enthalpy change (∆H°), and Gibbs free energy change (∆G°), is essential for understanding the spontaneity and feasibility of chemical and physical processes.

Design model

A preliminary determination of the range of circumstances associated with increased removal rates was made based on the AR35 dye elimination effectiveness under various settings. There were three levels (1, 0, -1) for adsorbent dose (A), contact time (B), initial AR35 concentration (C) and the pH of the mixture (D). The levels of sorbent dosage were 0.05 g, 0.1 g, and 0.2 g the levels of adsorption time were 30, 60, and 90 min, and the levels of pH value were 3, 5, and 7. Design of JMP Pro 17 was used to simulate a total of 14 tests, with the response (Y) for every parameter representing the AR35 removal efficiency28. The model’s fitness, the reasonableness of the equations, the significance of the response, and residual values were determined. To pinpoint the exact external condition variables necessary to attain the highest removal efficiency. To evaluate the individual impact of each element, the main effect plots were examined, and interaction effect plots were then used to establish the interactions between the factors.

Reusability evaluation of CH/BGA composite for AR35 dye removal

The initial concentration of AR35 dye solutions used in this study was 10 mg L− 1. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 3, and the experiments were conducted at a temperature of 20 °C. A pre-weighed amount (0.3 g) of Ch/BGA composite was added to 100 mL of AR35 dye solution. The solutions were continuously shaken at a speed of 160 rpm for 180 min to ensure proper mixing and interaction between the dye molecules and the adsorbent material. After the adsorption period, the solutions were filtered, and the remaining dye concentration was analyzed using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. After each adsorption cycle, the spent composite was treated with a desorbing agent to remove the adsorbed dye molecules. The composite was then rinsed thoroughly with deionized water and dried before being reused for subsequent adsorption cycles. The reusability of the composite was tested over three consecutive adsorption-desorption cycles.

Characterization of the nanocomposite

The characterization of the chitosan and blue-green algae nanocomposite was performed using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, covering a spectral vary between 4000 and 400 cm− 1 at a resolution of 4 cm− 1. The FTIR spectra of the examined ink samples were obtained using a Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo-Scientific, USA) fitted with an Attenuated Total Reflection (ATR) accessory. For ATR spectroscopy, samples were brought into close proximity to an ATR crystal, composed of germanium, zinc selenide, or diamond, and spectra were collected using the instrument (Version 7.2 Build: 7, 2, 139, 1294 20130108). 32 scans of each sample were made at a resolution of 4 cm− 1. Zeta potential assessments were carried out using the zeta potential report v2.3 software at a temperature of 25 °C. A total of 20 zeta runs, registering a count rate of 34.6 kcps were performed in this study. Measurements were obtained at a distance of 2.00 mm within a transparent disposable zeta cell using a Zetasizer Nano S instrument (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). Also, the zeta potential analysis was performed in 30 mM NaCl at 25 °C and 120 s of equilibration time. Data shown presents the average of three measurements, each with 10–100 runs. Additionally, utilizing the JEOL JSM-IT200 instrument, Energy Dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX) and SEM analysis of the Ch/BGA composite was performed using a JEOL JSM-IT200 scanning electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV in high-vacuum mode. Prior to imaging, the samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold (10 nm) to enhance conductivity and minimize charging effects. Images were captured at 20,000× magnification (scale bar = 1 μm) to accurately visualize particle size distribution and surface morphology.

Results and discussion

Characterization of adsorbent material

Investigation of results of zeta potential

Zeta potential is a valuable tool for characterizing the surface charge and stability of nanoparticles29. The zeta potential of the composite was 27.8 ± 5.63 mV (mean ± standard deviation, Fig. 1) indicating that the nanocomposite possesses a positive surface charge. The main peak observed in the zeta potential distribution also corresponds to a positive zeta potential of 27.8 mV, confirming the dominant surface charge of the composite material. The conductivity was 0.264 mS cm− 1, revealing the electrical conductivity of the solution, which is influenced by the presence of charged particles. In colloidal systems, this positive charge typically arises from the protonation of functional groups existing on the external of adsorbent material, such as amino groups in chitosan and carboxyl groups in blue-green algae. This positive charge can influence various aspects of colloidal behavior, including stability, aggregation, and interactions with other substances. Also, the positive surface charge of the composite material can electrostatically attract negatively charged dye molecules30. Alternatively, AR35 dye molecules generally have negative charges as a result of sulfonate groups within their structure. Hence, the electrostatic attraction among the negatively charged dye molecules and the positively charged composite surface facilitates the attachment of dye molecules to the composite material. Additionally, by discouraging similarly charged particles, the positive zeta potential can influence the colloidal system’s stability by preventing aggregation and preserving a large surface area that is accessible for dye adsorption31.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectroscopy is an effective analytical technique that can be used to identify and analyze the chemical structure of functional groups32. The assigned functional groups before adsorption are presented in Fig. 2a and show the presence of absorption bands at 3440 cm–1 and 1638 cm–1 indicating that the adsorption process may involve hydroxyl (O-H), carbonyl (C = O), or amino (N-H) groups33. These groups are likely present on the Ch/BGA and could interact with the dye molecules through hydrogen bonding or other electrostatic interactions. Changes in the intensity or position of this band upon adsorption could suggest interactions between the aromatic rings and the nanocomposite. For example, the bands at 2374 cm–1 and 1400 cm–1 could be attributed to C-H stretching and bending vibrations, respectively. These are likely related with the alkyl chains existing in the Ch/BGA34. The absence of peaks at 3440 cm–1 and 1638 cm⁻¹ in the spectrum after adsorption (Fig. 2b) suggests that the functional groups responsible for these peaks (for example O-H, N-H, or C = O) are involved in the adsorption process. These functional groups may have also interacted with the AR35 dye molecules through hydrogen bonding or other electrostatic interactions. The presence of C-H bending vibrations at 633 cm⁻¹ indicates the existence of aromatic rings, likely from the AR35 dye molecule35. The broad peak around 1500 cm⁻¹ and the peak around 1100 cm⁻¹ could be due to vibrations in the dye molecule itself. This suggests that the dye molecules have been adsorbed onto the Ch/BGA composite.

Elemental composition

It is important to compare the EDX results with the expected theoretical composition of the Ch/BGA composite. This can help to identify any discrepancies and potential contamination. According to EDX examination (Fig. 3b), the fundamental structure of the composite nanomaterial of Ch/BGA is primarily composed of carbon and oxygen, indicating that both substances are organic37. In addition, the attendance of nitrogen content approves the presence of chitosan, which contains amino groups38. The existence of functional groups for nitrogen and oxygen in chitosan and maybe in blue-green algae suggests that the composite could be very effective at adsorbing pigments from watery solutions. These functional groups can cooperate with dye particles through mechanisms such as hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, and other types of interactions. Furthermore, the presence of sulfur and chlorine suggests potential impurities or residues from the synthesis process.

Batch adsorption experiments

Effect of pH

The influence of pH on AR53 dye removal by the 0.1 g Ch/BGA nanocomposite within the pH range of 3–10 presents intriguing insights into the adsorption behavior of the nanocomposite material under varying pH conditions. The results designate a significant increase in the percentage elimination at equilibrium, rising from 68.4% to 96% (Fig. 4a), which suggests that pH has a critical influence on the effectiveness of AR35 dye removal. At lower pH levels (pH = 3.0), the adsorption of AR35 reaches a peak of 96%. This is likely attributed to the formation of quaternary ions, such as hydronium ions and ammonium ions, on the surface of the Ch/BGA nanocomposite, leading to enhanced electrostatic interactions between the positively charged surface and the negatively charged AR35 dye molecules. This observation aligns with the theory that the electrostatic attraction among the adsorbent material and dye molecules is stronger under acidic conditions, thereby promoting higher adsorption percentages 39,40. Chitosan, derived from chitin, possesses amino groups that can undergo protonation and deprotonation reactions in response to changes in pH. The incorporation of blue-green algae nanoparticles into the chitosan matrix can introduce additional surface functionalities, further influencing the pH-dependent adsorption behavior41. Scientific literature supports the pH-dependent adsorption of dyes on chitosan-based materials, and the role of pH in modifying the surface charge and chemical environment is well-documented (Sabzevari et al., 2018).

Effect of initial AR35 dye concentration

According to the initial dosage of AR35 dye, the experimental findings show a noteworthy trend in the adsorption behavior of the Ch/BGA nanocomposite. The results in Fig. 4b showed that at lower concentrations (10–20 mg L− 1), such as 10 mg L− 1, the Ch/BGA nanocomposite exhibited a remarkably high percentage removal of 97.6%. This high efficiency can be attributed to the availability of numerous active adsorption sites relative to the number of dye molecules present in the solution. At 20 mg L− 1, the removal percentage remained high due to the strong driving force created by the concentration gradient, which enhanced the interaction between dye molecules and the adsorbent. While, in the moderate concentrations at 30 mg L− 1, the percentage removal continued to increase, demonstrating the adsorbent’s capacity to handle higher dye concentrations without site saturation. Nevertheless, at 40 mg L− 1, the percentage removal peaked at 99.8%, indicating optimal utilization of active binding sites on the nanocomposite surface. The adsorption capacity (Qe) also reached its maximum at this concentration, reflecting the balance between available binding sites and dye molecules43.

Initially, when dye concentrations are high, there is a greater gradient in concentration among the solution and the adsorbent surface, leading to increased transfer of dye molecules to the adsorption sites on the Ch/BGA nanocomposite44. Furthermore, as the initial concentration rises, the adsorbent’s surface area and accessible active sites may be essential in allowing for a greater number of dye molecules45. The Ch/BGA nanocomposite likely provides an extensive network of binding sites, allowing for a more effective and thorough elimination of AR35 dye from the solution. Beyond 40 mg L− 1, a noticeable decline in percentage removal was observed. For example, at 70 mg L− 1, the removal percentage began to drop significantly. This decrease is attributed to the saturation of adsorption sites, where most active sites become occupied, leaving fewer sites available for further adsorption. At 100 mg L− 1, the adsorption efficiency decreased further as the system approached equilibrium between adsorption and desorption. The saturation of the nanocomposite’s accessible adsorption sites could be the cause of this decline46.

These tested concentrations are also relevant to practical applications. In textile industry wastewater, dye levels typically range from a few mg L− 1 in treated effluents to several hundred mg L− 1 in raw streams, with acid dye concentrations commonly reported between 10 and 200 mg L− 1 47, and in some cases reaching 377 mg L− 1 in concentrated waste from batch dyeing operations48. The concentration range employed in this study (10–100 mg L− 1) therefore represents the lower-to-moderate portion of the real wastewater spectrum: the lower end (10 mg L− 1) simulates treated or diluted effluents, while the higher values (80–100 mg L− 1) reflect untreated or partially treated textile waste streams. This ensures that the results obtained are directly relevant to realistic industrial scenarios.

Effect of contact time

The influence of duration time on the removal of AR35 dye by the Ch/BGA nanocomposite is a critical factor that greatly affects the efficiency of the adsorption process. The observed increase in adsorption from 53.14% to 99.68% within the time range of 15 to 150 min (Fig. 5a) indicates a time-dependent nature of the adsorption kinetics. This behavior is commonly associated with the dynamic interplay between the surface properties of the nanocomposite, the dye molecules, and the available active sites for adsorption46. In the initial phase of the sorption process (15–60 min), a rapid increase in the removal of AR35 dye by the Ch/BGA nanocomposite is observed. This phenomenon can be ascribed to the abundance of vacant sorption sites available on the external of the nanocomposite49. At the onset of the process, these sites are easily accessible, resulting in a swift uptake of dye particles onto the Ch/BGA nanocomposite. Furthermore, the driving force for adsorption is significant because of the concentration gradient among the dye solution and the sorbent surface, which further increases the initial adsorption rates50,51. As the adsorption process progresses beyond the initial phase (60–150 min), the rate of adsorption begins to slow down. This phenomenon is likely associated with the saturation of available adsorption sites on the Ch/BGA nanocomposite. As the surface becomes increasingly occupied by adsorbed dye molecules, the remaining vacant sites decrease, leading to a reduced adsorption rate52. The system approaches equilibrium, and the incremental increase in adsorption becomes less pronounced. It is important to consider the chemical interactions and bonding mechanisms between AR35 dye and the Ch/BGA nanocomposite. The active functional groups on chitosan, such as amino and hydroxyl groups, facilitate electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and other adsorptive forces with the dye molecules53. Thus, optimizing the contact time is vital for achieving the highest sorption efficiency. Whereas the initial rapid adsorption within the first 15–60 min indicates the efficacy of the Ch/BGA nanocomposite, prolonged contact times might not significantly improve the adsorption capacity. Balancing the trade-off between achieving high removal efficiency and minimizing the required contact time is essential for practical applications.

Effect of adsorbent dosage

The ideal dose for the Ch/BGA nanocomposite to remove 40 mg L− 1 of the dye in 3 h at pH 5 was 0.3 g, yielding a removal rate of 97.7% (Fig. 5b). The adsorption rate did not increase with higher adsorbent dosages (0.5 g). At optimal concentrations, AR35 dye likely had more accessible active sites. Excessively high concentrations of adsorbent can cause agglomeration, which reduces the whole surface area of the sorbent material and limits the accessibility of active surface sites54. An observed pattern in the correlation between sorption capacity and elimination rate indicates that the adsorption capacity decreased from 3.9 to 0.7 mg·g⁻¹ when the adsorbent dosage increased from 0.1 g to 0.5 g. An observed pattern in the correlation between sorption capacity and elimination rate indicates that the adsorption capacity decreased from 3.9 to 0.7 mg g⁻¹ as the adsorbent dosage increased from 0.1 g to 0.5 g. This decline can be attributed to the saturation of surface adsorption sites, which reduces the adsorbent’s specific surface area and mass transfer efficiency. However, despite the reduction in adsorption capacity per unit mass, increasing the adsorbent dosage enhances the overall dye removal efficiency by providing more active sites for adsorption55. In conclusion, despite the fact that excessive AR35 dye concentrations may reduce adsorption, increasing the adsorbent’s dosage can help remove the dye.

Influence of temperature on AR35 dye removal

Understanding the temperature dependence of dye removal is important for optimizing the treatment method. Optimizing the temperature can maximize the efficiency of the composite and minimize energy consumption56. The knowledge of temperature effects allows us to predict the performance of the composite under varying environmental conditions. The provided data in Fig. 6 demonstrates a complex relationship between temperature and the effectiveness of the Ch/BGA composite in removing AR35 dye. High removal efficiency was noted at 20 °C (96.14%) indicating optimal adsorption conditions at this temperature. At such temperatures, the adsorption process is likely favored due to the Ch/BGA composite potentially having a higher surface area available for dye binding at lower temperatures, leading to greater adsorption. Low temperatures usually promote stronger interactions between dye molecules and the composite’s functional groups, leading to more effective binding57. At 30 °C, a slight decrease in efficiency was noted (87.44%), which may suggest a shift in the balance between adsorption and desorption processes. A major decrease was measured at 40 °C with 23.6% which suggests a dominant influence of factors like increased desorption or changes in the composite’s structure. Desorption may increase at 40°Cbecause the dye molecules may move around more at such temperature and could come off the surface easier58. Additionally, heat can change the structure of the material, making it harder for the dye to stick to it or get inside. At 50–60 °C, the adsorption behavior of the AR35 dye using the Ch/BGA composite demonstrates a reduction in removal efficiency, as shown in the data in Fig. 6. At 50 °C, the removal efficiency remains relatively high (~ 86%), indicating that the adsorption process is still effective at this temperature. This suggests that the functional groups on the Ch/BGA composite remain stable, and interactions between the dye molecules and the adsorbent surface are still favorable59. However, at 60 °C, the removal efficiency drops further (~ 75%), highlighting a declining trend as temperature increases. Higher temperatures provide more kinetic energy to dye molecules, leading to weaker binding interactions between the dye and the composite surface60. The increased molecular motion can promote desorption, causing previously adsorbed dye molecules to detach from the surface, reducing overall adsorption capacity. Elevated temperatures may induce structural changes in the composite, such as partial deformation or collapse of porous structures59. These changes can reduce the available surface area or alter the active functional groups required for adsorption, thereby decreasing the binding efficiency of the dye molecules. At higher temperatures, the adsorption-desorption equilibrium may shift towards desorption, limiting the ability of the composite to retain the dye molecules. This could also be linked to changes in hydrogen bonding or van der Waals forces that weaken at elevated temperatures. Also, the observed decline in adsorption capacity at 50–60 °C suggests that the process is likely exothermic. In exothermic adsorption, higher temperatures reduce the adsorption potential, as the system tends to release heat during the process, favoring lower temperatures for optimal binding61. These findings emphasize the importance of operating at lower temperatures (20–30 °C) to achieve optimal adsorption performance while minimizing thermal effects that compromise efficiency.

Isotherm and kinetic analysis

In this study, multiple adsorption isotherm and kinetic models were applied to provide a comprehensive understanding of the interaction between AR35 dye molecules and the Ch/BGA composite. Each model is based on distinct theoretical assumptions and describes different aspects of the adsorption process62. A positive standard change in enthalpy ∆H° (55.102 Kj mol− 1) indicates an adsorption process that is endothermic. During adsorption, heat is taken in from the surrounding environment85. The positive value of ∆H° indicates that the adsorption process requires heat energy from the surroundings. Based on the thermodynamic parameters, the adsorption is likely to be physisorption because the ∆H° value (55.102 Kj mol− 1) is relatively low compared to chemical adsorption, which typically has ∆H° values in the range of 80–200 Kj mol− 1.

The relatively low ∆H° value points to physisorption as the dominant mechanism, characterized by weak van der Waals forces or hydrogen bonding between the dye molecules and the composite surface. This mechanism does not involve significant electronic or covalent bond formation, differentiating it from chemisorption, which requires higher activation energy and stronger interactions. The negative standard Gibbs free energy change ∆G° (-7.603 Kj mol− 1) indicates that adsorption is spontaneous at standard conditions (298 K and 1 atm)87. Suggested mechanism of dye removal

The removal of AR35 dye utilizing a Ch/BGA composite likely relies on various processes, including surface charge interactions, chemical bonding, and mass transfer. The composite’s positive surface charge, resulting from protonation of functional groups, attracts negatively charged dye molecules through electrostatic interactions. The pH of the solution influences this charge, affecting adsorption efficiency. Chemical bonding occurs via functional groups like amino and carbonyl groups, confirmed by FTIR analysis, facilitating dye removal through hydrogen bonding and other interactions. Aromatic rings in the dye also interact with the composite. Surface morphology, observed through SEM imagery, revealed rough surfaces that provide more sites for adsorption. Adjusting adsorbent dosage is crucial, as increasing dosage enhances adsorption sites, while excessive amounts can reduce accessibility. The adsorption process follows a time-dependent pattern, with an initial rapid phase followed by slower kinetics due to saturation. Higher initial dye concentrations lead to faster adsorption rates, but saturation effects may occur. Optimizing factors like pH, dosage, and contact time are vital for improving adsorption efficiency and mitigating dye pollution in aquatic ecosystems. The present study, although conducted under batch conditions to optimize operational parameters and elucidate the adsorption mechanism, demonstrates clear potential for industrial application in textile wastewater treatment. The Ch/BGA nanocomposite can be produced on a large scale using abundant, renewable, and low-cost raw materials—chitosan obtained from crustacean shell waste and cyanobacteria biomass cultivated in open raceway ponds or photobioreactors88. Such large-scale biomass production is already established in aquaculture and wastewater treatment facilities, ensuring a continuous supply to meet industrial demands. The optimized adsorption capacity (38.2 mg g− 1) and relatively short equilibrium time (~ 150 min) enable adaptation to continuous-flow configurations such as packed-bed or fluidized-bed adsorption columns, which are standard in textile effluent treatment plants. Moreover, the composite demonstrated good reusability over multiple adsorption–desorption cycles, significantly lowering operating costs and reducing secondary waste generation89. In terms of operational compatibility, the composite maintained high removal efficiency at acidic pH, typical of many textile dyeing effluents, while still performing satisfactorily under broader pH ranges, making it robust under variable wastewater conditions. Compared to conventional chemical treatment methods, the Ch/BGA process avoids the use of hazardous reagents, reduces sludge production, and offers an eco-friendly alternative that aligns with sustainable wastewater management practices90. These attributes make the Ch/BGA nanocomposite a viable candidate for integration into existing industrial treatment lines, either as a polishing step after primary treatment or within modular adsorption units designed for continuous operation.

The current study using Ch/BGA provides a valuable steppingstone towards developing sustainable solutions for dye removal, while research on other adsorbents continues to enhance the overall comprehension of the field. Future research should focus on optimizing existing methods, developing novel materials, and evaluating the overall environmental footprint of different approaches to ensure responsible and effective dye removal.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrated the potential of a novel composite material, synthesized from natural biopolymer blue-green algae (Ch/BGA) and chitosan, for effective Acid Red 35 (AR35) dye removal from water solutions. The characterization techniques validated the composite’s favorable properties like porous, rough surface morphology, nanoparticle size (16.3–19.6 nm), a net positive surface charge favoring anionic dye adsorption, and the presence of required functional groups (hydroxyl, amino, carbonyl) for adsorption interactions. Batch adsorption experiments demonstrated that the removal process was significantly dependent on operational conditions. The optimal performance was achieved at a low pH (3), where removal efficiencies higher than 96–98% were achieved. Kinetic studies illustrated that the pseudo-second-order model better explained the rate of adsorption than the other models, while the Langmuir isotherm model well-described the equilibrium data with a maximum monolayer adsorption capacity of 38.2 mg g− 1. The adsorption process was spontaneous and likely exothermic, and was largely dominated by physisorption mechanisms like electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces, as indicated by thermodynamic data and temperature dependence studies. Application of Definitive Screening Design (DSD) was proven to statistically investigate the impact of various parameters (pH, adsorbent dose, contact time, initial dye concentration) and interaction among them. DSD showed initial dye concentration and contact time as the significant factors controlling AR35 removal efficiency and guided the optimization process. Moreover, the Ch/BGA composite exhibited satisfactory reusability, with good removal efficiency for at least two cycles of adsorption-desorption. In general, the developed Ch/BGA composite is a low-cost, eco-friendly, and efficient adsorbent for AR35 dye removal. Its basis on easily accessible natural materials raises its environmental level, and it can be considered a promising candidate for application in wastewater treatment schemes that aim to mitigate dye pollution of aquatic environments. Future studies could be focused on enhancing long-term stability and reusability, performance evaluation with actual industrial effluents, and scale-up feasibility.
