Machine learning-based prediction of intellectual disability in children with autism spectrum disorder: using behavioral observation techniques
Authors: Xiaolin Liu, Ting Han, Zhongquan Jiang, Lifei Hu, Wenhao Li, Chao Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo construct and evaluate a LightGBM prediction model for intellectual disabilities in children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD).MethodsA total of 384 ASD children who completed the Wechsler Intelligence Test and Adaptive Behavioral Assessment System were included in the analysis. The LightGBM model was trained using behavioral observation data and underwent hyperparameter tuning and feature selection.ResultsAmong the ASD children, 32.9% had comorbid ID. Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by impaired social interactions, repetitive and stereotyped behaviors [1]. In recent years, the prevalence of ASD has been rapidly increasing worldwide. Data published by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in 2021 indicates that the prevalence of ASD in children is higher than 2.27%, meaning that one in every 44 children is diagnosed with ASD [2]. This reflects a growing impact on the health and social functioning of an increasing number of children, imposing significant economic burdens on their families and the society [3, 4].

Despite a steady improvement in public awareness of ASD [5, 6], misconceptions persist, such as the belief that individuals with ASD often possess exceptional abilities or talents. In reality, a substantial proportion of individuals with ASD also suffer from intellectual disabilities (ID), at a rate significantly higher than that of the general population. Recent epidemiological studies suggest that approximately 56% of individuals with ASD have an IQ below 70, with 31% of children with ASD classified as having ID, 25% in the borderline range (IQ 71–85), and 44% scoring in the average to above-average range (i.e., IQ > 85) [7]. ASD is a disorder with high heterogeneity, and differences in IQ are one of the sources of the heterogeneity. IQ is associated with the ability to acquire and apply specific skills for dealing with everyday situations, organizing, planning, and acting, and adapting flexibly to changing environments [8, 9]. Thus, Children with autism require tiered management and intervention tailored to their IQ levels [10]. Children with ASD who have an average level of intelligence are more likely to attend integrated schools where they can be exposed to general peers from whom they can learn complex social interactions [11–13]. In fact, children who “lose” their diagnosis (i.e., the severity of their symptoms reduces to the point that they no longer meet the criteria for an autism diagnosis) tend to have an average level of intelligence and to have been placed in inclusive educational settings [14–16], and received more intensive behavioral treatment between the ages of 2 and 3 years old [17]. In contrast, ASD children with ID may have more comorbidities, such as epilepsy or self-injury and other behavioral problems [6, 18], which exacerbate core symptoms and lead to greater difficulties in social adjustment, as well as greater healthcare costs [7]. Meanwhile, there is a dose-response relationship between the duration of weekly intervention (dose) and IQ (response), indicating that children with ASD and comorbid ID may benefit from intensive intervention to improve their IQ level [10]. Therefore, early identification of children with ASD and prediction of their intellectual levels can help optimize intervention plans and promote the implementation of personalized, precise interventions.

Numerous clinical studies have confirmed that early, comprehensive, and intensive intervention can yield significant and long-term benefits for children with ASD [19]. However, to date, ASD is considered the result of a combination of genetic and environmental factors, with a high degree of heterogeneity and an unclear pathogenesis, lacking accurate biomarkers [20]. Therefore, clinical practice relies heavily on the child’s behavioral symptoms and information provided by caregivers for diagnosis. This dependence on the experience of a team of specialists and the time spent observing the child necessitates the use of standardized neuropsychological diagnostic tools, such as the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS), which is regarded as the “gold standard” for ASD diagnosis, and the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R), which is the most commonly used diagnostic tool for ASD. Additionally, according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), ID is characterized by a deficiency in intellectual and adaptive functions across conceptual, social, and practical domains, which manifests during the developmental period. Intellectual dysfunction, as confirmed through clinical evaluation and individualized, standardized intelligence assessments, is one of the diagnostic criteria for ID, and the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC) is the most commonly used standardized tool for assessing IQ [21], also applicable to children with ASD. However, most clinicians will not diagnose ID in preschool-aged children, it is typically referred to as developmental delay until at least school age. Meanwhile, the intellectual assessment results of ASD children in early childhood are not stable [22]. Therefore, there is a need for a simple and effective diagnostic method to aid in the early identification of preschool children with ASD who may have comorbid ID.

Regression analysis is a common diagnostic method in the study of pediatric neurodevelopmental disorders [23–27]. However, traditional regression methods are struggle to analyze higher-order nonlinear relationships [26, 28–30]. Machine learning (ML) is a set of computational methods that can discover complex nonlinear relationships between inputs and outputs and has been widely used in disease diagnosis and health research [25, 28, 31, 32].

Most previous research on diagnostic models has focused on diagnostic imaging, such as head MRI and EEG, and on biochemical markers, such as metagenomic biomarkers [33], with less emphasis on behavioral observation data. Meanwhile, there are diagnostic models based on behavioral observation data that aid in early screening for various degrees of autism [34], however, research on the early screening of preschool children with ASD who may have comorbid ID is limited [35, 36]. In developing countries with scarce and unevenly distributed medical resources, pediatric specialists in developmental and behavioral disorders are particularly rare. Therefore, an accessible, simple, and effective screening tool is required to reduce the strain on the healthcare system. This study aims to integrate machine learning with behavioral observation data of children with ASD, applying ML methods to the early screening of preschool children who may have comorbid ID, and optimize the diagnostic model through feature selection. Thus, the final model will offer a new potential clinical utility to assist in the early identification of preschool autistic children with potential ID, enabling timely referrals to specialists for further evaluation and individualized intervention.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This study retrospectively collected data from children with ASD who attended the Department of Developmental and Behavioral Sciences in Children’s Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine between January 2021 to September 2023. These children met the diagnostic criteria for ASD in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). We collected sociodemographic information, including gender and age, and conducted behavioral observations with the assistance of developmental behavioral physicians who had obtained ADOS assessment qualifications when these children and their parents first attended our department. Behavioral observation items were developed based on the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS). First, linguistic abilities were assessed and categorized into three types: non-verbal and single words, phrase speech, and fluent language. Other behavioral observations included: Autism-related Intonation (AutismIntonation), Stereotyped Language (StereotypeLang), Gestures and/or Postures (GesturePosture), Unusual Eye Contact (UnusualEye), Facial Expressions towards Others (FacialExpression), Sharing Enjoyment in Interactions (SharedJoy), Qualities of Expressing Social Intentions (SocialIntQualities), Quantity of Expressing Social Intentions: towards Evaluators (SocialIntQuantity), Quality of Social Reactions (SocialReactQual), Overall Quality of Relationships (RelationshipQual), Imagination/Creativity (ImagCreativity), Unusual Sensory Interests (SensoryInterests), Special Movements of Hands and Fingers (HandFingerMoves), Self-Injurious Behavior (SelfInjury), Repetitive Interests or Stereotyped Behaviors (RepetitiveInterests), Excessive Activity Level (HighActivity), Temper Tantrums/Aggressive or Disruptive Behavior (TantrumBehavior), and Anxiety (Anxiety). The scoring criteria are listed in the table below (Supplemental Table 1). Then, these children and their parents revisited our department for follow-up and completed IQ and adaptive assessments. The average follow-up duration was 343.50 ± 260.93 days. Intelligence level was assessed using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children, Fourth Edition (WISC-IV), which is suitable for adolescents aged 6–16, performed by qualified developmental behavioral physicians. Adaptive behavior was assessed using the Adaptive Behavior Assessment System, Second Edition (ABAS-II), which is suitable for adolescents aged 6–18, completed by parents. Children scoring below 70 on the WISC-IV and exhibiting adaptation difficulties on the ABAS-II were diagnosed as ASD comorbid with Intellectual Disability (ID). The study utilized sociodemographic information and behavioral observation data as variables and WISC-IV and ABAS-II assessment results as outcomes. See Fig. 1.

Model training and performance evaluation

According to the “no free lunch” theory of machine learning, 14 mainstream machine learning algorithms were introduced in the model training, encompassing common machine learning classifiers: Logistic Regression (LR), Gradient Boosting Classifier (GBC), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), Ada Boost Classifier (AdaBoost), Naive Bayes (NB), Decision Tree Classifier (DT), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Extra Trees Classifier (ET), Random Forest Classifier (RF), Dummy Classifier (Dummy), K Neighbors Classifier (KNN), SVM - Linear Kernel (SVM), Ridge Classifier (Ridge). The machine learning model with the best overall performance on the training set was included in the subsequent evaluations.

Data were split into a training set and a test set with a ratio of 6:4. During the modeling phase, data preprocessing and feature engineering were conducted exclusively on the training set to prevent data leakage. Data preprocessing included (1) the application of min-max normalization and (2) the removal of missing values and zero-variance variables. Variable selection is crucial to the final model’s performance. Consequently, this study employed the Boruta method [37], a feature selection approach built using random forest classifiers. It assesses the importance of variables by comparing the relevance between true features and shadow features. While traditional feature selection algorithms often use a filtering method, thus discarding potentially relevant features to minimize error, Boruta is a wrapper method that identifies all relevant feature sets through a fully correlated strategy, preserving even weakly predictive factors. Hyperparameter selection for the model was carried out using grid search with 10-fold cross-validation. Model discrimination was evaluated using metrics such as Accuracy, AUC-ROC, Precision, F1-score, Kappa, and MCC.

Additionally, the interpretability of the best model was assessed using the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method, focusing on the main effects and interactions of key features. The SHAP method, derived from game-theory-based SHAP values, illustrates the importance of variables and determines the direction of effects.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described using mean ± standard deviation (for normally distributed and homoscedastic variables) or median with interquartile range (IQR, for skewed distributions), while categorical variables were represented by percentages. Chi-square (χ2) or Fisher’s exact test were utilized to compare proportions, and t-tests and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to compare means. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. This study was conducted using Python 3.8 and R 4.2.0, with a random seed set to 123. Model construction, tuning, interpretability evaluation, and parts of the discrimination evaluations were performed using PyCaret 2.3.10.

A total of 384 ASD children were included in the study, with an average age of 5.10 ± 1.49 years and an average follow-up duration of 343.50 ± 260.93 days. Interpretability analysis

The selected LightGBM model demonstrated good performance in predicting preschool ASD children who may have comorbid ID. To further address the black box issue of the LightGBM model, the SHAP method was employed to evaluate its interpretability. The x-axis shows the SHAP values, representing each feature’s impact on the model’s prediction. The y-axis lists the features. Colors indicate feature values from low (blue) to high (red). The SHAP results indicated that the top three predictive features were Overall Quality of Relationships (RelationshipQual), Unusual Sensory Interests (SensoryInterests), and Gestures and/or Postures (GesturePosture) (see Fig. 2). The variables predominantly exhibited a unidirectional influence on the prediction of ASD comorbid ID, for example, by raising the scores for the top three features (Overall Quality of Relationships, Unusual Sensory Interests, and Gestures and/or Postures), the likelihood of predicting ID in children with ASD also increases.

Discussion

Machine learning in predicting ASD with intellectual disabilities

ML has increasingly been applied to the diagnosis, subtyping, and differentiation of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) in recent years [38]. Kupper et al. constructed a Support Vector Machine diagnostic prediction model using ADOS module scores, achieving an AUC of up to 0.86 [39], while Bone et al. classified children with ASD using ADIR module scores and SRS scale scores, reaching a sensitivity of 0.89 [40]. ML-constructed predictive models can facilitate auxiliary diagnosis and early warning, thus improving diagnostic efficiency and reducing the pressure on the healthcare system [41]. The present study built a predictive model to determine whether preschool children with ASD may have comorbid ID using behavioral observation data based on ADOS assessments and ML methods. The results indicate that ML can effectively differentiate whether preschool children with ASD may also have ID. Prior to model construction, substantial data preprocessing was undertaken, such as outlier detection, missing value imputation, normalization, and feature selection. The data integrity in our study exceeded 99%, showing a good data quality for prediction. The Boruta method was found to perform optimally for feature selection in our previous research [42]. According to the “no free lunch” theorem of machine learning, we ranked 14 mainstream ML algorithm models by AUC, with LightGBM showing the best sensitivity and accuracy (0.793 and 0.760), making it the optimal predictive model. Additionally, we used the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method to assess the interpretability of the best model, focusing on the main effects and interactions of key features to further explore the influence between them.

LightGBM demonstrated superior AUC in this study primarily due to its efficient gradient boosting algorithm design. Compared to traditional SVM and random forest models, LightGBM ‘s leaf-wise tree growth strategy enables better capture of complex nonlinear relationships and feature interactions in behavioral data. Additionally, its native support for categorical features and missing values enhances model robustness and generalizability. Combined with high-quality features selected via the Boruta method, LightGBM effectively leverages key information in the data to improve predictive accuracy. These advantages make LightGBM an ideal choice for assisting in the diagnosis of ASD with comorbid ID.

Clinical implications of the predictive model

The intellectual assessment results of ASD children in early childhood are not stable [22]. Using the LightGBM model to screen and predict whether these children might have ID based on their behavioral observations could, if positive, suggest the need for early cognitive function training and further genetic testing when necessary. This would help in formulating subsequent personalized intervention plans. Our study found that the top three key variables in the best LightGBM model were Overall Quality of Relationships, Unusual Sensory Interests, and Gestures and/or Postures.

The diagnostic model indicate that the higher the scores for unusual sensory interests in children with ASD, the higher the risk of comorbidity with ID. It is widely accepted that sensory perception forms the foundation of cognitive development, unusual sensory interests and repetitive stereotyped behaviors are among the core symptoms of ASD children but are not unique to autism; they are also seen in children with developmental and language disorders [43]. Therefore, the prediction model constructed in this study is only applicable to children with ASD. Unusual sensory interests occur in up to 90% of individuals with ASD [44, 45], and affect every sensory modality from early development [46, 47]. Moreover, although most individuals with ASD exhibit symptoms in more than one sensory domain, the average sensory domain scores are higher in younger, lower-functioning groups, suggesting they are affected in more areas than higher-functioning groups [48, 49]. Sensory symptoms may also predict higher-order deficits in social and cognitive functions in adults [50]. Some studies have already found sensory processing differences between high-functioning and low-functioning autistic patients (based on IQ assessment) at the neuroelectrophysiological level [51]. Different patterns of sensory abnormalities could stratify the ASD population into more homogenous subgroups to study common underlying neurobiological changes. To date, interventions for sensory abnormalities in children with ASD have mixed evidence for improvement [52], and whether IQ levels can be increased remains to be further studied.

Types of gestures can be categorized as directional, instrumental, and descriptive gestures. Although impairment of non-verbal communication are among the core symptoms of ASD children, there are differences between children with High-Functioning Autism (HFA) and those with Low-Functioning Autism (LFA). Studies have indicated that individuals with HFA exhibit lower sensitivity to biological motion [53] and impaired cross-modal processing of speech and co-expressive iconic gestures compared to typically developing individuals [54]. Individuals with LFA tend to communicate through very primitive means, such as manipulating people or objects or using simple gestures [55]. Existing meta-study analyses confirm that understanding and producing gestures are involved in subsequent language acquisition, with early gesture production significantly related to more advanced language abilities in later development [56]. Therefore, gestures can predict language ability in children with autism [57], particularly, directional and descriptive gestures [58]. Although language disorder (LD) is no longer a core symptom of ASD, it is a common comorbidity, with about 65% of children with ASD suffering from it [59]. Our previous studies [42] has found that language ability is an important predictor of comorbid ID in ASD, with poorer language ability indicating a higher risk of comorbid ID. Gesture interventions have been found to improve vocabulary in typically developing (TD) children [60], suggesting that including gestures in ASD intervention plans, especially in the pre-linguistic stages, could be very beneficial. However, some studies have indicated that for individuals with High-Functioning Autism (HFA), pairing dynamic iconic gestures with co-expressive speech may not be beneficial; they require more naturalistic learning environments [54]. So, personalized intervention plans are important.

The overall quality of relationships (RelationshipQual) was the top predictive feature of ASD comorbid ID in our study. It is a summative evaluation of the social interaction between the evaluator and child during the ADOS assessment, reflecting the degree to which the evaluator must adjust their behavior to maintain interaction, i.e., how much assistance the child needs. Meta-analysis research has found that a reduction in the severity of autistic symptoms is associated with being female, having a higher IQ, and/or not having ID [61].

Application of the diagnostic model

Our findings suggest that the LightGBM model has relative superiority in clinical benefit and discriminative ability for ASD comorbid ID. Healthcare providers can collect the necessary behavioral observation data for the diagnosis of children with ASD and use the recommended LightGBM model to predict the risk of comorbid ID when initial IQ assessment results (such as WISC-IV) are unstable. Classifying these children is a prerequisite for formulating personalized health guidance and intervention plans.

Our study has several potential advantages. First, we used behavioral observation data to build a low-cost, easily accessible diagnostic model. Second, the optimized LightGBM model is computationally efficient and can calculate quickly in real-time applications. Furthermore, we performed a relatively complete data preprocessing, ensuring the treatment effect of the predictive model. Finally, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the predictive model, including traditional indicators (identification and calibration) and clinical utility analysis (decision curves and prediction curves). To further explore the interpretability of ML models, the SHAP method was also utilized.However, in our study, the sex ratio is heavily skewed towards males, which may be expected in ASD samples but nevertheless limits generalizability. Meanwhile, the data in this study comes from a single site, which affects population diversity, may lead to potential bias, affecting the model’s generalization ability and potentially impacting the accuracy of predictions. Therefore, further validation in more diverse or external populations, or prospective testing, is necessary to verify the effectiveness of the predictive model.

To improve model generalizability and predictive accuracy, future research could consider expanding sample sizes, incorporating multi-center datasets to increase population diversity, and integrating multimodal data sources such as neuroimaging or genetic information. Additionally, exploring advanced feature engineering techniques and ensemble learning methods may further enhance model performance. Further research is also needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms to better inform clinical practice and to determine whether early interventions targeting these key behavioral domains can reduce the risk of comorbid ID in ASD.

Limitations

The limitations of our study include a relatively small sample size in the follow-up cohort, as the persuasiveness of ML training models increases with larger sample sizes. Moreover, the sex ratio is heavily skewed towards males, and the data comes from a single site, which affects population diversity, may lead to potential bias, affecting the model’s generalization ability and potentially impacting the accuracy of predictions. Meanwhile this study predicts ID in school-age children based on early behavioral observation data of ASD children, Overall Quality of Relationships, Unusual Sensory Interests, and Gestures and/or Postures were identified as the top three key variables. While some related studies have made similar findings, the complex biological and psychological mechanisms behind them are still unclear. Further research is needed to clarify these mechanisms to guide clinical practice.

Conclusion

Based on behavioral observation data from children with ASD in Zhejiang Province, China, this study demonstrates that machine learning models offer significant clinical utility in predicting comorbid ID in preschool-aged children. Among the evaluated algorithms, the LightGBM model exhibited the optimal predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.747, with a sensitivity of 0.793 and an accuracy of 0.760. The model identified Overall Quality of Relationships, Unusual Sensory Interests, and Gestures/Postures as the most critical predictive features. These findings suggest that early screening using this model can facilitate timely referrals and the development of personalized intervention strategies for high-risk children.
