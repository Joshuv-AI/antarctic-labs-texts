Anti-Nonstructural Protein 1 Antibody Kinetics and Their Association With Disease Severity in Pediatric Dengue
Authors: Sansanee Noisakran, Patchanika Hengtrakool, Kattaleeya Jindapornprasert, Chunya Puttikhunt, Tanapan Prommool, Nattaya Tangthawornchaikul, Nuttapong Kaewjiw, Pucharee Songprakhon, Kanokwan Sriruksa, Wannee Limpitikul, Dumrong Mairiang, Panisadee Avirutnan, Prida Malasit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue is an immune-mediated disease with severe manifestations often linked to secondary infections. Dengue virus nonstructural protein 1 (DENV NS1) and corresponding antibodies are thought to play a crucial role in disease pathogenesis.MethodsThis study investigated the kinetics of anti-NS1 antibody responses in relation to NS1 levels and NS1 immune complexes (ICs) in pediatric dengue patients with varying disease severity following secondary DENV infections with all four serotypes, using retrospective plasma samples collected in Thailand and analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs). Plasma samples from pediatric patients with other febrile illnesses (OFI) and healthy adults served as non-dengue control groups.ResultsAnti-NS1 IgG (particularly IgG1 and IgG4) and IgA persisted for at least 6 months after illness and were associated with increased dengue severity, whereas the shorter-lived anti-NS1 IgM was higher in patients with milder dengue. Anti-NS1 IgG also persisted notably longer than anti-DENV IgG. Anti-NS1 IgG3 showed a transient surge across all four serotypes, suggesting its potential as a marker of recent infection. NS1 proteins and NS1 ICs were positively correlated, inversely related to anti-NS1 IgG, and strongly associated with severe dengue.ConclusionsAnti-NS1 antibody isotypes and IgG subclasses exhibited distinct associations with dengue severity, influenced by both infecting serotypes and time since illness onset. Correlations among NS1, anti-NS1 antibodies, and NS1 ICs were observed during the acute phase of secondary infection, potentially influencing dengue disease outcomes. These findings enhance our understanding of dengue immunopathogenesis and may inform improved diagnostic and surveillance tools.Keywords: anti-NS1 antibody, antibody isotypes and IgG subclasses, dengue, immune complexes, NS1 protein

Dengue, a mosquito-borne viral disease caused by four dengue virus (DENV) serotypes, represents a significant global health burden [1]. Clinical manifestations of dengue vary widely, ranging from asymptomatic infection and mild dengue fever (DF) to severe manifestations such as dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) or dengue shock syndrome (DSS) [2]. While primary DENV infections often result in DF or subclinical disease, subsequent infections with a different serotype can increase the risk of severe outcomes like DHF and DSS [3]. The underlying mechanisms driving this increased severity in secondary infections remain poorly understood, hindering the development of effective treatments and predictive tools. Currently, there are no specific therapies for severe dengue, and the available vaccines have limited efficacy and applicability, particularly in populations without previous DENV exposure [4].

DENV infection leads to viremia and the release of viral nonstructural protein 1 (NS1) into circulation, which in turn trigger a complex host immune response [5]. NS1 exists in various forms within infected cells and extracellularly, and elevated levels have been linked to increased disease severity [6–8]. This protein plays a multifaceted role in DENV infection, contributing to viral replication, immune evasion, and endothelial disruption [6, 9, 10]. The host immune system responds to NS1 by producing anti-NS1 antibodies following primary and secondary infections [6, 11–13]. These antibodies have been implicated in both protective and pathogenic roles during DENV infection [14].

Studies in cell cultures and animal models have demonstrated that anti-NS1 antibodies can induce protective responses against DENV-associated endothelial dysfunction and hemorrhage [15–18]. In children, these antibodies have been associated with protection against symptomatic DENV infection [19]. However, anti-NS1 antibodies can also contribute to disease pathogenesis. For instance, the formation of immune complexes (ICs) between NS1 antigens and anti-NS1 antibodies can activate the complement system, potentially exacerbating inflammation and vascular leakage [7]. Furthermore, cross-reactivity of anti-NS1 antibodies with human proteins and cells has been implicated in abnormal coagulation, thrombocytopenia, and endothelial damage [20, 21].

Despite the recognized importance of anti-NS1 antibodies in dengue, their precise role in disease pathogenesis and their potential as diagnostic or prognostic markers, particularly in secondary infections, remain unclear. Previous studies investigating anti-NS1 antibody responses in patients with dengue have yielded conflicting results regarding their association with disease severity and serotype specificity [11–13, 22]. These discrepancies may be attributed to variations in study design, sample size, sampling time points, infecting serotypes, and patient demographics.

This study aimed to address these knowledge gaps by comprehensively characterizing the kinetic profiles of anti-NS1 antibody isotypes and IgG subclasses in pediatric patients with secondary DENV infections of varying severity. We further investigated the relationship between these antibody responses, NS1 antigen levels, and the formation of NS1 ICs, with a focus on their potential association with disease severity. Our findings provide valuable insights into the complex interplay between NS1, anti-NS1 antibodies, and disease pathogenesis in secondary DENV infection, with implications for the development of improved diagnostic and prognostic tools.

METHODS

Cell Lines, Viruses, and Antibodies

Aedes albopictus mosquito cells (C6/36) and African green monkey kidney cells (Vero) were cultured and infected with all four DENV serotypes (strains Hawaii, 16681, H87, and H241) [23]. Mouse anti-NS1 (clones 2E11 and NS1-1F) and anti-E (clone 4G2) monoclonal antibodies recognizing all four DENV serotypes [24] were produced in-house as described previously.

Human Plasma Samples

Longitudinal plasma samples (acute/convalescent phases; 2004–2012) were obtained from Thai pediatric patients (1–15 years) with secondary DENV infection (DF, n = 99; DHF, n = 101) or other febrile illnesses (OFI, n = 60) at Khon Kaen and Songkhla Hospitals (Ethical approval: Si 707/2016; details in the Supplementary Materials). Healthy adult controls (≥18 years, n = 50; samples from 2018) were also included. DENV infection was confirmed by RT-PCR, NS1 assays, and IgM/IgG ELISA [25]; disease severity classification used 1997 WHO criteria [26]. Sample sizes (n) for specific immunological assays varied due to limited pediatric plasma volumes; exact Ns are provided with relevant results or figure legends.

Determination of Anti-DENV IgM and IgG Antibodies

Anti-DENV IgM and IgG levels were determined by an in-house capture ELISA. Briefly, Nunc Maxisorp plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific), coated with rabbit anti-human IgM or IgG (Dako) (overnight, 4 oC) and blocked (0.5% BSA/PBS; 1 hour, 37 oC), received 1:100 diluted patient plasma, or positive (pooled dengue immune convalescent, PCS) and negative (pooled non-dengue immune, PND) sera. After incubation (1 hour, 37 oC), plates underwent sequential incubations (1 hour, 37 oC each, with appropriate washes) with (1) a mix of DENV 1–4-infected C6/36 culture supernatants, (2) mouse anti-E monoclonal antibody (4G2), and (3) horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Dako). Color development with 3, 3′, 5, 5′ –tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was stopped by 4 N H2SO4. Optical densities (ODs) were read at 450/620 nm (Anthos Microplate Absorbance Reader, Biochrom). Anti-DENV IgM/IgG levels were expressed as relative units [27]: (Sample OD − PND OD) × 100/(PCS OD − PND OD). Further details are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Determination of Anti-NS1 Antibody Isotypes and Anti-NS1 IgG Subclasses

Anti-NS1 isotypes/subclasses (IgM, IgA, IgG, and IgG1–4) were quantified by in-house indirect ELISAs. Nunc Maxisorp plates, coated with a mouse anti-NS1 monoclonal antibody (5 μg/ml; 4 oC, overnight) and blocked (3% BSA/PBS; 37 oC, 2 hours) were sequentially incubated (1 hour, 37 oC each, with washes) first with mixed DENV NS1 antigens [four serotypes (87.5 ng/ml each), affinity purified; or diluent (0.5% BSA/PBS) for background OD]. This was followed by 1:100 (or higher) diluted patient/healthy plasma or positive (PCS) and negative (PND) controls. Finally, relevant HRP-conjugated anti-human isotype/subclass-specific detection antibodies (IgG: Dako; IgM and IgG1–4: Southern Biotech; IgA: Jackson ImmunoResearch) were added. Subsequent steps (enzymatic reactions and OD measurements) followed the anti-DENV IgM/IgG protocol. Anti-NS1 levels were reported as relative units (RU): (Sample Dilution × [Sample OD − Background OD])/([PCS OD – Background OD]); positive > 30 RU). Further details are in the Supplementary Materials.

Determination of DENV NS1 Antigen–Antibody Immune Complexes

Nunc Maxisorp plates coated with a mouse anti-NS1 monoclonal antibody were incubated with patient plasma (1:100 dilution) from both acute and C1 convalescent phases (baseline control) for 1 hour at 37 oC. Plates were then incubated sequentially (1 hour each, 37 oC) with rabbit anti-human Ig (Southern Biotech) and HRP-conjugated mouse anti-rabbit Ig (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories). Enzymatic reactions and OD measurements were performed as described above. NS1 IC levels were reported as delta OD values (acute-phase OD − baseline OD). Detailed information is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9.3.1 (GraphPad Software). To compare anti-DENV and anti-NS1 antibody levels among three independent groups (DF, DHF, and OFI) across multiple longitudinal time points, the Kruskal–Wallis followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test was used. Changes in parameters within the same patient group across time points were assessed using the Friedman test, with Dunn's post-hoc test for pairwise analyses between time points. For direct comparisons of anti-NS1 antibodies, NS1 proteins, and NS1 ICs between two independent groups (DF and DHF) at each relevant time point, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed. Spearman correlation analysis was used to assess associations among these latter analytes. A P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Additional statistical details are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Patient Consent Statement. This study used retrospective samples collected from pediatric patients (1–15 years) with secondary DENV infections or other febrile illnesses and from healthy adults at Khon Kaen and Songkhla Hospitals. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Enrollment of pediatric patients in the cohorts was conducted with written consent from their parents or legal guardians. The study design was approved by the Ethics Committee of Siriraj Institutional Review Board (Certificate of Approval Number Si 707/2016), Mahidol University, Thailand.

RESULTS

Anti-NS1 and Anti-DENV IgM and IgG Responses in Secondary DENV Infection

Plasma samples were longitudinally collected from each patient with secondary DENV infection at the acute phase (day-1, one day before defervescence) and at three convalescent phases: C1 (days 7–27, median = 15 days), C2 (2–9 weeks after C1, ranging from days 28 to 79 and median = 59 days), and C3 (3.5–6 months after C2, ranging from days 153 to 212 and median = 182 days) after defervescence. Anti-NS1 antibody responses to a mixture of all four DENV serotypes were measured at each time point. These samples were also evaluated for anti-DENV antibodies (DENV-1 to DENV-4) to determine the kinetics of the overall antibody response and compare it to the NS1-specific antibody response. Both anti-NS1 and anti-DENV IgM and IgG peaked at C1 in patients with DF and DHF, and the IgG was predominant and persisted longer than the IgM following secondary DENV infection (Figure 1A). Compared with the baseline levels (IgM and IgG) in samples from patients with other febrile illnesses (OFI) (Figure 1A, B), anti-DENV antibodies had a higher frequency of detection in patients with DF and DHF than anti-NS1 antibodies at C1 (Figure 1B). Nevertheless, anti-NS1 IgG was detected at C3 with a markedly higher frequency than anti-DENV IgG among DF and DHF patient samples (Figure 1B). The persistence of anti-NS1 IgG up to C3 suggests its potential utility as a serological marker of past DENV exposure.

Anti-NS1 Antibody Isotype and IgG Subclass Responses in Patients With Different DENV Serotype Infections and Their Association With Disease Severity

Having analyzed the overall anti-NS1 antibody response in patients with secondary DENV infection (regardless of infecting serotypes), we next investigated whether the isotype and IgG subclass responses differed between DF and DHF patients infected with each individual DENV serotype.

To establish a baseline, we first analyzed anti-NS1 levels in non-dengue control groups recruited from Thailand, where dengue is endemic. In children with OFI, anti-NS1 IgM, IgG, and IgA, as well as all IgG subclasses, were detected at consistently low levels. Healthy adults exhibited higher levels of these antibodies, with some variations observed, except for anti-NS1 IgG3 (Figures 2A-C, 3A − B and 3D and Supplementary Figures 1−7 and 9). Notably, anti-NS1 IgG3 was mostly undetectable in both OFI patients and healthy adults compared to other antibodies (Figure 3C and Supplementary Figures 5 and 8).

Next, we examined the kinetic profiles of anti-NS1 antibody isotypes and IgG subclasses in DF and DHF patients. Overall, the isotypes and IgG subclasses showed similar kinetic response patterns over time in both patient groups, although some differences in antibody levels were observed (Figures 2−3 and Supplementary Figures 1–9). Anti-NS1 IgG was the predominant isotype in both DF and DHF patients, followed by IgA and IgM. Among the IgG subclasses, IgG1 and IgG3 were predominant during the convalescent phases, but IgG1 appeared to persist longer than IgG3 at C3. Anti-NS1 IgG2 peaked at C1 and subsequently decreased, while IgG4 seemed to increase over time, peaking at C3.

Despite the overall similarities in kinetic patterns, some notable differences were found between DF and DHF patients. In the convalescent phases, anti-NS1 IgG (specifically IgG1 and IgG4) and IgA were higher in DHF patients than in DF patients, whereas IgM was higher in DF patients. These differences were most pronounced following DENV-1 infection (Figures 2–3). Interestingly, anti-NS1 IgG3 exhibited a distinct pattern, being detectable in almost all DF and DHF patients at C1 and C2 but rapidly declining at C3 (Figure 3C), suggesting its potential as an indicator of recent DENV infection across all serotypes.

At the acute phase (day −1), anti-NS1 IgG (particularly IgG1) was the predominant antibody detected in both DF and DHF patients, followed by IgA (Figure 4A). Anti-NS1 IgG levels were significantly higher in DF patients than in DHF patients following DENV-2 infection at this time point (Figure 4A). Anti-NS1 IgM and other IgG subclasses were detected at very low frequencies in both patient groups at the acute phase (Figure 4A). Detection of low levels of anti-NS1 antibodies is very challenging for the assay conditions, at which the amount of available pediatric samples is a limiting factor.

Overall, this analysis reveals distinct kinetic patterns and differences in anti-NS1 antibody isotypes and IgG subclasses between patients with DF and DHF. Our results demonstrated higher levels of anti-NS1 IgG1, IgG4, and IgA in patients with DHF and anti-NS1 IgM in DF patients, particularly following DENV-1 infection. This suggests a potential association between these antibody responses and disease severity. The unique pattern of IgG3 further highlights its potential as a marker of recent DENV infection.

Association of NS1 Immune Complexes and NS1 Protein With Disease Severity

Severe dengue manifestations, such as plasma leakage and shock, typically occur around the time of defervescence [9]. To investigate the role of NS1 ICs in dengue severity, we analyzed paired plasma samples from DF and DHF patients at the acute phase (day −1) and C1 convalescent phase. These samples were compared to those from OFI patients as a baseline control. Notably, a higher percentage of DHF patients (57%) had detectable NS1 ICs at the acute phase compared to DF patients (31%), regardless of infecting DENV serotypes (Figure 4B). The frequency of IC detection was similar across different serotypes (14%–16% for DF vs 28%–30% for DHF) but was consistently higher in patients with DHF, particularly following DENV-2 infection (Figure 4B and Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, the levels of NS1 ICs were also significantly greater in DHF patients than in patients with DF, irrespective of infecting DENV serotypes (Figure 4B). Significant differences in IC levels were observed following DENV-2 and DENV-4 infections (Figure 4B). Taken together, these results indicate that both the frequency and levels of NS1 ICs are associated with increased dengue severity at the acute phase. Given that interactions between antigen and antibodies can occur spontaneously in circulation of human body, it is more likely that the NS1 ICs detected by our assay predominantly derive from those naturally present in circulation than those formed ex vivo during sample handling or assay performance.

In addition to ICs, we also measured DENV NS1 protein levels in the same acute-phase plasma samples. DENV NS1 was detectable at significantly higher levels in patients with DHF than in DF patients, regardless of infecting serotypes (Figure 4C). This trend was consistent across all four DENV serotypes (Figure 4C). To further explore the relationships between NS1, anti-NS1 antibodies, and NS1 ICs, we performed a correlation analysis. Anti-NS1 IgG and IgA showed significant positive correlations in both DF (r = 0.78) and DHF patients (r = 0.82), while no significant correlations were observed for IgM (Figure 5A). Interestingly, DENV NS1 was positively correlated with NS1 ICs, and it was negatively correlated with IgG and IgA (Figure 5A − B). A modest but statistically significant negative correlation between NS1 ICs and IgG was also observed (r = -0.261, P = .004). These findings suggest a complex interplay between DENV NS1, anti-NS1 antibodies, and IC formation during the acute phase of secondary DENV infection. The higher levels of NS1 and ICs observed in patients with DHF, coupled with the correlation analysis, further support their potential role in driving disease severity.

DISCUSSION

This study uniquely characterized anti-NS1 antibody kinetics alongside anti-DENV antibodies, NS1 proteins, and NS1 immune complexes (ICs) in Thai pediatric patients with varying severity of secondary DENV infections involving all four serotypes. In this endemic setting with common repeated exposures, longitudinal analysis showed that while both anti-DENV and anti-NS1 IgM/IgG were detectable in early convalescence, anti-NS1 IgG persisted notably longer (detectable at 6 months) than anti-DENV IgG. This differential persistence might reflect variations in the proportion and longevity of memory B cells targeting different viral antigens, as prior work on the prevalence and persistence of memory B cells against DENV E, prM, and NS1 proteins has been reported [28, 29].

Anti-NS1 IgG (mainly IgG1) and IgA were predominant and persisted for at least 6 months post-illness, consistent with prior reports of long-lasting anti-NS1 responses [12]. IgG's interaction with the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) potentially protects it from degradation and facilitates its recycling into circulation [30]. This extended persistence of anti-NS1 IgG, especially compared to anti-DENV IgG, strongly suggests its utility as a robust marker of past DENV exposure, valuable for pre-vaccination screening and serosurveillance. Low anti-NS1 antibody levels in patients with OFI (our endemic non-dengue febrile controls) could represent non-specific reactions or preexisting antibodies from previous DENV encounters. Notably, higher anti-NS1 IgG in healthy adults versus pediatric OFIs likely reflects cumulative DENV exposure with age, a pattern consistent with Thai seroprevalence data [31] and further supporting anti-NS1 IgG as an exposure marker.

Distinct kinetics were observed for anti-NS1 IgG subclasses. Anti-NS1 IgG3 was detectable in most patients in early convalescence but rapidly declined within 6 months, suggesting its utility as a pan-serotype marker of recent DENV infection, as previously noted for DENV-3 [12]; IgG3's unique structure may make it prone to rapid degradation [32]. In contrast, anti-NS1 IgG4 levels rose gradually, peaking at 6 months, similar to prior reports [12]. This delayed IgG4 response might be driven by cytokines like IL-4, IL-13, and IL-10 (associated with severe dengue and IgG4 class switching) [33, 34]; therefore, IgG4's specific role in dengue remains to be elucidated. Our assays, optimized for pediatric sample constraints, face challenges in measuring low-abundance antibodies that may have limited sensitivity for the least abundant anti-NS1 IgG subclasses; therefore, this should be kept in mind when considering their quantitative levels, particularly in early disease phases.

We found differing anti-NS1 isotypes and IgG subclass profiles between DF and DHF patients. Notably, pediatric DF patients had significantly higher acute-phase anti-NS1 IgG than DHF patients during DENV-2 infection. This finding contrasts with some studies in adults linking high acute anti-NS1 IgG with severe DENV-1/2 infections [22], while others found no such association with secondary DENV-3 [12]. These discrepancies underscore the complex interplay of infecting serotypes, host age, and other viral/host factors in shaping the anti-NS1 antibody responses and their association with dengue severity.

Despite variable anti-NS1 IgG associations, NS1 ICs were strongly related to dengue severity in our study. Acute-phase DENV NS1 and NS1 IC levels were significantly higher in DHF than DF patients, consistent with prior studies [8, 35, 36]. We found NS1 and NS1 ICs correlated positively with each other but negatively with anti-NS1 IgG. This suggests that while anti-NS1 antibodies may aid NS1 clearance [37], excessive ICs could drive pathogenesis by triggering inflammation and tissue damage [38]. As IgG1, predominantly in the anti-NS1 response, effectively fixed complement [39], IgG1-containing ICs could fuel hyperinflammation in severe dengue. Our findings thus link acute-phase NS1, anti-NS1 IgG (notably IgG1), and their ICs to dengue severity in secondary infections. While ELISAs for NS1 ICs face challenges in excluding ex vivo complex formation; the active NS1 release and antibody responses in dengue, plus our observed IC correlations with disease severity, suggest that our findings primarily reflect in vivo phenomena.

In convalescence, DF patients had higher anti-NS1 IgM, while DHF patients showed higher IgG (particularly IgG1 and IgG4) and IgA levels. These distinctions were most pronounced following DENV-1 infection, implying serotype influences on the antibody profile associated with disease outcome. This aligns with findings of elevated NS1-specific IgG1/IgG3 memory B cell responses in adults with a history of DHF versus DF, especially after DENV-1 infection [40], suggesting that convalescent anti-NS1 profiles might indicate past severity and potentially shape future infection outcomes. Anti-NS1 antibody profiles also reportedly differ between symptomatic and asymptomatic DENV infections. Some studies suggest that higher levels or greater functionality (eg, mediating complement-dependent lysis or antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity) of preexisting anti-NS1 antibodies are associated with subsequent subclinical or less severe DENV infections, hinting at potential protective roles [19, 41]. Collectively, these studies, including our own, underscore the multifaceted and complex roles of anti-NS1 antibodies in dengue pathogenesis and protection.

Our study has several important methodological considerations. First, the specific DENV infection history for individual participants was unknown. This is a potential confounder, as past exposures significantly shape subsequent antibody levels, their dynamics, and association with disease severity upon a new infection. Second, our analytical strategy prioritized direct comparisons of antibody levels at key discrete immunological time points; however, formal kinetic modeling of antibody dynamics may offer valuable insights into individual response parameters and represent an avenue for future research. Third, while our analysis focused on DF versus DHF comparisons, we did not correlate immunological markers with finer DHF severity grades (eg, WHO I-IV) due to insufficient subgroup sample sizes for statistically robust analysis; larger, specifically powered DHF cohorts are needed for these granular investigations. Finally, our study did not perform a direct comparative analysis of anti-NS1 antibody magnitudes between different currently infecting DENV serotypes within the same disease group because the immunological complexities of secondary DENV infections make it difficult to attribute differences in total antibody levels solely to the immunogenicity of the current infecting serotype.

We also considered potential cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses due to NS1 sequence similarities [42]. Although DENV NS1 antibodies can cross-react with NS1 from some flaviviruses [43, 44], studies report minimal to no cross-reactivity with Zika, Japanese encephalitis, yellow fever, or West Nile virus antibodies against DENV NS1 [12, 44–46]. Thus, significant influence from such cross-reactivity on our findings is unlikely.

In conclusion, our study identified distinct anti-NS1 antibody isotype and IgG subclass patterns in pediatric secondary DENV infections, with profiles differing between DF and DHF patients, suggesting roles in modulating disease severity. The strong acute-phase association between NS1 ICs and dengue severity further highlights the complex interplay of NS1, its antibodies, and pathogenesis in these infections. Further investigation, such as in-depth characterization of immune cells involved in orchestrating and sustaining the anti-NS1 antibody responses, is warranted to delineate the precise mechanisms by which these antibodies contribute to protection or pathogenesis, which could ultimately inform the development of improved vaccines and therapies.
