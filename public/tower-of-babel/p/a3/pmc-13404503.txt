Impact of different degrees of social engagement interventions (DDSEIs) on cognitive performance and brain activity in community-dwelling adults with subjective cognitive decline in Nanjing, China: a randomised controlled trial protocol
Authors: Jiahuan Li, Xi Yang, Hua Xu, Wei Pan, Chunxu Shi, Nan Xia, Yuqi Cheng, Fanfan Meng, Hanbo Li, Jin Liu, Ming Qi, Tong Wang, Jan D Reinhardt, Yi Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSocial isolation may have adverse effects on incidence and severity of cognitive impairment including subjective cognitive decline (SCD). An increase of environmental variety and social interactions may furthermore reduce progression from SCD to dementia. Research evaluating respective interventions is limited, particularly as regards studies comparing different degrees of social engagement interventions. We thus developed a clinical trial protocol to determine the efficacy of social interventions with different intensities in protecting cognitive function and white matter integrity in socially isolated adults aged 45–85 with SCD.Methods and analysisThis is a single-centre, three-arm randomised controlled trial with a 1:1:1 randomisation ratio, conducted from 1 December 2023 to 30 December 2026. The trial evaluates three study arms over a 12-week period: two social intervention arms (a social group intervention and a one-to-one social interaction intervention), both of which adopt a comprehensive model centred on social interaction and integrated with structured aerobic exercise and cognitive training; and a usual care arm in which participants receive only weekly health education. The sample size is estimated at 132 participants with SCD aged 45–85 years residing in Nanjing communities, China. Participants will be assigned to one of the three study groups via block randomisation stratified by gender. The principal outcome is improvement in mnemonic performance with delayed recall in the HuaShan version of the auditory verbal learning test between baseline and week 12. Secondary outcomes include improvement in white matter integrity measured by fractional anisotropy, along with changes in executive function, behavioural indicators and serum biomarkers assessed at baseline and week 12. Furthermore, follow-up evaluations with cognitive scales and serological data will take place at 3 months post-intervention.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval for this trial has been obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (approval No. 2023-SR-726.A1). Written informed consent will be secured from all participants before they are enrolled in the study. Findings from the trial will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed academic journals.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400080312.Keywords: Clinical Protocols, Cognitive dysfunction, Community Participation, GERIATRIC MEDICINE

Introduction

Subjective cognitive decline (SCD)1 and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) are early clinical stages of Alzheimer’s disease (AD),2 and it is estimated that within 3 years up to 40–62% of people with SCD process to MCI or AD combined.3 AD, in turn, stands as the world’s foremost cause of dementia, with no curative therapy available in sight.4 While lecanemab, a targeted therapeutic drug designed to clear pathological deposits of amyloid β-protein (Aβ) that may cause AD, has been approved for market, research has shown that this drug can only delay the progression of the disease in about 27% of subjects, and the earlier the treatment is initiated, the better the outcome.5 6 Given the limitations of current drug therapy and the lack of other effective clinical treatments, new community-based interventions that can prevent the deterioration of cognitive ability may provide a viable alternative.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention of USA,7 11.2% of people over 45 years report SCD, with a higher prevalence of 13.8% in those living alone as well as subjects with chronic diseases (15.2%). Subjects with SCD appear to withdraw from social and leisure activities 5–10 years before cognitive conversion to clinical dementia.8 The risk of conversion (to dementia) is dramatically increased in people with SCD who live alone. High loneliness, low group participation and infrequent social contact raise the risk of dementia by roughly 50%.9 Social isolation is strongly linked to cognitive function, with older adults who have closer social ties demonstrating better cognitive performance.10 Moreover, social interactions exert a protective effect on well-being, fostering more positive emotions and reducing feelings of loneliness. Additionally, the size and composition of social networks are known to impact the onset of dementia and AD as well. Individuals with smaller social networks had a 60% higher risk of dementia onset in a study by Fratiglioni and associates,11 whereas broader networks and more frequent social interactions are linked to a lower risk of dementia.12 13 Thus, there appears to be a positive correlation between cognitive function and the intensity of social interactions as well as the size of social groups. At the same time, in emerging countries such as China proportions of socially isolated older people are increasing dramatically due to higher mobility of younger people and an erosion of the traditional role of the family.14 15

Neuroimaging has shown that socially isolated individuals exhibit reduced grey matter volume in regions including the frontal lobe, temporal lobe and hippocampus, among others.16 17 This finding aligns with evidence that social isolation or loneliness correlates with neuroanatomical changes linked to AD vulnerability, thereby heightening the risk of AD and related dementia.18 Consequently, social isolation emerges as a valuable target for interventions aimed at slowing the progression from SCD to dementia.

To date, interventions studied to reduce social isolation include: (1) personal contact (regular contact with volunteers or relatives and friends); (2) group team contact (group social contact: (a) group participation: art or chorus, physical exercise, (b) group discussion); (3) animal assisted therapy; (4) skill guidance: internet training, mindfulness stress reduction, self-emotion management, music; (5) others: recall, public broadcasting, long-term care intervention, religious belief.19 Such interventional measures seem to be able to improve cognitive function in elderly persons to varying degrees.13 20–24 On the other hand, a variety of interventions appear to be conducive to reducing loneliness, and the more social components these interventions include, the more pronounced the effect seems to be.25–27

However, most of these interventions are conducted in healthy elderly persons or people with MCI, and studies among socially isolated older adults with SCD are lacking (see online supplemental table S1 for prior trials of social interventions for dementia onset and progression). To date, there have been no high-quality randomised controlled trials investigating the role of different intensities of social interaction in preventing the progression of SCD to AD.

Primary objective

To evaluate the efficacy of a 12-week group-based social interaction intervention in exerting better improving effects on memory performance-specifically episodic memory-compared with one-to-one social interaction or regular care alone in socially isolated people with SCD.

Secondary objectives

To assess the trends and correlations of changes in white matter integrity (measured by fractional anisotropy, FA) as well as neurocognitive, behavioural and serum biomarkers (eg, Aβ 1-42, P-tau-181) from baseline to 12 weeks post-intervention and at 3 months after the end of the intervention.

To investigate the impacts of social interventions on global cognitive function (assessed using Mini Mental Status Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)), executive function (measured via Trial Making Test A and B (TMT-A/B)), language ability (evaluated through Boston Naming Test (BNT)) and blood samples (eg, blood lipids and glucose) at 3-month follow-up, with a specific focus on exploring their long-term effects.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

This trial is designed as a single-centre, three-arm randomised controlled study involving a 12-week intervention phase followed by a 12-week follow-up. Its protocol was developed in adherence to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) 2013 statement. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University on 1 November 2023 (Reference No.: 2023-SR-726). Partial revisions were subsequently approved on 7 April 2026 (Reference No.: 2023-SR-726.A1). Additionally, the trial was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/) on 25 January 2024, with registration number ChiCTR2400080312.

Trial setting

This trial will be carried out at local communities in Nanjing, China. The subjects will be selected from communities where the permanent resident population exceeds 5000.

Sample size

Using PASS V.11 software, the sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome (long-term recall score of HuaShan version of the auditory verbal learning test (AVLT-H)). Based on a pilot study, a minimal important mean difference of 1.0 point (1.5 vs 0.5) between at least two groups, an SD of 1.4, and a two-sided α level adjusted to 0.0167 (reflecting Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons among the three groups) were used for calculations; 37 subjects per group are required to achieve 80% power. Accounting for 15% loss to follow-up, the recruitment target is 44 per group (total n=132). No formal procedure to control false discovery rate is applied; however, it will be discussed based on the number of secondary outcomes for which statistically significant signals are detected in relation to expectation by chance (ie, 1.67% of tests).

Participant timeline

Each participant in this trial will go through six phases: study enrolment (comprising eligibility screening (V0) and baseline assessment (V1)), randomisation (V2), a 12-week intervention phase (V3), a post-intervention visit (V4) and a follow-up assessment (V5). Enrolment (V0)

We will publish recruitment advertisements via community health centres and recruit participants through their clinics. We will conduct a screening process to verify the study eligibility of individuals who express interest. A standardised pre-screening interview conducted via telephone will be used to gather information regarding medical and demographic data, as well as suitability for MRI. During the interview, certain questions will be posed to confirm the presence of SCD along with related concerns.28 Additionally, a chief issue of decreased cognitive function with a duration of at least 6 months is mandatory for study inclusion.

Potential participants who meet the inclusion criteria identified during pre-screening will be invited to attend an on-site screening visit at the community health service centre’s clinic. On arrival, each individual will complete a standardised screening evaluation, which includes neuropsychological tests and questionnaires, to verify that they have no objective cognitive impairment or severe psychiatric disorders. Those who are eligible will then proceed to sign the written informed consent form and continue with the baseline assessments.

Baseline visit (V1)

After signing the informed consent form, eligible participants will proceed to complete scale assessments, have their blood samples collected and undergo imaging scans. Details of the specific items can be found in table 2.

Randomisation methods (V2)

Randomisation process

A block randomisation sequence (with a block size of 6), stratified by gender, will be generated via a computer algorithm. Following eligibility verification, completion of consent procedures and baseline assessment, participants will be allocated at a 1:1:1 ratio by a study investigator. This investigator will not be involved in participant enrolment and, aside from the statistician who generated the sequence, will be the only individual with access to the block randomisation list. This investigator is also not involved in intervention delivery or outcome measurements.

Blinding

Given the nature of the intervention, participants cannot be blinded to their group assignment. The trial administrator and community physicians know the group assignment, but research staff including assessors, radiology technicians and statisticians will be blinded to the group assignment information.

Interventions (V3）

Group social intervention

Participants in this group will receive a group social intervention with five to eight participants per subgroup, three times per week for a total of 12 weeks. Each session will last 90 min. Each session is divided into three parts: (1) a 25-minute group discussion, in which the therapists choose one or two topics to guide the discussion and ensure each subject is participating in the discussion. (2) a 35-minute group aerobic dance, originally created by our team, that has demonstrated effectiveness in improving cognition of elderly persons with MCI.29 During the dance training, a sports bracelet will be used for real-time heart rate monitoring to ensure 30 min of moderate intensity. In addition, there will be a 2-minute warm-up and a 3-minute cool-down period: (3) 30 min of group handicraft work, which involves paper-cutting, knitting and assembling, drawing, building blocks, etc. Therapists will prepare handicraft materials beforehand; activities can be finished in one or two sessions depending on their difficulty. The whole process will be supervised by a well-trained therapist to ensure each individual participating in the handwork, and the whole process will be recorded by real-time video recording.

One-to-one social intervention

The participants in this group will be trained one-to-one by a well-trained therapist or caregiver at home for three times a week, a total of 12 weeks. The training session will last 90 min with the same contents and procedures as reported for the group intervention.

Usual care

Participants will receive a 10-minute health education session each week for 12 weeks. Sessions include guidance on diet, medications, mood regulation and daily activities.

Participant involvement in trial

Before the implementation of the intervention, it is essential to actively obtain the consent and support of the participants’ families. We will regularly provide feedback on the ‘health check’ results to all participants and their families, maintaining their interest in the study through reports and regular education sessions. Prior to the final data collection, we will communicate with the participants and their families, reminding them of the upcoming data collection and possible rewards. Through real-time communication and reports, we will provide regular updates, informing participants and their families about the current status of cognitive decline, the next phase of the trial plan and express our gratitude for their support. We will arrange the intervention times as flexibly as possible and actively resolve any timing conflicts for the participants to ensure their smooth participation and enjoyment of the entire research process.

Post-intervention visit (V4)

The post-intervention assessment will be conducted on completion of the 12-week intervention period.

Follow-up (V5)

Follow-up evaluations with cognitive scales and serological data will be conducted 3 months after the intervention ends.

Outcome measures

Study outcomes will be collected at three time points: the baseline assessment, the post-intervention assessment (12 weeks after baseline) and the follow-up assessment (12 weeks after intervention completion). The outcome items are summarised in table 2. Study outcomes will be collected by experienced physicians who have been practising for 5 years and are blinded to the allocation of the participants.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this trial is the change in memory function between baseline visit (V1) and post-intervention visit (V4). Memory performance is measured by long-term recall score (N5) of AVLT-H.30 AVLT-H has been demonstrated to outperform the MMSE and MoCA in identifying and diagnosing amnestic MCI.31

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes include changes in FA values of cognition-related white matter on diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) from baseline visit (V1) to post-intervention assessment (V4). Though brain structural changes typically require a longer duration, our team’s prior research demonstrated that 12 weeks (≈3 months) of aerobic exercise improves FA in MCI patients, specifically enhancing white matter integrity in memory-related brain regions such as the cingulate fasciculus and parahippocampal fasciculus. Given that MCI and SCD are both pre-dementia stages with overlapping pathological mechanisms of white matter microstructural damage, this finding supports the rationale for observing FA change trends in SCD patients after our 12-week intervention.

Withdrawal from the trial

Considering the long duration of the intervention and the multicomponent intervention, combined with the daily habits of middle-aged and elderly people living in Chinese communities, subjects may face the risk of suspension or even withdrawal from the intervention. Therefore, when each participant signs the informed consent form, we will emphasise the importance of continuous participation and try to coordinate their schedules to reduce the dropout rate from the source. However, for some special circumstances, we understand and respect the decision of subjects to withdraw from the trial. We have set clear withdrawal criteria to ensure the fairness and rigour of the trial are maintained (table 1).

If participants withdraw, an early termination visit will be arranged to collect final outcome data, including all measurements specified in this protocol.

Data collection methods

Data to be collected from each participant will include demographic data, medical information, neuropsychological data, behavioural data, blood sample data and imaging data (including DTI). A detailed account of the data collection procedures is provided in table 2. All research assessors, tasked with ensuring standardised data collection, will be physicians boasting over 5 years of clinical experience and having completed systematic professional training. Data collection will follow a two-step process: initial documentation on paper forms, followed by electronic data entry after each study visit—with continuous quality checks throughout the research period. To further ensure data integrity, regular monitoring procedures will be implemented as part of quality control.

Data management and monitoring

Documentation will be performed using paper-based case report forms (CRFs). For archiving purposes, completed CRFs will be carefully scanned and uploaded to a dedicated Baidu Netdisk assigned exclusively to this research project at the end of each month. A research staff member independent of trial implementation will manage the Netdisk password, and login requests must obtain approval from this member to safeguard data security. Data collectors and monitors must log off promptly after finishing data upload and verification.

Data monitoring will be conducted by researchers independent of original data collection and data entry. These researchers will routinely verify data entry accuracy by cross-checking electronic records against paper CRFs. If any anomalies are detected, they will immediately conduct a comprehensive review of all data collected within 1 month before and after the time of the abnormal data (relative to its collection date) to ensure data integrity and accuracy. In addition to daily monitoring, periodic meetings will be held with the primary investigator, data safety monitoring board members, data analysts, statisticians and trial assistants to achieve the following objectives: (1) to review subject recruitment, enrolment and withdrawal processes; (2) to monitor and assess participant safety and adaptability; (3) to request interim data analyses; (4) to deliberate on whether to continue or modify the study; and (5) to discontinue the trial if severe adverse events attributed to the programme occur.

Adverse events monitoring and reporting

Before the clinical trial begins, all participating researchers will receive training on the definition, classification and recording requirements of adverse events. During the trial, once an adverse event is discovered, information such as the time of occurrence, symptoms, severity and duration should be recorded immediately, and as much as possible, relevant examination results and medical records related to the adverse event should be collected. The researchers will promptly report any adverse event to the principal investigator, ethics committee and other relevant parties as per the trial protocol. The report should include detailed information about the adverse event, treatment measures and results. Serious adverse events must be documented within 24 hours of their discovery. During the monitoring and reporting of adverse events, data security and confidentiality will be ensured. All information involving participant privacy should be strictly confidential. At the same time, data backup and recovery strategies will be developed to ensure data security and integrity. Lessons learnt throughout the trial will be regularly summarised to refine the adverse event monitoring and reporting system. When problems or deficiencies are identified, relevant adjustments and improvements will be implemented promptly. In summary, the rights and safety of all participants will be safeguarded throughout the trial. Ongoing optimisation will enhance the quality and efficiency of the clinical trials.

Statistical methods

All data will be analysed using IBM SPSS software V.25.0 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois, USA). The collected data will be organised into three key datasets. The full analysis set (FAS) is based on intention to treat and includes all randomised participants. The per-protocol set consists of participants who adhered to the intervention at the minimum required frequency and completed the primary efficacy endpoints. The safety set is used for safety evaluations and includes all participants who were administered at least one treatment following randomisation. The primary analysis will focus on the FAS.

Continuous outcomes will be compared across the three groups using analysis of covariance, that is, essentially a regression model adjusting for baseline/pre-intervention measurements of the outcome in question; if statistically significant group effects (p<0.05) are discovered, post hoc analyses will employ independent-samples t-tests with Bonferroni adjustment (α=0.0167, corresponding to three pairwise comparisons). Normality of residuals will be tested with Shapiro-Wilk normality test, Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances will be employed to assess potential heterogeneity of group variances, and linearity as well as equality of regression slopes will be tested by estimating and comparing models with and without covariate (baseline measurement of outcome)-group interaction via likelihood ratio (LR) test for nested models. Possible confounding will be tested by estimating models that include covariates such as age, gender and disease characteristics and comparison with crude models using LR tests for nested models.

Missing data from participant withdrawal, loss to follow-up or other causes will be addressed via multiple imputation (m=10) under the missing-at-random assumption, using chained equations that incorporate all study variables. Sensitivity analyses will compare complete-case and imputed results. The missing at Random (MAR) assumption cannot be tested directly; however, non-MAR scenarios using controlled reference-based imputation will be used under the assumption that missing data in the intervention groups follow the observed patterns of the control group.48

In addition to absolute treatment effects, Cohen’s d as standardised effect size (with 95% CIs) and p values (with statistical significance defined as p<0.0167) will be reported.

Discussion

This trial will mark a groundbreaking clinical study examining how DDSEIs influence cognitive changes in individuals with SCD who reside in the community and experience social isolation. In this programme, we have meticulously designed and implemented multifaceted social intervention measures, specifically incorporating an aerobic dance programme independently developed by our research centre and proven to effectively enhance cognitive function in individuals with MCI.29 Multicomponent interventions tend to achieve more significant reductions in social isolation, possibly due to increased interaction frequency and sustained novelty of activities.26 49 Additionally, we have established a comprehensive multimodal assessment system, encompassing clinical scale assessments, neuroimaging indicators and serum biomarker analyses. Based on these multimodal assessment results, we anticipate not only generating new evidence regarding the benefits of multifaceted group social activities in improving cognitive function among socially isolated SCD individuals but also providing a preliminary hypothesis for exploring the mechanisms underlying social-induced cognitive improvement.

On the selection of outcome indicators, AVLT-H has been widely proven to outperform the MMSE and MoCA in identifying and diagnosing aamnestic MCI.31 With its detailed and precise assessment methods, AVLT-H provides a powerful tool for the detection of memory function. As a specialised form of MRI, DTI provides a non-invasive method to assess white matter integrity by measuring water diffusion properties. As a key DTI metric, FA is a quantitative marker of white matter fibre orientation and structural integrity.50

There are several possible limitations of our study. The scarcity of social activities among the target group may complicate participant recruitment and screening. To address this, we will intensify AD prevention promotion, raise awareness and collaborate closely with community workers to identify the target population. We will maintain communication with participants and families, offering professional counselling and health guidance to aid their understanding and coping. This aims to build trust, laying a foundation for interventions and ensuring timely, effective assistance. Second, the study sample is exclusively sourced from Nanjing city, potentially limiting its generalisability to the elderly population in other regions. Future research could conduct multiregional surveys and incorporate longitudinal studies to further validate the hypotheses of this study.

The trial aims to verify whether group-based high-participation social interventions can slow or even reverse cognitive decline and improve brain function among socially isolated patients with SCD. Additionally, the trial will further explore the beneficial effects of enriched social activities and exercise settings on cognition and cerebral function. Findings from this trial are expected to advance understanding of social interventions for cognitive improvement in socially isolated individuals with SCD and provide robust evidence and practical guidance for community service development.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (reference number: 2023-SR-726.A1) and will be conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki.
