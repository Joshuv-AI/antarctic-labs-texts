Nuclear waste radionuclides unveil three decades of anthropogenic carbon in the Canada Basin of the Arctic Ocean
Authors: Annabel Payne, Lorenza Raimondi, Anne-Marie Wefing, Christof Vockenhuber, Habacuc Pérez-Tribouillier, William Williams, Sarah Zimmermann, Núria Casacuberta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ocean-atmosphere CO2 exchange is key in modulating global climate, but the Arctic Ocean’s anthropogenic carbon (Cant) uptake is poorly constrained. Most studies in the Canada Basin using the Transit Time Distribution (TTD) relied on two years of paired gas tracers, CFC-12 and SF6. Here we use nuclear reprocessing products 129I, 236U, and 137Cs to estimate Cant in the Atlantic Water layer by the TTD. We present the evolution of Cant in the Canada Basin from 1993 to 2023, and a high-resolution distribution of Cant across the basin in 2022. We observe a basin-wide inventory increase in the Atlantic Layer (400–1000 m) of 5.5 ± 0.9 Tg C yr−1. Cant content in water entering the Canada Basin tracks increasing atmospheric input, time-lagged by 15-20 years; we anticipate Cant content of up to 65 μmol kg−1 in core Atlantic Water entering the basin by 2050.

Introduction

The Arctic is experiencing profound impacts due to anthropogenically driven increases in atmospheric CO2 concentrations. The region is warming nearly four times faster than the global average1. “Atlantification” of the Arctic Ocean in the Eurasian Basin is weakening the strength of the halocline2, with a projected total loss of summer sea ice by the end of the century3. In addition, warming and freshening of inflowing Pacific Water to the Amerasian Basin4, accelerated rates of acidification5, and decreasing O2 solubility in Pacific Waters an order of magnitude greater than the global average6 have all been observed.

The oceans play a critical role in the Earth’s climate by absorbing approximately 25–30% of anthropogenic emissions7. The Arctic Ocean stores an estimated 2–3% of the global inventory of anthropogenic carbon (Cant)8. However, anthropogenic carbon accumulation is a primary driver of ocean acidification, as the increased concentrations of CO2 lower seawater pH and reduce carbonate ion content. In the Chukchi and Beaufort Seas, the Mackenzie shelf and the Canadian Archipelago, aragonite undersaturation is already being observed as a result of Cant influenced acidification. Quantifying the accumulation of Cant in this region is therefore especially urgent9–14.

Atlantic Waters account for over 90% of total inflow to the Arctic Ocean15, and form three main inflowing components: the Norwegian Coastal Current Water (NCCW); the Fram Strait Branch Water (FSBW); and the Barents Sea Branch Water (BSBW) (Fig. 1a). The FSBW and BSBW are subducted in the St Anna Trough and form the mid-depth Atlantic Water (AW, σθ = 27.7–27.97 kg m−3, temperatures > 0 °C), and underlying dense Atlantic Water (dAW, σθ > 27.97 kg m−3, temperatures > 0 °C) throughout the Arctic Ocean. The AW is isolated from the atmosphere by a cold halocline, enabling long-term carbon storage16,17. The FSBW contributes to the temperature maximum (Tmax) seen in the core AW18–20. From here on in the text, we refer to core AW as being centered on the σθ = 27.93 kg m−3 isopycnal, corresponding to the Tmax of the layer. The dAW is centered on the σθ = 28.00 kg m−3 isopycnal. Once subducted to the intermediate layer, AW is transported cyclonically in the Arctic Ocean Boundary Current (AOBC)22, distributing Cant throughout the Eurasian and Amerasian basins (Fig. 1b), with eventual export to the North Atlantic. Ericson et al.17 found increasing trends of total dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) concentrations, 0.7–0.9 μmol kg−1 yr−1, in the Atlantic Water layers of the Amundsen Basin, and slightly lower rates in the Nansen and Makarov Basins. These rates correspond well with earlier observations from inflow regions, such as the Norwegian Atlantic Current and West Spitsbergen Current, where increasing Cant content of up to 1 μmol kg−1 yr−1 has been reported23,24. A recent assessment by Rajasakaren et al.21 confirmed these trends, finding the greatest increases in the Nansen and Amundsen basins (up to 0.7 μmol kg−1 yr−1), and lowest rates of increase in the Canada Basin (up to 0.2 μmol kg−1 yr−1).

Since Cant cannot be measured directly, being chemically indistinguishable from natural carbon, quantifying its uptake by the ocean is challenging, necessitating the use of models or other indirect methods25. These latter include, for example, the extended multiple linear regression (eMLR)26, the ΔC*27,28 or the Transit Time Distribution (TTD) method, a simple 1-dimensional model which we use for this work29,30. There are only a handful of studies using these various methods for investigating Cant in the Arctic Ocean [e.g., refs. 8,17,21,31–36]. While all conclude the Arctic Ocean is storing greater concentrations of Cant and shows increasing acidification, none so far have provided a detailed study in the Canada Basin; here, we present the most up-to-date assessment based on data collected up to year 2023.

The TTD method has often been used with transient gas tracers (e.g., chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and sulfur hexafluoride (SF6)), which the few previous studies of Cant in the Arctic Ocean have relied on [e.g., refs. 8,21]. These have a global input from air-sea gas exchange; however, their saturation in seawater is highly variable and, in the Arctic Ocean, can be strongly affected by physical conditions such as sea-ice extent, temperature and salinity, which lends considerable uncertainty to their input functions8,37,38. Ideally, two tracers are used in the TTD to provide a robust constraint on the resulting parameters. In the Canada Basin, there are larger datasets of individual gas tracers from the 1990s and early 2000s, but only two years of paired gas tracers covering the Canada Basin have been published to date (GLODAPv2.2023 data product Global Ocean Data Analysis Project version 2, expocodes 77DN20050819 and 06AQ20150817; ref. 39), analysed in Tanhua et al.8, Rajasakaren et al.21 and Raimondi et al.32. Using single gas tracers does expand the spatial and temporal coverage somewhat. However, paired tracers with distinct input histories provide two independent constraints, as they can resolve the two moments of the TTD without assuming any degree of mixing.

Nuclear waste radionuclides as tracers of anthropogenic carbon

Recently, the TTD method using a new dual-tracer pair, the long-lived anthropogenic, nuclear-waste radionuclides iodine-129 (129I) and uranium-236 (236U), has been shown to provide a robust approximation to the TTD estimates calculated in the Arctic Ocean using the gas tracers32. This work also provided a sensitivity analysis of the TTD results obtained from the gas tracers and the radionuclides and demonstrated that uncertainty in the radionuclide input function leads to smaller changes in the resulting TTD parameters than varying saturation of the gas tracers. The resulting Cant inventories across all the Arctic basins from gas tracers exhibit approximately 20% greater variability than the radionuclides, depending on the gas saturation state used.

The TTD addresses advection-diffusion processes along the water-flow path, producing a probability density function (PDF). In this model, the TTD is an inverse Gaussian distribution, which assumes one-dimensional flow and requires a well-defined input function. The parameters obtained from the TTD using the radionuclides then provide the constraints for the TTD calculation to estimate Cant. The TTD for Cant assumes that Cant behaves as a passive tracer; therefore, its concentration at a known location and time can be described as for any other transient tracer (see “Methods”, Eqs. (1)–(4)).

The radioisotopes 129I, 236U and 137Cs were first released into the environment during nuclear weapons testing in the 50s and 60s. About 1000 kg of 236U, 300 kg of 137Cs and only 50 kg of 129I were released to the atmosphere40–42, forming a low-level global fallout input to the global oceans. Since the 1970s, the Nuclear Reprocessing facilities (NRP) in Europe, La Hague (France), and Sellafield (UK) (see Fig. 1a radioactive symbol) have also released these tracers into the North and Irish Seas in annually reported volumes, which now form the dominant input to the Atlantic Ocean. The time-dependent input functions (Fig. 2a) of all three radionuclides consist of two main components: the low-level global input and the nuclear reprocessing signal. The input function of 137Cs also incorporates the 1986 Chernobyl accident43. 137Cs was initially used in conjunction with 129I to investigate circulation in the Arctic Ocean through the 90s and early 2000s43,44. Developments in Accelerator Mass Spectrometry (AMS) methods allowed measurements of 129I in small sample volumes (< 1 L), which coincided with the significant increase in volume releases of 129I from NRPs. In contrast, 137Cs is measured via decay-counting, a time-consuming process requiring large sample volumes (20–30 L) due to its steadily declining NRP release and short half-life (30.2 years), further reducing its already low concentrations. As such, 137Cs has fallen out of favour as a tracer in the Arctic Ocean. In the last two decades, further advancements in AMS technology led to 236U emerging as a new oceanographic tracer40,45. 236U has all the same advantages as 129I: a well-defined input function, small sample volumes needed, long half-life, and conservative nature in the open ocean46–48. Given that 137Cs has similar tracer characteristics as 236U and 129I, the same well-defined, time-varying input from a point-source, and being conservative in the open ocean, we consider calculated TTD parameters between 137Cs- 129I and 236U- 129I pairs to be comparable, as already done in Casacuberta & Smith49 (unfortunately, no data exists with all three tracers available for a direct tracer intercomparison study).

Here, we present a high-resolution study of the distribution of Cant across the Canada Basin in 2022, using anthropogenic radionuclides 236U and 129I in the TTD method, first demonstrated by Raimondi et al.32. We expand the available tracer data in the Canada Basin with a high-resolution compilation of 236U and 129I spanning 2015–2023. Historical data of 137Cs and 129I extends the record of dual-tracer radionuclides in the Arctic Ocean back to 1993, allowing us to investigate changes in water mass transport and mixing rates, as well as the sub-decadal evolution of Cant in the Arctic Ocean using a dual-tracer approach.

Results and discussion

Distribution of 129I, 236U and 137Cs in AW and dAW, 1993–2023

We present mean profiles of 129I, 236U and 137Cs (Fig. 2b–d) across the surface and Pacific Water (PW) (≈ 0–200 m), Cold Halocline (CHL) (≈ 200–300 m, temperatures < 0 °C), AW (≈ 300–600 m), and dAW (≈ 600–1500 m). The southern and northern regions of the Chukchi Plateau are thought to be gateway regions of the AOBC50–52. In every year occupied, the highest concentrations of 129I were found at station CB10 (see Fig. 1b), located at the northern tip of the plateau where the AOBC enters the basin. Lower concentrations were observed towards the central basin regions (see Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2 for annual individual profiles of each station). All three radioisotopes exhibited similar vertical distributions, characterised by low, close-to-global fallout signals in the surface and PW. Currently, global fallout values are ≈ 1.6 × 107 at L−1 for 129I53, and ≈ 6 × 106 at L−1 for 236U54. For 137Cs, Smith et al.43 reported a global fallout value of 2.2 Bq m−3 at the time of publishing. All global fallout values are marked on the profiles in Fig. 2. We found that the highest concentrations are observed in the CHL. The CHL had not been treated as a separate layer in previous studies of the radionuclides, and refutes the conclusion that the highest concentrations of the tracers in the mid-depth Arctic Ocean are found in core AW (note that in 2020, the first high-resolution study of 129I and 236U in the Canada Basin, the CHL was not sampled52). High concentrations were also observed throughout the AW, which then decrease with depth through the dAW. Below 2500 m, any tracer concentrations approach zero, or are below the measurement detection limit, as the deep waters are old and isolated8,52,54–56.

The observed changes in the concentrations of all three tracers through time are directly linked to the different dynamics of the input function of each tracer (Fig. 2a). In Payne et al.52, the mean age of water entering the basin at the northeastern edge of the Chukchi Plateau and Northwind Ridge was estimated to be ≈ 20–26 years throughout the Atlantic Layer, which means water sampled there in 2020 would have picked up its tracer signal when entering the Arctic Ocean on the Eurasian side in the late 1990s—early 2000s, immediately after both input functions of 137Cs and 236U show a dramatic decrease. 137Cs had the highest activities (≈ 9 Bq m−3) measured in 1993, which steadily declined with time until 2008 (≈ 4 Bq m−3), reflecting the decreasing input function. 236U shows a similar decrease in mean concentration from 2015 (≈ 30 × 106 at L−1) to 2023 (≈ 25 × 106 at L−1), also due to its decreasing input function. In contrast, the input of 129I to the Arctic Ocean did not ramp up until the 1990s43,44, peaking in the early 2000s and observed as a steady increase of mean concentrations until 2023 (≈ 300 × 107 at L−1). This is particularly prominent in the CHL and AW (Fig. 2), although it is observed throughout both AW and dAW.

Although a full description of the TTD calculation and parameters is presented in the “Methods” section, we provide a brief overview of the parameters here. Γ is the mean age from the PDF of the TTD. tmode is the mode age, or the “most likely” age, which is always younger than the calculated Γ ages. Higher Γ and tmode ages indicate slower transport rates. Δ is the width of the TTD and describes the degree of mixing, with higher Δ values indicating more mixing. The ratio Δ/Γ is another mixing parameter, directly related to the Péclet number, as Pe = (Γ/Δ)257. For Δ/Γ, the larger the number (from 0.1 to 1.8), the more diffusive the transport. However, nearly all TTD studies in the Canada Basin indicate transport in the core AOBC and across the basin never exceeds Δ/Γ = 121,32,43,52,56,58. Importantly, our TTD parameters are derived without constraining the ratio of Δ/Γ, allowing both Δ and Γ to vary freely. This ensures that the shape and spread of each TTD are determined by the radionuclide observations, rather than pre-determined by assumptions about flow regime or mixing intensity, as is often done in TTD studies based on gas tracers8,21,37,38,59, with the choice of Δ/Γ contributing 3% uncertainty to global Cant estimates60.

At CB9 and CB10, transport times (mean ages, Γ) of core AW appear to remain relatively steady across all years (≈ 20 ± 4–27 ± 4 years), although CB9 does show a pronounced increase in 2020 (to 32 ± 2), before returning to a similar Γ as CB10 in 2022 (Fig. 3b, c). In the tmode ages, CB9 shows almost identical values and behaviour to CB10. CB9 tmode ages vary between 9 ± 1 and 16 ± 1 years, while CB10 tmode ages vary between 7 ± 2 and 15 ± 1 years. In contrast, our inner basin stations, CB5 and Stn A, while they have almost identical Γ and tmode ages to CB9 and CB10 in the early 2000s, (20 ± 2 to 26 ± 1 years at Stn A, 23 ± 1 years at CB5 for Γ, 15 ± 1 to 20 ± 1 years at Stn A, 20 ± 1 years at CB5 for tmode) they show clear increases in Γ and tmode to 2015–2020 (up to 44 ± 1 years at Stn A and 33 ± 1 years at CB5 for Γ, up to 24 ± 1 years at Stn A and 26 ± 1 years at CB5 for tmode), with a slight decrease then observed again from 2020 to 2022–2023. This finding is corroborated by the gas tracers in Rajasakaren et al.21, who also found a positive age trend in both the southern and northern Canada Basin from 2005 to 2015, but they did not distinguish between stations within the AOBC in the north of the basin. Similar trends across the Arctic Ocean are also described by Gerke et al.37, Körtke et al.61 and Wefing et al.62.

Interestingly, while CB10 and CB9 exhibit lower Γ and tmode in the early 2000s, they both have higher Δ values than the inner basin stations during the same period (Fig. 3d). However, this trend reverses in 2020 for Stn A, with a sharp increase in Δ observed. This deviation at Stn A was attributed to the possibility of eddy activity in Payne et al.52. CB9 also shows a shift to a higher Δ in 2020, while CB5 shows a slight increase, which may suggest a change in mixing regime. CB10 appears to remain steady in Δ, except in 2006 and 2023, when a slight increase in both mixing parameters is seen. The consistently low Δ/Γ values (generally < 0.5 across the entire basin in all years, not illustrated in Fig. 3, see Supplement Figs. S6 and S7) indicate that, in general, transport of core AW and even dAW is advective, a finding also corroborated by other works43,52,56. Rajasakaren et al.21 found slightly higher Δ/Γ values of 0.6 from the gas tracers fit their data best. An increase in Δ and Δ/Γ with depth is also expected, as deeper water mixes more with ambient water and interacts with the shelf topography.

The difference in the behaviour of the TTD parameters at the individual stations may result from an increase of mixing in the Eurasian Arctic Ocean over time. In that basin, Atlantification is enhancing vertical mixing and reducing stratification. However, if this were the case, we would expect to see an increasing trend in Δ and Δ/Γ, and similar behaviour at the boundary current stations and inner basin stations. Given that none of our time-series stations show an increasing trend in the mixing parameters, we propose an alternative explanation, involving changes in transport related to the state of atmospheric circulation. Previous works using 129I to investigate the transport of AW into the Canada Basin have suggested a shifting of the AOBC and transport pathways of AW to the Canada Basin in response to the atmospheric circulation state63,64. The Arctic Oscillation (AO) and Arctic Dipole (AD) are atmospheric modes describing Arctic variability65,66. Recent work by Polyakov et al.67 has shown that the relative proportions of AW making up the three branches vary on decadal timescales depending on the AD state. From 1992 to 2007, Polyakov et al.67 find that the inflow of the upper 50 m through the Fram Strait was 15% greater than in 2007–2019, with somewhat reduced inflows through the Barents Sea (5%) and also the Franz Joseph Land-Novaya Zemlya passage. These changes in AW branch volumes likely control the concentrations of each tracer carried by each branch, also leading to the observed changes in our TTD parameters. Given these circulation-induced controls on the relative proportions of isotope concentrations carried in each branch of AW, Cant content in each branch of Atlantic Water may be influenced similarly. In section “Temporal changes in Cant and response”, we discuss the Cant content in Fig. 3e in the context of the Arctic Ocean Oscillation (AOO), which, while being closely linked to the AO, is considered a more Arctic-centric index. The AOO is a shallow-water model, and as the Atlantic Water branches were at shallow depths in the Eurasian Arctic, where they picked up the tracer signals, it is therefore the most appropriate to use here68 (Fig. 3f).

We do not calculate Cant content in water deeper than 1500 m as the deepest (> 2500 m) waters have been isolated in the basin for 450–600 years8,55. In deep waters, tracer concentrations approach the detection limit8,52, making estimates of Cant unreliable. However, Cant content should be low21,32, and globally waters below 1500 m are estimated to contain less than 7% of the total Cant inventory28. We cannot estimate Cant in the Pacific layer, as the tracers used in 2022 (129I and 236U) are only present in Pacific Water in global fallout concentrations, and lack the time-dependent input function required for TTD calculations. In the cold halocline layer, concentrations of both 129I and 236U exceed those in the core Atlantic Water layer (see Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2). These elevated concentrations have now been observed across the Arctic Ocean basin62 and into the subpolar North Atlantic. Here, we excluded the cold halocline layer from Cant calculations as our input function is only defined for the surface and mid-depth layers. However, the CHL has a large lateral extent and should carry high Cant, being ventilated as it traverses the Russian shelf seas. A better understanding of halocline formation, including how it acquires tracer concentrations through the incorporation of high-concentration labelled surface waters, and its subsequent transport, should be a priority in future work to estimate accurate basin inventories.

Temporal changes in Cant and response to ocean circulation state

When we compare Γ and tmode from the early 2000s to 2015–2023, our boundary current stations CB9 and CB10 appear to have relatively consistent transport times (Fig. 3b, c), suggesting a constant presence of the boundary current to the north of the Chukchi Plateau, unaffected by atmospheric circulation. This is also supported by the fact that the highest tracer concentrations are consistently found in this region every year, indicating that this is the inflow region of the core AOBC. At CB10 and CB9, Cant in the AOBC increased from ≈ 33.7 to 45.2 μmol kg−1 and 33.8 to 46.3 μmol kg−1, respectively, over 2003–2022, yielding an accumulation rate of 0.76 ± 0.06 μmol kg−1 yr−1, comparable to estimates for total DIC in the Eurasian Basin17. In contrast, our interior basin stations show greater variability that may well result from the state of the AOO. In 2003–2008 (Fig. 3e), Cant content across the basin never exceeded 35 μmol kg−1, and the mean of Cant across these years showed little variation (31–32 μmol kg−1), with relatively young mode ages at the interior basin stations (Stn A and CB5) (< 20 years), suggesting relatively rapid transport. In 2015–2020, the estimated mode age of the core AW at both these stations increased to over 25 years, before declining again in 2023 (Fig. 3c), while mean Cant rose throughout this period (> 43 μmol kg−1), particularly sharply between 2020 and 2023. Basin-wide across the entire AW depth, the rate is lower (0.34 ± 0.09 μmol kg−1 yr−1) (Fig. 3e), aligning with prior Arctic-wide estimates (e.g., 0.2 μmol kg−1 yr−1 for dense Arctic Atlantic and upper Polar Deep Water21).

We report changes in column inventories from 400 to 1000 m only, as the 2003–2008 data does not extend past this depth, and data coverage from 300 to 400 m is incomplete across several years. We find a rate of increase in column inventory of 0.30 ± 0.05 mol C m−2 yr−1. Ulfsbo et al.31 report a slightly higher trend in the upper 1500 m of the Amundsen Basin (0.69 ± 0.09 mol C m−2 yr−1) and 0.5 ± 0.1 mol C m−2 yr−1 in the Nansen Basin for the 1996–2015 period using the eMLR method. Rajasakaren et al.21 report column increases in the AW/AAW layer of 0.50 ± 0.05 mol C m−2 yr−1 and 0.32 ± 0.16 mol C m−2 yr−1 in the western and southern Canada Basins, and 0.17 ± 0.21 mol C m−2 yr−1 in the northern Canada Basin; however, their total column inventories decrease due to an apparent decline in Cant at depths greater than 2000 m. Woosley & Millero69 report a column inventory increase of 0.13 ± 0.26 mol C m−2 yr−1, with changes only observed down to the 27.55 kg m−3 isopycnal. However, they observed a significant decrease in Cant content in the upper 100 m, linked to surface freshening that reduced both total alkalinity and DIC between 2005 and 2015. They estimate a basin-wide inventory increase of 2.34 Tg C yr−1 using a basin area of 1,524,664 km2. In comparison, using this same area, we estimate 5.5 ± 0.9 Tg C yr−1 in the 400–1000 m layer only. Because we exclude both the surface and deep waters, which other authors report to show decreasing trends, our value likely represents an upper bound on the true basin-integrated rate.

A broader view of the regional carbonate system may help explain why our observed mid-depth trends diverge from changes observed in the surface and the Pacific layers by Woosley & Millero69. Yamamoto-Kawai et al.70 report increasing aragonite undersaturation in both surface waters and the underlying Pacific Winter Water from 2003 to 2019, reinforcing the observations of Woosley & Millero69. Yet they also note that the aragonite saturation profile in the Canada Basin exhibits a subsurface maximum in the intervening Pacific Summer Water and also near 400 m, coinciding with the temperature core of Atlantic Water. Given the stratification between the Polar Mixed Layer, Pacific Waters and Atlantic Water, we expect that local Cant uptake has always been a minor contribution to the mid-depth Atlantic Water compared to the fraction transported from upstream. Any observed changes in Atlantic Water Cant content will likely be due to Atlantification and freshening in the Eurasian Basin before subduction. The tracer ages estimated with CFCs and SF6 in the Atlantic layer in Raimondi et al.32 agree well with the radionuclides, and do not indicate substantial recent ventilation of mid-depth water in the Canada Basin. If local ventilation had occurred at scale, we would expect to find significantly younger gas tracer ages than those observed. Yamamoto-Kawai et al.70 conclude that the most plausible drivers of changes in the surface and Pacific Winter Water lie upstream on the Russian shelf, where water masses equilibrate with the atmosphere before being subducted into the interior. Recent work by Polyakov et al.71 strengthens this interpretation by documenting recent Atlantification in the Makarov Basin. Characterised by sea-ice loss, warming and enhanced ventilation of shelf waters, these factors collectively alter carbonate chemistry in the upper halocline waters.

When we compare the mode ages to the approximate time the water would have been in the Eurasian Arctic Ocean (Fig. 3, panels (c) and (f) and arrows) higher mode ages appear to occur when the AOO is in a positive phase, which means the AOO is in an anticyclonic regime, forcing surface waters and sea ice to drift clockwise, strengthening the circulation and extent of the Beaufort Gyre, the volume of Pacific Water and location of the Transpolar Drift, which can in turn reduce the presence of Atlantic-sourced waters at the surface, and likely similarly affect the mid-depth AW (see arrows on the map in Fig. 3a). In contrast, when the interior basin stations had their youngest Γ and tmode ages, these appear to correspond to a negative AOO. The resulting cyclonic circulation weakens the Beaufort Gyre and reduces the extent of Pacific Water, allowing a greater influx of Atlantic-sourced waters to the region. However, regardless of the state of the AOO, mean Cant content across all years (1993–2023) has increased, reflecting the atmospheric increase in Cant.

Implications for future Cant accumulation

To assess whether the resulting Cant values we obtained in the Canada Basin are realistic and to investigate if there was a change in circulation/uptake regime from the early 2000s to 2020s, we compared them to an estimated range of surface Cant uptake in the Barents Sea and St. Anna Trough. We varied temperature (−1.5 to 2 °C), practical salinity (32–35), and total alkalinity (2150–2325 μmol kg−1) according to real, observed ranges 72,73, and a constant air-sea Cant equilibrium (i.e., 100% saturation of anthropogenic CO2). This is represented as a shaded region in Fig. 5a, reflecting the uncertainty in the actual surface conditions during ventilation. For each Canada Basin sample, calculated Cant is based on the atmospheric CO2 history, and the sample’s observed temperature, salinity, and alkalinity derived from salinity (see “Methods”), and the TTD parameters Δ and Γ calculated from the radionuclides. We then projected the calculated Cant of the sample back to the estimated time of ventilation using the mode age (tmode). Because the same surface history was used to construct the Cant input function for the TTD, alignment of the calculated Cant values with the input curve is expected, particularly since most samples have low Δ and Δ/Γ values, implying limited mixing. This is supported by tracer observations at the CB10 gateway station, where concentrations of all three radionuclides in the core AW closely align with their respective input functions when plotted at the sampling year minus the mean mode age of core AW for this station (11 years; Fig. 5, panels b, c & d). This agreement indicates almost total conservation of the tracer signal along the core of the AOBC, providing evidence of advective flow that is not necessarily captured in the T-S of a water mass. Whether this is due to different temperatures, salinity, alkalinity, saturation, or loss of Cant due to mixing and dilution along the flow path under different transport regimes is beyond the scope of this work. Still, it could be taken as evidence of varying uptake regimes in the Eurasian Basin. The difference may reflect the shift from a negative to a positive AOO regime, as occurred in the mid-1990s, or could reflect the increasing influence of Atlantification in the Eurasian Basin2,74. Even given these possible drivers, our tracer observations support our assumption that Cant in core Atlantic Water is transported near-conservatively from the Barents Sea into the Canada Basin. Our results indicate that future Cant accumulation and associated acidification of these waters will continue to track atmospheric CO2 increases, time-lagged by approximately 15–20 years (mean tmode age across the basin). This means that in another 20 years we should observe the Cant content entering the AW in the Barents Sea today of between ≈ 50 and 65 μmol kg−1 in the core AOBC as it reaches the Canada Basin. However, recent observations by Ericson et al.75 of increasing CO2 fugacity in the Barents Sea indicate that the uptake potential of surface water in the Eurasian Basin is rapidly declining, with implications for the future sequestration of Cant in Atlantic Water. The rate of Cant increase in mid-depth Atlantic Water throughout the Arctic Ocean in the following decades will most likely be influenced by the state of the Arctic Ocean circulation regimes, as well as the ongoing freshening of the surface Arctic Ocean and Atlantification in the Eurasian Basin, highlighting the importance of continuing monitoring efforts across the entire Arctic.

This study utilises nuclear-waste radionuclides (129I, 236U and 137Cs) to constrain transit times, mixing and anthropogenic carbon into the Arctic Canada Basin. Tracer signals measured in Atlantic Water over time reflect the evolution of the radionuclides’ input functions, and these signals undergo limited dilution in the core of AW transport. This supports the interpretation of advective transport pathways from the estimated TTD parameters Δ and Δ/Γ. Temporal shifts in age estimates of both mean and mode ages at stations CB5 and Stn A in the interior basin reflect changes in large-scale atmospheric forcing, most likely due to the AOO, which governs Atlantic inflow and tracer transport. However, the high tracer concentrations observed at the gateway stations CB9 and CB10, along with relatively invariable transport times in both mean and mode ages, suggest a constant presence of the Arctic Ocean Boundary Current north of the Chukchi Plateau, regardless of the AOO state. The distribution of Cant in the Canada Basin in 2022 reflects the transport and distribution of the radionuclides. Cant content increase is highest in the AOBC (0.76 ± 0.06 μmol kg−1 yr−1), with lower rates of Cant content increase across the basin (0.34 ± 0.09 μmol kg−1 yr−1). We find an increasing basin inventory of 5.5 ± 0.9 Tg C yr−1. Our estimates of Cant across the basin closely track atmospheric CO2 growth, lagged by ≈ 15–20 years. Radionuclide-based TTDs therefore provide a robust framework for monitoring and projecting future Cant accumulation and its resulting impacts, such as acidification, in the region. Our findings underscore the importance of continued monitoring of Cant in the Arctic Ocean. A more comprehensive comparison across methods, including the eMLR and models, would be beneficial, particularly in light of changing oceanic and atmospheric conditions. The study suggests that further investigation is needed to understand the role of Arctic Ocean circulation shifts in modifying Cant uptake and distribution to provide better insights for predicting future ocean acidification and climate change impacts in polar regions.

Methods

Sampling, isotope analysis and historical data

Samples for analysis of 236U and 129I were collected during the 2022 (280 samples) and 2023 (60 samples) expeditions of the JOIS/BGOS monitoring program on the CCGS Louis St Laurent in the Canada Basin. Complete depth profiles were collected at the stations indicated in Fig. 1. 3 L samples were collected from 10 L Niskin bottles attached to a 24-bottle CTD rosette. Hydrographic data, including conductivity, temperature, and depth, were recorded using SeaBird Electronics sensors. The samples were directly filled into rinsed 3 L containers, left untreated at ambient temperatures, and sent to ETH Zürich for analysis.

Uranium samples were processed following Christl et al.48. Samples were acidified to pH 2 with HNO3 and spiked with 1 pg of 233U (PTB 2014-1126). Uranium was concentrated by Fe co-precipitation, with ≈ 250 mg of purified Fe-solution added to each sample, allowed to equilibrate overnight and precipitated with NH3. After overnight settling, the supernatant was removed, and the precipitate was re-dissolved in 16 mL of 8 M HNO3 before uranium separation by ion-exchange chromatography using pre-packed Triskem UTEVA resin columns. The sample was loaded into the column, followed by 16 mL 8 M HNO3. The Np fraction was removed by 8 mL HNO3 (2 M) + 0.05 M H2C2O4, U was then eluted by 15 mL HNO3 (0.01 M) + 10 mL MQ water. 1 mg Fe in solution was added and precipitated by NH3. Samples were then centrifuged, rinsed with ethanol, and transferred to glass crucibles. These were oxidised at 650 °C for 3 h, and pressed into Al targets with 3 mg of Nb.

Measurements of 233U/238U and 236U/238U were performed on the 300 kV Milea AMS system at the Laboratory of Ion Beam Physics, ETH Zürich, normalised to in-house standard ZUTRI (nominal values: 233U/238U = 33170 ± 830 × 10−12 at/at; 236U/238U = 4055 ± 200 × 10−12 at/at). 236U and 238U concentrations were derived from the measured 233U spike concentration. Samples were corrected using chemistry blanks (n = 15) prepared at ETH Zürich using MilliQ water (18.2 MΩ). Analytical uncertainty is 1–10% for samples within the upper 1500 m.

Iodine samples were purified following Casacuberta et al.46. Samples were spiked with ≈ 1.5 mg of Woodward Iodine (127I). 5 mL of saturated Ca(ClO)2 was added and stirred for 15 min. 20 mL NH2OH ⋅ HCl (1 M) + 1 g Na2S2O5 was added and stirred for about 45 min. pH was adjusted by the addition of NaOH (7 M) in 0.8 mL increments. Iodine was separated by ion-exchange chromatography using 5 mL DOWEX 1 × 8 resin columns. Samples were loaded immediately after pH adjustment. The resin was then rinsed with 20 mL MilliQ water, followed by 50 mL KNO3 (0.5 M), then 10 mL KNO3 (2.25 M). The iodine fraction was eluted with 40 ml of 2.25M KNO3 and precipitated directly as AgI in prepared Falcon tubes containing 200 μL HNO3 (65%) + 500 μL AgNO3 (0.1 M).

Precipitates were triple-rinsed, dried, and pressed into Ti targets with 4 mg of Ag for measurement on the 500 kV Tandy AMS system. Each run included an in-house standard C2 in both concentrated and diluted forms (nominal values: diluted 5.055 × 10−12 at/at, concentrated 38.995 × 10−12 at/at 129I/127I). Samples were corrected using chemistry blanks (n = 15) prepared at ETH Zürich using MilliQ water (18.2 MΩ). A 4% analytical uncertainty is reported, incorporating an ETH internal standard and a 1–2% instrument uncertainty.

This work includes all tracer data with at least two radionuclides to constrain the calculated mean ages. For the radionuclides 129I and 236U, 2015 data was collected on the GN01 section on board the USCGC Healy (HLY1502), published in Wefing et al.56, and 2020 data was collected during the JOIS/BGOS cruise onboard the CCGS Louis St Laurent, published in Payne et al.52. In addition, we also include data from 2003, 2005, 2006, 2007 and 2008 JOIS expeditions, where 129I is paired with 137Cs, published in Smith et al.43 and the 1993 CCGS Henry Larsen expedition, where 129I is paired with 137Cs, published in Smith et al.44.

We present mean profiles of each tracer rather than time-series stations, as not all the same stations were occupied yearly (see Fig. 1b). We also include the 1993 stations west of the Chukchi Plateau to extend the time-series. We interpolated along 5 m intervals for each station sampled that particular year and then calculated the mean from all stations. However, it is worth noting that the 1993 stations are located upstream of the Canada Basin and, therefore, should not be directly comparable to the stations within the Canada Basin itself, as different transport times and circulation parameters are expected.

Input functions

For this work, to ensure consistency across all the data, we used the 137Cs input function from Smith et al.43, and calculated the 129I and 236U input functions at 60° N using the same method as for 137Cs described in Smith et al.43, based on the annual releases reported by Sellafield and La Hague. A transfer factor of 25 Bq m−3/PBq yr−1 at a 2 and 4 year time-lag after the release for Sellafield and La Hague, respectively, converts the releases (Bq yr−1) to the surface ocean input function (Bq L−1 or at L−1). An additional input from modelled global fallout of 236U from Christl et al.48, and for 129I of 1 × 107 at L−1 are included in each input function. The Atlantic Layer input function is calculated from the surface input function using a dilution factor of 10, coupled with a 1-year time lag. The Atlantic Water 137Cs input function was taken directly from Smith et al.43, also based on the global fallout, reported releases from the NRP, and includes the input from the 1986 Chernobyl disaster.
