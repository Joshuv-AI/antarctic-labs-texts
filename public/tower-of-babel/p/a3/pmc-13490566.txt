SIMS discovers invisible gold in pyrite from the high-grade seafloor hydrothermal deposits in the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera, Izu-Ogasawara arc, Japan
Authors: Yuichi Morishita, Tatsuo Nozaki, Junji Torimoto, Naoto Takahata, Yutaro Takaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Seafloor hydrothermal deposits with extremely high concentrations of gold were recently discovered in the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera hydrothermal field, Izu-Ogasawara arc Japan. We report here the discovery of ultra-high concentrations of “invisible gold” (up to 1.9 wt%) in pyrite from the sulfide mounds and a black smoker chimney using a secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS). A notable feature of this study is that quantitative gold concentrations were obtained from all analyzed pyrites due to the high sensitivity (7 ppb) and wide measurement range of the SIMS analysis. The concentrations of gold and arsenic in pyrite are higher than those in the whole rock on any hydrothermal sites within the caldera, suggesting that gold and arsenic are concentrated in pyrite. The gold concentrations in pyrite vary greatly depending on the site of the deposit and morphology of pyrite. The extremely high gold concentrations are found from colloform pyrites with high lead and/or copper concentrations in addition to arsenic concentration in pyrite, which might have induced the high gold concentration in pyrite. No gold nanoparticles were observed in high spatial resolution SIMS depth profiles of pyrite, and it is believed that gold and other elements are contained within the pyrite crystal structure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-58760-z.

Actively forming gold-rich sulfide black smoker chimneys and mounds in the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera in the Izu-Ogasawara arc Japan (Fig. 1), about 360 km south of Tokyo, have been discovered in 20151,2. The most noteworthy feature of the hydrothermal sulfide deposits is their extremely high bulk-rock Au concentrations at the mound (up to 275 ppm; av. 102 ppm, n = 15)2. Since the water depth of the sulfide deposits is relatively shallow (down to 820 m below sea level (mbsl)) as seafloor hydrothermal deposits, future commercial development challenges are expected to be less difficult than other deeper deposits. Compared to other submarine hydrothermal fields, compositional data of 130 seafloor massive sulfide deposits around the world show a wide range of gold concentrations, from 0.01 to 43 ppm3. Therefore, the gold deposits in the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera are extremely high-grade that do not fit into existing databases, and this study reports even higher gold concentrations in pyrite of the deposits.Fig. 1The Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera is located east of Aogashima Island in the Izu-Ogasawara arc. The occurrence of Au is not limited as native gold or electrum in most hydrothermal deposits, but considerable amount of Au is contained in ubiquitous pyrite in many Au deposits as “invisible gold”4–9, which are either Au nanoparticles (NPs; < 100 nm)10–12 or as a solid solution with pyrite. Moreover, the Au concentration is generally positively correlated with As in pyrite13. First, we will investigate whether there is invisible gold in the Higashi-Aogashima hydrothermal deposit and whether the relationship between Au and As in pyrite holds true in the deposit. Since Iizasa et al.2 only dealt with visible Au grains in sulfide mounds and chimneys, we will explore “invisible gold” and “invisible arsenic” in pyrite using secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) from the sulfide mounds and a black smoker chimney in the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera hydrothermal field. SIMS is highly sensitive and can detect Au at extremely low concentrations down to ppb, and its wide dynamic range also allows for the quantitative analysis of high concentrations of Au. The most distinctive feature of SIMS is its depth profiling capability with nanometer-order resolution, a function not found in any other analytical instruments. This capability allows for the confirmation of gold presence within microscopic regions, thus aligning with the objectives of this study.

In addition to Au and As, the ability to incorporate trace elements such as Ni, Co, Cu, Zn, Se, Ag, Cd, Sb, Pb and Bi draw our attention to pyrite12,14,15. Since pyrite represents the most abundant sulfide mineral in submarine hydrothermal vent system16,17 and it forms from fluids during various stages of chimney growth14,18,19, we will also analyze such trace element concentrations in pyrite by electron probe microanalysis (EPMA). As to the positive correlation between Au and As concentrations in pyrite mentioned above, Reich et al.20 claimed that the solubility of Au is proportional to the As concentration. We would like to verify whether the extremely high Au concentration in this study can be explained by this, or whether other trace elements besides As might also affect the solubility of Au. We aim to elucidate the mechanism by which Au is incorporated into pyrite by integrating trace element analysis using EPMA and Au-As analysis using SIMS.

Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera

Aogashima Island on the Izu-Ogasawara Quaternary volcanic front is made up mostly of basaltic rocks21. The seafloor hydrothermal deposits are located ca. 12 km east of Aogashima Island (Fig. 1). The deepest part of the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera floor is at the water depth of 820 mbsl. The length and width of the caldera floor are about 7.5 km and 6.5 km (Fig. 2). Three active hydrothermal fields (Central Cone Site, Southeast Site, and East Site) are confirmed in the caldera floor (Fig. 2)1,2,22,23. A brief description of the rock composition is metalliferous sediments associated with various amounts of concretions, massive sulfide fragments, volcaniclastic materials, and scoria at Central Cone Site, and barite-bearing sphalerite chimney fragments and black ash at Southeast Site2. Main constituent minerals of the massive sulfide fragments from a mound are mainly sphalerite, galena, chalcopyrite, pyrite, barite, and Ag-bearing tennantite2, and Cu, Zn, and Pb are major elements that comprise mound sulfides and black smoker chimneys in the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera.Fig. 2The location of Central Cone Site, Southeast Site, and East Site are shown in the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera. The water depth of the caldera floor is indicated by contour lines with corresponding colors shown in the legend.

Materials

Rock samples (Table 1) containing pyrite were taken from three hydrothermal sites by R/V Shinsei-maru and Remotely Operated Vehicle (ROV) Hyper-Dolphin (HPD) during the cruise KS-21–20 in 2021. The mound samples HPD#2133R02 and HPD#2133R04 were collected within the Southeast Site on September 1st, 2021 (Fig. 3a). The mound samples HPD#2134R02 and HPD#2134R03 were collected at East Site on September 2nd, 2021 (Fig. 3b). The mound sample HPD#2136R05 was collected at Central Cone Site on September 5th, 2021 (Fig. 3c). The rock sample 6 K#1617R03 was collected from near the top of the large active chimney (Fig. 3d; relative height: 40 m) at Central Cone Site during the cruise YK22-05 on March 11th, 2022. According to Okada24, the maximum temperature and pH of the hydrothermal fluid were 242.3 °C and 4.55–4.74 at Central Cone Site; 280.4 °C and 3.86 at Southeast Site; 271.6 °C and 6.15 at East Site, respectively. The mineral compositions found at the three sites are not distinctive. Since the mounds are formed from the collapse of chimneys that crystallized from hydrothermal fluids, the variations within each site are more significant, however, the main constituent minerals are sphalerite, barite, galena, pyrite, and chalcopyrite. The proportion of pyrite ranges from 0 to 20 wt%, with an average of about 5 wt%. Around 20 mm × 40 mm rock chips were cut out with a diamond saw from the center of each collected rock sample for the bulk rock analysis performed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Pyrite grains in the polished sections were microscopically observed under a reflected light, and they are classified into four types according to the morphology, which are framboidal, colloform, euhedral, and subhedral pyrites (Fig. 4, Supplementary Figure S1 online). Framboidal pyrite often precipitates with barite in the early stages of mineralization, and as mineralization progresses, colloform and subhedral pyrite are associated with sulfide minerals such as sphalerite, galena, and chalcopyrite, which is consistent with previous research19. Euhedral pyrite grows in druse during the final stage of mineralization. Results

Bulk rock analysis (ICP-MS)

Whole-rock Au and As concentrations of the mound and chimney samples are shown in Table 1. The Au concentration of the mound sample decreases in the order of Central Cone Site (178 ppm), Southeast Site (17 and 21 ppm), and East Site (0.5 and 14 ppm). The chimney sample of Central Cone Site has a lower Au concentration (30 ppm) than the mound sample in the site. On the other hand, the As concentration (1530–6675 ppm) shows little variation from site to site.

Microanalysis (EPMA)

Among the 15 analyzed trace elements (See Method), the Cu, Zn, As, and Pb concentrations are relatively high and variable (0–19 wt%), while the concentrations of other elements are generally low and less variable (mostly less than 0.2 wt%) in pyrite from Central Cone Site (Supplementary Table S1 online). Therefore, the results of EPMA analyses of Cu, As, and Pb in addition to the major elements of S and Fe are discussed in this study. In addition to the above elements, the concentrations of Sb and Tl are also shown in Supplementary Table S2 online for the sample HPD#2134R02-6.

Chalcopyrite, galena and sphalerite were analyzed to get approximate values using SIMS (Table 2). The Au concentration of chalcopyrite from East Site, Southeast Site, and Central Cone Site are low (0.24 ppm, 0.07 ppm, and 0.24–0.46 ppm, respectively). Although relative sensitivity factor RSFAs-chalcopyrite is not available, low As concentration (30 ppm, 50 ppm, and 30–200 ppm, respectively) is calculated when using RSFAs-pyrite value for As in chalcopyrite, even if RSFAs-chalcopyrite may slightly differ from RSFAs-pyrite (see Methods and Supplementary Equations S1 online for RSF). Chalcopyrite values are shown to one significant digit because some errors due to matrix effects are expected. Although there are no RSF values for galena and sphalerite, the Au and As concentrations in both minerals are very low or below the detection limit (Table 2) even if there are large errors in the values ​​calculated using the RSF values for pyrite. Therefore chalcopyrite, galena and sphalerite are considered poor Au and As hosts.

Discussion

Au and As concentrations in pyrite with those in the whole rock

The Au and As concentrations in pyrite from the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera range between 0.43 and 19,231 ppm (av. 844 ppm, n = 135), and between 6 and 53,126 ppm (av. 7,491 ppm, n = 135), respectively (Tables 2, 3 and 4). Au and other trace element concentrations for pyrite collected from around the world have been obtained using laser ablation–inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) so far, and the following are two examples of those with high Au concentrations. The colloform pyrite from the Okinawa Trough hydrothermal fields is relatively enriched in Au (up to 201.63 ppm; av. 7.48 ppm, n = 22)25. Au concentration of colloform pyrite from the Niuatahi North vent site in Niuatahi submarine caldera, Tonga rear-arc is relatively high (up to 198.0 ppm; av. 13.5 ppm, n = 34)26. Thus, the pyrite from the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera currently boasts the highest Au concentration in the world.

The Au and As concentrations of rocks in epithermal hydrothermal deposits tend to exhibit positive correlations in hand specimens obtained from geochemical Au explorations27. Pyrite is a ubiquitous sulfide mineral in the most hydrothermal systems, and arsenian pyrite is a common host for “invisible gold”5. The Au concentration in pyrite generally increases with increasing As concentration13,25–31. The mechanism for the positive correlation between Au and As concentrations is thought to be as follows: The pyrite structure is significantly distorted when As is incorporated into the structure, and relatively large Au+ ions are then able to enter the structure9,11,32,33. An inverse correlation of As with S concentrations of pyrite grains can be sometimes evidence for the substitution of As for S, which is consistent with the substitution of As1- for S in the S22- unit to form Fe (S1-x, Asx)2 solid solution of such arsenic pyrite, and it is called As1--pyrite, as previously documented11,12,20,29,30,32,34–36.

Figure 5 shows the Au and As concentrations of pyrite in the sample HPD#2133R02-1 from Southeast Site (Table 2). While the Au and As concentrations in framboidal pyrite are positively correlated, some values deviate from this correlation line in the subhedral pyrite. The framboidal texture results from rapid nucleation in environments where pyrite is strongly supersaturated37. The Au and As concentrations of colloform pyrite in the sample HPD#2136R05-1 from Central Cone Site are relatively high (Table 2), which shows a positive correlation between Au and As concentrations (Fig. 6). Subhedral pyrite in the sample has low Au concentrations but a wide distribution of As, which is likely due to precipitation from a barren hydrothermal fluid. The relationship between the Au and As concentrations of pyrite from East Site is shown in Supplementary Figure S4 online. As described above, the positive correlation between Au and As has been pointed out in many papers, and some papers suggest that the solubility of Au in pyrite is determined as a function of As concentration20. However, aside from the examples presented above, this study did not find more data showing a clear positive correlation between Au and As concentrations. Pyrite morphology

High concentrations of trace elements are found in colloform pyrite occurring within the outer wall of the chimneys and likely result from rapid precipitation under high-temperature-gradient conditions14. Rapid nucleation of pyrite under kinetic conditions due to the extensive influx of surrounding seawater into hydrothermal fluids results in colloform pyrite with incorporating abundant impurities, including Au, As and trace metals37,38. The zone between the inner conduit and outer wall is characterized by recrystallization of colloform pyrite to euhedral pyrite14, which is classified as subhedral pyrite in this paper. Although the sulfide-rich mound is composed of mixture by several origins such as collapsed chimney, direct hydrothermal precipitates from hydrothermal fluid, and product of the replacement mineralization within the mound structure, the morphology of the pyrite contained therein could provide a major clue to understand the precipitation condition. Pyrite precipitates as varied morphologies at different conditions37–39. Framboidal pyrite formation indicates that an extremely high FeS2 supersaturation in the hydrothermal fluids, that is, the pyrite nucleation rate is significantly higher than the crystal growth rate36. It is thought that the evolution of hydrothermal fluid involves the recrystallization of framboidal pyrite into colloform and subhedral pyrites17,19,40. Subhedral to euhedral pyrite crystals commonly correspond to higher fluid temperatures and less fluid-seawater mixing during precipitation; whereas colloform pyrite represents rapid, disequilibrium process at low temperatures during abundant influx of ambient seawater38. We classified pyrite grains into four types according to the morphology under the microscope, which are framboidal, colloform, euhedral, and subhedral pyrites (Fig. 4). Although some euhedral or subhedral pyrites are recrystallized from framboidal or colloform pyrite, most euhedral pyrite that exhibits a perfect crystal form, often grows slowly in an open space from dilute solutions in the latest hydrothermal stage (e.g., euhedral pyrite in Fig. 4). The experimental solubility of Au into pure pyrite without impurities is 3 ppm41. In this study, Au and As concentrations of euhedral pyrite in the sample HPD#2134R03-1 from East Site are 0.43 and 6 ppm, respectively (analysis no. 240208_5 in Table 2). The Au and As concentrations of euhedral pyrite in the sample HPD#2136R05-15 from Central Cone Site are 0.83 and 162 ppm, respectively (SIMS analysis no. 0210_13 in Table 3), and the concentrations of other trace elements are also low (EPMA analysis no. 41 in Supplementary Table S1 online). In contrast, pyrite grains that appear to be recrystallized exhibit subhedral morphology in most cases (Fig. 4). Although the crystal faces are partially visible at the outside of the subhedral pyrite grains in Fig. 4, it appears that the recrystallization from the colloform pyrite grains is ongoing, and it is still too far from being euhedral crystal. Although the range of Au concentrations in colloform and subhedral pyrite is large, the As concentrations are relatively constant. On the other hand, euhedral pyrite has low concentrations of both Au and As (Tables 2 and 3).

Polymorphism

Marcasite (polymorph of pyrite) is often found in colloform pyrite under the microscope. Marcasite precipitates at lower pH42 and lower temperature38 relative to pyrite. Therefore, the complex colloform texture of pyrite and marcasite indicates fluctuations in the physicochemical conditions of the hydrothermal fluids mixed with ambient cold seawater in various proportions38,43.

The mineral grain with EPMA analysis nos. 159, 160 and 161 (Supplementary Table S2 online) was identified as marcasite by examining its pleochroism and anisotropy. Marcasite and pyrite have the same chemical composition (FeS2) but different crystal structures. Considering the possibility that the RSFs for Au and As in marcasite may be different from those in pyrite, the difference in crystal structure has little effect though the SIMS analysis is subject to chemical enhancement of secondary ion yields44. Therefore, Au and As concentrations in marcasite calculated using the RSFAu-pyrite and RSFAs-pyrite are considered reliable. Chemical composition of pyrite and the presence of dissolved Au

The elements analyzed by EPMA in this study were selected as those commonly concentrated in hydrothermal deposits. However, it was found that only Pb and Cu in addition to As were rich in pyrite (Supplementary Table S1 online), and other elements might exist in other constituent minerals. Trace elements such as Au, Ag, Sb, and Pb in pyrite are commonly enriched closely associated with As45,46. Enrichment in Cu, Pb, and Zn, especially Cu, are observed at deeper part under the higher temperatures at modern seafloor hydrothermal systems in arc-related settings47. The As1- incorporation into pyrite crystal structure as Fe (As1-, S)2 suggests reducing and low-sulfidation condition of the hydrothermal fluids32. As can replace S, and Cu or Pb can replace Fe in this type of pyrite. As in the Fe site of pyrite, the negative correlation between Cu and Fe concentrations indicates that Cu2+ can be structurally bound in pyrite reaching weight percent levels by substituting Fe2+12,48,49. In this regard, Chouinard et al.9 proposed the following electrification formula: Au3+ + Cu+ = 2Fe2+. The Pb concentrations in pyrite from Volcano 19 cone and caldera sites at the southernmost part of the Tonga intraoceanic arc are resemble the relative distribution of As38. Cu, Pb, Zn, and Au are known to replace the Fe site of pyrite11. On the other hand, under oxidizing conditions, As3+-pyrite11 in which As3+ substitutes for Fe to form (Fe, As) S2 solid solution of arsenic pyrite commonly related to high-sulfidation epithermal conditions50. In such As3+-pyrite, Au can enter pyrite crystals in the following forms: As3+ + yAu+ + (1 – y) [vacancy] = 2Fe2+ 11. Solubility of Au in pyrite rich in Cu and Pb as well as As

The solubility curve proposed by Reich et al.20 was derived from pyrites of Carlin-type and some epithermal deposits that are low in trace element concentrations other than As. However, we have to work with pyrites that are rich in other elements into consideration for seafloor hydrothermal deposits. Table 4 shows very high Pb and/or Cu concentrations where Au concentrations are high. Although the relationship between Au and As concentrations was investigated in Figs. 8 and 9, the role of Pb and Cu in the solubility of Au in pyrite must also be considered. Lattice-bound Au and Pb ions probably occupy the structural distortions and vacancy caused by substitution of As1- at the S1- site of pyrite10,11,29,36. Therefore, trace metals other than As might also play a role similar to As, suggesting that Au exists as Au1+ in pyrite lattice. The SIMS analysis (Table 4) shows that pyrite with high Pb and/or Cu content contains high concentrations of Au. If trace metals of Pb and/or Cu have similar effect as As, pyrites above the Au solubility curve of Reich et al.20 do not necessarily mean that they are Au nanoparticles though it is not known how effective Pb and Cu are, compared to As in its ability to incorporate Au into pyrite crystal structure. It can be concluded that the presence of high concentrations of Pb and/or Cu, in addition to As, is crucial in explaining the extremely high Au concentration obtained in this study. If this is the case, the solubility of Au in pyrite, which is a function of As concentration proposed by Reich20 as shown in Figs. 8 and 9, would need to include Pb and Cu concentrations, but further data accumulation is necessary for that to be determined. On the other hand, Pb–Te–Sb–Au–Ag–Bi–S nanoparticles are found by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) in Cu-poor pyrite from the Pueblo Viejo deposit12, and our results do not rule out the possibility for existence of such type of Au nanoparticles. Although our SIMS analysis showed no variation in the Au concentration along with depth in all the high spatial resolution SIMS depth profiles, the possibility that extremely small nanoparticles are homogeneously distributed in pyrite cannot be ruled out. Okada et al.24 claims that Au nanoparticles exist on and around Au mineral grains in contact with adjacent sphalerite, galena, and opal in the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera hydrothermal field, if focusing only on electrum or native gold grains (not focusing on pyrite). It is thought that Au nanoparticles are formed in hydrothermal fluids below the seafloor, which then rise and grow into Au minerals in the deposit2,24,51,52. The analytical target mineral is different here because the Au nanoparticles we deal with are not like that. We are discussing Au in pyrite where no Au minerals are found in the surrounding. It should be emphasized that pyrite is the most ubiquitous sulfide mineral in the hydrothermal deposits and all of them contain invisible Au (Tables 2, 3 and 4).

Conclusions

Au-rich seafloor hydrothermal deposits were recently discovered within the Higashi-Aogashima knoll caldera, Izu-Ogasawara arc Japan. The deposits are characterized by their relatively shallow water depth and very high Au content. We first found “invisible gold” in pyrite from the sulfide mound and active chimney of the deposits through the SIMS analysis. A notable feature of this study is that quantitative Au concentrations were determined for all analyzed pyrites due to the high sensitivity and wide dynamic range of SIMS analysis. The average Au concentration in pyrite is the highest in Central Cone Site, followed by Southeast Site and East Site. The Au and As concentrations in pyrite determined by SIMS are higher than those in the whole rock determined by ICP-MS at all three hydrothermal sites, suggesting that Au and As are concentrated in pyrite. The pyrite grains were classified into four types according to the morphology, which are framboidal, colloform, euhedral, and subhedral pyrites. The well-known positive correlations between Au and As concentrations are observed in framboidal pyrites and some colloform pyrites, however, this relationship is not observed in most pyrites in the hydrothermal field. Euhedral pyrite has both low Au and As concentrations, and subhedral pyrite usually has low Au concentration regardless of the As concentration. Since the upper limit of Au solubility in pyrite is generally known to increase with increasing As concentration, this relationship is partially observed in this study, however, extremely high Au concentrations are found in colloform pyrites with high Pb and/or Cu concentrations in addition to the As concentration in pyrite. No gold nanoparticles were observed in the SIMS depth profiles with high spatial resolution on the order of nanometers, and it is the most plausible that Au and trace elements like Pb and Cu are contained within the pyrite crystal structure. We think that the presence of Pb and/or Cu, as well as As, might help the Au get into the pyrite structure, but elucidating the mechanism remains a topic for future study.

Methods

Bulk rock analysis (ICP-MS)

Major and trace element analyses of bulk rock samples were performed by inductively coupled plasma-quadrupole mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) on an Agilent 7500ce instrument at Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC). The 2 cm × 4 cm rock piece was pulverized with an agate mortar and pestle. The powdered sample weighing ~ 50 mg was dissolved using the HNO3-HClO4-HF digestion method in 30-mL PFA screw-cap beakers, then heated overnight on a hot plate at 110 °C. The digested samples were progressively evaporated at 110 °C for more than 12 h, at 130 °C for 3 h, and at 160 °C until dryness. The residue was dissolved in 5 mL Milli-Q deionized water (> 18.2 MΩ cm) combined with 4 mL HNO3 and 1 mL HCl, then diluted to 1:100 by mass (total dilution factor ~ 20,000) before being introduced into the ICP-MS instrument. Details of the analytical procedures, including instrumental drift and mass interference correction methods, are reported53,54.

Microanalysis (EPMA)

All samples were subjected to EPMA analysis prior to the SIMS analysis. SIMS Au and As measurement points were selected from those where the concentrations of trace elements other than As were less than 1 wt% by the EPMA analysis though the EPMA data are not shown in Table 2. The samples were cut with a diamond saw, mounted on 25 mm diameter glass discs using epoxy resin (EpoFix, Struers), polished with a diamond plate and diamond pastes of 70, 30, 13, 6, and 1 μm roughness, and carbon coated. Chemical compositions of standard materials and pyrite grains in the rock samples from the hydrothermal fields were determined on the JEOL JXA-8500F EPMA equipped with a field-emission source at Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC) operating with an accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a beam current of 12 nA (target elements: S, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, Ga, Ge, As, Se, Ag, Cd, Sb, Te, Pb, and Bi). Since the presence of Tl was suspected in the sample HPD#2134R02-6, Tl measurement was added to the above 17 element-set at the analysis for the sample HPD#2134R02-6. The incident electron beam was focused to a diameter of 100 nm, and the counting time was 10 s for each element (20 s only for Tl). The acquired X-ray intensities were corrected by the ZAF method55.

Microanalysis (SIMS)

Samples analyzed using EPMA were used for SIMS analysis. The SIMS analysis spot is set to be the same point as the EPMA analysis in pyrite, though several analytical spots (indicated by the letter b in Tables 3 and 4) might be slightly (~ 10 μm) deviated from the EPMA analysis because of unexpected primary beam deflection of SIMS due to fine surface unevenness, etc. Au and As concentrations of pyrite were determined using a NanoSIMS 50 (Cameca, France) at the Atmosphere and Ocean Research Institute (AORI) of The University of Tokyo. Regarding the polished discs (samples HPD#2134R02-6, HPD#2136R05-15, HPD#2136R05-17, and 6 K#1617R03), a SIMS analysis was performed at the same location as the EPMA analysis point of pyrite grain.

After pre-sputtering with a 300 pA Cs+ rastered beam over a 20 μm × 20 μm area to remove the carbon coat from the sample surface and stabilize the secondary ion intensity by Cs+ implantation, a focused Cs+ primary ion beam is scanned over the inner 16 μm × 16 μm area of the pre-sputtering area for analysis. One analytical cycle is 80 scans of 0.25 s (20 s in total), and an electron flood gun was not used because no charge-up phenomenon was observed in the fast scanning. Negative secondary ions were extracted by an accelerating voltage of 8 kV, and they were introduced into the mass spectrometer. The matrix 34S−, as internal standard, and the secondary 75As− and 197Au− ions were measured simultaneously using multi-collectors with electron multipliers (EMs) for 20 s (1 cycle). The beam current is adjusted to 30 pA, and the 34S− count per second (cps) is approximately 2 × 105. The dead time (~ 44 ns) of the EM counting system was corrected. The entrance slit was set to a width of approximately 40 μm, and the exit slits for each EM were set to a width of 75 μm. The isobaric interference of 133Cs32S2 is clearly separated from 197Au (M/ΔM = 1728) without energy filtering by a mass-resolving power of 8,000 with adequate flat-topped peaks. To reduce background noise and contamination from the area surrounding the analyzed part, beam blanking was adopted. Only the inner approximately 8 μm × 8 μm area of the scanned area was measured (32 × 32 pixels out of 64 × 64 pixels), meaning that 25% of the total secondary ions produced were counted. It took about 1000 s to carry out one analysis run of 50 cycles, which drills 0.17μμ depth into the pyrite as measured by a diamond stylus surface profiler. This indicates that the depth resolution of the analysis is 3 nm, which is unattainable with other analytical methods.

Quantitative SIMS analysis requires the use of reference standards for each element and the analyzed host mineral because of matrix effects on secondary ion intensities. Standard samples for Au and As measurements in pyrite were prepared. Calibration of the Au concentration in pyrite was conducted using external standard sample of pyrite that was implanted with 197Au ions at a dosage of 1 × 1015 atoms/cm2 with an implantation energy of 1.6 MeV using the 1.7 MV Tandetron accelerator located at the University of Western Ontario, Canada30. Calibration of the As concentration in pyrite was also performed using external standard samples that were implanted with As ions at a dosage of 3 × 1015 atoms/cm2, with an implantation energy of 0.4 MeV. After a SIMS depth profiling analysis of the standard sample, the depth of the sputtered borehole was measured using a surface profiler to determine the relative sensitivity factor (RSF). The RSF is a correction factor used to convert signal intensities into quantitative elemental concentrations. An RSF represents the analytical sensitivity of a specific element relative to a reference element. The RSF calculated for Au in pyrite (RSFAu-pyrite) and that for As in pyrite (RSFAs-pyrite), and conversion equations for concentrations are shown in Supplementary Equations S1 online. The RSF and coefficients for the determination of Au in chalcopyrite are also obtained (Supplementary Equations S1 online). The reproducibility of the Au concentration is typically around 1%, assuming that the Au in the sample is homogeneously distributed. The accuracy is slightly worse than this value because the measurement error of the standard sample is added to this, but this uncertainty does not affect the discussion in this study. The detection limits, computed as three times the standard deviation of the background noise, for the Au and As measurements were 7 ppb and 0.3 ppm, respectively if the background noise is assumed to have a Poisson distribution.
