The potential predictive value of cardiac mechanics for left ventricular reverse remodelling in dilated cardiomyopathy
Authors: Ao Kan, Qimin Fang, Shuhao Li, Wenying Liu, Xinwei Tao, Kaiyao Huang, Mengyao Hu, Zhaofeng Feng, Lianggeng Gong
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsLeft ventricular reverse remodelling (LVRR) is an important objective of optimal medical management for dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) patients, as it is associated with favourable long‐term outcomes. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) can comprehensively assess cardiac structure and function. We aimed to assess the CMR parameters at baseline and investigate independent variables to predict LVRR in DCM patients.Methods and resultsNighty‐eight initially diagnosed DCM patients who underwent CMR and echocardiography examinations at baseline were included. CMR parameters and feature tracking (FT) based left ventricular (LV) global strain (nStrain) and nStrain indexed to LV cardiac mass index (rStrain) were measured. The predictors of LVRR were determined by multivariate logistic regression analyses. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of CMR parameters and were compared by the DeLong test. At a median follow‐up time of 9 [interquartile range, 7–12] months, 35 DCM patients (36%) achieved LVRR. The patients with LVRR had lower LV volume, mass, LGE extent and stroke volume index (LVSVi) and higher left ventricular remodelling index (LVRI), nStrains, rStrains, and peak systolic strain rate (PSSR) in the longitudinal direction and rStrains in the circumferential direction at baseline (all P < 0.05). In the multivariate logistic regression analyses, LVRI [per SD, odds ratio (OR) 1.79; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.08–2.98; P = 0.024] and the ratio of global longitudinal peak strain (rGLPS) (per SD, OR 1.88; 95% CI 1.18–3.01; P = 0.008) were independent predictors of LVRR. The combination of LVSVi, LVRI, and rGLPS had a greater area under the curve (AUC) than the combination of LVSVi and LVRI (0.75 vs. 0.68), but not significantly (P = 0.09).ConclusionsPatients with LVRR had a lower LV volume index, lower LVSV index, lower LGE extent, higher LVRI, and preserved myocardial deformation in the longitudinal direction at baseline. LVRI and rGLPS at baseline were independent determinants of LVRR.Keywords: Cardiac magnetic resonance feature tracking, Dilated cardiomyopathy, Reverse remodelling, Strain

Introduction

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a heterogeneous myocardial disease characterized by systolic dysfunction and enlarged ventricles not owing to abnormal loading conditions (hypertension or valve disease) or coronary artery disease. 1 , 2 In recent decades, the prognosis of patients with DCM has improved significantly with advances in the treatment of heart failure. 3 Approximately 40% of patients receiving optimal medical therapy (OMT) experience left ventricle reverse remodelling (LVRR), indicating a favourable prognosis. 4 , 5 LVRR is defined as a significant reduction in the left ventricular (LV) dimension accompanied by recovery of the left ventricle ejection fraction (LVEF). 6 Accurately predicting LVRR is crucial for guiding clinical therapies. Patients expected to respond to optimal medicament treatment (OMT) may avoid unnecessary device therapies, while aggressive treatment with invasive procedures may be retained for high‐risk patients.

Previous studies have identified several clinical variables as predictors of LVRR in DCM, including TTN gene mutations, absence of family history of DCM, recent‐onset disease, lower initial LVEF and normal electrocardiogram without left bundle branch block. 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) can help characterize the heart tissue and can be indicative of the likelihood of LVRR. Recently, Xu et al. found that DCM patients with LVRR, apart from lower LVEF, worse NYHA, lower T2 and ECV values at baseline, had also significantly decreased myocardial T1 value, LV matrix and cell volume after guideline‐directed medical therapy. 10 Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) has been proven to be an independent predictor of LVRR. 10 , 11 In a study with 56 DCM patients, LV contractile reserve at stress CMR was the only independent predictor of LVRR. 12 Myocardial strain, which quantifies the relative deformation of fibre length during ventricle contraction, can sensitively measure the motion and deformation of the myocardium. Jung et al. used speckle‐tracking echocardiography (STE) analysis to find that patients with better‐preserved LV deformation are prone to experience LVRR. 13 Recently, cardiac magnetic resonance feature tracking (CMR‐FT) based on the standard steady‐state free precession (SSFP) sequence has been shown to reliably and accurately assess myocardial strain. Compared with STE, CMR‐FT can overcome defects such as acoustic window limitation, operator dependency, and low spatial and temporal resolution. 14 The LV strains derived from CMR‐FT can detect subclinical myocardial damage 1 and independently predict adverse cardiac outcomes. 15 , 16 , 17 However, little research has been done to investigate the value of LV strain in predicting LVRR in DCM patients. Thus, this study aims to (1) compare the differences in baseline CMR parameters between LVRR and non‐LVRR groups in DCM patients and (2) determine the CMR parameters that independently predict LVRR in DCM patients.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study was approved by our institutional ethics review committee with informed consent waived. We enrolled 175 patients who were initially diagnosed with DCM 18 and underwent both baseline CMR and echocardiography examinations at our hospital from March 2018 to December 2021. The interval between CMR and baseline echocardiography was ≤3 days. The inclusion criteria were (i) reduced LVEF ≤45% on CMR and (ii) elevated left ventricular end‐diastolic dimension (LVEDD) (≥55 mm) on CMR. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) a history of myocardial infarction or severe coronary artery stenosis (>50%) assessed by coronary angiography or coronary artery computed tomography (n = 6); (ii) abnormal loading conditions caused by other myocardial diseases (primary valvular disease, acute myocarditis, history of cardiotoxic agents, hypertensive cardiomyopathy, metabolic or endocrine diseases) (n = 30); (iii) incomplete clinical record (n = 4); and (iv) poor image quality (n = 3). We also excluded patients who underwent implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) implantation or cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) during the follow‐up period (n = 15), those not treated with continuous OMT (n = 9) and those without follow‐up echocardiography (n = 10). Ultimately, 98 DCM patients were included in this study. Figure 1 is the detailed flow diagram of this study.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging protocols

All CMR data were acquired on a 3.0 T whole‐body scanner (Discovery MR750W; GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, CA, USA) using a 30‐element body phased‐array coil with electrocardiogram triggering and respiratory gating technology. Cardiac cine images in short‐axis and long‐axis planes (including 2‐chamber, 3‐chamber, and 4‐chamber planes) from the base to the apical level were performed by steady‐state free precession sequences. The dose of gadobutrol (Gadovist, Bayer HealthCare Pharmaceuticals, Germany) was 0.1 mmol/kg. The intravenous injection rate was 3.0 mL/s, followed by a 10–20 mL saline flush. At 8–10 min after contrast administration, an inversion recovery gradient echo sequence was used to acquire LGE imaging. Sequence parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) 3.9 ms; echo time (TE) 1.6 ms; slice thickness 6 mm; field of view (FOV) 38 × 38 cm2; matrix 256 × 256 pixels; flip angle 55°.

Image analysis

All image analyses were performed by CVI42 software (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Inc. Calgary, Canada). The optimal LV endocardial and epicardial borders from short‐axis and long‐axis cine images were delineated automatically at end‐diastole and end‐systole. The endocardial contour did not include papillary muscles. In cases where the traces were inaccurate, the observers made manual adjustments. The cardiac geometry and function parameters were extracted from short‐axis cine images, including LV end‐diastolic volume index (LVEDVi), LV end‐systolic volume index (LVESVi), LV cardiac mass index (LVMi), LV stroke volume index (LVSVi) and LVEF. The indexed values were indexed to body surface area (BSA). Additionally, the LV remodelling index (LVRI) was calculated as LV mass divided by LV end‐diastolic volume (LVEDV).

The natural LV global strain parameters (nStrain) were quantified from short‐axis and long‐axis cine images through the tissue tracking module of CVI42 (Figure 2 ). LV global radial peak strain (GRPS) and global circumferential peak strain (GCPS) were derived in the short axis. Global longitudinal peak strain (GLPS) was determined in the two and four‐chamber views. Peak systolic strain rate (PSSR), peak diastolic strain rate (PDSR), and the ratio strain (rStrain) parameters in the corresponding three directions were also included. rStrain was defined as the absolute value of LV global peak strain divided by LVMi. 19 RStrains were exclusively positive, and their unit is m2g%. The positive and negative symbols of global peak strain parameters represent different directions of motion. LGE was considered when the extent of LGE beyond the localized ventricular insertion areas could be seen in two phase‐encoding directions, in long‐axis and short‐axis planes. 20 Two independently experienced operators confirmed the presence of LGE, and a third experienced operator provided adjudication. LGE quantification was performed on short‐axis LGE images by standard deviation (SD) threshold through the tissue characteristic module of CVI42. The LGE extent is expressed as a percentage of the LV mass. 21

Follow‐up and left ventricular reverse remodelling definition

The included patients received continuous optimal medical therapies in accordance with the guidelines for managing heart failure (HF) patients. 22 According to the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines, 23 experienced sonographers performed two‐dimensional transthoracic echocardiography at baseline and follow‐up by commercially available echocardiography systems (General Electric Vivid‐E95). LVEF was assessed by the modified Simpson's method. LVEDD was measured in the parasternal long‐axis view. LVRR was defined as an absolute increase in LVEF ≥ 10% to a final value of >35% in combination with a decrease in LVEDD ≥ 10% compared with the initial echocardiography. 5 , 24

Reproducibility analysis

The intra‐ and interobserver variability were calculated for the LV strain parameters in 30 randomly selected patients. LV strain parameters were measured twice by the same observer with an interval of 4 weeks to determine the intraobserver reproducibility. The interobserver repeatability was evaluated based on the measured values of the same population by a second independent observer blinded to the result of the first observer and the medical record of the patients.

Statistical analysis

The normality of distribution was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables with normal distribution were shown as mean ± SD, and variables with non‐normal distribution were presented as medians with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables of the two groups were compared by independent t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test. The difference in categorical variables was evaluated by the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test. Univariable logistic regression analyses were used to determine predictors of LVRR. Variables with a P value <0.05 in univariable analysis without collinearity were included in multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify the independent determinants of LVRR. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidential intervals (CIs), sensitivity, and specificity of the related diagnostic indices. Furthermore, the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity −1) was used to calculate the optimal cutoff value of the diagnostic index. The DeLong test was used to compare the performance of the AUCs. We used a nomogram and decision curve analysis (DCA) to better illustrate clinical utility of the model. Inter‐ and intraobserver reproducibility for the LV strain parameters were evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Two‐sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0 and RStudio 4.1.2.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Our study included 98 DCM patients (mean age: 48.02 ± 13.81 years old; 66% male), and LVRR was observed in 35 patients (36%). The median interval between baseline and follow‐up echocardiography examinations was 9 [IQR, 7–12] months. Table 1 shows the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study cohort. Most patients (56%) presented with New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III or IV. Comparison of cardiac magnetic resonance parameters between patients with and without left ventricular reverse remodelling

The LV geometry, function, and global strain parameters of the two groups are presented in Table 2 . The LVEF was similar between the two groups (14.21 [IQR, 10.68–19.75] vs. 15.38 [IQR, 11.14–22.16], P = 0.277). Compared with patients without LVRR, patients with LVRR had significantly decreased geometry, and function parameters but increased LVRI at baseline. A total of 86 patients showed LGE. There was no difference in its occurrence between the LVRR and non‐LVRR groups (80.00% vs. 92.06%, P = 0.071), but the extent of LGE was smaller in the LVRR group (1.83 [IQR, 0.50–3.92] vs. 3.41 [IQR, 1.06–6.92], P = 0.025).

Predictors of left ventricular reverse remodelling

The predictors of LVRR were evaluated by performing univariate and multivariate logistic analyses, and the results are presented in Table 3 . The univariate analysis showed that LVEDVi, LVESVi, LVSVi, LVMi, LVRI, and rGLPS at baseline were significant predictors of LVRR. Due to collinearity, LVEDVi, LVESVi, and LVMi were excluded while LVSVi, LVRI, and rGLPS were taken for further analysis. In the multivariate logistic regression analyses, LVRI (per SD, OR 1.79; 95% CI 1.08–2.98; P = 0.024) and rGLPS (per SD, OR 1.88; 95% CI 1.18–3.01; P = 0.008) were independent predictors of LVRR. ROC curves analysis of the LVSVi, LVRI, and rGLPS for LVRR are shown in Table 4 . ROC curve analysis indicated that the cut‐off values for rGLPS, LVSVi, and LVRI were 0.045 ( m2g%), 19.26 (mL/m2), and 0.435 (g/mL), respectively. No significant differences in diagnostic performance were found among the three predictors, as shown in Table 4 . The AUC of a combination of three variables was 0.75, which was higher than that of the combination of LVSVi and LVRI (0.68), but no significantly (Figure 3 and Table 4 ) (P = 0.09). Incorporating the three indicators, a nomogram was built to predict the individual LVRR probability (Figure 4 ) with an AUC of 0.75 (95% CI, 0.65–0.85). The DCA of the nomogram was shown in Figure 5 .

Discussion

The main findings of this study are as follows. (i) Patients with LVRR had lower LV volume index, lower LVSVi, higher LVRI, and better myocardial deformation in the longitudinal and circumferential directions at baseline; (ii) LVRI and rGLPS at baseline were independent determinants of LVRR, regardless of the initial degree of LV dysfunction.

DCM patients may experience various clinical outcomes due to heterogeneous responses to heart failure therapy. While some experience rapidly progressive heart failure or sudden cardiac death, others achieve LVRR. Current guidelines recommend that the main indication for ICD placement in symptomatic DCM patients (NYHA class ≥II) is LVEF ≤35%. 25 However, several studies have found that this indicator has poor sensitivity and specificity in identifying high‐risk patients and may lead to increased risk and cost. 26 Thus, early prediction of LVRR after OMT is crucial for determining appropriate treatment plans and stratifying patients' risk precisely. In our study, LVSVi, LVRI and rGLPS were significant predictors of LVRR. All three can be easily obtained in clinical practice without contrast and extra sequences. We also added a nomogram and decision curve analysis to help to better understand and apply the model (Figures 4 and 5 ). A nomogram including the three indicators was drawn to calculate the probability of LVRR for each individual. A nomogram is a user‐friendly graphic to inform clinical decision‐making. DCA revealed a net clinical benefit of adding rGLPS to LVSVi and LVRI over using LVSVi and LVRI only. In previous studies, the combination of multiple parameters from clinical findings, imaging and circulating biomarkers has shown promising performance. Xie et al. built an XGBoost model to predict LVRR in first‐diagnosed dilated cardiomyopathy, in which cystatin C, right ventricular end‐diastolic dimension measured by echocardiography, electrocardiogram, and HDL‐C were the most important features. 27 Other studies found HF duration, baseline LVEF, and QRS duration were associated with LVRR. 5 , 8 , 9

CMR examinations are commonly done in clinical practice and can detect the presence and extent of abnormal myocardial tissue, such as myocardial edema, scarring, fibrosis, and infiltration. 6 Regional and diffuse myocardial fibrosis can be noninvasively visualized by LGE and myocardial T1 mapping respectively, which can independently predict adverse cardiovascular events in DCM patients. 28 , 29 , 30 However, the relationship between myocardial fibrosis and LVRR in DCM patients needs further investigation. 10 , 11 , 12 , 25 Toshiro et al. found native T1 < 1,366 ms remained a significant predictor of LVRR (odds ratio, 11.3), whereas the presence and segmental extent of LGE did not. 31 Nevertheless, only a lower LVEF and absence of LGE remained independent predictors of LVRR in another prospective study. 10 In our study, the extent of LGE was smaller in the LVRR group (1.83% vs. 3.41%, P = 0.025), but the occurrence of LGE was not different between the two groups (80.00% vs. 92.06%, P = 0.071). Neither of them was an independent predictor of LVRR. The reason might be that patients with implanted devices were excluded, and LGE has been reported to be a strong independent predictor of LVRR in these patients. 32 In our study, DCM patients with LVRR had a lower LV volume index and LVSV index at baseline, although LVEF was similar between the two groups. One possible reason was that a minor degree of LV dilation could reduce LV wall stress and neurohormonal activation, which can better trigger or amplify LVRR in DCM. 27 Recently, LVRI, a novel index, has been proposed to quantify LV remodelling and has proven helpful in differentiating cardiomyopathy phenotypes. 33 , 34 Our study demonstrated that DCM patients with LVRR had a higher LVRI at baseline, which independently predicted LVRR in DCM patients. The left ventricular dilatation of DCM is accompanied by irregular myocyte hypertrophy, fibrosis, and myocyte damage. 1 Pathological ventricular remodelling in DCM can affect LV passive elastic properties, myocardial mass, and LV chamber size. 35 In DCM patients, eccentric remodelling occurs when the massive volume overload and progressive chamber dilation cannot be compensated by wall‐thickening. Eccentric remodelling leads to a decreased LVRI value since it combines LV mass and LVEDV. 27 A higher LVRI at baseline indicates a lighter degree of cardiac remodelling and less damage to myocardial tissue.

Strain is a normalized variable that reflects the relative change in the length of myocardial segments. rStrains further normalized strain by LVMi and showed additional value in differentiating pathologies with increased myocardial mass from each other. 19 We found that DCM patients with LVRR had higher nStrains in the longitudinal direction and higher rStrains in the longitudinal and circumferential directions at baseline in this study. These results indicate that patients with better‐preserved LV deformation are prone to experience LVRR. The results of the intra‐ and interobserver analyses showed that natural LV strains had excellent reliability, which means that the LV strains derived from CMR are reproducible parameters to comprehensively explore the cardiac dysfunction of DCM patients. GLPS defines longitudinal contraction of the LV from base to apex, closely relating to the contraction of the subendocardial layer. Biere et al. found that the subendocardial myocardium and GLPS were first impaired in myocardial ischemia. 36 STE and CMR‐FT studies have shown that decreased GLPS has prognostic value for DCM patients, improving risk stratification and predicting major cardiovascular events. 37 , 38 , 39 Moreover, Jung et al. demonstrated that LV GLPS values measured by STE at baseline were higher in DCM patients with LVRR (11.9 ± 1.6 vs. 8.2 ± 2.9, P < 0.001). 12 The LV GLPS values of DCM patients have improved after a median follow‐up of 6–9 months after OMT. 40 , 41 Our study, further found that rGLPS (cutoff value of 0.045), rather than GLPS, was an independent determinant of LVRR in DCM patients. 13 , 41 Myocardial strain depends not only on myocardial contraction but also on intracellular, extracellular, and molecular components of the myocardium. 42 This can be reflected by the mass of the myocardium. The rStrain, which is defined as the absolute value of LV global peak strain divided by LVMi, is less susceptible to changes in myocardial mass and can better reflect myocardial contraction function. We found that the LVMi differed significantly between the two groups and that rGLPS was an independent determinant of LVRR in DCM patients. The significant difference in LVMi contributed to the change in nStrain and thus diluted the change in contraction. This could explain why rGLPS, rather than GLPS, was an independent determinant of LVRR in DCM patients. The AUC of prediction was the highest when combining LVSVi, LVRI and rGLPS, though it was not significantly different from that of LVSVi plus LVRI. Further research is needed to verify the incremental value of rGLPS.

Limitations

There were several limitations to our study. First, we excluded patients with device implantation and patients who did not undergo follow‐up echocardiography. This may have led to some selection bias. Second, although we demonstrated that LV strain derived from CMR‐FT was reproducible, it varies widely depending on various imaging parameters, equipments, postprocessing software packages, etc. The generalizability of this result is limited. Third, the median follow‐up time was 9 months. Longer follow‐up periods are necessary to confirm the predictive value of LVRI and rGLPS. Fourth, this was a single‐center study, and the sample size was relatively small. Larger‐scale and prospective studies are warranted to validate our findings.

Conclusions

In this single‐center study, patients with LVRR had a lower LV volume index, lower LVSV index, higher LVRI, and preserved myocardial deformation in the longitudinal and circumferential directions at baseline. In addition, LVRI and rGLPS at baseline, obtained by the cine‐MRI sequence without contrast agent, are independent determinants of LVRR. These findings may have clinical and research implications for the management of DCM.
