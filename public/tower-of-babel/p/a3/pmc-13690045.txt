Association Between Daytime Liver Transplantation and Hospital Length of Stay and Graft and Patient Survival
Authors: Elizabeth A. Swanson, Christina M. Fleischer, Seth A. Waits, Christopher J. Sonnenday, Michael J. Englesbe, Kyle H. Sheetz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Prior evidence suggests no “daytime effect” in liver transplantation, where the outcomes of daytime transplants are better because of rested surgical teams and the availability of full hospital resources. Daytime transplantation has recently increased with the rapid adoption of normothermic machine perfusion (NMP). The association with patient outcomes and whether this can be attributed to procedural time of day or NMP remains unclear.Materials and methods:We used the United Network for Organ Sharing data for liver transplants between October 2018 and September 2024 to examine the outcomes of daytime (10:00–21:59 reperfusion) versus nighttime (22:00–9:59 reperfusion) transplants.Results:Transplants with NMP were more likely to occur during the day than those with static cold storage (69.8% versus 54.4%, P < 0.001). After accounting for donor and recipient characteristics using multivariable regression, no significant differences were observed in hospital length of stay or in 90-d patient survival rate between daytime and nighttime transplantation. There was a statistically significant, but not clinically meaningful, decrease in 90-d graft survival for daytime transplantation (94.8 versus 95.2%, relative risk 0.995, P = 0.03).Conclusions:Given that we observed equivalent outcomes between daytime and nighttime liver transplantation, the potential benefits of daytime liver transplantation may instead be related to nuanced clinical outcomes, such as operative duration and transfusion requirements, and nonclinical outcomes, such as workforce satisfaction, operating room logistics, and hospital finances.

Introduction

The association between time of day and surgical outcomes is debated. Although nighttime operations have been associated with increased morbidity and mortality, this association has not been consistently observed in thoracic or abdominal transplantation.1–6 In liver transplantation, a single-center retrospective analysis found that daytime transplantation was associated with shorter operative times, higher rates of extubating in the operating room, and decreased need for postoperative blood transfusions.7 An association between the time of day and liver transplant patient and graft survival has not yet been identified.7–10 However, there have been significant practice changes in liver transplantation since these analyses, including the rapid adoption of normothermic machine perfusion (NMP), which has allowed unprecedented transplant surgeon discretion over the timing of liver transplants and permitted an increase in daytime transplantation.11,12

Whether the historical findings of no association between procedural time of day and clinical outcomes in liver transplantation persist in modern practice, where NMP permits discretion over operative start times, remains unclear. On the one hand, NMP has been demonstrated to improve outcomes in liver transplantation, particularly for donation after circulatory death donors which represent a growing proportion of organ donors.11,13,14 On the other hand, improved outcomes for daytime transplantation could be secondary to rested surgical teams and full operating room and hospital resources.

We used United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) data to identify adult deceased-donor liver transplant recipients from October 1, 2018, through September 30, 2024. We evaluated the association between reperfusion time and transplant outcomes using multivariable regression models to account for donor and recipient characteristics. We chose hospital length of stay, a surrogate for postoperative complications, as our primary outcome and patient and graft survival as our secondary outcomes.15

Patients and Methods

Data source and patient population

We used UNOS data to develop a retrospective cohort of adult patients who underwent deceased donor liver transplantation from October 1, 2018, through September 30, 2024. We selected the timeframe of the study cohort to include a period of 3 y before and after initial approval of NMP by the United States Food and Drug Administration. We excluded patients who underwent simultaneous transplantation of other organs, split or reduced liver transplantation, or whose grafts were preserved using a method other than NMP or static cold storage.

Outcomes and covariates

The primary outcome, hospital length of stay, was chosen as a surrogate for postoperative complications given the lack of nuanced transplant outcomes in the UNOS data.15 The secondary outcomes were 90-d graft and patient survival. We accounted for the following covariates in our regression models: recipient age, recipient sex, recipient diagnosis, recipient final model for end-stage liver disease score, donor type (donation after brain death versus circulatory death), organ preservation method (NMP versus static cold storage), normothermic regional perfusion, and daytime versus nighttime transplantation. Daytime transplantation was defined as transplantation with liver reperfusion between 10:00 and 21:59, and nighttime transplantation was defined as transplantation with liver reperfusion between 22:00 and 09:59.

Statistical analysis

We performed univariate analyses using the χ2 test or t-test, where appropriate, to compare the characteristics of patients who underwent daytime and nighttime transplantation. Tests of statistical significance were two-sided with an alpha value of 0.05. We used multivariable logistic regression to estimate hospital length of stay and Poisson regression to estimate the 90-d graft and patient survival rates. No imputation of missing data was performed. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 19.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies.16

Informed consent

This retrospective cohort study used deidentified data and was exempted from human subjects research review and informed consent by the University of Michigan Medical School Institutional Review Board (HUM00242129).

Results

A total of 51,822 adult patients underwent deceased-donor liver transplantation during the study period. After excluding 602 patients who underwent simultaneous transplantation of other organs, 511 patients who underwent split or reduced liver transplantation, and 417 patients whose grafts were preserved using a method other than NMP or static cold storage, 50,299 patients were included in the study. Of these, 22,276 underwent daytime and 28,023 underwent nighttime liver transplantation. There were no clinically significant differences in recipient characteristics between those who underwent daytime and nighttime liver transplantation (Table 1).

The organ preservation method was associated with the procedural timing of both the donor and recipient operations (Fig.). For donor operations, 65.2% of donors whose livers were preserved with NMP had a cross-clamp time during the daytime, whereas 58.0% of donors whose livers were preserved with static cold storage had a cross-clamp time during the daytime (P < 0.001). For recipient operations, 69.8% of the livers preserved with NMP were reperfused during the daytime versus 54.4% of the livers preserved with static cold storage (P < 0.001).

Compared with patients who underwent nighttime transplantation, those who underwent daytime transplantation had equivalent hospital length of stay after accounting for donor and recipient characteristics using multivariable regression (14.5 d for both daytime and nighttime transplantation, P = 0.73, Table 2). There was no statistically significant difference in 90-d patient (relative risk [RR] 0.997, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.993–1.002) survival between daytime and nighttime liver transplantation. There was a small statistically significant, but not clinically meaningful, difference in 90-d graft survival between daytime and nighttime liver transplantation (RR 0.995, 95% CI 0.991–0.999). These findings were not modified by the use of NMP.

Discussion

In an analysis of adult deceased-donor liver transplant recipients from October 1, 2018, through September 30, 2024, patients who underwent daytime transplantation had equivalent hospital length of stay to those who underwent nighttime transplantation. Although a small statistically significant difference in 90-d graft survival was seen between the daytime and nighttime liver transplantation groups, there were no clinically meaningful differences in either 90-d graft or 90-d patient survival. Furthermore, we identified that NMP enabled transplant surgeons to shift the timing of both donor and recipient operations to the daytime.

An association between procedural time of day and decreased patient morbidity and mortality during daytime hours has been reported for surgical patients as a whole.1,4 However, differences in morbidity and mortality between daytime and nighttime operations have not been consistently observed in liver transplantation.7–10,17,18 A retrospective analysis of UNOS data for nearly 95,000 adult liver transplants between 1987 and 2010 found no statistically significant differences between daytime (07:00–19:00) and nighttime (19:00–07:00) transplants for either graft or patient survival at 30, 90, or 365 d.9 A single-center analysis of 578 liver transplants from 1995 to 2008 similarly found no differences in complications or long-term survival between daytime (03:00–15:00) and nighttime (15:00–03:00) transplants.17 However, the authors did find that nighttime transplants with a longer duration were associated with a twofold greater risk of early mortality than daytime operations of similarly long duration, suggesting that there may be an association between procedural time of day and clinical outcomes for a subset of more complex liver transplants. A separate single-center retrospective analysis found that daytime transplantation was associated with shorter operative times, higher rates of extubating in the operating room, and decreased need for postoperative blood transfusions, suggesting that daytime operation may be associated with nuanced clinical outcomes not included in national transplant data.7

Since these studies, there have been significant changes in liver transplantation, including the adoption of NMP, increased use of organs procured from donation after circulatory deaths, and a shift toward daytime transplantation.11,12 NMP has been associated with improved liver transplant outcomes, including shorter operative times, shorter intensive care unit and hospital length of stay, reductions in early allograft dysfunction, reductions in ischemic biliary complications, and improved graft and patient survival.11,13,14 NMP has also facilitated a shift toward daytime liver transplantation in both the United States and Europe.19,20 The benefits of NMP are likely associated with the technology itself rather than the shift toward daytime transplantation, given our finding of no clinically meaningful difference in outcomes between daytime and nighttime transplantation.

NMP has enabled unprecedented discretion in the timing of liver transplants. When permitted to choose when to operate, our analysis demonstrated that transplant surgeons shift donor recipient operations to the daytime. Although graft and patient outcomes are equivalent between daytime and nighttime transplantation, evaluating these outcomes alone does not capture the full benefits of discretion over operative timing. These benefits include improved work satisfaction, reduced burnout, and increased willingness of surgical trainees to pursue training in abdominal transplantation.21 Given that transplant surgeons have a higher number of hours worked and nights on-call per week than surgeons in other specialties, both of which are established risk factors for burnout and depression among surgeons, the shift to daytime transplantation could ameliorate these risk factors.22–26 Daytime transplantation may also attract trainees to the field by changing surgical resident perceptions of the specialty and by allowing greater exposure of surgical residents to liver transplantation while complying with duty hour requirements.7,27 Anecdotally, daytime transplantation is associated with improved morale and retention of transplant teams, including anesthesiologists, operating room nurses, and surgical technicians, although this remains to be explored in the literature.

Our study should be interpreted with the following limitations in mind. We could not fully account for all factors that influence the procedural timing of liver transplantation, including recipient factors, such as previous abdominal surgery and anatomical anomalies, and logistical factors, such as the timing of donor operations and availability of operating room staff. Additionally, we could not evaluate outcomes that were not captured by UNOS data. Finally, donor recovery centers can also be used to shift liver transplantation to daytime and our analysis does not address the outcomes and cost-effectiveness of NMP versus donor recovery centers to facilitate daytime transplantation.

NMP has transformed liver transplantation, allowing an operation that was performed, until recently, regardless of the time of day to a semielective operation that can be performed primarily during the daytime. This represents an important change in practice. Our study revealed that there continues to be equivalent outcomes between daytime and nighttime liver transplantation in the NMP era. Thus, the value of discretion over operative timing may need to be assessed by other measures including nuanced clinical outcomes not captured by UNOS data and associations with transplant workforce wellbeing, operating room logistics, and hospital finances.
