Staphylococcus aureus: Antimicrobial resistance, quorum sensing, and antibiofilm approaches
Authors: Christian Joseph N Ong, Oluwagbemisola Elizabeth Elesho, Bolorunduro Babatunde Bramwell, Kevin Smith P Cabuhat, Grace D Bacalzo, Jose Jurel M Nuevo, Jamil Allen G Fortaleza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStaphylococcus aureus is a clinically important bacterial pathogen causing infections from superficial skin lesions to life-threatening systemic diseases. The emergence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) has compounded the global health burden, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, as its quorum-sensing (QS) mediated mechanisms contribute to its persistence, resistance, and evasion from host immune responses and antimicrobial treatments. Thus, these features compromise the effectiveness of conventional antibiotics, urging the need for alternative therapeutic approaches. To resolve these issues, several non-antibiotic antibiofilm approaches have been developed. Bacteriophages and phage-derived enzymes show promising specificity in lysing bacterial cells and disrupting biofilms. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), with their broad-spectrum activity, destabilize bacterial membranes and modulate immune responses. Monoclonal antibodies can neutralize toxins or inhibit adhesion molecules within biofilms. Phytochemicals have demonstrated activity against QS pathways and efflux pumps. Metal ion chelators like deferiprone interfere with iron acquisition, which is essential for biofilm stability. Nanoparticles (NPs), ranging from metallic and polymeric to lipid-based and cyclodextrin-based systems, enhance drug delivery and biofilm penetration. CRISPR-Cas systems provide precise genome editing to target resistance genes and virulence factors. Rhamnolipids disrupt biofilm matrix integrity, while enzymes such as dispersin B degrade extracellular polymeric substances. Photodynamic and laser therapies offer localized disruption of biofilm structures through oxidative stress. Collectively, this review offers a transformative complementary approach to traditional antibiotics, enhancing treatment efficacy while potentially reducing the emergence of resistance. Continued research on delivery systems, safety profiles, and synergistic combinations will be pivotal for their clinical translation against S. aureus infections.Keywords: antimicrobial resistance, antibiofilm approaches, biofilm formation, MRSA, quorum sensing

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is an escalating global health crisis, primarily driven by the overuse and misuse of antimicrobial agents. This selective pressure fosters the emergence and proliferation of resistant pathogens, enabling them to evade conventional treatments and persist in both clinical and environmental settings [1]. AMR is currently responsible for an estimated 700,000 deaths annually and, if left unmitigated, is projected to cause up to 10 million deaths per year by 2050, surpassing cancer as a leading cause of mortality [2]. The growing resistance crisis threatens the efficacy of existing therapies, increases the burden of infectious diseases, and underscores the urgent need for alternative treatment strategies to avoid a looming post-antibiotic era. Among the pathogens of concern, Staphylococcus aureus has emerged as a significant contributor to AMR-associated morbidity. Once a commensal gram-positive bacterium commonly found on the skin and mucosa of humans and animals, S. aureus has evolved into a formidable pathogen responsible for a broad spectrum of infections. These range from superficial skin and soft tissue infections to life-threatening conditions such as osteomyelitis, endocarditis, and pneumonia [3, 4]. Even non-lethal infections, such as furuncles, abscesses, and wound infections, exert a substantial burden on public health due to their high prevalence and associated pain and morbidity. In addition, S. aureus has been implicated in the pathogenesis of chronic inflammatory skin disorders, including atopic dermatitis [5, 6].

A critical factor contributing to the persistence and therapeutic resistance of S. aureus is its capacity to form biofilms, structured microbial communities embedded in a self-produced extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix. Biofilm development begins with the adhesion of planktonic bacterial cells to surfaces, followed by proliferation and maturation into complex, multilayered structures. The EPS matrix, composed of exopolysaccharides, extracellular DNA (eDNA), RNA, proteins, and lipids, plays a crucial role in protecting the bacteria from antimicrobial agents and host immune responses [7]. In clinical settings, particularly in infections associated with indwelling medical devices and catheters, biofilm formation is a central virulence strategy of S. aureus [3]. Bacteria within biofilms can exhibit tolerance to antibiotics at levels 100 to 1,000 times higher than their planktonic counterparts, posing substantial challenges for treatment and eradication [8]. This review will discuss the mechanisms underlying AMR in S. aureus, its regulation through quorum sensing (QS), and the current and emerging strategies aimed at disrupting biofilm formation and improving treatment outcomes.

Mechanism of inducing resistance in S. aureus

S. aureus is a highly adaptive bacterium capable of rapidly developing resistance to a broad range of antimicrobial drugs. This resistance is primarily acquired through horizontal gene transfer (HGT), or the lateral transmission of AMR genes between organisms. Such genetic exchanges facilitate the emergence of antibiotic-resistant variants without requiring spontaneous mutations [9], thereby intensifying selective pressure in clinical settings and enabling resistant strains to spread into communities. Drug-resistant S. aureus infections were first documented in the 1940s, initially emerging in hospitals due to the widespread misuse and overuse of penicillin [10]. These strains produce a plasmid-encoded β-lactamase (penicillinase), an enzyme that hydrolyzes the β-lactam ring of penicillin, rendering the drug ineffective. By the 1950s and 1960s, penicillin-resistant S. aureus (PRSA) had spread extensively into the general population, with over 90% of current S. aureus isolates now producing penicillinase. The rise of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) has further exacerbated the global AMR crisis, posing serious threats to public health. In 2019 alone, MRSA was responsible for an estimated 100,000 deaths and 3.5 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [11]. As AMR continues to expand globally, many clinical antibiotics are becoming increasingly ineffective against MRSA [9]. β-Lactamase synthesis and β-lactam inactivation

The synthesis of β-lactamases, such as penicillinases, is a common resistance mechanism employed by methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) and some methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) strains. These enzymes have a narrow substrate spectrum, primarily targeting natural penicillins and aminopenicillins. β-Lactamases are encoded by the blaZ gene, which is typically part of the blaI-blaR1-blaZ operon located on plasmids and transposons. These genetic elements mediate the inactivation of β-lactam antibiotics, such as penicillin and cefazolin, by hydrolyzing the β-lactam ring, thereby preventing the antibiotics from reaching their target penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) [12].

Overexpression of efflux pumps

Efflux pumps in S. aureus play a critical role in virulence, cellular homeostasis, and AMR They contribute to the release of virulence factors, the elimination of toxic endogenous metabolites, and the regulation of cellular stress responses [13]. By preventing the intracellular accumulation of lethal drug concentrations, these pumps provide an initial defense mechanism that enhances bacterial survival prior to the acquisition of genetic resistance. S. aureus possesses several plasmid-encoded multidrug-resistant (MDR) efflux pump proteins embedded in the cell membrane, including QacA/B, NorA, and Smr, which confer resistance to a broad range of antibiotics [13]. In certain MRSA strains, increased tolerance to chlorhexidine and enhanced resistance to non-β-lactam antibiotics have been associated with the qacA and qacB genes. These genes are linked to heightened efflux activity and overexpression of efflux pumps [14]. Their ability to recognize and expel a wide variety of structurally unrelated compounds contributes not only to multidrug resistance but also to reduced susceptibility to biocides, ultimately resulting in specialized MDR phenotypes [14].

Penicillin-binding proteins

Penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) are important β-lactam antibiotic targets due to their importance for cell wall biosynthesis and maintenance [15]. These transpeptidase enzymes found in the cell membrane catalyze pentapeptide chain cross-links to synthesize S. aureus peptidoglycans. Most native strains contain PBP1, PBP2, PBP3, and PBP4, while MRSA strains include an additional PBP2a transpeptidase encoded by the mecA gene. This allows them to become highly resistant to β-lactam ring antibiotic inhibition and maintain peptidoglycan crosslinking during peptidoglycan biosynthesis, at the same time producing β-lactamases that hydrolyze the four-membered β-lactam ring amide bonds, inactivating β-lactam antibiotic PBP target inhibition [16].

Target mutation in DNA and RNA synthesis

S. aureus exhibits extensive fluoroquinolone resistance due to: (i) mutations in the norA gene encoding for a membrane-associated active efflux pump; (ii) mutations in the gyrA and gyrB genes encoding for DNA gyrase; and (iii) mutations in the gyrlA and gyrlB genes encoding for DNA topoisomerase IV [17]. Mutations in DNA gyrase or topoisomerase IV enzymes result in modifications that lead to resistance to fluoroquinolones through changes that influence the drug's availability and modify how fluoroquinolones interact with their target sites [17]. The topoisomerase II gyrA and gyrB genes in particular exhibit the quinolone-resistance-determining-region (QRDR) mutations that confer resistance, rendering the enzyme less susceptible to fluoroquinolone inhibition [17]. The amino acid alterations for some of the more prevalent QRDR mutations appear to decrease the enzyme-DNA complex's affinity for fluoroquinolones.

QS-mediated activities in S. aureus

Bacteria utilize communication systems known as QS to respond to environmental stressors. This process is mediated by the production, release, and detection of autoinducer (AI) signaling molecules [18, 19]. QS activation is influenced by increasing cell density and the composition of the surrounding microbial community. As AI concentrations rise proportionally with population density, these molecules mediate both intraspecies and interspecies communication by binding to specific bacterial receptors [19]. Once a critical threshold of AIs is reached, bacterial populations synchronize their gene expression, resulting in collective behaviors such as antibiotic resistance, biofilm formation, bioluminescence, symbiosis, sporulation, and various virulence-associated activities [19]. These include swarming motility (a coordinated surface movement driven by flagella) and swimming motility (individual flagella-mediated propulsion in liquid environments). QS also regulates the secretion of extracellular enzymes, such as proteases, highlighting its central role in microbial adaptation and survival [20].

S. aureus exhibits a remarkable capacity to cause a wide range of infections, largely due to its extensive arsenal of virulence factors. The regulation of many of these factors is controlled by the accessory gene regulator (agr) QS system. This system is encoded by the agr locus, which includes two key promoters, P2 and P3, responsible for the transcription of RNAII and RNAIII, respectively. RNAII encodes the core components of the QS system: AgrD, the precursor of the autoinducing peptide (AIP) [21]; AgrB, an enzyme that processes and exports AIP [21]; AgrC, a membrane-bound histidine kinase that detects AIP [22]; and AgrA, a response regulator [23]. AgrB processes AgrD into a thiolactone-containing cyclic AIP, which is then secreted outside the bacterial cell. As the bacterial population increases, AIP accumulates in the environment and binds to AgrC, triggering its autophosphorylation [24]. This activates AgrA through phosphorylation, initiating a positive feedback loop by enhancing the activity of both the P2 and P3 promoters, thereby increasing RNAII and RNAIII production. Additionally, AgrA directly promotes RNAIII synthesis through the P3 promoter [25]. RNAIII functions as a key effector molecule that upregulates the expression of numerous virulence genes (e.g., leukocidins, hemolysins, toxic shock syndrome toxin, exoproteases, and lipases) while downregulating genes associated with adhesion and colonization, including surface proteins, biofilm-associated proteins, protein A, and fibronectin-binding proteins.

Beyond the agr system, S. aureus also utilizes the luxS/AI-2 QS pathway, which is conserved among both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. This system is mediated by the autoinducer-2 (AI-2), derived from the activity of the luxS gene, which catalyzes the formation of the precursor molecule 4,5-dihydroxy-2,3-pentanedione (DPD) [26, 27]. The luxS/AI-2 system coordinates gene expression in response to cell density and plays a key role in biofilm regulation. AI-2 has been shown to enhance biofilm formation by acting as a negative regulator of biofilm repression pathways [28, 29]. In luxS-deficient mutants, expression of the repressor gene icaR is downregulated, while icaA—a key component of the biofilm matrix synthesis operon—is upregulated, suggesting that AI-2 indirectly represses the icaADBC operon through icaR activation. Notably, introducing low nanomolar concentrations of DPD was sufficient to alter biofilm formation, whereas higher concentrations had no observable effect [30, 31].

Furthermore, the rbf gene has been identified as a positive regulator of biofilm formation, promoting polysaccharide intercellular adhesin (PIA)-dependent biofilms by binding to the sarX promoter [32, 33]. This enhances sarX expression and subsequently suppresses icaR levels. In luxS mutants, rbf transcript levels are elevated, but can be restored to baseline by either complementing with a luxS-expressing plasmid or through the addition of exogenous AI-2 [32]. These findings suggest that luxS/AI-2 signaling inhibits rbf expression and limits icaA transcription, thereby constraining PIA-mediated biofilm development. Biofilm formation in S. aureus and its AMR association

Biofilm formation in S. aureus is a complex, QS-regulated process that significantly contributes to the development and persistence of AMR (Fig. 2) [3]. This process begins with the initial attachment of bacterial cells to a surface, followed by adherence, proliferation, and the production of EPS that form a dense, protective matrix around the bacterial community [29]. As the biofilm matures, the EPS thickens, creating a physical barrier that limits the penetration of antimicrobials and reduces their minimum effective concentrations [34]. The resulting heterogeneous environment, characterized by gradients in nutrients and oxygen, induces some bacteria to enter a slow-growing or dormant state, thereby decreasing their susceptibility to antimicrobials that target active cellular processes [35].

Biofilm formation mechanism in S. aureus. Its cell initially attaches to surfaces, followed by adherence, proliferation, and EPS production. The EPS thickens following biofilm maturation, creating a physical barrier that limits antimicrobial penetration

Furthermore, biofilm-associated S. aureus cells often upregulate genes involved in stress responses and antimicrobial efflux pumps, enhancing their ability to survive treatment. The biofilm matrix also promotes HGT, facilitating the spread of resistance genes among bacterial populations. In addition, persister cells within biofilms can tolerate antimicrobial exposure without harboring genetic resistance, contributing to chronic and recurrent infections [36, 37]. S. aureus cells within biofilms can be up to 1,000 times more resistant to antimicrobials compared to their free-floating, planktonic counterparts. This remarkable increase in resistance, driven by physical protection, altered physiology, and genetic exchange, makes S. aureus biofilms particularly resilient to antimicrobial agents, posing a significant challenge in both clinical and environmental settings, such as hospitals and food processing facilities [38].

Antibiofilm approaches

Historically, antibiotics have been the primary treatment for combating biofilm infections, but their effectiveness is increasingly compromised by antibiotic resistance. Conventional antibiotics can sometimes eradicate biofilms by disrupting their formation or by targeting the bacteria when they are in a more vulnerable state. For example, high doses of antibiotics or prolonged exposure can penetrate and break down the biofilm matrix, allowing the antibiotics to reach and kill the bacteria [39]. However, this approach often leads to insufficient eradication due to the aforementioned resistance mechanisms. To address these limitations, current and novel approaches have been developed to enhance the treatment of biofilm infections.

Phage therapy in biofilm

Phage therapy has garnered renewed interest for its potential to treat S. aureus biofilm-related infections, particularly those caused by MRSA [40]. For instance, the MRSA-specific phage AB-SA01 has demonstrated significant effectiveness in preventing MRSA biofilm formation [41]. Similarly, phages UPMK_1 and UPMK_2 have shown potent antibiofilm activity, reducing S. aureus and MRSA biofilms by 52% and 51%, respectively, after 6 h of treatment [42]. After 8 h, more than 58% of the biofilm biomass was eliminated. These phages possess unique abilities to penetrate the biofilm matrix, infect bacterial cells, and induce lysis, thereby disrupting biofilm integrity and reducing bacterial populations. In parallel, the endolysin MR-10, at a concentration of 18 μg mL−1, reduced the bacterial count by approximately 1.1 log units, while a higher concentration of 36 μg mL−1 resulted in a reduction of around 1.18 log units after 6 h of incubation [43]. Endolysins SAL-2 and SAP-2 also demonstrated substantial effectiveness in disrupting biofilms, with greater concentrations producing more pronounced effects. SB-1 endolysins were similarly effective in significantly reducing both biofilm thickness and bacterial load [44].

Phage therapy presents a promising strategy for managing biofilm-associated infections caused by S. aureus and MRSA by leveraging the specificity and lytic capabilities of phage within the biofilm environment [45]. However, current limitations include the emergence of phage resistance, restricted phage penetration, strain specificity, challenges in high-throughput production, regulatory hurdles, phage neutralization by the host immune system, and limited understanding of phage-host-microbiome interactions [46, 47]. Future research should focus on engineering phages with enhanced potency, promoting synergism between antibiotics, phages, and the host microbiome, minimizing immune neutralization, and developing scalable, consistent phage-based therapies. The creation of broad-spectrum, low-cytotoxicity phage cocktails will be crucial in advancing phage therapy as a viable alternative or adjunct to conventional antimicrobial treatments [48].

Antimicrobial peptides

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are short secondary metabolite molecules, typically comprising 10 to 100 amino acid residues, found across all biological domains as part of their innate immune response [49, 50]. Characterized by their amphipathic nature, containing both hydrophilic cations and hydrophobic residues, AMPs target negatively charged bacterial membranes, causing cell lysis and inhibiting pathogen proliferation [51]. Some AMPs can also cross membranes intact to interact with intracellular substrates, such as proteins and nucleic acids [52]. AMPs have gained increasing attention as alternatives to traditional antibiotics due to their broad-spectrum antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities, low cytotoxicity, biodegradability, immunomodulatory properties, low propensity for resistance development, and synergistic interactions with conventional antimicrobials [50, 53]. Their structural diversity, which depends on their biological origin, enables them to kill microbes via multiple mechanisms [54]. According to the Antimicrobial Peptide Database (APD), as of January 2024, 3,940 peptides have been cataloged, including 3,146 natural AMPs and 574 with anti-MRSA activity.

Anti-MRSA AMPs, derived from mammals, insects, and microorganisms, are categorized into four structural groups: (1) linear α-helical peptides, (2) β-sheet peptides with multiple disulfide bonds, (3) β-hairpin or loop peptides with a single disulfide bond, and (4) extended structures [55]. Most fall into the α-helical and β-sheet categories. Cyclic AMPs and α-helical peptides generally show enhanced membrane-penetrating ability [52, 56]. Representative examples include cathelicidin and defensin, cecropin and phormicin, and epifadin, fengycin, gramicidin, nisin, surfactin, and teixobactin. AMPs exert antimicrobial effects through two primary mechanisms: membranolytic and non-membranolytic [57]. Membranolytic mechanisms involve disrupting the bacterial membrane through pore formation or detergent-like action. These include the barrel-stave, carpet, toroidal-pore, and disordered toroidal models [55]. In the barrel-stave model, peptides insert into the membrane to form pores [58]. In the carpet model, AMPs coat the membrane surface, disrupting lipid organization and increasing permeability [53]. The toroidal and disordered toroidal models involve curvature and pore formation via AMP-lipid interactions.

AMP composition significantly influences anti-MRSA efficacy, selectivity, and stability [56]. Proline-rich AMPs use the SbmA transporter to enter cells and inhibit protein synthesis by disrupting aminoacyl-tRNA function [59]. Tryptophan-rich AMPs interact with lipid bilayers, while arginine-rich peptides enhance binding via electrostatic and hydrogen bonding [60, 61]. Histidine-rich AMPs improve membrane penetration and reduce inflammation by suppressing TNFα. Glycine-rich AMPs affect peptide structure and promote phagocytic microbicidal activity [56]. The advent of machine learning tools like Anti-SMASH and BAGEL has enabled the discovery of novel AMPs by mining biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) from genome data [62, 63]. Future research should integrate transcriptomic and metabolomic data to connect BGCs with their products and biosynthetic pathways, accelerating the development of next-generation AMPs with broad-spectrum anti-MRSA activity.

Antibodies

Antibodies, also known as immunoglobulins, are naturally occurring Y-shaped proteins with remarkable specificity and diversity, weighing approximately 150 kDa [64]. Produced by B lymphocytes (B cells), antibodies perform multiple roles in host defense, including pathogen neutralization, elimination of infected or malignant cells, and modulation of inflammatory responses [64–66]. Their activity is mediated by two functional domains: the Fab (fragment antigen-binding), responsible for antigen recognition, and the Fc (fragment crystallizable), which triggers immune effector mechanisms. Some antibody functions rely more heavily on one domain than the other [64]. The structural and genetic complexity of microbial species is further compounded by their ability to form multicellular biofilms—highly organized communities encased in an extracellular matrix, which present a significant challenge for conventional antimicrobial therapies [67–69]. In response to the growing issue of biofilm-related infections, antibodies have been proposed as a novel therapeutic approach. They can either inhibit biofilm formation or actively disrupt established biofilms.

For instance, human neutrophil-derived antibodies have demonstrated bactericidal activity against S. aureus strains expressing de-N-acetylated PNAG (dPNAG). Similarly, passive immunization using anti-dPNAG antibodies conjugated with diphtheria toxoid (DT) significantly enhanced bacterial clearance in murine models [70]. However, the primary challenge in designing anti-biofilm antibodies, similar to antiviral and antibacterial antibodies, is the identification of conserved, structurally accessible biofilm components that can serve as reliable targets across multiple species [64]. It is important to recognize that antibodies can occasionally produce unintended effects. Some studies have reported that certain antibodies may paradoxically promote bacterial adhesion or stimulate key processes in biofilm development [71, 72]. Thus, careful selection of antibody targets is essential to maximize efficacy while minimizing adverse outcomes.

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting S. aureus biofilm components have shown considerable potential in both therapeutic and diagnostic contexts [64]. For example, mAbs directed against wall teichoic acid (clone 4497) specifically localized to S. aureus biofilms in vivo, offering a platform for targeted drug delivery. TRL1068, an antibody targeting conserved epitopes within the biofilm matrix, has demonstrated efficacy in disrupting biofilms and reducing bacterial load, particularly when combined with antibiotics [73]. Other promising targets include PIA and the surface-exposed recombinant protein RSesC of Staphylococcus epidermidis, both of which have significantly inhibited biofilm formation in immunized murine models. TRL1068 also binds DNABII family proteins, crucial for stabilizing extracellular DNA in biofilms, exhibiting biofilm-disruptive activity in both in vitro and in vivo settings [74, 75]. Furthermore, anti-S. aureus polyclonal antibodies functionalized on nanoparticles have shown enhanced biofilm inhibition compared to non-functionalized controls. These antibody-based strategies offer a promising alternative to traditional antibiotics by targeting specific biofilm components, enhancing immune responses, and improving treatment outcomes for persistent staphylococcal infections [76]. Phytochemicals

Phytochemicals have emerged as a promising approach in combating biofilm-related infections caused by S. aureus [81–84]. In Table 3, various studies have demonstrated the efficacy of these natural compounds in inhibiting biofilm formation and disrupting established biofilms. For instance, epicatechin and its derivative ent-epicatechin-(4α→8)-epiafzelechin (EEE), isolated from Cassia fistula, exhibit significant antibiofilm activity by forming stable ligand-protein complexes with the transcriptional regulatory protein SarA [85]. Likewise, anthraquinones such as alizarin, purpurin, and quinalizarin inhibit biofilm development and hemolytic activity by chelating calcium ions and repressing biofilm-associated genes [86].

Anti-S. aureus phytochemicals with biofilm inhibitory activity

The combination of phytochemicals with conventional antibiotics has also been explored to enhance antibiofilm efficacy. For example, co-administration of cuminaldehyde with gentamicin significantly increases antibiofilm activity against S. aureus, likely due to elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and enhanced cell membrane permeability [87]. These mechanisms contribute to biofilm disruption and reduced secretion of virulence factors. Other phytochemicals, including morin, pyrrolidine, and quercetin, when combined with antibiotics [88], have shown substantial efficacy, particularly against strains overexpressing antibiotic efflux pumps. Moreover, novel delivery systems such as Pickering emulsions have improved the stability and bioavailability of phytochemicals [89]. Citral and borneol/citral-based Pickering emulsions, for instance, have demonstrated potent antibiofilm effects in both in vitro and in vivo models, including synthetic cystic fibrosis medium and wound environments [90]. These formulations not only reduce the minimum biofilm inhibitory concentration (MBIC) but also eliminate preformed biofilms effectively, underscoring their potential in clinical applications.

Chelators and sulfhydryl compounds

Chelating agents are substances whose structures allow two or more donor atoms (or sites) to bind simultaneously to a metal ion, forming one or more rings. This process is known as chelation, and the resulting molecules are referred to as chelates or chelating groups. These metal complexes can form optically active isomers (R & L), and their stability varies depending on the pattern of complex formation. This variability becomes particularly significant in biological systems such as serum or tissue, where solutions are more diluted. Chelators such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid can interfere with metal ions essential for QS signaling molecules, disrupting the ability of S. aureus to detect and respond to quorum cues [94, 95]. On the other hand, sulfhydryl compounds, which contain sulfur atoms bound to hydrogen atoms (–SH), play crucial roles in biological systems. Commonly referred to as thiols, these compounds are vital for protein structure and function, redox reactions, and antioxidant defense [96, 97]. They are found in key biomolecules, including enzymes, glutathione, and cysteine, and are essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis.

Iron is essential for the development and maturation of bacterial biofilms. It is required for several physiological processes in bacteria, such as energy metabolism, oxygen transport, gene expression regulation, and stabilization of the biofilm polysaccharide matrix [98]. Iron also enhances bacterial cell motility and reduces the formation of persistent biofilms in iron-deficient environments [99]. However, iron is among the most difficult minerals for bacteria to obtain due to its limited bioavailability, often precipitating as insoluble ferric hydroxides under aerobic conditions [100]. Iron also plays a significant role in bacterial pathogenicity. Its availability can regulate the expression of virulence factor genes, including those for toxins and enzymes that support colonization and immune evasion [101]. The signaling role of iron in biofilm formation has been well documented in several bacterial species, including S. aureus. In S. aureus strain SA113, iron is crucial for initial adhesion and the production of PIA, a major component of the biofilm matrix [102].

S. aureus acquires iron through two main pathways: the secretion of siderophores (staphyloferrin A and B) and the Isd (iron-regulated surface determinant) system [102]. Disruption of either pathway significantly impairs colonization, underlining their importance in the virulence of this pathogen. Iron-chelating drugs offer a promising therapeutic approach by reducing iron availability to bacteria. These agents form stable iron complexes, thereby inhibiting biofilm formation and virulence factor production [103]. Moreover, chelating compounds can act as antibiotic adjuvants, disrupting the protective biofilm environment and enhancing bacterial susceptibility to antimicrobials [104]. Chelators vary in their iron-binding affinities, contributing uniquely to their antibacterial activity. Examples include synthetic iron chelators used clinically for treating iron overload, such as deferiprone, deferasirox, and deferoxamine.

Nanoparticles

Metallic nanoparticles (NPs) are increasingly investigated as adjuncts or carriers for antimicrobial therapy due to their ability to enhance antibiotic efficacy and overcome resistance mechanisms. Silver nanoparticles (Ag-NPs), in particular, have shown synergistic activity with conventional antibiotics. For instance, Ag-NPs combined with ampicillin restored its effectiveness against MRSA, with stronger synergy at lower antibiotic concentrations. However, higher antibiotic levels reduced NP activity due to surface coating effects [105]. Beyond silver, other metallic nanostructures demonstrate significant anti-MRSA potential through functionalization with targeting ligands or drug conjugates. Gold nanoparticles (Au-NPs) functionalized with anti-S. aureus peptidoglycan antibodies markedly reduced MRSA survival and biofilm mass [106, 107]. Gold (Au) nanorods and nanocages functionalized with aptamers or antibodies enhanced bacterial killing and antibiotic delivery, with daptomycin-loaded nanocages achieving superior MRSA eradication while sparing mammalian cells [108–110]. Vancomycin-functionalized Au-NPs further improved wound healing and biocompatibility in vivo [111–113].

Similarly, functionalized Ag-NPs exhibited strong antibacterial activity. When conjugated with polymers or platelet membranes, they enhanced MRSA killing, accelerated wound repair, and improved biofilm eradication when loaded with vancomycin or chlorin e6 [114–118]. Hybrid Au–Ag NPs conjugated with anti-MRSA antibodies achieved an 11-fold increase in targeting ability and reduced inflammation in vivo [117]. Magnetite nanoparticles also displayed therapeutic potential. Functionalization with antibodies, chitosan, or aptamers enabled selective MRSA killing, minimal host cytotoxicity, and reduced infection severity in vivo, with up to 98% bacterial reduction in vitro [119–122]. Likewise, zinc-based nanomaterials improved specificity and localized accumulation. Zinc oxide quantum dots functionalized with UBI29-41 peptide enhanced methicillin activity, while zinc gallogermanate NPs coated with chitosan-benzeneboronic acid targeted infection sites, effectively eliminating abscesses and inflammation [123, 124].

Parallel to nanotechnology, immunotherapeutic strategies targeting MRSA biofilms are gaining traction. Clone 4497, an antibody against wall teichoic acids, has been conjugated to drug-loaded nanoparticles, enabling precise delivery into biofilm matrices and enhancing therapeutic localization [73, 125]. TRL1068, which targets DNABII family proteins stabilizing extracellular DNA, disrupted biofilm structure and reduced bacterial burden in murine models, underscoring clinical potential [126]. Antibodies against extracellular matrix components such as PIA and RSesC protein have also shown promise. Immunization with RSesC inhibited biofilm development and virulence in vivo [127]. Furthermore, polyclonal anti-S. aureus antibodies immobilized on nanoparticle surfaces improved targeting, opsonization, phagocytic killing, and drug delivery efficiency [128].

Collectively, metallic nanoparticles, including gold, silver, magnetite, and zinc-based systems, exhibit strong anti-MRSA efficacy, with many demonstrating in vivo biocompatibility and therapeutic benefits. Nevertheless, most evidence remains at the preclinical level, highlighting the urgent need for translational and clinical evaluation. Together, advances in nanoparticle–antibiotic combinations and antibody-based immunotherapies provide complementary strategies for tackling MRSA biofilms. Integration of these approaches into translational pipelines may pave the way for effective clinical interventions against antibiotic-resistant pathogens.

CRISPR

The CRISPR-Cas system serves as an adaptive immune defense in bacteria and archaea, enabling sequence-specific recognition and cleavage of invading nucleic acids. Structurally, it consists of a CRISPR array composed of short repeat sequences interspersed with unique spacers, preceded by an A-T-rich leader region containing the transcriptional promoter [129]. Based on effector protein composition, CRISPR-Cas systems are classified into Class 1, which employ multi-protein complexes, and Class 2, which rely on single-protein effectors such as Cas9, Cas12, and Cas13 [130]. Among these, type II Class 2 systems, particularly Cas9, are widely studied due to their structural simplicity and applicability in targeting antibiotic resistance genes. More recently, type I systems have also been engineered for resistance gene eradication [131]. Mechanistically, CRISPR-Cas immunity proceeds in three stages: (i) adaptation – acquisition of new spacers from invading DNA; (ii) expression – production of CRISPR RNA (crRNA) and Cas proteins; and (iii) interference – crRNA-guided recognition and cleavage of complementary nucleic acids [132, 133]. Mature crRNA forms a ribonucleoprotein complex with Cas proteins, which cleaves target DNA or RNA with high specificity [134]. This precision can be harnessed to disrupt resistance determinants such as mecA, which encodes PBP2a and confers methicillin resistance in S. aureus. By inducing double-stranded DNA breaks, Cas9 disables resistance genes and restores antibiotic susceptibility, while minimizing collateral effects on commensal microbiota. Several proof-of-concept studies highlight the therapeutic potential of this approach. Bikard et al. [135] employed phagemid-mediated CRISPR-Cas9 delivery to selectively eliminate virulence and resistance genes in S. aureus, restoring antibiotic sensitivity. More recently, Ates et al. [136] engineered CRISPR-Cas antimicrobials targeting mecA, aacA, grlA, and grlB in MRSA. Electroporation-mediated delivery efficiently disrupted these genes, re-sensitizing isolates to β-lactams, quinolones, and aminoglycosides. Expanding beyond DNA targeting, Shimamori et al. [137] developed a CRISPR-Cas13a system packaged into non-replicating phage capsids (AB-capsids). By cleaving RNA transcripts of mecA, this strategy disrupted essential bacterial functions and induced cell death, providing an alternative RNA-targeting approach. Efficient delivery remains a critical determinant of CRISPR's clinical translation. Current strategies include physical methods (e.g., electroporation, microinjection), which are direct but potentially damaging; chemical carriers (e.g., Cas9–sgRNA complexes with organic or composite materials), which offer flexibility; and biological vectors such as phages, viruses, and extracellular vesicles, which remain the most efficient and widely explored [111]. Taken together, CRISPR-Cas systems, whether targeting DNA or RNA, offer a precision-based antimicrobial strategy that selectively disrupts resistance determinants in MRSA. By neutralizing resistance genes rather than relying on broad-spectrum killing, CRISPR technology represents a paradigm shift, restoring the efficacy of existing antibiotics while limiting horizontal gene transfer and the spread of resistance.

Conclusion

The persistent challenge of AMR in S. aureus, driven largely by its robust biofilm formation and intricate QS systems, necessitates innovative therapeutic strategies beyond conventional antibiotics. Emerging approaches—such as phage therapy, AMPs, mAbs, phytochemicals, metal chelators, NPs, and CRISPR-based gene editing—show significant promise by targeting various facets of biofilm architecture, bacterial communication, and resistance mechanisms. These novel interventions not only disrupt biofilm integrity and function but also enhance the efficacy of existing antibiotics and reduce the likelihood of resistance development. Collectively, they offer renewed hope for more effective management of S. aureus infections in both clinical and environmental contexts. As research continues to advance these technologies and address key challenges related to delivery, specificity, and safety, a multidisciplinary and integrated approach will be essential in combating the global threat posed by S. aureus and its biofilm-associated resistance.
