Actinomycosis of the Gallbladder in a Young Diabetic Woman with Acute Lithiasis Cholecystitis: A Case Report and Review of the Literature
Authors: Mojgan Akbarzadeh-Jahromi, Seyed Mohammad Kazem Tadayyon, Sahar Asadi, Neda Soleimani, Sahand Mohammadzadeh, Hossein Afrakhteh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionActinomycosis is a rare, chronic bacterial infection caused by the genus Actinomyces. The cervicofacial form is most common, while gallbladder involvement is exceptionally uncommon, with fewer than 50 cases reported in the literature.Case ReportA 21-year-old diabetic woman presented with one day of nausea, vomiting, and persistent right upper quadrant abdominal pain. Ultrasonography and computed tomography scan revealed an enlarged gallbladder with biliary sludge and a single gallstone. A preoperative diagnosis of acute cholecystitis was made, and cholecystectomy was performed. Histopathological examination confirmed acute cholecystitis and demonstrated numerous filamentous, gram-positive bacteria, consistent with actinomycosis. Following 6 months of penicillin therapy, the patient remains well with no clinical or radiological evidence of recurrence.ConclusionThis case underscores the importance of routine histopathological evaluation of gallbladder specimens. Although rare, gallbladder actinomycosis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of gallbladder disease, particularly in immunocompromised individuals.

Introduction

Actinomycosis is a rare, chronic bacterial infection caused by filamentous, gram-positive organisms of the genus Actinomyces. These organisms are part of the normal flora of the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and female genital tract, and usually exhibit low virulence. When mucosal barriers are disrupted by trauma, surgery, or inflammation, however, they may penetrate tissues and cause invasive disease.1–3 The most common form of actinomycosis is cervicofacial, followed by female genitourinary involvement. Abdominal actinomycosis is uncommon and usually affects the appendix, cecum, or colon. Gallbladder involvement is exceptionally rare, with fewer than 50 cases reported in the literature to date.4–9

Here, we present a case of gallbladder actinomycosis in a young female patient. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the youngest documented case of gallbladder actinomycosis reported in the literature to date.

Case Presentation

A 21-year-old Iranian woman presented to the emergency department with a one-day history of nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, predominantly localized to the right upper quadrant. She denied diarrhea, constipation, or fever. Her past medical history was notable for type 1 diabetes mellitus (DM) for 11 years, managed with insulin (Lantus 16 units subcutaneously at bedtime and NovoRapid 5 units subcutaneously before each main meal). She was single without any history of smoking or alcohol consumption. Psychological and family histories were unremarkable. On admission, her vital signs were stable: blood pressure 110/70 mmHg, pulse rate 72/min, and temperature 36.9 °C. Chest examination was unremarkable. Abdominal examination revealed right upper quadrant tenderness without hepatosplenomegaly or jaundice. Laboratory tests showed a fasting blood sugar of 91 mg/dL and mild leukocytosis [white blood cell (WBC): 12.8×103/μL, hemoglobin: 12.5 g/dl, and platelet count: 319×103/μL]. Liver enzymes, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), were within normal ranges. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a well-distended gall bladder with bile sludge and a 12-mm gallstone. A subsequent abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan confirmed a semi-distended gallbladder with sludge, one stone, and several small intraluminal projections (Figure 1). Based on a pre-operative diagnosis of acute cholecystitis, laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed. Gross examination revealed a large, congested gallbladder with green mucosa, 3 mm wall thickness, bile sludges, and one gallstone (Figure 2). Microscopic examination showed isolated and loose clusters of filamentous, cotton wool-like Actinomyces organisms within areas of mural inflammation and the surface exudate (Figure 3a and b). No sulfur granules were identified, and there was no evidence of mural perforation. Histochemical stains confirmed the mycelial and club-shaped morphology of the organisms (Gram positive, acid-fast negative). The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was discharged on a six-month course of penicillin therapy. One year after surgery, no recurrence has been noticed. Discussion

The first case of gallbladder actinomycosis was described in 1905. Since then, fewer than 50 cases have been reported in the literature. The exact pathogenesis remains unclear, although mucosal disruption due to gallstones, chronic irritation, or immunodeficiency states such as diabetes has been proposed as a predisposing factor.10–13 In the present case, the patient’s long standing diabetes may have contributed to her susceptibility.

The clinical and radiological features of gallbladder actinomycosis are nonspecific, and the diagnosis is usually established only after histopathological examination of the cholecystectomy specimens. Histologically, the infection is characterized by filamentous Gram-positive organisms and, in some cases sulfur granules are visible. Because of its rarity and the lack of distinctive clinical manifestations, a high index of suspicion is required, particularly in atypical cases or in patients with suboptimal responses to standard managements. Treatment typically requires surgical removal of the gallbladder, followed by prolonged antibiotic therapy. Extended courses of antibiotic therapy are often recommended to prevent actinomycosis recurrence.10,11

A total of 21 cases were identified, including the present case, with reports originating from diverse geographic regions. A review of these cases indicates that gallbladder actinomycosis has been observed more often in males, whereas stone-induced cholecystitis is generally more common in females. Most patients were older adults; notably, our patient, at 21 years of age, represents the youngest case documented to date. Immunodeficiency states, such as those associated with DM or chemotherapy-induced immunosuppression, were identified as potential risk factors in several reports, consistent with our case.

Clinically, the majority of patients presented with features of acute cholecystitis, while others had chronic cholecystitis, either preoperatively or identified on histology. In a minority of cases, gallbladder actinomycosis mimicked a mass lesion, often leading to diagnostic delays. This underscores the importance of considering actinomycosis in the differential diagnosis of gallbladder pathology, particularly in patients with predisposing conditions.

Conclusion

Gallbladder actinomycosis, though rare, should be considered in atypical cholecystitis cases, especially in immunocompromised patients. This case emphasizes the diagnostic importance of histopathology. It also highlights that actinomycosis can occur in younger individuals than previously documented.
