Efficacy and safety of remimazolam for sedation during gynaecologic procedures: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Guo Yu, Chun-Lian Zhou, Min-Shu Zhu, Rui Lu, Jun-ming Chen, Jian-bin Cao, Feng Lin, Hai-Fei Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe efficacy of remimazolam compared to propofol for sedation during gynaecologic procedures remains unknown, due to the increased severity of pain associated with these procedures. Therefore, a systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to address this issue.Materials and methodsThe protocol has been registered with PROSPERO, number CRD42024511475. Medline/PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science were searched to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) covering the period up to 10 January 2025. The primary outcomes comprised the success rate of sedation and the incidence of body movement. Secondary outcomes included recovery time and incidence of adverse effects (i.e. hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), dizziness, hiccup and injection pain).ResultsNine RCTs (1129 patients) were included in this meta-analysis. There were no significant differences for the success rate of sedation (RR = 0.98, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.96–1.00) or the incidence of body movement (RR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.49–2.14). Remimazolam had a lower incidence of adverse effects, including hypotension (RR = 0.21, 95% CI 0.11–0.41), bradycardia (RR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.97), respiratory depression (RR = 0.30, 95% CI 0.15–0.61), injection pain (RR = 0.06, 95% CI 0.02–0.17) and dizziness (RR = 0.29, 95% CI 0.10–0.82). However, remimazolam increased the incidence of hiccup (RR = 10.16, 95% CI 2.48–41.61). No significant differences were found regarding the recovery time (MD = 1.58, 95% CI −0.20 to 3.35) or the incidence of PONV (RR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.15–3.05).ConclusionsIn gynaecologic procedures, remimazolam has comparable efficacy to propofol in the success rate of sedation and the incidence of body movement, while exhibiting superior respiratory and cardiovascular safety profiles. These findings should be cautiously generalized to other populations, and warrant further validation.Keywords: Remimazolam, propofol, sedation, gynaecologic procedure, meta-analysis

Introduction

Simple gynaecologic procedures, such as hysteroscopy, can be performed under sedation. However, they can elicit severe pain owing to the abundance of sensory nerves in the uterus [1,2]. These procedures are associated with heightened patient anxiety, which exacerbates the pain experience [3]. While VR technology mitigates anxiety, it fails to relieve pain [4]. This pain induced an increased nociception intensity, closely associated with intraoperative body movement [5], which can disrupt the surgical procedure and potentially elevate the risk of uterine perforation [6]. Therefore, effective and safe sedation is essential during these procedures. It further facilitates the work of surgeons and notably reduces the risk of perioperative adverse events, ultimately enhancing patient satisfaction and tolerability.

As a commonly used intravenous anaesthetic agent, propofol has gained wide acceptance due to its smooth induction and rapid recovery from anaesthesia [7]. However, it exhibits potent inhibitory effects on the cardiorespiratory systems [8], and its injection can be painful [9]. Remimazolam, a novel ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine, exhibits distinct advantages and offers new possibilities for sedation. It rapidly induces loss of consciousness within 1.6 min of intravenous administration in healthy adults, reaching peak sedation within the 3–5 min range [10]. Additionally, its pharmacological effects can be fully reversed using flumazenil [11]. A previous meta-analysis comparing remimazolam with propofol for procedural sedation revealed that there was no significant difference observed in either the success rate of sedation or the time required for loss of consciousness [12]. Furthermore, remimazolam is considered a safer option for sedation in gastroscopy and ERCP, as it reduces the incidence of respiratory depression, hypotension, bradycardia, injection pain and dizziness compared to propofol [13,14].

However, the efficacy of remimazolam for sedation during gynaecologic procedures remains unknown, due to the increased severity of pain associated with these procedures. Therefore, we conducted this comprehensive review of the current literature to analyse the efficacy and safety of remimazolam in comparison with propofol during sedation for gynaecologic procedures.

Materials and methods

Study registration

This systematic review adheres strictly to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and the protocol has been registered with PROSPERO, number CRD42024511475.

Literature search

A systematic literature search was conducted independently by two authors across five international databases: Medline/PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science. This search targeted eligible studies published in English, covering the period up to 10 January 2025. The search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Table S1. To ensure the comprehensiveness of the search, the reference lists of the retrieved studies were manually examined to identify any potentially eligible studies that may have been initially overlooked.

Study selection

All studies were initially independently assessed for eligibility by two reviewers who screened titles and abstracts. The full texts of all potentially relevant studies were further evaluated according to the ‘PICO’ criteria, as follows: (a) population, all adult patients who underwent gynaecologic procedures with sedation; (b) intervention, receive remimazolam for sedation; (c) comparison, receive propofol for sedation; (d) outcome, the success rate of sedation or the incidence of body movement; and (e) study type, randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) non-English language publications; (b) full text was unavailable; and (c) study protocols or ongoing clinical trials. Disagreements during the screening process were resolved through consensus discussion involving a third senior investigator.

Data extraction

During the data extraction process, two authors independently reviewed the included studies and a third senior investigator was consulted to resolve discrepancies. The following data were extracted: first author, publication year, country, procedure type, details of sedation (i.e. initial dose, supplemental dose or maintenance dose, and analgesic), participant characteristics (i.e. age, BMI, ASA grade and sample size) and outcomes. The primary outcomes of the study comprised the success rate of sedation and the incidence of body movement. Secondary outcomes included recovery time and the incidence of adverse effects (i.e. hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), dizziness, hiccup and injection pain). The authors’ definitions of each outcome are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Efforts were made to establish contact with the principal investigators of included studies, with the objective of acquiring any pertinent unpublished data.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers independently evaluated the risk of bias for each study using the Cochrane risk of bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2.0) [15]. This tool assesses risk of bias across six domains: randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, selection of the reported result, and overall risk of bias. Each domain is then classified as low risk, some concerns or high risk of bias. The overall quality of the evidence for each outcome was then graded using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) approach, which categorizes evidence into four levels: high, moderate, low or very low [16]. Disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion involving a third senior investigator.

Statistical analysis

All meta-analyses were performed using the meta package in R software (version 4.1.3). The mean difference (MD) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated for continuous variables, and relative risk (RR) and corresponding 95% CI were calculated for dichotomous variables. Continuous data presented as median (with minimum–maximum values or interquartile ranges) were converted to mean and standard deviation using previously reported methods [17,18]. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistic, and statistical significance (p < .10) was determined through the Cochrane Q test. The I2 statistic was also employed to quantify the degree of heterogeneity, with thresholds of ≥25%, ≥50% and ≥75% indicating low, moderate and high levels of heterogeneity, respectively [19]. A random-effects model was used for all analyses due to the anticipated high clinical heterogeneity resulting from diverse clinical and methodological factors [20]. The same methodology was employed in another paper [21]. Sensitivity analyses were only conducted for primary outcomes using the leave-one-out method. Funnel plots and the Harbord test were used to assess potential publication bias for primary outcomes [22]. Differences were considered statistically significant if the p < .05.

Results

Literature search results

The initial literature search retrieved 472 studies from the five electronic databases: Medline/PubMed (n = 42), Embase (n = 68), The Cochrane Library (n = 119), Scopus (n = 69) and Web of Science (n = 174). After eliminating 217 duplicates, 255 records underwent screening, excluding 227 irrelevant studies. Subsequently, 28 studies remained for full-text review, but one study was further excluded due to the unavailability of the full text. Ultimately, nine RCTs were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis [6,23–30] (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection.

Study characteristics

Main characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. A total of 1129 patients were enrolled in the nine included RCTs, with 630 patients allocated to the remimazolam group and 499 patients to the propofol group. The ASA classifications of these patients ranged from I to III. These studies, published between 2021 and 2024, were all conducted in China. Six studies focused on hysteroscopy [6,24,26–29], two on surgical abortion [23,25] and one did not specify the type of gynaecologic procedure [30]. Administration of sedatives varies across these studies. The initial dosage of remimazolam ranges from 0.2 mg/kg to 0.3 mg/kg, whereas for propofol, it varies from 1.5 mg/kg to 3.0 mg/kg. To maintain a sufficient sedative effect, four of the studies opted for a continuous intravenous infusion method [6,23,27,28], while four studies used supplementary dosing strategies [24–26,30]. And one study adopted both methods to maintain sedation [29]. Furthermore, these studies employed diverse analgesics, encompassing fentanyl [26], alfentanil [6,24], sufentanil [27,29], esketamine [25], butorphanol [23], remifentanil [30] and a combination of flurbiprofen axetil and remifentanil [28].

Risk of bias

The risk of bias is summarized in Figures 2 and 3. One study [26] was evaluated as having a high risk of overall bias while four studies [23,27–29] were evaluated as presenting some concerns, and another four studies [6,24,25,30] were evaluated as having a low risk of bias. Specifically, five studies were evaluated as presenting some concerns in the randomization process, due to insufficient disclosure of allocation concealment details [23,26–29]. Furthermore, one study was evaluated as having a high risk of deviations from the intended interventions and presenting some concerns of missing outcome data [26]. This was attributed to the exclusion of over 5% of the patients after randomization and the absence of an appropriate analysis. Additionally, two studies raised concerns related to the selection of reported results, as the pre-specified analysis plan was unavailable [23,26].

Outcomes

Primary outcomes

Seven studies [24–30] reported the success rate of sedation, revealing no significant difference between remimazolam and propofol groups (RR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.96–1.00; I2 = 0%; Figure 4). Six studies [6,23–25,28,30] reported the incidence of body movement, revealing no significant difference between the two groups (RR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.49–2.14; I2 = 74%; Figure 5).

Secondary outcomes

Results for secondary outcomes are presented in Table 2. Five studies [6,23,25,28,30] assessed the incidence of hypotension. The meta-analysis showed that remimazolam was associated with a lower incidence of hypotension (RR = 0.21, 95% CI 0.11–0.41; I2 = 0%). Four studies [6,25,28,30] reported the incidence of bradycardia, and the meta-analysis showed that remimazolam was associated with a lower incidence of bradycardia (RR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.02–0.97; I2 = 0%). Seven studies [6,24,25,27–30] reported the incidence of respiratory depression, revealing a lower incidence of respiratory depression in the remimazolam group (RR = 0.30, 95% CI 0.15–0.61; I2 = 69.5%). Seven studies [6,24–29] reported the incidence of injection pain. The meta-analysis showed that remimazolam was associated with a lower incidence of injection pain (RR = 0.06, 95% CI 0.02–0.17; I2 = 40.7%). Three studies [24,25,27] reported the incidence of hiccup. The incidence of hiccup was significant higher in the remimazolam group (RR = 10.16, 95% CI 2.48–41.61; I2 = 0%). Five studies [6,23–25,27] reported the incidence of PONV, revealing no significant difference between the remimazolam and propofol groups (RR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.15–3.05; I2 = 66.9%). Five studies [6,23,24,28,29] reported the incidence of dizziness, finding a lower incidence of dizziness in the remimazolam group (RR = 0.29, 95% CI 0.10–0.82; I2 = 54%). Seven studies [6,24–26,28–30] assessed the recovery time and observed no significant difference between the remimazolam and propofol groups (MD = 1.58, 95% CI −0.20 to 3.35; I2 = 97.8%).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the leave-one-out approach to assess the robustness of our primary findings. For the success rate of sedation, the exclusion of either study [27,28] individually resulted in an upper bound of the 95% CI less than 1 (Supplementary Table S3). The results for the incidence of body movement were relatively stable. The pooled RR estimates ranged from 0.86 to 1.24, with all 95% CIs including 1. The heterogeneity decreased from high to low after the removal of one study [6] (Supplementary Table S4). Exploratory leave-one-out analysis was performed for the recovery time to address high heterogeneity, but excluding single studies did not alter the statistical heterogeneity (Supplementary Table S5).

Exploratory subgroup analysis was performed for the body movement to address high heterogeneity. Heterogeneity within subgroups remained consistently high. Furthermore, there was no significant interaction based on procedure type (test for subgroup differences, p = .5714, Supplementary Figure S1).

Publication bias

We assessed the publication bias for primary outcomes using funnel plots and the Harbord test. According to the Harbord test, no evidence of publication bias was found for the success rate of sedation (p = .34) or the incidence of body movement (p = .93). The funnel plots are presented in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Figures S2 and S3).

Certainty of evidence

The quality of evidence for the success rate of sedation was judged to be moderate, due to a one-level downgrading stemming from the high risk of bias in one of the included studies. The quality of evidence for the incidence of body movement was judged to be very low, owing to moderate heterogeneity, wide CIs and included studies rated as having some concerns for risk of bias. The quality of evidence for secondary outcomes ranged from very low to moderate. Additional details are given in Table 2.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the efficacy and safety of remimazolam for sedation during gynaecologic procedures. No significant differences were found in the success rate of sedation or the incidence of body movement between remimazolam and propofol. Furthermore, our results revealed that remimazolam significantly reduced the incidence of adverse effects, including hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, injection pain and dizziness. However, remimazolam was associated with an increased incidence of hiccup. Additionally, no differences were found for the recovery time or the incidence of PONV.

The sedation success rates observed with remimazolam and propofol were comparable, indicating that both sedative agents achieved their intended purpose of inducing unconsciousness and procedure completion without significant differences. Furthermore, the statistical heterogeneity of the results was negligible (I2 = 0%). A previous meta-analysis pertaining to diverse procedural sedation yielded analogous findings [12]. However, other previous reviews reported conflicting results that remimazolam had a reduced success rate of sedation during gastrointestinal endoscopy [31–33]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the depth of sedation and procedure types. The higher remimazolam doses in gynaecologic procedures studies included in our meta-analysis (0.25–0.3 mg/kg) versus gastrointestinal endoscopy studies in other reviews (0.1–0.15 mg/kg) provide a pharmacological explanation for this divergence. First, gynaecologic procedures (e.g. hysteroscopy, dilation and curettage) involve stronger nociceptive stimuli, necessitating deeper sedation targets and consequently higher dosing than gastrointestinal endoscopy. Second, pharmacodynamic analysis indicated a dose-dependent sedative efficacy of remimazolam [34]. Furthermore, dose escalation directly correlates with improved success rate of sedation [24]. Notably, comparative efficacy between remimazolam and propofol remained consistent across younger and older populations when dosing regimens were comparable [33]. These findings indicated that remimazolam dosage optimization should prioritize procedural requirements over age.

Even under deep sedation, the absence of neuromuscular blocking agents may lead to intraoperative body movements, indicating potential issues with sedative efficacy or patient response. It could serve as a warning about inadequate sedation levels. Prior to our study, only one meta-analysis for gastroscopy had synthesized body movement incidence data [13]. Our finding, consistent with that meta-analysis, shows no statistically significant difference in body movement incidence between remimazolam and propofol. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed, limiting our confidence in this finding. Sensitivity analysis suggested that one study [6] might contribute to this heterogeneity. However, specific factors remain elusive, due to variables in clinical practice such as drug dosage, administration speed, use of analgesics and surgeon expertise may influence this result. Furthermore, definition of body movement varied across studies. For the practicing clinician, this heterogeneity means the equivalence between propofol and remimazolam is uncertain and should be interpreted with caution. Careful individual titration of sedation remains essential to prevent disruptive movements, regardless of the chosen agent. Notably, most included studies did not conduct a graded assessment of body movement, and not all movements impact procedural safety. Minor movements may be clinically insignificant, whereas pronounced movements could disrupt procedures. We strongly recommend future research incorporates this to enable precise comparison of sedative agents in inhibiting body movements induced by surgical stimulation.

Transversus abdominis plane block and wound infiltration provide effective analgesia with a favourable safety profile, making them ideal for open abdominal surgery like caesarean section [35]. In contrast, outpatient gynaecologic procedures under sedation often require deeper sedation levels to manage the intense pain associated with uterine manipulation. The deep procedural sedation used by the studies included in our analysis is more adept at managing this procedural pain and mitigating the incidence of body movement [5]. However, deep procedural sedation results in a higher incidence of adverse respiratory events in comparison to moderate sedation [36]. Furthermore, propofol-based sedation is associated with respiratory depression in a dose-dependent manner [37]. Our meta-analysis indicated that, when used for sedation during outpatient gynaecologic procedures, remimazolam was associated with a reduced incidence of respiratory depression compared to propofol. Additionally, our study revealed that remimazolam exhibited more stable hemodynamic characteristics than propofol, with lower incidences of hypotension and bradycardia. Similar results have also been observed when remimazolam is applied to other clinical scenarios [12–14]. Recent research reveals the potential mechanism behind these observations, suggesting that remimazolam causes less reduction in peripheral vascular resistance and maintains a more stable cardiac output compared to propofol [38]. However, a multicentre RCT comparing remimazolam and propofol for induction in patients with high ASA classifications revealed significant differences in events of MAP < 65 mmHg and heart rate < 45 bpm, yet no significant differences were found in the relative decrease of MAP by >20% or >30% from the patient’s preoperative baseline [39].

Despite the prevailing evidence suggesting that remimazolam may offer advantages over propofol in terms of respiration and haemodynamics, this does not mean absolute safety. Remimazolam exhibits dose-dependent respiratory and cardiovascular depression [24,40]. In patients at risk of hypotension, the hemodynamic superiority of remimazolam over propofol is limited [39]. Therefore, anaesthesiologists still need to administer remimazolam with caution, particularly in gynaecologic procedures requiring deep sedation for pain relief and minimizing intraoperative body movements, as well as in high-risk populations prone to hypotension and respiratory depression.

Our findings regarding injection pain and PONV are consistent with previous systematic reviews [12,13,31]. Specifically, our study revealed a significant advantage of remimazolam over propofol in terms of injection pain during sedation for gynaecologic procedures, with a large effect size and a relatively narrow CI. This could be attributed to remimazolam potentially mitigating pain by downregulating bradykinin receptor B1 [41], while propofol acts on vein endothelial tissue, stimulating bradykinin production and increasing vessel permeability, leading to pain upon contacting nerve endings [42]. Both propofol and midazolam were shown to have antiemetic effects [43,44]. Our study found that there was no significant difference in the incidence of PONV between remimazolam and propofol during sedation for gynaecologic procedures. This finding, along with its observed heterogeneity, might be influenced by the diverse analgesic agents employed in the included studies, as opioids were found to be a high-risk factor for PONV [45]. Different opioids exert distinct impacts on PONV, with recent research showing that in hysteroscopy under remimazolam sedation, remifentanil was associated with a significantly lower incidence of PONV compared to alfentanil [46].

Moreover, our finding indicated that remimazolam was associated with a higher incidence of hiccup than propofol. This is a side effect that has received relatively little attention, and its mechanism remains unclear. Benzodiazepines are considered to be associated with hiccups [47], and hiccups induced by midazolam can be reversed by flumazenil [48]. In most instances, hiccups are self-limiting and do not disrupt the procedure [49]. However, hiccups cause sharp fluctuations in intra-abdominal pressure and may suppress oesophageal peristalsis while promoting relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter, which could theoretically increase the risk of reflux [50]. This is particularly relevant in outpatient sedation, which is commonly performed without tracheal intubation and the airway remains unprotected. Consequently, remimazolam should be used with caution in patients with increased risk of reflux, and a strict fasting protocol should be rigorously implemented.

This is a side effect that has received relatively little attention, and its mechanism remains unclear. Hiccups have also been observed with midazolam and can be reversed by flumazenil. Benzodiazepines are considered to be associated with hiccups. Hiccups are typically self-limiting and do not disrupt the procedure in most instances. However, caution should be exercised in individuals who are at risk of regurgitation and aspiration.

Sedation for outpatient gynaecologic procedures requires shorter recovery time and higher recovery quality to enhance patient safety and improve turnover efficiency. This meta-analysis revealed no significant difference in recovery time between remimazolam and propofol. However, the certainty of evidence for this outcome was downgraded due to very serious inconsistency arising from substantial statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 97.8%). Leave-one-out analysis indicated that the heterogeneity was not driven by any single study. Widespread variations in clinical practice across included studies may contribute to the heterogeneity. Consequently, this finding is highly dependent on clinical context and cannot be generalized. Future research should standardize these variables. In terms of recovery profiles, propofol was associated with a higher incidence of postoperative dizziness, potentially attributable to its greater accumulation in adipose tissue [51]. However, patients administered remimazolam experienced a significantly longer time to recover orientation [39]. Although available evidence precludes an accurate assessment of recovery quality, remimazolam sedation can be reversed by flumazenil, which ensures a predictable time of recovery [52]. This feature makes remimazolam particularly applicable in outpatient settings.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results. First, despite conducting a comprehensive search across all relevant databases, the number of included studies was insufficient, and the sample size was small, thus reducing statistical power and precision. Second, four included studies were rated as having some concerns of overall bias, while one was rated as high risk. This limits our confidence in the quality of the data obtained from these RCTs. Third, although some results showed little statistical heterogeneity, high clinical heterogeneity existed due to diverse clinical and methodological factors, such as analgesic and sedation strategies, procedure types and outcome measures. Subgroup analyses stratified by these factors were limited by insufficient data. Fourth, the generalizability of these findings to other regions should be interpreted with caution, as all included studies were conducted exclusively in China. Further validation in populations of other regions is needed. Fifth, potential publication bias and the overestimation risk of remimazolam cannot be excluded given the limited number of included studies, small sample size [53]. Sixth, English-language restrictions may also have introduced publication bias by excluding relevant non-English studies, particularly negative or neutral findings from regional journals. Seventh, the interpretation of results requires caution, as some outcomes (e.g. injection pain, dizziness and PONV) were inconsistently defined across studies, and several studies did not provide definitions. This may introduce bias into the pooled effect estimates and limit the comparability of the findings. Finally, sensitivity analysis indicated fragility of the sedation success rate estimates. Future studies require standardized protocols and larger samples to strengthen evidence robustness.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated comparable sedation success rates and incidence of body movement between remimazolam and propofol for sedation in gynaecologic procedures. Remimazolam exhibited superior respiratory and cardiovascular safety profiles compared to propofol. However, caution must be exercised when extrapolating these conclusions to other regions. Further multicentre research with standardized methodology is needed to confirm these findings in diverse populations.
